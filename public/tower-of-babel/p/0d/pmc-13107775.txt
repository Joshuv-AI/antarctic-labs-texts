Predictors of self-reported sexually transmitted infections (STIs) among men in 54 low and middle income countries (LMICs): a comparison of deep learning and classical machine learning algorithms
Authors: Mequannent Sharew Melaku, Lamrot Yohannes, Berhanu Sharew, Agazhe Aemro, Anas Ali Alhur, Melaku Alelign Mengstie, Alexander Takele Mengesha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSexually transmitted infections (STIs) remain a global health challenge, with 374 million new cases annually. Among men, STIs cause serious complications, increase the risk of HIV infection, and contribute to stigma and economic burdens, especially in low and middle income countries. Traditional models often miss complex risk interactions, while machine learning and deep learning offer stronger predictive tools. This study applied deep learning and traditional machine learning algorithms to predict self-reported STIs and to model predictors of self-reported STIs among men in 54 LMICs to support targeted interventions that aligned with the global STI prevention and control strategies.MethodsThis study used data from Demographics and Health Surveys conducted in 54 LMICs across the globe between 2010 and 2024. A weighted sample of 390,608 sexually active men aged between 15 and 64 years was included in this study. The data were cleaned and weighted by STATA version 17 software and Python version 3.9 software. Descriptive statistics was executed using STATA version 17 software. Python 3.9 software was used for deep learning and traditional machine learning prediction of self-reported STIs. Furthermore, CatBoost, Logistic Regression, Random Forest, LightGBM, AdaBoost, XGBoost, MLP, and TabNet were employed to identify the most influential predictors of self-reported STIs among men. In addition, accuracy, and Area under the curve was used to evaluate the performance of the predictive models.ResultThe meta-analysis showed that 4.44% of men reported STIs (95% CI: 3.46, 5.43), with substantial variation across nations and regions. The highest prevalence was observed in Liberia (18.66%), Lesotho (15.91%), and Uganda (13.32%), while the lowest were observed in Jordan (0.05%), Kyrgyzstan (0.50%), and Armenia (0.38%). Moreover, the prevalence of self-reported STIs was highest in Sub-Saharan Africa (5.50%), followed by Asia (3.99%), Latin America (1.03%), and lowest in the Middle East & North Africa (0.13%). The prevalence of self-reported STIs also showed heterogeneity by income subgroup among LMICs, with low-income countries showing the highest prevalence (5.53%), lower middle-income countries a moderate prevalence (5.19%), and upper middle-income countries the lowest prevalence of self-reported STIs (1.15%). CatBoost achieved an accuracy of 80.10%, an AUC of 88.80%, a precision of 76.00%, and a recall of 89.00%. The SHAP analysis plot based on the CatBoost model identified country income status, region, age, age at first sex, religion, education, HIV knowledge, wealth index, marital status, and place of residence as the top-ranked predictors of self-reported STIs among men in LMICs.ConclusionSelf-reported STIs among men in LMICs remains a significant public health concern, with wide national and regional variation. The highest prevalence was observed in Liberia, Lesotho, and Uganda, while the lowest effects were observed in Jordan, Kyrgyzstan, and Armenia. Moreover, Sub-Saharan Africa was with the highest prevalence of self-reported STIs, followed by Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean, and lowest in the Middle East and North Africa. The prevalence of self-reported STIs also showed a clear heterogeneity by country income status, with low-income countries showing the highest prevalence of self-reported STIs, lower middle-income countries a moderate, and upper middle-income countries showed the lowest prevalence of self-reported STIs among men. CatBoost was identified as a top performing machine learning algorithm to predict self-reported STIs. Country income status, region, age, age at first sex, religion, education, HIV knowledge, wealth index, marital status, and place of residence were identified as the strongest predictors of self-reported STIs. Findings underscore the potential of machine learning, particularly CatBoost, to capture complex determinants of self-reported STIs and to inform targeted STI prevention and control strategies among men living in LMICs.Keywords: Sexually transmitted infections, Self-reported STI, Classical machine learning algorithms, Deep learning algorithms, Low and middle income countries, Men, Meta analysis, Predictors

Introduction

Over 30 pathogens including bacteria, viruses, and parasites can be transmitted sexually, although only 8 to 12 account for most of the global sexually transmitted infection burden. The most common infections are chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, trichomoniasis, human immune deficiency virus (HIV), human papilloma virus (HPV), genital herpes, and hepatitis B virus [1–4]. The most common clinical presentations of sexually transmitted infections are genital ulcers and genital discharge, with other symptoms including itching, pelvic or abdominal pain, warts, and systemic signs such as fever, fatigue, and rash [5–7]. STIs remain a major global health challenge, causing over 374 million new infections annually [8]. More than 520 million people live with genital herpes, and around 300 million women are infected with human papillomavirus. STIs account for approximately 2.5 million deaths each year and contribute substantially to long-term disability and lost healthy life years. The burden is disproportionately high in low and middle income countries, where limited healthcare access, stigma, socio-economic inequalities, and weak surveillance exacerbate the problem. Sub-Saharan Africa alone represents nearly 40% of the global burden.

Untreated sexually transmitted infections pose serious health risks at individual, reproductive, and population levels. Untreated chlamydia and gonorrhea can lead to urethritis and epididymitis, causing chronic pain and infertility [9], untreated syphilis alone contributes to approximately 200,000 stillbirths and neonatal deaths annually and may progress to severe systemic complications such as neuro-syphilis and cardiovascular damage [10]. HPV increases the risk of penile, anal, and oropharyngeal cancers, and ulcerative infections like syphilis and herpes simplex virus significantly heighten susceptibility to HIV acquisition and transmission [11]. In addition to these biological effects, men living with STIs often experience psychological distress, stigma, and relationship difficulties. persistent infections increase the risk of infertility, with a substantial proportion of the estimated 1 in 6 couples worldwide affected by infertility likely linked to STI complications. These consequences highlight the urgent need for early detection, treatment, and prevention strategies targeted at men to mitigate both individual and public health burdens [12, 13].

Although the magnitude and consequences of STIs are substantial, individuals’ reporting behaviors and treatment-seeking practices remain low [14]. Men in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) generally exhibit low to moderate health-seeking behavior for STIs, with roughly 25% to 50% of men with STI symptoms not seeking treatment [15]. In some urban youth and key populations, such as men who have sex with men, the proportion not seeking treatment can reach 72%, depending on the local context [16, 17]. Self-reported sexually transmitted infections among men are influenced by a set of well documented variables. Sociodemographic variables include age, educational level, household wealth index, employment status, marital status, place of residence, and exposure to media. Behavioral variables commonly associated with higher odds of reporting an STIs include number of lifetime sexual partners, condom use at last sex, frequency of condom use, age at first sexual intercourse, engaging in transactional sex, alcohol use before sex, and use of other psychoactive substances. Health system and knowledge related variables include awareness of STI symptoms, knowledge of prevention methods, recent health facility visits, HIV testing history, and distance to health facilities. Additional contextual variables include partner’s STI history, partner concurrency, and mobility or migration status [18–31].

Though a few studies had been explored the determinants of self-reported STIs in some LMICs region [24, 26, 28–30] using a conventional statistical analysis, previous studies have limited evidence on synthesizing findings across other low and middle income countries (LMICs) where STI are increasingly affecting men. Moreover, country specific studies across LMICs regions including Tanzania [18], Nigeria [27], Ghana [32], Ethiopia [20, 22, 23], Mexico [21], Peru [19], China [19], Senegal [31], Kenya [25], and Papua New Guinea [33] had been tried to explored predictors of self-reported STIs. However, previous studies relied on small samples and conventional statistical methods, which may not adequately capture the complexity of factors influencing self-reported STIs. Furthermore, the available evidence is highly fragmented, conducted in diverse settings across LMICs countries. In addition, an up to date evidence is highly demanding for tailored programmatic intervention for STI prevention and control. Though conventional statistical models have provided valuable insights regarding self-reported STIs [20, 26, 30], previous evidences are limited by the assumptions of linearity and are less adept at capturing complex interactions among predictors. This limitation is particularly pronounced in multi-country datasets such as the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS). Machine learning algorithms can process high-dimensional data, uncover nonlinear relationships, and improve predictive accuracy beyond traditional regression techniques reveal [34, 35]. To address the limitations of conventional regression models when applied to DHS data, we employed traditional machine learning and deep learning algorithms. DHS dataset is large, multi-country, and highly complex, containing numerous sociodemographic, behavioral, and contextual variables with nonlinear relationships and frequent heterogeneity across surveys. Machine learning and deep learning algorithms, are specifically designed to handle high dimensional tabular data, capture nonlinear associations, and improve predictive accuracy. Moreover, interpretability tools such as SHAP values allow us to identify and explain key predictors of self-reported STIs in ways that are transparent and actionable that can inform targeted interventions and resource allocation. Although there are studies in Ethiopia [23] and in Zimbabwe [36] using machine learning algorithms, findings are country specific and difficult to draw a conclusion for the largest population of LMICs. Hence this study was aimed to determine the pooled prevalence of self-reported STIs and to explore deep learning and traditional machine learning algorithms for modeling predictors of self-reported STIs among men in 54 LMICs using nationally representative DHS data.

The study highlighted key factors linked to self-reported STIs among men in LMICs, offering insights for policymakers, governments, and researchers. By integrating sociodemographic, behavioral, and structural variables across multiple contexts, the analysis generated robust, generalizable evidence that can strengthen STI surveillance, enhance risk stratification, and inform tailored interventions. Findings supports evidence-based strategies to improve STIs detection, treatment, and prevention and aligns with the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Health Sector Strategy on STIs (2022–2030), which calls for innovation and digital health solutions to accelerate progress in reducing the global STIs burden.

Methods

Study design and study period

This study employed a cross-sectional design based on secondary data obtained from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) conducted in 54 LMICs between 2010 and 2024. The DHS program provides nationally representative, population-based data on a wide range of health and demographic indicators, including self-reported STIs. The cross-sectional nature of the data reflects information collected at a single point in time for each country. The study utilized these datasets to perform further statistical analyses aimed to understand the predictors of self-reported STIs.

Study area

This study included 54 LMICs across the four WHO regions. The study area encompasses diverse nations across four major world regions including Sub Sahara Africa (SSA), North Africa and the Middle East, Asia, and Latin America and the Caribbean, highlighting broad geographical coverage relevant for global analyses. In SSA, a total of 35 countries were represented, namely: Zimbabwe, Zambia, Uganda, Togo, Tanzania, South Africa, Sierra Leone, Senegal, Rwanda, Niger, Nigeria, Namibia, Mozambique, Mauritania, Mali, Malawi, Madagascar, Liberia, Lesotho, Kenya, Guinea, Ghana, Gambia, Gabon, Ethiopia, Côte d’Ivoire, Democratic Republic of Congo, The Republic of Congo, Comoros, Chad, Cameroon, Burundi, Burkina Faso, Benin, and Angola. From the North Africa and Middle East region, the countries included are Armenia, Albania, and Jordan. The Asia region includes Afghanistan, Cambodia, India, Indonesia, Kyrgyzstan, Maldives, Myanmar, Nepal, Pakistan, and Timor-Leste. In Latin America and the Caribbean, the countries included were Colombia, the Dominican Republic, Guatemala, Haiti, Honduras, and Papua New Guinea (Fig. 1).

Source and study population

The source population comprises all sexually active men aged between 15 and 64 years who have a complete information for STIs in the most recent DHS surveys across LMICs. The study population includes men within the same age group who have a complete information for STIs in the selected countries.

Study variables

Dependent variable and measurement

The dependent variable in this study was self-reported STIs, indicating whether a man has experienced a sexually transmitted infections or not. This variable is derived from the Demographic and Health Survey using two items: mv763b and mv763c, which ask whether men have experienced genital ulcers or genital discharge, respectively, with responses coded as ‘Yes’ or ‘No’. Men reporting at least one of these symptoms are classified as STI cases, while those reporting neither symptom are considered STI-negative. Thus, the STI variable is a binary outcome distinguishing men who have self-reported STIs from those who have not.

Independent variables

This study examined determinants of self-reported STIs among men in LMICs, considering sociodemographic, behavioral, and environmental factors. Sociodemographic factors included age, education, sex and age of the household head, marital status, religion, working status, age at first sexual intercourse, country income status, and household wealth index. Behavioral factors captured health-related knowledge and practices, such as media exposure, comprehensive HIV knowledge, STI knowledge, STI history, health insurance coverage, tobacco use, HIV testing, and engagement in paid sex. Environmental and structural factors encompassed place of residence and regional context.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The study analyzed a weighted sample of 390,608 men from 54 LMICs, selected using a stratified multistage cluster sampling design with urban rural stratification. The final sample included 175,822 urban and 214,786 rural men.

Data collection tool and procedures

The DHS employs five standard questionnaires: household, women’s, men’s, biomarker, and health facility. Survey instruments, sampling procedures, and data collection methods are harmonized across countries. The men’s questionnaire collects information from eligible men aged 15–64, including self-reported STIs, which are documented in the DHS men’s record file.

Data quality control

The questionnaire was pretested in regions outside the actual sample to support training for data collectors and supervisors before fieldwork. This pretest evaluated the data collection tools and the Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing (CAPI) system. It also assessed staff competence, workload, training procedures, data collection timelines, as well as administrative, financial, and logistical arrangements. Additionally, data transmission reliability, quality monitoring, publicity efforts, and data processing methods were assessed. To ensure data integrity, pretest clusters were excluded from the main survey. After field practice, a debriefing session was conducted to identify challenges and inform necessary questionnaire revisions. Feedback was shared with teams during debriefings and daily meetings. Participants also trained on electronic data entry using tablet-based CAPI, transitioning from paper forms used in practice. Insights from the debriefing refined the questionnaires, CAPI programming, and field logistics prior to main data collection. Data extraction, cleaning, and recoding were performed using STATA version 17 and Python version 3.9 software.

Data management and analysis

Data Source

This study used secondary data from DHS conducted between 2010 and 2024 in 54 LMICs to develop and validate models predicting self-reported STIs among men. These nationally representative dataset is publicly accessible via the DHS Program, include detailed information on men’s health, socioeconomic factors, sexual behaviors, healthcare access, and STI status. The data are organized in tabular form, with rows representing individuals and columns representing variables, and are available in formats compatible with SPSS, STATA, and CSV. To ensure comparability across the 54 DHS, we systematically harmonized core variables relevant to self-reported STI prediction. Categorical variables were aligned to common definitions, continuous variables were standardized using uniform cut points, and country-specific variables without cross-survey equivalents were excluded. Data extraction, cleaning, and analysis were conducted using python 3.9 software for machine learning analysis. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies, percentages, and text, through tables and graphs using STATA version 17 software. Moreover, meta-analysis was conducted using STATA version 17 software.

Data preprocessing

Prior to model development, the datasets underwent rigorous preprocessing to ensure readiness for analysis. The pipeline included data cleaning, handling missing values, feature encoding, normalization, feature engineering and selection, data transformation, and dataset balancing, all are essential for improving model performance and generalizability.

Data cleaning

All variables were thoroughly examined to identify any missing values or potential outliers prior to model development. During this process, missing data were detected in key numerical variables, specifically age at first sex and paid sex. Approximately 4% missing was found for age at first sex variable and about 6% to the paid sex variable. These missing values were subsequently treated using the median imputation method, which replaces missing entries with the median of the observed values for each variable. This approach ensures that the central tendency of the data is preserved while minimizing distortion in downstream analyses. Moreover, Outlier detection was performed on continuous variables using both the IQR and Z-score methods, supported by visual inspections such as boxplots. Categorical and binary variables were assessed using count plots, confirming normal distributions without anomalous values. The analysis showed that all features of each variables fell within normal statistical ranges, and no true outliers were identified.

Data transformation

To prepare the dataset for machine learning, several feature-engineering steps were applied, including recoding, discretization, and binning. Variables such as media exposure were transformed into binary indicators coded as 0 for “No” and 1 for “Yes.” Age at first sex was grouped into meaningful intervals, with categories representing no sexual activity, ages 11–19, 20–24, and 25 years or older. Similarly, the wealth index was re-categorized into poor, middle, and rich.

Data splitting

After completing all preprocessing steps, the combined dataset including 390,608 instances from the DHS data was partitioned into training and testing subsets to ensure robust model evaluation and generalizability. The dataset was initially split into training (80%) and testing (20%) sets. Within the training set, a validation subset (25%) was created to support hyperparameter tuning and early stopping during model training. Stratified sampling was applied to maintain consistent proportions of the target class (self-reported STIs) across all subsets, while random shuffling with a fixed seed ensured reproducibility and minimized selection bias. Furthermore, K-fold cross-validation was used to validate the model’s performance by dividing the data into K subsets, or “folds.” The model was trained and tested K times, with each test set being a separate fold and the remaining folds serving as the training dataset.

Class balancing

In the training set, men with self-reported STIs was highly imbalanced, with roughly 1:20 (one man reporting yes for every twenty men). Class imbalance in the target variable (self-reported STIs) can bias predictions, particularly for the minority class. To address this, Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied only to the training dataset, generating synthetic minority instances by interpolating existing ones, which increased representation while preserving data distribution [37]. Hence, SMOTE transformed the imbalance in the training dataset from a 1:20 (one to twenty) ratio to a perfectly balanced 1 to 1 ratio, in both the no and yes classes [38].

Feature selection

Feature selection was performed, specifically Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) feature selection technique using a Random Forest classifier was applied to identify the most important predictors. Correlation analysis further removed redundant and highly correlated features. This process reduced the original 28 features to 17, focusing on variables most strongly associated with self-reported STIs.

Model training

Eight traditional machine learning and deep learning algorithms was applied to predict self-reported STIs among men across LMICs including Logistic Regression, CatBoost, Random Forest, light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), AdaBoost, multilayer perceptron (MLP), TabNet, and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) [39–43]. Logistic regression was used as the baseline model for comparison, while ensemble methods such as Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM were emphasized for their strength in capturing non-linear relationships, managing high-dimensional data, and reducing overfitting through bagging and boosting techniques. Ensemble methods combine multiple weak learners to improve predictive accuracy and robustness, particularly in complex, high-dimensional survey data. Deep learning models such as TabNet was incorporated to capture complex nonlinear relationships and higher order interactions between sociodemographic, behavioral, and contextual predictors of self-reported STIs. Both bagging and boosting techniques were employed to reduce variance and prevent overfitting. Explainable AI (XAI) techniques, such as SHAP values, were applied to identify and interpret the most influential predictors of self-reported STIs. Each model was trained on the training dataset, and K-fold cross-validation was used to validate performance and reduce the risk of overfitting. Hyperparameter optimization was performed using Randomized Search with stratified 5-fold cross-validation. Key hyperparameters, including the number of estimators, maximum tree depth, minimum samples per leaf, and learning rate (for boosting algorithms), were systematically tuned. The best hyperparameter combinations were selected based on the highest Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC-AUC) on the validation set, ensuring strong performance on both training and unseen data.

Model selection

After training, multiple classifiers were evaluated to identify the most suitable model for predicting self-reported STIs among men. Since the outcome variable is categorical with two mutually exclusive classes, the problem was treated as a binary classification task. Model performance was compared using a set of evaluation metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (ROC-AUC). During cross-validation, the consistency of each model’s performance across different folds was assessed to ensure reliability and stability. The final model was selected based on the best balance between predictive power and generalization to unseen data. This approach ensured that the chosen model was both accurate and robust, making it the most appropriate for predicting self-reported STIs among men.

Evaluation metrics

The performance of the models was assessed using a wide range of evaluation metrics to ensure a balanced and thorough measurement of predictive accuracy and reliability. Model performance was compared using a set of evaluation metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (ROC-AUC). These metrics collectively ensured a robust evaluation, highlighting both the strengths and potential limitations of each model in predicting self-reported STIs [44, 45].

Shapley additive explanations (SHAP)

The relationship between predictors and the outcome was analyzed using SHAP, which also highlighted the most influential variables in predicting self-reported STIs [46]. SHAP analysis uses a game theory framework to offer a global or local interpretation and explanation for any machine learning model prediction (Fig. 2) [46].

Meta-analysis

Prevalence and standard error for each country were calculated using the binomial distribution formula. Heterogeneity was assessed using the chi-square test, I² statistic, and p-value. Subgroup analysis was conducted based on region (SSA, Middle East and North Africa, Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean) and world bank income classification of countries. The prevalence estimates of self-reported STIs was presented using forest plot with 95% confidence intervals.

Heterogeneity

In meta-analysis, heterogeneity is typically evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic. A p-value less than 0.10 from the Q test indicates the presence of heterogeneity. The I² statistic shows the percentage of variation due to differences between studies, ranging from 0% to 100%, with higher values indicating more inconsistency [47]. When heterogeneity is significant and high, it indicates that there is a significant variation between studies and suggest the choice of the model.

Result

Descriptive statistics

The study sample represented a diverse set of LMICs, with nearly half of respondents originating from SSA (49.00%), followed by Asia (29.49%) and Latin America and the Caribbean (18.14%), while smaller proportions came from the Middle East and North Africa (3.37%). Most participants resided in lower middle income countries (58.55%), reflecting the focus on resource-limited settings where STI prevention is particularly important. More than half lived in rural areas (54.99%), and most households were headed by men (86.91%), with high employment levels (84.35%), suggesting potential gendered influences on health decision-making and access to services. The population was largely educated, with 83.80% having attended primary, secondary, or higher education, while 16.20% had no attended formal education.

The majority of respondents were adults aged 25 to 44 years (56.80%), 22.80% aged 45 + years, and 20.35% aged 15 to 24 years, highlighting that most were in the prime working and reproductive age groups. In terms of religious affiliation, Catholics represented the largest group (46.27%), followed by Methodists (15.21%), and other Christian denominations (14.69%), which could influence cultural and social practices related to STIs. Economic status, measured by the DHS wealth index, showed 43.64% were rich, 20.18% middle, and 36.18% poor, reflecting disparities in access to resources. More than half of participants (51.93%) practiced sexual intercourse between age 15 and 19, and 5.91% men experienced an STIs in the previous 12 months, underscoring ongoing public health challenges and the need for targeted sexual health interventions among sexually active populations (Table 1).

Meta-analysis findings

The pooled prevalence of self-reported STIs among men across LMICs

The meta-analysis revealed that the pooled prevalence of self-reported STIs across all LMICs among men was 4.44% (95% Confidence Interval (CI): 3.46, 5.43), demonstrating a significant overall effect. However, considerable heterogeneity was detected (I² = 63.3%, p < 0.001), reflecting substantial variability between LMICs. Country-specific estimates ranged markedly, with the highest prevalence of self-reported STIs observed in Liberia 18.66% (95% CI: 8.27, 29.06), Lesotho 15.91% (95% CI: 6.15, 25.66), and Uganda 13.32% (95% CI: 4.26, 22.38), while the lowest was found in Jordan 0.05% (95% CI: -0.55, 0.65), Kyrgyzstan 0.50% (95% CI: -1.38, 2.37), and Armenia 0.38% (95% CI: -1.26, 2.02) (Fig. 3).

The pooled prevalence of self-reported STIs among men in low and middle income countries

Subgroup analysis of self-reported STIs prevalence among men across LMICs

The analysis of self-reported STIs among men across LMICs detected a substantial regional differences. In SSA, the pooled prevalence of self-reported STIs was 5.50% (95% CI: 4.33, 6.66), particularly in Liberia 18.86% (95% CI: 2.77, 29.05) and Lesotho 15.91% (95% CI: 6.15, 25.66), while the lowest prevalence of self-reported STIs was observed in Niger 1.05% (95% CI: -1.87, 3.76) and Mauritania 1.76% (95% CI: -1.75, 5.27). In the Middle East and North Africa, the prevalence of self-reported STIs was generally minimal 0.13% (95% CI: -0.43, 0.69), with the highest prevalence in Albania 1.50% (95% CI: -1.74, 4.74) and the lowest in Armenia 0.38% (95% CI: -1.26, 2.02). Latin America and the Caribbean showed moderate prevalence of self-reported STIs 2.08% (95% CI: 0.34, 3.81), with Haiti recording the highest prevalence 8.29% (95% CI: 0.94, 15.64) and Colombia 1.03% (95% CI: -1.66, 3.72) the lowest prevalence of self-reported STIs. In Asia, the highest prevalence of self-reported STIs was observed in Pakistan 10.27% (95% CI: 2.18, 18.37), Nepal 9.31% (95% CI: 1.56, 17.07), and India 8.41% (95% CI: 1.00, 15.81), whereas Maldives 1.52% (95% CI: -1.74, 4.87) and Indonesia 1.84% (95% CI: -1.74, 5.42) had the lowest prevalence of self-reported STIs; the pooled regional prevalence of self-reported STIs in Asia was 3.99% (95% CI: 1.84, 6.13). Overall, significant heterogeneity between regions (I²=63.3, p < 0.001) was observed, indicates that self-reported STIs varies considerably by geographic region (Fig. 4). Moreover, self-reported STIs varies by country income classification. Low-income countries exhibit the highest pooled prevalence estimate of 5.53% (95% CI: 3.64, 7.41), followed by lower middle income countries with a pooled prevalence of 5.19% (95% CI: 3.76, 6.61). Upper middle income countries demonstrate a lower prevalence of self-reported STIs 1.15% (95% CI: 0.11, 2.19). This pattern is reinforced by the statistically significant heterogeneity observed between groups (I²=63.3, p < 0.001), confirming that income level is a major explanatory factor for the variation in the prevalence of self-reported STIs (Fig. 5).

Machine learning analysis findings

Machine learning algorithms

The models evaluated can be broadly classified into traditional machine learning algorithms and deep learning algorithms. Among the traditional machine learning algorithms, CatBoost demonstrated the best performance. CatBoost achieved an accuracy of 80.10%, an AUC of 88.80%, precision of 76.00%, and recall of 89.00%, indicating superior ability to correctly identify both positive and negative cases. XGBoost also performed well with an accuracy of 76.00%, AUC of 79.80%, precision of 72.00%, and recall of 85.00%, while LightGBM showed balanced performance with an accuracy of 70.10%, AUC of 72.70%, precision of 67.00%, and recall of 80.00%. Logistic Regression had the lowest performance with an accuracy of 60.80%, AUC of 65.80%, precision of 61.00%, and recall of 62.00%. Random Forest and AdaBoost had moderate results, with accuracies of 64.80% and 63.80%, AUCs of 69.80% and 68.90%, precision of 62.00% each, and recall of 77.00% and 71.00%, respectively (Table 2; Fig. 6).

To aid interpretation, accuracy reflects overall classification success, but in imbalanced datasets (STIs prevalence of 4.44%), recall and precision provide more meaningful insights. The high recall achieved by CatBoost (89.00%) indicates strong ability to correctly identify men reporting STIs, which is critical for public health surveillance in LMICs. Boosting algorithms such as CatBoost and XGBoost outperformed deep learning models because the DHS dataset is structured tabular data with categorical and ordinal variables. Boosting methods are specifically optimized for this type of data, efficiently handling categorical features and capturing nonlinear interactions without extensive preprocessing (Table 2; Fig. 6).

Discussion

Interpretation of the pooled prevalence of self-reported STIs

The objective of this study was to determine the pooled prevalence of self-reported STIs and to explore deep learning and machine learning algorithms for modeling predictors of self-reported STIs among men in 54 LMICs using nationally representative DHS data. The pooled prevalence of self-reported STIs among men across LMICs was 4.44% (95% CI: 3.46, 5.43), with substantial heterogeneity, indicating marked variation between countries. The highest prevalence of self-reported STIs was observed in Liberia, Lesotho, and Uganda, while very small prevalence of self-reported STIs was found in countries such as Jordan, Kyrgyzstan, and Armenia. Moreover, the prevalence of self-reported STIs varied substantially across regions and by country income status. Regional differences were evident in the prevalence of self-reported STIs, reflecting variations in epidemiology, health system capacity, and sociocultural factors. SSA, where many countries are in the low income category, showed the highest pooled regional prevalence of self-reported STIs, whereas regions with more upper middle income countries, such as the Middle East and North Africa and parts of Latin America and the Caribbean, demonstrated much lower prevalence of self-reported STIs. Asia showed moderate prevalence of self-reported STIs, reflecting its combination of lower middle income and upper middle income countries. The clear gradient, where low income and lower middle income countries showed higher pooled effects than upper middle income countries, indicates that economic capacity strongly influences self-reported STIs. These differences are justified by well-known structural and health system disparities across regions and income groups. Countries with the highest prevalence often face limited access to STIs screening and treatment, high background prevalence, and socioeconomic constraints that reduce health seeking behavior and prevention uptake. Weak sexual health infrastructure, fewer diagnostic resources, and gaps in sexual health education also contribute to sustained transmission. Overall, the combined influence of regional context and economic level explains the observed variation, emphasizing the need for context specific strategies that address structural, behavioral, and health system drivers of self-reported STIs across LMICs setting.

Comparison of the pooled prevalence of self-reported STIs with previous literatures

The magnitude of this study is in line with findings from East Africa [26], while the finding is higher than findings from SSA [30]. This reflect differences in study populations, methodological approaches, or local epidemiological patterns, including higher exposure to risk factors in the specific communities included in this study. On the other hand the finding is lower than from findings among adolescents in Mali [48] and among young women in SSA [49]. Adolescents and young women are more vulnerable to STIs due to early sexual debut, limited education, and social pressures, while study differences also affect reported prevalence, highlighting the need for context-specific interventions.

Interpretation of machine learning findings

To predict self-reported STIs among men in LMICs, a total of eight algorithms were evaluated, including six traditional machine learning models (CatBoost, XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest, AdaBoost, and Logistic Regression) and two deep learning models (MLP and TabNet). These models were trained on both balanced and imbalanced datasets and assessed for their predictive performance. Among the machine learning algorithms, CatBoost achieved the highest performance, with an accuracy of 80.10%, an AUC of 88.80%, a precision of 76.00%, and a recall of 89.00%. An AUC of 88.80% demonstrates strong discriminative ability, meaning the model can reliably distinguish men with and without self-reported STIs across thresholds. A recall of 89.00% indicates that most self-reported STIs cases were correctly identified, which is critical for public health screening and risk stratification in LMICs. These results suggest that boosting algorithms can support targeted interventions by identifying high risk groups with reasonable accuracy and sensitivity.

XGBoost also performed strongly, achieving an accuracy of 76.0%, AUC of 79.80%, precision of 72.00%, and recall of 85.00%, while LightGBM demonstrated moderate performance with an accuracy of 70.10%, AUC of 72.70%, precision of 67.00%, and recall of 80.00%. Random Forest and AdaBoost yielded intermediate results (accuracies of 64.80% and 63.80%, AUCs of 69.80% and 68.90%, precision of 62.00%, and recall of 77.00% and 71.00% respectively), whereas Logistic Regression showed the lowest performance overall (accuracy of 60.80%, AUC of 65.80%, precision of 61.00%, and recall of 62.00%). The deep learning algorithms, MLP and TabNet, achieved moderate predictive performance with accuracies of 68.10% and 68.70%, AUCs of 74.80% and 75.50%, precision of 64.00% for both, and recall values of 82.00% and 84.00% respectively. While effective in capturing complex nonlinear relationships, these models did not surpass the top-performing boosting-based machine learning algorithms. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Despite the use of 10-fold cross validation, the risk of overfitting remains, particularly given the high dimensionality of DHS data. The class imbalance (self-reported STIs prevalence of 4.44%) may bias predictions, although SMOTE was applied to mitigate this. Finally, generalizability across diverse LMIC settings may be constrained by differences in cultural norms, health system capacity, and survey quality. These limitations highlight the need for cautious interpretation and further validation in country specific contexts.

Comparison of machine learning findings with previous literatures

The superior performance of boosting algorithms, particularly CatBoost, highlights their ability to uncover complex patterns between self-reported STIs and its determinants that traditional methods may miss. SHAP analysis was applied to improve interpretability and identify the most influential predictors of self-reported STIs. A comparative analysis of machine learning models for predicting self-reported STIs using national demographic health surveys reveals that model performance is highly context-dependent. A study leveraging the Zimbabwe DHS demonstrated that MLP algorithm was exceptionally effective. It achieved an accuracy of 87.54%, supported by very high recall (97.29%) and precision (89.64%), resulting in a robust F1-Score of 93.31%. These metrics suggest the model was particularly adept to correctly identifying true positive cases while maintaining a strong balance between sensitivity and precision, though its AUC (66.78%) indicates a potential area for improvement in its overall ranking capability [36]. Conversely, research conducted on the Ethiopian DHS identified Random Forest algorithm as the top performer. It attained a balanced accuracy of 69.48% and an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 68.50% [23]. This discrepancy in results underscores a critical insight: there is no universally “best” algorithm. The optimal model for self-reported STIs prediction is not inherent to the algorithm itself but is instead determined by the specific characteristics of the dataset, the features available, and the demographic and behavioral nuances of the population being studied.

The analysis revealed that men’s country income status, region, age, age at first sex, religion, education, comprehensive HIV knowledge, wealth index, marital status, and place of residence were the top ten predictors of self-reported STIs across LMICs. The analysis of key predictors of self-reported STIs across LMICs reveals both shared and region specific factors. The CatBoost SHAP analysis identified social and structural determinants, particularly education, wealth, age, and religion, as the strongest predictors in SSA, highlighting the importance of socioeconomic status, cultural norms, and access to healthcare were the top predictors of self-reported STIs. Personal behaviors, including age at first sex, marital status, and HIV testing, were also significant, reflecting how life stage and health seeking behaviors influence self-reported STIs. Regional analyses further underscore these patterns: in Middle East and North Africa, early sexual intercourse, age, education, and socioeconomic status were the dominant factors, with cultural and relational variables such as religion and marital status shaping self-reported STIs; in Latin America and the Caribbean, broader structural conditions, particularly national income, emerged as the primary determinants, while age, education, socioeconomic status, cultural norms, and behavioral indicators also contributed to shape self-reported STIs; in Asian, structural determinants such as education, national income, and wealth strongly predicted self-reported STIs, with age, age at first sexual activity, religion, tobacco use, employment, and marital status further illustrating the interaction between systemic inequality, personal circumstances, and sociocultural context. Overall, across LMICs, education, wealth, age, and religion consistently emerged as the most influential predictors of self-reported STIs, while behavioral, cultural, and relational factors varied by region, emphasizing the complex interplay of structural and individual determinants in shaping self-reported STIs.

Country income status and household wealth index are identified as the top predictors of self-reported STIs among men, that dictates men with lower economic status and residing in the low and lower middle income countries are high likely to self-reported STIs. Similarly, a study conducted in Ethiopia using machine learning algorithms revealed that wealth index was among the top ten predictors of self-reported STIs [23]. This may be explained by the fact that self-reported STIs among men align with socioeconomic theory and previous empirical evidence. Men from lower-income households or countries often face limited access to sexual health education, preventive services, and healthcare facilities, which increases their risk of acquiring STIs. These factors collectively contribute to higher reported STIs prevalence in economically disadvantaged populations. Similarly, LMICs region also identified as a top predictor of self-reported STIs among men in LMICs. Previous literatures also reported region among the best predictors of self-reported STIs [23]. Moreover, traditional statistical analysis also reported that region as a significant determinant of self-reported STIs [20]. Regional variation may reflect differences in cultural norms, sexual behaviors, healthcare accessibility, and public health infrastructure, all of which influence STI. These consistent findings suggest that geographical context plays a critical role in shaping men’s vulnerability to STIs in LMICs, emphasizing the need for region-specific interventions and policies.

Moreover, age of men was identified as the top performing predictors of self-reported STIs. Previous literatures conducted in Ethiopia [23], and Zimbabwe [36] also revealed that age as a top performing predictors of self-reported STIs. Moreover, studies conducted in Ethiopia [20] and SSA [30] using traditional statistical analysis revealed that age was a significant determinant of self-reported STIs. Age is a well-established determinant of sexual health outcomes, as it is closely linked with sexual debut, behavioral risk exposure, and biological susceptibility across the life course. The convergence of evidence across different settings and methodological approaches underscores the critical role of age in shaping men’s vulnerability to STIs in LMICs. Likewise, age at first sex was identified as among the top performing predictors for self-reported STIs. Previous literatures also documented the effect of age at first sex in determining self-reported STIs [23, 36]. Likewise traditional statistical analysis also reported age at first sex as a predictor of STIs [30]. Early sexual practice is strongly associated with increased STI risk, as it often extends the period of sexual activity, increases the likelihood of multiple partnerships, and may occur in contexts with limited protective knowledge or resources. These findings highlight that earlier sexual experience substantially contributes to men’s vulnerability to STIs in LMICs, underscoring the importance of interventions that delay sexual debut and promote safer sexual practices.

On the other hand, religion was identified as one of the top-performing predictors of self-reported STIs similar with previous literatures [36]. Similarly, studies conducted in Ethiopia [20] using multilevel logistic regression revealed that religion of the participant was a significant determinant of self-reported STIs among men. Religious affiliation can influence sexual behavior, norms, and practices, including attitudes toward premarital sex, condom use, and multiple partnerships, thereby shaping STI risk. These findings suggest that religious and cultural contexts play a pivotal role in men’s vulnerability to STIs and should be considered when designing targeted prevention strategies.

Moreover, educational status of men also identified as one of the top performing predictors of self-reported STIs. Previous literatures conducted using machine learning algorithm in Ethiopia [23], and Zimbabwe [36] revealed that age as a top performing predictors of self-reported STIs. Evidence suggests that men with higher education are less likely to develop STIs because education improves health literacy, promotes safer sexual behaviors, and enhances access to health services and preventive resources. Educated men are also more likely to adopt protective practices such as consistent condom use, delay sexual debut, and seek timely treatment when symptomatic. In contrast, men with lower educational attainment may have limited awareness of STIs transmission, prevention, and treatment, increasing their vulnerability.

Similarly, place of residence was identified as one of the top-performing predictors of self-reported STIs among men in LMICs. Previous study conducted in SSA [30] revealed that place of residence was a significant determinant of self-reported STIs. Men residing in rural areas are often at higher risk due to limited access to healthcare services, inadequate sexual health education, and reduced availability of preventive measures such as condoms and STI screening. Rural settings may also be characterized by stigma surrounding sexual health, leading to delays in seeking care and underutilization of available services.

Moreover, marital status of men was identified as among the top ten predictors of self-reported STIs supported with previous literatures [23]. Evidence suggests that unmarried, divorced, or widowed men are more likely to engage in multiple or casual sexual partnerships, transactional sex, or inconsistent condom use, all of which heighten the risk of STIs. In contrast, married men are more likely to have stable partnerships and reduced exposure to high-risk behaviors, although marital instability and infidelity can still contribute to vulnerability. These findings underscore the role of marital dynamics in shaping sexual behavior and STI risk, highlighting the need for interventions that consider relationship status in STI prevention strategies. Likewise, comprehensive HIV knowledge identified as among the top ten predictors of self-reported STIs, consistent with previous literatures [30]. Individuals with greater HIV knowledge are more likely to engage in preventive behaviors, including consistent condom use, regular STI testing, and avoidance of high-risk sexual practices, thereby reducing their susceptibility to STIs. Conversely, limited knowledge may contribute to misconceptions, risky sexual behavior, and delayed care-seeking, increasing the likelihood of infection.

Implications of the study

This study has several important implications for public health policy, program development, and research in LMICs. First, the high overall prevalence of self-reported STIs, particularly in SSA and low income countries, underscores the urgent need for targeted prevention and treatment interventions. Structural barriers, including limited access to healthcare, weak sexual health infrastructure, and socioeconomic constraints, must be addressed to reduce STI vulnerability. Second, the findings highlight the importance of context specific strategies. Regional differences indicate that interventions should consider cultural norms, healthcare system capacity, and local sexual behavior patterns. The clear income gradient further suggests that economically disadvantaged populations should be prioritized for STI screening, sexual health education, and access to preventive and treatment services.

Third, the study demonstrates the potential of machine learning models, particularly boosting algorithms such as CatBoost, to identify high risk groups and support evidence-based decision making. Key predictors identified, including age, age at first sex, marital status, education, religion, place of residence, and HIV knowledge, provide actionable insights for designing targeted interventions, such as age specific sexual health education, culturally sensitive awareness campaigns, and early prevention programs. Finally, the study reinforces the role of demographic and behavioral factors in STI risk. Early sexual practice, limited education, rural residence, and low HIV knowledge were consistently associated with higher self-reported STIs prevalence, emphasizing the need to strengthen health education and expand access to preventive and treatment services, particularly for vulnerable populations. Integrating predictive analytics with traditional public health strategies can enhance the effectiveness, efficiency, and equity of STI prevention and control efforts in LMICs.

Conclusion

STI remains a significant public health concern among men in LMICs. The pooled prevalence of self-reported STIs among men was 4.44% (95% CI: 3.46, 5.43) across LMICs, accompanied by substantial and regional variability. The highest prevalence was observed in Liberia, Lesotho, and Uganda, while the lowest effects were observed in Jordan, Kyrgyzstan, and Armenia. Moreover, regional analysis revealed that SSA was with the highest prevalence of self-reported STIs, followed by Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean, and lowest in the Middle East and North Africa. Furthermore, the prevalence of self-reported STIs also showed a clear heterogeneity by country income status, with low-income countries showing the highest prevalence of self-reported STIs, lower middle-income countries a moderate, and upper middle-income countries showed the lowest prevalence of self-reported STIs among men.

This study applied multiple traditional machine learning and deep learning algorithms. Among these, CatBoost, demonstrated superior performance, achieving an accuracy of 80.10%, AUC of 88.80%, precision of 76.00%, and recall of 89.00%. SHAP (SHapley Additive ExPlanations) was used to interpret the CatBoost model, identifying the most influential predictors of self-reported STIs: country income status, region, age, age at first sex, religion, education, HIV knowledge, wealth index, marital status, and place of residence are the top predictors of self-reported STIs. This study highlights the heterogeneous distribution of self-reported STIs among men in LMICs and underscores the importance of socioeconomic, demographic, and behavioral factors in shaping predictors of self-reported STIs among men. It also demonstrates the practical value of machine learning, particularly CatBoost algorithms, in identifying key predictors and informing targeted STI prevention and control strategies.

Targeted STI prevention and control efforts should focus on high-burden regions such as SSA, and low income countries, addressing populations at elevated risk due to socioeconomic, demographic, and behavioral factors. Interventions could include comprehensive sexual health education, promotion of HIV and STI testing, and improved access to preventive services. Additionally, machine learning tools such as CatBoost can be used to identify high-risk groups and guide efficient allocation of resources for more effective and evidence-based STI prevention strategies.
