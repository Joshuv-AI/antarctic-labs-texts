“I’m in pain, and I’m not faking it”: A qualitative exploration of the reactions of youth with juvenile idiopathic arthritis and their caregivers to pain-related stigma
Authors: Blair N Irish, Christine T Chambers, Justine Dol, Jennifer A Parker, Adam M Huber, Yvonne N Brandelli, Devyn Nichols, Jennifer Brause
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJuvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is a chronic inflammatory condition often marked by pain in childhood. JIA pain has a variable course and often lacks any visible signs. As such, youth with JIA can experience negative judgement from others in response to their pain (i.e., pain-related stigma). Theoretical models indicate that pain-related stigma experiences contribute to affective, behavioural, and cognitive reactions from youth in the moment (i.e., situational) and over time (i.e., enduring) that ultimately influence their health. Caregivers also play a crucial role in supporting their children’s health. While caregivers may protect their children from the potential negative impacts of pain-related stigma, caregivers can also be affected by their children’s stigma experiences. Thus, this qualitative study aimed to explore both the situational and enduring reactions of youth with JIA and their caregivers to pain-related stigma. It also examined similarities and differences in their reactions to identify potentially protective reactions and areas where additional support may be needed.MethodsTen youth aged 15–19 years with JIA and 12 caregivers participated. Youth and caregivers completed separate semi-structured interviews. During interviews, participants were asked about the youth’s experiences of pain-related stigma and how these experiences affected their situational and enduring cognitive, affective, and behavioural reactions. Interview transcripts were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis. Themes generated between groups were triangulated to assess for convergence and divergence. A matrix analysis was subsequently used to develop meta-themes capturing overlapping concepts.ResultsSeparate themes for each group were developed. The matrix analysis resulted in five meta-themes that capture the overlapping situational and enduring reactions of youth and caregivers to pain-related stigma: (1) addressing stigma and increasing perceived credibility; (2) responding to individual needs; (3) fear and avoidance due to anticipatory stigma; (4) long-term impacts on mental health and well-being; and (5) education and advocacy.ConclusionsPain-related stigma can impact youth with JIA and their caregivers in various ways, with some reactions being protective of their health and well-being. Strategies to support youth with JIA and their caregivers with navigating pain-related stigma experiences are necessary to promote positive health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-025-03651-3.Keywords: Arthritis, juvenile; Social stigma; Chronic pain; Youth; Caregivers; Qualitative research

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is the most common rheumatic condition in childhood [1], with global prevalence estimates ranging widely from 3.8 to 400 cases per 100,000 youth under 16 years [2]. JIA is characterized by chronic joint inflammation contributing to pain and stiffness [3]. JIA pain varies in intensity over time and has an unpredictable course [4], however, many youth report experiencing daily mild-to-moderate pain [5]. Among youth with JIA, higher pain levels are associated with more internalizing symptoms, such as anxiety and depression, as well as lower quality of life and overall well-being [6]. Although joint inflammation can cause visible swelling and redness, these signs are not always present or easily noticeable by others [7], meaning JIA pain is often invisible [8]. Given the presence of both physical and psychological symptoms, managing JIA often requires complex, ongoing pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments [9], with caregivers playing a central role in supporting their implementation [10].

Due to the unpredictability and invisibility of JIA pain, and limited societal understanding and awareness of the condition, youth with JIA are at risk of experiencing negative judgement from others in response to their pain (i.e., pain-related stigma) [11]. Stigma is a phenomenon whereby individuals are devalued when they possess a characteristic considered undesirable by society (e.g., pain) [12, 13]. Stigma negatively affects individuals through four processes: (1) enacted stigma, (2) felt stigma, (3) internalized stigma, and (4) anticipatory stigma [14]. Enacted stigma describes the negative evaluations and biases others hold towards those with a stigmatized characteristic. Felt stigma occurs when those with a stigmatized characteristic perceive to have been negatively judged because of that attribute. When individuals adopt others’ negative evaluations and apply these to themselves, it is referred to as internalized stigma. Lastly, anticipatory stigma occurs when individuals expect negative judgement in the future because of their stigmatized characteristic. Youth with JIA have reported experiencing pain-related felt stigma in the form of pain dismissal, pain disbelief, and discrimination [8, 15]. Moreover, they have described experiencing pain-related internalized and anticipatory stigma [15]. Due to their close involvement in their children’s JIA journeys, caregivers are acutely aware of their children’s experiences of pain-related stigma, and have reported observing felt, internalized, and anticipatory stigma among their children [15].

As with stigma in other contexts, pain-related stigma is associated with worse physical and psychological health and well-being. Specifically, pain-related stigma has been linked to greater functional disability, depressive symptoms, and pain intensity among adults with chronic pain [16, 17]. Among pediatric chronic pain populations, pain-related stigma is associated with lower quality of life and greater pain interference [18, 19]. A study by Boileau and colleagues [20] also found that youth with JIA who reported experiencing more pain-related stigma from peers endorsed higher levels of pain severity under experimental pain conditions.

Theoretical and empirical work on stigma indicates that stigma experiences lead to reactions from stigmatized individuals that can influence the impact of such experiences on their health-related outcomes, including quality of life and well-being [14, 21]. These reactions can include affective (e.g., anger, anxiety, embarrassment), cognitive (e.g., schemas, appraisals), and behavioural reactions [14, 21]. Wakefield and colleagues [21] further suggest that the reactions of youth with chronic pain to pain-related stigma can be categorized into situational reactions (i.e., reactions that occur in response to a particular stigma experience) and enduring reactions (i.e., reactions that persist beyond the immediate stigma experience) in their proposed model of pain-related stigma.

It is well-known that caregivers play a key role in ensuring the health outcomes of their children [22]. Qualitative work has demonstrated that mothers of youth with JIA describe becoming their child’s advocate during interactions with healthcare providers and school staff [23]. It is postulated that caregiver support as an environmental factor may help mitigate the potential negative impacts of pain-related stigma on their children [14]. However, it remains unknown how caregivers react to their children’s pain-related stigma experiences and how their reactions may influence their and their children’s health outcomes. Thus, not only is it necessary to understand the impacts of pain-related stigma on caregivers, but integrating this information will also further expand existing theories and models of pain-related stigma.

The objective of this qualitative study was to highlight the perspectives of youth with JIA and their caregivers regarding their situational and enduring reactions to youth’s experiences of pain-related stigma and assess the similarities and differences between their reactions. Gaining this insight can provide a more comprehensive view of how pain-related stigma affects youth with JIA and their families, including the strategies they use to protect against its negative impacts and opportunities to better support them in navigating these experiences to promote positive health outcomes.

Methods

Design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive design, allowing for a straightforward, yet rich description of a particular phenomenon from the participant perspective [24]. Though interpretive, qualitative description aims to stay close to the data obtained through participants’ accounts [24, 25], which was well-suited to the current study due to its goal of highlighting the experiences and perspectives of the participants. This study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ), a checklist designed to support the comprehensive reporting of qualitative studies [26], during the development of this manuscript (see Additional File 1).

Participants and setting

All participants were recruited from a pool of 235 individuals from multiple countries around the world (consisting of Canada, United States, England, Scotland, South Africa, and Ireland) who had previously completed a research study investigating the impact of youth and caregiver perfectionism on internalizing symptoms (i.e., depressive and anxious symptoms) in youth with JIA [27], and who had provided consent to be contacted about future research opportunities. Participants in this original database were recruited via online and social media outreach (e.g., patient communities and organizations, blog posts, Facebook and Instagram advertisements), posters in clinics, and research registries between November 2021 to April 2023 [27]. To be eligible for the current study, youth with JIA had to be 13 to 19 years of age, and all participants had to be able to read and speak fluently in English. This age group aligns with the World Health Organization’s definition of adolescence [28], as well as research and developmental theories suggesting that youth in this age range can understand complex concepts (e.g., justice, stigma) [29], have a well-developed emotional vocabulary [30], and may show behavioural responses to stigma [31].

In line with relevant guidelines specific to the analysis approach, a target sample size of 20 (10 youth and 10 caregivers) was selected a priori and informed by pragmatic factors (e.g., available resources and timelines) and the anticipated richness of data [32]. A purposive sampling strategy was utilized for the current study. Initial recruitment prioritized contacting youth approaching the upper age limit (i.e., about to turn 20 years old). Subsequent recruitment focused on dyads with youth and/or caregivers with diverse gender and racial identities, as well as youth with varying JIA diagnoses to enhance the diversity and representativeness of the study sample. A total of 90 (53 youth, 37 caregivers) out of 200 eligible individuals were contacted via email about the study opportunity, 24 expressed interest, and 22 participated in interviews (see Additional File 2 for a recruitment flowchart).

Patient partnership

The project team included two patient partners – a youth with JIA (DN) and their caregiver (JB) – to enhance the relevance of this research and its potential to lead to meaningful outcomes for the patient population. Patient partners were recruited through Cassie and Friends, a Canadian organization supporting children, youth, and families with pediatric rheumatic conditions [33]. As members of the research team, DN and JB were engaged in this research from the study design stage to the dissemination of study results.

Materials

Demographic and medical variables

Youth and caregivers reported on demographic variables (e.g., sex, gender, age), including race [34]. Both youth and caregivers also provided information on youth medical variables (e.g., diagnosis, disease status) and arthritis disease activity (e.g., pain severity) using an 11-point numeric rating scale (NRS) ranging from 0 (“no activity”) to 10 (“maximum activity”) [35].

Interview guide

Two separate semi-structured interview guides (youth and caregiver) were created for the purposes of this study (see Additional File 3 for the youth and caregiver interview guides). Each guide included two open-ended introductory questions that asked participants about the youth’s experiences with arthritis and pain to build rapport and create a relaxed environment [36]. The guides also contained eleven open-ended questions asking about the youth’s experiences of pain-related stigma. These interview questions were adapted from a series of focus group questions used in a previous study that examined pain-related stigma experiences among youth with JIA and their parents [15]. The original focus group questions were developed based on stigma theory and in collaboration with an expert on pediatric stigma [15]. As such, adapting these questions for the current study supported building on established research, while ensuring theoretical consistency. In line with the proposed pain-related stigma model for adolescents with chronic pain [21], when participants described an experience of pain-related stigma, follow-up questions were used to probe their situational and enduring affective, behavioural, and cognitive reactions to these experiences. Prior to beginning data collection, patient partners (DN and JB) reviewed the interview guides to ensure question clarity and relevance. Field testing of the caregiver interview guide was also done through pilot testing with JB to ensure the questions elicited relevant information and that the interview length was appropriate [36].

Procedure

An initial email invitation was sent to eligible youth and caregivers with information about the study and a link to an online Qualtrics survey that included informed consent and questions about eligibility, demographics, and youth medical variables. All survey items were forced response, and the demographic and medical questions had a “prefer not to answer” option [34]. After completing the survey, participants were redirected to an online scheduling system (Calendly) to select a date and time to complete the virtual interview. All interviews were done through Zoom, though participants were able to request a phone interview if they were unable to connect to Zoom. After booking, participants were sent a confirmation email with a copy of the study consent form and Zoom meeting link. All interviews were conducted by the first author (BNI), who is a White cisgender woman and PhD student in Clinical Psychology with training in clinical interviewing. At the beginning of each interview, the interviewer discussed the purpose of the study, reviewed the study consent form, and obtained verbal consent from participants. Although the interviews were both audio and video recorded, only the audio recordings were retained and used to produce interview transcripts. Verbatim transcripts of the interviews were first generated using the transcription feature in Microsoft Word and were subsequently verified for accuracy by BNI. The research participants did not review the transcripts. The duration of interviews ranged from 26 to 109 min (average of 58 min). All participants who completed the study received a $20 CAD online gift card. Dyadic participation was encouraged by entering both youth and caregiver participants into a draw for a chance to win one additional $100 CAD online gift card when they both completed the study. This study was approved by IWK Health’s Research Ethics Board (#1031003).

Data analysis

Demographic and medical variables

Descriptive statistics and frequency counts were calculated for demographic and youth medical variables using SPSS.

Interview

The interview data was analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis (RTA). There are numerous ways to conduct RTA depending on the research objectives, which influence decisions pertaining to the guiding qualitative framework (i.e., how data is interpreted), theoretical underpinnings (e.g., ontology, epistemology), level of meaning analyzed (semantic or latent), and orientation to the data (inductive or deductive) [37]. The theoretical flexibility of RTA allowed for an experiential analysis of the data, which focused on highlighting the lived experiences and perspectives of the research participants [37]. As such, language was viewed as intentional and a tool used by participants to communicate their perspectives [37]. Ontologically, the analysis was grounded in critical realism, which acknowledges the existence of reality, though individuals’ perceptions of reality are influenced by their experiences and social contexts [37]. In terms of epistemology, the analysis followed contextualism, with the position that knowledge is shaped by the context within which it was generated [37]. Therefore, it was recognized that pain-related stigma experiences occur for youth with JIA in the real world due to a lack of understanding, misinformation, and negative societal beliefs about childhood arthritis and pain. However, experiences of pain-related stigma were expected to vary across individuals and be based on their unique contexts.

RTA was also well-suited to this study due to the flexibility around orientation to the data [37]. Despite the interview guides being informed by previous research and an existing theoretical framework (i.e., proposed pain-related stigma model) [15, 21], a primarily inductive analysis was desired to allow themes to be developed from the data collected in the current study [37]. Therefore, codes were categorized into situational and enduring reactions (deductive), whereas code labels were developed inductively based on the content of the data in the current study. Primarily semantic coding (i.e., exploring surface-level meaning) was used, though latent coding (i.e., exploring underlying or implicit meaning) was also used when participants’ verbatim responses did not appear to adequately capture the meaning they intended to convey [37].

The analysis was led by the first author (BNI) using Braun and Clarke’s [37–39] six phases for conducting RTA as a guide throughout the iterative analytic process. Phase one of becoming familiar with the dataset was done by revisiting the audio-recorded interviews during transcription and re-reading the data. Line-by-line coding of the interview transcripts was done by BNI for phase two using NVivo (a qualitative data analysis software). In phase three, initial codes were grouped into broader categories that captured similar meanings. Phases four and five involved reviewing, refining, and naming themes. This process was done for both the youth and caregiver groups separately. The coding was reviewed by another investigator (JD) who has expertise in qualitative methods. While the purpose of having a secondary coder was not to reach agreement, this facilitated a meaningful discussion around alternative ways the data could be interpreted [37]. Theme development was carried out in consultation with JD, DN, and JB to ensure clarity and relevance.

Once the themes for both groups were finalized, a descriptive matrix analysis was conducted in two steps to systematically assess the similarities and differences in the results between youth and caregivers. First, a data triangulation process (i.e., assessing overlap between multiple data sources) was performed to assess the level of convergence between the descriptions (including meaning and examples) provided by youth and caregivers for like-themes [40]. Themes showed “full agreement” if they converged on the meaning and examples provided by each group, “partial agreement” if there was convergence in either the meaning or examples provided between groups, “dissonance” when themes demonstrated disagreement on the meaning and examples provided by the groups, and “silence” if a theme only came up in one group and not the other. After determining the level of convergence, themes were organized into a matrix, and meta-themes (i.e., overarching concepts) were created where there were overlapping themes between groups [41].

Positionality and reflexivity statement

The knowledge generated in this study was actively shaped by the experiences and identities of the researchers. The first author (BNI) led the data collection and analysis, and is a White, cisgender woman, and young adult who is pursuing a PhD in clinical psychology. BNI is affiliated with a large healthcare and academic institution. Further, BNI does not have arthritis and is not a caregiver. These dynamics influenced conversations with participants during interviews and how BNI approached the data analysis. As such, BNI engaged in reflexive notetaking throughout the analytic process and had frequent discussions with other team members (with expertise as clinicians, researchers, and/or individuals with lived experience) to consider alternative interpretations of the data. As the secondary coder, the third author (JD) played a key role in shaping the data analysis. JD is a White, cisgender woman who has a PhD and is a postdoctoral fellow at a large hospital. She is a caregiver to two children. She does not have personal experience with arthritis, either individually or as a caregiver. JD has experience with qualitative data and analysis.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 10 youth (15–19 years) and 12 caregivers (40–56 years) participated in the virtual interviews, with 10 complete youth-caregiver dyads (i.e., two caregivers participated independently). Youth and caregivers primarily identified as White (80% and 91.7%, respectively) cisgender girls (60% of youth) and women (91.7% of caregivers) (see Table 1 for complete participant demographics). All caregivers were biological parents of their child with JIA, most of whom were biological mothers (91.7%). Nearly all caregivers had some post-secondary education (91.7%). In terms of medical characteristics, youth had various diagnoses of JIA, with just over half experiencing an active flare at the time of their participation (60%; see Table 2 for complete youth medical variables). All youth reported having been diagnosed with JIA more than five years prior to their participation in the study.

Youth situational reactions

Challenging stigmatizer despite lacking perceived credibility. Many youth described reacting to pain-related felt stigma by actively challenging the stigmatizer’s response to their pain symptoms. They explained doing so in various ways, such as educating the stigmatizer on childhood arthritis and pain, advocating for their needs, and expressing their preferences. For example, one youth who had her laptop taken away by a teacher, despite it being a documented accommodation on her Individualized Education Plan (IEP) stated, “I took it [the laptop] back and I got pretty upset with her” (Youth 7). However, youth described a sense of powerlessness and lack of perceived credibility when challenging sources of stigma in positions of authority. As such, youth frequently mentioned using strategies to enhance their perceived credibility when reacting to pain-related stigma in this way, including seeking support from individuals in positions of higher authority (e.g., parents, principals, guidance counsellors), citing their diagnosis, and providing documentation.

Utilizing internal and external coping strategies. When reacting to pain-related stigma experiences in the moment, most youth described using either internal, external, or both types of coping strategies to help mitigate the negative affective impacts of such experiences. Internal coping strategies used by youth consisted of distraction, engaging in hobbies, ignoring minor stigma experiences, and self-compassion. For instance, this youth expressed how he uses self-compassion when he said, “I kinda just have to […] remind myself that this may not entirely be – like this is not my fault. Um, you know? It’s not my fault that I have this disease. It’s not my fault that people are […] not understanding” (Youth 5). In terms of external coping strategies, youth often described seeking emotional support from family and friends, including other youth with arthritis pain.

Self-doubt in the face of stigma. Youth frequently described experiencing pain-related internalized stigma due to negative judgement from others. For instance, youth expressed that pain-related stigma caused them to feel embarrassed and insecure in the moment. Moreover, youth explained that pain-related stigma experiences cause them to question the validity of their pain.

In the moment, internalized stigma caused many youth to have negative thoughts about themselves, their pain, or both. For instance, one youth was teased by her peers because of how she was walking and described having negative thoughts about herself: “No, you’re right, because I do look stupid” (Youth 4). She also reported trying to change how she was walking as a result, which led to more pain at the time: “[…] I try my best [to walk differently], but it really does hurt […]” (Youth 4). Internalized stigma also causes some youth to reconsider how they manage their pain in the moment. In some cases, youth considered following stigmatizing recommendations (i.e., the recommendation itself was stigmatizing) on how to manage their pain, despite these suggestions potentially being harmful. For example, this youth described how her gym teacher insisted she participate in class, despite her significant pain, believing that physical activity would ‘fix’ her pain: “It did make me wonder whether, you know, if I just pushed through, if it would get better and stuff. But then, I do know that that isn’t true” (Youth 8).

Caregiver situational reactions

Confronting the source of child’s stigma despite lived experience being undervalued. Caregivers often described directly confronting the source of their child’s stigma in the moment by correcting the stigmatizer, advocating for their child’s needs, educating the stigmatizer, and appealing to the stigmatizer’s empathy. As an example, in response to school staff not believing her son’s arthritis pain, this mother explained, “[…] we had sent a note to the teacher to just say, you know, he was telling the truth - he cannot sit like that [cross-legged] […], but he can, he can sit in a chair and he can do these things instead” (Caregiver 11). It was also described by some caregivers that their lived experience supporting their child with arthritis pain is often undervalued, which can make confronting the source of their child’s stigma more difficult. Therefore, caregivers frequently expressed using strategies to increase their perceived credibility when addressing stigmatizers such as providing medical documentation, using medical terminology, and seeking support from higher authorities (e.g., principals, the Human Rights Commission).

Navigating challenges together: supporting child with problem-solving and achieving relief. This theme captures how caregivers often react to their child’s pain-related stigma experiences by addressing their child’s needs and facilitating collaborative problem-solving. To accomplish collaborative problem-solving, many caregivers described facilitating open discussions with their child so the youth could determine how they wanted their caregiver to be involved. In terms of addressing their child’s needs, when stigma experiences caused their child to suffer more pain, caregivers mentioned prioritizing supporting their child with achieving pain relief. To illustrate, when school staff neglected to consider or meet her daughter’s needs during a school trip – resulting in the child having to endure increased pain due to the lack of appropriate support – this mother prioritized helping her child achieve pain relief: “[…] my first response was to get my kid some relief, like to give her pain medication and put her in a place where she could rest and warm up […]” (Caregiver 10). Most caregivers also described supporting their child in achieving emotional relief in the moment by listening to their child, offering reassurance, and validating their pain experience.

Taking space to respond to own needs. In addition to supporting their child, many caregivers also described tending to their own needs in moments when their child experienced pain-related stigma. Caregivers utilize internal and external coping strategies to achieve emotional relief in the moment. Internal coping strategies that were described by some caregivers included mindfulness and journaling. One mother also described writing out an email to assist with emotion regulation: “I usually, like, write it out, whether I send the e-mail to them or not […], just to be able to release that like anger and frustration” (Caregiver 2). Caregivers more frequently described using external coping strategies by drawing on their social networks. When responding to their own needs, some caregivers stated that they often choose to separate from their child.

Qualitative results

Three themes for each group were generated that capture the situational reactions of youth and caregivers to the pain-related stigma experiences of youth. Additionally, three themes for youth and four themes for caregivers were created that describe their enduring reactions to youth’s experiences of pain-related stigma. Youth enduring reactions

Avoidance due to a lack of trust and anticipation of future stigma experiences. After experiencing pain-related stigma, almost all youth described being fearful of and anticipating future stigma experiences. Pain-related stigma experiences also contributed to diminished trust in the source of stigma (including school staff, healthcare providers, family members, and peers) for many youth. Due to anticipatory stigma, youth described engaging in numerous avoidant behaviours over time such as avoiding past sources of stigma, potential future sources of stigma, the use of mobility aids, pain disclosure, and accessing healthcare services. For instance, one youth who experienced pain-related stigma from a healthcare provider described reduced help-seeking: “Part of the big hang-up of me going to find a new specialist […] was I, I was very concerned that like the next one would also be the same” (Youth 1). As a result of this prolonged fear of negative judgement in response to their pain symptoms, some youth described enduring more pain and pushing themselves through painful activities.

Suffering in silence: ongoing mental health struggles. Pain-related stigma experiences had negative long-term impacts on the mental health and well-being of youth. For example, one youth explained how pain-related stigma has affected her mental health over time: “I’ve gone through times with struggling with it [pain-related stigma] on a more mental level. I go through ups and downs really with my mental health a lot. I have really bad periods of anxiety and stuff like that” (Youth 8). Youth also described feeling isolated and noted that pain-related stigma experiences negatively affected their social skills and made it more difficult to make friends in the long term. Pain-related internalized stigma also led to sustained negative self-perceptions for youth. For example, this youth stated, “[…] today, I think I still feel the impacts of having it [pain-related stigma] affect my self-image” (Youth 6). Relatedly, many youth described that they continue gaslighting themselves and questioning the validity of their pain in the long term. Some youth mentioned that they utilize mental health supports to assist with managing the negative long-term mental health impacts of pain-related stigma.

Promoting awareness through long-term advocacy to combat stigma. Youth consistently reflected on how childhood arthritis is poorly recognized and understood among the public. Some youth also discussed that they believe pain-related stigma results from this lack of awareness. As such, numerous youth reported being involved in education and advocacy to enhance the public’s awareness and understanding of childhood arthritis and pain. Some youth also described using educational resources to proactively educate their teachers about childhood arthritis and advocate for their needs at school. For instance, this youth explained, “[…] the Arthritis Foundation has a pamphlet for like educators […]. I print that out for my teachers every single year and I give them a copy” (Youth 5).

Caregiver enduring reactions

Expectation of future stigma: fear, avoidance, and preparing to support. Many caregivers described anticipating that their child will endure future pain-related stigma. They also described being fearful of how these future stigma experiences will affect their child. As a result of this pain-related anticipatory stigma, caregivers frequently described being selective of with whom they discuss their child’s pain and arthritis experiences. To illustrate, this caregiver stated, “[…] I’m very careful what I share with [critical] people […] because they will just take all that extra information and manipulate it or twist it. And […] they don’t need to know. So, I’m careful” (Caregiver 3). Caregivers also occasionally described avoiding their child’s past sources of stigma and potential future sources. Due to expectations of future pain-related stigma experiences, most caregivers emphasized that they are always prepared to defend their child. Relatedly, caregivers often described proactively advocating for their child’s needs, primarily in the school setting, due to the belief that future stigma experiences are likely to occur.

From experience to education: promoting understanding and openness through advocacy. After their child experienced pain-related stigma, some caregivers expressed that this caused them to purposefully be more open and understanding of others. Many caregivers also explained that they believe pain-related stigma results from a lack of awareness of childhood arthritis and pain. As such, most caregivers explained that they take on the role of educating others to enhance awareness and prevent future stigma experiences for their child and other children with arthritis pain. While educating and advocating, some caregivers described drawing from their child’s experiences of pain-related stigma to highlight the importance of being open and inclusive. For example, this mother who works in the education system described how she used her child’s experience of pain-related stigma to encourage other school staff to be open and understanding: “And I will tell other phys-ed teachers and other teachers, like, I know it’s hard, but this is my experience. Like, just take the minute to listen if you can” (Caregiver 2).

Preparing child to independently navigate stigma. Although caregivers described numerous ways that they intervene to support their child through pain-related stigma experiences, most caregivers expressed an ongoing responsibility and commitment to preparing their child to independently navigate pain-related stigma experiences. One way caregivers described preparing their child is by supporting them in developing their self-advocacy skills. This mother explained how she has done this:

[…] I was trying to teach her to navigate how to advocate for herself […]. I tried to not step in if I didn’t have to step in, like, I was a backup […]. I also wanted her to understand that she had to advocate for herself too. Like, […] I wasn’t always going to be able to do it for her, and it wasn’t always going to be appropriate for me to do it for her. So, to teach her to be like, […] I can’t do that, […] and just stand her ground if she couldn’t do it. (Caregiver 1)

Some caregivers also mentioned supporting their child with developing a strong network of people, including peers and trusted adults, they can rely on to support them during future pain-related stigma situations.

Striking a balance: caring for child while managing own well-being over time. As a result of their child experiencing pain-related stigma, many caregivers described negative long-term impacts on their mental health and well-being. For instance, this mother described how she often ruminates on the injustice of her daughter having to endure such experiences: “[…] I’m an overthinker at bedtime, so, then I’ll lay in bed, and I’ll be like, […] why is this even fair? […] It isn’t fair at all” (Caregiver 4). However, several caregivers described taking steps to care for themselves to support their long-term well-being and ensure they are equipped to meet their child’s needs. Some caregivers also described utilizing mental health services to support their mental health and well-being in the long term. However, some caregivers expressed challenges in balancing caring for their own needs with the demands of caring for a child with a chronic health condition, as they often prioritize their child’s needs over their own: “[…] if she’s not hurting, then I don’t worry so much. So, I guess, maybe in the long run, taking care of her is taking care of me” (Caregiver 1).

Triangulation and matrix analysis

Five meta-themes were generated that capture the overlap in the reactions between youth and caregivers to experiences of pain-related stigma. The meta-themes include: (1) addressing stigma and increasing perceived credibility; (2) responding to individual needs; (3) fear and avoidance due to anticipatory stigma; (4) long-term impacts on mental health and well-being; and (5) education and advocacy (see Table 5 for complete descriptions of each meta-theme and components of themes that did or did not converge). There was full agreement between youth and caregivers for addressing stigma and increasing perceived credibility as a situational reaction to pain-related stigma experiences. The meta-themes of responding to individual needs, fear and avoidance due to anticipatory stigma, long-term impacts on mental health and well-being, and education and advocacy had partial agreement between groups. Three themes did not converge between groups including two for caregivers (i.e., navigating challenges together: supporting child with problem-solving and achieving relief and preparing child to independently navigate stigma) and one for youth (i.e., self-doubt in the face of stigma). These themes appear to be specific to the reactions of youth and caregivers to pain-related stigma.

Discussion

This study explored the perspectives of youth with JIA and their caregivers on their situational and enduring reactions to youth’s experiences of pain-related stigma and examined similarities and differences between their reactions. The findings of this research contribute to identifying strategies that youth and caregivers use to maintain their well-being in the face of pain-related stigma, as well as highlighting risk factors where additional support is needed to prevent negative health outcomes.

Self, parent, and public advocacy

The results highlighted situational and enduring reactions from youth and caregivers related to advocacy. The meta-theme of addressing stigma and increasing perceived credibility captures how both youth and caregivers advocate for the needs of youth in response to pain-related stigma in the moment, which can protect the health outcomes of youth. Self-advocacy has been associated with improved patient outcomes and satisfaction across various health contexts [43, 44]. Moreover, parent advocacy contributes to enhanced access to supports and accommodations for children with disabilities, as well as improved relationships between families and school staff [45]. However, self-advocacy skills are crucial for youth with disabilities during their transition into adulthood [46]. The current research identified an enduring reaction from caregivers related to supporting their child in developing their self-advocacy skills, so they are prepared to independently navigate future pain-related stigma experiences. As such, caregivers play a vital role in promoting the long-term health outcomes of their children.

Although youth and caregivers described addressing pain-related stigma in the moment, they also described a sense of powerlessness and lack of perceived credibility as a result of others dismissing or not believing their accounts, which can pose a barrier to patient advocacy [47]. This appears to reflect a larger systemic issue related to how knowledge and credibility are distributed amongst individuals in society and can be understood as a form of epistemic injustice, which is harm inflicted on individuals when their capacity as knowers is unfairly diminished [48]. In this context, youth with JIA and their caregivers experience epistemic injustice when individuals in positions of power and authority devalue their insight into their experiences and symptoms, which can prevent their access to appropriate treatments and supports. Children and youth are particularly vulnerable to epistemic injustice, as they may have limited epistemic resources (i.e., tools necessary for knowledge production and sharing; 49) due to their developmental stage and may be automatically judged as providing uncredible testimonies [50]. This demonstrates a need for those in positions with greater power and perceived credibility (e.g., medical doctors, educators) to recognize their potential biases that could cause such harms and to proactively support families with advocacy across contexts [50]. Future research should identify strategies that can effectively enhance the perceived credibility of youth with JIA and their caregivers to support their advocacy efforts.

The meta-theme of education and advocacy describes how youth and caregivers engage in sustained efforts to increase awareness and understanding of childhood arthritis and pain. Not only can this enduring reaction help prevent future stigma experiences, but public advocacy can also offer opportunities for healing and personal growth for youth and caregivers [51]. However, more extensive efforts are necessary to assist with legitimizing the pain experiences of youth with JIA to combat stigma. Consistent with the Canadian Pain Task Force Report [52], public education and awareness campaigns are needed to enhance awareness of childhood chronic pain among the public. Anti-stigma interventions specific to childhood arthritis pain should be developed and evaluated that utilize strategies proven to effectively reduce stigmatizing knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs in other health contexts (e.g., HIV, mental health). For example, interventions could combine childhood arthritis pain education with contact (either direct or indirect) with individuals who experience pain-related stigma [53].

Responding to needs

The meta-theme of responding to individual needs captures how youth and caregivers use internal and external strategies to cope with pain-related stigma in the moment. Self-compassion was an internal coping strategy identified by youth, which can promote resilience and be a protective factor for youth who experience stigma [54]. Both youth and caregivers described seeking social support as an external coping strategy, which has been shown to effectively mitigate the harmful effects of stigma for individuals with chronic pain and reduce internalized stigma among those with type 1 diabetes [45, 55]. The results also demonstrate that caregivers proactively offer emotional support to their child by providing reassurance and validating their pain symptoms. This situational reaction from caregivers can support youth in achieving emotional relief during the acute stigma experience.

Avoidance due to anticipatory stigma

As highlighted in the meta-theme of fear and avoidance due to anticipatory stigma, pain-related stigma experiences also contribute to anticipatory stigma for youth and caregivers in the long term, which was associated with fear and avoidance behaviours. For instance, youth described avoiding pain disclosure and the use of mobility aids, which aligns with previous research indicating that youth with primary chronic pain conceal their pain in the long term to prevent future stigma experiences [56]. Moreover, this aligns with the results of a study examining pain-related injustice appraisals among youth with chronic pain, where participants also described pain concealment due to the desire of living a normal life and fear of judgement [57]. Youth in the current study also described reduced help-seeking behaviours, similar to findings from research examining the impact of stigma on help-seeking behaviours among individuals with concealable stigmatized identities [58]. Therefore, pain-related anticipatory stigma can have significant long-term implications for youth with JIA and their families.

Impacts on mental health and well-being

In line with previous research, youth with JIA in this study experienced internalized stigma [15], which has been associated with worse physical and psychological outcomes [59]. The findings from the current study identified several mechanisms that may explain this relationship. Specifically, youth who internalized stigma described situational reactions including negative thoughts about self, attempts to push through pain, and reconsiderations in how they approach pain management. The meta-theme of long-term impacts on mental health and well-being also captures the descriptions of youth and caregivers around the negative impacts of pain-related stigma on their mental health and ability to manage their well-being in the long term, which is consistent with past research in chronic pain and sickle cell disease [17, 60]. In particular, caregivers highlighted the challenge of balancing their own long-term needs with supporting those of their child. As such, healthcare teams of children and youth with JIA should focus on supporting the whole family unit and may be well-positioned to offer resources to caregivers that can assist them with supporting their mental health and well-being in the long term.

Theoretical implications

Although the analysis was primarily inductive in nature, this research was informed by the proposed pain-related stigma model by Wakefield and colleagues [21]. Consistent with the model, the current study found that experiences of pain-related stigma contribute to situational and enduring affective (e.g., fear of future stigma, anxiety), cognitive (i.e., negative thoughts about self, long-term negative self-appraisals), and behavioural (e.g., addressing stigma, seeking social support, avoidance) reactions among youth with JIA. The findings also suggest that existing pain-related stigma theory should be extended by providing preliminary insight into how caregivers could buffer the potential negative impacts of pain-related stigma for their children (e.g., by advocating for their needs, providing emotional support). This aligns with the conceptual model of the effects of stigma processes on health by Major and colleagues [14], which suggests that environmental characteristics can moderate the impact of stigma. Therefore, incorporating an interpersonal component to the proposed pain-related stigma model is necessary.

Strengths and limitations

This study fills an important gap by identifying numerous ways through which pain-related stigma can impact the health outcomes of youth with JIA and their caregivers. A notable strength of this study was the dyadic approach that was used, which highlights the dynamic process of how youth and caregivers react to youth’s pain-related stigma experiences. Another strength was the use of a qualitative approach to gain in-depth insights from participants who have lived experience of pain-related stigma. The use of a primarily inductive approach to the analysis helped produce data-driven results that convey the perspectives of the research participants.

In terms of limitations, despite efforts to obtain a diverse sample, participants were relatively homogenous due to low response rates and a limited recruitment pool. Therefore, the results of this study may lack representational generalizability, (i.e., the results may not be representative of the experiences and perspectives of the global JIA community) [61].

For instance, this study had a lack of racial representation across youth and caregiver participants. Studies that have used an intersectional approach when investigating pain-related stigma among adults have found that the combined effects of racial discrimination and pain-related stigma are associated with greater pain severity, depressive symptoms, and stress [62]. Therefore, racially diverse youth with JIA and their caregivers may react to experiences of pain-related stigma in different ways and benefit from different types of supports to promote positive overall health outcomes. In addition, the age distribution of the youth sample was skewed, with most participants falling on the upper end of the eligible age range. Although youth discussed their reactions to experiences of pain-related stigma over the course of their JIA journeys, the applicability of the study findings for younger youth with JIA may be limited. Specifically, due to their older age, the youth participants may have had more opportunities to engage in self-advocacy, and their caregivers may have been more focused on supporting them with independently navigating stigma experiences. There was also a lack of gender diversity among caregivers and the results may differ when investigating the reactions of individuals in other caregiving roles besides biological parents. Lastly, given that initial participant recruitment efforts involved recruiting through arthritis community organizations and all dyads in this study reported annual household incomes above the 2023 Canadian median [63], it is possible that the youth and caregivers in this study had greater access to resources and supports for education and advocacy, which could have shaped their reactions to pain-related stigma.

Future research should assess whether the findings resonate with groups of individuals who were unrepresented or underrepresented in the current study’s sample. Meaningfully and collaboratively partnering with individuals from underrepresented groups within the JIA community throughout the research process may help improve participant diversity in research involving youth with JIA and their families. This requires developing strong and trusting relationships with the broader community and can support enhanced diversity of the study team to foster trust among potential participants, facilitate recruitment strategies that are appropriate and inclusive, and contribute to study designs that minimize potential harms [64–66].

Furthermore, while this study focused on pain-related stigma, it is often difficult to disentangle this from arthritis-related stigma, as childhood arthritis is itself a stigmatized health condition. Although participants were encouraged to reflect on experiences of negative judgement specifically related to their pain symptoms, such judgement may not be attributable to one specific source. Therefore, subsequent studies could focus on better distinguishing between these intersecting forms of stigma among children and youth with JIA.

Conclusions

Pain-related stigma experiences can have significant negative impacts on the health and well-being of youth with JIA. Prior to this study, it was poorly understood how these experiences result in negative outcomes for youth and what factors can buffer the potential negative effects of pain-related stigma on their health. This study identified numerous protective reactions from youth and caregivers, as well as opportunities for healthcare teams to provide additional support for youth and caregivers to ensure they are well-equipped to navigate pain-related stigma experiences in a way that prevents negative health outcomes. Specifically, youth with JIA and their caregivers would benefit from proactive advocacy support, healthcare services that emphasize caring for the whole family unit (including caregivers), and connections to mental health supports and resources early in their care journeys. On a larger scale, broader education and advocacy efforts are needed to target the lack of awareness and understanding of childhood arthritis and pain among the public to address stigma in the long term. Integrating these insights into practice and policy can meaningfully enhance the health and well-being of youth with JIA and their caregivers.
