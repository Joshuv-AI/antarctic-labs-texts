Chitosan-mediated copper nanohybrid attenuates the virulence of a necrotrophic fungal pathogen Macrophomina phaseolina
Authors: Qudsia Fatima, Amna Shoaib, Nafisa Gull, Shakra Khurshid, Uswa Fatima
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study reported the synthesis and characterization of chitosan-copper nanoparticles (Ch-CuNPs) using a 1% copper sulfate solution in 0.2% w/v chitosan. The Ch-CuNPs, displaying a stable brick-red hue, showed an absorption peak at 572 nm, indicative of monodisperse nanoparticle formation and surface plasmon resonance. X-ray diffraction confirmed the face-centered cubic structure with peaks at 36.78°, 43.38°, 50.56°, and 74.26°, and an average particle size of 87–89 nm. FTIR analysis showed interactions between chitosan and copper, particularly around 3370 –3226 cm⁻¹, 1633 cm⁻¹, and 680 cm⁻¹. In vitro assays revealed that Ch-CuNPs inhibited Macrophomina phaseolina growth by 18–71% at 0.03–0.09% concentrations, achieving complete inhibition at 0.12–0.15%, with PCA analysis confirming that growth peaked at lower concentrations and sharply declined at higher levels. Ch-CuNPs also altered fungal morphology and enzyme activity, with notable degradation at higher concentrations. The Cu uptake by the fungus peaked at 29.9% with 0.03% Ch-CuNPs and decreased at higher concentrations. FTIR analysis showed shifts and disappearance of peaks in fungal biomass treated with Ch-CuNPs, indicating molecular interactions and potential structural changes. This study underscores the potential of Ch-CuNPs as an effective antifungal agent and elucidates their interaction mechanisms.

Introduction

The ubiquitously notorious pathogen, Macrophomina phaseolina, holds a global presence as a soil-borne fungus with a formidable impact. It afflicts a staggering array of approximately 700 plant species across more than 100 families, inciting a range of diseases including stem and root rot, charcoal rot, and seedling blight1. Its persistence is notably fueled by fungal sclerotia that can remain infective within the soil and crop residue for extended periods. This pathogen thrives under hot (28–35 °C) and moderately humid (below 60%) climatic conditions, a milieu that encourages hyphal germination from sclerotia, facilitating fungal invasion into host plants and culminating in substantial yield losses of up to 100% in crops, consequently denting the livelihoods of farmers2. Efforts to thwart the havoc caused by M. phaseolinahave encompassed a repertoire of strategies, including the deployment of resistant varieties, biological control agents, crop rotation, chemical fungicides, and natural products1–3. Yet, the resilient nature of the pathogen’s sclerotia underpins its persistent virulence, rendering existing disease management approaches largely ineffective. This pressing challenge has underscored the urgency to explore novel avenues to efficiently combat this devastating pathogen4.

Various types of nanomaterials have exhibited remarkable potential as potent antifungal agents, highlighting their versatility in combatting phytopathogens4,5. Notably, metals such as Ag, Cu, Fe, Zn, Se, Ni, and Pd have emerged as frontrunners in the realm of antifungal properties, signifying their utility6. Among these metals, copper (Cu) stands out as an economically viable and widely available option, boasting the added advantage of cost-effective nanoparticle synthesis. Cu-nanoparticles has demonstrated their efficacy as exceptional antifungal agents, effectively impacting phytopathogens both intracellularly and extracellularly7. Notably, Cu itself is an essential redox-active transition metal, contributing to several vital physiological processes within plants. These encompass functions within the photosynthetic and respiratory electron transport chains, ethylene sensing, cell wall mechanisms, transcriptional signaling, protein trafficking machinery, protection against oxidative stress, iron mobilization, and molybdenum cofactor biogenesis8.

Chitosan (Ch), derived from the deacetylation of chitin, emerges as a versatile compound readily extractable from diverse sources including crustaceans (crab, shrimp or lobster), insects (cockroach, butterfly or ant), mollusks (oyster shells or squid pen), and even organisms like green algae, yeast, and fungi. This polysaccharide possesses notable antifungal and antibacterial properties, as evidenced by its profound antimicrobial activity9. Moreover, chitosan exhibits a unique attribute-an elicitor of a plant’s defense mechanisms on both local and systemic levels. The physical barrier it forms, akin to a protective film, around the seed surface assumes the role of a conveyer for other antimicrobial compounds that may complement seed treatments. Beyond this immediate interaction, its impact reverberates, inducing a cascade of plant defense responses. When harnessed as a soil supplement, chitosan demonstrates the potential to curtail pathogen assault and infection, manifesting as a boon for various plant species10.

Ch-CuNPs have emerged as a multifaceted solution with applications spanning the realms of pharmacy, medicine, and agriculture. Notably, the synthesis process yields a highly porous chitosan nanomaterial matrix capable of entrapping copper with a remarkable 70% encapsulation efficacy11,12. This characteristic porous structure holds promise for diverse applications. Multiple investigations have elucidated the potential of Ch-CuNPs as potent antifungal agents against a spectrum of phytopathogenic fungi, underscoring their relevance in the context of sustainable agriculture11–13. Through a series of meticulously designed in-vitro assays, the work aimed to uncover the extent to which Ch-CuNPs in managing notorious fungal pathogen, M. phaseolina.

Results

Characterization of nanoparticles

In this study, the successful synthesis of colloidal Ch-CuNPs was demonstrated using a 1% copper sulfate solution in 0.2% w/v chitosan. The resulting nanoparticles exhibited a striking brick-red hue, which remained stable for 20 months without precipitation or color change. This stability was confirmed by the UV-Vis spectrum, which revealed a prominent absorption peak at 572 nm (Fig. 1A), corresponding to the reduction of Cu²⁺ ions into monodisperse nanoparticles and indicating the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) characteristic of nano-sized particles. Typically, smaller metallic nanoparticles exhibit a blue shift in their SPR, with nanosized copper particles showing peaks around 500–600 nm14,15.Figure 1A-D: (A) UV-Visible absorption spectrum of Ch-CuNPs, (B) X-ray diffractogram image of Ch-CuNPs, (C) Particle size distribution of Ch-CuNPs & (D) FT-IR spectrum of Ch-CuNPs.

A-D: (A) UV-Visible absorption spectrum of Ch-CuNPs, (B) X-ray diffractogram image of Ch-CuNPs, (C) Particle size distribution of Ch-CuNPs & (D) FT-IR spectrum of Ch-CuNPs.

The crystalline essence of the fabricated Ch-CuNPs, examined via X-ray diffraction spectroscopy, underscored the resonant presence of sharp peaks. Specifically, the peaks at 36.78°, 43.38°, 50.56°, and 74.26° were indicative of the face-centered cubic structure, effectively reflecting the 111, 111, 200, and 220 reflection planes. Additionally, the smaller peak manifesting at 22.98° was attributed to the semi-crystalline nature intrinsic to chitosan. Notably, the absence of any pronounced peaks convincingly negated the presence of entities like cuprous oxide or cupric oxide (Fig. 1B). The average size of Ch-CuNPs measured by zetasizer ranged between 87 and 89 nm, with a polydispersity index of 24.74% (Fig. 1C).

In pursuit of molecular interactions, FTIR analysis emerged as a crucial avenue. The resulting FTIR spectrum of Ch-CuNPs, showcased in Fig. 1D, was particularly revealing. The expansive band within the 3370 –3226 cm−1 interval seamlessly aligned with the -OH groups of chitosan, their resonance overlapped with the concurrently present amine groups-an intricate facet within the chitosan structure. Distinctive bands at 1633 cm−1 and 1016 cm−1 portrayed the bending vibration of protonated amine groups and the presence of -C-O-C groups, respectively-a distinctive imprint of the glycosidic linkage within chitosan. Of equal significance was the resolute band manifesting at 680 cm−1, shedding light on the interaction that transpired between chitosan and copper nanoparticles.

In vitro assays of Ch-CuNPs against M. phaseolina

Impact on fungal growth, morphology, and biochemistry

The results garnered from the antifungal study unveiled a pronounced inhibitory effect on the fungal mycelial growth, marking a noteworthy reduction of 18, 41, and 71% at concentrations of 0.03, 0.06, and 0.09% of Ch-CuNPs, respectively (Fig. 2A). The impact intensified with escalating Ch-CuNPs concentrations at 0.12 and 0.15%, which exhibited complete inhibition of the fungus (Fig. 2B). Building upon these observations, the study extended its focus to ascertain the effects of nanoparticles on fungal mycelial growth in liquid conditions. Notably, the mycelial dry weight of M. phaseolina declined, attaining reductions of 20–95% at Ch-CuNPs concentrations ranging from 0.03 to 0.15%, in comparison with the control. Remarkably, the ultimate threshold was reached at 0.18% of Ch-CuNPs, rendering mycelial dry weight assessment unattainable. Fungus’s copper uptake efficacy

In parallel investigations into fungal growth patterns and morphological attributes, noticeable fluctuations in the fungus’s Cu uptake potential were observed across Ch-CuNP concentrations. The growth capacity of the fungus peaked at a threshold of 0.12%, beyond which further expansion was not sustained. This, the Cu analysis in the fungal biomass was carried out over the range of 0.03 to 0.12%, where a significantly maximum uptake efficacy (29.9%) was observed with a 0.03% Ch-CuNP concentration. As the concentration of Ch-CuNPs increased, the uptake efficiency significantly decreased to 14.5, 10.7, and 6.4% at concentrations of 0.06, 0.09, and 0.12%, respectively, indicating reduced copper absorption by the fungus at higher Ch-CuNP levels (Fig. 5).

Fungus’s Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy analysis

FTIR analysis was executed to discern the intricate molecular interactions between Ch-CuNPs (0.03–0.12%) and the biomass of M. phaseolina, juxtaposed with untreated (raw) fungal biomass (Fig. 6). In the raw fungal biomass, a total of around 14 peaks were observed, including a broadband signal spanning the 3379 –3229 cm⁻¹ range corresponding to –NH stretching groups, indicating the presence of chitin. Small bands at 2920 and 2852 cm⁻¹ were attributed to –CH asymmetric and symmetric vibrations, respectively. A distinct band at 1743 cm⁻¹ was associated with lipids, and a band at 1651 cm⁻¹ corresponded to functional groups of proteins. Additional features were noted at 1239, 1159, 1074, and 1028 cm⁻¹, corresponding to C–O–C cyclic and acyclic vibrations in the phosphate region. Discussion

Among the prominent plant pathogens, M. phaseolinastands out as a culprit behind severe yield losses (ranging from 40 to 100%) in a variety of economically vital crops, including vegetables, pulses, oilseeds, and fruit-bearing plants16. Despite extensive efforts to curb its impact through various control strategies, this pathogen’s resilience remains pronounced, necessitating the exploration of alternative approaches. In the context of advancing agricultural techniques, nanotechnology has introduced innovative precision farming methodologies aimed at augmenting both food quality and safety. These techniques encompass the facilitation of nanoscale nutrient absorption from the soil, elevation of crop productivity and soil health, and reduction of overall agricultural input requirements13. The current approach aligns with the broader trend of developing sustainable solutions to combat persistent threats posed by formidable plant pathogens like M. phaseolina.

The study began with the synthesis and characterization of Ch-CuNPs, which were distinguished by a distinctive brick-brown color in the aqueous solution, attributed to the interaction of free electrons in the outer orbitals with incident light. Spectral analysis revealed an absorption peak at 572 nm in the synthesized solution, aligning with the characteristic wavelength range (500–600 nm) associated with the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) of CuNPs, as reported in previous studies17,18 This absorption profile confirms the successful reduction of Cu²⁺ into CuNPs. Additionally, the interaction between Cu²⁺ ions and chitosan is facilitated through electrostatic forces, where the hydroxyl and ether groups of chitosan engage with the metal cations, enabling chitosan to function effectively as both a nucleation controller and stabilizer15.

The XRD pattern of Ch-CuNPs elucidated their crystalline orientation and nature, affirming the formation of a crystalline monoclinic structure as reported previously19. The distinctive peaks detected at 2θ values of 43.38°, 50.56°, and 74.26° corresponded to the crystallographic plane indices of 111, 200, and 220 of Cu, respectively reflecting a face-centered cubic structure. Furthermore, a minor peak evident around 2θ = 20° was attributed to chitosan, confirming its presence and role in encapsulating the nanoparticles to prevent agglomeration during the synthesis process20–22. Furthermore, no peaks corresponding to CuO or Cu2O phases were detected, which are common impurities in copper synthesis. This absence might be due to chitosan’s protective effect, preventing the oxidation of Cu nanoparticles into CuO or Cu2O20,23.

The average size of Ch-CuNP measured by zetasizer was between 87 and 89 nm with a narrow size distribution (polydispersity index of 24.74%)24. Furthermore, the interaction between Ch-CuNPs was assessed using FTIR spectroscopy. A broad peak at 3370 cm−1 was attributed to the stretching vibrations of O-H and N-H groups coupled with a peak at 1637 cm−1signified to bending vibrations of N-H of chitosan20. Absence of peaks around 1200–1430 cm⁻¹ range likely due to the coverage of Ch-CuNPs. Additionally, a peak at 680 cm⁻¹, corresponds to Cu-O stretching vibrations, indicative of the presence of copper-oxygen bonds between chitosan and copper nanoparticles20,25. These spectral patterns underscore the notably hydrophilic nature of the Cu nanoparticles, in agreement with observations documented26. The mechanism underpinning the encapsulation of CuNPs by chitosan through chemical reduction is schematically depicted in the inset image in Fig. 7. The demonstrated stability of the synthesized nanoparticles can be attributed to their higher surface charge, which fosters increased electrostatic repulsion between the particles, in accordance with the findings presented27.

Ch-CuNPs demonstrated pronounced antifungal efficacy against M. phaseolina as analyzed in agar and broth medium. Notably, at concentrations of 0.03 to 0.09% of Ch-CuNPs, the mycelial growth exhibited reductions to 20–71%, while complete inhibition of M. phaseolina was achieved with further raising concentrations. These findings were consistently observed in the broth assays, where a significant reduction of 20–95% in mycelial dry weight was achieved with 0.03 to 0.15% concentrations of Ch-CuNPs. Previously, Ch-CuNPs demonstrated significant fungicidal properties, achieving 74% antifungal activity at a 0.12% concentration against the phytopathogens Alternaria solani and Fusarium oxysporum28. Another study reported that Alternaria alternata, Macrophomina phaseolina, and Rhizoctonia solaniexhibited 89% inhibition of fungal mycelial growth at a 0.1% Ch-CuNP concentration29. Additionally, a 0.1% concentration of Ch-CuNPs effectively inhibited the mycelial growth of Sclerotium rolfsii and R. solani30. Likewise, Ch-CuNPs demonstrated potent antifungal effects against damping-off pathogens, R. solani and Pythium aphanidermatum, achieving up to 98% inhibition of mycelial growth at a 0.1% concentration24. These results highlight the broad-spectrum antifungal efficacy of Ch-CuNPs at ≥ 0.1% concentration, demonstrating their potential as an effective bio-nanopesticide.

Furthermore, microscopic examination unveiled transformations such as the distortion and decreased compactness of sclerotia, along with colorless and fragmented hyphae, in correlation with escalating Ch-CuNPs concentrations. The larger surface area of Ch-CuNPs, allows for tighter adherence and diffusion to the fungal cell surface, leading to compromised membrane integrity and effective fungal inhibition24,31,32. A similar observation was reported30, with Ch-CuNPs on sclerotia formation in Rhizoctonia solani and Sclerotium rolfsii, demonstrating significant antifungal activity.

The concentration-dependent response of Ch-CuNPs, as reflected in the fluctuating enzymatic activities (CAT, POX, PPO, and PAL), correlates with observed growth patterns: initial enzyme enhancement at 0.03% aligns with peak fungal growth also indicate, while subsequent declines at higher concentrations coincide with reduced fungal expansion. This suggests heightened stress levels in the fungus, leading to membrane permeabilization, lipid peroxidation, protein alteration, and nucleic acid denaturation, which contribute to cell death33,34. The increased Cu content in the fungus, confirmed by atomic absorption spectrophotometry, further substantiates the impact of Ch-CuNPs on the fungal biochemical profile and growth dynamics. The antifungal impact of Ch-CuNPs could also be attributed to the release of Cu particles from chitosan at the site of fungal infection, typically an acidic environment, leading to cellular leakage24,35. As depicted in Fig. 8, earlier studies have shown that CuNPs possess antifungal potential through a ‘contact killing’ mechanism, which creates a surface that extensively damages the cell membranes of microorganisms, resulting in cell vacuole enlargement and the eventual disappearance of the microorganisms36,37. The observed effects on fungal growth, morphology, and biochemical changes suggest that the antifungal action of Ch-CuNPs against M. phaseolinais likely enhanced by their targeted disruption of the protein, lipid, and carbohydrate regions of the fungal cell, as revealed by FTIR analysis. Due to its poly-cationic nature, chitosan can disrupt negatively charged cell membrane components (e.g., proteins, phospholipids), inactivate replication processes, and inhibit chitin synthesis, complementing the properties of CuNPs, which increases the toxicity to the fungal cell. This dual action not only disrupts vital cellular processes but also interferes with the structural integrity of the fungal cells33,34,38.

Ch-CuNPs have demonstrated exceptional antifungal properties against M. phaseolina, disrupting cellular processes and structural integrity at concentrations of 0.1% or higher. This effectiveness is attributed to their dual-action mechanism: the poly-cationic nature of chitosan promotes strong adhesion of the nanoparticles to fungal cell walls, while the copper nanoparticles induce oxidative stress, causing substantial cellular damage. The promising results observed in laboratory settings highlight the substantial potential of Ch-CuNPs for agricultural applications12,13. The dual-action mechanism not only makes them effective in combating fungal pathogens but also positions them as a viable alternative to traditional chemical fungicides, which are often associated with environmental harm. By targeting fungal pathogens more specifically, Ch-CuNPs could contribute to more sustainable crop protection strategies30,39. To reinforce their practical significance, further studies should explore the performance of Ch-CuNPs in diverse agricultural environments and against a broader range of pathogens. Evidence from field trials and long-term studies would provide a more comprehensive understanding of their efficacy and safety. Additionally, assessing the potential impact on non-target organisms and the environment would be crucial for evaluating their overall suitability as a biopesticide. In parallel, nano-fertilizers-sharing similarities with Ch-CuNPs-offer additional advantages, such as cost-effectiveness, excellent solubility, and controlled nutrient release. These features enhance crop growth while minimizing waste and environmental impact. Combining nano-fertilizers with Ch-CuNPs could revolutionize crop protection and nutrient management, advancing towards more sustainable agricultural practices. This integrated approach harnesses the strengths of nanotechnology to improve crop yields and environmental stewardship.

Conclusions

Ch-CuNPs demonstrated significant antifungal activity against M. phaseolina, effectively inhibiting fungal growth and disrupting cellular integrity at concentrations ≥ 0.1%. The nanoparticles, characterized by a stable brick-red hue, were confirmed through UV-Vis and X-ray diffraction analyses. Ch-CuNPs exhibited a dual-action mechanism: chitosan enhanced nanoparticle adhesion, while copper induced oxidative stress. This dual mechanism led to significant fungal inhibition, as evidenced by reduced mycelial growth, altered fungal morphology, and decreased copper uptake. The potential of Ch-CuNPs as a biofungicide appeared promising, and integrating them with nano-fertilizers could have further advanced sustainable agricultural practices.

Material and methodology

Experimental materials and reagents

Reagents of analytical grade were employed without the necessity for additional purification steps. Copper sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4.5H2O) was procured from Riedel-de Haen, Germany, while acetic acid and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA. Chitosan (90% deacetylated) of high purity was obtained from Chemsavers. Ascorbic acid and hydrazine monohydrate (NH2NH2.H2O) were supplied by Arkema, Germany. Throughout the course of the experiments, double-distilled water remained the solvent of choice.

For cultivating the pure culture of M. phaseolina (MPSS-01), a consecutive sub-culturing process was executed on 2% Malt extract agar (MEA) medium, which comprised 20 g of Malt extract followed by 20 g of agar in 1000 mL of distilled water. This enriched medium served as the nurturing substrate for the fungus, and the cultures were duly stored at 4 °C for subsequent utilization.

Synthesis and characterization of Ch-CuNPs

Synthesis of Ch-CuNPs

The synthesis procedure for Ch-CuNPs, depicted in Fig. 9, was a modified adaptation of methodologies previously employed24,40. The synthesis commenced with the preparation of an aqueous solution of bulk copper sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4.5H2O) by dissolving the compound (1 g) in 25 mL of double-distilled water (25 mL). Subsequently, this solution was added dropwise to a chitosan solution (0.2%) within an acetic acid medium (0.1%). A distinct transition from blue to light blue was witnessed, indicative of the commencement of the reaction. The ensuing steps comprised of a series of precise interventions. The solution was stirred at 60 °C for an hour, after which ascorbic acid solution (0.05 M, 1.25 mL) was introduced. Following an additional 20 min of stirring, sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution (0.6 M, 5 mL) was introduced into the mixture. This stage did not elicit any immediate change in coloration, but after approximately 30 min of continuous stirring, a subtle transition to a light green hue became perceptible. The final addition of hydrazine monohydrate (NH2NH2.H2O) (1.25 mL) resulted in a rapid transformation, yielding a swift shift from a light brown to a deep red color within about 5 min of stirring. To ensure the completion of the reaction, the solution was further stirred for an additional 30 min, following which it was allowed to cool to room temperature. The subsequent phase involved filtration of the reaction suspension, leading to the isolation of particles that formed the basis for further characterization and analysis of the Ch-CuNPs.

Characterization of Ch-CuNPs

To confirm the successful formation of Ch-CuNPs, UV-visible spectrophotometric analysis was executed using the Rittun Ultra-3000 spectrophotometer. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted (Siemens D500 X-ray diffractometer, Germany) to ascertain the crystalline structure of Ch-CuNPs. The assessment of average nanoparticle size and polydispersity index (PDI) was conducted to assess uniformity and stability of the nanoparticle suspension. The experimental procedure involved performing triplicate analyses of each sample at a scattering angle at 90˚ and a constant temperature of 25 ℃. The dispersing medium for the sample was pure water. To elucidate the functional groups responsible for the reduction of copper to nano-size, FTIR (Prestige-21, Shimadzu, Japan) was employed using potassium bromide (KBr) pellet, which served as a medium for obtaining spectral information.

In vitro assays to evaluate antimycotic activity of Ch-CuNPs against M. phaseolina

The potential inhibitory effects of Ch-CuNPs on fungal growth were assessed using the Poison Food Technique. Various concentrations of Ch-CuNPs (0.03, 0.06, 0.09, 0.12, and 0.15%) were prepared by adjusting the volume to 15 mL with 2% Malt extract agar (MEA). The control group was established by solely adding 15 mL of MEA to the Petri plate. To initiate the experiment, a 6 mm mycelial disk was carefully excised from a 7-day-old fungal culture and positioned at the center of each Petri plate. Subsequently, three sets of replicates for each of the six treatments were arranged, and the Petri plates were incubated for 7 days at a temperature of 30 °C. Assessment of total protein content and defense-related enzymes

To determine total protein content, 0.2 g of fungal biomass was ground in 5 mL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 20 min. A final volume of 1 mL was prepared with 0.1 mL enzyme extract and 0.9 mL distilled water. Reagent B (0.5% CuSO4) and reagent C (0.4 g NaOH, 2 g Na2CO3) were mixed and incubated for 30 min. Folin Phenol Reagent (0.1 mL) was then added. Total protein content was determined at 650 nm using a standard curve from bovine serum albumin (BSA)41.

To assess catalase (CAT) activity, solution A was prepared by diluting 0.1 M phosphate buffer with distilled water at a 1:1 ratio. Solution B was then created by adding 0.3% H2O2to solution A. A portion (100 µL) of the enzyme extract was mixed with solution B (2.9 mL), and the absorbance was measured at 240 nm42. For peroxidase (POX) activity, the enzyme extract (0.5 mL) was suspended in a reaction mixture containing 0.1 M phosphate buffer (2 mL, pH 6.8) and 0.01 M pyrogallol (1 mL). The reaction was conducted at 25 °C for 5 min and was quenched by the addition of 5 N H2SO4(1 mL). The absorbance at 420 nm was measured to determine the amount of purpurogallin formed43. The reaction mixture for polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity consisted of enzyme extract (100 µL) and 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (1.5 mL, pH 7.0). The assay was initiated with the addition of 0.01 M catechol (200 µL), and the changes in absorbance were recorded at 30-second intervals over a 3 min period at 495 nm44. For phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity, the assay mixture comprised of L-phenylalanine (0.5 mL), 0.1 M phosphate buffer (0.5 mL), plant extract (0.1 mL), and distilled water (1.9 mL). After a 10 min pre-incubation at 37 °C, the substrate was added, and the absorbance was recorded at 270 nm. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the plant extract pre-incubated in a buffer without substrate was used as a blank before each assay45.

Quantification of remaining metal content in fungal biomass

The protocols for atomic absorption analysis were employed to quantify the concentration of copper (Cu) in the fungal biomass. To prepare the sample, 0.1 g of powdered fungal biomass was heated in a China dish until it turned to ash. The ash was dissolved in a mixture of 50 mL purified water and 3 mL pure HNO₃. Transparency was achieved by adding 3 mL H₂O₂, followed by filtration to remove any particles. The clear filtrate was then collected in Falcon tubes for further analysis16. The concentration of copper was then measured using an XplorAA, GBC.

FTIR analysis of fungal biomass

To analyze the surface characteristics of the metal-loaded fungus using a Cary 630 FT-IR Agilent spectrophotometer, 1 mg of powdered biomass was mixed with 100 mg of dry potassium bromide and compressed into solid disc. Infrared spectra were recorded over the 400–4000 cm−1range, with background interference from pure potassium bromide automatically subtracted45.

Statistical analysis

The data were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and one-way ANOVA analysis was used in order to measure the statistical significance followed by LSD test using Statistix Software (8.1). Differences were taken to be significant for P < 0.05.
