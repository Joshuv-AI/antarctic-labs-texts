Prognostic Role of Platelets in Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension
Authors: Caio Julio Cesar dos Santos Fernandes, Ilma Alves de Oliveira Nascimento, Talita Oliveira, Carlos Vianna Poyares Jardim, Susana Hoette, Rogerio de Souza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlatelets have emerged as immune‐inflammatory cells that may contribute to the pathogenesis and prognosis of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), although their clinical relevance remains uncertain. We retrospectively evaluated platelet indices in 243 patients with PAH from a tertiary referral center and compared them with 50 normopressoric controls undergoing right heart catheterization. Platelet count, mean platelet volume (MPV), and survival outcomes were analyzed. PAH patients exhibited significantly lower platelet counts compared with controls (203,666 ± 90,466 vs. 246,025 ± 126,778/µL; p < 0.001). MPV was also reduced in idiopathic PAH compared with controls (9.8 ± 1.68 vs. 10.44 ± 1.25 fL; p < 0.05). Survival analysis demonstrated that PAH patients with platelet counts within the normal range (150,000–450,000/µL) had significantly better outcomes than those with thrombocytopenia (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that alterations in platelet indices may reflect disease severity and prognosis in PAH. While exploratory and subject to potential confounders such as connective tissue disease, the results reinforce the potential role of platelets as biomarkers in pulmonary vascular disease and highlight the need for further mechanistic and longitudinal studies.

To the Editor,

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a severe disorder characterized by increased pulmonary arterial pressure and subsequent right ventricular failure, leading to substantial morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of PAH involves increased pulmonary vascular resistance caused by progressive endothelial dysfunction, marked inflammatory response, and extensive vascular remodeling [3, 4]. The precise roles of platelets in this mechanism of vascular disease remains only partially elucidated, but growing evidence suggests their involvement in both: inflammatory endothelial remodeling in PAH potentially influencing the clinical course and prognosis [5, 6, 7].

Platelets, traditionally recognized for their central function in hemostasis, have emerged as significant inflammatory and immune effector cells that actively contribute to vascular remodeling and injury through mechanisms distinct from their role in coagulation [5]. Platelet activation and associated inflammatory signaling pathways are recognized as relevant in pulmonary vascular disorders, particularly in PAH. Platelets contribute to endothelial dysfunction, pulmonary vascular remodeling, and subsequent pulmonary hypertension progression through several pathways, including release of inflammatory cytokines, microparticles, and modulation of immune cell functions [5, 6].

Mean platelet volume (MPV), an indicator of platelet size and activation state, has been implicated in various cardiovascular diseases [8]. Larger platelets possess higher metabolic and enzymatic activity, thus carrying greater thrombotic and inflammatory potential [9, 10]. The precise significance of platelet counts and MPV in the context of PAH requires further elucidation.

In our study, we evaluated retrospectively the platelets of 243 PAH patients managed at a specialized referral center (Table 1), compared to a control group (50 individuals submitted to the right heart catheterization during the same period, during an investigation of pulmonary hypertension, but that presented normal levels of mean pulmonary artery pressure, and who underwent complete blood count (CBC) testing).

In our study, platelets characteristics showed distinct patterns between PAH and normopressoric patients. PAH patients presented significantly lower platelet counts compared to controls (203,666 ± 90,466 vs. 246,025 ± 126,778/µL, p < 0.001). Specifically, patients with idiopathic PAH exhibited significantly reduced MPV compared to controls (9.8 ± 1.68 vs. 10.44 ± 1.25 fL, p < 0.05). These distinct platelet profiles might reflect the potential relevance of platelet role in PAH. In this setting, it is possible that platelet activation and subsequent consumption within the pulmonary vasculature may indicate heightened inflammatory responses and endothelial injury, aligning with studies showing significant inflammatory involvement in PAH progression and severity [11, 12].

It is important to speculate on the mechanisms underlying these differences. Alterations in MPV between PAH and normopressoric patients could represent distinct platelet activation states, potentially driving differential release of inflammatory mediators, contributing uniquely to vascular remodeling processes and clinical outcomes [12]. Thus, exploring platelet biology further may elucidate novel therapeutic targets and biomarkers for better risk stratification and personalized treatment in pulmonary hypertension [13].

We acknowledge, however, that the concept is not novel. Two decades ago, platelets were proposed as a therapeutic target in PAH, and the ASA‐STAT trial [14] demonstrated that aspirin and simvastatin failed to improve the primary clinical endpoint, although biological effects were evident. Our results should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating rather than as evidence for therapeutic intervention.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective and single‐center design may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the observational nature of the analysis precludes establishing causal relationships between platelet indices and clinical outcomes. Additionally, while the control group was matched for age and sex, other potential confounding factors, such as comorbidities and medication use, were not systematically evaluated. Finally, we did not assess longitudinal changes in platelet indices or their dynamic behavior in response to disease progression or treatment.

Despite these limitations, our findings may still be of interest. They reinforce that alterations in platelet indices, easily accessible through routine laboratory tests, might reflect disease severity in PAH. Although far from establishing causality, this observation supports the role of platelets as immune‐inflammatory cells and encourages further mechanistic studies integrating platelet biology into the complex landscape of pulmonary vascular disease. Enhanced understanding of platelet‐mediated pathways may provide crucial insights into more tailored and effective therapeutic strategies for these challenging diseases.
