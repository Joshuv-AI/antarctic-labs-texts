Identification of potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for liver cirrhosis based on Mendelian randomization and machine learning
Authors: Kang Zhang, Ting Chen, Zhangyu Jia, Junxia Zhao, Na Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLiver cirrhosis(LC) represents the end stage of chronic liver disease, yet reliable molecular markers remain limited. This study aimed to uncover potential diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for LC.MethodsWe integrated differential gene analysis from LC datasets in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) with Mendelian randomization (MR) using eQTLGen and FinnGen summary data to prioritize LC-associated genes. LASSO, SVM-RFE, RF, and XGBoost algorithms were applied to refine candidate genes. Based on these, we constructed a nomogram risk prediction model and evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and immune infiltration profiling were conducted to explore potential biological functions. Additionally, potential therapeutic compounds targeting these genes were screened using Drug Signatures Database (DSigDB) via Enrichr platform. Finally, hub genes were validated by immunohistochemistry (IHC).ResultsThrough integrative analysis,we identified five hub genes: ENPP2, FAM134B, PPARGC1A, SLFN11, and TRIM22. A nomogram based on these genes demonstrated strong predictive performance (AUC = 0.944 in the training set; AUC = 0.909 in the validation set). GSEA linked these genes to antigen processing, cell adhesion, and immune regulation. Immune infiltration analysis indicated that abnormal levels of resting NK cells (P < 0.001), M2 macrophages (P = 0.002), activated dendritic cells (P < 0.001), and neutrophils (P = 0.038) in LC. Drug prediction provides promising treatment options for LC, including valproic acid and tamoxifen.ConclusionOur integrated approach identified five hub genes associated with LC, providing valuable clues to predict and treat LC.

Introduction

Liver cirrhosis(LC) is a common, chronic, and progressive liver disorder characterized by inflammation, hepatocellular necrosis, advanced fibrosis, and regenerative nodule formation. Persistent inflammation leads to sustained liver tissue damage, increased vascular tension, and portal hypertension [1]. Globally, LC accounts for approximately one million deaths annually, ranking as the 11th leading cause of death and the third among individuals aged 45–64 years. LC progresses through two prognostic stages: compensated and decompensated [2]. Mortality risk is five times higher in patients with compensated LC and increases tenfold in those with decompensated disease compared to the general population [3]. Despite burgeoning research into the underlying pathogenic mechanisms and therapeutic options for LC, highly sensitive and specific biomarkers for early identification and intervention remain elusive. Therefore, the present study aims to identify novel diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets to improve early management and treatment strategies for LC.

Liver inflammation and fibrosis are key drivers of LC progression [4]. In the early stages, various pathogenic factors induce hepatocellular injury, activate immune cells such as lymphocytes and Kupffer cells, and trigger the release of inflammatory mediators. This promotes hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation and proliferation, leading to excessive collagen deposition, disruption of extracellular matrix balance, and ultimately drives LC. Effective antifibrotic strategies are therefore considered central to LC treatment. However, despite multiple therapeutic approaches aimed at reversing or preventing fibrosis, clinical outcomes remain unsatisfactory [1]. For example, cell-based therapies have shown promise due to their anti-inflammatory, regenerative, and immunomodulatory effects and have been explored in various cell types, including bone marrow-derived cells, mesenchymal stem cells, and macrophages [5]. Nevertheless, limited understanding of their underlying cellular and molecular mechanisms and the lack of clinical translation continue to restrict their therapeutic potential.

Thanks to the rapid development of bioinformatics, public databases such as the Gene Expression Omnibus(GEO) provide us with extensive datasets from various biological samples and experimental conditions, greatly accelerating research on gene functions, disease mechanisms, and drug discovery [6]. Meanwhile, Mendelian randomization (MR), a widely used statistical approach, employs genetic variants as instrumental variables(IVs) to infer causal relationships between exposures and disease outcomes [7]. Owing to the random allocation of alleles during meiosis, MR effectively reduces the impact of confounding factors in traditional epidemiology, and since genotype allocation occurs before disease onset, it further rules out the possibility of reverse causation [8,9]. Moreover, leaps in computational capacity have propelled the integration of machine learning(ML) into biomedical investigations [10,11]. As a key component of artificial intelligence, ML can efficiently detect hidden patterns in complex datasets and generate accurate predictions. When applied to genomic data, it is particularly effective in identifying critical genes involved in disease progression [12].

This study aims to identify key genetic biomarkers of LC and develop a risk prediction model to support early management and treatment of LC while advancing the understanding of its underlying mechanisms.

Material and methods

Data collection and preprocessing

The study workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1. Four LC-related microarray datasets (GSE139602, GSE25097, GSE89377, and GSE36411) were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) [6]. The first three datasets served as the training set, including 97 samples (25 healthy controls and 72 LC samples), while GSE36411 was used as an independent validation set (21 healthy controls and 21 LC patients). Raw expression data spanning different platforms were merged, normalized, and corrected for batch effects using the “limma” and “sva” packages in R, with principal component analysis(PCA) confirming the efficacy of correction [13,14]. Two-sample MR was then performed to assess causality between candidate genes and LC, using genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary data. Exposure data were sourced from the cis-eQTLs of the eQTLGen Consortium (https://eqtlgen.org/), which includes 31,684 blood samples across 19,960 genes [15]. The selection of IVs was performed in accordance with the three core assumptions of MR. SNPs were chosen with the exposure at the genome-wide significance level (p < 5 × 10−8),linkage disequilibrium r2 < 0.1 within 500 kb, and F-statistics >10 to reduce weak-instrument bias. Outcome data were obtained from FinnGen release 12 (accessed November 4, 2024; https://www.finngen.fi/en), comprising 2653 cases and 485,213 controls [16]. Identification of differentially expressed genes

Differential expressed genes(DEGs) between LC samples and healthy controls were identified using the limma package in R(version 4.4.1), with the criteria of |log2 fold change| > 0.585 and adjusted p-value <0.05. Heatmaps and volcano plots were generated to visualize the DEGs using the pheatmap and ggplot2 packages, respectively.

MR analysis

MR analysis was conducted using the TwoSampleMR package, applying five complementary approaches: inverse‐variance weighted (IVW), MR‐Egger regression, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode [17]. Causal estimates were primarily based on IVW at a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Genes showing inconsistent odds ratios across methods were excluded. Sensitivity analyses, including heterogeneity tests, horizontal pleiotropy assessments, and leave-one-out analysis, were performed to verify the robustness of the findings. Genes with evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (p < 0.05) were excluded. Overlapping genes between MR-identified candidates and DEGs were identified for further validation. Each intersecting gene underwent a full MR workflow with sensitivity analyses to reconfirm causal associations. Scatter plots, forest plots, and funnel plots were generated to visually support the findings.

Functional enrichment analysis of intersecting genes

Functional classification and enrichment analyses were performed using R packages to explore the biological roles of the intersecting genes [18]. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis identified associated biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC), while Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis determined enriched signaling pathways. Disease Ontology (DO) enrichment was also applied to assess disease associations. For all tests, a P value cutoff of <0.05 was applied.

Identifying hub genes by ML

To construct the diagnostic model, four machine learning algorithms were applied: Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO). Lasso performs feature selection by penalizing the coefficients of less relevant features, shrinking them to zero. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was chosen by 10-fold cross-validation based on the minimum mean squared error. Genes with non-zero coefficients were selected [19], Support Vector Machine–Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE), SVM-RFE recursively removed features using the SVM weight coefficients as an importance measure. This process aimed to identify a minimal, optimal gene subset that preserved high classification performance [20], Random Forest (RF) [21], and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [22]. The top-ranked genes were selected using RF and XGBoost, which are powerful ensemble learning algorithms with built-in feature importance scores. Model complexity was controlled by their inherent regularization mechanisms. Genes identified by all four algorithms were selected as the final hub genes, and the overlaps were visualized using a Venn diagram.

Construction of hub gene interaction network and functional enrichment analysis

The interaction network of the hub genes was mapped using the GeneMANIA web server (http://genemania.org) [23]. Their biological roles were characterized via GO and KEGG pathway enrichment using the Enrichr platform (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) [24].

Construction of nomograms and validation of hub genes

A logistic regression model was constructed to predict individual LC risk and was visualized as a nomogram, converting predicted probabilities to a 0–100 point scale [25]. Calibration plots assessed the agreement between predicted and actual outcomes, while decision curve analysis evaluated the model's clinical utility. To evaluate diagnostic performance, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for both the hub genes and the nomogram in the training and validation cohorts using the pROC package in R.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed to assess whether functions or pathways associated with the hub genes were significantly enriched at the top or bottom of the ranked gene list, indicating potential upregulation or downregulation trends [26]. Functions or Pathways with a p-value <0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

CIBERSORT was used to estimate the infiltration levels of 22 immune cell subsets in LC samples, evaluate correlations between hub gene expression and immune cell abundance, and investigate the potential regulatory roles of these genes in immune modulation [27].

Identification of candidate drugs

Potential therapeutic compounds targeting LC-related mechanisms were identified using the Drug Signatures Database (DSigDB) via the Enrichr (https://amp.pharm.mssm.edu/enrichr/).

Clinical samples

Human liver tissues were collected from surgical patients at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University, including 9 with HBV-related LC and 9 with benign liver disease. The study was conducted with approval from the Ethics Committee and informed consent from all participants.

IHC staining

The surgically resected liver tissues were fixed in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin. Subsequently, the tissue sections were deparaffinized and dehydrated in xylene. Following heat-induced antigen retrieval, sections were sequentially incubated with primary antibodies, biotinylated secondary antibody, and peroxidase-conjugated streptavidin. Immune complexes were visualized with diaminobenzidine chromogen. Image analysis was employed for the semiquantitative evaluation of immunostained specimens. For each section, ten randomly selected fields were observed under a magnification of × 200, followed by quantification using Image J software. Specifically, the software was used to determine the mean density of positively stained areas for ENPP2, PGC1α, and TRIM22, respectively, and the percentage of positively stained areas for FAM134B and SLFN11, respectively. The mean value calculated from all ten examined fields of each specimen was designated as the representative data point for that sample.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with R (v4.4.1) and GraphPad Prism (v8.0). Categorical and continuous variables were analyzed by Chi-square and Student's t-tests, respectively, with a p-value <0.05 defining statistical significance.

Using R (version4.4.1), we merged the normalized expression matrices from the three training datasets and applied the ComBat algorithm (sva package) to correct for platform‐specific batch effects. Significant batch differences were observed prior to correction, which were effectively eliminated after adjustment, ensuring the reliability of subsequent analyses. Subsequent differential expression analysis between LC and healthy controls identified 178 upregulated and 117 downregulated DEGs, details in Supplementary Table S1. MR evaluates the causal effect genes of LC

We used cis-eQTLs that affect the expression of nearby genes as exposures to explore the causal association with LC. After screening, 172,890 SNPs were selected as IVs, all meeting the three core MR assumptions with F-statistics >10. Detailed data are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Based on MR results and predefined criteria, 1236 LC-related genes were identified (Supplementary Table S3). Intersection analysis with DEGs revealed six upregulated (TRIM22, SLFN11, ENPP2, SCRN1, IL7R, GPNMB) and four downregulated genes (HSD17B14, CLEC4G, PPARGC1A, FAM134B) (Fig. 3A and B).Fig. 3(A) 6 upregulated intersecting genes. Subsequently, we performed MR analysis on these ten intersecting genes closely related to LC to ascertain the causal effects of each gene on the disease. The results indicated that in MR analysis employing the IVW method, the upregulated gene TRIM22(OR = 1.274; 95% CI:1.001-1.621; P = 0.049),SLFN11(OR = 1.261; 95% CI:1.033-1.538; P = 0.023),ENPP2(OR = 1.214; 95% CI:1.063-1.386; P = 0.004),SCRN1(OR = 1.096; 95% CI:1.005-1.195; P = 0.038), IL7R(OR = 1.322; 95% CI:1.160-1.507; P < 0.001)and GPNMB(OR = 1.139; 95% CI:1.036-1.252; P = 0.007)showed significant positive causal relationships with LC. Conversely, all four downregulated genes—HSD17B14(OR = 0.845; 95%CI:0.746-0.956; P = 0.008)、CLEC4G(OR = 0.892; 95%CI:0.817-0.973; P = 0.010)、PPARGC1A(OR = 0.921; 95% CI:0.857-0.990; P = 0.025)and FAM134B(OR = 0.720; 95% CI:0.607-0.855; P < 0.001)were negatively associated with LC(Fig. 3C).

No evidence of heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy was detected (p > 0.05), supporting the stability of the findings (Table 2). Additionally, leave-one-out analysis demonstrated that the effect size of the included IVs is consistent with the overall effect size, underscoring the robustness of the analysis. Discussion

In this study, we systematically integrated genomic and transcriptomic data to identify risk genes closely linked to LC through differential expression analysis and MR. ML algorithms were employed to further screen and validate hub genes, offering novel insights into the underlying mechanisms and therapeutic targets of LC. Based on these results, we constructed a nomogram prediction model, which holds promise for clinical application by facilitating the early identification of patients with LC, thereby enabling timely intervention to significantly slow or potentially reverse disease progression. While liver biopsy, the invasive gold standard, has limited clinical adoption, biomarker-based screening for LC is increasingly utilized. Previous radiomics studies demonstrated that liver-spleen morphological radiomics (AUC = 0.909) outperformed standard laboratory-based models, including APRI (AUC = 0.69) and FIB-4 (AUC = 0.76), in predicting LC. In this study, the LC risk prediction model constructed with the five feature genes achieved AUCs of 0.944 and 0.909 in the training and validation sets, respectively, significantly surpassing the aforementioned models. Moreover, it effectively overcomes the subjective interpretation bias inherent in imaging examinations [28].

Integrated bioinformatic and experimental validation revealed that ENPP2 and TRIM22 were upregulated in LC, while FAM134B and PPARGC1A were downregulated. Interestingly, SLFN11 was downregulated in human hepatitis B-related LC, which is inconsistent with our previous findings. We speculate that this discrepancy may stem from variations in disease stages and etiological heterogeneity. Additionally, clinical sample size and inter-individual differences could also influence the results. Beyond enhancing LC risk assessment, these biomarkers may provide insights into its biological processes and pathogenesis. GSEA indicated that these hub genes are closely associated with biological processes such as antigen processing and presentation, cell adhesion, and immune response regulation. Given the crucial role of immune cells in the pathogenesis and progression of LC [29], we further evaluated differences in immune cell infiltration between normal and LC samples using the CIBERSORT algorithm. We observed a significant reduction in NK cell proportion in LC, consistent with previous reports. During hepatic fibrogenesis, NK cells exhibit decreased abundance and impaired function, weakening their cytotoxic effects on HSCs and thereby exacerbating fibrosis [30]. Furthermore, we found strong correlations between several hub genes (e.g., ENPP2) and both M1 and M2 macrophages. During liver injury, M1 macrophages secrete multiple pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic cytokines, such as IL-6 and TNF-α, promoting hepatic inflammation and HSC activation. In contrast, M2 macrophages, which showed a negative correlation with ENPP2, demonstrated impaired synthesis of anti-inflammatory cytokines, hindering tissue remodeling and repair, and thus accelerating fibrotic progression [31]. In summary, our results suggest that the regulation of hub genes may influence the enrichment of specific immune cell subsets, thereby affecting immune homeostasis in the context of LC.

ENPP2, also known as ATX, encodes a protein with lysolipase D activity, which hydrolyzes lysophosphatidylcholine(LPC) to lysophosphatidic acid(LPA) [32]. LPA binds specifically to G protein-coupled receptors, influencing biological processes such as cell growth, proliferation, differentiation, and intracellular signaling [33]. The ENPP2-LPA axis plays a critical role in various physiological and pathological processes, including vascular and neural development, lymphocyte homing, inflammation, fibrosis, and tumor progression [34]. In healthy adults, ENPP2 is highly expressed in adipose tissue, the central nervous system, and reproductive organs, with overexpression observed in chronic inflammatory and fibrogenic diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, fibrosis in the liver, lung, and kidney, as well as cancer [35]. Previous studies have shown that serum ENPP2 levels are elevated in patients with LC, consistent with our findings [36]. Furthermore, ENPP2 levels significantly correlate with liver fibrosis stages and liver stiffness, outperforming serum hyaluronic acid and the aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index (APRI) in predicting LC [37]. The upregulation of ENPP2 expression may promote LC by activating HSC and enhancing pro-fibrotic signaling. Thus, it is particularly necessary to further investigation into the relationship between ENPP2 and LC, as well as the underlying mechanisms.

FAM134B, also known as RETREG1, is a well-characterized endoplasmic reticulum (ER) autophagy receptor. It facilitates the selective removal of damaged or dysfunctional ER fragments through ER-phagy, supporting ER turnover and membrane remodeling to maintain ER homeostasis and manage ER stress [38]. Dysfunction of FAM134B has been linked to various pathological conditions, including viral infections, inflammation, hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy type II, and cancer [[39], [40], [41], [42]]. ER stress is a hallmark of chronic liver diseases and plays a central role in the progression of hepatic disorders such as alcoholic liver disease, viral hepatitis, liver fibrosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [43]. Studies have shown that ER stress contributes to liver fibrosis by promoting hepatocyte injury, inflammatory responses, and HSC activation [44]. However, the specific role of FAM134B in LC remains largely unexplored. In our study, we observed a significant downregulation of FAM134B in cirrhotic liver tissues, suggesting a potential protective role in chronic liver injury. It is plausible that FAM134B may help alleviate ER stress by maintaining ER-phagy activity, facilitating the clearance of misfolded proteins, and subsequently reducing inflammation and HSC activation.

PPARGC1A is a critical transcriptional coactivator involved in regulating mitochondrial biogenesis, energy metabolism, antioxidant defense, and adaptive stress response [45]. It is abundantly expressed in oxidative tissues such as brown adipose tissue, heart, kidney, skeletal muscle, and brain, where it promotes the expression of genes related to fatty acid oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation [46]. Previous studies have reported that PPARGC1A expression is significantly reduced in the livers of rats with secondary biliary cirrhosis, and treatment with silymarin can restore its levels by reducing hepatic oxidative stress [47]. Additionally, markedly lower PPARGC1A expression has been observed in the livers of patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis compared to healthy individuals [48]. The downregulation of PPARGC1A may contribute to the development of LC through several adverse mechanisms. Reduced PPARGC1A expression impairs mitochondrial respiration, resulting in ATP depletion and excessive reactive oxygen species production. Accumulated ROS can directly damage hepatocytes and activate key pro-fibrotic pathways, including NF-κB and TGF-β signaling, which drive HSC activation and proliferation, promoting fibrosis. Additionally, decreased PPARGC1A may weaken the hepatic antioxidant defense capacity, intensifying inflammatory responses under persistent oxidative stress and further accelerating fibrosis and cirrhosis progression [49].Our findings confirm that PPARGC1A expression is significantly lower in cirrhotic liver tissues compared to healthy controls, supporting its potential role as a protective factor in maintaining hepatic homeostasis.

SLFN11 is involved in antiviral defense and cellular responses to replication stress [50]. It has been identified as a key regulator that can trigger irreversible replication arrest and subsequent cell death under replication stress conditions [51]. Previous studies have also demonstrated that SLFN11 enhances the sensitivity of various tumor cells to DNA-damaging agents, with higher SLFN11 expression linked to better survival outcomes in patients with ovarian and colorectal cancers [52,53]. Interestingly, our analysis of public databases indicated that SLFN11 expression was significantly upregulated in cirrhotic tissues, whereas small-sample tissue validation showed its downregulation in LC. This discrepancy may be influenced by factors such as sample size, inter-individual variability, and etiological heterogeneity in LC. Future studies should validate the role and mechanism of SLFN11 in LC using larger cohorts that account for diverse etiologies and include comprehensive functional experiments.

TRIM22 is highly inducible by both type I and type II interferons and is involved in antiviral responses, tumorigenesis, and inflammatory processes [54,55]. Studies have shown that TRIM22 can promote senescence in hepatocellular carcinoma cells through activation of the AKT-p53-p21 signaling cascade [56]. TRIM22 also plays a role in controlling NF-κB transcriptional activity, thereby affecting the progression of various diseases. Research by Ye et al. [57] demonstrated that TRIM22 was significantly elevated in the intestinal tissues of patients with ulcerative colitis and in related mouse models, where TRIM22 knockdown alleviated inflammation by suppressing NF-κB signaling. Consistently, TRIM22 expression has been found to be upregulated in liver tissues of patients with alcoholic cirrhosis and hepatitis C virus-related cirrhosis [58], which aligns with our results. We propose that TRIM22 upregulation in cirrhotic liver tissues may contribute to fibrosis by enhancing local inflammation via NF-κB pathway activation, leading to increased pro-inflammatory cytokine production, HSC activation, and collagen deposition.

Currently, pharmacological treatment for LC primarily focuses on controlling complications, slowing disease progression, and improving quality of life. While significant progress has been made in antiviral therapy, its effects on patients with decompensated cirrhosis remain inconsistent [59,60]. Developing specific antifibrotic or proregenerative drugs is a key research focus. However, the development of new drugs requires substantial time and financial investment, and no effective anti-fibrotic or pro-regenerative drugs have been approved for clinical use [61]. Therefore, using bioinformatics approaches to identify potential drugs based on target genes may facilitate new drug discovery and reduce associated costs. In this study, we utilized the DSigDB database to search for potential therapeutic compounds, offering new insights into targeting LC. Valproic acid, a first-line antiepileptic drug, is also a validated histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor [62]. Numerous studies indicate that HDACs play a crucial role in fibrosis and HSC activation. Valproic acid treatment has been shown to prevent HSC activation induced by various pathogenic factors, reducing collagen deposition and inflammatory cell infiltration. Furthermore, experimental studies demonstrate that valproic acid treatment significantly enhances DNA damage and apoptosis in activated HSC [63]. Tamoxifen, a selective estrogen receptor modulator widely used in breast cancer treatment, has demonstrated potential as an anti-fibrotic agent. A previous in vitro study showed that tamoxifen can directly inactivate HSC by reducing hypoxia-inducible factor-1α levels and extracellular matrix protein synthesis [64]. Furthermore, tamoxifen may indirectly suppress HSC activation by antagonizing estrogen signaling and subsequently downregulating pro-fibrotic factors such as TGF-β1 [65]. In this study, we identified potential therapeutic agents that may reverse or regulate the expression patterns of five key biomarkers related to LC. Drug candidates were ranked based on mechanistic relevance, pharmacodynamic properties, and potential multi-target effects. These findings may help accelerate clinical research on LC therapeutic strategy and provide new avenues for improving treatment options for patients with this condition.

Although the algorithmic model developed in this study shows promising potential for assessing LC risk, several limitations remain to be addressed. First, this study primarily relied on data from public databases. Although we obtained a limited number of local human LC specimens for validation, the relatively small sample size constrains the generalizability of our model. Moreover, etiological information was only clearly available in the local validation dataset, which limited cross-etiology comparisons. Future studies should urgently incorporate LC patients with diverse etiologies from clinical practice, enabling extensive validation and investigation of potential etiological heterogeneity in LC risk model. Second, although the five hub genes are closely associated with LC, their specific roles in LC progression require broader investigation. Follow-up studies will utilize in vitro approaches (e.g., overexpression or knockdown of hub genes in HSCs) and in vivo models (e.g., genetically modified mice) to further elucidate their causal relationships. Finally, although identified drugs such as valproic acid and tamoxifen have been proposed as potential therapeutics targeting the hub genes, experimental studies to explore their detailed mechanisms of action are still necessary. Subsequent research will employ in vitro phenotypic assays and in vivo animal models to evaluate their therapeutic potential in LC.

Conclusion

Our research integrated differential gene expression analysis, MR, and ML algorithms to identify five key genes closely associated with LC. Based on these genes, we established a novel disease risk prediction model. Moreover, by screening the DSigDB database, we identified potential therapeutic compounds targeting these feature genes, which may offer new options for future drug development in LC. Overall, our work presents a multidimensional strategy for the identification of candidate biomarkers and therapeutic targets in LC.
