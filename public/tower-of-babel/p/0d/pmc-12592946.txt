A New Emerging Threat in Critical Care: Myroides odoratimimus Outbreak in a Tertiary Hospital
Authors: Unsal Savci, Baris Eser, Mustafa Sungur, Serap S Yildiz, Ozlem Akdogan, Selahattin Caliskan, Mustafa Sahin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims and backgroundMyroides spp. is an environmental pathogen and causes disease in immunocompromised patients. In this study, we report an outbreak of urinary tract infections (UTIs) caused by Myroides odoratimimus (M. odoratimimus) in a university hospital in Turkey.MethodsA total of 25 M. odoratimimus strains isolated from the clinical samples of 20 patients in our intensive care units and clinics were included in the study. Phenotypic and genotypic identification of isolates was performed using conventional methods, VITEK®-2 automated identification system, Matrix Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization-Time of Flight Mass Spectrometry, and 16S-RNA Microbial Diagnosis methods. In addition, the repetitive extragenic palindromic (REP) elements polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay method was applied for molecular epidemiological analysis.ResultsAll cases were diagnosed with nosocomial UTI, except for one case diagnosed with nosocomial bacteremia. One of the M. odoratimimus isolates was sensitive to piperacillin/tazobactam (MIC: ≤4 µg/mL), and one isolate was moderately sensitive to cefepime (MIC: 16 µg/mL). Other all M. odoratimimus isolates were resistant to the tested antibiotics beta-lactams, monobactams, carbapenems, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and sulphonamides. When 10 isolates were evaluated with the REP PCR method, DNA fingerprint similarities were visually determined, and there was a similar DNA pattern among them. The source of Myroides infection could not be identified from the environmental samples.ConclusionsThe increasing population of immunosuppressed patients suggests that M. odoratimimus and other opportunistic multi-drug resistant pathogens with resistance to broad-spectrum antibiotics may be encountered more frequently in critical units in future.In order to choose the optimal antibiotic regimen, awareness and an index of suspicion regarding this atypical pathogen are important for rapid identification and appropriate susceptibility testing.Clinical significanceIt has been observed that the pathogenicity of M. odoratimimus bacteria has increased recently, and it can not only cause UTIs but more serious manifestations such as sepsis with bacteremia, which can be life-threatening.How to cite this articleSavci U, Eser B, Sungur M, Yildiz SS, Akdogan O, Caliskan S, et al. Highlights

Myroides odoratimimus caused a nosocomial outbreak of multidrug-resistant urinary tract infections (UTIs) in a Turkish tertiary hospital, primarily affecting immunocompromised intensive care unit patients.

Despite extensive environmental sampling, no external source of contamination was identified, while repetitive extragenic palindromic (REP) – polymerase chain reaction (PCR) revealed clonal similarity among isolates, suggesting a possible healthcare-associated transmission.

The isolates showed resistance to nearly all tested antibiotics, underlining the critical need for rapid identification and tailored antibiotic therapy in future outbreaks.

Introduction

A hospital-acquired infection refers to an infection that develops during a patient's stay in the hospital, with no indication that it was present or incubating at the time of their admission.1 Hospital-acquired infections are a major healthcare hazard affecting hospitals in both developed and developing countries. This condition tends to occur in all areas of healthcare, including long-term care facilities, outpatient clinics, and emergency departments, and even after a patient has been discharged from the hospital. A new pathogen recently seen in nosocomial outbreaks is Myroides species, which is not normally pathogenic but can cause hospital-acquired urinary tract and other infections under certain conditions, such as immunosuppression.2 Urinary tract infections are among the most frequently encountered infections linked to healthcare settings.3

Myroides species, which was isolated from the human intestine in 1923 for the first time, was classified as Flavobacterium spp., and Gram-negative, aerobic, non-lactose fermentor, oxidase-positive, and non-motile bacteria with yellow pigment (positivity of flexirubin).4–6 Due to important genomic and phenotypic characteristics, Myroides spp. were reclassified as a new genus.7–9

The genus Myroides originally included two species: Myroides odoratus and Myroides odoratimimus. These are environmental microorganisms seen in soil and water, but not a part of human microflora.8 Myroides injenensis was first isolated from human urine in 2012.10,11

M. profundi, M. marinus, M. phaeus, M. pelagicus, M. guanonsis, and M. xuanwensis are the other species in this family, but are not associated with human infections.10–13 Myroides species can also be isolated from meat-processing plants, the gut of adult flesh flies, and seafood products.14–16

Myroides infections are mostly reported in immunocompromised individuals; they rarely cause disease in immunocompetent patients.6,17 Myroides genus can also cause community or hospital infections as they are low-grade opportunistic pathogens. In addition, Myroides species can cause soft tissue infections, septic shock, pneumonia, systemic infections, necrotizing fasciitis, UTI, or erysipelas.18 M. odoratimimus causes outbreaks of life-threatening UTIs due to its multiple drug resistance and unknown pathogenicity.19

In addition to sporadic cases, the incidence of M. odoratimimus has been increasing as a cause of hospital infections. M. odoratimimus has emerged as a significant cause of nosocomial infection, especially in immunocompromised patients with indwelling urinary catheters.20

The reasons for this increase include non-compliance with antimicrobial stewardship, increased frequency of invasive procedures, expansion of the immunocompromised patient population, and widespread use of modern diagnostic methods.21,22

In this study, which presents a large case series, it was to investigate the epidemic potential, antibiotic resistance profile, and risk factors of M. odoratimimus isolates, which have been reported with increasing frequency in the intensive care units (ICUs) of our hospital.

Despite its increasing incidence, till date, hospital-acquired outbreaks of infection caused by Myroides ssp. have been reported in limited numbers in the literature.18,22–24

Our aim in this study, in which we report a hospital outbreak due to M. odoratimimus causing UTI and bloodstream infection in a hospital in Turkey, is to investigate the potential of hospital outbreaks, antibiotic resistance profile and risk factors of M. odoratimimus infection due to isolates, which are increasingly reported in ICUs and other clinics of our hospital.

Methods

Between October 2018 and December 2019, a total of 25 M. odoratimimus isolates were included in the study, isolated from samples from 20 patients who were admitted to a hospital in Turkey. The study was approved by the local ethics committee and carried out in accordance with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient or their relatives.

Bacterial Identification

All samples were inoculated onto 5% sheep blood agar, eosin methylene blue agar, and chocolate agar media for initial identification and incubated at 37°C under aerobic and microaerophilic conditions for 18–24 hours. After incubation, all the isolates were first stained with Gram stain and examined under a microscope, and then an oxidase test was applied to the isolates detected as gram-negative bacillus. Further phenotypic and genotypic identification of Gram-negative and oxidase-positive bacilli was performed using three different methods: VITEK®-2 automated identification system, Matrix Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization-Time of Flight Mass (MALDI-TOF MS) Spectrometry with MALDI Biotyper (Bruker Daltonics, Germany) Identification, and 16S-RNA Microbial Diagnosis methods.

Antibiotic Susceptibility Test Procedure

VITEK®-2 GN AST-N222 (BioMérieux, France) cards were used for antibiotic susceptibility tests, and the cards were loaded into the VITEK®-2 (BioMérieux, France) automated system according to the manufacturer's recommendations. The 15 included for susceptibility testing antibiotics tested were: Amikacin, aztreonam, ceftazidime, ciprofloxacin, cefepime, gentamicin, imipenem, levofloxacin, meropenem, netilmicin, piperacillin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, tetracycline, tobramycin, and piperacillin/tazobactam with VITEK®-2 (BioMérieux, France) automated system according to EUCAST (European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing) standards.

MALDI-TOF Spectrometry Identification System

Using the VITEK®-2 (BioMérieux, France) automated system, isolates identified as Myroides spp. with a probability of ≥95% were subsequently redefined using the MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry system (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany).

16S-RNA Microbial Diagnosis

16S-RNA Microbial Diagnosis (DNA isolation + PCR + DNA Sequence analysis + Bioinformatic Analysis) was performed for three isolates. DNA extraction was performed on freshly harvested bacterial colonies using the Qiagen Blood and Tissue Isolation Kit (Hilden, Germany) according to the instructions of the manufacturer. For the detection of Myroides spp. 16S-RNA, the primers 907R ‘ CCGTCAATTCMTTTGAGTTT 3’ 27F 5’ AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG 3 were used in a touch-down PCR assay. DNA sequencing was performed on the ABI 3100 Genetic Analyzer using the BigDye Cycle Sequencing kit v.3.1.

Epidemiological Analysis

Environmental Surveillance Sampling from ICUs and Clinics

Environmental surveillance samples taken from the ICU where these patients were admitted and clinics included urine bags, urine bag taps and urine bin, wet wipes used for perineum cleaning, area of urinary culture containers, cabinets for sterile gauze/pads, clean laundry cart, care pads, sinks antiseptic handwash solutions, liquid soap, taps and tap water, etc.

REP Elements PCR Assay

GeneMATRIX Tissue & Bacterial DNA Purification KIT (EURx Ltd., Gdańsk, Poland) was used for DNA extraction. The obtained DNA was stored at −20° until the analysis was done. The REP-PCR was conducted using the methods and primers (REP1R-I (5’- IIIICGICGICATCIGGC-3’), REP2-I (5’- ICGICTTATCIGGCCTAC-3’)) developed by Reboli et al.25 Acinetobacter baumannii strain was used as a control to show that the REP-PCR method can make good DNA typing in different epidemiological groups.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using MedCalc statistical software (demo version 17.6; Ostend, Belgium, 2017). Continuous variables such as age, duration of hospitalization, and laboratory markers (e.g., WBC, neutrophil count, CRP, procalcitonin) were assessed for distribution. These variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data and median (range) when appropriate. Categorical variables, including sex, presence of comorbidities, ICU admission, device use, antibiotic resistance profiles, and clinical outcomes, were summarized using counts and percentages. Due to the descriptive nature of the study and the limited sample size, no inferential statistical comparisons or hypothesis testing (e.g., t-tests, Chi-square tests, regression models) were performed. Data interpretation focused on identifying epidemiological trends, resistance patterns, and clinical features associated with M. odoratimimus infections. A p-value threshold was not applied, as no statistical tests were conducted.

Results

The mean age of the patients was 69.85 ± 15.57 years (range 24–88). Twelve of 20 patients (60%) were male, and eight were female. One of the patients was hospitalized in the surgical clinic, one in the chest diseases clinic, and the other 18 were in the second and third level ICUs. Four of the patients had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or pneumonia, and two had suffered cardiac arrest due to these diseases, were successfully resuscitated and transferred to the ICU. Two patients were connected to a mechanical ventilator after surgery for acute subdural hematoma, and two of them were in the ICU due to ischemic cerebrovascular disease. One patient was hospitalized for an incisional hernia operation, one for a hip prosthesis operation, and one patient for acute pancreatitis. All patients had indwelling urinary catheters to monitor urine output. Patients other than those with COPD or pneumonia were catheterized in the emergency room. All the infections met the criteria of hospital-acquired infections M. odoratimimus was isolated from blood in one patient and urinary samples in all other patients. Discussion

The spectrum of community-based and hospital-acquired infections caused by atypical pathogens is constantly being updated. There has been an increase in the number of newly described microorganisms due to the use of molecular methods such as 16SrRNA sequencing and MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry in clinical microbiology laboratories.26

Myroides spp., which is generally found in external environments such as soil and water, often causes various opportunistic infections in immunocompromised patients with a history of organ transplantation, solid malignancy, rheumatologic/inflammatory diseases, neutropenia, HIV diseases, diabetes mellitus, and those on immunosuppressive therapy.27–30 In our patient group, six patients had diabetes mellitus.

As a result of re-isolation of M. odoratimimus in follow-up cultures of eight patients, high infection markers in this period (Table 3), and not isolating an additional pathogen in the cultures, these patients were evaluated as urinary tract infection caused by M. odoratimimus.

Myroides spp. can be classified as a multidrug-resistant environmental organism and can accommodate different resistance patterns simultaneously, as shown in this and in other studies.31 Intrinsic resistance to β-lactamases is due to the presence of two metallo-β-lactamases, MUS-1 and TUS-1, which share 73% amino acid identity.32 Also, a resistance gene island was found on the chromosome of the bacterium [10]. This region contains different types of resistance genes, such as text (conferring tetracycline resistance), cat (chloramphenicol resistance), bla-OXA-347, and bla-OXA-209 (conferring β-lactam resistance).33

Combined use of tigecycline and quinolones with rifampicin for the treatment of M. odoratimimus was reported to provide successful results.18,22 In the treatment of infections caused by other Myroides spp., there are articles reporting that the use of cotrimoxazole, meropenem, or piperacillin/tazobactam constitutes a good clinical response.5,17,34

All M. odoratimimus isolates we detected in our study were resistant to almost all antimicrobials tested. In our study, a patient receiving meropenem and tigecycline treatment was successfully treated. Four patients who received tigecycline, meropenem, piperacillin-tazobactam, colistin, and tigecycline responded well to the treatment. Five patients remained hospitalized in the ICU at the time of data collection, while the other 10 patients died. In addition to Myroides infections, we think that the reason for these patients' deaths is the contribution of primary diseases, mechanical ventilation, patients' severe comorbidities, and critical illness.

Antimicrobial resistance has reached alarming levels in different parts of the world. As a result of inappropriate and often uncontrolled use of antibiotics, many treatment options are ineffective.

In our hospital, especially in ICUs, pan drug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections have increased recently, and the use of antibiotics such as third-generation cephalosporin, carbapenem, colistin, and linezolid has increased for the treatment of these infections. It is likely that the widespread use of these broad-spectrum antibiotics may have contributed to the selection of M. odoratimimus isolates.

Also, M. odoratimimus isolates are strong biofilm producers. Biofilms are sessile bacterial communities that adhere to both biotic and abiotic surfaces, such as medical devices. The bacteria are trapped in an extracellular polymeric matrix that they produce.35 The production of a strong biofilm is a serious problem because it increases pathogenicity in device-related infections and is often associated with therapeutic failure, as well as persistence of infections.36 In the current study, all patients had a urinary catheter, and we consider it as a likely predisposing factor for M. odoratimimus infections.

Holmes et al. identified six isolates of Flavobacterium odoratum from urine and three isolates from infected wounds, but they didn't consider their strains actedas pathogens.37

In this study, we hypothesized 10 isolates typed by REP-PCR came from the same epidemiological origin because of similar DNA patterns and clusters of cases in the same period may be due to a nosocomial outbreak (Fig. 1). In addition, this study is the first study in the literature where Myroides spp. isolates were investigated using REP-PCR.

Yağcı et al.23 reported an outbreak of UTIs caused by F. odoratum at a hospital in Turkey. They reported that all infected patients were catheterized or had urinary tract neoplasia or urinary calculi (stones located anywhere in the urinary tract, including renal, ureteral, or bladder stones).

In their report, two of the four patients had urological interventions (transurethral resection of the prostate and bladder cancer cystectomy with bilateral cutaneous ureterostomy for benign prostatic hyperplasia).

Ktari et al.22 reported 7 cases of UTI originating from M. odoratimimus who had undergone endourological surgeries in a Tunisian hospital urology unit in 2012 and had urinary calculi (except one). Although the history of urinary calculi, neoplasia, and endourological surgery were common in both outbreaks, these conditions were not present in our patients.

Licker et al.18 also reported a nosocomial outbreak in four immunocompromised patients in Romania in 2017. Risk factors for Myroides spp. infection in our patient group may be immune suppression, long-term hospitalization (especially in intensive care), multiple drug resistance due broad spectrum antibiotic use, concomitant infections, and urinary catheterization.

Kutlu et al.24 reported that M. odoratimimus strains were isolated from 6 immunocompromised in Turkey in 2020. All patients had indwelling catheter in situ.

Aygar et al. conducted a study in Turkey in 2023 in which M. odoratimimus was isolated from clinical samples of 66 patients, 63 of which were urine samples and three were wound samples, and M. odoratus was isolated from a urine sample of one patient. Myroides species were not detected in the blood, other bodyfluids, or other clinical samples of the patients.38

As previously reported, the source of nosocomial Myroides spp. infections is unknown, although the transmission of bacteria in the hospital environment is suspected.17,22,23

The increase in Myroides infections, which started in November 2018 in our hospital, decreased with the review and the improvement of general infection control and prevention procedures, but it has not.

Although comprehensive surveillance screening was performed in the departments where patients with M. odoratimimus were hospitalized in our hospital, the source of the pathogen could not be shown. While the increase in the number of UTIs caused by this unusual organism occurred over a period of 13 months, it probably suggests a common source, which is yet to be identified.

Since the source of endogenous infection could not be determined in these patients, it was thought that the bacteria might have been acquired from an environmental source. The patients' hospitalization periods of at least two weeks and the presence of urinary catheters in all patients suggest that there could be lapses in the policies and procedures involving urinary catheter insertion or maintenance.

We recommend that if urinary catheterization will be performed for a long time, use of silicone catheters or antimicrobial coated catheters that are more resistant to chemical effects than latex catheters may be considered.

There are some limitations to the current study. This study covers a limited number of patients and only one health center that limits generalizability of results.

The presence of multiple comorbidities and prolonged intensive care stays in all patients may complicate the assessment of Myroides spp. as an independent pathogen, raising the possibility that its pathogenic role could be partly opportunistic; further studies are needed to clarify this issue.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that Myroides odoratimimus, previously considered a rare opportunistic pathogen, may play a more active role in healthcare-associated UTIs, particularly in patients with prolonged hospitalization, indwelling urinary catheters, and exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics. The clonal similarity among isolates supports the likelihood of nosocomial transmission. Host-related immune dysfunction may also contribute; however, further studies are needed to fully understand the clinical significance and transmission dynamics of this pathogen.
