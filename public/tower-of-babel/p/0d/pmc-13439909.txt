The role of P4C-based teaching in developing listening skills and lesson motivation: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Oğuzhan Sevim, Ali Uzun, Yakup Ulusal, Seher Sevim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe purpose of this study is to examine the effect of P4C-based teaching on the development of listening skills and lesson motivation.MethodologyThe study used an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. The study group consisted of 48 fifth-grade students (24 in the experimental group and 24 in the control group) enrolled in a state middle school in Turkey. Turkish lessons for the experimental group were conducted over 12 class hours across 6 weeks using lesson content developed according to the P4C Education Program. At the end of the intervention, interviews were conducted with students in the experimental group to determine their views on the P4C applications.FindingsQuantitative findings showed that listening performance and motivation scores increased significantly in the experimental group, while there was only a limited change in the control group. Qualitative data, on the other hand, showed that students were able to focus for longer periods during P4C sessions, understood the coherence of events and texts better, felt more successful and motivated, participated actively in class, and were more willing to listen to their peers.Originality/valueResearch has shown that the intervention, delivered using P4C-based teaching, was significantly associated with an increase in children’s listening comprehension skills and their motivation towards the subject. Furthermore, the research showed that P4C supports not only cognitive outcomes but also the affective components of the learning process.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-05127-8.Keywords: P4C, Listening skills, Lesson motivation, Mixed-methods study

Introduction

Individuals who engage in social interaction require certain communication skills and the ability to use them effectively. Enabling individuals to realize their potential and use their communication skills effectively is a fundamental goal of contemporary education [10]. Language skills are central to this goal, encompassing expression (speaking, writing) and comprehension (reading, listening). Among these, listening is the first and primary skill acquired [65]. Listening is an active process that requires the conscious participation of the receiver and involves constructing meaning from verbal and nonverbal messages [3, 63, 75]. It has been reported to account for approximately 40–50% of communication time [70]. However, research shows that listening skills in schools are often treated as passive reception in both theory and practice,accordingly, students' performance in understanding what they hear shows significant deficiencies [6, 43, 82]. Although the Middle School Turkish Language Teaching Program targets critical thinking and social-emotional learning in addition to language skills, there remains a need for interaction-based models that structure listening in an effective and motivating way in classroom practice [69, 101]. The Turkey Century Education Model and the Middle School Turkish Language Curriculum emphasise dimensions such as listening and watching, critical thinking, social-emotional learning, self-awareness, communication, and collaboration. As outlined in the Turkey Century Education Model, it is important for children’s development that they learn to question, ask questions, engage in discussion, seek answers, listen to others, and respond to them [67]. The Turkey Century Education Model also aims to cultivate individuals who internalise philosophical knowledge through philosophical thinking, inquiry, reading, writing, and discussion skills [69]. For this reason, Philosophy for Children (P4C) can be considered an appropriate model for concretising the programme’s vision within the classroom. Additionally, while Turkish language courses are responsible for developing the four basic language skills, low achievement in listening and weak interest and motivation in classes have been reported for a long time [12, 83, 91].

Motivation is defined as a multidimensional construct that initiates and sustains behaviors in the interaction between the learner and their environment. In language learning, it is characterized by the desire to learn, a positive attitude, and effort [11, 36, 85, 86]. Research shows that highly motivated students are more successful in language learning courses and manage strategic listening comprehension processes more effectively [4, 8, 24–26, 31, 92]. In contrast, low motivation and attention problems in classes have been reported to hinder the lasting development of listening skills [15, 82, 104]. This indicates that listening skills have both cognitive and affective dimensions,therefore, the relationship between listening and motivation should be addressed together in instructional design. In this study, motivation is not considered as general school or classroom motivation, but as a subject-specific construct related to students’ interest in, positive attitude towards, willingness to participate in, and tendency to exert effort in Turkish lessons. Therefore, the research focuses on the affective orientation demonstrated by students within the context of a specific subject. The literature also emphasises that examining motivation in a context- and subject-sensitive manner leads to a more accurate assessment of the effectiveness of instructional interventions [25, 36].

This integrated perspective requires a shift toward contemporary educational approaches that prioritize affective engagement alongside cognitive activity in the learning process. Models that simultaneously engage students' thinking, listening, and communication skills are considered especially effective in meeting the complex learning demands of the twenty-first century. This educational perspective does not limit language teaching to grammatical accuracy; it also aims to develop life skills such as critical thinking, creativity, communication, collaboration, and problem solving [84, 97, 98]. Additionally, autonomous learning environments and activities provide students with opportunities to practice and develop their listening and speaking skills in a supportive and collaborative setting [18]. In this context, the P4C approach offers a noteworthy pedagogical model. P4C is a teaching method based on Socratic questioning, constructivism, and democratic classroom principles, centering on student participation and active listening [61, 66, 105]. P4C sessions require students to focus on the flow of conversation, listen to their peers without interruption, and reflectively re-evaluate their own thoughts. Mindfulness is defined as directing attention to the present moment in a conscious and non-judgemental manner [48]. In this context, although P4C is not a direct mindfulness training model, it provides a learning environment that aligns with classroom processes related to mindfulness, as it encourages students to focus on the flow of conversation, listen to their peers without interrupting, and reflectively re-evaluate their own thoughts. Mindfulness literature emphasises that attention regulation, present-moment focus, and non-judgemental awareness are associated with learning engagement and emotional regulation [16, 48]. From this perspective, the fact that students maintain attention, listen attentively to their peers, and re-examine their thoughts during P4C sessions provides a theoretical framework that helps explain the findings regarding listening and motivation.

In P4C sessions, students generate questions from prompts, develop reasoned arguments, and revise their views by listening carefully to their peers [13, 21, 29, 89, 102]. Research shows that P4C significantly improves students' language skills, cognitive flexibility, motivation, and classroom participation [2, 14, 52, 56, 90]. In this context, P4C has a twofold effect on listening skills: dialogue-based interaction develops students’ listening strategies and meaning-making processes, while inquiry-based learning environments increase students' intrinsic motivation through idea generation and participation in discussions. Increased intrinsic motivation naturally has a positive effect on the students’ academic performance [62]. Thus, P4C supports lasting learning by integrating the cognitive and affective dimensions of listening (Fig. 1).

Key conceptual dimensions in the listening–motivation–P4C interaction

A review of the existing literature shows that most studies have focused on the effects of P4C on higher-order cognitive and social skills such as critical thinking, speaking, and empathy. However, studies directly examining the interaction between listening skills and motivation toward the lesson are quite limited [7, 9, 23, 40, 45, 47, 49, 53, 54, 56, 64, 68, 76, 78–80, 98]. The literature generally addresses the cognitive and affective aspects of listening [33, 94], the decisive role of motivation in language learning success [25, 77], and the effects of P4C on critical thinking, problem solving, communication, and social skills [51, 57, 88]. However, studies examining these three variables – P4C, listening, and motivation – as a holistic model within the same theoretical framework are quite limited both internationally and in language teaching contexts. This gap has not been systematically addressed, particularly in native language teaching samples, and the present study aims to address this deficiency. To this end, the following research questions were investigated:

This study is unique in conceptualizing P4C as an original pedagogical model that integrates cognitive and affective processes in listening instruction and explains the interaction between listening and motivation. The research results are expected to provide theoretical and practical contributions to applications aimed at increasing listening motivation in Turkish language teaching. They are also expected to guide teacher training and program development processes by revealing the effectiveness of P4C in the context of language teaching.

Research methodology

This study used an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design to examine the effects of P4C practices on students’ listening skills and motivation towards the subject. In this design, the research process occurs in two main stages: the quantitative stage and the qualitative stage, which are connected through integration processes [20, 46]. The general flow of the study is shown in Fig. 2.

Explanatory sequential design

First, students' motivation levels toward the course were measured using a validated and reliable 5-point Likert scale (Quantitative Phase). Next, listening comprehension skills were assessed with a 20-item achievement test consisting of multiple-choice, true–false, and short-answer questions based on two different texts. At this stage, a pretest–posttest quasi-experimental design was used; data were analyzed with mixed ANOVA (Group × Time). Normality (Shapiro–Wilk) and variance homogeneity (Levene) were tested, and the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied when necessary; the Bonferroni correction was used for significant differences. Findings are reported with F, p, ηp2, and 95% confidence intervals [35].

Second (Qualitative Phase), an in-depth explanation of the quantitative findings was provided. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with students in the experimental group; the recordings were transcribed and analysed using MAXQDA 24 software. The qualitative data were analysed through a content analysis approach, and a code–category–theme structure was developed [55, 73]. Coding was performed independently by the researchers, and reliability was ensured by calculating Krippendorff’s α coefficient. Credibility was enhanced through member checking (participant validation), peer review, and audit trail strategies [74].

In the final stage (Integration & Interpretation), quantitative and qualitative data were integrated using a joint display approach. In this process, qualitative themes revealed the explanatory elements underlying the quantitative findings; thus, the relationships between listening and motivation variables were interpreted in a multifaceted manner [34].

This structure incorporates the two integration points required by the explanatory sequential design: connecting and merging. In the connecting phase, quantitative findings guided the qualitative data collection process, while in the merging phase, findings were synthesized comparatively [46]. Thus, the study comprehensively revealed the cognitive (listening) and affective (motivation) effects of P4C in the context of language learning across both quantitative and qualitative dimensions.

Participants

The study included 48 students from two fifth-grade classes at a state middle school in Turkey (experimental group n = 24, control group n = 24). Participants were informed about the process before the application. In addition, written consent was obtained from the participants' parents. Researchers administered a preliminary listening test to all fifth graders at the school and reviewed previous listening assessments conducted by teachers. The two classes with the closest baseline scores were selected; one was assigned to the experimental group and the other to the control group. This selection method is not simple random sampling but a quasi-experimental approach matched for baseline equivalence. Therefore, the initial equivalence of the groups was tested in the quantitative analysis using a pretest covariance model, and the standardized difference was reported according to What Works Clearinghouse (WWC) thresholds. The calculated standardized difference Δ = 0.01 SD, and since |Δ| was within the range of < 0.05 SD, the groups were considered statistically equivalent at baseline. As there was no significant difference between the pretest scores, covariance adjustment (ANCOVA) was not necessary, and analyses were conducted directly using repeated measures ANOVA [35, 99]. The experimental group included 13 boys and 11 girls, while the control group included 12 boys and 12 girls. Inclusion criteria were regular class attendance, no reported cognitive or auditory impairment (according to teacher records), and attendance in Turkish language classes.

In both the experimental and control groups, the duration of instruction, class schedule, grade level, implementation timeline, assessment tools, and the teacher leading the sessions were kept constant. Both groups used the same learning outcomes framework for the Turkish language course; instruction was delivered over 12 sessions, with two 40-min classes per week. In the experimental group, these learning outcomes were addressed through P4C-based sessions. Using stimuli such as stories or short videos, students engaged in generating questions, developing reasoned thoughts, listening to their peers, and structuring their views through discussion. In the control group, instruction was conducted using activities based on the existing Turkish language textbook, with the same duration and class schedule maintained. Students participated in processes such as listening to or watching texts, answering comprehension questions guided by the teacher, completing text-related activities, and doing exercises in the textbook. Listening instruction in the control group used texts from the textbook and related comprehension activities; however, unlike in P4C, there was no provision for structured question generation, the concept of a community of inquiry, or student-centred extended discussion cycles. Having the same teacher conduct lessons for both groups helped control for teacher influence. Therefore, the fundamental methodological distinction between the groups was not the duration of instruction or the teacher variable, but the pedagogical approach used to address the same learning objectives.

One of the researchers, who was also the teacher conducting the intervention, participated in P4C training before the study and obtained a certificate. To monitor programme fidelity, all P4C sessions were evaluated using a 12-item observation checklist (Appendix 1). All 12 sessions were observed, ensuring 100% coverage. The checklist assessed whether the session began according to the planned P4C flow, whether the prompt was presented clearly and appropriately, whether students were given time to think, whether the process of generating and selecting appropriate questions was implemented, whether justification of views was encouraged, careful listening to peers and respect for turn-taking, allowing expression of differing viewpoints, the facilitator maintaining a facilitative role, conducting the discussion in accordance with the “community of inquiry” framework, supporting the listening, thinking, and speaking processes in a balanced manner, and conducting a brief reflection at the end of the session. Items were scored as follows: 0 = not observed, 1 = partially observed, and 2 = fully observed. In the observed sessions, total scores ranged from 20 to 23, and the average protocol adherence score was 21.75/24 (90.63%). This finding indicates that P4C sessions were generally conducted with high adherence. No instances of complete deviation from the protocol (0 points) were recorded in the observation forms; however, partial deviations (1 point) were observed in some sessions, particularly in the initiation of the session, presentation of the prompt, selection of questions suitable for discussion, maintenance of the “community of inquiry” flow, and the reflection phase at the end of the session. As the observations were conducted by a single observer, the inter-observer reliability coefficient was not calculated.

P4C applications provide an interaction-based teaching model designed to develop students' dialogue, reasoning, and metacognitive skills [1]. In this context, fifth grade is considered appropriate for P4C applications because it is the age when students' abstract thinking and philosophical inquiry skills begin to develop [14].

No a priori power analysis was conducted before determining the sample size. However, a post hoc power analysis was performed using Jamovi software to assess the adequacy of the sample in detecting the observed effects. The analyses used the t-test for independent samples, based on the observed effect sizes. The results indicated a power value of 0.924 for the listening skill variable and 0.709 for the class motivation variable. These findings suggest high statistical power for the listening skill variable and acceptable statistical power for the motivation variable [32].

Data collection tools

Listening comprehension achievement test (LCAT)

LCAT was initially developed by Özcan [78] as a 21-item assessment tool based on two texts (one narrative, one informative) from the Turkish Ministry of National Education's 5th grade Turkish textbooks and was used in doctoral thesis. Before being used in this study, the test items were reviewed by two faculty members and researchers specialising in Turkish language education. During this review, it was found that a multiple-choice item based on the informational text (Jules Verne) was structurally flawed. The item did not yield a single correct answer. This item, which targeted the sub-skill of accessing explicit information and distinguishing information provided in the text, was removed from the test, and the LCAT was used in this study in its final 20-item form. Despite the removal of this item, the final form retained its ability to cover both text types and the fundamental sub-skills related to the listening-comprehension process. When scoring the LACT, each question was evaluated on a scale of 1 point. To increase measurement precision and minimise the impact of chance, a partial scoring approach was applied to True/False items containing multiple statements. Accordingly, in Item 10, which consists of four statements, each correct response was weighted at 0.25 points,in Item 20, which consists of two statements, each correct response was weighted at 0.50 points. For scoring open-ended questions, objectivity was ensured by using a Keyword-Based Rubric prepared by the researcher and approved by subject matter experts.

A pre-test was conducted to evaluate the test's applicability, and the data obtained yielded Cronbach's α = 0.81. Independent raters coded the open-ended questions; inter-rater reliability was Cohen's [19] κ = 0.72. All statistical procedures and analyses were conducted on the final 20-item form.

For content validity, a panel study was conducted with five Turkish language education experts. The content validity ratio (CVR) for each item was CVR ≥ 0.80, and the content validity index (CVI) was 0.92. To reduce the habituation effect, the listening test was administered in two equivalent forms (Form A as the pre-test and Form B as the post-test), with the order randomized. In the form comparison analyses conducted on pilot/pre-test data (n = 42), a strong and significant correlation was observed between the scores of Form A (M = 11.89, SD = 3.50) and Form B (M = 12.03, SD = 3.28) (r = 0.86, p < 0.001). Additionally, no significant difference was found between the mean scores of the two forms, t(41) = 0.54, p = 0.59. These findings support the conclusion that the forms have similar levels of difficulty. The internal consistency coefficients of the forms were also examined separately; Cronbach’s α was 0.82 for Form A and 0.85 for Form B. These results indicate that both forms are sufficiently reliable. However, an IRT-based form-equating analysis was not conducted in this study.

The listening conditions were identical for all students, using the same audio recording (listening time for the Mosquito text was 3 min 12 s, and for the Jules Verne text was 3 min 40 s; reading speed was 86.85 for the Mosquito text and 111.17 for the Jules Verne text), the same speaker, and the same classroom acoustics [5]. The lowest possible test score is 0, and the highest possible score is 20.

Turkish language course motivation scale (TLCMS)

The TLCMS, developed by Erdem and Gözüküçük [30], was used to assess participants' motivation levels toward Turkish language classes before and after the intervention. The scale is a 5-point Likert-type instrument consisting of 15 items. Participants were asked to select one of the following options for each item: ‘5 = Strongly agree’, ‘4 = Agree’, ‘3 = Undecided’, ‘2 = Somewhat agree’, and ‘1 = Disagree’. Of the 15 items, 8 are positive and 7 are negative. Negative items were reverse-coded during scoring. The total score on the scale ranges from 15 to 75,higher scores indicate greater motivation for the Turkish language course.

In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated using the study data based on the scale’s 15-item structure, after the reverse-coded items were rescored. In the scale development study, Cronbach's alpha internal consistency coefficient was 0.75, and in this study, it was 0.78, indicating that the scale has sufficient reliability [30]. For this research, the scale was administered to both the experimental and control groups as a pre-test and post-test,students were given 20 min to complete it.

Semi-structured interview form

Semi-structured interviews were conducted using a six-question interview guide developed according to the stages proposed by Kallio et al. [50]. The interview questions focused on students’ perceptions of their motivation for P4C activities in class, their listening and speaking patterns with peers, the effects of these activities on their lives outside school, and their experiences with sustaining attention, listening, and comprehending what they heard over extended periods. Following the pilot study, two questions that overlapped with others were removed, and a final interview guide comprising four questions was prepared for use during data collection (see Appendix 2). Face-to-face interviews lasting 12–18 min were conducted with 24 students selected from the experimental group through purposive sampling. The interviews were recorded, transcribed, and analysed in MAXQDA 24 software within a content analysis framework. The data were coded independently by three researchers, and recurring meaning units were organised into codes, categories, and broader themes. Data saturation was observed around the 20th interview and confirmed at the 24th [41, 42]. Member checking (participant validation), peer review, and an audit trail were used to enhance trustworthiness, and COREQ was used for reporting [60, 74, 87].

Data analysis

The quantitative and qualitative data obtained in this study were analyzed in two stages, in line with the explanatory sequential design. Quantitative data were analyzed in the first stage, and qualitative data were analyzed in the second stage.

In the analysis of quantitative data, the normality of the data set was first tested. Due to the relatively small sample size, the Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality [35]. The Shapiro–Wilk test results showed p > 0.05 in all subgroups. Therefore, the listening and motivation scores met the normal distribution assumption, and the use of parametric methods was deemed appropriate. A mixed-design ANOVA with repeated measures on time was conducted to examine pre- and post-test changes as a function of group. Effect sizes were calculated using the ηp2 (eta-squared) coefficient. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 29 software. This analytical approach aimed to reveal not only between-group differences but also the effects of time and the group × time interaction [35].

In the qualitative phase, audio recordings from semi-structured interviews were transcribed and analyzed using MAXQDA 24 software. A content analysis approach was adopted to systematically identify recurring meaning units in students’ responses and organise them into codes, categories, and broader themes [55, 72]. The coding process was conducted independently by the researchers, and Krippendorff’s α coefficient was calculated to assess inter-coder reliability. The overall reliability coefficient at the code level was α = 0.87, indicating that the qualitative coding has a high level of reliability [55]. After independent coding, the researchers met to discuss codes with discrepancies, and the final coding structure was established by consensus. For example, during the coding of the statement from S6, “Listening to what my friends say has helped me develop the ability to listen without interrupting anyone and while maintaining eye contact,” there was initially disagreement among the researchers. One researcher classified this statement directly under the “listening attentively to the other party” code, while another noted that the phrase “without interrupting anyone” more strongly reflected the “respect for the right to speak” code. During the consensus meeting, it was determined that the statement represented two related but distinct units of meaning rather than a single behaviour. Consequently, the statement was divided: the “listening while maintaining eye contact” portion was finalised under the “listening attentively to the other party” code, and the “without interrupting anyone” portion was finalised under the “respect for the right to speak” code. In this way, both the student’s attentive listening behaviour and their sensitivity to taking turns in communicative interaction were more accurately reflected in the analysis.

When creating themes, expert opinions were sought, and data were verified through member checking (participant validation). Participant identities were kept confidential, and students were coded as “P1, P2, P3.” Peer review, audit trails, and thick description strategies were used to enhance the reliability and transferability of the data [60, 74].

Qualitative findings were used to interpret the quantitative analysis results in an explanatory and complementary manner, consistent with the mixed methods design. Quantitative findings were compared with qualitative themes using the joint display approach, and points of similarity and divergence were identified [34]. This ensured consistency of the results in both statistical and interpretive dimensions.

Therefore, it is concluded that this practice provides a holistic pedagogical framework that enhances the in-class learning experience and can be applied in different contexts.

Discussion

This study used a mixed-methods approach to examine how a P4C-based teaching approach relates to listening comprehension skills and motivation towards the subject. Both quantitative and qualitative findings demonstrated that P4C is significantly associated with cognitive and affective outcomes. The increase in listening success (ηp2 = 0.24) and the rise in motivation (ηp2 = 0.12) were found to be meaningful both statistically and educationally. These effects were also supported by qualitative findings, including students' active participation, sustained attention, understanding of the plot, and increased perception of success. However, the relatively high effect size for listening skills (ηp2 = 0.24) should not be interpreted unilaterally, as if the finding resulted solely from the pedagogical structure of P4C. The fact that the programme provided students with an interactive and engaging experience, distinct from the usual lesson flow, may have produced a novelty effect in the short term. Particularly in six-week interventions, the increased interest and participation students showed in the new instructional format may have partly contributed to the improvement in listening performance. Therefore, to more clearly distinguish the sustainability of the observed effect and P4C’s structural contribution, studies with longer-term follow-up measurements are necessary.

These results are consistent with previous studies reporting that classroom philosophical dialogues improve students' listening, argumentation, and metacognitive strategy use [90, 93, 100]. Qualitative themes indicating that P4C activates the “attention – understanding – reasoning – remembering” processes in the speaking and listening cycle (e.g., being able to listen for a long time, understanding the coherence of the event) reinforce this view. Furthermore, the study revealed that listening in children must occur through active participation and attentiveness. Similarly, Ide [44] emphasized that activating children's wisdom is at the core of P4C and requires deep attention and awareness.

Qualitative data also showed that students perceived P4C sessions as a socially and emotionally safe learning environment. Participants noted that the circular seating arrangement and small group structure reduced social pressure and fear of judgment, facilitating free expression [71]. This finding parallels studies showing that the “intellectual trust” fostered by P4C in learning communities supports open communication by strengthening students’ sense of belonging.

Similarly, experimental studies based on dialogic teaching and P4C have reported significant increases in listening comprehension and textual coherence skills [38, 96]. These studies emphasized that the effect of P4C was particularly pronounced in outcomes directly related to higher-order cognitive processes. However, some experimental studies in the literature have reported mixed findings regarding the effect of P4C on academic skills. For example, Gorard et al. [38] observed limited increases in reading and mathematics in a large-scale experimental study and did not find a significant difference in character development or socio-emotional indicators. Similarly, Ventista [95] reported in a doctoral study that P4C did not have a significant effect on the critical thinking or creativity of fifth-grade students. These differences are generally explained by methodological variables such as teacher fidelity of implementation, session duration, type of output, and target age group [39]. The current study's focus on a skill that is a direct building block of P4C – listening – makes the observation of a meaningful effect both consistent with the literature and methodologically sound in this context.

However, the current findings should be interpreted in the context of the international P4C literature, taking cross-national contextual differences into account. In the United Kingdom, findings from P4C research appear to be mixed; while an early large-scale efficacy trial reported limited but positive gains in reading and mathematics, comparable effects on academic outcomes were not replicated in a later large-scale effectiveness trial [27, 28]. In the Hong Kong context, P4C has been shown to support teachers’ development of more dialogic and inquiry-based classroom discourse [57]. In Golding’s account of Philosophy in Schools, P4C is discussed primarily in relation to inquiry processes, meaning-making, reasoned judgement, and collaborative work within the community of inquiry [37]. Therefore, the development observed in listening skills in the current study should not be interpreted simply as a context-independent P4C effect, but rather in relation to how active listening, reason-giving, and collaborative meaning-making are culturally and pedagogically structured in Turkish language classes. In other words, the nuances observed across different contexts seem to relate less to the core principles of P4C itself than to the outcomes measured, the extent to which classroom conversation norms are dialogic, and the duration and maturity of implementation in each context.

Qualitative findings show that students responded positively to the P4C process, with codes such as Feeling well (F = 24), Feeling more successful (F = 24), and Increase in active participation (F = 10). This aligns with findings from Self-Determination Theory that autonomy, competence, and relatedness increase motivation [22, 58, 103]. It is also noted that students' motivation is strongly influenced by their linguistic lives [81]. P4C's peer feedback and evidence-based discussion structure strengthened students' perceptions of self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation for success, consistent with the significant increase in motivation scores (ηp2 = 0.12).

Additionally, mindfulness literature provides a theoretical basis for explaining this result. Mindfulness is defined as “the process of paying attention in a particular way, on purpose, in the present moment, and without judgment” [48]. As individuals become more attuned to the present experience through mindfulness, they also develop emotional regulation skills, which promote higher basic psychological need satisfaction [16]. Therefore, mindfulness supports the perception of autonomy and competence by making it easier for individuals to notice their thoughts and feelings [17, 59]. These mindfulness qualities inherent in P4C sessions – attention, nonjudgmental listening, and openly sharing thoughts – may have strengthened students' psychological need satisfaction. Thus, P4C not only has cognitive effects but also lays the groundwork for emotional well-being and mindfulness-based learning experiences.

In this context, the effects of P4C on listening and motivation can be evaluated not only pedagogically but also in terms of cognitive-emotional integration. The simultaneous development of students' skills such as empathy, attention, self-awareness, and self-regulation during the P4C process contributes to both academic achievement and social-emotional learning.

Result

This study has shown, using both quantitative and qualitative data, that the intervention based on P4C-based teaching is significantly associated with an increase in children’s listening comprehension skills and their motivation towards the subject. Quantitative findings show that listening performance and motivation scores increased significantly in the experimental group, while only limited change occurred in the control group. This result indicates that P4C supports both cognitive outcomes and the affective components of the learning process.

Qualitative data offered a deeper explanation of these quantitative results, showing that students were able to focus for longer periods during P4C sessions, had a better understanding of the coherence of events and texts, felt more successful and motivated, participated actively in class, and were more willing to listen to their peers. These findings demonstrate that P4C transforms listening from a passive skill into an active, meaning-based, and social process.

This study makes a twofold original contribution to the existing literature. First, it is one of the few studies to examine the effect of P4C on listening skills not only in terms of cognitive dimensions (attention, comprehension, memory) but also within a holistic framework that includes affective components (motivation). Second, it tests this effect using an explanatory sequential mixed methods design, revealing the statistical effect with quantitative findings and explaining the underlying mechanisms with qualitative data. Thus, it simultaneously answers the questions “what happened?” and “why did it happen?” regarding the effect of P4C in the field of listening.

In conclusion, P4C programmes provide a holistic learning environment linked not only to pupils’ academic performance but also to 21st-century skills such as critical thinking, empathy, collaboration, communication, active listening, and motivation to learn. In this respect, P4C can be considered an effective pedagogical alternative that should be included in contemporary mother tongue teaching programs.

Limitations and recommendations

While this study presents significant findings on the impact of P4C-based teaching on listening skills and motivation in Turkish lessons, it does have certain methodological limitations. The research involved two fifth-grade classes, comprising 48 students at a single public middle school. Groups were assigned at the class level by maintaining existing cohorts, rather than through individual random assignment. Although equivalence was established using pre-tests for listening and motivation, the use of intact classes did not permit true random assignment; therefore, the influence of certain unmeasured variables cannot be entirely excluded. In particular, factors such as classroom climate, peer interaction, group dynamics, students’ prior academic experiences, and their pre-existing attitudes towards Turkish language classes may have partially influenced the findings. Additionally, as students’ prior Turkish course performance, overall academic grade point averages, or socioeconomic indicators were not systematically collected in this study, the baseline equivalence assessment was limited to the available pre-test measurements. Therefore, the direct generalisability of the findings to different socioeconomic levels, age groups, school types, and broader student populations is limited. Moreover, the absence of a prior power analysis when determining the sample size should be considered a methodological limitation. Although post-hoc power estimates indicate that the observed effects could be detected, studies with larger samples, particularly regarding the motivation variable, will provide more robust conclusions.

Second, the qualitative data relies solely on students’ subjective views. Although the credibility of the findings is supported by direct student statements, aspects such as the quality of in-class interaction, the teacher’s role, and the dynamics of conversation in the P4C implementation are not corroborated by multiple data sources, such as observation, video analysis, or discourse analysis. Additionally, some question phrasing in the semi-structured interview form is limited by potential response bias. In particular, the structure of the first question, “How did P4C activities affect your motivation?” includes wording that partially presupposes the existence of such an effect. Similarly, the inclusion of both listening and speaking dimensions in the second question may have partially complicated the interpretation of participant responses. Therefore, in future studies, particularly in qualitative data collection processes involving child participants, it is recommended to use more neutral and single-dimensional question formulations. This would allow for a more in-depth explanation of how interaction patterns influence the development of listening skills and motivation [96].

Third, the effectiveness of P4C sessions depends on how fully teachers internalise their role as facilitators. Although adherence to the method was found to be high in this study, whether P4C pedagogy can be implemented similarly by different teachers remains a subject for further research. Therefore, standardisation of teacher training and widespread implementation of in-service training programmes focused on P4C pedagogy are recommended. Additionally, the relatively high effect size for listening skills suggests that the finding may have been influenced not only by the pedagogical structure of P4C but also by a potential novelty effect in short-term applications. Therefore, it is important to conduct studies with longer durations and follow-up measurements.

Finally, the measurement tools used in the study focused on students’ motivation and listening proficiency; however, mediating variables such as awareness, self-efficacy, and empathetic listening were not included in the model. Although no absolute floor or ceiling effects were observed in the pretest scores on the Turkish Language Course Motivation Scale, the relatively high initial motivation scores may have partially limited the scope for improvement for some students. This situation should be carefully considered, particularly when interpreting the magnitude of change in the motivation variable. Future studies may propose structural equation models (SEM) to test how P4C influences motivation through mindfulness-based mechanisms [16, 17, 48]. In this context, the current study serves as a proof of concept, demonstrating at a preliminary level the potential to establish an explanatory model for the relationship between P4C, listening skills, and class motivation. It lays the groundwork for testing the direct and indirect pathways between active listening, self-efficacy, participation, and motivation in structural models developed with larger samples.
