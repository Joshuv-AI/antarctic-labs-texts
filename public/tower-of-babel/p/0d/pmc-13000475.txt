Naltrexone/bupropion for binge-eating disorder: A human laboratory investigation of mechanisms
Authors: Sherry A McKee, Janet A Lydecker, Brian Pittman, Ania M Jastreboff, Ralph J DiLeone, Meaghan Lavery, Carlos M Grilo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBinge-eating disorder (BED) is a prevalent, refractory, and serious health issue associated with broad morbidities and psychosocial impairments. There is a pressing need to identify effective therapeutics for BED. Naltrexone/bupropion (NB) targets brain reward systems, suggesting that it may be an effective treatment for both obesity and BED. This is the first human laboratory study to examine potential mechanisms underlying NB effects on eating behaviors in those with BED.MethodsFifty participants with BED (72% with obesity) completed a human laboratory study to examine effects of NB on their (1) ability to resist eating preferred high-caloric snack food, (2) calories consumed, and (3) food craving at baseline and following + 6 weeks and + 12 weeks of NB treatment while enrolled in a 12-week randomized clinical trial (RCT). Hunger, mood, and trough plasma levels of NB were evaluated as secondary outcomes.ResultsNB did not influence decisions to eat but did significantly reduce the number of calories consumed, which were negatively associated with 6beta-naltrexol and bupropion levels. NB also reduced craving for preferred high-caloric sweet snacks and reduced hunger ratings at + 6 but not + 12 weeks.ConclusionsThis human laboratory investigation support and extend the findings of the RCT. NB may reduce weight in patients with BED by reducing calories consumed during eating episodes of highly preferred high-caloric food. Further, results identified reduced craving and hunger ratings as potential markers of early treatment response.Keywords: Binge-eating disorder, Naltrexone/bupropion, Eating behavior, Mechanisms, Craving, Hunger

Introduction

Binge-eating disorder (BED) is a prevalent, refractory, and serious health problem associated with broad morbidities and psychosocial impairments (Udo and Grilo, 2018, Udo and Grilo, 2019). BED is defined by recurrent episodes of binge eating without inappropriate compensatory weight control methods that characterize bulimia nervosa. Binge eating is defined as eating an unusually large amount of food given the context, coupled with a subjective sense of loss of control, and associated with marked distress. There continues to be a pressing need to develop pharmacological treatments for BED (Grilo, 2024). As BED frequently co-occurs with obesity (Udo and Grilo, 2018, Kessler et al., 2013), a medication would preferably target both BED and obesity. Currently, the only USFDA-approved medication for BED (lisdexamfetamine dimesylate) (McElroy, 2017) has important contra-indications (e.g., history of substance misuse) and current labelling specifies a “limitation of use” (i.e., not recommended for weight reduction and safety for obesity is unknown).

Naltrexone and bupropion are FDA-approved medications for alcohol use disorder and tobacco use disorder, respectively, and their combination is FDA-approved for obesity (Yanovski and Yanovski, 2015). Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) demonstrated the effectiveness of NB for weight reduction in patients with obesity (Greenway et al., 2009, Greenway et al., 2010, Wadden et al., 2011) and trials have converged indicating that the combination is significantly superior to either bupropion or naltrexone alone (Liu et al., 2024). The putative mechanisms of action for the two medications combined seem relevant for binge eating in addition to weight reduction. The NB combination was developed to target alterations in the hypothalamic melanocortin system and the brain reward system (Billes et al., 2014), although the neurobiological mechanisms underlying its efficacy for weight reduction are not fully understood. Within the hypothalamus, bupropion stimulates hypothalamic pro-opiomelancortin (POMC) neurons whereas naltrexone blocks opioid receptor-mediated POMC autoinhibition, resulting in decreases in appetite and increases in energy expenditure as hypothesized downstream effects (Billes et al., 2014). However, the preponderance of evidence appears to support NB’s action on reward.

Dopamine and opioids are key neurotransmitters underlying the striatal mechanisms of the neurocircuitry of feeding (Joshi et al., 2022). Dopamine is released in response to food and is known to drive the motivation to eat and to reinforce behaviors associated with pleasurable, high-calorie, or palatable foods. It acts as a signaling mechanism for "wanting" or craving food, creating a cycle that encourages repeated consumption of food, like addictive behaviors (Volkow et al., 2011). Similarly, opioid receptors, particularly mu-opioid receptors in the nucleus accumbens, are activated by the consumption of palatable foods, enhancing reinforcement. Mu opioid receptors are specifically involved in reinforcement associated with highly palatable sweet foods, with stimulation of these receptors driving consumption while antagonists reduce it (Nummenmaa et al., 2018). Preclinical studies identify that bupropion and naltrexone work synergistically on the reward system to reduce food intake (Billes et al., 2014, Sinnayah et al., 2007). When combined, naltrexone and bupropion attenuate food reward by antagonizing opioid receptors (naltrexone) and modulating dopamine levels (bupropion). Naltrexone is an antagonist of the mu-opioid receptor, and both animal and human studies identify that naltrexone reduces sweet preference (Eikemo et al., 2016, Laaksonen et al., 2011).

Recent findings from RCTs have supported the potential effectiveness of NB combination for BED with co-occurring obesity (Grilo et al., 2022). NB significantly reduced binge eating in one trial (Grilo et al., 2022) and weight in all three trials (Grilo et al., 2022, Grilo et al., 2023a, Grilo et al., 2023b), which is notable given evidence suggesting attenuated weight reduction among individuals with BED (Forman et al., 2023). Although NB targets systems thought to be highly relevant for those with BED (Simon et al., 2016) and obesity (Jastreboff et al., 2013), very little work has examined potential mechanisms of NB in humans. Within clinical trial investigations, NB was associated with self-reported ability to control food intake as this was associated with weight reduction and mixed findings regarding NB effects on food cravings assessed monthly (Dalton et al., 2017). Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2014) found that NB attenuated activation in the hypothalamus and enhanced activation in areas involved in inhibitory control and memory. The effect of NB on food craving has yet to be assessed in response to actual food cues.

The current investigation aimed to evaluate potential mechanisms underlying NB’s effects on eating behaviors in patients with BED with and without obesity. We included participants with and without obesity to increase the generalizability of findings given that epidemiological studies indicate that roughly 45% of those with BED do not meet criteria for obesity (Udo and Grilo, 2018), and studies of medication effects on eating behavior in BED participants without obesity are lacking. We used a validated human laboratory paradigm, which we developed to examine cue-induced eating of highly palatable food (Udo et al., 2013) to evaluate NB’s effects on the ability to resist eating and subsequent eating of preferred high-caloric food. Thus, our laboratory paradigm models restraint versus disinhibition, which have long been thought to be key aspects of BED. In addition, we examined ratings of food craving, hunger, and positive and negative mood. With regards to food craving, we specifically evaluated craving for sweet and salty high-caloric food. We hypothesized that NB would increase the ability to resist eating, and reduce calories consumed, food craving (particularly for sweet food), and hunger ratings.

Methods

Participants

Participants enrolled in an RCT testing NB for BED (Grilo et al., 2023b; NCT03539900) were eligible to participate in this human laboratory study. Eligibility criteria for the RCT and for this laboratory study were identical: met criteria for BED as defined by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (Fifth Edition; APA 2013), were 18–70 years old, and had a body mass index (BMI) of ≥ 21.5 to ≤ 50.0 kg/m2. Minimal exclusion criteria comprised clinical issues that dictate the need for alternative treatment or represent contraindications to naltrexone/ bupropion: undergoing concurrent treatment for eating/weight disorders, taking contraindicated medications (e.g., opiates), having uncontrolled medical conditions or contraindications to naltrexone/bupropion (e.g., seizure history, bulimia nervosa history, cardiovascular disease, psychosis/bipolar disorder, systolic blood pressure >160 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure >100 mmHg, or heart rate >100 beats/min), and being pregnant or breastfeeding. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Yale Human Investigation Committee. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Of the 89 participants that completed the RCT, 50 participants participated in the human laboratory study. Of those that did not complete the human laboratory study, this was primarily due to COVID-related restrictions during the data collection period. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Overall, the participants were well-matched in baseline characteristics between placebo and medication conditions as well as between participants who participated vs not in the current human laboratory study (from the n = 89 RCT participants). Participants had a mean age of 44.7 (SD = 13.5) years, 72% were female, 82% attended/finished college, and 76% were White. Mean BMI was 35.1 kg/m2 (SD = 6.2) and 72% were categorized as meeting BMI criteria for obesity.

Design

Each participant completed two laboratory sessions to capture potentially different facets of medication mechanism. Specifically, laboratory sessions were scheduled at baseline (LAB 1) prior to the start of medication, at 6 weeks of medication treatment (LAB 2, to capture early medication effects once participants reach steady state medication by week 4), and at 12 weeks of medication (LAB 3, to capture end-of-treatment medication effects). Participants completed LAB 1 and LAB 3 (n = 31, placebo=15, NB=16) at the start of the study. In the latter part of the study, participants primarily completed LAB 2 and LAB 3 (n = 19, placebo=12, NB=7) to minimize delays in starting study medication given COVID-related restrictions on availability and scheduling of in-person laboratory testing. However, when scheduling and COVID-related restrictions permitted, some participants completed LAB 1 and LAB 3 in the latter part of the study. Baseline characteristics were similar between participants completing LABs 1 and 3 vs 2 and 3 (see Supplementary Table 1) and between those completing one or two laboratory sessions. Attrition rates were similar across schedules. See Supplemental Materials for additional details regarding COVID-related changes to the design.

Procedures

Intake

All participants provided written informed consent. Procedures were in accordance with the ethical standards of the Yale School of Medicine Human Investigation Committee.

Randomization and medication

Randomization to medication versus matched placebo is described in the parent RCT (Grilo et al., 2023b). Briefly, randomization occurred in blocks of two or four and was stratified for sex and obesity status. Naltrexone/bupropion comprised naltrexone sustained release (32 mg/d) combined with bupropion sustained release (360 mg/d). Participants randomized to naltrexone/bupropion started with an initial dose of 8/90 mg every morning with weekly dose escalation over 4 weeks to final dose of 16/180 mg twice daily (Greenway et al., 2010, Grilo et al., 2022). Placebo was given in two doses taken twice daily, matched in appearance and frequency.

Laboratory sessions

Baseline assessment period

Laboratory sessions began at 7:30 am at the Yale Center for Clinical Investigation, New Haven, Connecticut (see Fig. 1 for study timeline). Participants were instructed not to eat past 10 pm the night before the laboratory session. Baseline assessments of craving, mood, hunger, and medication levels were obtained. Participants received breakfast (150 calorie meal bar) to standardize time since last food consumption and were then food-deprived for the next 3 h. Based on our prior work, this length of food deprivation is sufficient to increase food craving (Udo et al., 2013). Participants were instructed to administer their dose of medication (or matching placebo) just prior to coming to the hospital to standardize the timing of peak plasma levels (~11am). From 8:30 am to 10:30 am, participants were able to relax, watch TV, or read.

Timeline of study procedures.

Presentation of high-caloric snack food

During the intake appointment, participants named their top three salty and sweet snack options for a total of six options (see Supplemental Materials for the assessment). During the laboratory sessions, participants were presented with a standardized amount of each of their six preferred high-caloric foods. The snacks were portioned to 750 calories of each (approximately 4–5 servings of each item). In reviewing prior studies investigating ad-lib food consumption in the laboratory, sufficient quantities as well as multiple food options need to be available to participants to facilitate binge eating behavior (Laessle, Schulz, 2009). Each participant received the same presentation and amount of food across their two laboratory sessions.

Modeling the ability to resist eating

Following the presentation of food at 11 am, participants were informed that for each minute that they could resist eating, they would receive monetary compensation. The instructions were minimal and focused on the reward for delaying eating. The amount of money earned over the 3 h started at $0.35 per minute and reduced by a penny every 5 min (See Supplemental Material for subject instructions and de-escalating schedule). Money was used as the alternative reinforcer to provide some incentive to resist eating and to enhance the likelihood that the effect of medication on the reinforcing value of food will be detected (Carroll, 1993, Higgins, 1997, Lappalainen and Epstein, 1990). When participants could no longer resist and decided to start eating, the latency to eat was recorded in minutes.

Modeling binge-eating behavior

Once participants decided to eat, they could eat as much of their preferred high-caloric snacks as they wished until the end of the 3-hour ad-lib period. The weight of the food was assessed at the start and end of each session, and calories consumed was calculated based on calories per gram.

Discharge

All participants were discharged from the laboratory sessions at 3:30 pm, 90 min after the completion of the laboratory procedures. This additional time ideally prevented participants from altering their eating behavior during the laboratory session (i.e., participants were not able to immediately leave the experimental setting to go and eat).

Timing of assessments

Measures of food craving, hunger, and mood were assessed throughout the laboratory procedure (see Fig. 1) to assess reactivity to the presentation of preferred high-caloric snacks (-15 min, +5 min), and to assess ad-libitum eating behaviors (+5 min, +15 min, +30 min, +60 min, +90 min, +120 min, +150 min, +180 min).

Measures

The primary measures were latency to initiate eating (i.e., ability to resist eating, in minutes), calories consumed, and food craving. To measure food craving, we asked participants to rate their craving “right now” on a 100-point visual analogue scale (1 =Not at all to 100 =Extremely), for each of their six high-caloric snack foods. We calculated two average scores for sweet and salty items. Additional measures included hunger (single 100-point VAS item “how hungry do you feel right now” 1 =Not at all to 100 =Extremely; and 9-item measure of positive and negative mood ratings (Russell, 1980).

Assays

Plasma trough medication levels were collected at the start of LAB 3 (12-weeks of NB treatment) to assess naltrexone, 6beta-naltrexol, and bupropion analytes. Analysis was performed with liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) with the following ranges of lower quantification appropriate to detect the doses administered; naltrexone (4 pg/mL), 6beta-naltrexol (4 pg/mL), and bupropion (2 ng/mL).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared across medication groups with effect sizes. Mixed-effects regression models were used to examine medication effects on the primary outcomes: latency to start eating, calories consumed, and food craving. Models for latency and calories consumed included medication, laboratory session, and random-subject effects, which allowed for inclusion of each subject’s first lab session regardless of its timing (i.e., pre-drug vs. early post-drug). For food craving, which was measured repeatedly within sessions, time was included as an additional factor in the model. For each model, all multi-way interactions were modeled. Separate models tested reactivity to the presentation of food (pre- vs post-food presentation), and during the self-administration period (+5 min, +15 min, +30 min, +60 min, +90 min, +120 min, +150 min, +180 min). In the above models, the correlation in the data (i.e., repeatedly measured observations within each subject) was modeled by including random-subject effects (latency, calories) and, for food craving, random-subject effects and considering different error structures across time. The best-fitting model was selected based on Schwarz’ Bayesian Criterion (BIC). The linear and quadratic effects of BMI were considered in all models as were baseline characteristics (e.g., race, education), but their inclusion did not alter effects appreciably and they were subsequently dropped for parsimony.

Secondary outcomes included mood ratings and hunger ratings (all repeated) and were assessed using the same model as above for food craving. Post-hoc analyses focused on placebo vs NB differences and interactions with time, with type 1 error capped at 0.05 experiment-wise error.

We also conducted exploratory analyses to examine potentials associations between medication levels, and primary outcomes by estimating Spearman correlations. Stratification variables of sex and obesity status were evaluated as covariates across analyses and were retained if they were significantly associated with each outcome. Given that 72% of participants met criteria for obesity, there was insufficient cell sizes/power to examine interactions with obesity status.

Results

Latency to consume food and calories consumed

There was no significant medication effect associated with latency (i.e., ability to resist eating) following the presentation of preferred high-caloric snacks. Participants resisted eating from 0 min to 180 min, with an average latency of 99.3 (7.3 SE) minutes among participants who could not resist and began eating (collapsed across LABs). At baseline, 67.7% chose to consume food, at + 6 weeks of treatment 52.6% chose to consume food, and at + 12 weeks of treatment 64.7% chose to consume food. Supplementary survival analysis examining differences by medication were not significant. In terms of calories consumed, there was a significant effect of medication (F(1, 15)= 13.9, p = .002; see Fig. 2) among those who chose to eat. Post-hoc analysis demonstrated that NB versus placebo reduced the number of calories consumed by 1143 (95% CI: −1899, −386) at + 6 weeks (t(15) = 3.22, p = 0.006) and by 541 (95% CI: −1043, −39) at + 12 weeks (t(15) = 2.29, p = 0.036) of treatment. Effect sizes for reductions in calories consumed were large (Cohen d = 2.61 at 6 weeks, and d = 1.01 at 12 weeks).Calculating the change from baseline, at + 6 weeks, NB reduced calories consumed by 68% vs 2% increase in the placebo group. At + 12 weeks, NB reduced calories consumed by 44% vs 22% in the placebo group. Reactivity to the presentation of preferred high-caloric snacks

The presentation of food resulted in pre- to post-presentation increases in craving for sweet snacks (time; F(1, 108)= 40.1, p < .0001), craving for salty snacks (time; F(1, 108)= 23.2, p < .0001), hunger ratings (time; F(1, 108)= 17.5, p < .0001), and negative mood ratings (time; F(1, 108)= 11.4, p < .0001). However, none of these observed increases differed by medication. There were no effects of time for positive mood ratings. Hunger ratings showed a significant medication x time interaction (F=5.51, p = .021), however, NB vs placebo comparisons were not significant (see Supplemental Figure 1). Group differences (medication vs placebo) with 95% CI’s and effect sizes for measures of reactivity at each lab and time point are shown in Supplemental Table 5.

Self-administration period

During the 3-hour self-administration period, there were significant time effects for craving for sweet snacks (F=8.28, p < .0001), craving for salty snacks (F=8.35, p < .0001), hunger ratings (F=5.03, p < .0001), positive mood ratings (F=2.35, p = .023), and negative mood ratings (F=4.87, p < .0001). All ratings had decreased by 180 min, except for positive mood which had increased. Only craving for sweet snacks and hunger ratings had significant interactions with medication. Specifically, a significant medication x length of treatment (LAB 1, 2, 3) x time interaction (F=1.77, p = .039; see Fig. 3) was observed for craving for sweet snacks. Following + 6 weeks of treatment, NB significantly reduced craving for sweet food (versus placebo) during the self-administration period. Hunger ratings also demonstrated a significant effect of medication x length of treatment x time interaction (F=2.08, p = .011; see Fig. 4) owing to significant reductions in hunger among NB following + 6 weeks of treatment. Discussion

This study was the first human laboratory investigation to examine the efficacy of NB medication combination for altering eating behaviors in patients with BED. While NB versus placebo did not impact decisions to eat preferred high-caloric food (i.e., did not increase the ability to resist eating), NB reduced significantly the number of calories consumed in participants who chose to eat, with large effects. With regard to mechanisms, we found that NB - relative to placebo - reduced craving for sweet foods and hunger ratings, supporting hypothesized mechanisms of NB. Overall, these results are highly consistent with and provide explanatory insights regarding the RCT findings (Grilo et al., 2023b) that—relative to placebo—NB did not significantly reduce the frequency of binge-eating episodes but significantly reduced weight in participants with BED (27.9% NB vs 6.5% placebo >=5% weight loss). Our laboratory findings suggest that NB may result in weight reduction in patients with BED, in part, by reducing the number of calories consumed during eating episodes of highly preferred, high-caloric food (i.e., binge eating episodes).These findings are consistent with the clinical hypothesis that combining NB with specific evidence-based psychological interventions for BED such as cognitive-behavioral therapy known to reduce binge eating but not weight (Grilo, 2024) might yield a synergistic effect (Grilo et al., 2023b).

For this investigation, we adapted a human laboratory model of eating behavior we had previously developed (Udo et al., 2013). In the current study, we demonstrated that presentation of preferred high-caloric food increased ratings of craving (for both sweet and salty snacks), hunger, and negative mood ratings. These findings identify that participants with BED were highly reactive to the presentation of food. However, ratings of positive mood did not change pre- to post-food presentation. At baseline prior to the start of medication, most participants (68%) chose to consume food and then consumed more than 1400 calories exceeding most accepted definitions of binge eating which involve ‘eating an unusually large amount of food’ (Forney et al., 2015). It should be noted, however, that the majority of participants in this study met criteria for obesity and some laboratory investigations of binge-eating behavior in individuals with obesity have reported much higher calorie consumption (Guss et al., 2002). Nonetheless, the paradigm was sensitive in detecting medication effects on calorie consumption.

Overall, medication treatment did not alter decisions to eat, and most participants chose to eat across all three laboratory sessions. Overall, the effect sizes of medication on calories consumed were large. Compared to baseline, participants in the NB group reduced their calorie consumption by 68% relative to a 2% increase in the placebo group following 6 weeks of medication treatment (between-subject comparison). Following 12 weeks of medication treatment, participants receiving NB continued to consume fewer calories than those receiving placebo, relative to baseline (44% reduction vs 22% reduction; within-subject comparison). The use of monetary reinforcement as an alternative reinforcement to food was effective in highlighting medication effects. The de-escalating schedule used during the laboratory session was designed to model how motivation to resist wanes over time. In other words, at some point the monetary reinforcement becomes so minimal that decisions to not eat become harder to rationalize. In other research with our ‘ability to resist’ models, we have demonstrated that participants are highly sensitive to reinforcement schedules (McKee et al., 2012). Our ‘ability to resist’ model can be conceptualized as a behavioral economic delay discounting task, in that decisions that fail to maximize outcomes represent reduced self-control and impulsivity (Weinsztok et al., 2021). While the task itself can be conceptualized as an indicator of self-control, it is important to note that we did not assess self-reported ‘loss of control’ during the laboratory sessions.

Importantly, we demonstrated that 6beta-nalrexol levels and bupropion levels were negatively associated with calories consumed, suggesting that both naltrexone and bupropion were influencing the number of calories consumed. These findings support both human and preclinical findings that naltrexone and bupropion work synergistically to reduce food intake (Liu et al., 2024, Sinnayah et al., 2007). With regard to mechanisms, we found that NB reduced craving ratings. While the combination of NB was developed to target alterations in brain reward systems (Billes et al., 2014), prior studies had found only modest support for NB reducing craving within the context of clinical trials (Dalton et al., 2017). Importantly, however, previous studies had not specifically evaluated the effect of NB on craving in response to the presentation of food, nor had they separately evaluated craving in response to sweet or salty food. In the current study, we specifically evaluated craving in response to preferred sweet or salty options, given naltrexone’s known effect on reducing sweet preference based on its antagonism of the mu-opioid receptor (Eikemo et al., 2016, Laaksonen et al., 2011). We found that participants receiving NB, relative to placebo, reduced their craving for their preferred sweet snacks but not their craving for salty snacks. This medication effect was demonstrated when participants had been at steady state medication levels for only two weeks (plus a prior 4 weeks of titration) but was no longer evident by the end of the medication period (at 12 weeks treatment). Sweet preference has been successfully used as a phenotypic marker, predicting the efficacy of naltrexone to reduce drinking behavior (Garbutt et al., 2016). However, it is unknown how naltrexone may change sweet preference over time as studies have typically examined sweet preference at baseline or after an acute dosing of naltrexone (Laaksonen et al., 2011, Yeomans and Gray, 1996). Nonetheless, given that the reduction of food craving is an early indicator of weight reduction success, the ability of NB to reduce craving for sweet substances following two weeks of steady state medication should be further evaluated as a potential phenotypic marker of this medication’s efficacy for weight reduction.

We also found some limited support for NB reducing hunger ratings. Similar to the craving findings, these results were observed following two weeks of steady state medication administration. These findings are consistent with preclinical investigations demonstrating reductions in food intake in food-deprived animals when NB has been directly injected into the ventral tegmental area (Sinnayah et al., 2007), and with neuroimaging studies showing that NB reduced activation in the hypothalamus (Wang et al., 2014). It is possible that the effect of NB on food regulation and reward may reduce the experience of hunger.

This study's findings should be considered within the context of methodological strengths and limitations. Study strengths include double-blinded pharmacotherapy delivered within the context of a rigorous RCT (Grilo et al., 2023b), limited exclusion criteria to increase generalizability, use of a validated laboratory paradigm, thorough and repeated assessment of key putative mechanisms to capture early (6-weeks) and later (12-weeks) medication effects, and evaluation of plasma trough medication levels. Several study limitations exist. The findings may not be generalizable to people with BED who seek different treatments or via different clinical settings (than an academic medical school research program). Given that 72% of our sample met criteria for obesity, there was insufficient power to examine obesity status as a between-subjects variable. Thus, findings may not generalize to those with BED without co-occurring obesity. Nonetheless, obesity status was controlled in analyses where appropriate. While we, importantly, assessed early and later medication effects, the reality of scheduling laboratory sessions during COVID necessitated that each participant completed two laboratory sessions. Thus, participants completed either LAB 1 and 3, or LAB 2 and 3, but no participant completed LABs 1 and 2 (i.e., all comparisons of LAB 1 vs 2 are between-subject comparisons). While all participants were well matched across the design (see Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1), and all analyses appropriately dealt with this complexity, this nonetheless remains a limitation. Finally, we examined eating behavior between 11 am and 2 pm and this might not be a typical binge time for some participants, possibly limiting generalizability to other times when binge-eating behaviors also occur.

In conclusion, our laboratory findings identify for the first time that NB treatment in participants with BED did not impact on decisions to eat, but robustly reduced calories consumed, and these reductions were associated with medication levels for both naltrexone and bupropion. Medication effects on mechanisms of craving and hunger were evident after 6 weeks (4 weeks of titration + 2 weeks of steady state), but not after 12 weeks of NB treatment suggesting that effects on mechanisms occur quickly but might not be sustained. Overall, these laboratory findings support and extend our RCT findings (Grilo et al., 2023b) that NB did not reduce binge eating frequency but did result in weight reduction. Our human laboratory findings suggest that NB may support weight reduction, in part by reducing the calories consumed of highly preferred high-caloric foods. In this study, NB was delivered without any additional behavioral or psychotherapeutic interventions, which might be needed to better address binge eating. We conclude by noting that while a previous distinct RCT failed to find significant interaction effects for combining NB with behavioral weight-loss therapy (Grilo et al., 2022), a synergistic effect might occur if combined with CBT for BED, a leading psychological treatment that reduces binge eating but not weight (Grilo, 2024).
