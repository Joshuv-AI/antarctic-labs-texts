Visualization of coronary small branch at ultra-high field 5T Noncontrast Dixon Whole-Heart Coronary MR Angiography: compared with 3T MRI
Authors: Runzhi Zhang, Chuangwei Wei, Yan Xu, Jiahuan Dai, Nan Zhang, Jiayi Liu, Lei Xu, Zhaoying Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDetection of the small coronary branches using 5T Dixon (5TDixon) sequence, analysis of visualization and performance comparison with 3T Dixon (3TDixon) sequence.Materials and methodsA prospective study was conducted involving 22 healthy adult volunteers (mean age, 25 years ± 3 [SD]; twelve male participants) who underwent navigator-gated three-dimensional MR angiography at both 5TDixon and 3TDixon. The visualization rates, image quality scores and visualized lengths of the small coronary branches were quantitatively compared between 5TDixon and 3TDixon sequences.ResultsThe 5TDixon sequence demonstrated superior performance to the 3TDixon sequence, yielding a significantly shorter scan time (10.01 ± 3.17 min vs. 13.71 ± 3.36 min, P = 0.002), a significantly higher SNRRCA−pro (76.08 ± 13.68 vs. 29.08 ± 6.63, P < 0.001) and a significantly higher CNRRCA−pro (72.25 ± 14.48 vs. 24.72 ± 5.72, P < 0.001).The visualization rates of the right anterior ventricular branch, acute marginal branch, and atrioventricular nodal artery were significant higher at 5TDixon compared with 3TDixon, whereas no statistically significant differences were observed for the conus branch, sinoatrial nodal artery and the anterior septal artery. Score of image quality of small coronary branches was higher for 5TDixon compared with 3TDixon (all P < 0.05). Background

Coronary MR angiography (CMRA) provides a noninvasive, radiation-free, and contrast-free method for coronary imaging. Unlike CT or contrast-enhanced MR angiography, CMRA offers high reproducibility and safety, particularly for patients with renal impairment, though unenhanced flow-dependent techniques face challenges in visualizing distal arteries with slow blood flow [1–3]. A previous study demonstrated that ultra-high field strengths, such as 5T, enhance the visibility of small intracranial arteries such as the lenticulostriate arteries [4]. Scholars have successfully used 5T MRI to visualize peripheral vasculatures such as foot vessels, highlighting the clear advantages of 5T MRI in revealing anatomical details and small vessels. However, there is limited research on the visualization of small coronary branches in CMRA. Recently, the Dixon water-fat separation sequence has gained increased use in CMRA at 3T imaging. Compared to 3TDixon, 5TDixon has been confirmed to offer higher image quality [5]. Dixon method generates four sets of images: water, fat, in-phase, and opposed-phase images. Previous CMRA studies have primarily focused on the water images [6–8].

The major coronary branches supply myocardial blood flow, and their occlusion may cause infarction. Small branches, including the sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodal arteries, are crucial for the cardiac conduction system, while others, such as the septal and acute marginal branches, supply the septum and right ventricle. Their considerable anatomical variations warrant careful evaluation [9, 10]. Understanding their course and location can help prevent accidental injury during cardiac surgery and coronary stent implantation. It also allows for a better understanding of the anatomical features of specific branches, such as the sinoatrial nodal artery (SNA), and their relationships with related diseases, which can assist in preoperative planning to avoid damage during surgery, preserving sinoatrial node function and preventing arrhythmias.

Therefore, this study explores the feasibility of visualizing small coronary branches using 5TDixon, comparing its advantage in the visualization of small branches and overall image quality to 3TDixon.

Materials and methods

Participants

This prospective study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional ethics committee for human research at the Beijing Anzhen Hospital (No. 2025232x). Written informed consent was acquired from all participants subsequent to enrollment. A cohort of 22 healthy young adult volunteers was imaged using three-dimensional magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) between January 2025 and August 2025. Each volunteer underwent scanning on both 5T and 3T platforms without prior nitroglycerin administration. For all participants, the two scanning sessions were completed within a two-week interval. To minimize potential image-quality bias related to acquisition order, heart-rate variation, and volunteer fatigue, the scan order was randomly assigned using a random-number table. Before imaging, a random integer between 0 and 9 was generated for each participant. Participants assigned an odd number underwent the 5T examination first, whereas those assigned an even number underwent the 3T examination first.

MR acquisitions

CMR examination was performed on a 3T scanner (Gyroscan Intera, Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands) with a 32-channel body coil and a 5T scanner (uMR Jupiter, United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China) using an 8-channel volume transmit coil in combination with 24-channel body and 48-channel spine receiver arrays. Table 1 presents the acquisition parameters for the 5TDixon and 3TDixon sequences. Following localizer scans, a high-temporal-resolution four-chamber cine sequence was used to determine the individual trigger delay and the optimal acquisition window, defined as the period of minimal right coronary artery motion. Respiratory motion was corrected using a Cross-Excitation navigator positioned over the hemidiaphragm opposite the heart. Both the 3TDixon and 5TDixon sequences were acquired in the axial orientation during free-breathing, with the total scan time dependent on navigator efficiency. For each examination, both the participant’s heart rate and the acquisition time were documented.

Imaging analysis

Two radiologists (R.Z. and C.W., each with 10 years of CMRA experience) with expertise in cardiovascular imaging independently assessed the subjective and objective image quality at a post-processing workstation (uOmnispace.MR, United Imaging Healthcare Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), unaware of both the acquisition technique and the field strength used.

The signal to noise ratio of blood (SNRblood) and contrast to noise ratio between blood and fat (CNRblood−fat) were measured on the original water-phase (W) images using the proximal segment of the right coronary artery (RCA-pro). A circular Regions of interest (ROI, ~ 5 mm²) was manually placed on the vessel lumen center to obtain mean intraluminal blood intensity (SIblood). On the same axial slice, a comparably sized ROI was placed within the pericoronary fat to estimate fat signal intensity (SIfat) and the standard deviation (SD) of the background noise (SDnoise). SNRblood and CNRblood−fat were calculated as SNR = SIblood / SDnoise and CNR = (SIblood – SIfat) / SDnoise, respectively.

We evaluated the small coronary branches in terms of image quality scores and visualization rates. The evaluated branches were those not included in the Society of Cardiovascular Computed Tomography coronary segmentation [11], primarily comprising the conus branch, SNA, right anterior ventricular branch, acute marginal, atrioventricular nodal artery (AVNA), and anterior septal artery. The assessment of small branches visualization was performed independently by the two radiologists(R.Z. and C.W.), with consensus reached in cases of disagreement. For the binary assessment of visualization rate, a small coronary branch was considered visualized when a linear or tubular vascular structure could be confidently identified from the expected origin or along the expected anatomical course of the target branch, with at least a proximal segment traceable on source images, multiplanar reformations, or curved planar reconstructions. Isolated high-signal foci or structures that could not be confidently distinguished from artifacts, adjacent venous signal, or myocardial signal were classified as not visualized. Accordingly, branches with image-quality scores of 1–3 were considered visualized, whereas a score of 0 indicated non-visualization.

Image quality of small coronary branches was scored based on established criteria [12, 13]. using a four-point scale: 0 = no visualization of the small coronary branch; 1 = entire course affected by severe artifacts with poorly defined trajectory, or only the proximal segment is visualized while the distal segment is not clearly depicted; 2 = partially visualized with artifacts; 3 = entire course clearly visualized without visible artifacts.

The lengths of the small branches were measured on the Vitrea post-processing. Select the target artery, adjust the lumen’s center point, generate a curved planar reconstruction of the artery, choose the starting and ending points of the vessel, and measure its length.

Consistency analysis was performed for image quality, SNR, CNR, and small branch length measurements. Intraobserver reproducibility was examined by a single reader (R.Z.), who analyzed each dataset twice with a two-week separation to mitigate potential bias. Interobserver reproducibility was assessed by a second reader (C.W.) through independent evaluation of the same CMRA images at the time of the first reader’s initial review.

Statistical analysis

The normality of all continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± SD, non-normally distributed data as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are shown as numbers and percentages. Comparisons between 5T and 3T for continuous variables were analyzed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Differences in the proportion of small coronary branches were evaluated using paired χ2 tests. Inter- and intraobserver agreements for qualitative (image quality scores) and quantitative (small branch length) measurements were assessed with intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Cohen κ value, respectively. For vessel-length analysis, only branches that were visualized on both 5T and 3T in the same participant were included in paired comparisons. For AVNA, only two paired observations were available for vessel-length analysis; therefore, the length difference was reported descriptively without inferential statistical testing.

Analyses were performed by IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0). P < 0.05 was considered as statistical significance.

Image quality and visualization of small coronary branches

The 5TDixon sequence demonstrated a significantly shorter scan time than 3TDixon sequence (10.01 ± 3.17 min vs. 13.71 ± 3.36 min, P = 0.002). Additionally, it yielded significantly superior SNRRCA−pro (76.08 ± 13.68 vs. 29.08 ± 6.63) and CNRRCA−pro (72.25 ± 14.48 vs. 24.72 ± 5.72) (both P < 0.001; Table 2).

In terms of subjective quality scores, the 5TDixon demonstrated significantly better overall image quality compared to 3TDixon (all P < 0.05), as summarized in Table 2 and illustrated in Fig. 1. Representative image comparisons for specific small coronary branches are shown in Fig. 2. The conus branch (A1-A2) was clearly visualized along its full course at 5T (score 3), whereas only the proximal segment was visible and the distal portion is not depicted at 3T (score 1). While the sinoatrial nodal artery (B1-B2) was fully visualized (score 3) by both sequences, with a longer visible vessel length observed at 5T. For the acute marginal branch (C1-C2) and the right anterior ventricular branch (D1-D2), 5T provided clear full-course visualization 5T (score 3), in contrast to 3T where partial artifacts compromised the lumen assessment (score 2). Furthermore, the atrioventricular nodal artery (E1-E2) was entirely visible at 5T (score 3) but undetectable at 3T (score 0), and the anterior septal artery (F1-F2) was clearly defined at 5T (score 3) but appeared blurred and poorly defined at 3T (score 1).

Discussion

This study evaluated the use of 5T MRI for visualizing coronary small branch arteries and compared its performance to 3T MRI. Our results demonstrate that 5TDixon provided superior visualization of small coronary branches compared with 3TDixon, with shorter scan times, higher image quality and longer vessel visualization lengths.

High-field strength and sequence in whole-heart CMRA

Whole-heart coronary MR angiography (CMRA) is conventionally performed at 1.5T or 3T. While increasing the field strength from 1.5T to 3T is known to improve SNR and CNR [14], ultrahigh-field MR systems such as 5T offer the potential for further gains in both SNR and spatial resolution. Although early high-field cardiac MRI faced challenges related to B₀ inhomogeneity and specific absorption rate (SAR), recent studies have demonstrated the clinical feasibility and superior image quality of 3T compared to 1.5T [15–17]. More recently, 5T time-of-flight MR angiography has shown improved visualization of distal intracranial and small branch arteries over 3T [4]. This finding lends further support to the ongoing migration toward higher field strength such as 5T, a trend that is primarily driven by the substantial gains in SNR these systems provide [18, 19].

Consistent with these observations, our study confirms that 5TDixon CMRA provides significantly higher SNRRCA−pro and CNRRCA−pro compared to 3T, in line with previous reports [5, 20]. It should be noted, however, that the SNR and CNR values reported in this study should be interpreted as apparent SNR and apparent CNR rather than absolute noise-corrected measurements. Both 3TDixon and 5TDixon acquisitions used parallel imaging, in which noise distribution may be spatially non-uniform because of coil sensitivity profiles and g-factor-related noise amplification. Therefore, the standard deviation measured from pericoronary fat may not fully represent the true spatially varying noise level. This limitation is particularly relevant when comparing two different imaging platforms with different receiver coil configurations and acceleration characteristics. The 5T system used 24-channel body and 48-channel spine receiver arrays, whereas the 3T system used a 32-channel body coil. Thus, part of the observed improvement in apparent SNR/CNR at 5T may be related not only to the higher field strength, but also to differences in coil architecture, parallel imaging performance, and noise texture between the two systems. Notably, these gains were achieved alongside a reduction in acquisition time—a combination that underscores a major practical advantage of 5T imaging. Furthermore, both 5TDixon and 3TDixon protocols in this study employed a small isotropic voxel size (1.2 × 1.2 × 1.2 mm3), indicating that 5T not only improves quantitative metrics but also supports high-resolution acquisitions more efficiently.

A key factor behind this performance is the intrinsic behavior of water–fat imaging at higher field strengths. At 3T, the Dixon -based CMRA is often limited by modest SNR and susceptibility to field inhomogeneity, which may compromise the visualization of small coronary branches. In contrast, the 5T MR system yields an approximate 1.67-fold increase in SNR and a larger chemical shift, which significantly improves water–fat separation and uniformity in fat suppression. The 5T sequence used here—QUICK 3D WFI (Water-Fat Imaging), generates in-phase, opposed-phase, water-only, and fat-only images in a single acquisition. This technique is relatively robust to magnetic field inhomogeneity and, when combined with the inherent benefits of 5T, delivers sharper vessel borders and more reliable coronary artery visualization. Thus, 5TDixon not only mitigate the limitations observed at 3T but also provide superior coronary small branch visualization.

Factors influencing coronary small branches visualization

The improved visualization of specific small branches—such as the right anterior ventricular branch, acute marginal branch, and atrioventricular nodal artery—with 5TDixon can be primarily attributed to the higher intrinsic SNR available at ultrahigh field strength. At equivalent spatial resolution, the increased SNR substantially enhances vessel conspicuity and enables longer visualized segment lengths, as reflected in the significantly superior image quality scores and quantitative length measurements obtained at 5T (all P < 0.05).

In contrast, no statistically significant differences between 5T and 3T were observed for the conus artery, sinoatrial nodal artery, and anterior septal branches. This may be related to two limitations of the sequence. First, the Dixon sequence employs a dual-echo acquisition, which separately capture water and fat signals, necessitating a longer repetition time to accommodate the extra echo. This extended repetition time leads to prolonged scan durations and an increased likelihood of motion artifacts [21]. This may be attributed to the conus artery and sinoatrial nodal artery’s proximity to the great arteries, making them more susceptible to motion artifacts. Another important technical factor was the omission of T2 preparation at 5T because of SAR constraints [22]. T2 preparation is commonly used in coronary MRA to improve blood–myocardium contrast by suppressing myocardial signal. Its omission at 5T may have resulted in less effective suppression of myocardial and venous blood signals, thereby reducing vessel conspicuity in some regions. This effect may be particularly relevant for branches with an intramyocardial course, such as the anterior septal branches, because their small caliber and close relationship with the myocardium make them more susceptible to reduced blood–myocardium contrast. Therefore, the lack of significant improvement in anterior septal branch visualization may partly reflect this technical limitation rather than the intrinsic capability of 5T imaging alone. In addition, the observed differences between 5T and 3T should not be attributed solely to magnetic field strength. The two protocols used different coil configurations, with an 8-channel transmit coil combined with 24-channel body and 48-channel spine receiver arrays at 5T, and a 32-channel body coil at 3T. These differences in receiver architecture, together with platform-specific acceleration and reconstruction characteristics, may have influenced noise distribution, apparent SNR/CNR, and vessel conspicuity. Therefore, the improved apparent SNR/CNR and visualization performance observed at 5T may reflect the combined effects of higher field strength, coil configuration, and sequence implementation.

Beyond technical factors, the intrinsic development of the coronary arteries also affects the visualization of small branches. There are known congenital variations in the origin and course of small coronary branches. Most literature reports that the SNA primarily originates from the RCA (50%–85%), followed by the left circumflex artery (10%–45%) [23–25]. In this study, the visualization rate of the SNA was approximately 40%, and only one case originated from the left circumflex coronary artery. Notably, this particular SNA was visualized only at 5T, suggesting improved detectability of variant anatomy at higher field strength. The conus artery includes the right conus branch arising from the proximal RCA and the left conus branch arising from the left anterior descending artery. In this study, the overall visualization rate of the conus artery was approximately 45%. Among them, only one case originated from left anterior descending artery, while the others originated from RCA. Besides, vessel diameter also influences detectability. The right ventricular anterior branch was mostly observed arising from the proximal to mid-RCA, with considerable variation in diameter and length. Small vessels such as the AVNA and SNA may not be visualized when their diameter is less than 1.2 mm, as the spatial resolution is insufficient to clearly depict them. In cases where small coronary branches were not visualized, several explanations are possible, including true anatomical absence, very small vessel caliber below the spatial resolution of the sequence, motion-related image degradation, or technical non-detection. Because this study did not include coronary CT angiography (CCTA) or invasive coronary angiography as a reference standard, these possibilities could not be reliably distinguished.

Clinical and diagnostic value of visualizing small coronary branches

The ability to reliably visualize small coronary branches, significantly enhanced by 5T CMRA, holds considerable clinical relevance. This is particularly critical for the sinoatrial nodal artery (SNA) and atrioventricular nodal artery (AVNA).

Visualization of the SNA is of significant clinical value [26, 27]. First, due to the high variability in the course of the SNA, it is essential for surgeons to identify its course prior to routine cardiac incisions and suturing. This allows for the protection of the SNA during surgery, reducing the risk of intraoperative injury and preventing postoperative complications such as arrhythmias [28]. In elderly patients with atherosclerosis, proximal coronary artery stenosis occurring before the origin of the SNA may readily compromise its blood supply. This may impair the physiological function of the sinoatrial node and result in various types of sinus node arrhythmias. The AVNA often arises from the coronary artery as it crosses the region of the atrioventricular junction, near the upper end of the coronary sinus or adjacent areas. The AVNA can be located by identifying the junction between the coronary sinus and coronary artery. The AVNA is located near the coronary sinus and the right atrial wall. The impedance heat generated during radiofrequency ablation can easily damage the AVNA, leading to atrioventricular conduction block. If the anatomical relationship between the AVNA and surrounding structures can be clearly visualized preoperatively, it provides valuable reference data for clinical decision-making [29]. The significantly improved detection rate of the AVNA with the 5T protocol demonstrated in this study directly enhances its potential for clinical decision-making. Therefore, in patients with arrhythmias, imaging evaluation should extend beyond the major coronary branches to include these critical small branches.

Beyond assessing specific small branches, 5T CMRA offers broader diagnostic value as a noninvasive modality. It avoids ionizing radiation and contrast administration, presenting a significant advantage for patients contraindicated for CCTA, such as those with severe iodine allergy, renal impairment, or active hyperthyroidism. While CCTA currently outperforms CMRA in overall diagnostic consistency [5], and the positive predictive value of 5T CMRA requires further improvement, its high negative predictive value is a key strength. For the mentioned patient populations, non-contrast 5T CMRA offers a viable and valuable alternative, enabling concurrent assessment of coronary artery morphology and stenosis.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, as a prospective study, the relatively small cohort of healthy volunteers may introduce variability due to congenital variations in small vessels and image quality, thus affecting the generalizability of the results. Second, SNR and CNR were estimated using an ROI-based method with the standard deviation of pericoronary fat as a noise surrogate. Because both protocols used parallel imaging, spatially non-uniform noise distribution may affect the accuracy of these measurements, particularly when comparing different coil configurations and acceleration characteristics between 3T and 5T. Therefore, the reported SNR and CNR values should be interpreted as apparent relative measures rather than absolute noise-corrected estimates. Third, the 3T and 5T protocols differed in several technical aspects, including coil configuration and the omission of T2 preparation at 5T due to SAR constraints. These factors may have contributed to differences in vessel conspicuity independently of field strength. Fourth, this study did not include a reference standard, such as CCTA or invasive coronary angiography. Thus, non-visualized branches could not be definitively attributed to true anatomical absence, very small vessel caliber, motion-related degradation, or technical non-detection. Further studies with reference-standard imaging are required to validate the diagnostic accuracy of 5T CMRA for coronary small branch visualization. Finally, the absence of patient data underscores the need for subsequent research to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of 5T CMRA in identifying calcification and stenosis within small coronary branches.

Conclusions

5T Non-contrast Dixon Whole-Heart Coronary MR Angiography provides an effective tool for visualizing small coronary branches and demonstrates superior performance compared with 3T.
