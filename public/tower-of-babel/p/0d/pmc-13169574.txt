Background

Propofol (2,6-diisopropylphenol) is a widely utilized intravenous anesthetic for procedural sedation during gastrointestinal endoscopy, valued for its rapid onset and short recovery time [1, 2]. It is generally considered safe for use in patients with epilepsy and is even employed in the management of status epilepticus [3, 4]. However, a small subset of patients, including those without any history of neurological disorders, may experience rare but clinically significant seizure-like phenomena (SLP) following its administration [5, 6]. These excitatory events—such as myoclonus, opisthotonos, and tonic-clonic movements—though occurring in less than 1% of cases, carry risks of physical injury, procedural interruption, and diagnostic uncertainty. The neurophysiological mechanisms underlying propofol-induced SLP remain incompletely understood but are thought to involve a complex interplay of GABAergic potentiation, glycinergic antagonism, subcortical disinhibition, and individual susceptibility. This paradoxical capacity to both suppress and provoke seizure-like activity highlights the need for safer sedative options in at-risk individuals [7]. Remimazolam, a recently developed ultra-short-acting benzodiazepine, offers a promising alternative, featuring rapid and predictable sedation, minimal cardiorespiratory effects, and the distinct advantage of reversibility with flumazenil. This report presents a case of recurrent SLP following propofol administration during endoscopic procedures and discusses the successful use of remimazolam as a safe and effective alternative in this clinical scenario.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old woman (height 160 cm, weight 52 kg, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I) was scheduled for gastroscopy due to “upper abdominal fullness and discomfort for 3 days.” The patient had no remarkable medical history, specifically denying any history of hypertension, diabetes, psychiatric disorders, or epilepsy. She had no known drug allergies and was not taking any regular medications. Preoperative laboratory tests, electrocardiogram, and chest X-ray were all within normal limits. Specifically, the patient denied any prior exposure to propofol or any history of undergoing general anesthesia for surgical procedures.

First endoscopic procedure (gastroscopy with propofol)

After fasting for 8 h, the patient received nasal oxygen inhalation at 3 L/min. Monitoring included electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure, and pulse oximetry. Baseline blood pressure was 110/68 mmHg, heart rate was 80 beats/min, and SpO₂ was 99%. An intravenous injection of propofol 100 mg was administered slowly. The patient complained of injection pain but tolerated it. About 30 s after administration, the eyelash reflex disappeared, and vital signs remained stable. Gastroscopy was performed and lasted approximately 4 min. During the procedure, the patient’s heart rate and blood pressure decreased, but not more than 20% from baseline. However, during emergence in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), the patient developed generalized tonic-clonic movements, limb twitching, and unresponsiveness, lasting approximately one minute before resolving spontaneously. Vital signs showed transient fluctuations, but post-ictal vital signs remained stable. A brief neurological assessment performed immediately after the event revealed no focal deficits. Given the clear temporal relationship to propofol administration and the absence of any history or signs of underlying neurological disorders, differential diagnoses such as epileptic seizures or psychogenic non-epileptic seizures were considered less likely. The event was initially attributed to an unknown cause, possibly emergence delirium.

Second endoscopic procedure (colonoscopy with propofol)

Several weeks later, the patient presented to an external hospital for a colonoscopy to investigate persistent abdominal symptoms. According to the available medical records, propofol was again selected as the sedative agent. Tragically, during the induction of anesthesia, the patient experienced a severe recurrence of SLP. The episode was markedly more pronounced than the first, featuring prominent opisthotonos and a prolonged duration. The event was successfully terminated with intravenous midazolam and supportive care. The planned colonoscopy was aborted, and the procedure was subsequently completed on a different date using a standard, unsedated technique.

Third endoscopic procedure (with remimazolam)

For the next required endoscopic procedure, the medical team opted to avoid propofol. After obtaining informed consent, remimazolam was selected as the alternative sedative agent. Several other sedative options were considered but ultimately excluded. Dexmedetomidine, while providing sedation without significant respiratory depression, was associated with potential hemodynamic instability (bradycardia and hypotension) and a slower onset and recovery profile, which may not be ideal for a brief ambulatory procedure like endoscopy. Midazolam alone was considered but would require higher doses to achieve adequate sedation depth, potentially leading to prolonged recovery time and increased risk of respiratory depression. Etomidate was excluded due to its well-documented adverse effect profile, including myoclonus and adrenal suppression. Given these considerations, remimazolam was selected as the most appropriate alternative.

The patient received an initial loading dose of remimazolam 5 mg, followed by supplemental doses of 2.5 mg as needed, along with fentanyl 50 µg for analgesia. The procedure was completed successfully without any complications. The patient maintained stable hemodynamics and respiration throughout. Notably, no SLP or other adverse neurological events occurred during the induction and recovery periods. The recovery time was slightly longer but characterized by good quality, without agitation or cardiorespiratory depression.

Discussion and conclusions

Mechanisms of propofol-induced seizure-like phenomena

The case presented offers a unique opportunity to explore the complex bidirectional effects of propofol on neuronal excitability. While primarily enhancing GABAergic inhibition, propofol demonstrates remarkable potential for triggering excitatory phenomena through several interconnected mechanisms:

The concentration-dynamic hypothesis and network instability

The temporal pattern of SLP in our case—occurring during emergence and induction—strongly supports the concentration-dynamic hypothesis. Systematic reviews indicate that approximately 40% of propofol-related SLP occur during emergence, with another 34% during induction [7]. This phenomenon represents a fundamental paradox: the same drug produces opposite effects depending on its pharmacokinetic phase.

Recent advances in neurophysiological monitoring provide compelling evidence for this hypothesis. Studies utilizing Single Pulse Electrical Stimulation (SPES) demonstrate that propofol produces a characteristic pattern of network modulation: while preserving fundamental network topology, it significantly reduces connection density and increases N1 peak latency from 22.0 ms to 26.4 ms (p < 0.001). This suggests that propofol “simplifies” brain networks by reducing functional connections while slowing information transfer. The rapid re-establishment of these connections during emergence likely occurs asynchronously, creating temporary network instability that facilitates aberrant synchronization [8].

The transition periods represent critical windows where the balance between inhibition and excitation becomes unstable. As propofol concentrations rapidly change, different neuronal populations recover at varying rates, potentially creating temporary imbalances in network inhibition that manifest as SLP.

Genetic susceptibility and calcium signaling pathways

Emerging evidence suggests individual genetic vulnerability may predispose to propofol-induced SLP. Whole-genome sequencing in similar cases has identified significant enrichment in calcium ion binding pathways, particularly involving genes MCTP2 and SHH. These findings illuminate a potential molecular mechanism: propofol-induced increases in intracellular calcium concentration ([Ca²⁺]i) may overwhelm the buffering capacity of genetically compromised calcium-binding proteins, leading to calcium dysregulation and neuronal hyperexcitability [9].

This genetic vulnerability may explain why SLP occurs in only a small subset of patients receiving propofol. The case’s normal baseline neurological status and unremarkable diagnostic workup further support the concept that these genetic variations may represent functional polymorphisms rather than pathological mutations, becoming clinically apparent only under pharmacological challenge.

Glycinergic disinhibition and motor manifestations

The dramatic opisthotonos observed in the second procedure provides crucial clinical evidence for propofol’s effect on glycinergic neurotransmission. Glycine serves as the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brainstem and spinal cord, particularly regulating motor neuron excitability. The clinical similarity between opisthotonos and strychnine (a glycine receptor antagonist) poisoning is striking and mechanistically informative [10].

Experimental models have demonstrated that propofol can function as a glycine receptor antagonist in certain contexts, potentially through allosteric modulation [11, 12]. This glycinergic antagonism, combined with simultaneous GABAergic potentiation, creates a unique neuropharmacological scenario: cortical inhibition coexisting with brainstem and spinal cord disinhibition. This regional dissociation may explain the characteristic motor phenomena while consciousness is impaired.

Remimazolam: a mechanistically rational alternative

Pharmacological advantages in SLP-prone patients

Remimazolam’s success in this challenging case stems from its distinct pharmacological profile. Unlike propofol’s complex multi-receptor interactions, remimazolam demonstrates selective affinity for GABA_A receptors containing α1-subunits, producing pure anxiolytic and sedative effects without the neuroexcitatory potential [13].

The drug’s metabolism via tissue esterases to an inactive metabolite (CNS7054) ensures rapid, predictable recovery independent of hepatic function. This contrasts with propofol’s hepatic metabolism, which shows greater interindividual variability. Notably, our patient maintained stable respiration and hemodynamics throughout the remimazolam-based procedure, consistent with its documented superior cardiorespiratory stability [14].

Intrinsic anticonvulsant properties

Perhaps most importantly, remimazolam possesses inherent anticonvulsant properties as a benzodiazepine derivative. This pharmacological class enhances GABAergic inhibition through a mechanism distinct from propofol, primarily by increasing the frequency of chloride channel opening rather than prolonging channel open time. This fundamental difference may explain the absence of paradoxical excitation while maintaining potent antiseizure activity [13, 15].

The flumazenil safety advantage

The availability of flumazenil as a specific antagonist provides an unparalleled safety advantage. In our case, knowledge of this reversal option significantly increased clinical confidence in using an alternative sedative in a patient with documented sensitivity to standard agents. This represents a crucial risk mitigation strategy unavailable with propofol.

Clinical implications and risk management

Recognition and prevention

This case underscores several critical clinical considerations. First, propofol-induced SLP should be recognized as a genuine pharmacological adverse effect rather than an “idiopathic reaction.” Second, the worsening severity upon re-exposure mandates that a history of propofol-induced SLP constitutes an absolute contraindication for subsequent use.

Strengths and limitations

This case report provides detailed documentation of recurrent propofol-induced SLP and demonstrates the safe use of remimazolam as an alternative. However, as a single case report, generalizability is limited. Complete records from the external hospital were unavailable, and genetic testing was not performed. Further studies with larger patient cohorts are needed to validate our observations.

Conclusion

This comprehensive case report and literature analysis demonstrate that patients experiencing emergence SLP following propofol should be considered at high risk for recurrence, and alternative sedative agents should be employed. Remimazolam, with its pure GABAergic mechanism, inherent anticonvulsant properties, predictable pharmacokinetics, and availability of a specific reversal agent, represents an excellent and safe alternative for procedural sedation in this patient population. Anesthesiologists and gastroenterologists should be aware of propofol-related SLP and consider patient-specific risk stratification when choosing a sedative regimen.

Patient perspective

The patient reported that the first two procedures were frightening experiences due to the unexpected seizures. She expressed relief that the third procedure was completed safely without any unusual events. She is grateful that her medical team identified a safe alternative and emphasized her willingness to share her experience to help other patients with similar reactions.
