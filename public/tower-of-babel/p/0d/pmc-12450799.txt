Epicardial, visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction
Authors: Michelle Lobeek, Lea P A Timmann, Dirk J van Veldhuisen, Just Dronkers, Riemer H J A Slart, Michiel Rienstra, Alain R Viddeleer, Thomas M Gorter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsObesity is common in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), and adipose tissue (AT) plays a key role in its pathophysiology. We examined the distribution and association of visceral AT (VAT), subcutaneous AT (SAT) and epicardial AT (EAT) in HFpEF using multimodality imaging.MethodsHeart failure patients with left ventricular ejection fraction >40% were prospectively enrolled. All patients underwent cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging and total body computed tomography (CT). EAT was measured on CMR, and VAT, SAT and waist circumference (WC) were measured on CT. Linear regression analysis was used to assess associations between EAT and other AT depots.ResultsIn total, 68 patients were included. Mean age was 73 ± 8 years; 34 (50%) were women. Twenty‐six patients (38%) were obese, while 60 (91%) had increased WC and 58 (85%) had increased VAT. Body mass index (BMI) and WC were comparable between men and women, but men had more EAT (217 vs. 180 mL, P < 0.001) and VAT (306 vs. 196 cm2, P < 0.001), but less SAT (177 vs. 276 cm2, P < 0.001), as compared with women. After adjusting for age, gender and BMI, VAT was only associated with EAT, albeit modest (β = 0.40, P < 0.001, R 2 = 0.40), but not with SAT.ConclusionsThe distribution of various AT depots is markedly different between men and women with HFpEF. Men have more VAT and EAT, but less SAT. The observed variation in AT depots may potentially contribute to gender‐related differences in cardiovascular risk.Keywords: AI‐assisted software, epicardial adipose tissue, HFpEF, multimodality imaging, obesity, subcutaneous adipose tissue, visceral adipose tissue

Introduction

Obesity is a global epidemic, and it is an important risk factor for heart failure (HF), especially HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). 1 , 2 While obesity is defined by the World Health Organization as abnormal or excessive adipose tissue (AT) accumulation that presents a risk to health, the single most used criterion for the presence of obesity remains an increased body mass index (BMI) above 30 kg/m2. However, this general measurement provides limited information on the actual amount and distribution of AT. 3 Numerous studies have suggested that information on AT distribution, in particular on visceral adiposity, importantly adds to the risk prediction for cardiovascular disease. 4 , 5

A recent surge in interest has emerged in epicardial AT (EAT), given its proximity to the heart, which may modulate cardiac function. 6 In patients already known with HFpEF, increased EAT is associated with, for example, higher cardiac filling pressures, left atrial (LA) mechanical dysfunction, worse exercise capacity and poor outcomes. 7 , 8 , 9 Subsequently, a number of potential mechanisms how EAT may influence cardiac function have been postulated, including an inflammatory imbalance due to pro‐inflammatory paracrine effects of AT, lipid infiltration in the myocardium and mechanical impairment due to pericardial constraint. 10

There are limited data on the distribution of individual AT depots in patients with HFpEF. In particular, few studies have included volumetric assessment of EAT, which can be assessed by CT or MRI, as these modalities provide detailed information on both amount and distribution. It remains unclear to what extent EAT is associated with other AT depots, such as visceral AT (VAT) and subcutaneous AT (SAT), despite the suggested major role of visceral adiposity in the pathophysiology of HFpEF. Hypothetically, EAT is merely a reflection of overall visceral adiposity and thus EAT volume may be directly associated with the amount of VAT rather than SAT. Moreover, the amount and distribution of the different AT depots may potentially vary significantly between men and women. The AT depots may also have different biological characteristics, with each depot having its own response to weight gain. 11 Therefore, each AT depot may contribute differently to cardiovascular disease and HF development, making it important to gain more insights into the amount and distribution of these depots. In this study, we used multimodality imaging to determine the amount and distribution of VAT, EAT and SAT in men and women with HFpEF.

Methods

Study population

This study included patients from the Ventricular Tachyarrhythmia Detection by Implantable Loop Recording in Patients with Heart Failure and Preserved Ejection Fraction (VIP‐HF) study, an investigator‐initiated, prospective, multicentre, observational study conducted between January 2015 and December 2019 in the Netherlands. 12 All patients had symptomatic HF with New York Heart Association class II or III, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) > 40% and N‐terminal pro‐hormone of brain natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) level > 125 ng/L. In addition, all patients had echocardiographic evidence of left ventricular (LV) diastolic dysfunction, LA dilatation and/or LV hypertrophy. The study complied with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and all patients provided written informed consent.

Patients underwent a thorough diagnostic work‐up, including medical history, physical examination, laboratory testing and echocardiography. In addition, the majority of patients also underwent cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging and a non‐contrast, non‐electrocardiogram (ECG)‐triggered computed tomography (CT), either as part of routine clinical care or, if not performed during clinical care, in the context of this study after obtaining informed consent. The MRI and the CT scans were not routinely acquired on the same day. For the present study, patients who did not have a CMR available or whose non‐contrast CT did not include the third lumbar vertebra in the field of view were excluded. Patients were also excluded from the present analysis when images were of too low quality to assess the AT depots.

CMR imaging

CMR was performed using a standard protocol for the acquisition of cardiac volume, function and mass. The used protocol has been described previously. 13 In brief, CMR acquisitions were performed on a 1.5 Tesla scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany & Philips, Amsterdam, the Netherlands). Typical sequence parameters for the two scanners were as follows: for Siemens, TE 1.08 ms; TR 700 ms; flip angle 40°; voxel size 2.3 × 2.3 × 6.0 mm; and for Philips: TE 0.93 ms; TR 2.1 ms; flip angle 25°; voxel size 1.6 × 1.8 × 8.0 mm.

ECG‐triggered cine loop images were obtained during breath hold at end‐expiration using a retrospectively gated cine steady‐state, free‐precession sequence. Approximately 15 short‐axis slices from base to apex were obtained, including the atria. Cine loop images were analysed off‐line by experienced observers using dedicated software (QMass 7.6 and 8.1, QStrain 2.0, Medis, Leiden, the Netherlands). 13

EAT was defined as the AT situated between the outer wall of the myocardium and the visceral layer of the pericardium. 14 , 15 EAT was measured using the cine short‐axis for contouring and the cine long‐axis stacks for referencing.

Short‐axis T1 mapping at three levels (base, mid and apex) was used to confirm AT, as described previously. 13 After confirmation, the EAT part on the cine short axis slices was extrapolated beyond the part that T1‐maps were available. EAT volumes were calculated by summation of EAT volume of each slice using the modified Simpson's rule. 16 The amount of EAT was expressed in millilitres.

Computed tomography

CT images were obtained using standard multi‐slice CT scanners and a clinical non‐contrast enhanced low‐dose CT protocol, with a slice thickness of 3 mm and a 512 × 512 matrix. Abdominal AT and muscle compartments were delineated semi‐automatically using artificial intelligence‐assisted analysis (SarcoMeas AI v1.00) at the level of the third lumbar vertebra (L3). 17 All delineations were manually checked and if necessary, adjusted by an experienced board certified radiologist (A. R. V.). Within these outlines, muscle and AT compartments were defined based on their specific differences in attenuation. The thresholds used were −29 to 150 Hounsfield units for skeletal muscle density, and −190 to −30 Hounsfield units for AT. 18 The measurements conducted by SarcoMeas AI v1.00 correspond with other commonly used analysis software. 19 Measurements included VAT and SAT in square centimetres and waist circumference (WC) in centimetres. For exploratory purposes, the amount of VAT and SAT were standardized by dividing the measured areas by the squared patient height (indexed VAT and SAT: VATi and SATi in cm2/m2). Figure 1 shows an example of the determination of CT‐based body metrics on an axial CT slice. Increased WC was defined as >102 cm for men and >88 cm for women. Increased VATi was defined as >66.5 cm2/m2 in men and >28.5 cm2/m2 in women, based on previous literature. 2 , 20 Currently, there are no well‐defined cut‐off values for increased SATi.

(A) Transversal CT image of one of the patients. (B) Abdominal AT and muscle compartments measured semi‐automatically using artificial intelligence‐assisted analysis (SarcoMeas AI v1.00). SAT is highlighted in blue, muscle tissue in red, intramuscular AT in green and VAT in yellow.

Statistical analysis

Continuous normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) while continuous non‐normally distributed data are presented as median [interquartile range, IQR]. Categorical data are presented as numbers (percentage). Differences between genders were analysed using the independent T‐test, the Mann–Whitney U test or χ 2 test, where appropriate. After checking assumptions, linear regression analysis was used to assess the associations between EAT and other AT depots. First, the association between VAT, SAT, WC and EAT was univariably tested (Model 1). Second, multivariable regression analyses were used to adjust for age, gender and BMI (Model 2). Third, to check if the associations of VAT, SAT and WC with EAT were modified by gender, Model 3 included gender * SAT, gender * VAT or gender * WC interaction terms. Tests were performed with SPSS version 28 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 113 patients participated in the VIP‐HF study, of which 68 were included in the present analysis. Of the 45 excluded patients, 8 patients did not undergo CMR, 34 did not undergo a non‐contrast CT including the third lumbar vertebra and 3 patients were excluded as no sufficient CMR images were available to assess the total EAT volume. SAT and WC data were not available for two patients due to a limited field of view.

The mean age was 73 ± 8 years, 34 (50%) were women, mean BMI was 29.3 ± 5.0 kg/m2 and 37 (54%) had atrial fibrillation. Mean EAT volume was 198 ± 66 mL, mean VAT was 251 ± 121 cm2 and mean SAT was 226 ± 109 cm2. In total, 26 (38%) patients were obese, while 60 (91%) had increased WC and 58 (85%) had increased VATi. Gender differences

No significant differences in patient characteristics were found between men and women, except for the prevalence of coronary artery disease (CAD) (56 vs. 18%, P < 0.001). Additionally, there were no significant differences in BMI and WC between men and women. On the contrary, men had a significant higher EAT volume (217 vs. 180 mL, P < 0.001) and VAT compared with women (306 vs. 196 cm2, P < 0.001) and a lower amount of SAT (177 vs. 276 cm2 P < 0.001) (Table 2, Figure 2).

Association between AT depots and EAT

The linear regression analysis is shown in Table 3. In the unadjusted model (Model 1), VAT was significantly associated with EAT (β coefficient 0.59, P < 0.001, R 2 0.35). There was no significant association between SAT and EAT (β coefficient 0.21, P = 0.086, R 2 0.05). After adjusting for age, gender and BMI (Model 2), only the association between VAT and EAT remained significant (β coefficient 0.40, P = 0.007, R 2 0.39). In an exploratory analysis using VATi and SATi, no differences were observed in the linear regression analysis compared with the non‐indexed values (Table S2).

Model 3 showed significant interactions in the association with EAT between WC and gender (P = 0.009) and SAT and gender (P = 0.024). The interaction between VAT and gender was not significant (P = 0.134).

The median interval between CMR and CT was 14 [3–47] days. In 14 patients, the time interval between CMR and CT was longer than 2 months. In these patients, no change in weight was noticed after 1 year of follow‐up (80.7 ± 12.8 vs. 81.1 ± 11.2, P = 0.70). A sensitivity analysis of the patients with an interval between CMR and CT shorter than 2 months (n = 54) had similar results (Table S3).

Discussion

Although less than half of the patients were obese based on their BMI, the vast majority had an increased WC and elevated amount of VAT. The distribution of various ATs was markedly different between men and women with HFpEF. While BMI and WC were comparable between men and women, men had significantly more EAT and VAT, but less SAT, as compared with women. VAT was associated with the amount of EAT after adjustment for age, gender and BMI, but this association was only modest. There was no significant association between EAT and SAT.

Biologically, the characteristics of EAT are clearly distinct from VAT and SAT, with different responses to obesity in terms of hypertrophy, hyperplasia, expression of proteins and inflammatory response. 11 Therefore, the different AT depots may have a different contribution to cardiovascular disease and HF development. The results of this study support the features that, despite overlapping characteristics, there is only a modest association between the various AT depots located throughout the body suggesting the EAT volume may also be explained by factors other than visceral adiposity in patients with HFpEF. Additionally, there are large differences between men and women with HFpEF. Given the toxic effects of EAT and also VAT, 21 this may be associated with more adverse cardiovascular effects in men.

To date, there are limited data available on the associations between the amounts of various AT depots including EAT, within the same cohort, especially in patients with HFpEF. The limited available data may be due to the different methods for optimal measuring of AT depots, namely, CT or CMR, which are often not readily accessible. Some data on the relation between EAT and VAT have been reported in other cardiovascular populations. In Japanese individuals with suspected CAD, EAT and VAT measured by CT were moderately correlated with each other. 22 , 23 Strong associations between echocardiographic‐derived EAT thickness and CMR‐derived VAT have been reported in individuals with clinical markers of metabolic syndrome as well as healthy individuals along a broad spectrum of BMI values. 24 , 25

Although echocardiography is the easiest and most readily available method of measuring EAT, EAT thickness measured by echocardiography is limited in estimating total EAT volume, possibly due to a non‐equally distribution of EAT around the heart. 26 , 27 Additionally, the use of echocardiography is limited by considerable interobserver variability. 17 A more precise method to measure EAT volume is by using CT or CMR. Similar to EAT, CT and CMR are considered to be accurate and precise imaging modalities for measuring both VAT and SAT. 28 , 29 In this study, we used both of these modalities to measure AT, namely CMR for EAT and CT for VAT and SAT. The available CT scans in this cohort were not optimal for measuring EAT because they were non‐ECG triggered.

Gender‐related differences

In literature, minimal data are available comparing the different AT depots, including EAT, and exploring the differences between men and women in patients with HFpEF. The present study shows a difference in EAT volume between men and women, but also well‐known gender differences in abdominal AT distribution, namely, that men have more VAT and women have more SAT, despite having similar BMI and WC. 30 It is well established that VAT increases cardiovascular risk while SAT is less associated with cardiovascular risk. 1 WC is thought to provide additional information for predicting morbidity and mortality 31 but includes both VAT and SAT. As different genders have different AT distributions and have different cardiovascular risks, it seems that collecting more data on AT distribution will help in selecting patients at risk for cardiovascular diseases. Additionally, the observed gender difference in LVEF may be partially explained by the higher prevalence of CAD and amyloidosis in men. This highlights the need for future studies to explore how gender‐specific differences in underlying cardiac pathology may interact with AT distribution and influence clinical outcomes in patients with HFpEF.

Limitations

First, it is important to acknowledge the relatively small sample size, which limits statistical power, the ability to adjust for confounders and the strength of conclusions. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Second, there was a variable interval between CMR and CT. Theoretically, the quantity of the AT depots can change over time. In this study cohort, 14 patients had a CMR–CT interval longer than 2 months. Since there were no differences in weight in the patients with a long CMR–CT interval, we decided to retain all selected patients included in the analysis and performed a sensitivity analysis that showed comparable results. Third, another limitation of this study is the absence of a control group, making it unclear whether the observed findings are specific to patients with HFpEF. Lastly, given the post‐hoc nature of this analysis, conclusions should be considered hypothesis generating.

Conclusions

The distribution of various AT depots is markedly different between men and women with HFpEF. While BMI and WC were similar between men and women, men had predominantly a higher amount of EAT and VAT, whereas women had primarily increased SAT. The observed variation in AT depots may potentially contribute to gender‐related differences in cardiovascular risk. The findings suggest that assessment of AT distribution may have clinical relevance, for example, in the context of risk stratification or individualized treatment. Future studies focused on obesity in patients with HFpEF should therefore take into account the amount and distribution of different AT depots.
