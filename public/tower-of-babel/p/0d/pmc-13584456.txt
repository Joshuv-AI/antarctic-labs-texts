Chromosomal scale genome assembly of medicinal plant Sophora tonkinensis
Authors: Ying Liang, Shanshan Chen, Zhu Qiao, Yang Lin, Guili Wei, Shuangshuang Qin, Fan Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sophora tonkinensis (shandougen) is a medicinal legume valued for its bioactive compound matrine, which has significant anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and anticancer properties. In this study, we assembled a high-quality chromosomal-scale genome of S. tonkinensis and integrated transcriptome analysis under various abiotic stress conditions to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying matrine biosynthesis. The genome of S. tonkinensis was sequenced to a size of 899 Mb with 95.9% completeness, revealing 37,930 protein-coding genes. Evolutionary analysis revealed two whole-genome duplication (WGD) events in S. tonkinensis. Transcriptomic profiling under drought stress treatments demonstrated that certain stress conditions upregulate key genes involved in the matrine biosynthesis pathway, suggesting that matrine accumulation may be modulated by environmental factors. Furthermore, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the cytochrome P450 (CYP450) gene family, identifying 226 StCYP450 genes.These genes form multiple gene clusters and primarily originated from WGD and single-gene duplication events. They are likely involved in matrine biosynthesis and stress responses. Our findings provide valuable insights into the genetic and transcriptional basis of stress-induced matrine biosynthesis and offer foundational resources for future functional genomics studies in S. tonkinensis and other medicinal legumes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13126-7.

Background

As global health awareness rises, natural medicines have garnered increasing attention in modern medical research due to their unique therapeutic effects and favorable safety profiles [1, 2]. Medicinal plants, with their extensive history of use, serve as a valuable resource for drug discovery. Fabaceae (legumes) is the third-largest family of flowering plants, with members distributed worldwide [3]. It encompasses approximately 650 genera and 18,000 species, including numerous plants with medicinal importance [4]. Sophora tonkinensis (2n = 18) (Sophora, Faboideae, Leguminosae), a medicinal legume within this family, is documented in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia for its roots and rhizomes [5]. S. tonkinensis is traditionally used to treat ailments such as sore throat, swollen gums, lung cough, jaundice, and nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Phytochemical studies have revealed that its roots contain bioactive compounds such as matrine and oxymatrine, which promote coronary blood flow, inhibit tumor cell proliferation, and enhance immune function [6, 7]. With continuous in-depth research into modern pharmacology and chemical natural products, various bioactive compounds contained in S. tonkinensis have gradually been discovered [8–10]. Notably, alkaloids and flavonoids exhibit remarkable pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory [9], anti-tumor [11], antiviral [12], antibacterial [13]. However, the genomic and molecular basis underlying the biosynthesis of these active compounds remains largely unexplored, warranting further investigation.

Recent advancements in whole-genome sequencing across various plant species have led to the identification of numerous genes associated with specific metabolic pathways and stress response in plant genomes [14, 15]. A comprehensive collection of genes related to plant secondary metabolism can serve as a valuable resource for engineering non-natural pathways and developing enzymes for the production of novel compounds in synthetic biology [16, 17]. Over the last decade, numerous chromosome scale draft genomes for various legume species have been published, including Glycine max [18], Medicago truncatula [19], Pongamia pinnata [20], Cajanus cajan [21], Trifolium pratense [22], Cicer reticulatum [23], Phaseolus vulgaris [24], Lupinus angustifolius [25], Pisum sativum [26] and Spatholobus suberectus [27] and mitochondrial genome of Sophora japonica [28]. However, there are few reports of Trib. Sophoreae Spreng., containing six genera of Leguminosae. Therefore, the publication of the S. tonkinensis genome fills the gap in the genome of the Sophora.

As one of the largest gene superfamilies in plant genomes, P450 enzymes are ubiquitous across all plant species and play crucial roles in various biochemical pathways. They are essential for multiple biological processes, including growth, development, and secondary metabolism, which encompasses phenylpropanoids, alkaloids, terpenoids, lipids, cyanogenic glycosides, and so on [29]. For instance, the CYP716 family is involved in the biosynthesis of triterpenoids in eudicots and tomatoes [30, 31]. Additionally, CYP728B70 catalyzes the oxidation of a methyl group to form the acid moiety of dehydroabietic acid during triptolide biosynthesis [32]. Furthermore, four CYP450s from M. dauricum play a crucial role in the biosynthesis of benzylisoquinoline alkaloids [33]. Moreover, this gene family is also implicated in stress responses. These stressors encompass abiotic factors such as drought, salinity, extreme temperatures, and heavy metals, as well as biotic factors including insect infestations and pathogen infections [34–36]. When plants experience drought stress, various physiological and biochemical responses mediated by the P450 gene family facilitate their adaptation to arid environments. For instance, the Arabidopsis CYP707A family has been shown to play a critical role in regulating abscisic acid (ABA) levels through the 8'-hydroxylation of (+)-ABA [37]. Abscisic acid induces stomatal closure and reduces water loss via evaporation, a mechanism essential for plant survival under drought conditions. CsCYT75B1 is crucial for the accumulation of antioxidant flavonoids that mitigate oxidative damage caused by drought stress. Furthermore, overexpression of CsCYT75B1 can enhance drought tolerance in Arabidopsis [38]. GmCYP82A3, which participates in jasmonic acid and ethylene signaling pathways, improves plant resistance to abiotic stresses [39]. Through regulatory pathways such as these, the P450 gene family promotes plant survival and adaptability to drought conditions. Investigating the functions of P450 genes deepens our understanding of the mechanisms underlying stress resistance in these medicinal plants and contributes to developing varieties with enhanced drought tolerance.

Recent advances in genomics and transcriptomics provide a valuable opportunity to elucidate these pathways, particularly through the analysis of key enzyme families, such as cytochrome P450s, that are involved in alkaloid biosynthesis. Studying these pathways could enhance the therapeutic potential of S. tonkinensis and support sustainable cultivation practices for its medicinal use.

However, legume genomes are often challenging to assemble due to high repetitive content and complex polyploid histories, limiting our understanding of the genetic basis for their pharmacologically active compounds. S. tonkinensis, a medicinal legume species widely used in traditional medicine, offers a valuable opportunity to explore the genomic foundation of bioactive compound synthesis. In this study, we report a high-quality draft genome of S. tonkinensis, assembled using HERA with PacBio sequencing reads, yielding a genome size of 900 Mb with a contig N50 of 0.99 Mb. BUSCO analysis confirmed 95.9% gene completeness, and 96% of the sequences were anchored to 9 pseudochromosomes through Hi-C scaffolding, achieving a final chromosome-scale assembly with a scaffold N50 of 104 Mb. Additionally, we focus on the cytochrome P450 (CYP) gene family, which plays a pivotal role in synthesizing secondary metabolites and in plant responses to environmental stresses. This study provides an essential genetic resource for this medicinal plant and lays the groundwork for future functional research into its valuable natural products.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

S. tonkinensis seeds used in this study were collected from a cultivated plant grown in Guangxi, China, with the landowner’s permission. Seeds were sown in pots filled with a 2:1 (w/w) mixture of soil and vermiculite, and grown in a laboratory under a 14 h light/10 h dark photoperiod with day/night temperatures of 26 °C and 20 °C, respectively. After germination, 30-day-old seedlings at similar developmental stages were selected for experimentation. The selected seedlings had an average height of about 8 cm and a stem diameter of roughly 2 mm. They were equally divided into three groups (60 seedlings per group) and subjected to different irrigation regimes for 10 days: a control group received water to maintain 75–80% available soil water (ASW); a mild drought group was kept at 55–60% ASW; and a severe drought group was maintained at 30–35% ASW. ASW levels were monitored and sustained by measuring water potential. At the end of the treatment period, roots were collected, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent sequencing analyses.

The extracted genomic DNA was randomly fragmented using an Ultrasonic Crusher, and specific fragment lengths were purified via electrophoresis. Next-generation sequencing libraries were prepared with different insert sizes (226 bp, 300 bp, 393 bp, and 513 bp). Adapter ligation and DNA cluster preparation were conducted, followed by sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform using the PE-100 protocol. A total of 220.54 Gb of raw data was generated, corresponding to approximately 219‑fold coverage of the estimated genome (estimated genome size 892 Mb based on k‑mer analysis, Figure S1). Raw S. tonkinensis data were generated with Illumina Sequencing technology. To ensure data quality, the raw reads were processed with the fq_filter_V1.5 tool (parameters: -q 33 -t 20 -ta 5 -tb 10 -tc 5 -td 10) to remove low-quality sequences. Filtering criteria included: ① reads with > 5% N or poly-A bases, ② reads with > 30 low-quality bases, ③ reads containing adapter contamination, ④ excessively short reads, and ⑤ duplicate paired-end reads. After filtering, 219.64 Gb of clean reads were error-corrected using the SOAPec_v2.0.1 package with default parameters.

In order to obtain high-quality assembly results, S. tonkinensis genomic DNA was used to construct 12 PacBio libraries with insert sizes ranging from 12,450 bp to 18,108 bp (Table S1) using the SMRT Template Prep Kit (Pacific Biosciences, USA). Sequencing was performed on a PacBio Sequel II platform following the manufacturer’s protocols. Reads with quality scores below 0.8 were discarded, yielding a total of 111.49 Gb of clean subreads with a mean read length of 52.64 kb, providing approximately 125 × coverage of the genome (based on the 892 Mb estimated genome size).

RNA extraction library construction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the roots, stems, leaves, and seeds of S. tonkinensis using the Hipure HP Plant RNA Mini Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China). For transcriptome analysis under drought stress, roots and leaves were harvested from the control, mild drought (55–60% ASW), and severe drought (30–35% ASW) treatment groups after 10 days of treatment as described above (see Plant materials and growth conditions section). Three biological replicates were collected per treatment. RNA quality and purity were assessed with an RNA 6000 Nano LabChip Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), ensuring an RNA integrity number (RIN) > 7.0. Poly(A)-mRNA was purified with the NEBNext® Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). mRNA was fragmented and used as a template for first-strand cDNA synthesis with reverse transcriptase, followed by second-strand synthesis using buffer and the Second Strand Synthesis Enzyme Mix. Double-stranded cDNA fragments underwent end-repair, adapter ligation, and selection through gel purification. The uracil in adapters was excised using the USER enzyme, and adapter-modified fragments were PCR-amplified to produce the final cDNA library. Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs) and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Raw RNA-seq reads were filtered and trimmed using Trimmomatic Version 0.39, and clean reads were aligned to the S. tonkinensis reference genome using HISAT2 Version 2.1.0.

Genome size estimation

Genome size estimation was performed using k-mer analysis with Jellyfish v.2.2.5 on Illumina error-corrected data. K-mers, or subsequences of length K, were extracted from raw reads, and their frequency distribution across sequencing depth was calculated. Genome size (G) was derived as G = K_num/Peak_depth, where K_num is the total k-mers, and Peak_depth is the k-mer depth. Data quality was assessed with FastQC [40], and heterozygosity was estimated using the ‘–m 17’ option in GenomeScope2.0 [41].

Genome assembly

The PacBio sequencing data was utilized for the de novo assembly of the S. tonkinensis genome through a commercial pipeline provided by the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology, Beijing. Initially, CANU v1.8 [42] was used to correct raw PacBio data and assemble contigs under default settings. Contigs were further refined using HERA with default parameters [43]. To minimize redundancy, minimap2 was employed to align contigs and next-generation sequencing (NGS) data, followed by Purge_haplotigs to remove duplicated sequences [44]. The assembly was then polished with Illumina data using NextPolish [45]. Finally, genome assembly completeness was evaluated using Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) with the embryophyte_odb10 [46].

Hi-C library construction and pseudomolecule clustering

Fresh leaves of S. tonkinensis (approximately 2 g) were crosslinked with 2% formaldehyde to stabilize chromatin interactions. The crosslinked chromatin was digested with DpnII, and the resulting sticky ends were biotinylated and ligated under dilute conditions to favor intramolecular ligation.

After reverse crosslinking and DNA purification, biotinylated ligation products were enriched using streptavidin beads. The purified DNA was then randomly fragmented using a Covaris ultrasonic breaker. After the end repair, the A tail was added, followed by the sequencing adapter before purification. The PCR amplification and other steps were completed to construct the entire library following the recommended protocols. After the library was qualified, the different libraries were pooled according to the requirements of effective concentration and target data volume for Illumina sequencing on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA). To ensure the quality of information analysis, the raw reads obtained by sequencing were filtered to obtain clean reads. The filtering criteria are as follows: ① all the reads pair with adapters were removed; ② the paired reads with the N content in the single-end sequencing read exceeding 10% of the length of the read were removed; ③ the paired reads with the number of low-quality (Q < = 5) bases contained in a single-ended sequencing read exceeding 50% of the length of the read were removed.

The draft assembly contigs were anchored into pseudo-chromosomes using a Hi-C proximity-guided assembly approach. Clean Hi-C reads were processed using the Juicer (version 1.5.6) and 3D-DNA (version 180,922) [47] Hi-C analysis and scaffolding pipelines. First, the Illumina Hi-C reads were aligned to the contigs using BWA-MEM (version 0.7.17-r1188) [48]. Contigs were ordered and oriented by the 3D-DNA pipeline with the parameter ‘–editor-repeat-coverage 3’, and the resulting Hi-C contact matrix was visualized in Juicebox [49], where misassemblies and misjoins were manually corrected based on interaction patterns. Finally, using the “finalize” function of 3D-DNA, the manually validated assembly was used to construct pseudomolecules ordered by size.

Genome annotation

To identify repetitive sequences in the S. tonkinensis genome, a combination of homology-based and de novo methods were used. RepeatMasker [50] was employed to identify repeat content against the Viridiplantae database from Repbase (Release 16.10; http://www.girinst.org/repbase/index.html), masking well-characterized transposable elements (TEs) under default settings. Additionally, RepeatProteinMask (http://www.repeatmasker.org/) was used for homologous repeat prediction. De novo detection of LTR retrotransposons was performed using LTRharvest (version 4.9.1) [51], LTR_FINDER_parallel(release 09/27/2019) [52], and LTR_retriever, while RepeatModeler (version 2.0) (http://www.repeatmasker.org/) generated a custom repeat library. Finally, Tandem repeats in the genome assembly were identified using TRF (version 4.07b) [53].

GlimmerHMM [54], SNAP [55], GenScan [54], and Augustus [56] were used for ab initio prediction of protein-coding genes with default settings. The protein sequence from Vigna unguiculata, Vigna angularis, Lupinus angustifolius, Glycine max and Vigna radidata were downloaded from Joint Genome Institute (JGI) phytozome database for homology-based prediction. We then aligned the protein sequences to the S. tonkinensis genome using TBLASTN v2.2.29 + [57] with a cutoff E-value of 1e-5. The genomic regions of each TBLASTN hit were further evaluated using GeneWise v2.4.1 [48] to predict gene models.

All RNA reads were aligned to the S. tonkinensis genome using TopHat [58], and transcripts were assembled with Cufflinks [59] using default parameters. The assembled transcripts were mapped back to the genome and analyzed with PASA v2.4.1 [60] to produce transcript-based gene models. These models were used to train AUGUSTUS v3.2.2 [56] via the ‘autoAugTrain.pl’ script. The trained AUGUSTUS was subsequently applied to predict protein-coding gene structures. Finally, the gene models were combined into weighted consensus gene structures with EvidenceModeler v1.1.1 [60], prioritizing homology > transcript > ab initio models, with redundant genes removed and repetitive elements masked.

The tRNAscan-SE tool [61] was used to predict tRNA in the genome sequence with an E-value set to 1e-5. Plant RNA sequences from Rfam database were selected as a reference to predict the rRNA by BLASTN with E-value set to 1e-5. The miRNA and snRNA genes were also predicted by BLASTN against the Rfam database with E-value set to e-1.

Gene functional annotation was performed by searching against public database using BLASTP. SwissProt (http://www.uniprot.org/), TrEMBL (http://www.uniprot.org/), KEGG (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/), and InterPro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) were used for screening the functional domains of the proteins. Gene Ontology (GO) terms for each gene were extracted from the corresponding InterPro entries.

Tandem repetitive element analysis

Tandem repeats were identified across the genome with Tandem Repeats Finder with default settings. According to their characteristics and redundancy, the repeat consensus sequences were first classified using tEsort with the REXdb database (http://repertexploreer-elixir.cerit-sc.cz). Multiple sequence alignment of the extracted repeats were conducted by MAFFT [62] and the phylogenetic tree constructed with IQTREE [63]. Itol (https://itol.embl.de/) was used to visualize and edit the tree.

Evolutionary analysis

To investigate the evolutionary history of S. tonkinensis and its relationship with other legume species, the protein sequences of Arabidopsis thaliana, Vigna unguiculata, L. albus, Vigna radiata, Medicago truncatula, Vigna angularis, Glycine max, Glycyrrhiza uralensis and Lupinus angustifolius were downloaded from JGI. For the protein-coding sequence (cds) of each species, we filtered the cds sequences encoding every gene and extracted the longest cds sequence for alignment. A full alignment protein search was performed using BLASTP with the parameter E-value = 1e-5 to verify the gene family clusters in these species and S. tonkinensis.

Ortholog clustering and gene family clustering analyses were performed using OrthoMCL [64]. Venn diagram format was drawn using a web tool (http://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html) [65]. An all-against-all BLASTP comparison with a cutoff E-value of 1e-5 was performed, and the results clustered into groups of homologous proteins using Markov chain clustering with the default inflation parameter. All 6506 single-copy orthologous genes identified in the gene family cluster analysis from the species mentioned above were used to construct a phylogenetic tree. Multiple sequence alignments were performed for each gene using MUSCLE v.3.7 (http://www.drive5.com/muscle) with default settings [66]. CAFÉ [67] was used to analyze gene family expansion/contraction along the branches of the phylogenic tree with default settings.

Differential expression analysis and functional enrichment

To identify DEGs (differential expression genes) between two different samples, the expression level of each transcript was calculated according to the transcripts per million reads (TPM) method. Essentially, differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 [68]. DEGs with |log2FC|≧1 and FDR < 0.05(DESeq2) or FDR < 0.001(DEGseq) were considered to be significantly different expressed genes. In addition, functional-enrichment analysis including GO and KEGG were performed to identify which DEGs were significantly enriched in GO terms and metabolic pathways at Bonferroni-corrected P-value < 0.05 compared with the whole-transcriptome background. GO functional enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis were carried out by Goatools and Python scipy software, respectively.

Ks analysis and WGD analysis

First, we identified homologous gene pairs within S. tonkinensis and between S. tonkinensis and other legume species using MCscan (Python version) (https://github.com/tanghaibao/jcvi/wiki/MCscan-(Python-version)). To ensure the accuracy of homologous gene identification, we utilized LAST [69] to detect homologs within the S. tonkinensis genome. The resulting gene pairs were further refined by filtering out tandem duplications and weak hits. A single-linkage clustering algorithm was then applied to the filtered gene pairs to group them into syntenic blocks.Next, we performed multiple sequence alignments of coding sequences (CDS) using MUSCLE (Edgar, 2004) based on the aligns file generated by MCscan. These alignments were used to calculate the synonymous substitution rate (Ks) values using the Nei and Gojobori method implemented in KaKs_calculator [70]. Given the varying evolutionary rates across species, KS values were corrected for more precise identification of WGD events and divergence times using ksrates [71]. First, ksrates was used to estimate the KS values of one-to-one orthologs and paralogs. Then, rate-adjustment was performed based on the phylogenetic tree with branch lengths equal to the KS distances estimated from the ortholog KS distributions.Finally, we classified genome-wide duplication events in S. tonkinensis into five categories using DupGen_Finder (v1.12) [72] with default parameters. These categories included tandem duplication, segmental duplication, whole-genome duplication, proximal duplication, and dispersed duplication.

Identification of CYP450 genes in S. tonkinensis genome

To identify potential CYP family proteins in S. tonkinensis, we first obtained 251 CYP family protein sequences from A. thaliana. Using these known CYP sequences, a hidden Markov model (HMM) was constructed with HMMER v3.0 to search against all protein-coding sequences of S. tonkinensis, detecting potential CYP sequences. Additionally, BLASTP (ncbi-blast-v2.10.1 +) was performed to align S. tonkinensis protein sequences against the CYP reference sequences with an E-value threshold of 1e-5, capturing additional potential CYP sequences. The results from both approaches were combined to generate a candidate set of CYP family proteins. To confirm CYP family membership, we annotated these candidate sequences using PfamScan (v1.6) with the Pfam-A database (v33.1), retaining sequences containing the PF00067 domain as definitive CYP sequences. The MAFFT was utilized for sequence alignment with default parameters. The IQTREE [73] was used to obtain a tree file. The iTOL v 4.3 was then used to construct and edit the phylogenetic tree [74].

Gene structure analysis, motif identification and chromosomal locations of StCYP450

The length and physical location of StCYP450s on chromosomes were obtained from the S. tonkinensis genome annotation file. Genes were mapped to chromosomes with TBtools. The intron and exon structures of StCYP450s were obtained using the Gene Structure Display Server 2.0 website (http://gsds.gao-lab.org/) with the genomic and cDNA sequences. The motif of StCYP450 protein was analyzed via the online MEME Suite (http://meme-suite.org/) with default parameters, and the number of motifs was set as twenty. CDD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi) was used to identify the conserved domains of the predicted StCYP450s protein sequences.

Results

S. tonkinensis genome sequencing, assembly and quality assessment

To obtain a high-quality chromosome-scale genome of S. tonkinensis, we employed a combination of PacBio long-read and Illumina short-read sequencing. A total of 111.49 Gb of PacBio Sequel II data (mean read length: 52.64 kb) and 220.54 Gb of paired-end Illumina data across multiple insert-size libraries (226 bp, 300 bp, 393 bp, and 513 bp) were generated (Table S1). After quality filtering, 219.64 Gb of clean Illumina data were retained, providing approximately 219-fold coverage of the estimated genome size. The k-mer analysis of the Illumina data estimated the genome size of S. tonkinensis to be approximately 892 Mb (Figure S1).

Using the PacBio long reads, we performed a de novo assembly with the HERA assembler, yielding an initial draft of ~ 1.68 Gb with a contig N50 of 299.03 kb. After removing redundant sequences and applying NextPolish for polishing with illumina data, the final genome size of S. tonkinensis was approximately 900 Mb with contig N50 length of 987 kb (Table 1). The N50 was higher than that of most other legume genomes, for example, chickpea (77 kb) [75], G. uralensis (109 kb), and pea (416 kb) [26]. Genome annotation

Repeat annotation

Analysis of the S. tonkinensis genome revealed a highly repetitive landscape, with 78% of the assembly consisting of repetitive sequences (Table S3),.with more than half of repeats (73% of the genome) matching known transposable elements (TEs). Among the classified TEs, Long Terminal Repeat (LTR) retrotransposons were the most abundant, accounting for 50.8% of the genome. DNA transposons, Long Interspersed Nuclear Elements (LINEs), and Short Interspersed Nuclear Elements (SINEs) represented smaller fractions at 8.31%, 2.1%, and 0.02%, respectively (Table S4). This high repetitive content aligns with observations in other legume genomes, such as pea (83%) [26], and is notably higher than that reported for white lupin [76], Cajanus cajan [21], and pigeonpea (Table S5).

Non-coding RNA annotation

We identified 6,591 non-coding RNA genes, representing ~ 0.1% of the genome, including 628 tRNAs, 126 rRNAs, 243 miRNAs, and 575 snRNAs (Table S6). Among the non‑coding RNAs, tRNA was the most abundant, followed by snRNA.

Prediction and characterization of protein-coding genes

Furthermore, based on a combination of ab initio, homology, and RNA-Seq, we predicted 37,930 protein-coding genes in the S. tonkinensis genome (Table S7). Complete orthologs for 88.2% of the embryophyte BUSCOs were identified, indicating that the predicted protein-coding genes are largely complete.

Analysis of gene duplication events revealed that 21,255 genes (56.0%) arose from duplication. These were classified into five categories: 8,605 whole-genome duplicates (WGD), 6,460 dispersed duplicates (DSD), 5,082 transposed duplicates (TRD), 2,256 tandem duplicates (TD), and 2,154 proximal duplicates (PD) (Figure S2). Functional annotation

To infer the biological roles of the predicted protein-coding genes, we performed functional annotation against major public databases. In total, 87.1% (33,027) of the genes received functional assignments from at least one database, providing a substantial resource for downstream analysis (Table S8). Specifically, 86.3% of genes had matches in the TrEMBL database, while 73.5% contained known protein domains as cataloged in InterPro. Furthermore, 72.1% and 55.5% of the genes could be mapped to KEGG biochemical pathways and assigned Gene Ontology (GO) terms, respectively, laying a foundation for explorations of metabolic pathways and biological processes in S. tonkinensis.

Phylogenetic analysis reveals evolutionary history and WGD events in S. tonkinensis

To elucidate the evolutionary position of S. tonkinensis, we performed comparative genomic analyses with nine other plant species, including eight legumes and Arabidopsis thaliana as an outgroup. Orthologous clustering analysis showed that the S. tonkinensis genome contained 6,506 single-copy orthologs, 8,604 multiple-copy orthologs, 2,714 unique paralogs, 6,732 other orthologs, and 6,019 unclustered genes. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that S. tonkinensis, L. albus, and L. angustifolius came from the same ancestor and diverged at 48.5 million years ago (Mya) (Fig. 3A). The lineage leading to S. tonkinensis and the lineage containing G. uralensis and M. truncatula split around 53.4 Mya (Fig. 3A). Gene family evolution analysis revealed significant dynamics in the S. tonkinensis lineage, with 2,324 gene families contracting and 914 expanding since its divergence from the common ancestor (Fig. 3A). We investigated the history of whole-genome duplication (WGD) by analyzing the distribution of synonymous substitution rates (Ks) for paralogous gene pairs in S. tonkinensis genome. The Ks distribution for S. tonkinensis paralogs displayed three distinct peaks at approximately 0.11, 0.42, and 1.99 (Fig. 3B). The most recent peak (Ks ~ 0.11) likely reflects bursts of LTR retrotransposon activity rather than a WGD. The two older peaks (Ks ~ 0.42 and ~ 1.99) strongly indicate two independent, ancient WGD events in the S. tonkinensis ancestry.

To temporally anchor these events, we compared Ks values from orthologous gene pairs between species. The estimated divergence Ks between S. tonkinensis and L. albus is ~ 0.39. Notably, the S. tonkinensis-specific WGD peak at Ks ~ 0.42 is older than this interspecific divergence value. This indicates that the more recent WGD event (Ks ~ 0.42) occurred approximately 52.2 Mya, prior to the divergence of S. tonkinensis from the Lupinus lineage but after its split from the G. uralensis and M. truncatula lineages (Fig. 3C). The oldest peak (Ks ~ 1.99) corresponds to a second, more ancient WGD event that occurred roughly 200 Mya. Synteny block analysis between S. tonkinensis and L. albus revealed a 3:2 syntenic relationship, suggesting a shared whole‑genome duplication event (Fig. 3D).

Dynamics of transposable elements and genome size evolution

The S. tonkinensis genome exhibits a higher repetitive content (78%) than several closely related legumes (Table S5), correlating with its relatively large assembled size (~ 900 Mb). To investigate the contribution of recent transposable element activity to this expansion, we estimated the insertion time of Long Terminal Repeat (LTR) retrotransposons. The analysis identified a predominant burst of LTR retrotransposon insertions centered at approximately 0.3 million years ago (Figure S6). The insertion time is significantly after the speciation of S. tonkinensis from its close relatives (e.g., divergence from L. albus at ~ 48.5 Mya), indicating that lineage-specific LTR amplification is a key driver of its comparatively larger genome size.

We further investigated the dynamics of specific TE lineages (Ty3-gypsy and Ty1-copia) by analyzing their reverse transcriptase (RT) domains. In total, 34 distinct LTR retrotransposon lineages were annotated. The Tekay, SIRE, and Ale lineages were among the most abundant, each comprising over 10,000 copies (Fig. 4A; Table S10). Phylogenetic analysis of RT sequences from high-copy-number lineages revealed distinct evolutionary patterns (Fig. 4B, C). For example, elements of the Angela lineage exhibited shallow phylogenetic divergence, consistent with a recent, rapid amplification. Genome-wide identification and characterization of the StCYP450 gene family

Identification, classification, and conserved features

Cytochrome P450 (P450) is a functionally diverse and multifamily class of enzymes which catalyze vast variety of biochemical reactions. They are involved in many biosynthetic pathways, including the biosynthesis of alkaloids, flavonoids, lignans, isoprenoids, phenolics, antioxidants, and phenylpropanoid [77, 78]. In plants, the P450 gene family contributes to metabolic reprogramming through chemical modifications, often in response to environmental challenges [78]. We identified 226 cytochrome P450 genes (StCYP1-226) in the S. tonkinensis genome (Table S11). These genes encode proteins with considerable diversity in physicochemical properties, including ORF length (221–1,085 aa), molecular weight (25.6–123.3 kDa), and isoelectric point (pI 4.95–9.54). Phylogenetic analysis with Arabidopsis P450s classified the StCYP450s into 40 distinct subfamilies (Fig. 5A). Conserved domain analysis confirmed the near-universal presence of the essential heme-binding motif (FxxGxRxCxG; 225 genes) and the PERF motif (216 genes). Genomic distribution and duplication history

The StCYP450 genes were unevenly distributed across all 9 chromosomes, with several regions harboring large gene clusters (Fig. 5C). This clustered arrangement, particularly evident for members of the CYP71 and CYP85 subfamilies, likely arose from tandem and segmental duplication events, suggesting a history of local expansion that may have facilitated functional diversification.

Tissue-specific and stress-responsive expression patterns

Transcriptomic analysis revealed tissue-specific expression patterns for StCYP450 genes (Fig. 5B). A subset of genes showed predominant expression in roots, aligning with the root-localized biosynthesis of quinolizidine alkaloids like matrine. Promoter analysis revealed an enrichment of cis-regulatory elements related to stress responsiveness (e.g., TATA-box and CAAT-box), indicating potential for transcriptional regulation under environmental challenges (Figure S8).

Drought-induced metabolic shifts and associated StCYP450 expression

Drought stress alters matrine accumulation and triggers a secondary metabolic response

Consistent with previous findings [6, 79], our drought treatments modulated the accumulation of the bioactive alkaloids matrine and oxymatrine in S. tonkinensis. Under moderate drought, root levels of these compounds increased significantly compared to control plants, whereas severe drought led to a marked decrease [79].

Transcriptomic analysis of roots and leaves under these stress conditions revealed widespread differential gene expression (Fig. 6A, B, C; Figure S9, S10). Identification of drought-responsive StCYP450 genes

Focusing on the cytochrome P450 family, we identified a distinct set of StCYP450 genes responsive to drought (Table S12). Under moderate stress, a limited number of StCYP450s were upregulated (5 in leaves, 2 in roots). In contrast, severe stress triggered a broad downregulation of StCYP450 expression, affecting 39 genes in leaves and 50 genes in roots. This divergent expression pattern highlights specific StCYP450 members as key candidates linking drought stress perception to the regulation of alkaloid biosynthesis.

Discussion

A high-quality chromosome-scale genome for the Sophoreae

The chromosome-scale genome assembly of S. tonkinensis (899 Mb, contig N50 = 987 kb, BUSCO = 95.9%) provides a high-quality reference for a medicinal legume that accumulates quinolizidine alkaloids. Like many legume genomes characterized by high repetitive content (e.g., pea, ~ 83% [26]; white lupin, ~ 60% [80]), the S. tonkinensis genome is also highly repetitive (78%). Our successful assembly, achieving high continuity and completeness, demonstrates the efficacy of integrating PacBio long-read sequencing with Hi-C scaffolding to overcome challenges posed by complex, repeat-rich plant genomes. This high-quality reference enables reliable gene annotation and downstream evolutionary analyses, filling a notable gap in genomic resources for the Sophoreae.

WGD and TE dynamics shaping genome evolution

Our phylogenomic analysis clarifies the evolutionary position of S. tonkinensis, showing its close relationship to Lupinus species and divergence from them approximately 48.5 Mya. The Ks distribution analysis reveals a complex evolutionary history marked by two ancient whole-genome duplication (WGD) events (at ~ 200 Mya and ~ 52 Mya). The more recent WGD event likely contributed to the early genomic repertoire of the Sophora-Lupinus clade.

Notably, while the genome size of S. tonkinensis (~ 900 Mb) is substantially larger than that of L. albus (~ 600 Mb) [80] and M. truncatula (~ 450 Mb) [19], this expansion is not explained by the shared ancient WGDs. Instead, a recent, lineage-specific burst of LTR retrotransposon insertions around 0.3 Mya (Figure S6) is the primary driver, a pattern also observed in pea [26] and some other legumes. This highlights how different mechanisms (ancient WGDs contributing functional novelty versus recent TE amplification inflating genome size) can operate on distinct timescales to shape genome architecture within a single lineage.

CYP450 gene family as a key resource for matrine biosynthesis

A key focus of our study was the cytochrome P450 (CYP450) gene family, crucial enzymes for plant chemical diversity. We identified 226 StCYP450 genes in S. tonkinensis, a number comparable to L. albus but larger than in M. truncatula [81]. These genes are often found in clusters on chromosomes, likely resulting from local gene duplication events that drive functional evolution. Importantly, many of these genes show root-specific expression patterns (Fig. 5B), consistent with the root-localized accumulation of medicinal alkaloids like matrine.

Strikingly, candidate biosynthetic genes for matrine, including lysine decarboxylase and several cytochrome P450s, form a putative gene cluster on a single chromosome. Metabolic gene clusters are known in other plants (e.g., benzoxazinoid in maize, avenacin in oat) and are thought to facilitate co‑regulation and substrate channeling [82].To our knowledge, this is the first report of a biosynthetic gene cluster for matrine-type alkaloids, suggesting that the pathway may be transcriptionally coordinated and evolutionarily optimized for high production in roots. Furthermore, genes derived from proximal duplication (PD) are strongly enriched in secondary metabolic pathways (Figure S3) and exhibit significantly higher Ka/Ks ratios than other duplication types (Fig. 2D), indicating positive selection for adaptive divergence of metabolic genes. This suggests that PD has contributed to adaptive divergence of metabolic genes in S. tonkinensis, a role previously reported for other plants [82]. This functional bias aligns with the chemical demands of matrine biosynthesis, which involves multiple oxidation steps likely catalyzed by cytochrome P450s. Together, these genomic features argue that the genetic basis for matrine production lies not only in a gene cluster but also in the targeted expansion and adaptive evolution of enzyme families directly relevant to pathway chemistry.

Drought stress differentially regulates alkaloid metabolism via StCYP450s

By integrating data on drought stress, alkaloid levels, and gene activity, we gained insights into how the plant responds to the environment. Under moderate drought (55–60% ASW), which increased alkaloid levels, a specific, small set of StCYP450s was activated, and genes involved in general "alkaloid biosynthesis" pathways were more active. In contrast, severe drought (30–35% ASW), which reduced alkaloid content, led to a broad decrease in activity for many StCYP450 genes. This biphasic expression pattern mirrors changes in matrine content, linking these candidates to stress-induced alkaloid accumulation. While stress-responsive CYP450s have been reported in M. truncatula [81], the set in S. tonkinensis appears more specifically tailored to alkaloid biosynthesis, suggesting that StCYP450s may serve dual functions: matrine diversification and drought adaptation [34, 37, 38].

Limitations and future directions

While this genome provides a valuable tool, the exact roles of the candidate StCYP450 genes require confirmation through functional experiments, such as heterologous expression or gene knockout/overexpression in S. tonkinensis systems. Furthermore, how these genes are transcriptionally regulated by drought (e.g., via specific transcription factors) needs further investigation. This genome will also enable valuable comparisons with other Sophora species to understand how medicinal traits evolve.

In summary, this study provides a high-quality genome for S. tonkinensis and uses it to explore the plant's evolution and the genetic basis of its medicinal properties. We describe its evolutionary history, characterize the important CYP450 gene family in detail, and identify specific genes that respond to drought and are likely involved in alkaloid metabolism. This work establishes a foundation for future research aimed at improving the understanding and sustainable use of the medicinal plant.

Conclusions

This study presents a high-quality, chromosome-scale genome assembly of Sophora tonkinensis. Phylogenomic and evolutionary analyses revealed that its substantial genome expansion was primarily driven by a recent, lineage-specific burst of LTR retrotransposons (~ 0.3 Mya), rather than by ancient whole-genome duplications. Systematic characterization identified 226 CYP450 genes, with specific members showing root-specific expression correlated with medicinal alkaloid accumulation. Furthermore, drought stress was found to differentially regulate a key subset of these StCYP450 genes, directly linking environmental cues to alkaloid biosynthesis. This genomic resource provides a foundation for understanding the evolution and medicinal trait formation in S. tonkinensis and related legumes.
