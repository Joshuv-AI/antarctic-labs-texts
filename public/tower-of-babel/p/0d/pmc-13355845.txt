Hollow MnO2 Nanozyme with NO Prodrug to Boost Synergistic CDT/PDT/Gas Therapy via Oxidative Stress Cascade
Authors: Ting Yin, Xuyi Liu, Jinyu Bai, Long Qiu, Junjian Hu, Zhilong Ma, Jianbo Sun, Yusen Wu, Lijing Fang, Daxiang Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePhotodynamic therapy (PDT) utilizes photosensitizers to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) to kill tumors. However, the tumor’s hypoxia and the limitations of a single ROS mechanism severely restrict its efficacy. This study aims to develop a synergistic nano-catalytic system (HMO) based on hollow manganese dioxide (HMnO2) loaded with a novel nitric oxide (NO) donor (Methylene Blue - NO, MB-NO), which overcomes these obstacles through multiple synergistic effects.MethodsPreliminary experiments confirmed the synthesis of HMO. Systematic studies were conducted on the chemodynamic therapy (CDT)/PDT/NO properties of HMO as well as its anti-tumor activity in vivo and in vitro. Finally, the in vivo safety of HMO was evaluated.ResultsPreparation by the HMO is 189 nm nano particle size, Zeta potential for −37 ± 1.4 mV, exhibit excellent stability. In vitro experiments showed that the cellular uptake of HMO was time-dependent. In terms of cytotoxicity, the cell survival rate of the HMO group was 65.9%, significantly lower than that of the free HMnO2 (89.1%) and MB-NO (85.1%); after 5 min of 660 nm laser irradiation, the cell survival rate of the HMO group further dropped to 48.5%. Introduction

Malignant tumors remain a major public health concern.1–3 Although conventional therapeutic modalities, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, are extensively employed in clinical settings, their efficacy is often compromised by drug resistance, systemic toxicity, and non-specific damage to healthy tissues.1,4–7 Photodynamic Therapy (PDT) has emerged as a non-invasive, precision treatment modality, demonstrating considerable potential for the clinical management of various solid tumors due to its spatiotemporal controllability, tumor selectivity, and minimal toxicity and side effects.8,9 PDT operates by activating photosensitizers localized within tumor tissues using specific wavelengths of light, leading to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and subsequent oxidative damage and apoptosis of tumor cells.10,11 Nevertheless, the clinical translation and therapeutic efficacy of PDT are hindered by several challenges, particularly the hypoxic tumor microenvironment (TME) and the reliance on a single ROS-mediated cytotoxic mechanism.12–14

Tumor hypoxia is a hallmark of solid malignant neoplasms.13 Rapid cellular proliferation and aberrant vascular architecture within these tumors create a pervasive and severe hypoxic microenvironment.15,16 This condition not only facilitates tumor invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance but also directly compromises the efficacy of PDT.17,18 The generation of ROS is critically dependent on oxygen availability. During PDT, the interaction between photosensitizers and oxygen further depletes intratumoral oxygen levels, exacerbating hypoxia and significantly diminishing ROS generation efficiency.19 Concurrently, elevated expression of hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α) activates multiple pro-survival signaling pathways, enhancing tumor cell resilience to ROS-induced damage and ultimately attenuating the anti-tumor effects of PDT.20–22 Therefore, alleviating tumor hypoxia to ensure adequate oxygen availability for PDT has emerged as a crucial focus in overcoming therapeutic limitations. Furthermore, conventional PDT predominantly relies on a single type of ROS to induce cytotoxic effects.23 However, tumor cells frequently counteract oxidative stress by upregulating antioxidant molecules such as glutathione (GSH), thereby establishing a natural defense barrier.24,25 This not only poses a challenge for a single ROS to breach this defense but also limits its specificity to a single target.26 Additionally, the short diffusion distance and brief half-life of ROS hinder their ability to comprehensively disrupt essential biological processes within tumor cells, allowing some cells to survive.19 Consequently, it is imperative to develop a combined therapeutic strategy that synergistically enhance oxidative damage and overcome the tumor antioxidant defenses, thereby improving the efficacy of PDT.

To address the aforementioned challenges, researchers have conducted extensive investigations, the strategy of combining nano-catalytic medicine and gas therapy has emerged as a novel approach for tumor treatment.27–30 Among various nano-catalytic platforms, nanoenzymes based on manganese dioxide (MnO2) have attracted much attention due to their inherent multi-enzyme mimicking activities (such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-like and GSH peroxidase-like activities), which enable the TME to responsively regulate hypoxia and redox homeostasis.31–34 However, most of the previously reported MnO2-based nanozyme systems only have single or dual therapeutic modes, and their therapeutic effects are often limited by insufficient enhancement of oxidative stress and the lack of a synergistic mode for further enhancing and maintaining oxidative damage. To overcome these limitations, we have combined Nitric Oxide (NO) gas therapy, Methylene Blue (MB)-mediated PDT with MnO2 nanozyme-mediated CDT. NO, a multifunctional gaseous signaling molecule, has attracted considerable in cancer therapy due to its capacity to induce apoptosis, inhibit angiogenesis, and sensitize tumors to other therapeutic modalities.35,36 NO not only directly triggers tumor cell apoptosis but also interacts with ROS to generate reactive nitrogen oxygen species (RNOS), such as peroxynitrite anion (ONOO−), which exhibit stronger oxidative damage potential than ROS alone.37,38 Furthermore, RNOS can bypass the antioxidant defense mechanisms of tumor cells, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, exacerbating oxidative stress, and ultimately promoting tumor cell death.39,40 Therefore, this study selected a dual-functional Methylene Blue - NO (MB-NO) molecule as the NO donor, which can not only be used as a photosensitizer for PDT, but also release NO, enabling each molecule to simultaneously possess the capabilities of treatment and NO supply, thus simplifying the system design. Secondly, MB-NO has a reduction-response characteristic, which is suitable for the situation where the reduction level is relatively high in the TME, enabling specific tumor-targeted release of MB and NO, while reducing the impact on non-target areas. Thirdly, under 660 nm laser irradiation, MB can simultaneously generate ROS, which helps to form RNOS in situ, maximizing the synergistic therapeutic effect. The rational design of this MB-NO ensures efficient and spatially controllable release of NO, which is crucial for achieving the expected cascade amplification therapeutic effect.

This study proposes an innovative anti-tumor nano-catalytic system that combines the catalytic oxygen- producing capabilities of hollow HMnO2 carriers with the controlled release property of reduction-responsive MB-NO, thereby establishing a cascade anti-tumor strategy (Scheme 1). In the acidic TME, HMnO2 catalyzes endogenous H2O2 to generate oxygen, mitigating tumor hypoxia and enhancing the ROS production associated with PDT.41,42 Concurrently, the liberated Mn2+ trigger a Fenton-like reaction, further catalyzing H2O2 to produce additional ROS, which synergize with PDT-generated ROS to amplify oxidative damage. The elevated reduction in the TME specifically activate MB-NO to release NO. Subsequently, NO reacts with ROS generated within the system to form RNOS, which exhibit enhanced cytotoxicity. This leads to mitochondrial dysfunction in tumor cells, disruption of their energy metabolism and redox balance, and ultimately induces apoptosis. By seamlessly integrating hypoxia amelioration, ROS enhancement, controlled NO release, and mitochondrial disruption, this system formulates a highly effective anti-tumor strategy leveraging the synergy of PDT, NO, and ROS. This research offers novel insights and methodologies to overcome the therapeutic limitations of conventional PDT.

Materials and Methods

Materials

For the experiments, a variety of chemicals and kits were procured from multiple suppliers. DCFH-DA, Hoechst 33,342, NO assay kit, DAF-FM DA, and MMP assay kit from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai), O56 and O58 probes from BestBio Biotechnology (Shanghai), PBS, penicillin-streptomycin, and trypsin solution from Solarbio Science & Technology (Beijing), RPMI-1640 medium from Servicebio Technology (Wuhan), CCK-8 from ZETA life, and Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining kit from KeyGEN BioTECH (Jiangsu).

Synthesis of HMnO2 and HMO

HMnO2 Synthesis: HMnO2 was synthesized according to the previous literature.43 In short, 2 mL TEOS, 2.5 mL ethanol, and 0.64 mL NH3·H2O were dropwise introduced into 10 mL deionized water, and the solution by mixing 3 days under medium speed. The resulting SiO2 nanoparticles were harvested via centrifugation, rinsed, and then dried thoroughly. Subsequently, 100 mg of potassium permanganate (KMnO4) was combined with 13 mg of the prepared SiO2 nanoparticles. Polyacrylamide hydrochloride (PAH) was added portionwise until the solution turned light yellow, and then mixed was stirred away from light for 24 h to form SiO2@MnO2 nanoparticles. Next, 40 mg of these nanoparticles was stirred in Na2CO3 solution at 80 °C for 12 h, then centrifuged and rinsed to obtain hollow HMnO2 nanoparticles with nanomembrane enzyme activity.

HMO Synthesis: MB-NO was added to HMnO2 under ultrasonication, followed by stirring of the mixed system away from light for 12 h. The obtained suspension was subsequently centrifuged at a rotational speed of 3500–4000 rpm for 5 min. The precipitate was rinsed with PBS to remove free MB-NO, and HMnO2@MB-NO (HMO) was obtained. The MB-NO of HMO was quantified by UV-Vis spectrophotometer. The encapsulated efficiency (EE) was calculated using the following formula: EE (%) = Weight of drug encapsulated / Total weight of drug used × 100%. The loaded efficiency (LE) was calculated using the following formula: DL (%) = Weight of drug loaded / Weight of drug and nanoparticals × 100%.

Characterization of HMO NPs

The morphology of HMO nanoparticles was examined using transmission electron microscopy (H-6009IV, Japan). Their hydrodynamic size and zeta potential were measured with a Malvern particle size analyzer (Nano-ZS90, UK). UV-visible absorption spectra were recorded using a Xiangli spectrophotometer (UH-5700, China) to identify characteristic absorption peaks. Oxygen release from HMO was measured using a Mettler-Toledo dissolved oxygen spectrometer (Switzerland).

Oxygen Production Capacity

To evaluate the oxygen production capability of HMnO2 in a tumor-like microenvironment, PBS at pH 5.5 was first deoxygenated by ultrasound for 1 h, then cover the surface of the solution with ethyl acetate. The initial oxygen levels in the solution were measured using a dissolved oxygen meter. Subsequently, H2O2 and HMO+H2O2 were added to the deoxygenated PBS, and the oxygen content was measured again. The results were recorded and plotted.

Detection of ROS, NO and RNOS in HMO Aqueous Solution

The ROS generation ability of HMO was analyzed using DCFH-DA. Firstly, 1 mM DCFH-DA was mixed with 10 mM NaOH in a volume ratio of 1:9, and the mixture was reacted in the dark for 30 min to obtain DCFH. The nanodelivery formulations (HMnO2 at 10 μg/mL, MB-NO at 200 μg/mL) were reacted with 1 mM H2O2 and 5 mM DTT for 24 h, and the light exposure group was exposed to light (660 nm, 0.1 W·cm−2, 5min). Then, different formulations were mixed with DCFH (10 μM) and the fluorescence spectra at 524 nm were obtained using a fluorescence spectrophotometer.

The Gress reagent was used as an indicator of NO to assess the NO release capacity of HMO. Firstly, the nanodelivery agents (HMnO2 at 10 μg/mL, MB-NO at 200 μg/mL) were reacted with 1 mM H2O2 and 5 mM DTT for 24 h. The light exposure group was exposed to light (660 nm, 0.1 W·cm−2, 5min). Subsequently, Gress I and Gress II were mixed in a 1:1 ratio and added to different samples. The fluorescence spectra at 538 nm were obtained using a fluorescence spectrophotometer.

The ONOO-specific probe was used as an indicator of RNOS to evaluate the RNOS release ability of HMO. Firstly, the nanodelivery system (with HMnO2 at 10 μg/mL and MB-NO at 200 μg/mL) was reacted with 1 mM H2O2 and 5 mM DTT for 24 h, and the light exposure group was exposed to light (660 nm, 0.1 W·cm−2, 5min). Subsequently, the ONOO− specific probe was added to different samples at a ratio of 1:100, and its fluorescence spectrum at 525 nm was obtained using a fluorescence spectrophotometer.

Cell Culture

4T1 cells, obtained from Zishan BioTECH. (Wuhan), were cultured in 90% RPMI-1640 medium supplemented, 10% FBS, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. They were incubated in an environment of 37°C and 5% CO2. Every two days for extend the using 1 mL of trypsin.

Evaluation of Intracellular Uptake

The cellular uptake of nanoparticles was assessed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and flow cytometry. HMO was labeled with the fluorescent dye Cy5.5 to enable tracking of its intracellular localization. For CLSM imaging, 4T1 cells were cultured overnight in confocal dishes, then co-incubated with HMnO2@Cy5.5 for various time periods (1, 2, 4, 7 h). Afterward, abandon to medium, rinsed, and imaged were acquired using CLSM. For flow cytometry, cells were cultured in 24-well plates, co-incubated with HMnO2@Cy5.5, washed, digested with trypsin, filtered, and finally analyzed on a flow cytometer.

Cytotoxicity Study

The toxicity of nanozyme toward tumor cells were assessed using CCK-8 assay. 4T1 cells were cultured in 96-well plates and co-incubated with various samples (HMnO2, MB-NO, and HMO) after overnight incubation, the equivalent concentration of HMnO2 was 4.4 µg/mL, and the equivalent concentration of MB-NO was 2.5 µM. Cells exposed to light were treated with nanozyme for 4 h, followed by 5 min of 660 nm laser irradiation. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing CCK-8 and incubated for 20 min. The absorbance at 450 nm was then measured with a microplate reader.

Live and Dead Cell Staining

In the Calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) staining assay, cells were incubated in media containing various experimental preparations, the equivalent concentration of HMnO2 was 4.4 µg/mL, and the equivalent concentration of MB-NO was 2.5 µM, followed by staining with Calcein-AM and PI kits. Subsequent to washing.

Flow Cytometric Detection of Apoptosis

4T1 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5×104 cells per well and treated with various agents, the equivalent concentration of HMnO2 was 4.4 µg/mL, and the equivalent concentration of MB-NO was 2.5 µM. After 4 h, the irradiation group was exposed to 660 nm laser (0.1 W/cm2) for 5 min, and then continued to be cultured until 24 h. Subsequently, collect the cells, stain them using the Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining kit, and analyze them.

Evaluation of Intracellular ROS, NO and RNOS In Vitro Levels

Intracellular levels of ROS, NO, and RNOS were labeled with specific fluorescent probes, observed via CLSM, and quantified by flow cytometry. In brief, 4T1 cells were inoculated into a four-compartment glass dish, with 5×104 cells in each compartment, then exposed to medium containing HMnO2, MB-NO, and HMO for 12 h. For the light exposure group, a 660 nm laser was applied to the cells at the 4th h after administration, with a power density of 0.1 W/cm2 and a duration of 5 min. Specific fluorescent probes DCFH-DA, DCF-AM, and O56 probe were used to stain the ROS, NO and ONOO− in the cells respectively, and this process was carried out for 30 min at 37 °C. Cells were then rinsed and stained with DAPI staining solution for the cell nuclei for a continuous 8 min. Images were acquired with CLSM. Similarly, cells were harvested and analyzed for flow cytometry following the same 12 h treatment protocol.

Assessment of Mitochondrial Damage and Mitochondrial Membrane Potential

The impact of nanomedicine on mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed via the JC-1 detection assay. Briefly, 4T1 cells were seeded into glass four-chamber dishes and maintained at 5×104 cells per dish. They were then exposed to different preparations in complete culture medium for 12 h. The light exposure group received 5 min of laser irradiation (660 nm, 0.1 W/cm2) at the 4 h. Afterward, JC-1 working solution was added to the cells and the cells were stained continuously at 37°C for 20 min, rinsed with provided buffer, and mitochondrial membrane potential was examined using CLSM.

For ATP level measurement, 4T1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and grown to 80% confluence before treatment. After 4 h, the light exposure group was irradiated with a 660 nm laser (0.1 W/cm2) for 5 min. After 12 more hours, the medium was discarded under ice-bath conditions, cells were rinsed with PBS, lysed, and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. ATP was measured using a luciferin-luciferase kit, and chemiluminescence at 560 nm was detected using a microplate luminescence analyzer.

Animal Tumor Model

To establish a murine xenograft tumor model, 4T1 cells were collected in PBS and then injected subcutaneously into the lateral abdominal region of 4–6 week-old female BALB/c mice, with 1.0×106 cell per mouse. The mice were obtained from Guangdong Zhiyuan Biopharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. All experimental procedures were in accordance with the veterinary guidelines of the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA). All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Medical University (GDMU-2025-000014).

In Vivo Biological Distribution Test

To investigate the biodistribution of HMO in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice, tumor volume was initially assessed. 4T1 tumor-bearing mice with 200–300 mm3 tumors were randomly split into two groups. Following tail intravenous administration of equal amounts of free Cy5.5 and Cy5.5 + HMO, fluorescence imaging was performed by the IVISspectrum imaging Software at 0, 0.5, 2, 6, 10, 24, and 36 h post-injection. After 36 h, the mice were euthanized using isoflurane anesthesia, tumors and major organs were excised, and fluorescence analysis was conducted to assess tissue distribution.

In Vivo Photoacoustic Imaging

This study aimed to investigate the oxygen-generating dynamics of HMO in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice. Once tumor volume of mice reached 200–300 mm3, the mice were randomly split into two groups. Following tail intravenous administration of HMO, photoacoustic (PA) imaging was performed in the range of 700–900nm using a PA imaging system at 0, 0.5, 2, 6, 10, and 24 h post-injection.

The Anti-Tumor Efficacy in Vivo and Its Mechanism

Once tumor volume of mice reached 100–200 mm3, all animals were randomly split into six groups (n = 5), namely PBS, HMnO2, MB-NO, MB-NO+L, HMO and HMO+L groups. The equivalent concentration of HMnO2 was 0.4 mg/kg, and the equivalent concentration of MB-NO was 1 mg/kg. Mice in each group were tail intravenously administered with the corresponding preparation at a dose volume of 100 μL per animal, and the tumor dimensions as well as body weight of the mice were recorded every other day over a total observation period of 16 days. The following formula was used to calculate the tumor volume: V = L × W2 / 2 (L refers to the shortest diameter of tumors, and W refers to the longest diameter of tumors). At the end of the study, the mice were euthanized using isoflurane anesthesia, tumors and major organs were collected and fixed for subsequent histopathological analysis, serum was separated via centrifugation afterwards, and the hepatic and renal function indices were assessed.

Statistical Analysis

The normality of the data distribution was confirmed by the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of the differences was verified by the Brown-Forsythe test. The pairwise comparisons between the two groups were conducted using the unpaired t-test, while the differences between the groups were evaluated through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by a post-hoc Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons. Data were shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with significance levels set at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, and “ns” for non-significant results. All analysis results were derived from Prism.

Results and Discussion

Preparation and Characterization of MB-NO

MB is a widely utilized and well-characterized small-molecule photosensitizer. In its oxidized form, MB exhibits significant mitochondrial localization within cells and can trigger endogenous apoptosis upon PDT. By modifying the secondary amine of its reduced form with a cleavable disulfide linker, a stable and non-phototoxic prodrug can be synthesized. To overcome the limitation of insufficient ROS generation in the hypoxic TME during PDT, a controllable NO donor, specifically a NONOate, was introduced. The terminal of this donor was modified with a dinitrophenyl group to respond to tumor-overexpressed reduction, enabling NO release. The released NO not only reacts with ROS to enhance cytotoxicity but also improves intracellular oxygen supply. Based on this design, MB-NO was developed to co-release MB and NO within the reduction-rich TME. One reduction molecule specifically cleaves the disulfide bond to liberate active MB, while another reduction molecule acts on the O-phenyl moiety to release two NO molecules. This dual-release mechanism enables synergistic photodynamic-gas therapy, as illustrated in Figure 1a.

Structure, characterization and chemical mechanism of MB-NO. (a) The structure and schematic diagram of the chemical mechanism of MB-NO releasing MB and NO under the action of reduction. (b) 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectrum of MB-NO (f1 denotes chemical shift). (c) HRMS spectrum of MB-NO.

To synthesize MB-NO, the following steps need to be followed (Materials S1, Supplementary Synthetic Procedures). MB was initially reduced using Na2S2O4. The resulting reduced MB derivative was subsequently transformed into the corresponding acyl chloride using triphosgene (BTC). This intermediate underwent condensation with HO-SS-Azide to afford MB-SS-Azide. The azide functional group in MB-SS-Azide was reduced to an amine group via the Staudinger reaction, yielding MB-SS-NH2. Concurrently, a NONOate derivative was synthesized according to established procedures and subsequently reacted with 2,4-dinitrofluorobenzene to form HO-NONO-Ph.44 The hydroxyl group of HO-NONO-Ph was activated to the corresponding acyl chloride using BTC, and subsequent condensation with MB-SS-NH2 resulted in the formation of the final conjugate, MB-NO (Figure 1a and Figures S1–11 and Table S1). The chemical structure of MB-NO was comprehensively characterized utilizing NMR spectroscopy and HRMS (Characterization Methods S1 and Figure 1b and c).

Preparation and Characterization of HMO NPs

The template-assisted etching strategy was employed to fabricate HMnO2 nanocarriers.43 Subsequently, a reduction-responsive MB-NO prodrug was loaded into these nanocarriers, resulting in a cascade-responsive nanodelivery system denoted as HMnO2@MB-NO (HMO). The synthesis pathway is illustrated in Scheme 1. Briefly, monodisperse silica (SiO2) nanoparticles were first synthesized via the hydrolysis of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS). These nanoparticles were then treated with KMnO4 and PAH to form a uniform mesoporous MnO2 shell on their surface, yielding SiO2@MnO2 core-shell nanoparticles. Next, sodium carbonate solution was employed to etch the SiO2@MnO2 nanoparticles, selectively removing the SiO2 core to obtain HMnO2 nanocarriers with hollow cavities and mesoporous shells. Finally, MB-NO was encapsulated within the hollow cavity of the HMnO2 nanocapsules, forming the HMO nanomedicine, which was subsequently utilized for further experimental investigations. The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images distinctly revealed that the synthesized HMO exhibited a uniform spherical morphology (Figure 2a). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis demonstrated that the average diameter of HMnO2 and HMO were 167.1 nm and 189 nm, respectively. The hydrodynamic diameters of the nanoparticles remained relatively unchanged, indicating successful drug encapsulation within the hollow cavity of HMnO2 (Figure 2b). Moreover, the hydrodynamic diameter of HMnO2 and HMO remained stable without any substantial change in H2O, PBS and RPMI-1640 for 7 days (Figure S12–13), which indicated its good stability. The encapsulation efficiency of HMO was approximately 70.79 ± 0.87%, and the drug loading efficiency of MB-NO reached 92.86 ± 0.98%, indicating that HMnO2 possesses a relatively high loading capacity. Zeta potential measurements were employed to monitor surface charge variations. The zeta potentials of HMnO2 and MB-NO were −54 ± 0.3 mV and −50 ± 0.9 mV, respectively, while that of HMO was measured at −37 ± 1.4 mV. This gradual change in Zeta potential further corroborated the successful synthesis of HMO nanoparticles (Figure 2c).

Dithiothreitol (DTT) exhibits similar to those of reduced GSH, and both compounds demonstrate comparable cleavage effects. However, DTT has been shown to cleave the MB-NO molecule more efficiently.45,46 Therefore, DTT was selected as the cleavage agent for subsequent solution-based experiments. To confirm the effective loading of MB-NO and its responsive decomposition properties, DTT was employed to simulate the elevated reduction levels characteristic of the TME. Detection was conducted using UV-Vis absorption spectroscopy and fluorescence spectroscopy. The UV-Vis spectrum analysis revealed that the characteristic absorption peak of methylene blue in the ultraviolet region was located at 660 nm (Figure 2d). In the absence of DTT, neither MB-NO nor HMO exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at 660 nm, indicating that the disulfide bond within the MB-NO prodrug remained intact, thereby preventing its decomposition into methylene blue molecules. Conversely, in the presence of DTT, both HMO and MB-NO displayed pronounced characteristic absorption peaks at 660 nm, indicating that DTT cleaved the MB-NO molecule and released methylene blue. Additionally, fluorescence spectra of MB-NO and HMO solutions were recorded without DTT (Figure 2e). No absorption peak was observed at 680 nm, further confirming that the MB-NO molecule did not decompose to release methylene blue. Upon the introduction of DTT, a distinct MB characteristic fluorescence signal was observed in the HMO solution at 680 nm. Collectively, these findings indicate that the disulfide bond in MB-NO can be selectively cleaved by the elevated concentration of reducing agents present in the TME. This cleavage facilitates the controlled release of MB and NO while maintaining excellent stability under normal physiological conditions.

The results of the oxygen generation capacity test (Figure 2f and S14) demonstrate that the dissolved oxygen concentration in the H2O2-only group remains largely unchanged. In contrast, within a simulated TME containing H2O2, HMO effectively catalyzes the decomposition of H2O2 to produce oxygen. The O2 concentration in the system increased by approximately 1.8 times, highlighting the superior catalase-like activity of the HMnO2 carrier, which can significantly mitigate the hypoxic conditions of the TME. Consequently, the responsive release characteristics of the HMO nanosystem facilitate precise drug activation specifically at the tumor site, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing exposure to normal tissues. This capability also establishes a foundation for subsequent 660 nm laser-mediated photodynamic therapy. Furthermore, the HMnO2 carrier possesses a high specific surface area and large pore volume, providing ample capacity for drug loading, while its MnO2 component can degrade in response to key features of the TME, such as mild acidity, elevated H2O2 levels, and high glutathione concentrations.

This study introduces additional mechanisms for microenvironmental regulation and drug release, utilizing TEM to assess the degradation behavior of HMO in buffers that mimic various physiological conditions (Figure 2g). Following a 24-h incubation in a physiological environment at pH 7.4, HMO retained its intact spherical hollow structure with no observable signs of degradation, confirming its stability. In contrast, under mildly acidic condition (pH 5.5), partial degradation of the HMO shell was observed, indicating that acidic environment can initiate HMO degradation. Notably, in a pH 5.5 environment supplemented with H2O2, the HMO structure was severely compromised, with collapse of the hollow architecture and disintegration of the shell. This degradation behavior unequivocally demonstrates that HMO is capable of specifically responding to endogenous stimuli characteristic of the TME, resulting in structural disintegration. Subsequently, under physiological-related GSH concentration conditions that simulate the tumor microenvironment, the cumulative release of MB-NO was monitored. As shown in Figure S15, its release kinetics exhibited a clear GSH-responsive characteristic. When 10 mM GSH was present, the release rate of the NO donor significantly accelerated. Approximately 52% of MB-NO was released within the first 2 h, and the cumulative release reached approximately 83% within 36 h. In the absence of GSH, the cumulative release of MB-NO within 36 h still remained at approximately 19%. This significant difference confirmed that the release of MB-NO was triggered by HMnO2 in the tumor microenvironment.

Consequently, the HMO nanosystem not only facilitates the accelerated release of encapsulated MB-NO but also promotes the decomposition of H2O2 during the degradation of HMnO2, resulting in oxygen generation. This effectively mitigates tumor hypoxia, depletes glutathione, and enhances oxidative stress damage. These findings establish a foundation for subsequent multi-pathway synergistic anti-tumor therapy. The experimental results collectively confirm the successful fabrication of the HMO nanosystem, which exhibits appropriate particle size distribution, robust physiological stability, responsiveness to TME degradation, and on-demand drug release. From a material design perspective, these features provide theoretical support for future in vitro and in vivo anti-tumor studies and introduces a novel conceptual framework for the development of multi-responsive nanosystems for synergistic tumor treatment.

The Characteristics of ROS, NO, and RNOS Generation

To investigate the therapeutic efficacy and underlying action mechanisms of the responsive NO release nanoplatform HMO, we systematically assessed its capabilities in generating NO, ROS, and RNOS, along with its free radical properties. Initially, to assess the fluorescence recovery capability of HMO under simulated TME conditions, we analyzed MB release using fluorescence spectroscopy (Figure 3a). In a simulated TME buffer containing H2O2 and DTT, the characteristic fluorescence intensity of MB at 680 nm exhibited a time-dependent increase as the reaction time was extended from 1 h to 24 h. This study not only confirmed the efficient release of MB-NO from HMO but also demonstrated that the released MB could gradually regain its fluorescence activity in a reducing environment. The NO release capability of HMO was further assessed using the Griess reagent method. The ultraviolet-visible absorption spectrum (Figure 3b) revealed that neither the MB-NO group nor the HMO group without DTT treatment exhibited the characteristic absorption peak associated with the Griess reaction, indicating that the MB-NO molecules remained structurally stable under physiological conditions. In contrast, following DTT treatment, both groups displayed a pronounced characteristic absorption peak at 538 nm, suggesting that the reductive environment effectively induced disulfide bond cleavage and subsequently triggered NO release. The released NO was then oxidized to nitrite (NO2−), which specifically reacted with the Griess reagent via a diazotization coupling reaction. Furthermore, using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrrolidine N-oxide (DMPO) as a spin trap, we examined the release of NO from MB-NO and HMO via electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy, both in the presence and absence of DTT treatment. The results indicated that only the MB-NO and HMO groups treated with DTT exhibited the characteristic seven-peak pattern of NO (Figure 3c). These findings confirm the specific responsiveness of HMO to the highly reducing TME, ensuring that therapeutic gas release is selectively activated at the tumor site.

In addition to its gas treatment effects, the HMO nanosystem is capable of simultaneously generating ROS via both photodynamic processes and Fenton-like reactions, thereby achieving a synergistic therapeutic effect by integration of photodynamic and chemodynamic therapies. The ROS generation capacity of DCFH was assessed using a ROS fluorescence probe. The fluorescence spectra of various treatment groups, as depicted in Figure 3d, revealed that both the HMnO2 group and the HMO group without DTT treatment exhibited high fluorescence intensity. This observation suggests that the HMnO2 nanocarrier alone can catalyze endogenous H2O2 in the TME via a Fenton-like reaction to produce ROS, thereby exerting a chemodynamic therapeutic effect. Upon DTT treatment, the fluorescence intensity of HMO was further significantly enhanced. This increase is likely attributable to the release of NO induced by DTT, which reacts with ROS to form more oxidizing RNOS. RNOS possess an oxidative toxicity approximately 1000 times greater than that of conventional ROS, thereby markedly enhancing the tumoricidal efficiency. Subsequently, we investigated the temporal profile of ROS generation by HMO (Figure 3e). The results indicated a progressive increase in DCFH fluorescence intensity from 1 h to 24 h, indicating time-dependent accumulation. In addition, we assessed the ROS generation capacity during the photodynamic process of the photosensitizer methylene blue (MB) under laser irradiation (Figure 3f). Upon exposure to a 660 nm laser (0.1 W/cm2 for 5 min), the DCFH fluorescence intensity in the HMO + laser group was markedly higher compared to the group without laser irradiation. This result confirms that 660 nm laser excitation effectively triggers the photodynamic reaction of the released MB, leading to substantial ROS production. This process amplifies the oxidative stress effect in conjunction with ROS generated by the Fenton-like reaction mediated by the HMnO2 carrier.

To characterize the ROS generated by the HMO-based nanoplatform, targeted radical identification was performed under simulated TME conditions using electron spin resonance (EPR) spectroscopy. With TEMP employed as a singlet oxygen (1O2) scavenger, the EPR spectra exhibited a distinct triplet peak with an intensity ratio of 1:1:1, confirming the successful generation of 1O2 in the presence of DTT and H2O2 (Figure 3g). For superoxide anion (·O2−) detection, EPR spectra obtained using DMPO as a scavenger displayed clearly distinguishable split peaks, confirming the production of ·O2− within the reaction system (Figure 3h). Meanwhile, when DMPO was employed as a hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenger, a typical quadruplet EPR signal with an intensity ratio of 1:2:2:1 was observed in the HMO(D) group, directly confirming the presence of ·OH in the generated ROS pool (Figure 3i). Furthermore, EPR analysis revealed that activation with 660 nm light increased the production of all three detected radicals (Figures S16–S18). To further verify the CDT effect of the HMO nanosystem, the concentration of hydrogen peroxide and the time-dependent generation of •OH were detected using MB. As shown in Figure S19, as the concentration of hydrogen peroxide increased, the absorption peak at 660 nm of MB gradually weakened, indicating an increase in the generation of •OH; similarly, as shown in Figure S20, the absorption peak at 660 nm of MB also continued to decrease over time, again indicating an increase in the generation of •OH. These results demonstrate that in the CDT mediated by HMO, the generation of •OH not only depends on the concentration of hydrogen peroxide but is also related to the time factor. In conclusion, these findings demonstrate that the developed nanoplatform is capable of simultaneously generating three highly cytotoxic ROS, namely ·OH, 1O2, and ·O2−, upon near-infrared light activation. This capability surpasses conventional PDT systems, which typically produce only a single ROS species, thereby offering a more potent cytotoxic effect against tumor cells. Moreover, the near-infrared light activation further enhances ROS generation, highlighting the significant potential of this nanoplatform as an intelligent and responsive delivery system for precise and controllable cancer therapy. Additionally, the O56 probe was utilized for the detection of RNOS.

The fluorescence spectra of the O56 probe across various treatment groups are illustrated in Figure 3j. Notably, the fluorescence intensity in the HMO group, subjected to both DTT and laser irradiation, was significantly elevated compared to other groups. This finding highlights the synergistic effect of the reduction response and photostimulation in effectively inducing RNOS generation, providing evidence for oxidative stress-mediated tumor cell cytotoxicity. Figure 3k presents the fluorescence spectra of the O56 probe in HMO following DTT treatment over time, revealing a gradual increase in fluorescence intensity with prolonged exposure. This observation indicates a time-dependent accumulation of RNOS, consistent with the trend observed in ROS generation. Figure 3L compares the fluorescence intensities of the O56 probe in HMO treated with both DTT and laser irradiation versus treatment without laser exposure. The laser-irradiated group exhibited significantly higher fluorescence intensity, further confirming the synergistic effect of the photodynamic action and NO release in enhancing the production of highly toxic RNOS.

Based on the aforementioned results, a comprehensive cascade response mechanism of the HMO nanosystem is proposed. In the presence of elevated concentrations of reduction within the TME, the cleavage of disulfide bonds triggers the release of MB and NO. Concurrently, the HMnO2 carrier mediates a Fenton-like reaction, generating endogenous ROS. Upon exposure to exogenous 660 nm laser irradiation, MB is further activated to produce photodynamic ROS. The released NO subsequently reacts with the in situ generated ROS to form highly cytotoxic RNOS. Through the synergistic integration of “endogenous chemical kinetics, exogenous photodynamic therapy, and gas therapy,” tumor cells are effectively eradicated. This response mechanism not only addresses critical limitations of traditional photodynamic therapy, such as hypoxia resistance, the constraints of single ROS-mediated cytotoxicity, and off-target drug leakage, but also substantially enhances therapeutic efficacy through multi-effect synergy, that amplifies oxidative stress. This approach offers a novel design paradigm for the development of precise, responsive, and collaborative tumor therapy nanoplatforms and provides a robust foundation for subsequent in vivo anti-tumor research.

In Vitro Cellular Uptake and Toxic Effects of HMO on 4T1 Cells

To evaluate the cellular uptake of the HMO nanosystem, the fluorescent dye Cy5.5 was employed to label the HMO nanoenzyme (Cy5.5 + HMO), which was then co-incubated with 4T1 tumor cells. Uptake was monitored at 1, 2, 4, and 7 h using laser confocal scanning microscope, and cell nuclei were visualized with DAPI staining. The results revealed that a faint red fluorescence of Cy5.5 was detectable after 1 h of incubation, with the fluorescence intensity progressively increasing at 2, 4, and 7 h and reaching its maximum at the final time point, indicating effective and time-dependent cellular uptake of HMO (Figure 4a). In addition, the fluorescence intensity of Cy5.5 + HMO at various time points was quantitatively analyzed via flow cytometry, further confirming the time-dependent uptake pattern. Relative to 0 h, the fluorescence peak shifted rightward after 1 h of incubation, attaining maximum intensity at 7 h, consistent with confocal microscopy observations (Figure 4b and Figure S21). Collectively, these experimental findings demonstrate that HMO is effectively internalized by cells, establishing a foundation for subsequent anti-cancer effects.

The cytotoxicity of HMO was further assessed utilizing the CCK-8 assay. The cell viability of HMnO2 remained consistently high, ranging from approximately 80% to 100%, across concentrations of 3.4 μg/mL to 12 μg/mL, indicating favorable biosafety (Figure S22). In contrast, the MB-NO group exhibited a concentration-dependent decline in cell viability, indicating that the cytotoxicity of MB-NO is influenced by its concentration. Notably, the cytotoxic effect of HMO surpassed that of MB-NO, implying that the integration of oxygen and ROS enhanced cell death. This finding highlights the superior cytotoxicity of HMO and supports the synergistic cytotoxic effect of HMnO2 and MB-NO. Such enhanced cytotoxicity may be attributed to the in situ generation of ROS via the nanozyme-mediated Fenton reaction within tumor cells, thereby facilitating chemodynamic therapy (CDT). Additionally, the catalytic decomposition of H2O2 by oxygen generated upon laser activation alleviates hypoxia in the TME. The synergistic amplification of oxidative stress damage, combined with NO-mediated gas therapy, constitutes a multifaceted strategy for tumor cell eradication.

Following irradiation with a 660 nm laser (0.1 W/cm2, 5 min), both the control group and HMnO2 group exhibited minimal effects, indicating that the laser parameters employed did not induce significant cytotoxicity. In contrast, the cell survival rate in the MB-NO group was reduced by 11% compared to the non-irradiated group, attributable to PDT effect of MB. Notably, the cell survival rate in the HMO group decreased from 66% in the absence of light to 48% post-irradiation, demonstrating a significantly enhanced cytotoxic effect relative to the MB-NO + L group. This enhancement highlights the potent triple synergistic effect of CDT, PDT, and gas therapy in promoting tumor cell eradication (Figure 4c). Meanwhile, we conducted the CCK8 experiment on normal breast epithelial HC11 cells. The results showed that the cell survival rates of all groups were lower than that of the 4T1 cells, verifying the in vitro biological safety of the nanomedicine (Figure S23).

To further elucidate the cytotoxic potential of HMO, the Annexin V-FITC/PI assay was employed, and apoptosis levels were quantified via flow cytometry. The results (Figure 4d and S24) revealed that in the absence of laser irradiation, the total apoptosis rate in the HMO group was 42.1%, substantially higher than those in the control group (4.53%), the HMnO2 group (12.31%), and the MB-NO group (25.5%). These results underscore the efficacy of the synergistic interaction between ROS generated by CDT and NO in effectively inducing tumor cells apoptosis. Following irradiation with the 660 nm laser, the apoptosis rate in the control group remained unchanged, further confirming the biosafety of the laser. In contrast, the apoptosis rates in the MB-NO + L and HMO + L groups increased. Notably, the apoptosis rate in the HMO + L group reached 55.7%, significantly higher than the 34.9% observed in the MB-NO + L group. This suggests that PDT combined with endogenous CDT and gas therapy can synergistically induce tumor cell death.

The therapeutic effects were further corroborated by live/dead cell staining results (Figure 4e). Calcein-AM (green) was used to label live cells, while propidium iodide (PI, red) labeled dead cells. In the absence of laser irradiation, all groups predominantly displayed green live cells, with only the HMO group showing a minor presence of red dead cells. However, post-laser irradiation, the HMO + L group exhibited a substantial increase in red dead cells and a significant reduction in green live cells. In contrast, the number of dead cells in the other experimental groups was significantly lower than that in the HMO + L group. These findings further corroborated that the triple synergistic treatment facilitated by the HMO nanosystem exhibited a highly effective cytotoxic effect on tumor cells. Collectively, these results demonstrated that the HMO nanodelivery system could be efficiently internalized by tumor cells. By activating CDT, NO gas therapy, and PDT within the TME, the system achieved robust tumor cell eradication. This therapeutic strategy not only addressed the limitations of traditional monotherapies, such as low efficacy and susceptibility to drug resistance, but also enabled precise and controllable therapeutic outcomes through the integration of endogenous responses and exogenous regulation. These findings provide a robust experimental foundation for its subsequent application in in vivo anti-tumor studies.

In Vitro Production of ROS, NO, and the Cascade Product RNOS

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which HMO nanosystems induce tumor cell apoptosis, we analyzed the intracellular levels of ROS, NO, and RNOS across various treatment groups. This analysis integrated laser confocal imaging and flow cytometry quantification. Concurrently, we assessed alterations in mitochondrial membrane potential and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) content. Intracellular ROS levels were detected using the non-fluorescent probe DCFH-DA. Upon cellular entry, DCFH-DA is hydrolyzed by lipoxygenase to form DCFH, which is subsequently oxidized by ROS to produce the strongly fluorescent DCF. Confocal imaging results revealed an absence of significant green fluorescence in the control group, whereas both the HMnO2 and HMO treatment groups exhibited pronounced green fluorescence aggregation. This observation indicates that the HMnO2-mediated Fenton reaction facilitates in situ ROS generation within cells. Notably, the fluorescence intensity in the HMO group was lower than that in the HMnO2 group. This discrepancy can be attributed to the release of NO by MB-NO in response to the TME, which reacts with some ROS, leading to a slight reduction in fluorescence intensity.

The impact of laser irradiation on the production of ROS by MB-NO and HMO was also evaluated. Following exposure to 660 nm laser irradiation, increased green fluorescence was observed in the groups treated with free MB-NO and HMO, suggesting that laser irradiation enhances ROS generation from the photosensitizer MB. Notably, the green fluorescence intensity in the HMO + L group was markedly higher than in all other groups, indicating that the combined CDT effect of HMnO2 and the PDT effect of MB synergistically generate substantial ROS. Concurrently, the oxygen catalyzed by HMnO2 mitigates intracellular hypoxia, further augmenting the ROS generation efficiency of PDT (Figure 5a). The quantitative results from flow cytometry DCF fluorescence analysis were in complete agreement with the trends observed in fluorescence imaging. The DCF fluorescence peak for the HMO + L group exhibited a significant rightward shift, corroborating that the intracellular ROS level reached its maximum (Figure 5b).

Intracellular NO production levels were assessed utilizing the DAF-FM probe, revealing distinct distribution patterns compared to ROS. In both the control and HMnO2 groups, specific fluorescence was nearly absent. The NO signal was most pronounced in the MB-NO group, whereas the fluorescence intensity slightly diminished in the MB-NO group subjected to illumination. In contrast, the HMO treatment group without illumination exhibited only faint NO fluorescence, and the fluorescence in the HMO + L group was further attenuated (Figure 5c). Quantitative analysis of DAF fluorescence via flow cytometry corroborated this trend. Relative to the control and HMnO2 groups, the MB-NO group demonstrated the most significant rightward shift in the fluorescence peak, followed by the MB-NO + L group. The fluorescence intensity in the remaining groups was comparatively lower (Figure 5d). These findings not only confirm that MB-NO effectively facilitates intracellular NO delivery and release, but also suggest a decreasing gradient of NO levels, implying that NO interacts with locally generated ROS to form RNOS. Utilizing the O56 probe to specifically detect highly toxic RNOS, the study demonstrated minimal red fluorescence in the control, HMnO2, and MB-NO groups. Conversely, the MB-NO + L group exhibited weak red fluorescence, while the HMO group displayed pronounced red fluorescence. Notably, the red fluorescence intensity was significantly enhanced in the HMO + L group (Figure 5e). Quantitative analysis of O56F fluorescence via flow cytometry corroborated these results, indicating that the fluorescence intensity in the HMO + L group was markedly higher compared to the other groups (Figure 5f). These observations elucidate the intracellular cascade process involving HMO, wherein HMnO2 generates ROS through a Fenton-like reaction, prompting MB-NO to release NO. The intracellular interaction of these species results in the formation of RNOS, which possess greater oxidative toxicity than conventional ROS. Furthermore, laser irradiation amplifies ROS production via the PDT effect, thereby enhancing RNOS generation. This mechanism underlies the potent cytotoxic efficacy of HMO.

Furthermore, alterations in mitochondrial membrane potential were assessed using JC-1 staining. The results indicated that cells in the control group exhibited prominent orange-red fluorescence, signifying normal mitochondrial membrane potential. In contrast, cells in the HMnO2 and MB-NO groups also displayed considerable orange-red fluorescence, suggesting that these single treatments exerted a relatively minor impact on mitochondrial function. However, in the HMO + L group, nearly all cells exhibited green fluorescence, indicating significant depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane potential and severe disruption of mitochondrial function (Figure 5g). Analysis of intracellular ATP content (Figure 5h) revealed that ATP levels were highest in the control group, with a slight decrease observed in the HMnO2, MB-NO, and MB-NO + L groups. In the HMO group, ATP levels further declined to approximately 50% of those observed in the control group, with a statistically significant difference compared to the other groups. This finding corroborates the hypothesis that RNOS can directly impair mitochondrial function and disrupt energy metabolism, thereby significantly inhibiting the energy metabolism of tumor cells and ultimately inducing apoptosis or necrosis. Collectively, these results suggest that the HMO nanosystem is capable of generating ROS in situ within the cytoplasm and controllably releasing NO. This synergistic action leads to the production of highly toxic RNOS, which directly targets and compromises mitochondrial function and energy metabolism. Consequently, this approach overcomes the limitations of traditional single-treatment modalities and enhances the efficacy of combined therapeutic strategies.

In Vitro Fluorescence Distribution and Photoacoustic Imaging

To assess the in vivo biodistribution and tumor-targeting efficiency of the HMO nanosystem, Cy5.5 was employed as a fluorescent probe to label HMO (Cy5.5 + HMO). This labeled compound was administered intravenously to mice bearing 4T1 tumors. Fluorescence imaging was conducted at various time intervals (0, 0.5, 2, 6, 10, 24, and 36 h), followed by semi-quantitative analysis at the tumor sites. As shown in Figures 6a, the fluorescence signal at the tumor sites of mice injected with Cy5.5 + HMO via the tail vein progressively increased from 0.5 to 10 h post-administration, peaking at 10 h. Notably, significant fluorescence retention at the tumor sites was observed up to 36 h. Furthermore, the fluorescence signal intensity at the tumor sites in the Cy5.5 + HMO group was markedly stronger than that in the free Cy5.5 group at all measured time points, as corroborated by quantitative analysis of the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) at the tumor sites (Figure 6d). Following 36 h post-administration, the mice were humanely sacrificed, and major organs along with tumor tissues were harvested for ex vivo fluorescence imaging. The results demonstrated that the fluorescence intensity at the tumor site in the Cy5.5 + HMO group was significantly greater than that in the free Cy5.5 group. Furthermore, the fluorescence intensities in normal tissues, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were comparatively low (Figure 6b and e). These findings suggest that HMO exhibits superior accumulation capability at the tumor site while maintaining favorable in vivo safety in normal tissues.

Based on the intrinsic Fenton-like catalytic oxygen production activity of HMO, we conducted dynamic monitoring of blood oxygen level fluctuations within the tumor region of mice via photoacoustic imaging. In Vivo Antitumor Effect of HMO NPs

In this study, the in vivo anti-cancer efficacy of the HMO nano-delivery system under 660 nm laser irradiation was evaluated in mice bearing 4T1 tumors (Figure 7a). The mice were administered one of the following treatments: PBS, HMnO2, MB-NO, MB-NO+L, HMO, or HMO+L. The group designated for irradiation received injections followed by exposure to 660 nm laser irradiation (0.1 W/cm2) for 10 min at 10 h post-injection. Tumor volume and mouse body weight were measured every other day, with continuous monitoring of tumor progression over a 16-day period, after which the mice were euthanized for sample collection and imaging (Figure S25). The tumor growth curve (Figure 7b and S26) indicated that the PBS-treated group exhibited a rapid tumor proliferation trend, reaching a volume of 1103 mm3 on the 16th day. In contrast, the HMnO2, MB-NO, and MB-NO+L groups demonstrated only limited tumor suppression, with tumor suppression rates of 29.4%, 30.3%, and 39.5% respectively, suggesting that monotherapies involving carrier-generated oxygen, CDT, gas therapy, or photodynamic therapy alone are insufficient to overcome the multifaceted treatment barriers presented by the TME.

The HMO group demonstrated a significantly enhanced tumor suppression compared to the aforementioned treatment groups, with tumor suppression rate of 57.2%. Notably, the HMO+L group exhibited the most optimal anti-tumor activity. On the 16th day, the tumor volume reached 205 mm3, and the tumor inhibition rate was as high as 81.4%, suggesting a synergistic interaction among the functional components of the HMO nanosystem. This synergy involves the microenvironment responsiveness of the HMnO2 carrier, the photodynamic effect of the MB photosensitizer, and the therapeutic activity of the NO gas molecule, which collectively contribute to a cascade amplification of the therapeutic effect. Weight monitoring data of the mice (Figure 7c) indicated a steady increase in weight across all experimental groups throughout the study, with no statistically significant differences observed among the groups.

Upon completion of the treatment period, histological examination using H&E staining was performed on the five primary organs of the mice, specifically the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney. The results indicated an absence of discernible pathological differences in the histological morphology of all examined organs when compared to the PBS control group (Figure S27a). Subsequent serum biochemical assays demonstrated that the concentrations of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), indicative of liver function impairment, as well as urea (UREA) and creatinine (CREA), indicative of renal function impairment, did not exhibit statistically significant differences between the treatment groups and the control group (Figure S27b). Collectively, these results suggest that all formulations and treatment regimens possess excellent in vivo biological safety and do not induce significant systemic toxicity. Furthermore, the statistical analysis of tumor weights, collected post-mortem (Figure 7d), aligned with the observed tumor growth curve trends, thereby corroborating the potent anti-tumor efficacy of HMO in conjunction with phototherapy.

To comprehensively elucidate the anti-tumor mechanism of the HMO nanosystem, immunohistochemical analysis was performed at the tissue level (Figure 7e). Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining results indicated that tumor cells in the PBS group were densely packed with intact morphology and no significant necrotic areas. In contrast, the HMnO2, MB-NO, and MB-NO + L groups displayed only scattered necrotic foci, indicating the restricted capacity of these monotherapies to damage tumor tissue. Notably, the HMO + L combined treatment group demonstrated extensive tumor cell necrosis and pronounced disruption of cellular architecture, directly confirming the potent destructive capability of the cascade treatment on solid tumors. Furthermore, Ki67 staining revealed a high proportion of proliferative cells in the PBS group, with the Ki67- positive rate progressively decreasing as treatment intensity increased. The HMO + L group exhibited only a minimal presence of positive signals, indicating a significant inhibition of tumor cell proliferation.

TUNEL staining for apoptosis revealed a pattern opposite to that of Ki67. Minimal apoptotic fluorescence signals were observed in the PBS group, whereas the HMO + L group exhibited a substantial presence of red apoptotic fluorescence, indicating significant tumor cells apoptosis. To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying this cytotoxic effect, key signaling pathways were examined by staining for oxidative stress markers such as 3-nitrotyrosine (3-NT), mitochondrial uncoupling protein 2 (UCP2), and HIF-1α (Figure 7f). Notably, the 3-NT staining results revealed a significantly higher positive signal in the HMO + L group compared to other treatment groups. This suggests that the HMO nanosystem facilitates the in situ generation of highly toxic RNOS through a cascade reaction involving ROS and NO within the TME. This process overcomes the limitations associated with the short half-life of individual ROS and their susceptibility to quenching under hypoxic conditions, thereby amplifying local oxidative stress damage and effectively inducing tumor cell apoptosis.

UCP2 staining, a marker of mitochondrial function, revealed significantly upregulated UCP2 expression levels in both the HMO and HMO + L groups compared to the control group. This suggests that RNOS may specifically target tumor cell mitochondria, thereby enhancing cellular damage by disrupting mitochondrial membrane potential and interfering with energy metabolism. Additionally, staining for HIF-1α, a marker for the regulation of the hypoxic microenvironment, indicated strong positive expression in the PBS group, highlighting the intrinsic hypoxic characteristics of the 4T1 TME. In contrast, HIF-1αexpression was downregulated to varying extents in the formulation groups containing HMnO2 components (HMnO2, HMO, and HMO + L groups), with the most pronounced reduction observed in the HMO + L group. This finding suggests that the HMnO2 carrier catalyzes the decomposition of endogenous H2O2 within the TME to generate oxygen, thereby effectively alleviating tumor hypoxia and reversing hypoxia-mediated resistance to PDT, ultimately enhancing the synergistic therapeutic effect. In conclusion, the aforementioned research findings unequivocally illustrate that the HMO nanosystem effectively and safely exerts anti-tumor effects in vivo. This is accomplished through the concurrent alleviation of the hypoxic microenvironment, enhancement of PDT, amplification of oxidative stress damage, and impairment of mitochondrial function. These results offer a novel research paradigm for gas-synergized tumor therapy and offer robust in vivo pharmacodynamic evidence to support subsequent translational research of this nanosystem.

Limitations and Future Directions

The HMO nanosystem proposed in this study exhibits excellent anti-tumor activity, but its limitations must also be acknowledged. Firstly, the tissue penetration ability of 660 nm visible light laser is limited, making it suitable only for superficial tumors or in-situ treatments. Future research could explore deep tissue phototherapy mediated by near-infrared laser or up-conversion nanoparticles. Secondly, Mn2+ may have potential toxicity issues, so a systematic long-term safety assessment is needed. Overcoming these limitations in future research is crucial for enhancing the translational potential of this platform.

Conclusion

In summary, this study presents a TME-responsive HMO nanoparticle that functions as a multifaceted catalytic anti-tumor system. This nanoparticle system catalyzes the decomposition of endogenous H2O2 in acidic TME, generating oxygen, thereby alleviating tumor hypoxia and enhancing the efficacy of PDT. Combining CDT, PDT, and NO gas treatment can trigger a cascade-enhanced oxidative stress, effectively breaking through the antioxidant defense mechanism of tumor cells. It is noteworthy that the HMO + L group induced 56.7% apoptosis in 4T1 cells and achieved a 81.4% tumor inhibition rate. Moreover, the results of body weight changes and main organ H&E staining indicated extremely low systemic toxicity. These findings validate the multi-pathway cascade strategy, including alleviating hypoxia, enhancing ROS, the synergistic effect of RNOS, and mitochondrial damage, to overcome the limitations of traditional PDT, demonstrating excellent preclinical potential providing a new approach for nanocatalytic combined therapy.
