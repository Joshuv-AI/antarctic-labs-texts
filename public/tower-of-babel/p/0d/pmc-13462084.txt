Conserved macromolecular architecture of poplar secondary cell walls revealed by ssNMR and atomistic modeling
Authors: Bennett Addison, Malitha C. Dickwella Widanage, Meagan F. Crowley, Vivek S. Bharadwaj, Lintao Bu, Michael F. Crowley, Anne E. Harman-Ware, Peter N. Ciesielski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The macromolecular architecture of plant secondary cell walls governs wood’s mechanical and biochemical properties, yet its natural intra-species variability remains poorly characterized. Here, we combined ¹³C solid-state NMR (ssNMR), multivariate statistical analysis, and molecular modeling to profile nanoscale structure across 13 genetically diverse Populus trichocarpa genotypes grown in ¹³C-enriched atmospheres. SsNMR-derived phenotypes spanning composition, structure, mobility, and inter-polymer proximities reveal a conserved architecture, with a subtle yet coordinated variation organizing into dominant structural and secondary mobility axes. A representative atomistic model captures these features and reproduces experimental metrics. Molecular dynamics simulations support a weak but consistent positive correlation between cellulose abundance and crystalline-like order, with interior cellulose chains enriched in tg (trans–gauche) conformations without expanding crystalline cores. Together, experiment and simulation reveal a genetically buffered, broadly conserved nanoscale architecture across genotypes, where subtle fine-tuning of cellulose bundling and matrix packing balances mechanical performance with biological function.

Introduction

The robust multi-polymeric matrix of the plant secondary cell wall (SCW) is comprised primarily of cellulose, hemicelluloses, and polyaromatic lignin, and fulfills key biological roles by providing mechanical support for upright growth, facilitating long-distance water transport, and defending against pathogens. These vital functions emerge directly from the cell wall’s polymeric structure and macromolecular architecture, defined as the 3D spatial organization of these polymers into a nanoscale composite1–4. Just as the arrangement of steel, concrete, wood, and drywall dictates a building’s structural integrity, it is this architecture, rather than mere composition, that governs emergent properties like stiffness, flexibility, and biochemical recalcitrance4–9.

Gaining a predictive understanding of how this nanoscale architecture drives emergent bulk material properties remains a fundamental research challenge. Atomistic molecular models offer a powerful approach to bridge this gap, enabling researchers to subject structural hypotheses to quantitative physical constraints and test structure-function relationships in silico within an active, physics-based environment10. However, constructing accurate representative atomistic models of lignocellulose requires quantitative structural metrics to constrain and ground-truth the simulations, a formidable hurdle given its dense, heterogeneous, and multi-polymeric nature. While techniques like atomic force microscopy (AFM) and infrared (IR) spectroscopy offer valuable ultrastructural insights11, they struggle to resolve intact, 3D biopolymer packing. Similarly, techniques like X-ray diffraction and electron microscopy which are used to inform molecular models of homogenous structures like proteins and inorganic crystals fail to provide atomic resolution of plant cell walls due to their lack of long-range periodicity and local irregularities at the macromolecular scale12.

Solid-state Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (ssNMR) spectroscopy of 13C-enriched lignocellulose bypasses some of these limitations by directly probing local atomic environments and through-space interactions without requiring long-range periodicity13. As such, ssNMR has driven key advancements in our structural understanding of SCW architecture over the past 15 years1,3,4,14–22. Building on these foundational advancements, our laboratory recently utilized ssNMR methods on 13C-enriched Populus wood (DN34 Hybrid Poplar) to quantitatively define SCW nanoscale polymer arrangements, yielding a highly detailed, experimentally constrained atomistic model of a plant SCW assembly (PDB-IHM entry 9A3U) and providing a critical link between nanoscale architecture and computational physics21.

Despite these achievements, a significant limitation persists in the experimental design of these previous ssNMR studies, including ours: only a single sample or paired samples (e.g., wild-type vs transgenic) are typically examined, which overlooks the phenotypic variability inherent in natural populations. This raises critical biological questions: How does SCW architecture vary across naturally diverse genotypes, and how are these structural traits coordinated with other phenotypes like polymeric abundances? For example, natural phenotypic variation is well-documented. Across natural populations of Populus trichocarpa, trees exhibit substantial compositional variability in both polymer abundances and lignin monomeric ratios23. This biochemical diversity is accompanied by significant variation in macroscopic physical and mechanical properties like height and mechanical moduli24–27. While studies suggest these compositional and mechanical traits are correlated27–30, and their genetic heritability is increasingly understood31–33, there remains no direct experimental evidence connecting this natural variability to the underlying 3D nanoscale biopolymer architecture.

In this study, we selected 13 natural Populus trichocarpa variants spanning the documented range of cell wall polymer abundances and lignin monomeric (S/G) ratios, and cultivated them in a 13C-enriched atmosphere for advanced solid-state NMR analysis. This approach enabled us to quantify a suite of NMR-accessible phenotypes including polymer abundances, inter-polymer proximities, crystalline-like conformational order of cellulose domains, xylan conformation, and polymer mobilities across a diverse population rather than a single genotype. Under controlled growth conditions, these experimental measurements, when integrated with multivariate statistical analysis and atomistic modeling, reveal a largely conserved secondary cell wall organization, despite genetic and compositional variation. Subtle architectural adjustments, such as modest shifts in cellulose bundle size or lignin–xylan packing, emerge as the primary means of accommodating compositional variations. These findings provide a broad view of SCW architecture and establish a foundation for connecting genetic diversity to nanoscale organization and breeding design for bio-based material performance.

Results

Composition and Spectral Profiling by ¹³C ssNMR

To survey natural variation of plant cell wall architecture, we selected 13 diverse Populus trichocarpa genotypes from a previously characterized panel of over 1,000 natural accessions, deliberately sampling across the full natural spectrum of polymeric abundances and lignin syringyl-to-guaiacyl (S/G) ratios23. Among these, we included specific archetypal lines to establish baselines at the phenotypic extremes: GW_9947 is a representative low-lignin variant, widely utilized as the standard reference genotype by the Center for Bioenergy Innovation, while BESC_393 represents the opposing high-lignin archetype. All plants were grown under identical conditions and uniformly labeled with ¹³C, with an isotopic enrichment of roughly 89% (Fig. S1), enabling advanced 1D and 2D solid-state NMR analysis to probe cell wall architecture across diverse genotypes21,23.

In the exemplar low-lignin genotype GW_9947, deconvolution of the quantitative 13C Multi-CP spectrum resolved contributions from cellulose, acetylated xylan (acetate substituents and backbone signals), and lignin (aromatic and methoxy units), with a minor protein/lipid component (Fig. 1a). Hardwood lignin is dominated by syringyl (S) and guaiacyl (G) subunits, which can be quantified from spectral deconvolution of the aromatic region near 150 ppm23,34. Genotypes with contrasting lignin abundances showed clear spectral differences within 110–150 ppm. For example, the high-lignin line BESC_393 exhibited stronger S3/5 ( ~ 153 ppm), G2 ( ~ 111 ppm), and G6 ( ~ 119 ppm) signals compared to the low-lignin line GW_9947. These shifts were corroborated by 2D 13C-13C correlation experiments including through-bond CP-SQ/DQ INADEQUATE, which resolved intra-ring connectivities and confirmed subunit distributions. In GW_9947, weaker cross-peaks in the S1/4–S2/6 and G3/4–G5 regions further indicated lower lignin/aromatic density (Fig. 1b).

Deconvolution of the 1D Multi-CP spectra across the genotype panel revealed expected variability in cell wall polymer composition (Fig. 1c and Suppl. Table 1). Lignin content ranged from ~21% to over 25% and cellulose content ranging from ~45 to 50% by relative mass abundance, with hemicelluloses (predominantly acetylated xylan) accounting for the remaining major fraction.

Inter-Polymer spatial interactions inform an updated representative atomistic model

In our prior work we leveraged a selective 1D 13C-13C spin-diffusion ssNMR technique to quantify the relative fraction of a “sink” polymer that is spatially located within ~1 nm of a selected “source”21. These quantified inter-polymer contacts, described as Source-to-Sink (lignin-to-cellulose, L2C; lignin-to-xylan, L2X; xylan-to-cellulose, X2C; xylan-to-lignin, X2L), serve as key quantitative inputs for building experimentally-informed atomistic models of the SCW. Here, we applied the same methodology on our panel of diverse 13C-enriched variants to investigate how polymer-polymer spatial proximities vary across the natural Poplar population. Consistent with our prior study, every genotype exhibited a notable asymmetry regarding lignin-xylan interactions: roughly 75% of lignin is in spatial contact with xylan (X2L ~ 75%), but only about 60% of xylan is in spatial contact with lignin (L2X ~ 60%). We were previously able to partially, but not completely, explain this asymmetry with atomistic representations by embedding a subpopulation of xylan within cellulose bundles and shielded from lignin, while most lignin interfaces with xylan at the bundle periphery21. With the expanded dataset confirming the universality of lignin–xylan asymmetry, here we refined our atomistic models aiming to satisfy the complete set of constraints, including both compositional abundances and all inter-polymer contacts.

As shown in the polar plot in Fig. 2c, our best-performing model, seen in Fig. 2d and denoted 4x(CD418), achieves the closest overall match to both compositional and proximity constraints. The model naming convention is described with its core features in Fig. S2. In effect, the asymmetry is only replicated by building a larger system such that enough interstitial xylan is spatially isolated from lignin within the cellulose-fibril interior. Systems without interstitial xylan, or systems containing interstitial xylan but are too small, did not replicate the asymmetry (Fig. S3 and Suppl. Table S3). We propose that this model depicted in Fig. 2d contains the features of the core conserved architecture of hardwood secondary walls, although as will be argued in subsequent sections, compositional variation is accommodated primarily by adjusting the number of cellulose chains within contiguous domains. These domains are assumed to be multiples of 18, consistent with the rosette structure of the cellulose synthase (CesA) enzyme35.

Exploration of cellulose and xylan polymorphism by ssNMR and molecular simulation

Cellulose C6-hydroxymethyl groups on cellulose can adopt several favorable conformations, characterized as trans-gauche (tg), gauche-trans (gt), and gauche-gauche (gg) (Fig. 3a)36. Cellulose crystallinity can be measured directly by techniques like X-ray diffraction and solid-state ¹³C NMR, each offering different advantages in sensitivity and structural resolution37. By ssNMR, Crystallinity Index (CrI) is traditionally extracted by analyzing the ¹³C CP-MAS solid-state NMR spectrum, taking the ratio between the ~89 (crystalline, domain 1) and ~84 (amorphous, domain 2) ppm regions37. This ranged between 37-40% derived directly from 1D ssNMR spectra for our 13 genotypes. For native and intact lignocellulose, however, this traditional practice underestimates cellulose crystallinity due to overlapping signals from xylan and lignin, particularly in the ~80-90 ppm region where the Cβ of lignin inter-unit linkages and C4 signal of both 2-fold and 3-fold xylan conformers also resonate38. In our case, the use of ¹³C enrichment combined with 2D ¹³C–¹³C correlation ssNMR effectively disentangles overlapping signals, allowing precise, site-resolved quantification of cellulose crystallinity. Xylan polymorphism was similarly quantified by integrating the semi-resolved 2D CORD regions corresponding to linearized 2-fold and disordered 3-fold conformations (Fig. 3b). Unlike cellulose crystallinity, xylan conformational distribution proved quite consistent across the population, with the fraction of 2-fold xylan varying only slightly from 62 to 65% (Suppl. Table S6).

Analysis of xylan polymorphism of the 4x(CD418) model reveals that conformation is dependent on its proximity to cellulose and lignin. Distributions of the ϕ+ψ dihedral angles (presented in Fig. S4) indicate that xylan favors a 2-fold conformation, with a secondary population corresponding to more disordered 3-fold structures. As the distance from cellulose increases, xylan shifts away from the ordered 2-fold conformation toward more 3-fold, disordered states. This observation is fully consistent with the consensus opinion derived from prior solid-state NMR studies3,16. Further, we find increasing proximity to lignin similarly promotes the 3-fold conformation, also in agreement with conclusions from prior ssNMR interpretations of lignin-polysaccharide interactions20. A complementary analysis based on the O2–C5–C5–O2 dihedral angle (presented in Fig. S5), corresponding to the acetate orientation introduced previously, further supports these findings. This metric indicates that xylan in close proximity to cellulose is more ordered, with acetate groups consistently oriented away from the cellulose surface, whereas xylan located farther from cellulose or nearer to lignin exhibits increased disorder and more variable acetate orientations. In summary, these analyses support three key conclusions: (1) xylan near cellulose adopts a more ordered structure than xylan located farther away; (2) proximity to lignin is associated with increased xylan disorder; and (3) these behaviors reflect continuous trends rather than discrete structural transitions, as a broad distribution of conformations persists throughout the model.

There is a weak positive correlation between cellulose abundance and the more-reliable 2D-derived CrI, suggesting that higher cellulose levels slightly favor more ordered domains (Fig. 3c and Suppl. Table S6). We hypothesized that the positive correlation reflects an increased tendency for cellulose to form larger contiguous domains, a mechanism we next probed using molecular dynamics simulations. We constructed representative molecular models prepared with varying contiguous cellulose domain sizes, and used molecular dynamic simulations to compare the total number of residues adopting either tg (gold) or gg/gt (green) hydroxymethyl configurations to the ssNMR CrI observables (Fig. 3d). The simulation results were categorized according to “exterior” and “interior” position within the cellulose domain (Fig. 3e, f, Figure. S6 and Supplementary Table S4, S5).

These simulations predict several trends in both location and abundance of glucan residues with specific C6-hydroxymethyl conformations. Firstly, MD simulations revealed tg, gt, and gg conformers are found throughout both interior and exterior regions, indicating that specific conformations are not strictly limited to certain locations within the fibril cross-section. There is a slightly elevated representation of tg conformers in the fibril interior ( ~ 50% tg) relative to the exterior ( ~ 40% tg), but this pattern is only partly consistent with the classical core–shell model that posits a crystalline interior core surrounded by an amorphous outer shell2,39,40. Instead, MD results better align with recent ssNMR interpretations of native lignocellulose showing that the 89 ppm (crystalline-like, tg conformer) and 84 ppm (amorphous-like) cellulose environments are distributed throughout the microfibril, with tg conformations merely enriched, but not confined, within interior chains1,21.

Second, we observe that increasing the size of the contiguous cellulose domain did not result in a proportional increase in the size of the core highly-ordered region. In the model representing larger cellulose aggregates 1x(CD618), some coalescence of crystalline-like conformational domains was observed, but complete fusion into a larger core-shell crystallite did not occur. Rather, the resultant structure exhibited multiple predominantly tg regions separated by less-ordered chains at the interfaces between the 18-chain elementary fibrils. This prediction is consistent with recent observations of Tai et. al. who found that adjacent elementary fibrils are unlikely to fuse into larger, fully conjoined crystallites40. Evidence of this behavior is clearly visualized in the color-coded snapshot of the largest model shown in Fig. 3d wherein several small, predominantly tg domains are disrupted by glucan units adopting gt and gg configurations.

The slight increase in the total population in all chains of tg configurations with increasing contiguous cellulose domain size is consistent with the slight positive correlation between increasing crystalline-like conformational order and increasing cellulose content observed in the ssNMR results. However, this fairly weak correlation supports the concept that exceedingly large, highly ordered domains are unlikely to form by fusion of multiple adjacent elementary fibrils. Together, MD and ssNMR suggest that adjustments to crystalline-like conformational order arise from subtle increases in contiguous cellulose domain size and not from large, fused crystalline blocks, accounting for variation in cellulose fractional abundances.

Polymeric molecular mobilities from ssNMR relaxation are consistent across all genotypes

NMR relaxation and dynamics measurements are commonly used in plant cell wall ssNMR studies to probe polymer mobility on nanosecond timescales (¹H and ¹³C T₁) and on slower microsecond timescales (¹H T₁ρ)14,15. We sought to determine whether NMR relaxation behavior varied across the 13 Populus genotypes and whether molecular mobility correlated with other NMR-derived structural traits like polymeric abundances. In principle, substantial variation in relaxation times could signal differences in cellulose aggregate size, polymer packing, or other architectural features. For example, significantly larger uninterrupted cellulose aggregates might yield overall more rigid structures with reduced mobility, leading to longer relaxation times.

Our observations from NMR relaxation metrics for ¹³C and ¹H nuclei across 21 spectral regions corresponding to major plant cell wall polymers (Fig. S7a), reveals tight clustering of relaxation times across all genotypes. While relaxation times vary predictably by chemical environment (e.g., crystalline-like cellulose units exhibit longer relaxation times compared to amorphous-like units), they show minimal variability across genotypes and no discernable correlation with compositional or architectural phenotypes (Fig. S7b). These results support an interpretation in which nanoscale polymeric organization is generally conserved across the natural population, yielding consistent molecular mobilities despite genetic and compositional diversity.

Integrative correlation analysis of structure composition and architecture

To understand how structural and dynamical features covary across genotypes, we analyzed all ssNMR-derived traits using Pearson correlations and principal component analysis (PCA). This integrative view brings forth an understanding of cell wall architecture not accessible with single-genotype analysis. PCA reveals two dominant axes of variation (Fig. 4a). The first principal component captures a structural organization axis, defined by positive loadings for cellulose abundance and 2D CrI and negative loadings for lignin content and inter-polymer contacts, while the second component corresponds to a matrix mobility axis, dominated by hemicellulose abundance and proton and carbon relaxation values. Together, these axes describe 60% of total variance and reveal that the natural variation in Populus wood architecture is driven primarily along two dimensions of structural packing and polymer mobility (Suppl. A more detailed picture from the Pearson correlation matrix reveals some intriguing features (Fig. S8). For one, S/G ratios were positively correlated with lignin–xylan (X2L, r = 0.62; L2X, r = 0.48) and lignin–cellulose contacts (L2C, r = 0.53), suggesting that S-rich lignin promotes denser packing at the lignin–carbohydrate interface. This interpretation is consistent with recent ssNMR work in young Arabidopsis stems, which also showed that S-lignin preferentially interacts with acetylated xylan and stabilizes tighter lignin–carbohydrate associations22.

Another emergent pattern was the inverse relationship between cellulose fractional abundance and the relative proportion of cellulose in contact with surrounding matrix polymers. Specifically, as cellulose content increased, the relative proportion of cellulose in spatial proximity to lignin and xylan both decreased (L2C, r = −0.77; X2C, r = 0.81; Fig. S8). This correlated variation along the structural axis (PC1) can be explained geometrically: higher cellulose fractions are accommodated by slightly larger contiguous cellulose bundles, which create additional interior glucan chains (subtle increase in CrI) while reducing the relative fraction of cellulose within spin-diffusion range of lignin and xylan. To test this interpretation, inter-polymer contacts X2C and L2C were calculated from molecular models of varying sizes, and calculated metrics were compared to experimental results (Fig. 4b). For the simplified models without interstitial xylan, the calculated proximities indeed reproduced the overall experimental trend, showing that increasing cellulose domain size reduces the relative fraction of cellulose that resides within spin-diffusion range of xylan. However, the modeled values overshot experimental L2C and X2C for the smallest models and undershot them for the largest bundles. When adding interstitial xylan (4x(CD418)) we replicate the X2L vs L2X asymmetry, but our proximity calculations slightly under-predict both X2C and L2C, suggesting our average cellulose aggregate size across the sample set could be slightly smaller than 4×18-chain microfibrils imaged. We interpret these results as the core and conserved cell wall polymeric architecture representing the 4x(CD418) model, with natural variation of contiguous cellulose aggregate sizes ranging in a more narrow intermediate regime, consistently captured by bundles of ~3–4, 18-chain microfibrils. The total 4x(CD418) model represents a macrofibril unit size of roughly 15–20 nm (see scale bar in Fig. 2d). Crucially, our dimensions align very closely with the macrofibril assemblies observed in native woods by Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and cryo-SEM studies, which demonstrate that elementary microfibrils in Populus wood naturally aggregate into larger assemblies with diameters spanning ~10–30 nm41,42.

Discussion

Integrating atomistic modeling with population-scale ssNMR phenotyping across 13 natural Populus genotypes reveals a conserved secondary-wall architecture in hardwoods that is not evident in single-genotype analyses. Key observations that informed our broad conclusions are outlined as follows: (1) PCA analysis of all ssNMR-derived phenotypes produced a scattered cluster driven primarily by a structural (PC1) and dynamics/mobility (PC2) axis. The lack of separation among genotype classes (e.g., GW vs. BESC) indicates that secondary-wall variation is subtle and continuous, not discrete, and reflects coordinated adjustments within a conserved architectural framework. (2) Cellulose abundance modestly elevates crystalline-like conformational order. CrI derived from 2D 13C-13C CORD revealed a weak but consistent positive correlation between cellulose content and crystallinity, suggesting slightly larger or more ordered cellulose bundles. (3) MD simulations clarify the basis of crystalline-like conformational order trends by predicting that subtle increases in CrI reflect slightly larger bundle sizes, attributed to a higher relative proportion of interior glucans enriched in tg relative to gg/gt conformations. However, the conformations are distributed throughout the fibril, and full fusion of 18-chain bundles into larger crystallites generally does not occur, complicating the “core-shell” model of intact lignocellulose. (4) Matrix-to-cellulose through-space contacts scale with cellulose aggregate size. Selective 13C–13C spin diffusion showed that as cellulose content increases, the relative proportion of cellulose near lignin (L2C) and xylan (X2C) decreases, consistent with more interior glucans being encased within larger bundles. (5) Molecular models reconcile experimental metrics. Calculated inter-polymer proximities reproduce the observed trend that larger cellulose aggregates yield reduced L2C and X2C contacts, but models with either very small or very large aggregates deviate from experimental values. This suggests that under the shared greenhouse growth conditions used here, the 13 genotypes occupy a ‘sweet spot’ of subtle variation around ~3–4 18-chain fibrils per aggregate. (6) NMR-derived molecular mobilities show only modest and non-systemic variation across genotypes. ¹³C T₁, ¹H T₁, and ¹H T₁ρ values cluster tightly and contribute primarily to the secondary (PC2) axis, which is orthogonal to the structural axis (PC1), indicating that polymer dynamics are largely decoupled from changes in cellulose composition and polymeric packing. (7) Lignin–xylan asymmetry is universal in poplar wood. Across all genotypes, ~75% of lignin contacts xylan (X2L) but only ~60% of xylan contacts lignin (L2X), indicating a subpopulation of interstitial xylan is embedded within cellulose bundles and shielded from lignin. Only the largest molecular model that buries enough xylan is able to replicate this asymmetry (4x(CD418), see Fig. S3). The presence of this interstitial xylan likely leads to the formation of relatively uniformly sized cellulose bundles when isolated from matrixing polymers, which is consistent with our spin-diffusion data and the findings of Daicho et al.43. (8) S/G ratio reinforces lignin–xylan interfaces. Higher syringyl content was positively correlated with L2C, X2L, and L2X contacts, supporting a model where S-rich lignin preferentially packs with acetylated xylan and, through this interface, approaches cellulose.

Collectively, these ssNMR data and molecular models converge on a framework in which the secondary cell wall architecture of hardwoods like poplar is broadly conserved. Compositional variability appears to be accommodated primarily through subtle modulation of cellulose bundle size, lignin–polysaccharide packing density, and hemicellulose-mediated polymer mobility, rather than through wholesale reorganization of the matrix. The striking conservation of secondary wall architecture across a genetically and compositionally diverse population suggests that plants exert strong biological control to maintain nanoscale structural order when grown in consistent and optimized environmental settings.

This structural buffering, however, does not necessarily imply that subtle architectural variation is biologically insignificant. Returning to our building analogy, while the fundamental ‘floorplan’ of the cell wall remains generalizable across natural populations, the subtle architectural adjustments we observed may act as nanomechanical tuning knobs. It is plausible that the well-documented macroscopic variation in wood properties (e.g., stiffness and recalcitrance) arises from the synergistic scaling of these tightly regulated nanoscale shifts with higher-order, microscale tissue adaptations, such as variations in cell wall thickness and fiber-to-vessel ratios. This work can be used to address fundamental knowledge gaps and provide multiscale modeling improvements to efforts connecting macro and microscale properties, which have previously not considered biopolymer architectural data11,44.

Additionally, whether this representative architecture is conserved across developmental stages or environmental conditions remains an open question and will require extending these measurements beyond the common-garden setting used here. Understanding the natural variability in undomesticated bioenergy feedstocks is critical for identifying novel genetic traits that govern emergent material properties, such as mechanical strength and biochemical recalcitrance45,46. Exploiting this natural diversity is essential for breeding resilient, highly optimized feedstocks to support advanced biomanufacturing and secure domestic supply chains for critical biomaterials. This work therefore maintained a deliberate focus on natural Populus populations in order to establish a critical baseline for how these architectural motifs vary naturally. Investigating non-natural genetically modified plants represents a logical and necessary next step. Engineered transgenic variants with highly aberrant compositions, such as severe lignin depletion or drastically altered hemicelluloses, may push the cell wall assembly beyond its natural buffering capacity47. Applying an integrated analytical-computational framework to these variants may determine how strictly these biological design principles must be followed. For example, can the plant secondary wall survive the disruption of its precise hemicellulose decoration patterning (i.e., the ‘every-other-xylose’ motif), or does altering this fundamental interface ultimately result in macroscopic structural failure?

Overall, our results inform both the methodological scope of ssNMR and the biological principles governing SCW assembly. From the methodological perspective, these results reconcile the limitations of interpreting ssNMR data from a single biological sample or pair of samples (wild-type vs. transgenic), in that even a single well-characterized sample can capture many aspects of the conserved architectural motifs we observe across genotypes under common-garden conditions. Our findings therefore, support the interpretative power of past and future ssNMR analyses detailing lignocellulosic structure and architecture of limited scope, which is important due to the high cost of 13C enrichment. The molecular models with experimentally derived polymer-polymer proximities further advance the translation of ssNMR observables into representative atomistic representations of SCWs and enable significant insights via MD simulation. Our findings provide additional support for biological functionalities wherein the conserved organization and controlled microfibril packing arrangement creates nanoscale conduits for water and ion diffusion48,49, supporting vascular transport and stress responses. Further, restricting cellulose bundle size through interstitial xylan likely prevents the formation of highly ordered domains, thereby preserving ductility and flexibility of the wall, traits necessary for resisting wind, bending, and mechanical stress. It is important to note that while our quantitative metrics and atomistic models focus on xylan as the dominant hardwood hemicellulose, minor hemicelluloses such as glucomannan also populate the Populus SCW50. Given that glucomannans remain tightly associated with cellulose even after harsh extractions51, they likely serve a complementary role alongside interstitial xylan in shielding fibril surfaces and preventing the runaway aggregation of large cellulose domains. Interestingly, this idea that hemicelluloses limit growth of contiguous cellulose domains parallels collagen’s prevention of large-scale hydroxyapatite percolation during bone formation52, suggesting a shared design principle of interfacial biopolymers throttling stiff-phase connectivity to tune macroscopic mechanics. From an evolutionary perspective, this conservation suggests that while genetic diversity readily tunes wall composition, higher-order general architectural motifs are buffered against variation, potentially preserving mechanical and physiological integrity across environments. These findings imply that plant secondary cell walls are not only composites of polymers but are actively regulated biomaterials, optimized to balance stiffness, toughness, resilience, and transport across diverse genotypes.

Methods

Preparation of 13C-enriched poplar 13 genotype variants

Thirteen poplar genotypes were isotopically labeled with 13C as previously described in ref. 21 by cultivation in a controlled-environment chamber (Percival Scientific, Perry, IA, USA) under an atmosphere supplemented with 13C-enriched CO2 (Sigma-Aldrich). After initial rooting under ambient conditions, plants were transferred to the chamber and grown for six weeks under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod at 23 °C/18 °C and 65% relative humidity, with 13C-CO2 concentrations maintained at 600 ppm during the light period and 400 ppm during the dark period. Following labeling, leaves, stems, and roots were harvested separately. Stems were debarked immediately upon harvest, air-dried at room temperature, sectioned into 1–2 inch segments, and ground to 40 mesh using a Wiley mill. All milled samples were sequentially extracted to remove starch (enzymatic digestion with commercial amylases as described by Decker et al.)53 and soluble organics (Soxhlet extraction with 95% ethanol). Residual material was washed with deionized water, air-dried (20-40% relative humidity), and stored in glass vials prior to ssNMR analysis.

ssNMR experiments for structural analysis

All NMR experiments were performed as previously described in ref. 21, using either a Bruker Avance III 600 MHz (14.1 T) spectrometer equipped with a 1.6 mm PhoenixNMR HXY MAS probe operating in HC double-resonance mode, or a Bruker Avance III HD 200 MHz (4.7 T) spectrometer equipped with a 4 mm Bruker HX MAS probe, depending on the experiment set desired. Detailed experimental parameters are provided in Suppl. Table S6. For lower field (200 MHz) measurements, we used a Bruker 4 mm HX MAS probe. Biomass samples were in the air-dried state ( ~ 30–40% relative humidity) and packed into full 80 μL 4 mm rotors, then spun under magic angle spinning (MAS) at 10 kHz. Datasets collected under these conditions included quantitative 1D MultiCP for polymeric abundances and selective 1D 13C-13C spin diffusion measurements for inter-polymer proximity analysis. Higher-field (600 MHz) experiments were collected using a 1.6 mm PhoenixNMR HXY MAS probe operating in HC double resonance mode. Biomass samples were first water-hydrated in DI water and sealed in 1.6 mm rotors, using 30 kHz MAS. Datasets collected under these conditions included two-dimensional (2D) CORD, 2D CP-INADEQUATE, and all dynamics-related measurements. 13C chemical shifts were externally referenced to the CH2 resonance of adamantane at 38.48 ppm relative to tetramethylsilane (TMS). Relaxation times were all measured using the following protocols: 1H T1 by inversion recovery and CP detection (cpht1ir), 1H T1ρ by a variable spin-locking pulse prior to CP detection (cpht1rho), and 13C T1 by direct detection and saturation recovery (dpsatrect1). Typical radiofrequency field strengths for our 4 mm and 1.6 mm setups, unless otherwise specified, were respectively 80 and 125 kHz for 1H decoupling, 100 and 125 kHz for 1H hard pulses, 62.5 and 100 kHz for 13C hard pulses, and for cross polarization we typically used a 62.5 kHz square contact pulse on the 13C channel and the 1H channel matched to the +1 side band of the Hartmann Hahn profile using a ~ 15% linear ramp.

Quantitative 1D 13C-13C spin-diffusion measurements for evaluation of inter-polymer proximities were performed identical to our previous study, including both data acquisition and data processing procedures21. Briefly, we used MultiCP for quantitative excitation of all carbon types, then used a Gaussian cascade selective inversion pulse to selectively retain either aromatic or acetate carbon signal prior to a 13C-13C spin diffusion of period τm of 3 s. Each spectrum was acquired under three conditions: (i) selective inversion at 22 ppm targeting xylan acetate methyl, (ii) selective inversion at 150 ppm targeting lignin, and (iii) nonselective excitation to serve as a quantitative reference. The latter corresponds to the initial slice of a 2D DARR experiment with MultiCP polarization. These experiments were collected in replicates for each genotype to evaluate variability of the measurement. All the NMR experiment parameters are given in Supplementary Table S8.

Molecular dynamics methods

Preparation of atomistic molecular models

The methods for preparing atomistic models follow our previous work21. Briefly, the model for cellulose Iβ was constructed based on the 18-chain hexagonal 234432 motif with a degree of polymerization (DP) of 40. Cellulose domains were varied from 36 to 288 cellulose chains, consisting of 2, 4, 6, or 16 18-chain hexagonal bundles. Four different xylan models of DP 40 were constructed, each with different acetylation positions as described in Addison et al.12. Lignin 20mers were prepared based on the model by Ralph et al. for hardwood with an S:G ratio of 65:35. The number of xylan and lignin molecules in each model was varied to match compositional analysis of cellulose, xylan, and lignin weight percents.

Assembly of plant secondary cell wall models

Six different models were proposed to match interpolymer contacts from NMR experiments and to test the hypothesis of contiguous cellulose domain size correlating with crystallinity index, summarized in Supplementary Table 3. The models are named for the number of elementary cellulose 18-chain microfibrils in the contiguous cellulose domain. The six models evaluated are named: 1x(CD218), 1x(CD418), 1x(CD618), 2x(CD218), 3x(CD218), and 4x(CD418). The naming convention is described in Figure. S2. In all models, 70% of xylan molecules in two-fold conformation were placed on the surface of the cellulose domain while 30% were randomly placed away from the surface in three-fold conformation along with lignin molecules which were also randomly placed away from the cellulose surface.

Molecular dynamics simulations

To reach the cell wall density of 1.5 g/cm3, each of the assembled models was slowly compressed by reducing the simulation box dimensions. The initial structure first underwent MD simulations under a CPT ensemble for 200 ps to facilitate the aggregation of randomly packed lignin and xylan chains towards the centrally placed cellulose in the simulation box. Subsequently, the structure was compressed incrementally by reducing the dimensions of the x and y directions by 1 Å at each step, with the atomic coordinates re-scaled accordingly. After each adjustment in box size dimensions, the system was subjected to energy minimization for 2000 steps, followed by a 10 ps MD simulation under an NVT ensemble. Typically, 40 such iterations are performed to achieve the target density. The models were subsequently solvated with TIP3P water molecules to achieve a moisture content of 6%. Additionally, sodium ions were introduced to maintain charge neutrality. MD simulations were performed using NAMD54 ver. 3.0.2 software with the CHARMM36 force field for carbohydrates55, the lignin force field56, and parameters for acetylated xylosyl residues and glucuronic acid from the CHARMM General Force Field (CGenFF)57,58. The CHARMM36 forcefield has previously been demonstrated to be the most reliable forcefield for accurate depictions of cellulose microfibril conformations59. Polarizable forcefields were not used due to the lack of validation and computational cost. All simulations were performed with periodic boundary conditions. The particle mesh Ewald method was used to treat electrostatic interactions with a grid spacing of 1 Å and a short-range cutoff distance of 12 Å60. The bond lengths of chemical bonds to hydrogen atoms were constrained. Production runs were performed in the NVT ensemble at 300 K for 100 ns with a 2 fs timestep. All simulations were performed on NREL HPC’s Kestrel supercomputer.

Calculation of crystalline-like conformational order from MD simulations

Trajectories from the simulations of models consisting of SCW assemblies with a contiguous cellulose domain consisting of 36, 72, or 108 cellulose chains surrounded by xylan and lignin (1x(CD218), 1x(CD418), and 1x(CD618) models) were analyzed to calculate the percent crystallinity with increasing cellulose domain size. Note that in these systems, there was no interstitial xylan within the cellulose domain. Every 2 ns, The χ torsion angle describing the conformation of the C6-hydroxymethyl was calculated for each glucan residue using the positions of the O5-C5-C6-O6 atoms. Dihedral angles between 0 and 120 degrees were classified as belonging to the gt conformation, 120 to 240 were classified as tg, and 240 to 260 were classified as gg based on NMR experiments performed by Phyo et al.61. The percent of residues with tg conformation was calculated from the subset of interior, exterior, and all cellulose chains. Average percentages were calculated over the last 20 ns of the simulation trajectories.
