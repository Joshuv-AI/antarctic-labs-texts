Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals anti-tumor potency of CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells in humanized mice via PD-1 and TIGIT co-targeting
Authors: Wai Nam Liu, Sarah L. Harden, Shawn Lu Wen Tan, Rachel Jun Rou Tan, Shin Yie Fong, Sue Yee Tan, Min Liu, Isha Karnik, Timothy Wai Ho Shuen, Han Chong Toh, Yong Fan, Seng Gee Lim, Jerry Kok Yen Chan, Qingfeng Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In solid tumors, the exhaustion of natural killer (NK) cells and cytotoxic T cells in the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment poses challenges for effective tumor control. Conventional humanized mouse models of hepatocellular carcinoma patient-derived xenografts (HCC-PDX) encounter limitations in NK cell infiltration, hindering studies on NK cell immunobiology. Here, we introduce an improved humanized mouse model with restored NK cell reconstitution and infiltration in HCC-PDX, coupled with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to identify potential anti-HCC treatments. A single administration of adeno-associated virus carrying human interleukin-15 reinstated persistent NK cell reconstitution and infiltration in HCC-PDX in humanized mice. scRNA-seq revealed NK cell and T cell subpopulations with heightened PDCD1 and TIGIT levels. Notably, combination therapy with anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT antibodies alleviated HCC burden in humanized mice, demonstrating NK cell-dependent efficacy. Bulk-RNA sequencing analysis also revealed significant alterations in the tumor transcriptome that may contribute to further resistance after combination therapy, warranting further investigations. As an emerging strategy, ongoing clinical trials with anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT antibodies provide limited data. The improved humanized mouse HCC-PDX model not only sheds light on the pivotal role of NK cells but also serves as a robust platform for evaluating safety and anti-tumor efficacy of combination therapies and other potential regimens, complementing clinical insights.

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide, with primary liver cancer ranking as the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths in 2020, resulting in approximately 800,000 fatalities.1 Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) constitutes the majority of primary liver cancer cases, comprising 75%–85% of all instances.1 HCC is closely associated with chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) or hepatitis C virus infections, as well as other metabolic disorders.2 Despite the initial success of sorafenib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), as a first-line targeted therapy for advanced HCC, its anti-tumor efficacy is limited.3 Furthermore, some initially sorafenib-sensitive HCC patients eventually succumb to disease progression, suggesting that acquired drug resistance may impede favorable outcomes.4 Hence, there is an urgent need to develop second-line therapy or combination therapy for patients with HCC.

The human immune system plays a pivotal role in regulating cancer progression.5 In patients with HCC, it has been reported that the frequency of circulating and tumor-infiltrating regulatory T cells (CD4+CD25+Foxp3+) was significantly increased.6 Remarkably, the accumulation of regulatory T cells reduced the infiltration of CD8+ T cells in tumor regions compared with non-tumor regions, and abolished their degranulation and production of granzyme A, granzyme B, and perforin, possibly leading to disease progression.6 In another study, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from patient samples revealed that the number of effector CD8+ T cells was diminished in the tumor microenvironment (TME), accompanied by an increase in exhausted CD8+ T cells expressing high levels of CTLA4, PDCD1, and HAVCR2.7 Functionally, these exhausted CD8+ T cells exhibit decreased cytokine production and cytolytic ability, leading to failure of cancer eradication.8 Therefore, it is conceivable that the restoration of effector T cell function in the TME may improve clinical outcomes. Over the past few decades, clinical trials with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), and anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) antibodies, have demonstrated their ability to improve overall survival in HCC patients.9 For instance, patients who received tremelimumab (anti-CTLA-4 antibody) monotherapy had a response rate of 17.6%.10 Similarly, the response rate to nivolumab or pembrolizumab monotherapy (anti-PD-1 antibodies) was approximately 15.0%, with improved overall survival from 11 to 14 months.11,12 Apparently, combination therapy using tremelimumab plus durvalumab (anti-PD-L1 antibody) or nivolumab plus ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4 antibody) demonstrated better clinical outcomes in patients than monotherapy,13,14 underscoring the importance of evaluating novel combination therapies. To address this need, our group has established a humanized mouse HCC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model, providing a robust platform for assessing the anti-tumor efficacy of emerging combination therapies.15,16

Natural killer (NK) cells, innate effector lymphocytes with potent anti-tumor and anti-viral properties, play a crucial role in cancer immunity.17 However, in HCC patients, both the frequency and number of circulating NK cells are reduced compared with healthy individuals.18,19 Moreover, a decline in CD56dimCD16pos cytotoxic NK subsets within tumor regions, compared with non-tumor regions in the livers of these patients,18 further suggests an escape mechanism employed by tumors to evade NK cell-mediated anti-tumor responses, contributing to HCC progression.20 In addition, it was found that the cytotoxicity of circulating NK cells of HCC patients was markedly decreased compared with that of healthy controls, as reflected by a decrease in the production of cytoplasmic granules, such as granzyme A, granzyme B, and perforin.18,21 Besides, tumor-infiltrating NK cells were found to be dysfunctional, as revealed by their abolished ability to produce interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and kill K-562 targets, compared with non-tumor infiltrating NK cells,18 suggesting that the functional impairment of NK cells might contribute to HCC pathogenesis.20 Similar to T cells, the tumor inflammatory milieu can induce NK cell exhaustion, dampening their anti-tumor function.22,23 However, compared with T cells, mechanisms underlying NK cell exhaustion remain less explored, partly due to the lack of reliable pre-clinical models with substantial NK cell reconstitution. Human interleukin-15 (IL-15) is essential for the development of circulating NK cells and tissue-resident NK cells in vivo.24,25 In conventional humanized mice, the production of human IL-15 is minimal, and poor interspecies cross-reactivity between mouse and human IL-15 results in suboptimal NK cell circulation,26 potentially limiting their infiltration into tumors.15,16 Hence, it is essential to develop a humanized mouse cancer model with restored NK cell reconstitution and tumor infiltration for studies of NK cell dysfunction in the TME and identification of subsequent therapeutic strategies. Earlier studies have demonstrated the ability to enhance the percentage and number of circulating NK cells in humanized mice by injecting recombinant human IL-15 protein,27 plasmid DNA encoding human IL-15,28 IL-15/IL-15 receptor-alpha,29 or pro-IL-15 into mice.30 Alternatively, another group generated a human IL-15 knockin mouse with improved circulating and tissue-resident NK cells.31

In this study, we established an improved humanized mouse model aimed at restoring persistent NK cell reconstitution and subsequent infiltration into HCC-PDX. This improvement was achieved through a single administration of adeno-associated virus carrying human IL-15 (AAV-hIL15). By integrating scRNA-seq and flow cytometry data, we identified distinct subpopulations of CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells within the HCC-PDX. These subpopulations were marked by the expression of various inhibitory receptors, including but not limited to PD-1 and T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT). Importantly, our in vivo experiments revealed that the synergistic anti-tumor response induced by combination therapy with anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT antibodies was contingent upon the presence of CD56+ NK cells.

Results

Injection of AAV-hIL15 restores NK cell reconstitution in humanized mice

Human NK cells constitute approximately 15% of the lymphocytes in peripheral blood (Figure S1A), consistent with previous reports.32 However, in traditional humanized mice, less than 5% of circulating human NK cells have been observed.28 To restore NK cell reconstitution in humanized mice, a one-time delivery of AAV-hIL15 was administered intravenously, and reconstitution of the mice was monitored longitudinally (Figure 1A). Three days post-injection, there was an elevation of human IL-15 levels in the plasma (Figure S1B), which returned to basal levels by day 7 post-injection (Figures S1B and S1C). Importantly, there was a subtle increase in chimerism post-injection (Figure 1B), and the percentage and number of circulating NK cells (hCD45+CD3−CD56+; Figure S1D) in the mice increased and remained steady for at least 16 weeks (Figures 1C and 1D). Flow cytometric analysis revealed that the phenotype of NK cells in the blood of humanized mice (B-NK) was similar to that in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC-NK) (Figure 1E). Furthermore, these NK cells exhibited similar cytotoxicity toward their target K-562 cells (Figure 1F) and induced apoptosis in the K-562 cells (Figure 1G). Human NK cells can be divided into two main subsets based on the cell-surface density of CD16 and CD56.32 To explore the roles of hCD45+CD3−CD16+CD56+ NK cells and hCD45+CD3−CD16−CD56+ NK cells in the humanized mice injected with AAV-hIL15 (hIL15-humice), these subsets were isolated using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) (Figure S2A). Subsequent intracellular cytokine staining revealed a higher prevalence of granzyme A-positive and granzyme B-positive cells in the CD16+CD56+ NK cell subset, while granulysin-positive and IFN-γ-positive cells were more abundant in the CD16−CD56+ NK cell subset, as depicted in Figure S2B. Functionally, CD16+CD56+ NK cells exhibited greater cytotoxicity toward their target K-562 cells (Figure S2C), although they secreted less IFN-γ compared with the CD16−CD56+ NK cells (Figure S2D).

Next, we examined whether the injection of AAV-hIL15 modulated the NK cell proportion in the organs of humanized mice. As shown in Figure S3A, higher percentages of NK cells were observed in kidneys, livers, lungs, and spleens after injection. These results were further validated by in situ immunohistochemical staining for NCR1 (Figures S3B and S3C). In addition, the phenotypic profile of NK cells in these organs was similar to that of B-NK cells (Figure S3D). Collectively, our data suggest that B-NK cells in hIL15-humice are phenotypically and functionally comparable with PBMC-NK cells in humans, and higher percentages of NK cells are present in the major organs of hIL15-humice.

More tumor-infiltrating CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells in hIL15-humice

In the classic humanized mouse HCC-PDX model, a limited number of tumor-infiltrating NK cells were found,15 which hindered the study of NK cells in the TME. To address this limitation, HCC-PDX were transplanted into hIL15-humice, and the immune cell profile was examined (Figure 2A). Our results showed that more circulating NK cells, both CD16+CD56+ NK cells and CD16−CD56+ NK cells, were found compared with humanized mice injected with AAV-GFP (GFP-humice) (Figures S4A–S4C). scRNA-seq of CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells reveals distinct subpopulations in hIL15-humice

The heterogeneity of NK cell and T cell populations in HCC-PDX-transplanted humanized mice was investigated using 10x Genomics-based transcriptomics. Blood, spleen, and tumor samples were harvested from hIL15-humice, and CD56+ NK cells or CD8+ T cells were isolated and subjected to sequencing to analyze a combined total of 29,283 cells (Figure 3A). Dimensional reduction via Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) on the most varied genes separated the cells into 13 clusters. Based on the expression of known marker genes, such as IGHM and BLVRB, three clusters were identified as contaminating immune populations (monocytes, neutrophils, and macrophages) and the remaining 10 clusters were identified as NK cells or T cells (Figures 3B and S5; Table S1). For NK cell identification, transcript markers, such as KLRF1 and KLRD1, were used in addition to NCAM1 to define NK cells.34,35 As depicted in Figure S6, these markers were highly expressed in our identified NK cell clusters. Furthermore, we confirmed the presence and differential expression of NKG7 and GNLY, transcripts enriched in NK cells, in our dataset (Figure S6), which corroborates the finding from another study.36 Among the eight subclusters of NCAM1+ cells, one NKT cell population (NCAM1+CD8A+) was identified, along with seven subpopulations of NK cells, including activated CD56+ NK cells, cytotoxic CD16+ NK cells, inhibited CD16+ NK cells, CD56bright NK cells, dividing CD56bright NK cells, CD56brightCD161+ NK cells, and proliferating NK cells (Figures 3D and 3E; Table S1). Notably, activated CD56+ NK cells (47.45% of tumor-infiltrating cells), CD56brightCD161+ NK cells (8.00%), dividing CD56bright NK cells (5.97%), and NKT cells (5.34%) were found in greater proportions in the tumor tissue than in the blood (Figure 3F). Flow cytometry confirmed higher percentages of CD3+CD56+ NKT cells in the tumors (Figures S7A and S7B), accompanied by higher levels of inhibitory receptors, such as PD-1 and TIGIT (Figure S7C), suggesting the inhibitory status of NKT cells in the TME, potentially affecting their anti-tumor functions.

The CD8A+ cells were separated into 11 clusters, including 2 subpopulations of naive T cells, 3 subpopulations of effector or memory T cells, 1 cluster of mature cytotoxic T cells, 1 cluster of gamma delta-T (γδ-T) cells, 1 cluster of dividing central memory T cells, and 1 population of inhibited effector T cells (Figures 3G and 3H; Table S1). The 2 remaining clusters were an ambiguous CD8+ immune cell cluster and NK cells/XCL+CD8+ NK-like T cells, as recently defined in patients with HBV-associated chronic liver disease.37 Notably, four clusters were more abundant in the tumors, namely CD160+ effector T cells (20.76% of tumor-infiltrating cells), dividing central memory T cells (16.25%), effector T cells (11.39%), and effector memory T cells (11.39%), with an increased presence of effector memory T cells (CD3+CD8+CCR7−CD45RA−), as confirmed by flow cytometry (Figures 3I, S8A, and S8B). In addition, a small population of γδ-T cells expressing TRGV5, TRDC, TRDV1, and TRGC2 was detected (Figures 3I and S8C).

Transcriptional profiling of exhaustion landscape in NK cells and T cells in HCC-PDX-bearing hIL15-humice

Immune cell exhaustion is a common feature of cancer etiology. The overall combined expression of reported genes associated with exhaustion demonstrated an exhaustion gradient across NK cells,38,39 with the lowest expression in the CD56bright cells and the highest in the CD16+ NK cells (Figures 4A and 4B). Among the inhibitory receptors, KLRG1, a marker of senescence, was the most abundant among the inhibited NK cell subpopulations (cluster 2). However, its average expression within the NK cell fraction was 2.74-fold change (FC) lower in the tumor than in the blood samples. Other inhibitory receptors, such as PDCD1 and TIGIT, had higher expression within the inhibited NK cell subpopulation and NKT cells (cluster 7), and the average expression of PDCD1 and TIGIT was 3.97 FC and 1.88 FC higher, respectively, in the inhibited NK cells in the tumor than in the blood samples, suggesting that these would be more specific targets for treatment (Figures 4C and 4D; Table S2). Flow cytometry analysis showed that the tumor cells expressed the ligands for TIGIT and PD-1, such as CD112 and CD155, and PD-L1, respectively (Figure S9A), and the expression levels of PD-1 and TIGIT were upregulated in tumor-infiltrating NK cells compared with those in circulating NK cells (Figure S9B). To further elucidate the transcriptome changes in the HCC-PDX after treatment, bulk RNA-seq was performed (Figure S13). In particular, there were 940 upregulated genes and 305 downregulated genes in HCC-PDX following the combined anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT treatments compared with saline-treated mice. These genes included the downregulation of TKTL1, AFP, and CYP7A1, and the upregulation of MARCO, NNMT, CXCL9, and IL15RA expression (Figures 6D and 6E). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) against hallmark genes highlighted the suppression of fatty acid and bile metabolism, and the enrichment of several cytokine signaling axis, including TNF-α signaling by NF-κB, IL2/STAT5 signaling, and, importantly, the IFN-γ response (Figure 6F). To identify the importance of CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells on IFN-γ production and tumor burden, we depleted these immune cells in PDX-bearing hIL15-humice before administering combination therapy (Figures S14A–S14D). After 8 weeks of treatment, we observed a reduction in plasma IFN-γ levels in mice that had undergone CD56+ NK cell or CD8+ T cell depletion compared with those without immune cell depletion (Figure S14E). Notably, the tumor burden increased significantly after CD56+ NK cells were depleted in the mice (Figures 6G, 6H, and S14F). By ex vivo coculture of fresh tumor cells isolated from HCC-PDX with either tumor-infiltrating CD56+ NK cells or CD8+ T cells, we found that the former was more potent in lysing the tumor cells via necrosis and apoptosis, accompanied by an elevation in their IFN-γ secretion (Figure S15). Taken together, our results suggest that the anti-tumor efficacy of anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT antibodies is dependent on the presence of NK cells.

Discussion

Although sorafenib remains a first-line targeted therapy for patients with advanced HCC, the development of acquired drug resistance may hamper its beneficial outcomes.3,4 Therefore, identifying and validating second-line treatments, including but not limiting to other TKI, anti-angiogenesis drugs, and immunotherapy, are highly warranted.3 In recent decades, various ICIs, such as anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies, have been evaluated in patients with HCC,43 aiming to restore the anti-tumor function of exhausted T cells and improve clinical outcomes. Similarly, NK cells, which possess anti-tumor properties, become exhausted and lose their anti-tumor function under the influence of an immunosuppressive TME.23 However, the understanding of NK cells in tumors remains unclear, and drugs targeting exhausted NK cells are limited, possibly due to a lack of reliable pre-clinical models to simulate the interaction between human cancer cells and NK cells.

In this study, we first established an improved humanized mouse model to restore NK cell reconstitution through a one-time delivery of AAV-hIL15, achieving a percentage of circulating NK cells comparable with that in humans under physiological conditions. In contrast to previous methods of supplementing human IL-15 by injecting recombinant human IL-15 protein,27 plasmid DNA encoding human IL-15,28 or IL-15/IL-15 receptor-alpha into mice,29 the restoration of NK cell reconstitution in our current model was more substantial and persisted for at least 16 weeks post-injection, allowing ample time for subsequent analysis of the interaction between NK cells and tumor cells. Phenotypically, both B-NK cells and PBMC-NK cells expressed CD16, along with other NK cell markers, such as NKG2A, NKG2D, NKp30, and NKp46. Moreover, these NK cells exhibited similar functions, demonstrating cytotoxicity in inducing apoptosis of K-562 cells and an increase in CD107a expression and IFN-γ secretion upon stimulation by their target cells. Notably, a higher percentage of granzyme A-positive and granzyme B-positive cells was found in the circulating CD16+CD56+ NK cell subset in hIL15-humice. In contrast, a higher percentage of granulysin-positive and IFN-γ-positive cells was found in the CD16−CD56+ NK cell subset. Besides, the CD16+CD56+ NK cells were more cytotoxic toward the K-562 cells compared with CD16−CD56+ NK cells, despite the latter secreting more IFN-γ. This underscores a phenotypic and functional distinction between these NK cell subsets, paralleling those found in humans.44 We also investigated the modulation of the NK cell proportion in major organs in hIL15-humice, revealing an increase in the percentage of NK cells in the kidneys, livers, lungs, and spleens, consistent with previous studies.25,28

To investigate whether NK cell infiltration was enhanced in tumors, we transplanted HCC-PDX into hIL15-humice. Our results revealed an increase in both the percentage and number of tumor-infiltrating CD16+CD56+ NK cells and CD16−CD56+ NK cells. In addition, more CD8+ T cells were observed in the PDX compared with the GFP-humice, consistent with findings from a recent study that delivered IL-15 in mouse tumors.30 Previous research has shown a positive correlation between NK cell density in the intratumoral region and the infiltration of CD8+ T cells with survival in HCC patients.18,45 However, these NK and T cells may become exhausted due to the influence of the TME, leading to the loss of their anti-tumor function.23,46 Similar to scRNA-seq data reported from primary HCC samples, our scRNA-seq in the hIL15-humice demonstrated the division of NK cells based on the expression of cytotoxic genes and CD16+ status or CD56+ status, with the CD16+ cells having the highest exhaustion marker expression.47,48 Notably, fewer cytotoxic NK cells were observed in the HCC-PDX, suggesting a loss of cytotoxic activity and an inability to eliminate cancerous cells. In contrast, more tumor-infiltrating CD56brightCD161+ NK cells and NKT cells have been found, where the latter has been shown to promote HCC through interaction with hepatocytes.49,50 Remarkably, two distinct subsets of NKT cells, namely type I and type II NKT cells, exhibit different modes of antigen recognition, thus potentially playing varied roles in inflammatory liver diseases and cancer progression.50 Further analysis of different NKT cell subsets is warranted to define their roles in liver cancers.

T cells also demonstrated an exhaustion marker gradient, with cluster profiles similar to those found in scRNA-seq data from patients with HCC.37,47 Interestingly, we identified a distinct NK-like CD8+ T cell population in our samples. A recent study revealed that these NK-like effector T cells were significantly increased in patients with HBV-associated chronic liver disease37; however, their role in cancer progression remains unclear and warrants further investigation. In addition, the proportion of mature cytotoxic T cells was lower in the PDX than in the blood samples, consistent with previous studies demonstrating the downregulation of transcripts associated with cytotoxicity in HCC.47 Not only were the proportions of NK cells and T cells different within the tumor, but the TME also influenced NK cell and T cell exhaustion. Intriguingly, exhaustion marker expression varied among different NK and T cell populations, with a notable increase in the expression of both PDCD1 and TIGIT in inhibited NK cells and T cells within the tumor compared with the blood, suggesting that these markers could be specific targets for treatment.

In our comparative analysis using specific HCC patient data from scRNA-seq datasets GSE151530 and GSE98638, we have demonstrated a high degree of alignment between the CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells in our humanized mouse model and those from human samples. Key inhibitory receptors such as KLRG1 and TIGIT, which play crucial roles in modulating immune responses in the TME, showed similar expression patterns across the datasets. Interestingly, PDCD1 was minimally expressed in CD56+ NK cells in the abovementioned human dataset, despite evidence from other studies showing that PD-1 is expressed on tumor-infiltrating NK cells in HCC patients.51 These findings collectively affirm the accuracy of our model in reflecting human immune dynamics.

Despite initial success, a significant proportion of HCC patients still experience primary or secondary resistance to ICI therapy.52 Various factors, such as the immunosuppressive TME, lack of antigen expression and impaired antigen presentation, tumor heterogeneity, and gut microbiota, have been proposed as possible contributors to ICI resistance.53 Therefore, exploring the mechanisms of ICI resistance and developing novel combination therapies to overcome resistance are crucial. In light of the high expression of PD-1 and TIGIT on CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells in the HCC-PDX, we evaluated the anti-tumor efficacy of anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT antibodies in our hIL15-humice. This combination therapy led to a reduction in tumor burden using the PDX established from three independent donors. Notably, the tumor-infiltrating CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells isolated from the group treated with combination therapy were more potent in lysing the tumor cells via necrosis and apoptosis, accompanied by an elevation in their IFN-γ secretion. This suggests that the function of these tumor-infiltrating immune cells was partially restored compared with those from the saline-treated group. Clinically, the evaluation of the efficacy and safety of this combination has been conducted, although only a few results are currently available.54 Our hIL15-humice model offers a robust pre-clinical platform for assessing the efficacy, safety, and mechanisms of this combination, complementing ongoing clinical evaluations. Besides, our bulk RNA-seq analysis revealed significant alterations in the tumor transcriptome following combination treatment, including the induction of CXCL9, a gene associated with better survival rates in HCC patients after treatment. Mechanistically, CXCL9 could be secreted by myeloid cells within the TME, recruiting activated NK cells and T cells expressing the CXCR3 receptor to enhance their cytotoxicity.55 This suggests that the combined treatment may increase NK cell and T cell infiltration into the tumor and play a crucial role in responses to ICI therapy. However, the combination therapy also upregulated genes associated with poor HCC prognosis or treatment resistance, such as CD44 and MMP12. CD44 is implicated in the regulation of multiple signaling pathways that modulate cancer proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and therapy resistance.56 MMP12 expression is positively correlated with Foxp3+ regulatory T cell infiltration in HCC patients, promoting tumor progression and immune evasion.57 These findings suggest that these genes and their associated pathways may contribute to the development of secondary ICI resistance and warrant further investigation. GSEA highlighted the enrichment of numerous cytokine and chemokine signaling pathways, with the greatest impact on IFN-γ. Depletion of CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells in the HCC-PDX-bearing hIL15-humice further confirmed the importance of CD56+ NK cell infiltration in better treatment outcomes, as mice depleted of CD56+ NK cells showed a significant increase in tumor burden, while depletion of CD8+ T cells did not have the same effect. Our ex vivo data from coculture assays revealed that the CD56+ NK cells were more potent in lysing tumor cells from HCC-PDX by inducing necrosis and apoptosis, which was accompanied by an increase in IFN-γ release. Concurrence studies have shown significantly lower IFN-γ production and fewer NK cells in patients with poorer outcome, suggesting that NK cell infiltration is important for better treatment outcomes.58,59 These findings underscore the crucial role of NK cells in mediating anti-tumor response to ICI therapy.

In addition to CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells, other immune cell subsets were also modulated after combined treatment with anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT antibodies. Notably, the induction of MARCO expression was observed based on our bulk RNA-seq data. Macrophage receptor with collagenous structure (MARCO)+ macrophages are a subset of protumoral tumor-associated macrophages correlated with an increase in regulatory T cells, and a decrease in NK cells in tumors, leading to poorer clinical outcomes and resistance to therapy.60,61 Targeting MACRO with antibodies may further restore the cytolytic activity and anti-tumor function of NK cells and T cells.

In conclusion, our improved humanized mouse HCC-PDX model, coupled with scRNA-seq data, reveals that tumor-infiltrating NK cells exhibited elevated levels of inhibitory receptors, such as PD-1 and TIGIT. These markers can be effectively targeted using their respective antibodies to rejuvenate NK cell cytotoxicity, thereby enhancing the anti-tumor response. Importantly, our model acts as a crucial bridge between pre-clinical and clinical evaluations, offering valuable insights into potential strategies for developing more effective combination therapies to treat HCC and overcome ICI resistance.

Materials and methods

Isolation of human CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells

Human umbilical cord blood was collected from KK Women’s and Children’s Hospital (KKH), Singapore, and written consent was obtained from the guardians of donors in strict accordance with the institutional ethical guidelines of KKH. The SingHealth and National Healthcare Group Research Ethics Committees of Singapore specifically approved this study (IRB number 2020-040).

Leukocytes were enriched by density centrifugation with Lymphoprep (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) and red blood cells (RBCs) were lysed using EasySep RBC lysis buffer (STEMCELL Technologies). Thereafter, human CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) were isolated using the EasySep human cord blood CD34 positive selection kit (STEMCELL Technologies), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Generation of humanized mice

NOD-scid IL2Rγ−/− (NSG) pups were sub-lethally irradiated at 1 Gy, followed by intra-hepatic injection of human CD34+ HSCs (2 × 105 cells per pup). After 10–12 weeks, blood samples were collected from the mice to determine human immune cell engraftment (chimerism) via flow cytometry. Chimerism was defined as (% human CD45+ cells/(% human CD45+ cells + % mouse CD45+ cells)). Humanized mice with chimerism between 10% and 50% were allocated for downstream experiments.

Mice were bred and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Biological Resource Center (BRC) of the Agency for Science, Technology and Research (A∗STAR), Singapore. All animal experiments conformed to the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines, and were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes, which are released by the National Advisory Committee on Laboratory Animal Research, Agri-Food & Veterinary Authority of Singapore, and the International Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at A∗STAR. The IACUC specifically approved this study (IACUC numbers 221686 and 221738).

Cell culture

Human 293AAV cells (Cell Biolabs, San Diego, CA) were cultured in complete Dulbecco’s modified minimal essential medium (DMEM) (Cytiva Life Sciences, Marlborough, MA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Grand Island, NY). Human erythroleukemic K-562 cells (American Type Culture Collection [ATCC], Manassas, VA) were cultured in complete Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI 1640) medium (Cytiva Life Sciences) containing 10% FBS. All cell lines were maintained in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37°C and passaged, whenever necessary, using aseptic techniques.

AAV production

The AAV-DJ helper free bicistronic expression system (GFP) was purchased from Cell Biolabs. The coding sequence of human IL-15 was cloned into pAAV-IRES-GFP vector.

AAV-DJ-GFP (AAV-GFP) and AAV-DJ-hIL15-GFP (AAV-hIL15) were generated using a standard triple transfection method. One day before transfection, AAV293 cells were seeded in a T-175 flask. On the day of transfection, transgene vectors, pAAV-DJ vector and pHelper vector, were added to the cells, supplemented with TransIT-VirusGEN transfection reagent (Mirus Bio, Madison, WI). The viral supernatant was collected 3 days post-infection and filtered through a 0.45 μm cell strainer (Sartorius, Germany). To determine infectious titer, 293AAV cells (in 100 μL of DMEM) were seeded in a 96-well plate, and 100 μL of viral supernatant supplemented with polybrene (8 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO) was added. One infectious unit per mL (IU/mL) is defined as one GFP+ cell observed under a fluorescence microscope per mL viral supernatant added to the 293AAV cells.

ELISA assay for human IL-15 and IFN-γ

Humanized mice were intravenously injected with AAV-GFP or AAV-hIL15. Blood samples were collected into EDTA tubes (Greiner, Bio-One, Monroe, NC) via cheek bleeding at the indicated time points. Human IL-15 levels in plasma were determined using a LEGEND MAX Human IL-15 ELISA Kit (BioLegend, San Diego, CA). The absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a Hidex Sense microplate reader (Hidex Oy, Finland).

To determine IFN-γ release, CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells were isolated from human peripheral blood (normal donor; ATCC), and blood samples and HCC-PDX from hIL15-humice using CD56 MicroBeads, Human (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and CD8 MicroBeads, Human (Miltenyi Biotec), respectively. A circulating CD45+CD3−CD16+CD56+ NK cell subset and a CD45+CD3−CD16−CD56+ NK cell subset from hIL15-humice were isolated using FACS (BD FACSymphony S6 sorter; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). The same number of isolated immune cells (effector cells; 5 × 104 cells/well) were cocultured with K-562 cells or fresh tumor cells from HCC-PDX in NSG mice at an effector-to-target cell ratio (E:T ratio) of 1:1 at 37°C for 6 h. The release of IFN-γ was quantified using a LEGEND MAX Human IFN-γ ELISA Kit (BioLegend) and the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a Hidex Sense microplate reader.

Isolation of mononuclear cells from blood samples, tumors, and various organs

Blood samples from humanized mice were collected into EDTA tubes via cheek bleeding at the indicated time point. At the experimental endpoint, blood samples, tumors, and organs including kidneys, livers, lungs, and spleens were harvested.

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes were isolated from tumors digested into single-cell suspensions using a Tumor Dissociation Kit, Human (Miltenyi Biotec), with a gentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec). Mononuclear cells (MNCs) from kidneys, livers, and lungs were isolated by digesting these organs with collagenase IV (0.8 mg/mL, Worthington Biochemical, Lakewood, NJ), supplemented with DNAse I (1 μg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) in RPMI 1640 medium, and incubated in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37°C for 1 h. Spleen MNCs were isolated by meshing tissues in RPMI 1640 medium. All cells were filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) prior to downstream analysis.

Flow cytometric analysis

Before surface marker staining, cells were stained with a dye provided in a LIVE/DEAD Fixable Blue Dead Cell Stain kit (Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature for 15 min. NK cell subsets, isolated via FACS, were fixed and permeabilized with the provided fixation/permeabilization solution for 30 min. After incubation and centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in the supplied perm/wash buffer and incubated for an additional 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were stained with respective PE-conjugated antibodies in perm/wash buffer for 30 min prior to flow cytometric analysis.

Flow cytometric analysis was performed using an LSRFortessa X-20 flow cytometer with FACSDiva software (Becton Dickinson, Sparks, MD, USA). One hundred thousand events were collected per sample and analyzed using Flowjo software version 10 (Treestar, Ashland, OR).

Quantification of tumor-infiltrating NK cells and T cells

HCC-PDX were harvested from GFP-humice and hIL15-humice. The tumors were digested into single-cell suspensions as described above, and human CD45+ immune cells were isolated using CD45 (TIL) MicroBeads, human (Miltenyi Biotec). The number of viable cells was determined using a trypan blue solution (Gibco). The number of tumor-infiltrating NK cells and T cells was subsequently quantified based on their respective percentages in tumors, determined by flow cytometry analysis.

Lactate dehydrogenase release assay

CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells were isolated from human PBMCs, and from blood samples and HCC-PDX in hIL15-humice using CD56 MicroBeads, Human and CD8 Microbeads, Human, respectively. Specific NK cell subsets (CD45+CD3−CD16+CD56+ NK cells and CD45+CD3−CD16−CD56+ NK cells) were isolated from blood samples in hIL15-humice by FACS. To examine immune-cell cytotoxicity on target cells, a CytoTox 96 Non-radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Promega, Madison, WI) was used. NK cells (5 × 104 cells/well) or T cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were cocultured with K-562 cells or fresh tumor cells isolated from HCC-PDX in NSG mice at the indicated E:T ratio, or at a ratio of 1:1, at 37°C for 6 h. After incubation, 50 μL of supernatant was incubated with 50 μL of substrate solution at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance at 490 nm was measured using a Hidex Sense microplate reader. The percentage of cytotoxicity was calculated using the following formula: % cytotoxicity = corrected reading from test well/corrected maximum release of lactate dehydrogenase control.

Cell death detection assay

Induction of necrosis and apoptosis was examined using a Cell Death Detection ELISAPLUS Kit (Sigma-Aldrich). CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells were isolated as described above and cocultured with K-562 cells or fresh tumor cells isolated from HCC-PDX in NSG mice at an E:T ratio of 1:1 at 37°C for 6 h. After incubation, supernatant (for apoptosis detection) and/or lysate (for necrosis detection) were transferred to a streptavidin-coated 96-well microtiter plate, and the absorbance at 405 nm was recorded using a Hidex Sense microplate reader. The extent of necrosis and apoptosis was expressed as an enrichment factor, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, which was calculated by the following formula: enrichment factor = absorbance of the well with immune cells and cancer cells/absorbance of the well with immune cells or cancer cells only.

Cytotoxic granules release from NK cells

CD56+ NK cells were isolated from human PBMC and blood samples in hIL15-humice as described above. The NK cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were then cocultured with K-562 cells at an E:T ratio of 1:1. CD107a antibody was added to each well and incubated at 37°C for 6 h. After incubation, the cells were washed with FACS buffer and stained with anti-human CD16, CD45, and CD56 antibodies at 4°C for 15 min prior to flow cytometric analysis.

Immunohistochemical staining

Humanized mice were intravenously injected with AAV-GFP or AAV-hIL15. After 16 weeks of injection, kidneys, livers, lungs, and spleens were harvested. The samples were formalin-fixed (Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature for 1 day, paraffin-embedded (Leica, Germany), and sliced into 5 μm sections. For the detection of NK cells, the samples were stained for human NCR1 (ab224703, Abcam, Cambridge, MA), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Stained images were captured using a ZEN fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany) with ZEN 2 acquisition software (Zen Blue Version, Zeiss).

Preparation of single-cell suspensions from blood, spleen, and tumor samples for scRNA-seq

Humanized mice were intravenously injected with AAV-hIL15 before HCC-PDX were transplanted into the mice. Eight weeks post-transplant, blood samples, spleens, and tumors were harvested. These samples were processed as described above to generate single-cell suspensions. After that, the cells were subjected to dead cell removal and mouse cell depletion, using the Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) and Mouse Cell Depletion Kit (Miltenyi Biotec), respectively. Viable human CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells were then isolated using CD56 MicroBeads, Human (Miltenyi Biotec), and CD8 MicroBeads, Human (Miltenyi Biotec), respectively. Subsequently, the cells were resuspended in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline without calcium and magnesium ions (STEMCELL Technologies) at a density of 1,200 cells/μL, then processed for library construction using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ Reagent kit (version 3.1; 10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Computational analysis of scRNA-seq data, quality control, and cell selection

Following the subjection of paired-end sequencing reads to the Cell Ranger (version 2.02; 10x Genomics pipeline), quality control and cell selection were performed in R using the standard pre-processing workflow in Seurat (version 4.3.0) with some modifications. Data from blood, spleen, and tumor samples were merged into one Seurat Object. Cells with less than 250 or more than 5,500 genes were excluded. The maximum number of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) was set at 30,000, and cells were required to have a log10 genes per UMI score of 0.8 or higher to address cell complexity. The mitochondrial ratio was restricted to <0.1. A total of 29,283 cells and 18,467 genes were used in the study. At the gene level, only genes expressed in 10 or more cells were included in the analysis.

Data normalization and integration

The merged data file was subsequently split into individual samples to ensure that sample-related variation would be included. Data normalization and integration were performed using SCT transform with 5,000 variable features. The mitochondrial ratio and G2M.Score were regressed out during normalization. The samples were then integrated, and PCA analysis was conducted.

Cell clustering and visualization

Cell clustering was carried out using the FindCluster function based on a shared nearest neighbor modularity optimization on the top 18 principal components. The resolution was set at 0.5, determined computationally. UMAP were plotted, colored by sample or the featured CD8A or NCAM1 (the gene encoding CD56).

Subclustering and visualization

Subclustering was done based on the separation by microbeads prior to sequencing analysis using CD56, and then CD8 on the remaining cells. The same process was completed in the sequencing analysis. The CellSelector tool in Seurat was utilized to identify and subset the dataset by the NCAM1+ and CD8A+ status. Data normalization and integration steps were repeated for each subset. The NCAM1+ and CD8A+ subsets utilized the top 12 and 17 principal components, respectively. Both subsets were visualized at a resolution of 0.4.

Key marker genes of each cluster were depicted using dot plots, feature plots, or violin plots. Exhaustion markers variation across cells was assessed using the AddModuleScore function, focusing on transcripts associated with exhaustion in the literature,38,39,40 including KLRG1, TIGIT, PDCD1, LAG3, HAVCR2, CTLA4, FAS, CD244, CD160, NR4A1, TOX, TOX2, EOMES, PRDM1, DNMT1, and GZMB, visualized as feature and violin plots.

Differentially expressed genes

Differentially expressed genes were detected using Wilcoxon rank sum tests incorporated in the FindConservedMarker and FindMarker functions in Seurat. The top 5 cluster-specific transcripts with a log2-fold change >1 or <−1 from each cluster were represented in a heatmap using the ComplexHeatmap package in R. Differential expression analysis was performed to identify genes significantly upregulated and downregulated in each cluster compared with all other cells (false discovery rate adjusted p < 0.05), as well as genes differentially expressed between blood, spleen, and tumor samples.

Comparison with NK cells and T cells in HCC patients using external scRNA-seq datasets

NK cell and T cell scRNA-seq datasets for HCC patients were obtained from GSE151530 and GSE98638, respectively.7,41 NK cells from the dataset were extracted from the “unclassified” cluster and filtered for positive expression of either NCAM1 or KLRF1 (count > 0) and no expression (count = 0) for markers associated with B cells (CD19, MS4A1), myeloid cells (LYZ, C1QA, CD68), epithelial cells (EPCAM, KRT18, KRT19), and T/NKT cells (CD3D, CD3E, CD3G) according to the strategy as described elsewhere.34 CD8+ T cells were extracted from the dataset based on provided annotations.7 Collectively, these filters yielded 470 NK cells and 1,579 T cells. Louvain clustering of NK cells was further conducted (dims = 10, resolution = 0.4) and cluster annotations assigned by examining differentially expressed genes of each cluster. Original CD8+ T cell cluster annotations from the study were preserved. To compare these cells with our data, we created a reference UMAP of NK cells and T cells from our study using “return.model = TRUE” in the RunUMAP() function of Seurat. NK cells and T cells from the external datasets were then mapped onto the reference UMAP using FIndTransferAnchors() and MapQuery() functions in Seurat 5.0.0.

In vivo immunotherapy and ex vivo stimulation assays

To evaluate the anti-tumor efficacy of ICI, humanized mice were first intravenously injected with AAV-hIL15. When indicated, depletion of CD56+ NK cells or CD8+ T cells was achieved by intravenous injection of ultra-LEAF purified anti-human CD56 (NCAM) antibody (clone MEM-188, BioLegend; 50 μg/mouse) and ultra-LEAF purified anti-human CD8a Antibody (clone RPA-T8, BioLegend; 50 μg/mouse), respectively, prior to HCC-PDX transplantation, followed by bi-weekly injections until the experimental endpoint.16

After tumor transplantation, tumor size was measured weekly and the volume of the tumors was calculated by using the formula: tumor volume = (length × width2)/2, where length represents the longest tumor diameter and width represents the perpendicular tumor diameter.62 Upon reaching a size between 3 and 5 mm in one dimension, mice were intravenously injected with saline, anti-PD-1, and/or anti-TIGIT antibodies (5 mg/kg mouse body weight) bi-weekly for a total of 8 weeks. Following treatment, tumors were harvested, and tumor weights were measured. In our study, humanized mice were randomly assigned to different treatment groups, with no exclusions due to humane endpoints. Sample sizes were estimated by our pilot study with a sample size calculator (confidence level = 0.05, power = 80%) and researchers were not blinded to the treatments.

For ex vivo stimulation assays, tumor-infiltrating CD56+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells were isolated from mice treated with saline or anti-PD-1 plus anti-TIGIT antibodies, using CD56 MicroBeads, Human, and CD8 MicroBeads, Human, respectively. Subsequently, purified CD56+ NK cells (5 × 104 cells/well) and CD8+ T cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were stimulated with poly(I:C) (Sigma-Aldrich) for NK cells or T cell TransAct (Miltenyi Biotec) for T cells, over 2 days. Alternatively, these immune cells were stimulated with PMA (50 ng/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) and ionomycin (500 ng/mL; Sigma-Aldrich). The supernatant was collected after incubation, and human IFN-γ levels in the cell-free supernatant were determined using a LEGEND MAX Human IFN-γ ELISA Kit.

Cytokines quantification

Cytokine levels in plasma from humanized mice were quantified using a LEGENDplex Human CD8/NK Panel (BioLegend) and analyzed by flow cytometry and LEGENDplex data analysis software (BioLegend).

Bulk RNA-seq and data analysis

Bulk RNA-seq was performed on tumor samples from hIL15-humice treated with saline, and anti-PD-1 and/or anti-TIGIT antibodies. RNA was extracted by TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA of each sample was then quantified and qualified by NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA). Next, 1 μg of total RNA was used for library preparations according to the manufacturer’s protocol (VAHTS mRNA-seq V3 Library Prep Kit for Illumina). The poly(A) mRNA isolation was performed using the Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module. mRNA fragmentation and priming were performed using First-Strand Synthesis Reaction Buffer and Random Primers, where first- and second-strand cDNAs were synthesized using ProtoScript II Reverse Transcriptase and Second Strand Synthesis Enzyme Mix, respectively. The double-stranded cDNA was purified by beads and further treated with End Prep Enzyme Mix. Size selection of adaptor-ligated DNA was then performed using beads, and fragments of approximately 400 bp in length (insert size around 300 bp) were recovered. The samples were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using P5 and P7 primers. Subsequently, PCR products were cleaned up using beads, validated using a Qsep100 (Bioptic, Taiwan), and quantified using a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Libraries with different indices were multiplexed and loaded on an Illumina Novaseq instrument according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Sequencing was performed using a 2 × 150 paired-end configuration. Image analysis and base calling were conducted using NovaSeq Control Software + OLB + GAPipeline-1.6 (Illumina) on the NovaSeq instrument. Following quality control and adapter sequence removal using Cutadapt, the sequences were first aligned to grcm38 mouse genome using HISAT2. Subsequently, those unaligned sequences were aligned to the grch38 human genome, and the reads were counted using featurecounts. Genes with transcripts per million of three or above, in at least two of the three samples in a treatment group, were included in the analysis. Differentially expressed genes were defined based on the above criteria, with Benjamini-Hochberg corrected p values ≤ 0.05 and log2 fold change ≥2 or ≤−2.

Differentially expressed genes were plotted as a heatmap using ComplexHeatmap and EnhancedVolcano packages. To identify the pathways that were differentially regulated between the mice treated with saline or anti-PD-1 plus anti-TIGIT antibodies, fast gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the hallmark dataset from MSigDB.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA) and data are expressed as mean ± standard error of mean. The normality of distribution for all datasets was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, analysis was conducted using either the paired or unpaired Student’s t test, Mann-Whitney test, or Wilcoxon test as appropriate. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, or the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
