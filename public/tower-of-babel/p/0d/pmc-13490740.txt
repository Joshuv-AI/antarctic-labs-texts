Pre-operative neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio significantly predicts local recurrence in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma
Authors: Alwin Puthiyakunnel Saji, Bhamini Vadhwana, Manaal Bharadia, Madhava Pai, Duncan Spalding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgrounds/AimsRecurrence rates in patients with resected pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remain high, posing a significant clinical challenge. Pre-operative neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) has shown promise as a prognostic marker in various cancers. NLR’s role in PDAC recurrence has not been widely explored. This study aims to investigate the effect of pre-operative NLR on post-operative PDAC recurrence.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on patients who underwent surgery with curative intent for PDAC between 2015 and 2024. Patients were divided into two groups (high and low) using an NLR threshold of 3. Primary outcome was disease recurrence. The predictive value of NLR was compared using receiver operating characteristic curves and univariable and multivariable Cox regression models.Results125 Patients were included; high NLR group n = 53 and low NLR group n = 72. Both groups were well balanced across all baseline characteristics. NLR is a strong predictor of recurrence compared to platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) (AUC: NLR 0.716 vs. PLR 0.593, p = 0.022). The high NLR group had a significantly greater recurrence rate compared to the low NLR group (62.3% vs. 31.9%, p = 0.002) and a significantly greater local recurrence rate (43.4% vs. 19.4%, p = 0.004). A high NLR remains a significant negative prognosticator for recurrence in univariable (hazard ratio [HR] 2.540, p < 0.001) and multivariable analysis (HR 2.817, p < 0.001).ConclusionsA high pre-operative NLR (> 3) was associated with significantly greater risk of recurrence, especially local recurrence. NLR may be used within risk stratification models to identify patients at risk of PDAC recurrence.Keywords: Pancreatic ductal carcinoma, Neoplasm recurrence, Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is an aggressive malignancy and the sixth leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1,2]. Median overall survival remains low, at approximately 14–16 months in resectable disease, and is even lower in those with locally advanced or metastatic disease [3,4]. Poor overall and recurrence free survival is primarily associated with late diagnosis and high recurrence rates of up to 80% [5,6]. Currently, upfront surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy remain the only potentially curative options for these patients to improve survival [7].

Despite these evidence-based treatment strategies, recurrence rates remain high at 80% in patients with resected PDAC, with metastatic recurrence being the most common [8]. Median recurrence free survival is very low ranging between 11.6 to 17.4 months [8-10]. Therapeutic options for recurrence are limited to palliative systemic therapy which offer limited survival benefit [11]. Consequently, there is a need to identify factors that can predict recurrence. Currently, recurrence is predicted using post-operative histological factors such as resection margin involvement, greater tumor differentiation, increased nodal disease, and vascular involvement. These factors, however, cannot be used pre-operatively for risk stratification [12]. Pre-operative inflammatory markers, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), have been shown to be valuable prognostic indicators in various cancers, including non-small cell lung cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma [13,14]. The association between a poor cancer prognosis and a pro-inflammatory state is well established pathophysiologically [15].

A high NLR, reflecting a pro-inflammatory state, corresponds to a high neutrophil count and low lymphocyte count. Other inflammatory biomarkers, such as the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) and C-reactive protein-to-albumin (CRP/Alb) ratio, have also been investigated in PDAC patients and shown to predict survival. However, due to little consistency in results, these markers have not been clinically translatable [16-18]. A high NLR (> 3 and > 5) has been associated with worse overall survival in PDAC, with a significantly lower median survival of 10.2–13 months compared to 24.9–32.4 months in the low NLR group [16,19]. However, the role of NLR in predicting recurrence following potentially curative resection for PDAC has not been explored. This study’s primary aim is to investigate the correlation of NLR with postoperative recurrence in PDAC patients undergoing curative-intent surgery, directly comparing it to PLR, CRP/Alb, and CA19-9.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Design

This retrospective study included all patients who underwent curative-intent surgery for PDAC by two surgeons (DS and MP) at a tertiary Hepatopancreaticobiliary unit between 2015 and 2024. Patients were excluded if histology did not confirm PDAC, if intraoperative assessment revealed an unresectable tumor or a palliative procedure was performed, if there was evidence of metastases, or if key patient characteristics or outcome data were unavailable. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, the use of non-identifiable patient data, absence of any experimental intervention other than that of standard of care, ethical approval from an ethics committee was not required.

Patients with tumors in the head, neck, or uncinate process of the pancreas underwent a Whipple’s procedure or a pylorus-preserving pancreaticoduodenectomy (PPPD). Those with tumors in the body or tail of the pancreas underwent a distal pancreatectomy with splenectomy. In selected cases, multi-visceral and/or venous vascular resections were performed with the aim of achieving an R0 resection. All treatment plans were discussed in a multi-disciplinary team meeting to ensure standardized treatment in accordance with updated guidelines and relevant clinical trials. Baseline characteristics, including patient demographics, tumor staging, perioperative details, histological results, and outcome data, were collected from electronic CERNER patient records and added to a prospectively maintained datasheet.

Pre-operative peripheral blood counts were taken from the latest blood test prior to elective admission for surgery. Neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, platelet count, CRP, albumin and CA19-9 tumour marker levels were collected. This allowed for calculations of NLR, PLR and CRP/Alb ratio.

Patients were divided into two groups: high NLR and low NLR, using a threshold ratio of 3 according to literature findings [19]. The NLR ratio was devised by dividing the absolute neutrophil count (×109/L) by the absolute lymphocyte count (×109/L). There is substantial variability in the range of threshold values used in the current literature, ranging from 2.5 to 8, with 3 being the most commonly used [20-23]. Despite this, there is little evidence to explain such variability. Therefore, we performed multiple receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses, which revealed that a 2.9 NLR threshold had the highest Youden’s Index (sensitivity + specificity – 1). Since no patient had an NLR between 2.9 and 3, 3 was selected as the threshold. To further validate the results, outcome analyses were conducted across various NLR thresholds.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was overall recurrence rate defined as return of PDAC at any site detected radiologically or histologically. This included either:

(i) Local recurrence rate defined as return of PDAC to original site of cancer detected radiologically or via histopathological analysis.

(ii) Metastatic recurrence rate defined as presence of metastatic disease in other visceral or lymphatic tissue distant to the original site of disease.

Secondary outcomes included:

(i) Comparison of predictive ability of NLR to other inflammatory and cancer biomarkers such as PLR, CRP/Alb and CA19-9.

Statistical analysis

Continuous patient demographic variables were compared between the two groups using the Mann-Whitney U test, and dichotomous variables were compared using the Chi-squared test. Normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Kaplan–Meier curves were created to compare the overall recurrence rate outcome across the two groups. Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to assess associations between clinicopathological variables and recurrence-free survival. Variables significantly associated with recurrence-free survival on univariable analysis were entered into a multivariable Cox regression model. Results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. ROC analysis and the area under the curves (AUC) of NLR, PLR, and CA19-9 were compared using De-Long’s test. SPSS version 29 was used for statistical analysis. All figures were generated using R Studio version 2024.09.1 + 394 (Posit Software, PBC).

Using various NLR thresholds demonstrates significantly increased recurrence rates associated with high NLR

There is significant variability in NLR thresholds reported in the current literature, with no consensus on an optimal threshold. We aimed to identify the outcomes and reproducibility of NLR testing using various thresholds (NLR 2.5–5), as summarized in Table 2. Across all thresholds, overall recurrence remained significantly increased in the high NLR group. Fig. 2D illustrates how the difference in recurrence rates between the two groups changes with the NLR threshold. Local recurrence was significantly higher at an NLR threshold of 2.5. (40.0% vs. 14.0%, p = 0.002) and NLR threshold 3.5 (41.5% vs. 23.8%, p = 0.042). The remaining tested thresholds show a trend toward greater local recurrence. Metastatic recurrence, previously non-significant using an NLR threshold of 3.0, became statistically significant using an NLR threshold of 2.5 (30.7% vs. 14.0%, p = 0.033).

High NLR group remains a significant negative prognosticator for recurrence in both univariable and multivariable analysis

Nine variables were significant on univariable analysis: Performance status 2 vs. 0 (HR 2.649, p = 0.049), peripancreatic fat invasion (HR 2.520, p < 0.001), vascular invasion (HR 2.342, p = 0.006), adjuvant therapy (HR 3.396, p = 0.009), pT3 vs. pT1 (HR 4.461, p = 0.044), pT4 vs. pT1 (HR 5.012, p = 0.032), pN1 vs. pN0 (HR 2.087, p = 0.017), albumin (HR 0.941, p = 0.003) and high NLR group vs. low NLR group (HR 2.540, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

A total of two variables were significant in multivariable analysis (Table 3). High NLR vs. low NLR (HR 2.817, p < 0.001) and performance status 2 vs. 0 (HR 4.762, p = 0.006). On multivariable Cox regression, a high NLR remained independently associated with shorter recurrence-free survival, even after adjusting for performance status, pT stage, pN stage, peripancreatic fat invasion, vascular invasion, and adjuvant therapy.

Patients in the high NLR group had a significantly increased hazard of recurrence compared with those in the low NLR group, with an adjusted HR of 2.817 (95% CI 1.595–4.974, p < 0.001). This suggests that a high preoperative NLR is an independent predictor of recurrence, even after accounting for key clinicopathological and treatment-related factors.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that a high NLR (threshold > 3) is significantly associated with a greater risk of recurrence, particularly local recurrence, in patients who underwent potentially curative surgical resection. There was also a non-significant trend towards higher rates of metastatic recurrence. NLR has the potential to be a simple, cost-effective, and easily accessible tool for pre-operative recurrence risk stratification in patients undergoing potentially curative surgery for PDAC.

The findings of this study contribute to the growing literature on NLR in PDAC. While a correlation between high NLR and adverse survival outcomes is evident, the underlying pathophysiology, though not well defined, is subject to several hypotheses. Neutrophils are known to play a role in tumorigenesis, both locally and systemically [24]. At a local level, neutrophils induce epithelial damage leading to tumour promoting inflammation, leading to a sustained proliferation by immune cell infiltration and release of cytokines and interleukins [24]. Neutrophils are also known to secrete pro-angiogenic factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor and hepatocyte growth factor, which promote tumor development [25,26]. There is growing evidence suggesting that neutrophils form pre-metastatic niches facilitating the formation of metastases [27]. In contrast, an increased number of tumour infiltrating lymphocytes is associated with a good prognosis in PDAC patients [28]. Lymphocytes play an active role in harnessing the adaptive immune system to destroy cancer cells with their cytotoxic potential [29]. In patients with a high NLR, low lymphocyte levels may reflect the aggressive nature of the tumor and its susceptibility to recurrence. A significantly higher CRP and low albumin further support a pro-inflammatory state in these patients. The Glasgow prognostic score (GPS), which combines CRP and albumin, has been shown to be an effective prognostic tool for predicting survival and response to chemotherapy in colorectal, pancreatic, and liver cancer [30]. NLR showed a superior AUC than CRP/Alb in this cohort, although only 80 (64.0%) patients had a preoperative CRP level. A moderate AUC of 0.679 may be the result of a smaller sample size, considering a positive GPS showcased in other studies [30].

Comparisons between NLR and other biomarkers, particularly CA19-9 and CRP/Alb ratio, should be interpreted with caution due to substantial missing data for these variables. Although NLR appeared to perform favourably in this cohort, incomplete biomarker availability may have influenced comparative analyses and limits definitive conclusions regarding its superiority. Further validation in cohorts with complete biomarker data is required.

Neoadjuvant therapy is not currently offered routinely to patients, except for those with borderline resectable disease. Systemic therapy is better tolerated pre-operatively, with increased completion rates. Prolonged recovery following surgery may preclude patients from receiving adjuvant therapy [31], which has the potential to offer explicit benefits for high NLR patients. There may be an argument that neoadjuvant therapy could be beneficial in high NLR patients by addressing the aggressive systemic component of PDAC, thus potentially improving recurrence rates following surgical resection. This needs to be tested and validated through vigorous clinical trials, and the incorporation of NLR into these studies may help in consolidating the findings seen in this study. Adjuvant therapy was included in the multivariable Cox regression model given the significant difference in treatment rates between NLR groups and its established prognostic relevance in PDAC. Although in univariable analysis, adjuvant therapy was significant, after adjustment for adjuvant therapy alongside other clinicopathological factors, it became non-significant, whilst high NLR remained significantly associated with shorter recurrence-free survival, with an adjusted HR of 2.817 (95% CI 1.595–4.974, p < 0.001). This supports the independent predictive value of pre-operative NLR for recurrence, irrespective of differences in adjuvant treatment receipt.

Literature shows substantial variability in the threshold used to define high and low NLR groups, with values ranging from 2 to 5. A threshold of 3 is most common, but this variability makes comparisons between studies challenging [16,32-34]. To test the robustness of our chosen threshold and those used in the literature, we assessed outcomes against various NLR thresholds. This consistently demonstrated that a high NLR is significantly associated with higher recurrence rates. This finding should be considered in future studies assessing the role of NLR in cancer prognosis to facilitate better inter-study comparisons.

Pre-operative blood tests were selected from the closest available pre-operative sample to best reflect the inflammatory state of patients at the time of resection. Both groups had similar median times for pre-operative blood tests (10 vs. 9 days, p = 0.126). Inflammatory markers can be affected by a multitude of factors, including infection, biliary obstruction, and cholangitis. Although there were no significant differences between the two groups in jaundiced or biliary stent rates, future studies should externally validate these findings and seek to establish a consensus regarding the optimal timing of pre-operative blood sampling. This is particularly important given that inflammatory indices may be influenced by factors such as intercurrent infection, biliary obstruction, biliary stenting, cholangitis, or recent instrumentation. Standardizing the timing and clinical context of blood tests would improve comparability between studies and strengthen the interpretation of NLR as a prognostic biomarker.

PLR, a marker of systemic inflammation, was significantly associated with increased recurrence in several studies [16,35,36]. In this study, PLR had a low discriminatory effect in predicting recurrence and was overshadowed by the results of NLR. Unlike NLR, which showed distinct discriminatory groups, PLR appears to be more effective as a linear scale. A combination of inflammatory markers, largely NLR based on our findings, could be used to create a predictive algorithm and risk scoring system to guide clinical decision-making.

PDAC is a systemic disease, and surgical resection only tackles the local component of this disease [37]. Adjuvant chemotherapy in PDAC patients is essential to tackle the minimal residual disease and disseminated microscopic disease [38]. It is evident that patients in the high NLR group, despite having received the current standard of treatment, are at a significantly greater risk of recurrence. NLR can be used to pre-operatively risk stratify these patients and provide prognostic counselling. NLR may also be used to guide alternative treatment strategies or intensive surveillance programs for early detection of recurrence.

This study used a single NLR value and does not account for dynamic changes, which may be influenced by surgery or systemic therapy. One study that focused on changes in NLR demonstrated that patients who converted to a low NLR post-chemotherapy had significantly better survival outcomes, even if their initial NLR was high [39]. This is further supported by another study showing that a change in NLR post-chemotherapy in advanced PDAC has a significantly greater HR for overall survival on multivariable analysis (HR 5.817, p < 0.001) [40]. Similar findings have been reported in urothelial and oesophageal cancer [41,42]. Investigating dynamic changes in NLR pre- and post-treatment in PDAC may provide valuable insights into its efficacy in predicting real-time recurrence. However, the use of NLR has several limitations. As a non-specific marker of inflammation, NLR is not exclusive to cancer and can be elevated in infections, which are common in immunosuppressed cancer patients [43]. This study is retrospective. Future studies should focus on standardizing the way NLR is reported and investigating optimal thresholds to clearly define high and low NLR groups.

In conclusion, preoperative high NLR (> 3) is associated with a significantly increased risk of overall and local recurrence following curative-intent surgery for PDAC. PDAC patients already have a poor prognosis, and a high NLR identifies a subgroup of these patients with a potentially worse outcome. A high NLR remains a significantly strong predictor of recurrence in both univariable and multivariable analyses. NLR is a simple, non-invasive, and cost-effective tool, but larger prospective, randomized clinical trials are required to ensure its widespread implementation for clinical risk stratification and prognostication.
