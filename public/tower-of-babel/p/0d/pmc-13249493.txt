Task Shifting in Cardiac Ablations Compared With Percutaneous Coronary Interventions
Authors: Noriko Fukue, Takeshi Suetomi, Tomomi Matsuura, Mari Ishida, Makiko Taniyama, Natsuko Mukai-Yatagai, Takahiro Sakamoto, Tomoko Tamada, Shiro Uemura, Yukiko Nakano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs part of work-style reform for physicians, task shifting to non-physician healthcare professionals has been promoted in Japan. We previously reported task shifting results for percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI), but the status in arrhythmia treatment remains unclear.Methods and ResultsThis subanalysis of a survey conducted by the Japanese Circulation Society Chugoku-Shikoku Branch examined task shifting in facilities performing both cardiac ablations and PCIs. A 30-item questionnaire was sent in March 2024 to 93 facilities managing acute coronary syndrome. Among 81 responding facilities (87.1% response rate), 51 performed cardiac ablations. Approximately 40% had only 1 ablation operator. The engagement rate as second operators was lower for ablations than for PCI (21.6% vs. 29.4%, P<0.001). Task shifting implementation showed no significant difference based on ablation operator numbers (N=1: 30.0% vs. N≥2: 16.1%, P=0.304), whereas facilities with fewer PCI operators had higher task shifting rates (P=0.026). Clinical engineers were the predominant profession in task shifting for both procedures.ConclusionsTask shifting of sterile procedures was limited in ablation facilities compared with PCI, reflecting the procedural characteristics such as single-operator feasibility and mapping-intensive workflows. With substantial workload demands, continued collaboration with highly specialized medical staff is essential, along with appropriate incentives and attention to work-life balance.

Central Figure

As part of the “work-style reform for physicians” program of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, the transfer of duties to non-physician healthcare professionals (e.g., nurses and clinical engineers), referred to as task shifting/task sharing, is promoted. In 2019, “tasks and duties that can be transferred under the current regulations” were organized, and 193 duties were identified as candidates.1 Following this trend, systems are being developed to promote task shifting/sharing to multiple healthcare professionals, including surgical assistance, anesthesia support, intraoperative management, and medical device operation.2 As an extension of these task shifting/sharing frameworks in general healthcare settings, the feasibility of task shifting has begun to be explored in advanced medical fields such as arrhythmia treatment and catheter ablation procedures. We conducted a survey regarding task shifting in cardiac catheterization and have already reported the results for coronary artery interventions.3 In Japan, the aging of the population is increasing, and cardiologists are overburdened and frequently work overtime. In April 2024, work-style reform was implemented. The management of acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is relatively straightforward to discuss because urgent percutaneous coronary interventions (PCIs) are required even outside regular working hours. However, task shifting in the field of arrhythmia remains unclear. We had also surveyed this area in the same survey and use those results to compare the level of task shifting between PCIs and cardiac ablations.

Methods

This study was a subanalysis of a previous survey conducted by the Committee for Diversity Promotion of the Japanese Circulation Society Chugoku-Shikoku Branch. The cross-sectional study used a 30-item questionnaire to investigate the current status and task shifting in facilities managing ACS and performing cardiac ablations. The questionnaire, which included an open section for comments, was thoroughly reviewed by committee members and was designed to obtain demographic information such as the number of hospital beds, annual number of cardiac ablations, annual number of cardiac catheterizations, annual number of PCIs, annual number of device implantations, number of cardiologists, number of cardiac ablation operators, changes in the number of cardiologists, and task shifting for sterile procedures. Details of the survey questionnaire have been reported previously.3 In this study, we compared facilities that performed both cardiac ablations and PCIs. Task shifting of sterile procedures refers to the participation of non-physician healthcare professionals in the sterile field, including preparation of sterile materials such as catheter flushing, vascular access assistance, sheath handling, catheter exchange support, and assistance during device manipulation; however, it does not include independent catheter control or ablation energy delivery. Facilities were dichotomized based on operator numbers, reflecting both the distribution of the survey population and practical workload considerations: 1 ablation operator represented the most frequent category among ablation facilities, whereas ≤3 PCI operators were used as a threshold because this staffing level is more likely to require emergency on-call duties ≥2 days per week.

A Google form was used for the questionnaire, and valid responses were those who answered all questions in the survey. The questionnaire was sent by email to 93 facilities managing ACS in the Chugoku-Shikoku region in March 2024.

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yamaguchi Prefectural Grand Medical Center (approval number: 2023-012).

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and were exploratory and descriptive in nature, reflecting the survey-based design of this study.

Task Shifting Implementation in Ablation and PCI Procedures

Among the 51 ablation-performing facilities, 19 (37.3%) had implemented task shifting of sterile field tasks to non-physician staff, and 32 (62.7%) had not. The engagement rates as second operators were lower for ablations than for PCIs (21.6% vs. 29.4%, P<0.001) (Figure 1A). For both ablations and PCIs, clinical engineers were the profession most engaged as second operators (Figure 1B,C). Discussion

Among the facilities performing cardiac ablations in the Chugoku-Shikoku region, approximately 40% had only 1 ablation operator. Arrhythmia specialists are not exclusively engaged in ablation procedures; many of them often perform device implantation and management requiring advanced skills such as cardiac resynchronization therapy, and also manage cardiovascular diseases other than arrhythmias. Therefore, the workload of arrhythmia specialists is expected to be substantial. Among the cardiac ablation facilities in the Chugoku-Shikoku region, 5–7 full-time cardiologists was the most common staffing level, with 45% of facilities experiencing a declining trend in physician numbers. Atrial fibrillation, which affects a large patient population and accounts for approximately 75% of ablation procedures in Japan,4 has become a major target for ablation therapy, and ablation operators are being trained primarily among younger physicians; however, the current workforce may be insufficient to meet the workload demands.

In this study, the rate of task shifting for sterile procedures to non-physician medical staff was lower compared with PCI. These results should not be interpreted as indicating that task shifting in ablation is less advanced than in coronary intervention. The difference may be attributed to procedural characteristics: PCI is difficult for a single operator to perform, whereas ablation procedures can be conducted by a single operator alone. Unlike PCI for ACS, which often requires multi-operator coordination under time pressure, with separate operators for catheter/guidewire manipulation and balloon inflation, catheter ablation is typically elective and can be completed by a single operator performing electrode catheter placement, mapping, and ablation. This procedural structure of catheter ablation, characterized by high operator specialization, has been well described in contemporary reviews of atrial fibrillation ablation.4 Therefore, the lower rate of task shifting observed in ablation reflects procedural rationality rather than reluctance toward work-style reform.

In recent years, there has been increasing involvement of clinical engineers, nurses, and clinical laboratory technologists in ablation-related tasks, and reports regarding collaborative practices have become more frequent at academic conferences. However, the high level of expertise required for non-sterile tasks such as mapping operations may hinder the advancement of task shifting for sterile procedures. There is a paucity of published literature regarding task shifting in ablation procedures in Japan. International reports regarding task shifting in electrophysiology (EP) laboratories include studies examining sedation and anesthesia management during EP procedures as well as sheath removal performed by nurses.5–7

The Study on the Effects of Task Shifting on Physician Working Hours Reduction and Healthcare Facility Management (Fiscal Year 2023 report) conducted cost-effectiveness analyses of task shifting across multiple medical institutions, primarily focusing on areas such as diagnostic radiology and clerical support for physicians.8 That study demonstrated that when adequate capacity to accept transferred duties is secured among recipient healthcare professions and appropriate education and organizational frameworks are established, physician working hours can be reduced with minimal operational burden, yielding favorable cost-effectiveness overall. However, it has also been reported as a challenge that the specifics of task transfer (i.e., which duties, to which professionals, and through what methods) vary considerably across facilities, and that standardized procedures/protocols and nationwide dissemination of safety information are inadequate. Additionally, it has been pointed out that task shifting is difficult to advance when appropriate education and training systems for recipient professions are lacking or when consensus-building between physicians and other healthcare professionals is insufficient.2 In addition to interprofessional coordination and education, the cost-effectiveness of task shifting in Japan has been reported,8 and appropriate incentives for medical staff with advanced knowledge and technical skills are considered necessary. There are cases of clinical engineers trained in hospitals moving to higher-paying industry positions, so healthcare facilities must develop compensation frameworks that appropriately match workload and professional capabilities. Medical staff who willingly engage in arrhythmia care frequently face substantial workload demands, and both assessment of and interventions to improve their own work-life balance are warranted.

Arrhythmia specialists alone cannot manage the numerous challenges, including the increasing volume of ablation procedures and keeping pace with rapidly advancing arrhythmia treatment technologies. Collaboration with highly specialized medical staff for both sterile and non-sterile tasks will continue to be crucial.

Policy Message

Short-Term Actionable Measures In the short term, structured collaboration with highly specialized medical staff, particularly clinical engineers, is essential. Clear role delineation, institutional education programs, and standardized workflows may facilitate safe and efficient task sharing without compromising procedural quality.

Mid- to Long-Term System Issues From a mid- to long-term perspective, system-level issues must be addressed, including appropriate incentive design, national standardization of task boundaries, and sustainable workforce planning. Without adequate compensation frameworks aligned with professional expertise and workload, retaining highly skilled personnel within healthcare institutions may become increasingly difficult.

Study Limitations

This study included only facilities in a regional area, and the results may be subject to regional bias.

Conclusions

Task shifting of sterile procedures to non-physician medical staff in cardiac ablation facilities was limited compared with PCI in the Chugoku-Shikoku region. This reflects the procedural characteristic of ablation, which can be performed by a single operator, rather than indicating less developed task shifting. With approximately 40% of facilities having only 1 ablation operator and substantial workload demands, arrhythmia specialists alone cannot address the growing challenges. Continued collaboration with highly specialized medical staff is essential, along with appropriate incentives and attention to their work-life balance.
