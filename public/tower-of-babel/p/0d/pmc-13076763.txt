Unsupervised cluster analysis identifies risk profiles driving heterogeneity and survival patterns in aortic aneurysm patients
Authors: Maria Elisabeth Leinweber, Fadi Taher, Miriam Kliewer, Afshin Assadian, Amun Hofmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAortic aneurysms (AAs) are influenced by diverse modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors. The underlying heterogeneity in this patient population complicates efforts to optimize risk stratification. Phenomapping leverages unsupervised clustering algorithms to group similar patients based on phenotypic profiles and can identify homogenous clusters in complex data patterns. While clinical classification typically relies on aneurysm morphology and presentation, unsupervised phenomapping could uncover alternative, data-driven risk profiles. Unsupervised clustering (k-means) utilizing data from the UK Biobank was conducted. All patients recorded with aortic pathologies (ICD-10 group I71) were included. Clustering variables encompassed demographic and clinical parameters, emphasizing relevant comorbidities. The main analyses were conducted in abdominal AA, whereas validation analyses were conducted in the remaining aortic pathologies. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. The study population consisted of 4623 participants (21.2% female), including abdominal (66.9%), thoracic (25.4%), and thoracoabdominal AA (0.5%), as well as aortic dissections (7.2%). Cardiovascular comorbidity emerged as the primary driver of cluster differentiation both in the main and validation analysis outweighing morphological differences. Survival analysis highlighted higher all-cause mortality rates in these multimorbid clusters. Cardiovascular and renal comorbidities can be leveraged in an informative comorbidity index. The non-linear increase in mortality risk may be indicative of the superimposed effects of the aortic pathology itself. Cardiorenal multimorbidity appears to be the most relevant separator between AA patients. Introduction

Various modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors contribute to the development of aortic aneurysms (AA), including but not limited to age, sex, elevated blood pressure, smoking, or dyslipidemia1,2. Certain congenital connective tissue disorders also significantly increase the risk for aneurysmatic degeneration, particularly in the thoracic segment, including Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, or Loeys-Dietz syndrome3–5. Affected patients can therefore present with a manifold of combinations of different risk factors. Whereas several studies previously described factors associated with perioperative outcomes after aortic aneurysm repair, with the largest patient cohorts coming from population-based data and national insurance claims datasets, patient heterogeneity and possible interactions of patient characteristics have made the definitive identification of relevant outcome determining risk factors challenging6–12.

The integration of data science into the field of medicine has yielded fresh perspectives in the age of precision medicine. With routine clinical data becoming more accessible for research purposes, new opportunities are emerging for identifying outcome-related risk factors in vascular pathologies beyond traditional association studies13,14.

Phenomapping refers to the use of unsupervised clustering algorithms, trained on phenotypic features such as clinical and demographic patient information. This can identify potentially hidden patterns within complex datasets, aiming at improved risk stratification beyond traditional diagnostic criteria. This data-driven approach can uncover subgroups of patients with distinct disease features that might not be apparent through standard clinical assessments. Phenomapping has been successfully applied in conditions such as heart arrhythmias, where it has helped identify distinct rhythm patterns and risk profiles, and in patients with popliteal artery aneurysms, aiding in the discovery of phenotypic clusters with differing clinical outcomes and treatment responses15–19. Although phenotype clustering is gaining significance in the field of cardiovascular research, its application in vascular surgery remains limited20,21. To the best of our knowledge, only one phenotype cluster analysis has been conducted for AA patients limited by a small study population of 266 included patients22.

This study aimed to address the heterogeneity among patients with aortic aneurysms by leveraging phenomapping through k-means clustering, applied to the extensive UK Biobank dataset. Clustering algorithms in phenomapping studies group patients based on shared characteristics, enabling the identification of previously unrecognized subgroups with distinct phenotypic profiles. Unlike historic studies, where subgroups are predefined by researchers, this approach employs machine learning workflows to derive groupings directly from the data. Recognizing that aortic aneurysm patients present with diverse risk profiles and clinical characteristics, our objective was to systematically characterize this variability and identify distinct patient subgroups. By uncovering these phenotypic clusters, we hoped to enhance the understanding of this complex patient cohort, potentially paving the way for improved risk stratification and tailored therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Study design and dataset

This study is a retrospective observational analysis using unsupervised clustering of data from the UK Biobank23. The UK Biobank is a large population cohort study with 502,488 participants aged 40–69, enrolled during 2006–2010 in one of 22 assessment centers in England, Scotland and Wales. In addition to health, lifestyle and environmental information collected, a variety of physical measurements as well as blood, urine and salvia samples were taken. Additionally, participants consented to a linkage of their UK Biobank data set with their health-related records from the National Health Service (NHS)24. The World Health Organization’s ICD-10 codes were used to code clinical diagnoses of hospital inpatient data. The Office of Population, Censuses and Surveys: Classification of Interventions and Procedures (OPCS-4) codes were used to code operations and procedures. Data from the NHS Information Centre for participants from England and Wales and from the NHS Central Register Scotland for participants from Scotland were used to obtain date and cause of death25,26. In this analysis all patients with the ICD-10 group I71 were included. Included OPSC-4 procedure codes were L18*—Emergency replacement of aneurysmal segment of aorta; L19*—Other replacement of aneurysmal segment of aorta; L25.4—Operations on aneurysm of aorta, NEC; L26.6 Transluminal aortic stent graft with fenestration NEC; L26.7 Transluminal aortic branched stent graft NEC; L27*—Transluminal insertion of stent graft for aneurysmal segment of aorta; L28*—Transluminal operations on aneurysmal segment of aorta.

Ethic approval

Ethical approval for the UK Biobank has been obtained from the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee (11/NW/03820). All enrolled participants gave written informed consent prior to enrolment in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. All presented research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The present project received approval by the UK Biobank under the ID 291300.

Cluster analysis

Cluster analysis was performed using the k-means clustering algorithm, an unsupervised learning technique, on the scaled dataset. Cluster analysis groups observations based on their distance in a multidimensional space, i.e. similarity across a set of variables. In brief, input variables contribute to the final clustering, with their influence diminishing as their ability to distinguish between observations decreases. k-means clustering is an established method in medical research and has been used to identify phenotype clusters in various diseases15,18. Its popularity in healthcare is largely due to its simplicity and interpretability; by minimizing the distance between points within a cluster while maximizing the distance between cluster centroids, k-means efficiently groups patients with similar clinical profiles. The clustering variables were selected clinically for their association with aneurysm development and outcomes (e.g., demographics, modifiable risks like smoking, and comorbidities like cardiorenal conditions). Data-driven criteria included low missingness, sufficient variance for cluster separation, and alignment with prior evidence. The optimal value for K was determined through NbClust function in R, which utilizes 30 different metrics to optimize k. Clusters were formed based on 15 features, encompassing demographics and comorbidities. The list of variables is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Optimization suggested different k values using the NbClust package with the same level of recommendation (2 or 3 clusters). We prioritized the two-cluster solution as the primary analysis. This choice was based on post-clustering evaluation: k = 2 delivered the most parsimonious, interpretable, and consistent separation across main and validation cohorts, distinguishing patients primarily by lower versus higher cardiorenal multimorbidity burden. Clusters were generated with the centers set to 2 and nstart set to 25. Supplementary Figs. 1–4 show scatterplots of dimensions 1 and 2 after clustering. Optimization of k and cluster formation were carried out with a set seed. Since clustering requires no missing data, 111 patients (2.3%) out of 4734 had to be excluded from the dataset. This was considered reasonable with a low risk to introduce significant selection bias.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of our findings (validation), sensitivity analyses were performed by restricting the cluster analysis to subsets of the cohort defined by aneurysm location and type. The main analysis, focused on abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAA), was repeated for patients with thoracic aneurysms (TAA), thoraco-abdominal aneurysms (T-AAA), and aortic dissections. Input variables, clustering parameters, and the preprocessing steps remained consistent across all analyses. The resulting clusters were evaluated for concordance with the main analysis findings, particularly in the distribution demographic and clinical variables across clusters.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics, with continuous variables reported as means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on their distribution, and categorical variables presented as frequencies and percentages. Survival analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier curves to estimate survival, stratified by clusters or indices derived from the clustering analysis. Log-rank tests were used to compare survival curves between groups. Statistical significance was defined as p-value below 0.05. A post-hoc cardiovascular and renal comorbidity index was established as the sum of the following binary conditions (presence = 1, absence = 0): cardiac arrhythmia, arterial hypertension, heart failure, coronary heart disease, and chronic kidney disease. This novel index was derived from variables identified as key cluster differentiators in the results, with the rationale of quantifying multimorbidity burden for survival analyses. A cardiovascular and renal comorbidity index was calculated as the sum of the presence of the following conditions: cardiac dysrhythmia, arterial hypertension, heart failure, coronary heart disease, and chronic kidney disease. All analyses were conducted using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) version 4.4.1.

Results

Study population

The study included a total of 4623 participants, with a gender distribution of 979 (21.2%) females and 3644 males. The median age at recruitment was 64 years, with an interquartile range of 60 to 67 years. Regarding traditional risk factors for aortic aneurysm development, 77.7% reported a history of smoking and 77.8% had a recorded arterial hypertension. Cardiovascular comorbidities were highly prevalent as 56.5% had coronary heart disease, and 37.7% were diagnosed with heart failure. Other co-prevalent conditions are illustrated in Table 1. The cohort comprised 2617 cases (66.9%) with abdominal aortic aneurysms, 1173 (25.4%) with thoracic aneurysms, 21 (0.5%) were thoracoabdominal, and 335 (7.2%) had a recorded dissection. Additionally, 245 patients (5.3%) had a recorded aneurysm rupture. (Table 1).

Phenotypic clusters in abdominal aortic aneurysms

Regarding variables used for clustering (exposure), Main Cluster 1 (MC1) exhibited relatively lower rates of cardiovascular comorbidities compared to Main Cluster 2 (MC2). Specifically, 30.0% of participants had coronary heart disease, 2.4% had heart failure, and 17.8% had cardiac arrhythmia compared to 83.7% of participants had coronary heart disease, 65.7% had heart failure, and 80.1% had cardiac arrhythmia in MC2. Similar trends were observed in other major comorbidities, even though the clusters had similar age distributions, rate of ruptures and rate of smoking. Townsend Deprivation Index showed a slightly higher median (-1.7) in MC2, indicating more socioeconomic deprivation than in MC1. While the proportion of patients receiving invasive treatment was similar (39.3% vs. 41.7%), MC1 received more open surgical treatments. Additionally, a smaller proportion of patients in MC1 had recorded coronary procedures. (Table 2).

Similar trends were observed in other major comorbidities such as chronic kidney disease. Despite the higher prevalence of these conditions, both clusters had comparable age distributions and co-prevalence of smoking. However, VC1 included an almost threefold increase in ruptures. Compared to the analyses in the main cohort, less healthy clusters in the validation cohort in general underwent invasive aneurysm treatment more frequently. (Table 5).

Cardiovascular multimorbidity as a main driver of cluster separation

Cardiovascular comorbidities (coronary heart disease, heart failure, cardiac arrhythmia) followed by chronic kidney disease appeared to be the most relevant information for cluster groupings on all analyses. An index consisting of cardiovascular comorbidities and chronic kidney disease was calculated for each patient. Cluster means are shown in Figs. 3A–D. Fitting 3 clusters not only resulted in distinct rupture and connective tissue disease clusters, but interestingly these two clusters also fall in between the healthier and multimorbid cluster regarding mean index values. Cardiovascular and renal multimorbiditiy was also associated with significant differences in survival, even though patients with an index of 0 and 1 had similar survival rates, as did patients with an index between 2 and 4. (Fig. 4A, B) Inspecting the index distribution per cluster, it was identified that MC1 included no patients with values of 4 or 5, whereas MC2 featured no patients with values of 0 and 1. Patients with an index of 2 were predominantly found in MC1 (96.0%) whereas patients with an index of 3 were mostly assigned to MC2 (83.6%). Similar findings were observed when fitting 3 clusters and in the validation cohort. Having more than two cardiovascular and/or renal comorbidities appears to be a threshold for phenotypic profiles among patients with aortic pathologies.

Discussion

Leveraging an unsupervised cluster algorithm to group aortic aneurysm patients in the UK Biobank based on clinical profiles (phenomapping) resulted in multiple findings. Cardiovascular multimorbiditiy and chronic kidney disease appear to be the main separator between AA patients irrespective of aortic pathology. Both within strict AAA and other aortic pathologies one cluster with a higher co-prevalence of cardiovascular and renal disease emerged (MC2 and VC1). However, AAA patients might also be characterized by three distinct clusters, where the separation based on cardiovascular morbidity remains present but was appended by a rupture cluster (aMC2). Interestingly, in the validation cohort, the third cluster consisted exclusively of patients affected by connective tissue diseases such as Marfan or Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (aVC2). Interestingly, rupture rates in the AAA cohort were not associated with multimorbidity (5.5 vs. 6.1%), while this was observed in the validation cohort consisting of aortic aneurysms with thoracic involvement and dissections (6.0 vs. 2.2%).

When NbClust provided equivalent support for both two and three cluster solutions, we selected the two-cluster model as our primary choice due to its interpretability and consistency: it robustly captured the overarching cardiorenal multimorbidity gradient driving heterogeneity and outcomes across aneurysm locations, without fragmenting the data into smaller, less reproducible subgroups seen in the three-cluster alternative. Nevertheless, both cluster analyses replicated the dominant clinical/biological signal thereby supporting the internal stability of our findings.

The relative downweighting of morphology by the algorithm suggests that, from a broad phenotypic perspective, shared cardiorenal burden creates more homogeneous patient profiles than anatomical location. Only in alternative 3-cluster models did distinct rupture (AAA) or connective-tissue (validation) subgroups emerge clearly. These results do not invalidate morphological classification for treatment decisions but complement it by highlighting a comorbidity-centric lens for overall risk profiling and prevention strategies.

These patient clusters are also characterized by differences in outcomes including survival rates, both in general as well as after invasive aneurysm treatment, or specific outcomes reflecting impaired (cardiovascular) health including type of aneurysm treatment or history of coronary procedures. Especially in case of MC1 and MC2 this became evident, where a similar proportion of patients underwent aneurysm treatment but patients in MC2 had a strong tendency of undergoing endovascular repair. This is in line with current guideline recommendations that favor endovascular approached for multimorbid patients in light of lower peri-operative mortality rates27,28. While the peri-procedural mortality of patients with recorded rupture (aMC2) is quite high and in line with previous findings29,30, their long-term survival does not appear to be affected after passing this critical short-term period. The finding that connective tissue disease patients had very favorable survival outcomes after surgery is very likely an effect of a relevant selection bias in these patients.

When we combined the features that appeared to drive cluster separation in an additive index, we observed that there was a rather sharp demarcation at 2 co-prevalent conditions. However, compared to an outcome analysis focused on all-cause mortality, index levels of 0–1 and 2–4 had similar survival rates, respectively. Therefore, while patients with 0–2 co-morbidities might have a similar clinical appearance (cluster grouping), this does not necessarily correspond to clinical outcomes. In patients undergoing surgery, no significant differences between patients with lower and higher index were observed, indicating a positive selection in patients suitable for aortic surgery.

Previous findings have indicated a substantial long-term survival disadvantage in AAA patients. Two recent large population-based studies demonstrated that, despite significant improvements in short-term survival after AAA treatment—primarily due to the intensification of endovascular aneurysm repair and enhanced risk management—long-term mortality has not sufficiently improved over the past years31,32. The authors of these studies attributed the poor long-term survival mainly to the patients’ cardiovascular vulnerability with nearly half of patients experiencing fatal secondary cardiovascular events. Despite the substantial proportion of AAA patients suffering from coronary heart disease, cardiac arrhythmia, and heart failure in our cohort, our analysis presents a more complex picture. The non-linear relationship between cardiorenal multimorbidity and all-cause mortality in aneurysm patients suggests that aneurysm-related risks may superimpose on baseline comorbidities, but contribute modestly in the long term after successful repair. Literature consistently shows that post-repair aneurysm-related mortality is often low33, with the majority attributable to non-aneurysm causes such as other cardiovascular diseases or cancer. This might be at least partially explained by the high co-prevalence of shared risk factors like smoking, hypertension, and atherosclerosis. In our cohort, the inferior survival in multimorbid clusters could reflect the cardiovascular burden dominating long-term outcomes.

Improving long-term survival in patients with AAA remains a significant challenge. Given the high burden of cardiovascular and renal diseases in this population, more stringent risk-modifying strategies must be developed and implemented. For example, a time-trend analysis of AAA patients using data from Danish population registries demonstrated an increased utilization of cardioprotective medical therapies, alongside a decreasing prevalence of concomitant atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and a reduced incidence of new atherosclerotic admissions from 1998 to 2018. Despite these advances, mortality rates remained remarkably high, with one in five patients newly diagnosed with AAA between 2014 and 2018 not surviving a two-year period after diagnosis34.

While careful case selection and risk-modifying therapies at the time of aneurysm diagnosis or surgical planning are necessary, successful aneurysm repair remains the only established intervention to prevent rupture. Given the high burden of shared risk factors in this population, smoking cessation, optimized blood pressure control, and lipid management might further reduce cardiovascular mortality that dominates long-term outcomes after successful repair. Thus, both aneurysm treatment and aggressive comorbidity management are essential and complementary for improving overall patient survival. In clinical practice, cardiorenal multimorbidity clusters could enhance risk stratification by identifying patients who may benefit from intensified cardiovascular and renal risk management such as optimized blood pressure control, heart failure therapy, and cardioprotective medications, alongside aneurysm surveillance. The derived comorbidity index might complement morphological criteria to guide shared decision-making for intervention timing or approach, favoring endovascular repair in higher-burden patients while prioritizing aggressive medical optimization in those with very high indices. However, prospective validation is needed to develop actionable tools.

The growing application of artificial intelligence in cardiovascular care underscores its potential to address patient heterogeneity and enhance precision medicine, as evidenced by recent systematic reviews showing ML/DL superiority in prognostic assessment for coronary artery disease and innovative AI approaches for safer, more efficient imaging diagnostics35,36. Our phenomapping analysis complements such efforts by demonstrating that unsupervised clustering of routine clinical phenotypes can identify cardiorenal multimorbidity as a dominant separator of risk profiles in aortic aneurysm patients. As AI continues to redefine diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic paradigms in healthcare, such methods may facilitate more integrated, individualized management strategies for complex vascular diseases37.

Limitations

Several limitations persist and require further investigations. Despite the robustness of sensitivity analyses, the reproducibility of analogous clusters in other datasets has not been tested. External validation in independent cohorts is needed to confirm the phenotypic classifications. Since phenomapping is per definition based on phenotypical features such as demographic and clinical characteristics, genetic associations or parameters not included in the present clustering could alter results. Although the UK Biobank provides extensive linked health data, there may be misclassification or underreporting of diagnoses, treatments, or outcomes in the linked NHS records, which could influence study findings. It is important to note that the information on comorbidities in this analysis is derived from coded instances in routine clinical data. As a result, the information reflects that a patient has been diagnosed with a specific comorbidity based on ICD-10 codes but often does not inform on disease severity, which may have influenced findings in the present analyses. Additionally, the UK Biobank represents a highly selected patient sample, as participants willing to enroll in such studies may have a distinct health profile that does not necessarily reflect the broader patient population. Moreover, the presence of an AAA screening program in the UK—which is not established in many other countries—may further limit the generalizability of these findings. Aortic dissections, particularly type A, commonly manifest as life-threatening emergencies with cerebral ischemia, malperfusion, or hemodynamic instability, requiring immediate surgical or endovascular intervention to address acute complications38. In these urgent scenarios, our phenomapping approach based on chronic epidemiologic patient features may not be suitable for guiding hyperacute decision-making, as priorities focus on diagnosis39 rapid stabilization and repair rather than long-term risk profiling. Addressing these limitations in future research, including prospective studies and external validation, is essential for further refining the phenotyping of aneurysm patients and improving clinical applicability.

Conclusion

Despite including morphological classification in the input features, unsupervised phenomapping identifies cardiorenal multimorbidity as the dominant separator of heterogeneity and survival risk in aortic aneurysm patients. This data-driven insight challenges morphology-centric paradigms and supports integrated comorbidity assessment for improved risk stratification and preventive care.
