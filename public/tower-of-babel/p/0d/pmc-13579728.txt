Myocardial mechanics displaying on visual structural color hydrogel microcolumn arrays
Authors: Lingyu Sun, Minhui Lu, Zezun Xie, Feika Bian, Yu Wang, Yuanjin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cellular mechanics play significant roles in biological processes from cellular level to tissue level. Great efforts have been committed to developing simple and reliable mechanical sensing strategies. Here, we present the concept of cellular mechanics displaying on visual structural color hydrogel microcolumn arrays. The microcolumn arrays were fabricated by using colloidal crystal pregel to hierarchically replicate microwell array templates. With the cultivation of beating cardiomyocytes, the microcolumn arrays could occur synchronous and reversible deformations, accompanied with visible structural color changes and reflection peak shifts for cellular mechanics displaying. Benefitting from this principle, together with the similar dimension with cells, the structural color microcolumn arrays could self-report the contraction force of cardiomyocytes at single-cell level. Based on these features, we have established a myocardial hypertrophy model in microfluidic systems and verify the value of the structural color hydrogel microcolumn arrays in monitoring the mechanical behaviors of cardiomyocytes under pathological conditions. Thus, we believe that the proposed structural color hydrogel microcolumn arrays and their integrated chips are suitable for evaluating clinical diseases of aberrant cellular force and even providing possible therapeutic targets.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04745-z.

Introduction

Cellular mechanics play significant roles in cellular and tissue-level biological processes such as mechanotransduction [1–3], migration [4, 5], phagocytosis [6], concerted contractions of muscle [7], etc [8]. Owing to their importance in maintaining normal physiological functions, the dysregulation of cellular mechanics is usually considered to be involved in the occurrence and development of specific diseases [9–12]. For example, the hypercontraction of airway smooth muscle cells may lead to asthma [7], while the weakened mechanical force in muscle cells is in connection with diseases of cardiac failure [13] and muscular dystrophy [14]. To investigate the potential correlation of cellular mechanics with diseases, various mechanical measurement techniques have been proposed, such as atomic force microscopy [15,16], traction force microscopy (TFM) [17], cantilevers [18], micropipette force sensor [19], etc [20, 21]. Based on these techniques, highly sensitive and accurate monitoring on the biomechanical performances of cells under physiological and pathological states has been realized [22–24]. Although with encouraging progress, the majority of existing techniques tend to depend on expensive equipment and professional image processing procedures, thus limiting their universality for practical use. Therefore, a convenient and efficient strategy for cellular mechanical sensing is still highly anticipated.

In this paper, we implemented the concept of cellular mechanics displaying on structural color microcolumn arrays, as schemed in Fig. 1. As a natural phenomenon arising from the interaction between light and periodically arranged nanostructures, structural colors have been extensively investigated to guide the fabrication of artificial materials with similar photonic bandgap (PBGs) properties [25–29]. Among multifarious biomimetic materials, structural color hydrogels would occur swelling or shrinking in response to external stimuli, thus leading to the variation in PBGs and structural colors [30, 31]. Benefitting from this feature, structural color hydrogels have achieved widespread applications in displaying [32], flexible electronics [33], biological detection [34], biosensing [35], and so on [36, 37] In contrast, elastomeric post arrays are regarded as effective tools to resolve sub-cellular mechanics based on their deformations under cell actuation [38]. Benefitting from optimized mechanical compliance, topography, and chemical design, elastomeric post arrays have realized effective regulation of cell functions and high-throughput measurement of single-cell forces [39–41]. Therefore, we conceive that the integration of structural color and elastomeric array techniques may construct a simplified mechanical analysis platform to evaluate cellular forces.

Herein, the described structural color microcolumn arrays were manufactured by hierarchically replicating microwell array templates based on 3D printing technique. With the cultivation of cardiomyocytes and their recovered cyclic contraction and relaxation behaviors, the structural color microcolumns connected by cells occurred synchronous and reversible deformations, accompanied with visible structural color changes and reflection peak shifts. To improve the functionality of microcolumn arrays, carbon nanotube with broadband light absorption and good conductivity was added, which could either enhance the structural color contrast of microcolumns, or facilitate the electrical conduction and electrical activity of cultured cardiomyocytes. Benefitting from the optimized force-responsive optical sensing properties, together with the similar dimension with cells, the structural color microcolumn arrays could self-report the beating behaviors of cardiomyocytes at single-cell level. Based on these features, the structural color microcolumn arrays could effectively report the mechanical properties of cardiomyocytes under pathological condition when they were integrated with a myocardial hypertrophy model in microfluidic chips. These results indicate that the structural color microcolumn arrays and their integrated chips are promising methods to evaluate clinical diseases with aberrant cellular forces, or even providing therapeutic suggestions for these diseases.

Results and discussion

In a typical experiment, the structural color microcolumn arrays were fabricated depending on a projection micro stereolithography-based three-dimensional (3D) printing system (Figure S1, Fig. 2a). After achieving the 3D-printed templates with microcolumn arrays, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) was utilized to replicate these elaborate microstructure configurations as templates (Fig. 2b–e). By successively using polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) pregel containing negatively charged silica nanoparticles and pure PEGDA pregel to hierarchically replicate the templates, the structural color microcolumn arrays could be achieved after demolding. It could be clearly observed that the microcolumn arrays presented vivid structural colors (Fig. 2f, g and h) and characteristic reflection peaks (Fig. 2i). This phenomenon was attributed to the non-close-packed nanostructure formed by silica nanoparticles, based on the principle of charge repulsion and minimum energy theorem (Fig. 2j and k). Considering the microcolumn arrays were designed for cellular sensing, their mechanical property was optimized to achieve sensitive force-responsive deformation. In general, the mechanical strength would be enhanced with the increase of PEGDA concentration, companied with reduced strain rate (Fig. 2l). In addition, the elevated hydrogel concentrations also led to the rapid increase of elastic modulus (Fig. 2m). Ultimately, 15% (v/v) PEGDA was chosen as an appropriate concentration for fabricating microcolumns because of its favorable elasticity and sensitive response to external forces. As revealed by the formula, several parameters such as lattice spacing, refractive index and glancing angle could affect the λ value and corresponding structural color. Once hydrogel component and glancing angle are constant, λ was only decided by the lattice spacing of silica nanoparticles. In this case, either the adjustment of nanoparticle concentration (Figure S2), or the deformation through stretching or pressing, would both result in the variation of structural colors and reflection peaks. With the concentration increase of silica nanoparticles, the structural color occurred an obvious blue shift because of the reduced lattice spacing. Similarly, the blue shift of structural colors was also observed during the stretching process and pressing process (from top view), as shown in Figure S3a-S3e, Figure S4. In addition, we investigated the optical stability of the structural color hydrogel during 20 stretching cycles (Figure S3d). The results revealed that the reflection wavelength could stably and reversibly change in response to the stretching and relaxing processes. It could be anticipated that the structural color hydrogel could serve as a mechanical sensor based on its force-responsive optical signals.

Considering the structural color hydrogel would be shaped into microcolumn arrays for sensing of cardiomyocytes, the deformation of a series of structural color microcolumns with different length/diameter (L/D) values under a horizontal force was first investigated (Fig. 3a and b). It could be found that with the increase of applied force, the bending deformations of microcolumns L/D values would increase accordingly. When the applied force was same, the microcolumns with bigger L/D value generated greater bending degree and deflection (Fig. 3c). where F is the bending force, E represents Young’s modulus, I means the moment of inertia, L refers to the length of beam, and y is the maximal deflection. In addition to the displacement of microcolumns, their structural colors also generated obvious variations under external forces. During the application of a horizontal force, one side of the microcolumn was stretched, while the other side was pressed. Therefore, the tension side and pressure side presented an opposite structural color change tendency both from the side view and top view (Fig. 3a, b, d, e and f). Specifically, a blue shift of structural colors was observed at the tension side, whereas the pressure side generated a red shift in colors. Noted that, such force-responsive optical signal variation was reversible and maintained stable during several deflection cycles. With the increase of L/D, the shift values would enlarge proportionately, similar to the deflection change trend, as shown in Fig. 3g and h. By taking the force-sensitive deflection change and structural color change, together with the demolding difficulty into consideration, L/D of 2 was chosen as the appropriate parameter for the construction of structural color microcolumn arrays.

Since the contractile force is a significant indicator to evaluate the viability and function of cardiomyocytes, we first conducted finite-element method (FEM) analysis on the deformation of microcolumns under a horizontal traction force, with the purpose of mimicking the contraction process of cardiomyocytes that adhered to the surface of microcolumns. Under the effect of a constant traction force, the both sides of microcolumns are stressed and the bending degree would rise with the increase of heights (Fig. 4a). During the process, the maximal deflection of microcolumns displayed a linear positive correlation with the applied force (Fig. 4b, Figure S5), but had a negative correlation with the tensile modulus (Fig. 4c). As expected, these simulation results are in accordance with Eq. (2) and the testing outcome of Fig. 3. Therefore, the contractile force of cardiomyocytes could be estimated by measuring the deflection and assigning the value into the deflection-force curve. Because of the non-adherent property of PEGDA, the surface of structural color microcolumn arrays was decorated with a hydrophilic poly-dopamine (PDA) layer (Figure S6), followed by immersion in collagen type Ⅰ to obtain enhanced biocompatibility for cellular adhesion (Figure S7). Benefitting from these treatments, the cardiomyocytes could well adhere to microcolumn arrays, as displayed in Fig. 4d, e, f and g.

After cultured for two days, the cardiomyocytes on the microcolumn arrays could recovered spontaneous beating behaviors. With the cyclic contraction and relaxation processes, the microcolumns connected by cardiomyocytes would generate synchronous and reversible deformation (Fig. 4h). Under the actuation of rhythmical cardiomyocytes, the bending of one single microcolumn, two adjacent microcolumns, and multiple microcolumns was realized (Fig. 4i, j and k, Figure S8). As mentioned above, the structural color of microcolumns would change with the external force-induced deflection. In consequence, the beating of cardiomyocytes also resulted in the structural color change and reflection wavelength shift (Fig. 4l, m, n and o). Considering that the displacement, together with the structural color shift of microcolumns would increase with the applied force, their changes could in turn reflect the magnitude of force. That is, the greater the displacement and structural color shift, the contractile force of cardiomyocyte is greater. Therefore, the structural color microcolumn arrays could provide effective monitoring on the status of cardiomyocytes, through deformations and optical signals.

To mimic the microenvironment around cardiac tissue in vivo, the PEGDA pregel was doped with conductive carbon nanotube (MWNT) for fabricating structural color microcolumn arrays. It could be found that the introduction of MWNT component improved the contrast of structural colors either on white or black background (Fig. 5a), attributed to the broadband light absorption of MWNT. The corresponding reflection spectra also demonstrated slightly reduced full-width half-maximum (Fig. 5b). In addition, the integration of MWNT component significantly improved the electroconductivity of hydrogel arrays, which was expected to promote the transfer of electrical signals in cardiomyocytes. As the MWNT concentration increased, the resistivity of composite hydrogel decreased correspondingly, as shown in Fig. 5c. However, excessive concentration of MWNT would impede the polymerization of hydrogels, under which circumstance 0.04 wt% was chosen as the appropriate concentration. Such conductive substrate could well maintain the beating behaviors of cardiomyocytes, when compared to the ones cultured on normal substrates (Fig. 5d). Under the actuation of contractile cardiomyocytes, the adjacent microcolumns would occur corresponding bending, as demonstrated in Figure S9. In addition to the beating performance, the expression of specific proteins of cardiomyocytes was explored to reveal the detailed effect of conductive substrate on cardiomyocytes. Because of their important role in maintaining cardiac functions, sarcomeric Alpha actinin (α-actinin), cardiac troponin T (cTnT), connexin 43 (Cx43) were selected as representative markers to characterize the physiological activity of cardiomyocytes. Fluorescent imaging results suggested that the cardiomyocytes on the conductive substrate presented abundant expression of α-actinin, cTnT and Cx43 (Fig. 5e).

Because calcium ions play an indispensable role in the processes of electrical conduction and beating in heart, we conducted fluorescent calcium imaging of the cardiomyocytes on non-conducting microcolumn arrays (normal substrate) and MWNT-doped microcolumn arrays (conductive substrate), respectively. For better observing the calcium transients (ΔF/F0), the fluorescent images were converted into a color saturation mode to evaluate the electrophysiological activity of cardiomyocytes. Representative color-saturation images obtained from cardiomyocytes cultured on the MWNT-integrated conductive substrate are shown in Fig. 5f and g. Fig. 5f shows calcium activity at a representative time point, while Fig. 5g presents a magnified time-lapse view of a selected single cardiomyocyte from Fig. 5f. Because of the function recovery of cardiomyocytes, the fluorescent traces on normal and conductive substrates both presented periodic pulse, as shown in Fig. 5h and i. In addition, the calcium ion signals of three different regions on the normal substrate were demonstrated to be inconsistent (Fig. 5j). In contrast, the cardiomyocytes distributed on the different regions of the conductive substrate displayed regular and synchronized fluorescence traces (Fig. 5k), which indicated that conductive substrate could provide electrical connection for cultured cardiomyocytes. According to the normalized calcium ion traces, the pulse width and pulse interval of cardiomyocytes on conductive substrates remarkably reduced than that of cardiomyocytes on normal substrates (Fig. 5l and m). These results suggested that the conductive substrate would lead to faster burst duration of calcium ions spikes and enhanced electrical activity of cardiomyocytes.

To demonstrate their practical utility, the structural color microcolumn arrays were integrated into a microfluidic chip for drug testing and disease modeling (Fig. 6a). Upon cardiomyocyte actuation, the structural color microcolumns exhibited wavelength shifts ranging from 5 to 70 nm across 50 sampled microcolumns (Fig. 6b). This distribution reflects spatially heterogeneous local deformation induced by cardiomyocyte contraction. We therefore investigated the spatial correlation between optical signals and cardiomyocyte contractile force. A 4 × 4 region of the structural-color microcolumn array was selected and individually numbered (Figure S10), and the corresponding structural color changes (Fig. 6c and d) were displayed as shown in Fig. 6e–h. These structural color variations (Fig. 6i) were associated with different force magnitudes calculated from microcolumn displacement (Fig. 6j), suggesting that the structural color microcolumn array can potentially report cardiomyocyte mechanics with high spatial resolution and throughput. Notably, most wavelength-shift and calculated force values fell within a comparable range, whereas a small subset showed higher values. These high-end values may arise from spatial heterogeneity in cardiomyocyte contraction, including differences in cell maturation state, local cell density, cell-microcolumn coupling, and contraction synchronization, etc. Nevertheless, potential technical contributions, such as variations in microcolumn geometry, optical tracking accuracy, and deformation analysis, cannot be fully excluded.

Isoproterenol and metoprolol were then chosen as model drugs to modulate cardiomyocyte beating. Upon isoproterenol treatment, the structural color responses of the microcolumn arrays were enhanced, as reflected by increases in both deformation frequency (Fig. 6k and Figure S11) and wavelength shift magnitude (Fig. 6l and Figure S11). In contrast, metoprolol as a typical β1 receptor antagonist would inhibit the beating behaviors of cardiomyocytes, thus inducing the weakened structural color and reflection peak changes (Fig. 6k and l and Figure S12). Consistent with the observed changes in beating frequency and structural color variation, isoproterenol enhanced the contractile force of cardiomyocytes, whereas metoprolol induced a modest decrease in the mean contractile force relative to the control group, although this difference did not show statistical significance (Figure S13a). The limited response to metoprolol (1 µM) may be attributed to low basal activation of β-adrenergic signaling under the current culture conditions, where β1-adrenergic blockade is expected to be more evident under adrenergic stimulation. Higher metoprolol concentrations may induce a stronger contractile response, they could also introduce cytotoxic or non-specific effects, complicating interpretation of the force reduction. Therefore, in the present study, we selected 1 µM as a conservative concentration to assess metoprolol-induced modulation while minimizing potential off-target effects. Although complete pharmacological contractility-ablation controls were not performed, the bidirectional responses to isoproterenol and metoprolol support the association between the optical-mechanical readouts and cardiomyocyte contraction.

We then established a myocardial hypertrophy model in this system through the inducing of angiotensin Ⅱ. To ensure the viability of cardiomyocytes, the concentration and treatment time of angiotensin Ⅱ were optimized (Figure S14). After inducing, the cardiomyocytes presented obviously increased cell size which had positive correlation with concentration and treatment time (Fig. 6m and n, Figure S13b, Figure S15, Figure S16). In addition, the beating frequency and contraction force of cardiomyocytes during the disease process were further investigated by our structural color microcolumn arrays. At the early stage of inducing, the beating frequency would be accelerated, and the contraction force was enhanced, as manifested by the increased reflection shift values of the microcolumn arrays (Fig. 6o, Figure S17). This phenomenon may be attributed to the increased inward flow of calcium ions induced by angiotensin Ⅱ. With the extension of inducing time, calcium ion homeostasis would be affected, and the myocardial hypertrophy model was established. Such process could also be reflected by the weakened shift values of the microcolumn arrays. The above results indicated that the mechanical properties of cardiomyocytes under pathological conditions could be reflected by visible structural color changes, thus simplifying the calculation of weak cellular forces.

To investigate the electrophysiology of cardiomyocytes under the pathological state, the calcium transients were further conducted. The results demonstrated the remarkably prolonged pulse width and pulse interval (Fig. 6p, Figure S18) when compared with normal cardiomyocytes (Fig. 5g and i), suggesting their function decrease. Such results were in accordance with the optical displaying signals generated by structural color microcolumn arrays. The multi-channel heart-on-a-chip system (Figure S19) shows potential for parallel drug evaluation in myocardial hypertrophy. As a preliminary demonstration, we used the platform to assess the protective effect of Tanshinone IIA. Tanshinone IIA attenuated the hypertrophic phenotype of cardiomyocytes in a dose-dependent manner, as shown in Figure S20. Because Tanshinone IIA was applied as a pretreatment before angiotensin II stimulation, this experiment evaluated protection against hypertrophic remodeling rather than reversal of established hypertrophy. Accordingly, it did not allow direct tracking of recovered wavelength-shift within the same group. Given potential array-to-array variability and current calibration limitations, the protective effect was assessed by immunostaining-based morphology. Therefore, this experiment should be considered a preliminary protective-intervention assessment, while the optical-mechanical readouts may be more suitable for future post-induction rescue studies. Overall, these preliminary results demonstrate the ability of the multi-channel system to generate concentration gradients and support parallel drug-response evaluation.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a structural color microcolumn array for visual and quantitative monitoring of cardiomyocyte mechanics. The optimized array sensitively converted cardiomyocyte contractility into microcolumn deformation and structural color variations. Incorporation of conductive MWNTs enhanced color contrast and electrical conductivity, while supporting cardiomyocyte electrical activity and better mimicking the native cardiac microenvironment. Application to a myocardial hypertrophy model further demonstrated the platform’s ability to detect pathological changes in cellular mechanics.

Various mechanical methods have been developed to quantify cellular contractile forces such as well-developed TFM [17], hydrogel-based deformation assays [42] and elastomeric micropost arrays [38–41]. TFM is widely regarded as a gold-standard method for mapping cell-generated traction stresses on compliant substrates through reconstruction of substrate deformation fields. However, this continuous-substrate, reconstruction-based readout differs from discrete sensing platforms that provide direct element-by-element mechanical responses. Hydrogel-based deformation assays are useful for evaluating contractility, especially in multicellular or three-dimensional systems, but often report collective or spatially averaged mechanical responses. By contrast, elastomeric micropost arrays offer spatially discrete force-sensing elements and enable substrate stiffness to be decoupled from surface chemistry and adhesive properties. Our structural-color microcolumn array represents an optical extension of the discrete micropost-array concept. Compared with conventional elastomeric micropost arrays, this platform reports cellular forces through both microcolumn deformation and corresponding structural color changes, thereby enabling a more visualized readout. Although structural color materials have been explored for visualizing cell-generated mechanical forces, most reported systems rely on membrane-based architectures [43–46], which primarily reflect collective deformation or spatially averaged color changes over relatively large regions. In contrast, our individually addressable microcolumns enable local biomechanical responses to be resolved at the cellular or local multicellular-neighborhood scale. Moreover, the microcolumn array supports parallelized force sensing across multiple cells or cell clusters to improve throughput. This study also has several limitations. The structural color and dimensions of the microcolumn arrays showed some non-uniformity, and accurate single-column detection remains technically challenging during high-throughput cardiomyocyte sensing. These factors may contribute to the markedly elevated values observed in a subset of data points, together with possible biological heterogeneity. Future work should improve microfabrication uniformity, force calibration, signal normalization, and artifact discrimination to better distinguish true biological variability from measurement-related variation.

Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was obtained from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12), Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS), fetal bovine serum (FBS), cellular dyes including Alexa Fluor 488 Phalloidin and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole were derived from Life technologies. Collagenase type 2 was achieved from Worthington, while penicillin-streptomycin and phosphate-buffered saline solution were all from Gibco. Anti-Cardiac troponin T (cTnT, mouse), anti-Sarcomeric Alpha Actin (α-actin, rabbit), anti-Connexin 43 (Cx43, goat), Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor™ 594), Donkey anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor™ 488) and Donkey anti-Goat IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor™ 594) were gained from Abcam. Paraformaldehyde (4%) was from Biosharp, while glutaraldehyde fixative solution was bought from Wabcan. Angiotensin Ⅱ and Tanshinone IIA (98%) were bought from Aladdin. Deionized water with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm was utilized in all experiments. All chemical reagents were of the analytical grade available and used as received.

Preparation of structural color microcolumn arrays:

The microcolumn array templates were manufactured via a 3D printing system (nanoArch S130, BMF Material Technology Co., Ltd., China) based on Projection Micro Stereolithography (PµSL) technology. By importing the designed microcolumn array model and slicing it into sequential slices, the resin could be selectively polymerized layer by layer to obtain the desired samples with a resolution of 2 μm. Subsequently, a mixed solution of PDMS and curing agent (9:1) was poured on the templates for vacuum treatment to remove bubbles, followed by polymerization at 65 ℃ for 4 h. 15% (v/v) PEGDA pregel containing negatively charged silica nanoparticles was then filled into the multi-wells of PDMS templates for polymerization under ultraviolet (5 cm, 20 s). Afterwards, pure PEGDA pregel (20% v/v) was coated on the polymerized microcolumns and polymerized as rigid substrate, in which case the structural color microcolumn arrays could be achieved after demolding.

Surface decoration:

The surface of the structural color microcolumn arrays was modified with a hydrophilic PDA layer to promote cell adhesion. The hydrogel arrays were immersed in a freshly prepared dopamine solution containing 1.5 mL dopamine solution (13.3 mg/mL), 2.03 mL ultrapure water, 5 mL Tris base (0.1 mol/L), and 1.47 mL HCl (0.1 mol/L) for 6 h. After thorough washing with PBS to remove residual dopamine, the arrays were immersed in collagen type I solution at 4 °C overnight. The arrays were then gently rinsed with sterile PBS to remove unbound collagen before cell seeding.

Measurement of mechanical property:

The relationship between stress and strain was investigated by fixing the both ends of PEGDA hydrogel to a displacement-force test system, and then stretching with a 5 mm/min speed. The elastic modulus (E) was calculated by the equations of E = σ/ε, σ = F/S, ε = ΔL/L, where F represents the applied force, S means sectional area, L is the original length and ΔL refers to the length elongation.

Source of cardiomyocytes:

All animal care and handing procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Affiliated Drum Tower Hospital of Nanjing University Medical School (2023AE02016). The cardiomyocytes were isolated from neonatal Sprague-Dawley rats (1–2 days). Briefly, the cardiac tissues were extracted from rats, cut into pieces, and digested into cardiomyocytes by HBSS containing trypsin, pancreatin and collagenase type 2. After centrifugation and purification, the cardiomyocytes were resuspended and supplied with DMEM/F-12 solution containing 20% FBS and 0.1 mM 5-bromo-2-deoxyuridine for subsequent cell seeding on structural color microcolumn arrays.

Cell viability testing:

To evaluate the biocompatibility of microcolumn arrays composed of different materials, the arrays were placed in 48-well plates and seeded with primary cardiomyocytes at a density of approximately 5 × 10⁴ cells/well. Blank wells without microcolumn arrays served as the control group. At 24, 48, and 72 h of culture, MTT solution was added to each well of the experimental groups, including PEGDA, PEGDA/PDA, PEGDA/PDA/Col I, PEGDA/CNT, PEGDA/CNT/PDA, and PEGDA/CNT/PDA/Col I groups, followed by incubation for 4 h. The culture medium was then removed, and DMSO was added to dissolve the purple formazan crystals. The resulting solutions were transferred to a 96-well plate, and absorbance was measured using a microplate reader. The absorbance of the control group was defined as 100% cell viability, and the viability of each experimental group was calculated relative to the control. All experiments were performed with three replicates per group (n = 3).

The same MTT-based cell viability assay was used to optimize the safe treatment conditions for angiotensin II. Primary cardiomyocytes cultured on PEGDA/CNT/PDA/Col I microcolumn arrays were exposed to different concentrations of angiotensin II (0, 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 µM) or treated with angiotensin II for different durations (0, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h). Cell viability was then evaluated using the MTT protocol described above. The absorbance of the untreated control group was defined as 100% cell viability, and the viability of each treatment group was calculated relative to the control. All experiments were performed with four replicates per group (n = 4).

Cellular staining:

After respectively treated with paraformaldehyde and Triton X-100 dilution (0.25%) for 30 min, the cardiomyocytes cultured on structural color microcolumn arrays were stained by Alexa Fluor 488 Phalloidin and DAPI to visualize the distribution of filamentous actin and nuclei. Cardiomyocytes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, permeabilized and blocked with 5% BSA blocking buffer containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h, and incubated with primary antibodies against cTnT and α-actinin, or cTnT and Cx43, at a dilution of 1:200 overnight at 4 °C. The samples were then incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies, including Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L, Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG H&L, and Donkey Anti-Goat IgG H&L, at 37 °C for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 1 min before fluorescence imaging. Samples were washed three times with PBS between each step. Afterwards, the fluorescent images of these samples were recorded by a laser-scanning microscope.

Calcium imaging:

The electrophysiology performance of cardiomyocytes was explored by calcium imaging. Pluronic F-127 dissolved in DMSO (20% w/v) was mixed with Fluo-4 AM (2 mM) to incubate with the cardiomyocytes (1:1000 dilution) for 30 min. The calcium signals were captured by a confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM-710, Germany) equipped with a water objective. Image J was then utilized to further analyze the fluorescent images.

Establishment of myocardium hypertrophy model:

After successful adhesion of cardiomyocytes onto structural color microcolumn arrays, the culture medium was replaced with DMEM/F-12 solution without Foetal Bovine Serum. Then, angiotensin Ⅱ of different concentrations was added and incubated with cardiomyocytes for 48 h. For therapy investigation, the cardiomyocytes were pre-treated with Tanshinone IIA for 6 h, followed by the incubation with angiotensin Ⅱ for 48 h.

Characterization:

The microscopy photographs of structural color microcolumn arrays were recorded by a CCD camera (Media Cybernetics Evolution MP5.0) equipped microscope (Olympus BX51). While the detailed micro/nanostructures were achieved from a scanning electron microscope (Hitachi S-3000 N). During the measurement of characteristic reflection spectrum, the fiber-optic spectrometer (Ocean Optics, USB2000-FLG) was utilized. As for the electrical performance measurement, a digital multimeter (KEITHLEY, USA) was utilized to record the resistance changes of the hydrogel during bending process. Fluorescent images of cardiomyocytes were obtained by a Zeiss LSM700 laser-scanning microscope (Zeiss, Heidenheim, Germany). The mechanical properties were tested through a universal testing machine (Suns, UMT2000). For biocompatibility testing, the optical density values of different groups were calculated by a microplate reader (SYNERGY|HTX).

Statistical analysis:

ImageJ, IBM SPSS Statistics 22 and Origin were employed to process images, analyze data and graph diagrams, respectively. The results between two groups were analyzed by Student’s t test, while one-way ANOVA was utilized for comparing the different among multiple groups (*** p < 0.001, ** p < 0.01, * p < 0.05). All data in this study are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Sample numbers are provided in figure captions.
