FRRS1L gene replacement ameliorates disease phenotypes in the mouse model of developmental and epileptic encephalopathy 37
Authors: Mehrnaz Sheibani, Esther O Alao, Ummay Mariam, Jun Wu, Sahba Kasiri, Afsaneh Talai, Jochen Schwenk, Bernd Fakler, Steven J Gray, Berge A Minassian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGene therapy offers unique therapeutic potential for treating rare genetic disorders such as DEE37, a severe Developmental and Epileptic Encephalopathy caused by biallelic loss-of-function mutations in the Ferric Chelate Reductase 1 Like (FRRS1L) gene, presenting with seizures, developmental delay, dyskinesia, ataxia and progressive, eventually debilitating cognitive and motor impairments. There is currently no treatment for DEE37. FRRS1L encodes an auxiliary subunit essential for the tetrameric assembly of α-amino-3 hydroxy-5-methyl-4 isoxazolepropionic acid receptors (AMPARs) and their recruitment to synaptic membranes. FRRS1L is expressed brain-wide, with high levels in the cerebellum. Here, we developed an adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9) gene replacement vector expressing human FRRS1L driven by the JeT promoter. Administering the virus intrathecally in two doses (5 × 1010 and 3 × 1011 vg) to Frrs1l-knockout mice, we evaluated safety and efficacy in multiple phenotypic traits shared with affected patients. We obtained robust, near-normal, FRRS1L expression in the cerebellum and low levels of expression elsewhere in the brain, which were associated with corresponding levels of increased synaptic AMPAR abundance. Importantly, our phenotypic assessments revealed dose-dependent improvement trends, or rescue, across multiple brain structural, electrocorticographic, and behavioral domains. Our results indicate that cerebrospinal fluid-directed virally mediated FRRS1L gene replacement partially restores synaptic AMPAR and ameliorates disease-relevant phenotypes, supporting gene therapy as a promising therapeutic strategy for children suffering from this catastrophic, FRRS1L-related, 37th form of DEE.Keywords: FRRS1L, Gene therapy, AAV9, AMPA receptors, Epileptic encephalopathy, Neurodevelopmental disorders

Introduction

Dyskinetic Epileptic Encephalopathies are one of many etiologies for pediatric movement disorders with associated epilepsy [1]. Mutations in the Ferric Chelate Reductase 1-Like (FRRS1L) gene are known to cause a rare, yet very severe form of epileptic encephalopathy called FRRS1L-related Developmental Epileptic Encephalopathy-37 (DEE37), also known as Early Infantile EE37 (EIEE37) [[1], [2], [3]]. The 5-exon FRRS1L gene, alias C9orf4 [4], is located on human chromosome 9q31.3. A variety of biallelic mutations in FRRS1L, including missense, nonsense, and frameshift variants, are described in ClinVar to cause DEE37 [5].

In most cases, affected children initially display normal development or mild developmental delay. However, typically between 6 and 24 months of age, they begin developing different seizure types, followed by significant regression, resulting in severe developmental delay, intellectual disability, absent or very limited speech, and multiple other neurological impairments. Patients frequently exhibit complex movement disorders such as chorea, dyskinesia, diminished voluntary and hyperkinetic involuntary movements, hypotonia, spasticity, and ataxia [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. Notably, FRRS1L mutations have been recognized as one of the major causes of dyskinetic epileptic encephalopathy with early-onset parkinsonism [3,8,9].

In many patients the epilepsy onset is associated with infantile spasms, with hypsarrhythmia on electroencephalography (EEG), which progresses to other forms of intractable epilepsy including Lennox-Gastaut Syndrome and DEE-Spike wave activation, formerly Continuous Spike-and-Waves during Sleep [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. Although brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is initially normal, many patients develop progressive cortical, cerebellar, and caudate atrophy over time [1,9,14]. Routine laboratory screening is usually unremarkable, and there are no obvious dysmorphic features [1], except microcephaly in some cases [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Finally, a recent study demonstrated an association between certain non-inactivating FRRS1L variants and autism spectrum disorder [15].

Although FRRS1L protein has been detected in both the central nervous system (CNS) and non-CNS organs (e.g., endocrine and reproductive glands, kidney, and parts of gastrointestinal tract) [5,16], research on the FRRS1L protein-related dynamics mostly revolves around the brain. FRRS1L is expressed across several brain regions including cortex, hippocampus, basal ganglia, and thalamus [4,16] with particularly high expression observed in the cerebellum [1,16,17].

FRRS1L is a transmembrane protein, identified as an interactome constituent of α-amino-3 hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptors (AMPARs) [4]. It has a key contribution to the construction and biogenesis of AMPARs, and consequently their functionality in mediating fast excitatory neurotransmission in the brain. AMPARs are hetero-tetrameric ionotropic glutamate receptors that are formed of combinations of subunits GluA1, GluA2, GluA3 and GluA4. These must be properly assembled in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) in partnership with a variety of auxiliary and regulatory interacting proteins and then trafficked to synapse membranes. As part of a complex with carnitine palmitoyl transferase1c (CPT1c), FRRS1L functions primarily in the ER as a key chaperone for GluA oligomerization [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. In addition to the ER-localized role in AMPAR early assembly, a subset of FRRS1L is detectable also outside the ER and reaches the surface membrane [6,16,20]. Absence of FRRS1L results in immature receptor accumulation in the ER, a drastic decrease in the number of synaptic AMPARs, and the synaptic transmission and plasticity defects that present as the diverse neurological manifestations of the disease [6,16,[19], [20], [21], [22]].

There is presently no therapy that alters the devastating course of FRRS1L deficiency. With no prospect of near-term approaches to restore FRRS1L-dependent AMPAR assembly and other unknown functions of FRRS1L, we proceeded to develop a potential gene replacement therapy. Frrs1l knock-out (KO) mice recapitulate several key features of the human condition, including motor incoordination, memory/cognitive impairments, and neurophysiological abnormalities [17,19,22]. We treated 10–14 days old mice with intrathecally (IT) administered AAV9 carrying the human FRRS1L gene (AAV9-FRRS1L). We show sustained improvements of AMPAR expression levels and improvements in most of the murine phenotypes tested.

Materials and Methods

• Animal Models

Animal Models

Male and female Frrs1l KO [Frrs1ltm1b (EUCOMM)] mice on C57BL/6J background and wild-type (WT) littermates were used in all experiments. Features of this model were previously characterized by collaborators in the present project [19], and as part of an International Mouse Phenotyping Consortium (IMPC) project [22]. Mice were group-housed in standard cages under a 12h light/12h dark cycle with access to food and water ad libitum. All procedures were carried out according to NIH guidelines and approved protocols of the IACUC at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center.

•AAV Vector Design

AAV Vector Design

The scAAV9/Jet-h-FRRS1L (AAV9-FRRS1L) vector was specifically designed for this study. This is a recombinant AAV9 vector expressing the human-FRRS1L transgene. The vector (Fig. 1B) consists of the AAV9 capsid, packaging a self-complementary AAV genome containing a mutant AAV2 inverted terminal repeat (scITR) with the D element deleted, a weak “JeT” promoter to minimize potential cytotoxicity [23,24], a codon-optimized human-FRRS1L complementary DNA (cDNA) coding sequence, with start codon in the Kozak context, the bovine growth hormone (BGH) polyadenylation signal, and a wildtype AAV2 ITR. Transient transfection of HEK293 cells with human-FRRS1L plasmids confirmed efficient protein expression, supporting subsequent packaging into the scAAV9 viral backbone. The viral construct was packaged and purified (Baylor College of Medicine vector core) to a final concentration of 1 × 1014 gc/ml.

•Genotyping

Genotyping

Experimental design and viral construct. (a) Study overview. (b) Vector design: The AAV9-FRRS1L vector carries codon-optimized human FRRS1L driven by the JeT promoter and is packaged into a self-complementary backbone.

Genomic DNA was extracted from ear or tail biopsies at P7–P9 with Quick-extract DNA extraction solution (Qanta Biosciences). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based genotyping was performed using AccuStart-SuperMix (Qanta Biosciences), and the primers (Table 1) targeting Frrs1l Tm1a and Frrs1l Tm1b alleles as previously described [19]. Homozygous KO and WT littermates were identified before AAV administration.

WB, Western blot; IF, immunofluorescence.

Lumbar IT injections were performed on postnatal days P10–P14 (on KO pups ≥ 5g). Animals received low (5 × 1010 vg) or high dose (3 × 1011 vg) of AAV9-FRRS1L viral vector diluted in 5 μL phosphate buffered saline (PBS) containing 1:5 of Evans Blue dye (for visual injection confirmation). Control KO mice received sterile PBS. Pups were maintained under continuous isoflurane anesthesia through nosecone and administered 2 mg/kg meloxicam subcutaneously. A small incision was made over the lumbar spine, and the incision site was cleaned with 70% ethanol. A 28-G Hamilton syringe needle was perpendicularly inserted into the lumbar spinal canal through the L4-L5 intervertebral space (confirmed by the tail flick) and then lowered into a near-horizontal angle before total 5 μL of either vector or PBS solution was slowly injected into the spinal canal space (confirmed by blue dye forward movement). The needle was then gently withdrawn to prevent backflow, and the incision site was closed using surgical glue. After recovery on a warming pad, pups were returned to their home cages and subsequently monitored to ensure proper recovery and activity.

•RT-qPCR Analysis

RT-qPCR Analysis

To confirm successful transgene expression, RT-qPCR was performed on whole-brain or spinal cord lysates of treated Frrs1l-KO mice as well as untreated controls, 2–4 and 7–9 months post-injection. Our earlier RT-qPCR analysis at 2–4 months post-injection included both brain and spinal cord samples. Later 7–9 months analyses were mainly focused on whole-brain transcript persistence and thus spinal cord qPCR was not included as a longitudinal endpoint., Total RNA was isolated from liquid nitrogen-frozen grinded tissues using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, Life Technologies), followed by column purification with PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen) and on-column DNase treatment (PureLink DNase Kit; Invitrogen). RNA was eluted in RNase-free water and quantified prior to downstream processing. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized (500 ng total RNA) using the iScript Reverse Transcription SuperMix (Bio-Rad) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative PCR was performed using fast SYBR-Green master-mix with Taq DNA polymerase (ThermoFisher Scientific). Reactions were run in triplicate. Primer sequences targeting mouse-Frrs1l and GluA1–4 were based on Schwenk et al., (2019) [19] and Stewart et al., (2019) [22], and uniquely designed for human-FRRS1L (Table 1). Relative gene expression was analyzed by ΔΔCt method for calculating relative fold changes using ribosomal protein L4 (RPL4) as internal control reference gene, and normalized to WT genotype after acceptable primer efficiencies were validated. Designed primers were confirmed for human-transgene specificity, as no detectable expression was observed in WT or untreated KO samples (Ct ≥ 37 and/or undetermined).

•Western Blotting (WB)

Western Blotting (WB)

Liquid nitrogen-frozen grinded brain tissues were homogenized on ice using mechanical homogenizing in a detergent-free sucrose-based buffer containing phosphatase/protease inhibitors. For enrichment of membrane fractions, tissues were homogenized in 500 μl HEPES buffer (10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 320 mM sucrose, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitors), adapted from previously described protocols in Schwenk et al., (2019) [19], and Han et al., (2017) [16]. Homogenates were then subjected to low-speed centrifugation (1000g for 10 min at 4 °C) to remove nuclei and incompletely homogenized material. The post-nuclear supernatant (PNS) was collected for the next steps and subsequently centrifuged at high-speed (100,000g for 1 h at 4 °C) to enrich membrane fractions. The resulting membrane pellet was resuspended in 50–100 μl of 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, and used for downstream analysis.

For obtaining crude synaptosome fractions, following methods described in Stewart et al., (2019) [22], the synaptic protein extraction (Syn-Per) reagent and their recommended protocol were used. Briefly, liquid nitrogen frozen cerebellar tissue was homogenized in ∼400 μl of Syn-Per reagent with phosphatase/protease inhibitors. The homogenate was centrifuged at 1200g for 10 min to remove cell debris, and the resulting supernatant was then centrifuged at 15,000g for 25 min (4°). The pellet, containing crude synaptosomes, was gently resuspended in respective reagent. Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA protein assay kit (Cat. 23225, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). After denaturation at 65 °C for 10 min, equal amounts of protein (10 or 15 μl) were loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE gels and then transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane for overnight transfer at 4°. After acquiring total protein images using Revert™ Total Protein staining (IRDye 680) in Image-Lab (Bio-Rad), membranes were blocked for 2 h with 5% milk at room temperature (RT), followed by incubation with the primary antibodies overnight at 4°. Membranes were then washed thoroughly with PBS containing Tween-20 (PBST) (3 × 10 min) and subsequently incubated with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h (RT). All antibody information is provided in Table 1. The membrane was then thoroughly rinsed again by repeating washing steps with PBST, and images were obtained. Signals were visualized using chemiluminescence in Image-lab. When required, brightness/ and contrast adjustments were applied uniformly across the entire image to not alter relative signal intensities or bands.

•Protein Densitometry

Protein Densitometry

Densitometric quantifications were performed on independent repeats/blots, using Image-Lab, calculated as the ratio of adjusted total band to adjusted total lane volumes for all lanes. Data was normalized to the average of WT samples on each blot. For blots that were partially overexposed (burned) with chemiluminescence setting [which occasionally happened using Anti PAN-AMPA antibody], a corresponding colorimetric image of the same membrane was used for densitometry, to preserve accurate intensity correlations. Background subtraction was performed using the rolling-disk algorithm, with disk sizes of 50–90 for total-protein lanes (minimal subtraction range) and disk sizes of 20–2 for the target bands (maximal subtraction range), for background uniformity and clear signal to noise ratio, respectively.

It should be noted that while a FRRS1L protein band was often absent in untreated KO mice, a low-intensity background band was occasionally observed even in KO samples (previously confirmed by genotyping) representing a non-specific band with the Santa-Cruz FRRS1L antibody. This faint non-specific, background bandwas included in the densitometric analysis to maintain analytical consistency, and therefore, the zero-baseline on the Y-axis of protein densitometry graphs was set to the mean normalized densitometry of KO samples.

•Immunofluorescence Staining

Immunofluorescence Staining

After perfusion fixation done on mice euthanized at 7–9 months, brains were extracted, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 4 h at RT, and then placed in 30% sucrose solution overnight at 4°. Brains were then frozen-sectioned on a microtome (Leica SM2010R Microtome; UTSW Whole Brain Microscopy Facility) with 30 μm thickness. Free-floating sections were stored at 4° until ready for staining. For blocking and permeabilization, sections were first incubated in blocking-solution (10% normal goat serum with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at RT. Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking solution and incubation was done overnight at 4°. After repetitive washing steps with PBS, subsequent incubation with Alexa-flour conjugated secondary antibodies was done for 2 h at RT in the dark (Antibody details in Table 1). Sections were again washed with PBS thoroughly (2–4 times, for 5–10 min) and finally mounted on slides with mounting media.

•Image Acquisition

Image Acquisition

Using confocal microscopy (Laser scanning Zeiss LSM880 inv. + Airyscan; UTSW Qantitave Light Microscopy Core) 20x and 40x objectives were used for imaging FRRS1L and AMPAR signals. Z-stack images were collected at 0.5–1 μm intervals through the thickness of each section. Laser power, gain, and offset were optimized to avoid channel bleed-through and saturation, and acquisition parameters were kept consistent within imaging sessions and matched across experimental groups. Linear brightness and contrast adjustments were applied uniformly to entire images and equally across all conditions for presentation purposes only.

•Immunofluorescence Signal Quantification

Immunofluorescence Signal Quantification

Quantification of FRRS1L and AMPAR immunofluorescence was performed on frozen brain sections (2–4 sections per mouse, 3–6 mice per group) using ImageJ. Regions of interest (ROIs) encompassed comparable areas of Purkinje cell layer in the cerebellar sections, as well as CA1, CA3, and dentate-gyrus regions of the hippocampal sections. In case any residual vascular-associated autofluorescence or artifact was occasionally observed, it was excluded during ROI selection prior to density quantification. Background subtraction was performed using the rolling ball method (radius 50–70 pixels). Due to occasional presence of low background/nonspecific fluorescence signals with tested FRRS1L antibodies, quantification methods minimizing manual thresholding bias were prioritized. Accordingly, using Find-Maxima function in ImageJ, signal density was quantified as maxima counts normalized to corresponding ROI (μm2). Density values from section-level measurements were lastly averaged for each animal, prior to calculations and statistical analysis. This approach enabled relative comparisons across images and animals independent of absolute fluorescence intensity values, thereby minimizing inter-image variability.

Given the limited published immunostaining benchmarks for FRRS1L in this mouse model, and to ensure staining reliability for both FRRS1L and AMPARs fluorescence detection, multiple commercially available antibodies (listed in Table 1) were evaluated/screened during initial assay optimization experiments, to confirm regional localization and genotype-dependent differences. Subsequent analyses were focused on antibody/staining conditions that provided the most robust and reproducible signal, with comparisons made within matched staining batches. For consistency, all representative images shown and immunofluorescence quantitative analyses reported here were generated using one (Prestige/ATLAS anti-FRRS1L and Sigma Millipor anti-PAN-AMPA) antibody. Initial staining with alternative antibody yielded comparable genotype-dependent labeling but excluded in final quantification or figure presentation.

•Electrocorticography (Recording and Analysis)

Electrocorticography (Recording and Analysis)

To assess the effects of the treatment on electrocorticograph (ECoG) activity, mice aged 4–6 months were included in this part of the study. Following the implantation of electrodes (Data Sciences International Small Animal Telemetry), via stereotaxic surgery (UTSW Neuro Models Facility) mice were given a 7–10 days recovery period to ensure the washout of any pain medications potentially influencing the ECoG, before starting the recordings which lasted 48–72 h. After recording, brains and implants were carefully removed. Sensors were disinfected and consecutively washed in Tergazyme solution (24 h), DI water (3 times), Actril solution (overnight), sterile saline (3 times), and stored in sterile saline (up to 3days, short-term) or dry (long-term) before re-implantation.

ECoG data were analyzed using NeuroScore (DSI). WT mice, previously reported in Stewart et al., (2019) to not exhibit any of the seizure-related EEG abnormalities of Frrs1l-KO model, were mainly included to establish the baseline physiological ECoG activity. Accordingly, thresholds for spike detection were determined based on WT ECoG activity. A minimum threshold was set to exclude baseline non-pathological spikes, along with a maximum threshold to exclude high-amplitude activity-related noise. A filter of 5–10 Hz was applied to improve the detection of seizure-related activity, per DSI recommendation. Spike-trains were used to detect seizure events defined as follows: trains of high-frequency spikes lasting ≥1.1 s, minimum of 4 spikes per train, spike-span of 1–90 ms, and 0.01–0.5 s spike intervals.

Power-band analysis was also performed to assess changes in frequency and amplitude across different ECoG bands over the entire recording period. Synchronized ElectroMyoGraph (EMG) and video recordings were simultaneously looked at to ensure the detected events were indeed happening mostly during rest/sleep periods and were not movement-related and/or noise. Poly-spike frequency and total seizure burden were quantified and compared by condition within the KO group.

•Brain MRI

Brain MRI

Brain MRIs were carried out at 9 months (±2 weeks) at UTSW Pre-Clinical Imaging/Image-guided Radiation Core Facility to assess potential changes in brain morphology in the Frrs1l KO mice, and any possible positive outcomes in the treated KO mice. Consecutive transverse scans of the brain were outlined and automatically connected using the 3D Slicer image computing software, to acquire a three-dimensional whole brain followed by automatic extraction of volume and surface area measures.

•Short-term non-GLP Safety Study

Short-term non-GLP Safety Study

Four WT mice were injected with the high dose viral vector and euthanized approximately one-month post-injection. These injected WT mice were compared to four WT control mice in terms of serum biochemistry and hematology, as well as histopathological examination of the CNS and other organs (10% formalin fixed) carried out at the UTSW Animal Research Care (ARC) diagnostic laboratory and the UTSW Metabolic Core.

•Statistical Analysis

Statistical Analysis

All data are analyzed using Graph-pad Prism latest version and presented as mean ± SEM. To avoid any pseudo-replication effects, all technical replicates (e.g. qPCR triplicates, behavioral trials, and IF section-level measurements) were all averaged prior to calculations and statistical analysis, such that the individual mouse (the biological replicate) remained the statistical unit throughout all analyses. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For Western-blots and other multiple group comparisons one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by proper post-hoc tests were used, unless otherwise mentioned. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Experimental workflow

Fig. 1A depicts the experimental sequence and other details. Genotyping was done at postnatal day P7-9. After animals were genotyped as WT (Frrs1l +/+), KO (Frrs1l −/−), or heterozygous (Frrs1l +/−), KO pups were randomly assigned to receive IT injections of either PBS or AAV9-FRRS1L at P10, except for a few cases where injections were postponed by 1–3 days for KO pups weighing ≤ 5g at P10 to allow them to attain weight, as mice below this threshold proved unlikely to tolerate and survive the anesthesia and intrathecal injection. The Frrs1l −/− mice are small and frail, including in the youngest ages, with up to 90% pre-weaning lethality [22,25]. Fig. 1B depicts the AAV9-FRRS1L vector design. Briefly, the vector carries the codon-optimized human-FRRS1L driven by the low-expression JeT promoter and is packaged into a self-complementary backbone [23,24]. Two doses, low (5 × 1010 vg/mouse) and high (3 × 1011 vg/mouse) were tested. Behavioral, immunohistological and molecular assays were conducted at indicated ages (Fig. 1A).

Sustained restoration of FRRS1L mRNA and protein in AAV9-FRRS1L-treated Frrs1l−/− mice

RT-qPCR on tissues collected from 2 to 4 and 7-9 month-old mice showed that the human FRRS1L mRNA expression following treatment was clearly detectable in whole-brain lysates in treated-mice at both time-points. While this upregulation of vector-derived human FRRS1L transcript was dose-dependent and sustained over time, it remained below the endogenous WT mouse FRRS1L levels. Expression levels in the spinal cord were equal or higher than WT (Fig. 2A).

For Western blotting, considering the known role of FRRS1L as a membrane-anchored protein [6,16,19,20], monitoring of FRRS1L levels was initially studied in membrane-enriched fractions collected from three CNS regions (forebrain + midbrain, cerebellum, and spinal cord), as well as peripheral tissues at 2–4 months of age. This approach showed that in cerebellum and spinal cord of the high dose-treated Frrs1l −/− mice, FRRS1L protein amounts reached levels comparable to those of WT mice. Low-dose treatment achieved substantial but much lower levels (Fig. 2B). Western blot on the same fractions from the extra-cerebellar regions (i.e. forebrain + midbrain) did not show detectable improvements in FRRS1L levels with either dose (although we did detect extra-cerebellar FRRS1L-related partial restorations with immunofluorescence microscopy, described below). Results in kidney and liver extracts were similar to those from the cerebellum, with near-complete restoration of FRRS1L protein levels with high-dose treatment (Fig. 2B).

Given the important role of the cerebellum in this disease, the cerebellum's high natural expression of endogenous FRRS1L [1,16,17], and our strong WB results in this brain region following treatment (Fig. 2B), we consequently focused on the cerebellum. Based on the established FRRS1L role in AMPAR synaptic recruitment [16,19,20], we isolated synaptosome fractions from cerebellar extracts, to better study the FRRS1L connection with synaptic AMPAR distribution. These results are presented in the next section.

Here, we present results of FRRS1L itself, a proportion of which, as mentioned, can be detected in synaptosomes [6,16,20]. We found a significant and dose-dependent increase in FRRS1L protein, with levels reaching about 50% of WT levels with high-dose treatment (Fig. 2C), a pattern which was maintained over time into the later ages of 7–9 months (Fig. 2D).

Immunofluorescence microscopy in the cerebellum (Fig. 3A), conveyed similar results with FRRS1L-related signal improvements in the high-dose treated group. With this technique, we also found evidence of distinct dose-dependent increased FRRS1L-related immunoreactivity in the hippocampus (Fig. 3B).

WB on the cerebellar synaptosome fractions for the whole family of AMPARs (using a PAN-AMPAR antibody) showed significant restoration of synaptic AMPAR levels in the high-dose treated mice, which was sustained into older ages (Fig. 4A and B). Using subunit-specific antibodies, these improvements were seen also in individual AMPAR subunits GluA1, GluA2-3, and GluA4 (Fig. 4C). Notably, these improvement patterns were correlated with similar ones observed for FRRS1L within the same cerebellar synaptosomal fractions (example blots in Fig. 4A and C).

Established functions of FRRS1L in prior literature do not include transcription regulation. Indeed, AMPAR subunit transcript levels are shown to be normal in young Frrs1l−/− mice [19,22], suggesting that FRRS1L deficiency effects are primarily in the post-translational levels of AMPAR regulation [22]. We did however notice a report of transcriptional dysregulation of two subunits in adult cerebellum in a published graduate thesis [25].

We proceeded to measure whole-brain AMPAR transcript levels in our older age group and found significant and substantial reductions in mRNA levels for GluA1 and GluA2, and similar trends for GluA3 and GluA4 in untreated KO mice compared with WTs. Importantly for the present therapeutic work, FRRS1L replacement normalized AMPAR subunit mRNA expression toward WT levels for those subunits exhibiting significant deficits in untreated KO mice, with more variable effects observed in GluA3 and GluA4, for both low and high-doses (Fig. 4D).

Related to the above, is that our WB experiments consistently showed an age-related increase in AMPARs in WT samples and the reverse, a decrease, in Frrs1l KO mice (Fig. 4E; also compare Y (young) lanes to the other lanes in Fig. 4A). Possible explanations and implications of these observations are addressed in the Discussion section.

oECoG

ECoG

Recapitulating the epilepsy of DEE37, Frrs1l KO mice exhibit abnormal ECoG [22,25], which we reconfirm. Interictal rhythms are disturbed and the records are replete with ictal discharges. Regarding interictal rhythms (power band analysis): In WT mice, frequency bands show balanced power distribution between delta (0.5–4 Hz, red), theta (4–8 Hz, yellow), alpha (8–12 Hz, green), sigma (12–16 Hz, blue) and beta (16–24 Hz, purple) frequencies, indicating regular sleep-wake cycles and normal brain activity. In contrast, KO mice exhibit highly disturbed patterns, namely the slower delta/theta bands reaching 80–90% relative power across the record, with very frequent added erratic and high amplitude epileptiform spikes, consistent with epileptic encephalopathy [1]. Treated KO mice, especially with the high dose, have unequivocally improved power band distribution, which, however, does not fully attain the WT pattern (Fig. 6C).

Regarding ictal discharges: very frequent poly-spike waves were seen, as well as distinct spike-train events. The latter were predominantly observed during rest and sleep, and were not movement artifacts, as confirmed by EMG and simultaneous video-taping (Fig. 6D).

We set the automated spike-train detection protocol as: fast spikes in series lasting a minimum of 1.1 s (see Methods). These spike-trains were subsequently counted over a 24-h period and compared across experimental groups. We also measured the average number of spikes per train, the percentage of the recording time occupied by trains, and the maximum duration of spike-trains. In all measures there were clear trends of improvements with the high dose that reached close to statistical significance (p = 0.069, 0.081, and 0.084 respectively) (Fig. 6E). Finally, spike amplitudes were majorly reduced in treated versus untreated mice (Fig. 6C), indicating improved neural network synchronization, and predictive of reduced epileptogenesis.

No significant differences were observed between the two time-points for any of the groups for either forepaw or hind paw grip strength (data not shown on the graph), indicating sustained therapeutic benefit (Fig. 7A).

Horizontal ladder

Skilled locomotion and paw placement accuracy were evaluated using the horizontal ladder test. Compared with WT controls at both younger and older time-points, untreated Frrs1l KO mice exhibited a markedly higher rate of (both fore- and hind-limb) stepping errors, consistent with previous gate impairment reports [17,22], as well as a longer trial (ladder traverse) time.

oOpen field

Open field

Untreated KO mice moved significantly more compared to WT mice at both time-points, consistent with prior reports on hyperactivity of Frrs1l KO mice [17,19,22]. At the younger age, treated KO mice showed partial attenuation of this hyperactivity, with the high-dose group demonstrating a treatment effect approaching WT. The effect was not consistent at the older age, possibly due to small cohort size (Fig. 7D). While not typically used as a direct measure of cognition, the open field test can also provide indirect insights into behavioral patterns linked to cognitive processes such as anxiety-related, decision making and risk assessment behaviors, by considering time spent in center or periphery of the arena [26]. At both time-points, KO mice spent significantly more time in the central and less in the peripheral sides, in contrast to the normal periphery preference of WT mice, which is consistent with prior data on impaired cognitive aspects in Frrs1l KO mice [19,22]. This abnormal pattern was corrected by the treatment in the usual dose-dependent manner, with statistical significance at both time points and for both low and high doses (Fig. 7D, right). No significant changes were observed in any of the groups between the two time-points (not shown on the graph).

oFear conditioning

Fear conditioning

To evaluate hippocampal associative memory, mice were subjected to fear conditioning paradigm and tested after training. As previously reported [22], the untreated Frrs1l KO mice displayed markedly reduced freezing behavior relative to the WT controls, reflected by significantly fewer freezing episodes, and reduced time spent freezing during both context and cue re-exposure, and across both time-points (Fig. 7E). AAV9-FRRS1L partially improved associative memory with the low-dose treated KO mice showing modest, while high-dose treated mice major improvements, in both freezing parameters, in younger mice. This was particularly the case with the longer tone-triggered freezing times at both ages (Fig. 7E, right). Consistent with these behavioral changes, the Mean Motion Index which inversely correlates with freezing behavior (see Methods) was markedly elevated in untreated KO mice and progressively reduced in treated animals, indicating suppression of locomotor activity following fear conditioning, particularly following the tone (cue) with both doses and across both ages (Fig. 7E).

oMarble bury

Marble bury

In the marble burying assay untreated Frrs1l KO mice left nearly all marbles exposed at both age groups, supporting prior data on impaired cognitive function [19,22]. Though not attaining statistical significance, treatment led to a clear trend of dose-dependent improvement in this activity at both ages (Fig. 7F).

Short-term non-GLP safety study

Four WT mice were injected with the high dose viral vector and euthanized approximately one month post-injection. No significant differences were observed compared to the four age, sex and genetic background-controlled mice across any serum biochemistry, hematology or histopathological examination parameters, supporting the potential safety of the intervention.

Discussion

We evaluated the therapeutic potential of AAV-mediated FRRS1L re-expression in a Frrs1l-deficient mouse model of DEE, using a multi-level approach encompassing molecular, electrophysiological, behavioral, and physiological readouts. Across these domains, gene therapy resulted in partial but consistent improvements compared with untreated KO animals, with several outcomes showing dose-dependent trends. Importantly, these effects were observed across independent assays rather than relying on a single endpoint, supporting the conclusion that FRRS1L re-expression can meaningfully modify disease-associated phenotypes in this model.

To confirm transduction and sustained FRRS1L expression following vector-mediated gene-replacement, we performed RT-qPCR at 2–4 and 7–9 months post-injection. Probing with specifically designed primers, we found that human FRRS1L expressed in the brain in dose-dependent fashion, and that the expression was essentially constant from the 2–4 to the 7–9 months ages. Expression was stronger in the spinal cord, consistent with what is commonly observed with IT administration and use of the JeT (weaker) promoter [[27], [28], [29]]. Importantly, expression in the brain was not a supraphysiologic expression, which is an important observation, considering that the replaced gene product is intended to upregulate AMPARs, critical determinants of neuronal excitability.

FRRS1L protein is known to be present in different regions of the CNS, with high amounts in the cerebellum and thalamus, as well as in peripheral tissues, mainly the liver and kidneys [1,16,17]. As quantified by Western blotting, our IT-administered gene therapy, at high-dose, almost normalized total FRRS1L levels in the cerebellum, and resulted in 50% correction as quantified in synaptosomal fractions of this brain region. Western blotting was not sufficiently sensitive to detect the delivered protein product elsewhere in the brain, indicating low expression outside the cerebellum, likely due to diminishing viral spread further from the lumbar site of administration.

Nonetheless, there was protein presence outside the cerebellum, as detected and quantified by immunofluorescence in the hippocampus. These observations on patterns of vector distribution and transgene expression in the brain, are indeed expected, considering the route and age of injection in the present study [27].

Previous in-vivo and ex-vivo studies [6,16,17,22] have shown that FRRS1L is predominantly localized in the neuronal soma and/or proximal dendrites, particularly within the ER/vesicular transport compartments, and with a peri-nuclear pattern [6,16,25]. In fact, this localization pattern is explained by FRRS1L's function as part of a multi-protein assembly responsible for maturation and trafficking of AMPA subunits from the ER toward the Golgi, and ultimately the synaptic membrane [[18], [19], [20]].

In our immunofluorescence imaging results, a strong FRRS1L signal was observed in the Purkinje cell layer in WT mice with the expected peri-nuclear distribution in soma and proximal dendrites, which is consistent with previous reports [17,25]. While the untreated KO mice showed only background/noise level fluorescence, treated KO mice exhibited a clear though modest recovery of FRRS1L immunoreactivity with low and high dose treatments in both cerebellar and hippocampal regions. In the hippocampus, punctate FRRS1L immunoreactivity was observed across groups with a relative increase in signal density in WT and in treated animals compared to untreated KO, particularly within the dentate gyrus region and consistent with prior data on FRRS1l expression in dentate gyrus granule cells [22,25]. Quantitative signal density analysis confirmed these increasing trends in both treated groups reaching significance in the high-dose group, which also qualitatively resembled the WT distribution. This pattern of partial signal recovery in treated animals was also observed with anti-pan-AMPA staining within the same regions.

FRRS1L is an AMPAR assembly chaperone that helps secure physiologic synaptic pools of AMPARs [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. To date, the reduced synaptic AMPAR levels in Frrs1l KO mice have been studied mostly in younger mice up to 4 months of age [17,19,22]. For our therapeutic purposes, we took a longer view and make note that WT murine cerebellar AMPAR levels increase with age (at least up to 9 months). In Frrs1l KO mice the converse is the case, with levels decreasing with age. This age-dependent decrease in AMPAR protein levels in KO mice appears to be, at least in part, due to reduced transcription levels. These observations may indicate neurodegeneration and reflect the known neurological deterioration over time in affected patients [1]. With our gene therapy, whole-brain AMPAR mRNA levels improved, as did protein levels in the cerebellar synaptosomal fraction in a dose-dependent and persistent fashion. Immunofluorescence microscopy was able to confirm AMPAR level improvements also in the hippocampus.

Frrs1l KO mice are known to have lower body weights [17,19,22], generally an indicator of overall poorer health. Our gene therapy, at the higher dose, led to improvement in body weight across the age span.

As mentioned, older age in the Frrs1l KO model has received less attention. Our MR volumetry studies show reduced brain volume and surface area at the aged mice, again consistent with the possibility that DEE37 is ultimately a dual developmental and neurodegenerative disease. The reduced surface area and volume at the older age are both significantly rescued with the high-dose gene replacement treatment.

As DEE37 is an epileptic encephalopathy, in its animal model we expected, and found, abundant epileptiform and ictal discharges and disturbed cortical rhythms (power bands). Following gene therapy, there was clear and consistent qualitative trend toward reductions in amplitudes of epileptiform discharges and occurrences of trains of ictal activity, and improvements in background rhythm distributions. Quantitation did not achieve significance likely because of insufficient transduction across the cortex, wherefrom these electrical signals are recorded.

On the other hand, in a broad panel of behavioral assays our gene therapy resulted in consistent and dose-dependent directional and often statistically significant improvements across all behavioral paradigms, in particular with the high-dose. Importantly, the similar directional trends observed across multiple behavioral domains suggest that gene therapy improves overall functional capacity, rather than selectively modulating single behaviors.

The surprising extent of behavioral, compared to electrocorticographic, improvements suggest two mutually non-exclusive possibilities; (1) There may be greater extra-cerebellar viral distribution and transduction than meets the eye, and a larger experimental cohort might have raised the observed ECoG improvement trends to statistical significance; (2) The cerebellar dysfunction may indeed play an outsized role in DEE37, consistent with the higher levels of FRRS1L in that brain region, and thus the robust behavioral improvements would be explained by the near-normalization of FRRS1L levels in the cerebellum, following our lumbar IT gene replacement.

Apart from the treatment efficacy, findings from our short-term safety studies support a favorable treatment tolerability, with no evidence of systemic or histopathological toxicity.

Despite some limitations related to the biological and practical constraints inherent to working with this animal model the present findings have important implications for the development of gene therapy strategies targeting FRRS1L deficiency. Notably, our findings show that even partial restoration of molecular and network-level functions may translate into clinically meaningful benefits in severe DEE, where modest improvements can substantially impact quality of life.

Some considerations should be taken into account with regard to clinical translation. Due to the high pre-weaning lethality and fragility of Frrs1l KO mice, the treatment in the present study was administered intrathecally and during early postnatal period, and phenotypic rescue was observed despite a predominantly cerebellar FRRS1L transduction/expression. However, whether this approach could be directly translatable and clinically applicable in patients remains unclear, particularly in the context of developmental epileptic encephalopathies where diagnosis typically occurs later. Accordingly, the best therapeutic window for intervention and the treatment efficacy requires further investigations. Likewise, the degree to which this pattern of expression will translate to human patients, especially those in whom a cortical dysfunction is a major component of the disease, remains to be determined in the future.

It should be noted that although there is clear evidence that FRRS1L plays a key role in AMPAR assembly, its boarder functional implications beyond AMPAR contributing to the disease phenotype are incompletely understood, and should be explored in future more expanded studies. Nevertheless, by demonstrating convergent improvements across molecular, electrophysiological, and behavioral domains, along with conservative interpretation of immunofluorescence findings as complementary and spatial evidence of treatment-associated rescue, this study provides a foundation for further optimization of FRRS1L-targeted gene therapy approaches.

Our study is a proof of principle that FRRS1L replacement could change the course of this severe encephalopathy in childhood and well beyond. Access to the cerebellum is particularly important as it appears to contribute meaningfully to therapeutic benefit; but broader CNS distribution is likely needed. The optimal anatomical targets for transduction and delivery strategies, as well as the extent of CNS transduction required for achieving full therapeutic benefit in patients remain to be better defined; particularly because the Frrs1l KO mouse model, despite capturing key aspects of the disease, does not model the exact clinical trajectory, specially in terms of regression and seizures.

Finally, although no evidence of toxicity was observed under the conditions tested, the long-term tolerability of a supra-physiological FRRS1L (over) expression was not evaluated in this study and thus will require further evaluations. Future investigations using improved CNS delivery methods such as the newer AAV capsids that can piggyback on facilitated transport to cross the blood-brain barrier (e.g. through the transferring receptor [[30]]) could be tested for broad and uniform brain-wide distribution afforded by vascular system and its capillaries, to further evaluate the translational potential of FRRS1L gene replacement therapy.
