Accidental fish bone ingestion complicated by gastric abscess: A case report

Most of them pass spontaneously without symptoms, while 10%-20% fail to pass and less then 1% cause complications. Introduction

The presence of a foreign object in the gastrointestinal tract is not an uncommon finding in imaging studies.

The finding of an intra-abdominal foreign body will depend on the context in which the patient is involved. There are foreign bodies that are the result of a pathologic condition (bezoars), others that are placed by a medical team for a diagnostic and/or therapeutic purpose (dislodges tubes), and others that result from some complication or iatrogeny [1].

Identifying the normal radiologic anatomy of the upper aerodigestive tract and gastrointestinal system is vital to identifying dislodged foreign bodies.

Fishbone is a common foreign body that can be accidentally ingested. According to some of the studies it accounts up to 84% of the foreign bodies ingested. Most of them pass spontaneously without symptoms. Approximately 10%-20% fail to pass and less then 1% cause complications, such as perforation and abscess formation [2].

Fish bone perforation occurs in all segments of the gastrointestinal tract, although it tends to occur in regions of acute angulation, such as the ileocecal and rectosigmoid junctions. These foreign bodies may also perforate through a hernia sac, Meckel’s diverticulum, or the appendix.

It has diverse clinical manifestations and patients occasionally present with unusual or even bizarre clinical manifestations such as hemorrhage, bowel obstruction, and even ureteric colic. With these varied and nonspecific clinical presentations, as well as in the vast majority of cases, the antecedent of fish bone ingestion is usually forgotten, the correct preoperative diagnosis is seldom made [3].

CT is the imaging modality of choice for evaluating ingested fish bones and potential complications.

Case presentation

A 67-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with epigastric abdominal pain, dyspepsia, and weight loss over the previous weeks. Her medical history was unremarkable for prior abdominal surgery. Six months earlier, the patient had undergone gastroscopy, which yielded negative findings.

Laboratory tests showed a mild elevation of inflammatory markers, while amylase and lipase levels were within normal limits. The patient was afebrile.

Discussion

Fish bone ingestion is one of the most common causes of foreign body ingestion [4]. Most ingested fish bones pass spontaneously without causing symptoms However, certain risk factors predispose patients to complications. These include the use of dentures, which impairs palatal sensory feedback and the ability to identify sharp objects, advanced age, alcoholism, mental impairment, rapid eating, and speaking while eating [5,6].

Although rare, various complications may occur, such as perforation, abscess formation, and luminal obstruction. Most ingested fish bones become lodged in the oropharynx, particularly near the tonsils or at the base of the tongue. Other potential locations include the larynx, other parts of the gastrointestinal tract, or, rarely, the trachea [5,6].

Within the gastrointestinal system, fish bones may cause complications including perforation, bleeding, hematoma, abscess formation, fistulae involving adjacent structures such as the trachea or vessels, peritonitis, intra-abdominal abscesses and mediastinitis with severe hemorrhagic complications [7,8].

Clinical diagnosis can be challenging in some cases because a history of bone ingestion is not always recognized, and patients may present with nonspecific symptoms that mimic other pathologies. In many cases, the diagnosis of an ingested foreign body is made days or even months after ingestion [9]. Symptoms vary depending on the location and extent of injury, and patients may remain asymptomatic for prolonged periods [10].

Computed tomography (CT) is the imaging modality of choice for evaluating ingested fish bones and their potential complications. Often, there is no initial clinical suspicion of foreign body ingestion, and alternative diagnoses such as acute abdomen are considered. Consequently, diagnosis relies primarily on CT findings. Definitive diagnosis is achieved by direct visualization of the calcified fish bone, which is best detected using bone window settings. Using bone window settings and multiplanar reconstructions (MPR), an ingested fish bone typically appears as a linear calcified foreign body. Perforation may be partial, causing localized inflammation or abscess formation, or complete, allowing the fish bone to penetrate the gastrointestinal wall and migrate into adjacent organs, such as the liver [8,11].

Although CT has a reported sensitivity of up to 100% and a specificity of approximately 91%, accurate diagnosis depends on a high index of clinical suspicion, familiarity with CT appearances, and technical factors. These include slice thickness (thinner slices of 1.5-2 mm are preferred), use of multiplanar and 3D reconstructions, and awareness that opacified small blood vessels may mimic fish bones [11]. CT also provides valuable information regarding the characteristics of the foreign body, including size, shape, density, location, and associated complications. The use of intravenous contrast agents is recommended when complications are suspected [11].

In the present case, CT imaging allowed documentation of the extraluminal migration of the fish bone into the thickness of the gastric wall and the presence of an intramural abscess, thereby promptly guiding surgical management.

About treatment, approximately 80%-90% of ingested foreign bodies pass spontaneously. Endoscopic removal is required in 10%-20% of cases, while surgical intervention is necessary in only about 1% [2].

In the described case, surgical intervention was required for removal of the fish bone from the stomach.

Conclusion

Fish bone can cause various complications when accidentally ingested and some of these can be life threatening. CT is the most sensitive and specific tool for the diagnosis and it is recommended to perform it at the beginning when any complications are suspected. Knowledge of typical radiological manifestations is crucial to ensure timely diagnosis and prevent potentially fatal developments.
