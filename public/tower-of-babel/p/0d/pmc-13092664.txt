Clinical outcomes and prognostic determinants of dehydrated human amnion/chorion membrane for refractory venous leg ulcers
Authors: Kazuhito Nagasaki, Masuomi Tomita, Kyota Kikuchi, Satomi Takaoka, Mayuko Otaki, Maho Tsuchiya, Katsuya Hisamichi, Mamoru Kikuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of dehydrated human amnion/chorion membrane (dHACM) allograft for venous leg ulcers (VLUs) and to identify clinical factors associated with early healing within 12 weeks.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included 52 patients with CEAP C6 VLUs treated with dHACM at Shimokitazawa Hospital between February 2023 and June 2025. The primary outcomes were complete epithelialization and recurrence-free survival. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to estimate healing rates. Early healing (within 12 weeks) was analyzed using univariate and multivariable Firth-penalized logistic regression, with directional consistency confirmed using univariate Cox proportional hazards analysis. Sensitivity analyses were performed by altering early-healing thresholds to 81 and 90 days. Variables with odds ratios (ORs) approximating 1.0 were further evaluated using predefined clinical equivalence margins (OR, 0.80-1.25). Adverse events were retrospectively assessed according to World Health Organization–Upsala Monitoring Centre criteria.ResultsAmong the 52 patients, 43 (82.7%) achieved complete healing, and the 12-week healing rate was 52.1%. Early healing occurred in 26 patients (50.0%). Multivariable analysis identified higher body mass index (adjusted OR, 0.847; 95% confidence interval, 0.739-0.948) and infection (adjusted OR, 0.253; 95% confidence interval, 0.060-0.913) as independent inhibitory factors, whereas independence in activities of daily living showed a tendency toward promoting healing. Cox analysis demonstrated consistent directional results. Sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds (81 and 90 days) confirmed the robustness of key predictors. Ulcer area (OR, 0.999) and disease duration (OR, 1.002) were within the predefined equivalence margins, indicating minimal clinical impact. Recurrence occurred in six patients (14.0%), with recurrence-free survival rates of 90.5% at 1 year and 80.5% at 2 years. A total of 58 adverse events were observed in 33 patients (63.5%), most of which were mild and self-limiting; only two mild local reactions were considered possibly related to dHACM treatment.ConclusionsDespite being a descriptive study without a control group, favorable healing outcomes, low recurrence, and acceptable safety were observed in treatment-refractory venous leg ulcers managed with dHACM under Japan’s stringent reimbursement criteria. Higher body mass index and infection were major predictors of delayed early healing, whereas ulcer area and disease duration had limited influence. These findings suggest that dHACM represents a valuable therapeutic option for patients with hard-to-heal VLUs.Keywords: Chronic wound, Dehydrated human amnion/chorion membrane, Prognostic factors, Venous leg ulcer, Wound healing

Venous leg ulcers (VLUs) are chronic, hard-to-heal wounds characterized by delayed healing and high recurrence rates, resulting in impaired quality of life and a substantial health care burden for affected individuals.1 Standard management consists of wound debridement, local wound care, and sustained compression therapy; however, healing often remains protracted, particularly in patients with great saphenous vein reflux or perforator incompetence, even after surgical or endovenous interventions.2

In recent years, biologic wound therapies have been increasingly introduced for refractory VLUs. Among these, dehydrated human amnion/chorion membrane (dHACM) allografts, derived from human placenta, have attracted attention because of their rich extracellular matrix components, cytokines, and growth factors. Experimental and clinical studies suggest that dHACM can modulate the chronic inflammatory, hypoxic, and fibrotic wound microenvironment and promote angiogenesis, granulation tissue formation, and epithelialization.3 Randomized controlled trials conducted outside Japan have demonstrated significantly higher healing rates with dHACM compared with standard care alone, supporting its clinical efficacy in VLUs.3,4 In contrast, most overseas clinical trials employed protocols assuming repeated applications after treatment initiation, without explicitly addressing institutional restrictions on application frequency or treated wound area.

In Japan, dHACM was approved for clinical use in 2021 and subsequently reimbursed in 2022. However, the reimbursement framework is notably more stringent than those in other countries. Indications are limited to refractory ulcers showing less than a 50% reduction in wound area after at least 4 weeks of standard therapy; the initial application must be performed during hospitalization; the cumulative treatment area is capped at 224 cm2 within 12 weeks; and treatment is restricted to certified physicians at approved facilities.5 Under this reimbursement framework, dHACM tends to be selectively used in treatment-refractory cases, making the study population likely different from those enrolled in overseas trials. Furthermore, as a referral-based specialty center, our institution treats a disproportionate number of moderate-to-severe VLU cases, reflecting both systemic and institutional factors.

Despite these regulatory and practical limitations, real-world evidence evaluating the effectiveness and safety of dHACM for VLUs under Japan’s reimbursement system remains limited. Moreover, clinical factors associated with early healing and recurrence in this setting have not been sufficiently clarified. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the clinical outcomes of dHACM therapy for refractory VLUs at a single center operating under Japan’s strict reimbursement conditions and to identify prognostic determinants of early healing within 12 weeks, as well as recurrence and safety outcomes, in routine clinical practice.

Methods

Study design

This single-center retrospective cohort study included patients with VLUs who were treated with dHACM allograft at Shimokitazawa Hospital between February 2023 and June 2025. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.6 The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shimokitazawa Hospital (approval no. 240,706-1). Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived.

Setting

Shimokitazawa Hospital is a tertiary referral center that provides multidisciplinary wound management led by vascular and plastic surgery specialists, in collaboration with diabetology, orthopaedic surgery, and dermatology teams.

Participants

Consecutive patients with VLUs classified as CEAP C6 were screened. For patients with multiple ulcers, the largest ulcer was selected as the analytical unit. Eligibility criteria were defined in accordance with Japan's reimbursement requirements for dHACM therapy and included: (1) less than a 50% reduction in wound area after at least 4 weeks of standard therapy; (2) initial dHACM application performed during hospitalization; (3) cumulative treatment area not exceeding 224 cm2 within 12 weeks; and (4) treatment performed by certified physicians at an approved facility.5 Patients with neoplastic ulcers, diabetic foot ulcers, ischemic ulcers, or incomplete follow-up were excluded. When ischemia was suspected based on the initial ankle-brachial index (ABI) assessment, additional imaging was performed, and revascularization was undertaken as indicated. In revascularized cases, ABI was reassessed after revascularization and immediately before initiation of dHACM therapy, and this value was used as the ABI for analysis.

Treatment protocol

Treatment with dHACM was initiated only in patients who met the reimbursement criteria. The dHACM allograft used in this study was commercially available as EpiFix (MiMedx).

The timing of initiation, frequency of application, and number of grafts were determined by the treating physician based on wound condition, response to prior therapy, and clinical progression. Standard wound care, including appropriate debridement and local wound management, was provided throughout the study period. Compression therapy was maintained for all patients unless contraindicated, in accordance with established guidelines for VLU management.1,2 The presence of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and superficial and deep venous reflux was assessed. When DVT was suspected, pharmacologic therapy was initiated as appropriate, and surgical or endovenous interventions were considered for superficial venous reflux.

In selected cases, split-thickness skin grafting was selectively performed after dHACM therapy once adequate wound bed improvement was achieved. The decision to perform grafting was made at the treating physician’s discretion, based on ulcer size, wound condition, and clinical considerations such as the need to avoid prolonged hospitalization. However, the primary evaluation in this study focused on the healing trajectory following dHACM application, and grafted cases were included in the analysis according to the predefined outcome definitions.

Outcome definitions

The primary outcomes were wound healing and recurrence. Healing was defined as complete epithelialization without drainage and no requirement for dressings, confirmed at two consecutive clinical visits. Notably, healing was not determined based on the use of skin grafting but was strictly assessed according to the predefined criteria for complete epithelialization. Recurrence was defined as the development of a new ulcer at the same site after documented healing, consistent with definitions used in prior VLU studies.7, 8, 9 Secondary outcomes included early healing and factors associated with recurrence-free status.

Patients who did not reach the outcome during follow-up were right-censored. Death or major amputation was considered a potential competing event and was evaluated using the Fine-Gray model as a sensitivity analysis,10 although no major amputations occurred in this cohort. Deaths occurring before complete healing were treated as right-censored events in both healing and recurrence analyses.

Data collection

Clinical variables were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records and entered into a standardized, anonymized database. In cases of inconsistency, source documents were reviewed to ensure data accuracy.

Bias and missing data handling

Potential sources of bias and missing data were addressed in accordance with recommended practices for observational studies.6,11 Patients lost to follow-up were considered non-healed in the full analysis set (FAS) and excluded from the per-protocol (PP) analysis. No imputation was performed for missing data.

Statistical analysis

The FAS was used for the primary analyses, and the PP set for sensitivity analyses. Cumulative healing rates were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. The potential influence of competing events was assessed using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model.10

Early healing was defined a priori as healing within 12 weeks based on clinical relevance and consistency with prior VLU studies.7, 8, 9 The 12-week time point is commonly used to assess healing in randomized trials of VLUs and was considered clinically appropriate. In this study, healing within 12 weeks was not a primary endpoint but was analyzed as a secondary outcome. Candidate predictors were first evaluated using univariate Firth-penalized logistic regression, which is suitable for datasets with limited event numbers and reduces small-sample bias.12 Variables with P-values < .10, clinical relevance, and acceptable events-per-variable characteristics were entered into multivariable models.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative early-healing thresholds of 81 and 90 days. For variables with odds ratios (ORs) near 1.0, clinical equivalence testing was performed using predefined equivalence margins (OR, 0.80-1.25).13 Directional consistency of associations was assessed using univariate Cox proportional hazards models.

Skin grafting was not a prespecified baseline variable but was treated as a post-baseline, stepwise intervention introduced during follow-up after wound bed improvement following dHACM therapy. Therefore, grafting was not included as an independent covariate in the multivariable model for predictors of healing within 12 weeks. To assess its potential impact, a sensitivity analysis censoring observations at the time of grafting was performed to evaluate the robustness of the primary results.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3) with the survival, survminer, coxphf, and cmprsk packages.

Results

Participants

During the study period, 95 patients were diagnosed with CEAP C6 venous leg ulcers. Of these, 52 patients who met the reimbursement criteria for dHACM therapy and underwent treatment were included in the FAS. The PP set consisted of 48 patients after excluding four patients who were lost to follow-up or whose healing status could not be determined.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the 52 patients are summarized in Table I. The median age was 71 years (interquartile range [IQR], 63-76 years), and 42 patients (80.8%) were male. The median body mass index (BMI) was 25.6 kg/m2 (IQR, 21.1-30.5 kg/m2). Hypertension was present in 25 patients (48.1%), and six patients (11.5%) were receiving maintenance dialysis.

Ischemia, defined as an ABI <0.9, was observed in 12 patients (23.1%). After revascularization, the median ABI improved to 1.2 (IQR, 1.1–1.3). The median ulcer area was 14 cm2 (IQR, 6-24 cm2). According to the DESIGN-R classification, wound depth was categorized as D2 in 46.2% and D3 in 44.2% of patients. Infection (I ≥1) was present in 32 patients (61.5%), poor granulation (G4-G5) in 28 patients (53.8%), and necrosis (N3/N6) in 35 patients (67.3%). The DESIGN-R classification is a Japanese wound assessment scale that evaluates wound status based on depth, exudate, infection, granulation, necrosis, and pocket formation. The median duration from ulcer onset to initiation of dHACM therapy was 275 days (IQR, 131-630 days), and the median number of dHACM applications was 2 (IQR, 1-3). A history of DVT was present in three patients; no acute thrombus was detected, and no pharmacological treatment was administered. Despite prior varicose vein treatment in some patients, preoperative evaluation showed residual reflux in the distal saphenous, tributary, and deep veins.

Primary outcomes

In the FAS, cumulative healing rates estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method were 52.1% (95% confidence interval [CI], 35.9%-64.2%) at 12 weeks, 61.2% (95% CI, 44.6%-72.9%) at 16 weeks, and 65.8% (95% CI, 49.1%-77.0%) at 24 weeks (Fig 1). Similar trends were observed in the PP set. Sensitivity analyses using a Fine-Gray competing-risk model demonstrated comparable healing estimates across the 12- to 24-week period, indicating minimal influence of competing events.10

Overall, 43 of the 52 patients (82.7%) achieved complete healing, whereas nine patients (17.3%) remained unhealed or were censored. The median time to healing was 81 days in both the FAS and PP sets (95% CI, 65-364 days).

In a subset of patients, autologous split-thickness skin grafting was selectively performed after improvement of the wound bed following dHACM therapy. Healing assessment strictly followed the predefined study criteria and was not based solely on grafting, and these patients were therefore included in the FAS according to the protocol. Autologous split-thickness skin grafting was performed in 13 patients, whereas 39 patients did not undergo grafting. Complete healing was achieved in 92.3% (12/13) of the grafted group and 79.5% (31/39) of the non-grafted group. All grafted patients underwent skin grafting within 12 weeks after initiation of dHACM therapy, and no patients in the non-grafted group transitioned to grafting after 12 weeks.

Recurrence-free survival

Recurrence-free survival rates after healing were 90.5% (95% CI, 82.1%-99.8%) at both 6 and 12 months and 80.5% (95% CI, 66.5%-97.3%) at 24 months (Fig 2). These rates are favorable compared with those previously reported for compression therapy alone7,9 The median follow-up duration for recurrence analysis was 365 days (IQR, 217-509 days). One death occurred before complete epithelialization and was treated as a right-censored event in both healing and recurrence analyses.

In univariate Firth-penalized logistic regression analyses, higher BMI (OR, 0.841; 95% CI, 0.743-0.936; P = .0011) and infection (OR, 0.273; 95% CI, 0.081-0.847; P = .0241) were identified as inhibitory factors for early healing. Independence in activities of daily living (ADL) (OR, 3.645; 95% CI, 0.99-16.42; P = .0525), hypertension, and CEAP-A classification showed potential associations.

In the multivariable model including BMI, infection, and ADL status, higher BMI (adjusted OR, 0.847; 95% CI, 0.739-0.948; P = .003) and infection (adjusted OR, 0.253; 95% CI, 0.060-0.913; P = .036) remained independent inhibitory factors, whereas ADL independence showed a tendency toward promoting healing (adjusted OR, 2.73; P = .185). Model fit was acceptable (likelihood-ratio χ2 = 17.1; P = .0007).

Univariate Cox proportional hazards analyses demonstrated consistent directional associations: BMI (hazard ratio [HR], 0.912; P = .0015), infection (HR, 0.610; P = .118), and ADL independence (HR, 1.50; P = .308), confirming concordance with logistic regression results.

Sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds for early healing (81 and 90 days) yielded ORs comparable to those of the primary model. Ulcer area (OR, 0.999) and disease duration (OR, 1.002) fell within the predefined equivalence margins (OR, 0.80-1.25), indicating minimal clinical impact.13

To conservatively assess the impact of skin grafting, a sensitivity analysis was performed in which observation was censored at the time of grafting. As a result, cumulative healing rates were 43.2% (95% CI, 25.2%-56.9%) at 12 weeks and 59.0% (95% CI, 39.0%-72.5%) at 24 weeks. Even when all post-grafting healing events were treated as non-events, an increasing trend in healing rates over time was maintained.

Recurrence analysis

Recurrence occurred in six patients (14.0%). Given the limited number of events, multivariable analysis was not performed. Descriptive comparisons revealed that patients with recurrence had a higher BMI (33.9 vs 24.7 kg/m2) and a longer delay before initiation of dHACM therapy (439 vs 214 days). Recurrent cases more frequently exhibited unfavorable wound characteristics, including reduced ADL independence, infection (I ≥1), poor granulation, and necrosis, consistent with previously reported risk factors for re-ulceration.7,14, 15, 16

Adverse events

A total of 58 adverse events (AEs) occurred in 33 patients (63.5%) (Table III). The most common AE was xerotic eczema (26 events; 50.0%), all of which were mild and self-limiting. Mild local reactions, such as pain or erythema, occurred in two patients (3.8%) and required no additional treatment. One serious AE (1.9%)—death due to worsening congestive heart failure and pulmonary hypertension—occurred during the study period and was judged unrelated to dHACM therapy. No patients discontinued treatment because of AEs, indicating a favorable safety profile consistent with previous reports.3,4

Discussion

This single-center retrospective cohort study evaluated the clinical outcomes of dHACM allograft therapy for refractory VLUs under Japan’s uniquely stringent reimbursement system. Despite the inclusion of a substantial proportion of patients with moderate-to-severe disease—many with infection, ischemia, prolonged ulcer duration, or impaired functional status—favorable healing outcomes were observed, with cumulative healing rates of 52.1% at 12 weeks and 65.8% at 24 weeks. Recurrence after healing was relatively uncommon, and the overall safety profile was acceptable. These findings suggest that dHACM may represent a clinically meaningful adjunctive therapy for VLUs in real-world practice, even when its use is restricted by rigorous reimbursement criteria. These findings descriptively illustrate the healing course observed under real-world treatment strategies, including dHACM therapy, in this cohort managed within a stringent reimbursement framework.

Previous randomized controlled trials conducted outside Japan have demonstrated superior healing rates with dHACM compared with standard care alone.3,4 However, these trials predominantly enrolled patients with relatively mild disease, limited ulcer chronicity, and fewer complicating factors. In contrast, the present cohort consisted exclusively of patients who met strict reimbursement criteria, including insufficient response to at least four weeks of standard therapy and limitations on cumulative treatment area and treatment setting.5 Given that reported 12-week healing rates with compression therapy alone in moderate-to-severe VLUs range from approximately 26% to 42%,7, 8, 9 the healing rates observed in this study compare favorably and support the effectiveness of dHACM in a more challenging clinical population.

From a mechanistic perspective, dHACM contains extracellular matrix components, cytokines, and growth factors that are thought to modulate the chronic inflammatory, hypoxic, and fibrotic wound microenvironment characteristic of non-healing VLUs.3,4 Experimental and clinical studies have suggested that these biological properties promote angiogenesis, granulation tissue formation, and epithelialization. Although mechanistic endpoints were not directly assessed in the present study, the observed healing patterns—particularly the proportion of patients achieving early healing—are consistent with these proposed biological effects.

Analysis of factors associated with early healing within 12 weeks identified higher BMI and infection as independent inhibitory factors, whereas independence in ADL showed a tendency toward promoting healing. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that obesity contributes to elevated venous pressure, impaired microcirculation, and chronic inflammation, all of which delay wound repair.10,12,13 Infection is a well-established barrier to wound healing and has been incorporated into several prognostic models for VLUs, including the Wound Healing Index.17 In contrast, ulcer area and disease duration were not associated with early healing in this cohort, and equivalence testing suggested that their clinical impact was minimal. This may partly reflect that stepwise therapeutic interventions selected during the treatment course may have relatively mitigated the influence of conventional chronicity indicators.

In this study, the frequency and treated area of dHACM application were restricted in real-world practice under the Japanese reimbursement system for this high-cost regenerative product, making it difficult to evaluate a direct dose-response relationship between dHACM use and healing outcomes. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the results.

In some patients, skin grafting was selectively performed after improvement of the wound bed following dHACM initiation. Skin grafting was not a predefined baseline factor at study entry but was introduced as a stepwise therapeutic intervention during the treatment course, based on a comprehensive clinical assessment including ulcer size, wound condition, and the need to avoid prolonged hospitalization. In practice, skin grafting was selected to accelerate final wound closure on the basis of a wound environment optimized by dHACM therapy and thus likely functioned as a mediator in the overall healing process rather than as a predictor of healing. Although ulcers tended to be larger in grafted patients, the duration from ulcer onset to dHACM initiation largely overlapped between grafted and non-grafted groups. This suggests that ulcer size may have influenced the decision to perform grafting, whereas ulcer duration itself was unlikely to have directly affected the healing course. Accordingly, the absence of ulcer duration as a healing predictor is more plausibly attributable to attenuation of conventional chronicity indicators following dHACM initiation rather than to the effect of skin grafting. On this basis, skin grafting was considered a mediator within the healing process rather than a baseline prognostic factor and was therefore not included in the multivariable predictive model.

Recurrence occurred in 14.0% of patients, corresponding to recurrence-free survival rates exceeding 90% at 1 year and approximately 80% at 2 years. These outcomes are more favorable than those reported for compression therapy alone7, 8, 9,18 and are comparable to recurrence rates following endovenous or surgical venous interventions.14, 15, 16 Patients who experienced recurrence tended to have higher BMI, delayed initiation of advanced therapy, reduced ADL independence, infection, and unfavorable wound characteristics, consistent with previously identified risk factors for re-ulceration.7,14, 15, 16 These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive management addressing both systemic and local wound factors in addition to advanced biologic therapy.

The safety profile observed in this study was favorable. Most AEs were mild and self-limiting, and only one serious AE—death due to worsening cardiopulmonary disease—occurred and was judged unrelated to dHACM therapy. No patients discontinued treatment because of AEs. These findings are consistent with prior reports evaluating the safety of dHACM in VLUs and other chronic wounds.3,4

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This study was retrospective, conducted at a single center, and included a relatively small sample size without a control group. Healing assessments were performed at intervals of 2 to 4 weeks, introducing some uncertainty in the exact timing of healing events. Although healing is fundamentally a time-to-event outcome, instability in Cox model estimates for certain variables prompted the use of Firth-penalized logistic regression for early healing analyses, with Cox models used to confirm directional consistency.12 Competing events were infrequent, and the additional value of competing-risk analysis was therefore limited.10 Furthermore, criteria for performing skin grafting were not predefined and varied according to clinical circumstances, introducing some heterogeneity in treatment strategy. In this study, compression therapy and superficial venous interventions were performed when feasible; however, partial venous reflux remained in some patients, and the possibility that more aggressive venous treatment could have further improved healing outcomes cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

Under Japan’s stringent reimbursement system, dHACM therapy is subject to limitations in usage and treatment setting but is implemented within a regulatory framework that ensures appropriate use of regenerative products. The healing course observed in this study therefore reflects real-world clinical outcomes achieved under such controlled conditions. Although this was a descriptive study without a comparator group, we present observational data on cumulative healing rates, recurrence, and safety in a real-world cohort, including patients with refractory VLUs. Higher BMI and infection emerged as key inhibitory factors for early healing, underscoring the importance of addressing systemic and local barriers to wound repair. These findings support the positioning of dHACM therapy as part of an integrated treatment strategy and provide a rationale for future multicenter prospective studies to refine patient selection and optimize treatment algorithms.
