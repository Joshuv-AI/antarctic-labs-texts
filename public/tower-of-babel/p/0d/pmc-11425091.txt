Associations between plasma metals and hemoglobin in female college students with dysmenorrhea
Authors: Qingzhi Hou, Yuchen Zhang, Hua Yang, Yunjie Wang, Zexi Xu, Jiujing Lin, Jia Li, Chenyang Hou, Zhanhui Qiu, Haoran Zhang, Ping Zhang, Xiangsheng Xue, Xiaotong Shen, Xinghua Xu, Hui Zou, Zhenrui Ma, Jing Gao, Xiaomei Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHemoglobin (HGB) was the most important factors which could cause dysmenorrhea in women. Metals exposure and hemoglobin level in dysmenorrhea female was unclear. We aimed to explore the associations of multi-metal exposure and HGB level in female college students with dysmenorrhea.Methods253 female students who had dysmenorrhea was included in our study. The Last Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, generalized linear model (GLM), and Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR) models were used to explore the associations of multi-metal exposure and HGB levels in female college students with dysmenorrhea.ResultsGLM results showed that plasma Fe, Ni and Rb was positively associated with HGB and plasma Co was negatively associated with HGB. In menarche age ≤13 years old group, plasma Co and Rb only was negatively and positively associated with HGB level, respectively, and plasma Ni had positive association with HGB level in menarche age >13 years old group. BKMR results showed the reverse U-shaped relationship between the five metals mixture (Co, Fe, Ni, Cu and Rb) and HGB levels in overall and menarche age ≤13 years old group. However, there were positive association between the five metals mixture and HGB levels in menarche age >13 years old group.ConclusionOur present study revealed that metals (Fe, Ni, Co, Rb, Cu) mixture exposure could effect HGB levels in female college students with dysmenorrhea. And the relationships were different during different menarche age in female college students.Keywords: Multi-metal, Exposure, Combined effect, Hemoglobin, Dysmenorrhea

Introduction

Dysmenorrhea, which refers to appearances of lower abdominal pain, heave accompanied with lumbar acid or other discomfort in the period before and after menstruation or menstruation, is one of the most common gynecological symptoms [1]. It can be classified as primary and secondary dysmenorrhea [2]. It has been reported that 60%–93 % of adolescent girls suffered the discomfort from dysmenorrhea [3]. Severe dysmenorrhea negatively affects girls’ normal life and reduces their quality of life [4]. Previous studies reported that the risk factors associated with dysmenorrhea, including biological factors, internal secretion and other factors [5]. Previous studies showed that reduced HGB level was one of the most important factors which could cause dysmenorrhea in women [6]. The main function of hemoglobin is to combine oxygen. When the level of hemoglobin in human body is insufficient, tissues and organs will be deprived of oxygen, and then vasoconstriction will occur. Hypoxia will also lead to the release of prostaglandin, aggravating uterine vasoconstriction [7], so severe dysmenorrhea will occur [8]. In addition, metals exposed to the environment was ubiquitous. Some studies showed that the heavy metals titanium [9] and cadmium [10] in the blood could cause blood system disorders, resulting in changes in HGB concentration. Inorganic arsenic bind to hemoglobin in humans and animals, thereby reducing the concentration of hemoglobin in the blood [11]. Some population studies showed that metal lead and cadmium reduced the hemoglobin concentration of occupational patients [12,13], and vivo studies have found that metal cadmium could also reduce the red blood cell count and the level of hemoglobin in the blood [14,15]. In recent years, people pay more attentions to the influence of environmental metals exposure on human health, especially the high incidence rate of female college students of dysmenorrhea. However, the relationship between environmental metals exposure and HGB level is rarely discussed in women with dysmenorrhea, especially for multi-metal exposure. This study investigated the correlation of plasma multi-metal exposure and hemoglobin levels in female college students with dysmenorrhea, in order to find a new direction for the cause of dysmenorrhea research.

Methods

Study population

Our study populations were recruited from the physical examination for freshmen in the university hospital in September 2021. A total of 635 female students were enrolled in our study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Primary dysmenorrhea [2]; (2) Have a history of dysmenorrhea and have accumulated two or more months of pain in the past year; (3) Complete baseline data, physical examination data, questionnaire data and plasma metal detection data; (4) Voluntarily participated in this study and signed the informed consent form. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with secondary dysmenorrhea; (2) Have a history of dysmenorrhea, but have not had dysmenorrhea in the past year; (3) No complete baseline data, physical examination, questionnaire and metal detection data were available. Finally, 253 of female college students were included in our present study. Our study was supported by Ethics and Human Committee of Shan Dong First Medical University. Samples collection and interview were conducted after participants signed informed consent.

Hemoglobin level and plasma metals assessments

Fasting blood were draw in the morning as the freshmen physical examination. The fasting blood was collected in 5 ml of EDTA-2 anticoagulant tubes, and be measured immediately. Hemoglobin levels were measured with an automated hematology analyzer (He max 530AL, China). The blood samples, which collected in the coagulation-promoting tubes were coagulated, removed plasma and stored at −80 °C before processed in the laboratory test. The metals concentration of plasma samples were measured by Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Thermo Fisher Scientific) [16] following our previously reported protocols, including Magnesium (Mg), Aluminum (Al), Calcium (Ca), Titanium (Ti), Vanadium (V), Chromium (Cr), Manganese (Mn), Iron (Fe), Cobalt (Co), Nickel (Ni), Copper (Cu), Zinc (Zn), Arsenic (As), Selenium (Se), Rubidium (Rb), Strontium (Sr), Molybdenum (Mo), Cadmium (Cd), Tin (Ti), Antimony (Sb), Barium (Ba) and Lead (Pb).

Covariate

There were face to face and online interviewed to obtain the information of life style and other factors, which were conducted by well-trained workers. The main contents of the questionnaire include baseline characteristics (age, age of menarche, height, weight and body mass index (BMI)), lifestyle behaviors (midday napping, stay up late, breakfast, night snack, spicy greasy diet, passive smoking, sleep duration and boarding high school) and family medical histories (maternal dysmenorrhea).

Statistical analysis

In descriptive statistics analysis, continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD for normal distribution and as medians (25th, 75th) for non-normal distribution; categorical variables were used as frequencies (percentages) to describe baseline, lifestyle, and physical characteristics. The concentrations of plasma metals were logarithmic conversion because they were skewed distributions. Spearman's correlation was used to detect the correlation between different plasma metals and between plasma metals and HGB. The LASSO regression model was used to screen for special plasma metals associated with HGB. GLM was used to estimate the associations of plasma metals with HGB level, including single-metal and multi-metal models. Furthermore, we used the BKMR [17] models to explore the joint effect and interactions effect between 22 plasma metals which were selected by LASSO and GLM and HGB level. In BKMR model, the importance of plasma metals was assessed by posterior inclusion probabilities (PIP). When the other plasma metals concentrations were fixed at the median, the univariate exposure-response curve of plasma metals (95 % credible intervals) with HGB was presented. We evaluated the cumulative effect of the plasma metals mixtures by calculating the expected change HGB in relation to particular quartiles in all the plasma metals mixtures from their median levels. We also explored the dose-effect of metals and hemoglobin level by RCS regression model. All models were adjusted for BMI, mother dysmenorrhea, age of menarche, boarding high school, night snack and passive smoking. Previous studies have reported that age at menarche is one of the risk factors for dysmenorrhea in women [18,19]. And according to previous studies reported, and the mean age of menarche in our present study was 13 years old [20], the participants were stratified into two groups (Menarche ≤13 years old, Menarche >13 years old). Two-sided P value < 0.05 were identified as statistically significant. We performed analyses with IBM SPSS (Version 25.0), R (Version 4.1.0), and Excel (Version 16.0).

Results

Baseline characteristics and plasma metal levels for the participants

The general characteristics of the total girls (n = 253) were showed in Table 1. All female students had dysmenorrhea during physiological period. In the total study population, the average age was 23.61 ± 1.84 years old, the average BMI of the study participants was 20.32 ± 2.62 kg/m2. There were 155 female college students with menarche age ≥13 years old, and 98 female college students with menarche age<13 years old. The percentage of mothers with dysmenorrhea was 64.43 % (n = 163). The percentage of never taking a nap and frequently staying up late were 13.83 % (n = 35) and 47.83 % (n = 121), respectively. About 66.8 % of participants attended boarding schools during high school. There were 67 (26.48 %) participants ate breakfast occasionally, 35 (13.83 %) participants ate midnight snack and 120 (47.43 %) ate spicy food. The total participants slept an average of 8.19 h. The average concentrations of HGB were 130.07 ± 11.08 g/L (Table 1). Compared to individuals with menarche age of 13 years or younger, participants older than 13 years were more likely to board at high school (75.51 % vs. 61.29 %), and this difference between the two age groups had statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Table 1), other characters had no significant differences between the two groups (all P > 0.05) (Table 1). Moreover, the details of the concentrations of 22 plasma metals among the participants were shown in Table 2. Most of the plasma metals were moderately high correlated with each other (P < 0.05, r = −0.24-0.84, Fig. 1).

Associations between plasma metals and HGB level for the participants

In single metal model, when adjusted by BMI, mother dysmenorrhea, age of menarche, boarding high school, night snack and passive smoking, the results shown that plasma Fe, Ni, Se and Rb had positive correlation with HGB levels, the β (95%CI) were 21.01 (13.62, 28.40), 20.65 (13.09, 28.21), 18.95 (2.69, 35.21) and 35.33 (20.25, 50.41), respectively (Table 3). In addition, there was a significantly negative association between plasma Co and HGB level in overall, β was −11.66 (−18.06, −5.26). And the results of unadjusted model were consistent with adjusted model, and when adjusted factors excluded passive smoking or night snack from model a, the results of model b and model c were consist with model a(Table 3), which means our results were robust. Subgroup analyses were then performed according to the age at menarche of 13 and adjusted by BMI, mother dysmenorrhea, age of menarche, boarding high school, night snack and passive smoking. The associations between plasma Fe, Ni, Rb and HGB level were similar to those for overall in each subgroup. The result of plasma Co had a negative association with HGB level, which only showed in the group of menarche age ≤13 (β = −15.39, 95%CI: −23.32, −7.46, P < 0.001). The result of plasma Se had a positive association with HGB level, which only showed in the group of menarche age ≤13 (β = 21.08, 95%CI: 0.99, 41.17, P = 0.041) (Table 3).

Dose-effect relationship of plasma metals and HGB levels

We explored the dose-effect relationship of plasma metals and HGB levels by RCS. And the curves results showed that plasma Fe and Ni had non-linearly dose-effect relationship with HGB level in overall, menarche age ≤13 years old (All of P for overall association<0.05, All of P for nonlinearity association <0.05) (Fig. 2A2-A3,B2-B3), however, the dose-effect relationship was linearity in menarche age >13 years old (All of P for overall association<0.05, P for nonlinearity association>0.05) (Fig. 2C2-C3). In addition, the linearly dose-effect relationship was found between plasma Co, Se, Rb and HGB level in overall, menarche age ≤13 years old (P for overall association<0.05, P for nonlinearity association >0.05) (Fig. 2A1、A4-A5,B1、B4-B5), but, the dose-effect relationships were no statistically significant in menarche age >13 years old. (Fig. 2C1、C4-C5).

The associations between multi-metal exposure and HGB levels

The result of the LASSO regression model shown that plasma Fe, Ni, Co and Rb were selected as the significant stressors of HGB level in the parameters of lambda = 2.009 (Red dashed line in Fig. 3A1-A2) and the details of LASSO shown in Table S1. Furthermore, the plasma metals (Fe, Ni, Co, Cu) screened by LASSO model were incorporated into the multivariate GLM and BKMR to explore the association between multi-metal mixture exposure and HGB levels. Results of multivariate GLM revealed that plasma Ni (β = 15.18, 95%CI: 1.47, 28.89), Rb (β = 27.31, 95%CI: 11.49, 43.12) had positive associations with HGB level and plasma Co (β = −9.93, 95%CI: −16.12, −3.75), Cu (β = −14.14, 95%CI: −27.64, −0.64) were negatively associated with HGB level in overall. Model a were adjusted by BMI, mother dysmenorrhea, age of menarche, boarding high school, night snack and passive smoking. And the results of unadjusted model were consistent with adjusted model, and when adjusted factors excluded passive smoking or night snack from model a, the results of model b and model c were consist with model a(Table 4), which means our results were robust. In subgroup, plasma Co (β = −11.30, 95%CI: −18.92, −3.69), Rb (β = 31.16, 95%CI: 11.50, 50.81) and Cu (β = −22.90, 95%CI: −39.65, −6.16) only had statistical correlations with HGB level in the group of menarche age ≤13 years old, and plasma Ni(β = 36.16, 95%CI: 13.03, 59.30) only had a positive association with HGB level in the group of menarche age >13 years old (Table 4).

We further explored the over effect between multi-metal exposure with HGB level. Posterior inclusion probabilities (PIP) for BKMR analyses were shown in Table S2. The BKMR results indicate an overall inverted U-shaped relationship between five metal mixtures and HGB levels (Fig. 4A). This pattern persists within the group aged ≤13 years at menarche, while a positive relationship is observed in the group aged >13 years at menarche, with the five metals set at their 50th percentile as a reference (Fig. 4B and C). We found that the univariate relationship between plasma Fe and HGB levels exhibits an inverted U-shaped pattern in both the overall population and the ≤13 years at menarche group, and a negative correlation in the >13 years at menarche group (Fig. 5A–C). Additionally, plasma Rb shows a positive univariate relationship with HGB levels, while plasma Co shows a negative univariate relationship (Fig. 5A–C). In the total population and the >13 years at menarche group, plasma Ni shows a positive univariate relationship with HGB levels (Fig. 5A and C). Conversely, in the total population and the ≤13 years at menarche group, plasma Cu showed a negative univariate relationship with HGB levels (Fig. 5A and B). All univariate metal relationships generally align with RCS regression analysis results. There was no statistically significant change in HGB levels associated with specific metals shifting from P25 to P75 when other metals are fixed at P25, P50, or P75(Fig. 6A–C). Moreover, there were no interaction effect and over-all interactions between the metals on serum TBIL (Figs. S1–S2).

Discussion

Plasma metal combine exposure was associated with HGB level which was an important biomarker for dysmenorrhea in female college students. Our study firstly found that plasma Fe, Ni, Rb had positive associations with HGB level and plasma Co was negatively associated with HGB level in female college students with dysmenorrhea. As for menarche was an important marker of adolescent development, and also was a biological risk factor of dysmenorrhea [2] and menarche age was significantly negatively correlated with the occurrence of dysmenorrhea and the average age of menarche for female college students was about 13 years old [20], we divided the total study population into two groups according to the mean age of menarche (13 years old) for subgroup analysis. We found that plasma Co, Rb only had statistical correlations with HGB level in the group of menarche age ≤13 years old, and plasma Ni only had a positive association with HGB level in the group of menarche age >13 years old. Moreover, a reverse U-shaped relationship between the five metals (Fe, Ni, Rb, Cu and Co) mixture and HGB levels in overall and the group of menarche age ≤13 years old and the positive relationship was revealed between the five metals mixture and HGB levels in the group of menarche age >13 years old.

Cobalt is one of the essential trace elements for human body, but it can cause toxicity to human body due to environmental exposure [21]. This study firstly reported that plasma Co was negatively associated with HGB level in the female college students with dysmenorrhea, especially in the group of menarche age ≤13 years old. The same relationship has been reported in previous studies. Fort, Marta et al. reported that urinary cobalt concentrations had a negative association with HGB levels in the third trimester of pregnancy women [21]. Meltzer, Helle Margrete et al. [22] revealed that cobalt concentration in whole blood decreased with increasing hemoglobin concentration in Norwegian women. Animal studies have shown that iron depletion could promote the body's absorption of cobalt [23]. The previous studies shown that the status of iron in the blood could affect the concentration of cobalt [21,24]. Based on above studies, we speculated that female college students discharged menstrual blood during their menstrual period, resulting in a decrease in hemoglobin level and a large consumption of iron, which leads to the body absorbing more cobalt.

Our study firstly found that plasma Ni, Rb was positively associated with HGB levels in female college students with dysmenorrhea. The previous studies revealed that nickel deficiency led to a decrease in the iron concentration in the body organs, resulting in the concentration of hemoglobin [25,26]. However, Li, Jian et al. [27] reported that the concentration of hemoglobin in the blood of rats decreased as the concentration of nickel in the injection body increased and Fischer, Rebecca SB et al. reported the similar results in the cohort study [28]. However, the exposure level of nickel metal in the animal experiments is much higher than the absorption level in the human body. The results of studies on the effect of nickel on human hemoglobin are inconsistent, and the relevant mechanism is still unclear. Belej, Dominik et al. revealed that Rb could be compatible with human blood in the interaction of human proteins to promote nutrient absorption [29] and played an important role in maintaining electrolyte balance and promoting metabolism in the body [30]. In our study, the median (5th, 95th) of plasma Rb concentration was 266.11(211.14, 380.10) μg/L and it was basically consistent with that in China National Nutrition Survey (CNHS 2010–2012), in which the median (2.5th, 97.5th) was 22.3 (14.0–62.0) μg/dL [31]. The relevant mechanism is not clear, and we will verify it in future studies.

The present study has some advantages. First, this study was the first to investigate the association between environmental metals exposures and hemoglobin levels, given that hemoglobin was a known risk factor for dysmenorrhea. Second, we used different exposure models, including novel co-exposure methods (LASSO and BKMR) and the traditional single-pollutant GLM approach, to evaluate 22 plasma metals combined exposure and HGB levels. Finally, this is the first epidemiological study on the effect of the combined exposure of plasma Fe, Ni, Co, and Rb on HGB levels.

Our study also had some disadvantages. First, only 253 female college students with dysmenorrhea were included in this study. The sample size was small and representative, which could not better verify the causal relationship between metal exposure and hemoglobin level. Second, there are many molecular biology mechanisms is not further explored, we will expand research in the future, to more fully understand the mechanism of the process, and for the dysmenorrhea of physical health. Finally, we recognize the limitations imposed by our cross-sectional design on drawing robust causal inferences. We deem the necessity of incorporating a control group and conducting a prospective study to strengthen our methodology. With these considerations in mind, we are committed to enhancing our research design in subsequent investigations to more accurately deduce causality.

Conclusion

Our initial study found a reverse U-shaped relationship between exposure to a mixture of five metals (Fe, Ni, Co, Rb and Cu) and hemoglobin (HGB) levels in female college students with dysmenorrhea. Ni and Rb levels in plasma were positively correlated with HGB levels, while Co and Cu showed a negative correlation. Additionally, Co, Cu, and Rb showed statistically significant correlations with HGB levels in both the overall population and the subgroup aged ≤13 years at menarche, whereas Ni showed significance in both the overall population and the subgroup aged >13 years at menarche. Further exploration into the complex interactions of metals with HGB levels is warranted.
