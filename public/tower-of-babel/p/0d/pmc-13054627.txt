Seventy‐Five Years of NIH NIDDK Funding Has Increased Knowledge and Greatly Impacted Clinical Care
Authors: Ana G. Hernandez‐Reynoso, Stacy Jeong, Tyler Trump, Margot S. Damaser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSeventy‐five years of research funding in benign Urology from the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) at the National Institutes of Health has greatly advanced not only our knowledge of etiology and mechanisms of nononcologic urological conditions but has also advanced new technologies and treatments to improve clinical care. To illustrate the profound impact of NIDDK funding on research and clinical care, we undertook a survey of grants funded by NIDDK in the NIH Reporter website with a focus on translation of lower urinary tract health and disease research to the clinic. We highlight three examples of technologies whose development was funded by NIDDK: advanced artificial urinary sphincters, urodynamics, and neuromodulation. These examples demonstrate the difficulties in bringing innovations to clinical care, as well as successes. In addition, they illustrate how public investment through NIDDK funding can improve treatment outcomes, diagnostic capabilities, and provide multiple therapeutic options.

Introduction

Urological conditions, even excluding cancer, affect millions of men and women, particularly as they age, leading to challenges in urination, continence, sexual dysfunction, and reduction in quality of life. Seventy‐five years of research funding in benign Urology from the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) at the National Institutes of Health (NIH) has greatly advanced not only our knowledge of etiology and mechanisms of these nononcologic urological conditions but has also advanced research on new technologies and treatments to improve clinical care. To illustrate the profound impact of NIDDK research and development funding on clinical care, we undertook a survey of grants funded by NIDDK in the NIH Reporter website with a focus on translation of lower urinary tract health and disease research to the clinic. The NIH Reporter website (https://reporter.nih.gov/) includes grants since 1984, limiting a full 75‐year assessment. Nonetheless, since 1985, there has been an evident and profound impact of NIDDK funding which continues up to today. Note that in this review, we refer to each research grant via the NIH grant number, which is searchable on the NIH Reporter website, provided above, should more information be desired.

We have chosen to highlight three examples of technologies whose development was funded by NIDDK since a comprehensive report on all research funded by NIDDK is out of the scope of this paper. These three examples demonstrate research and development funding that has led directly to advanced technology and product development and show the difficulties in advancing new products to clinical care, as well as those that have advanced to the point of improving care of Urologic patients. Development of advanced artificial urinary sphincters, urodynamics, and neuromodulation illustrate how public investment through NIDDK funding improves treatment outcomes, diagnostic capabilities, and provides multiple therapeutic options, all of which improve care and can provide increased efficiency and effectiveness to the health care delivery system.

Before highlighting the specific illustrative examples of urologic technologies that have advanced clinical care with funding from NIDDK, it is worth noting that these achievements rely on (i) basic research on anatomy, physiology, and molecular mechanisms of benign Urology diseases/conditions; (ii) development and study of animal models essential for mechanistic studies and testing novel therapies; (iii) collaboration between clinicians and scientists to advance knowledge and novel therapies; (iv) the volunteerism and trust of patients; their bravery in volunteering to be one of the first to receive a new device or treatment cannot be underestimated and is essential for advancing clinical care; and (v) investigators and trainees sharing findings from these studies, through presentations and publications, which lays the groundwork for developing new technologies, diagnostics, and treatments. All of these have been supported and funded by the NIDDK over the past 75 years, leading to the high quality state of the art Urologic care available to patients today. Lastly and importantly, the trust of taxpayers that their money is being spent efficiently and wisely is essential to enable NIDDK research funding for improved clinical care.

Artificial Urinary Sphincter

Early Prototypes

The earliest invention to address urinary incontinence was patented in 1948 by E.B. Foley [1]. It consisted of an externally compressive device at the penile base but lacked the ability to regulate pressure. The first prototype of what we now know as the artificial urinary sphincter (AUS) was introduced in 1972 by Brantley [2]. This was first implanted in four patients with neurogenic bladder and one patient with stress urinary incontinence. The Brantley‐Scott AUS, later known as AMS 721, contained four components: the reservoir, inflatable cuff, and two valve‐pumps. Despite its successful initial implantation, the inability to regulate urethral pressure contributed to high erosion rates [3].

Over the next decade, further advancements improved the functionality and safety of the AUS. A significant improvement to the AMS 761 was the introduction of the pressure regulating balloon. Other changes included a narrow back cuff design to reduce pressure on underlying urethral tissue, change in synthetic material to decrease fracturing, and suture‐free tubing connectors [3, 4]. Notably, the introduction of the deactivation button allowed for post‐operative and nocturnal deactivation, thus significantly improving cuff erosion rates [5].

AMS 800: The Current Gold Standard

This series of developments have contributed to what we now recognize as the gold‐standard AMS 800. Introduced in 1981, this device has three parts: a pressure regulating balloon, a circumferential, inflatable cuff, and a control pump. Overall the AMS 800 demonstrates long‐term effectiveness, with literature reporting continence rates of 61%–100%, and one study reporting a 10‐year 91% continence rate in a post‐prostatectomy population [4, 5].

Despite its long‐standing success over the past few decades, the AMS 800 has several limitations. It requires patient dexterity to maneuver the control pump. Other complications include erosion and infection in 8.5% of patients (range: 3.3%–27.8%), mechanical failure in 6.2% (range: 2%–13.8%), and a reoperation rate of 26% (range: 14.8%–44.8%) [5].

NIDDK Funded AUS Research

NIDDK has funded efforts to improve the current design or create alternative devices given the limitations of the AMS 800. To overcome the challenge of patient dexterity, Whalen proposed a magnetically‐operated AUS (NIH R43DK042752). This utilized an external magnet that compresses the metal bellows of the reservoir and allows the sphincter to close. However, animal studies demonstrated decreased vascular supply and subsequent ulcer formation from prolonged compression of the magnet on the skin [6].

Several projects have focused on non‐hydraulic mechanisms that would avoid device failure from fluid leak. Timm received NIDDK funding (NIH R43DK067699, NIH R43DK076320, NIH R43DK092007, NIH R44DK07076397) for several patented prototypes. The Timm‐AUS was one such prototype that consisted of a one‐piece device with a urethral occlusive sheath activated by a button [7], but was hindered by the development of a tough capsule when inserted into humans. Another prototype applied tension to a compressible tape wrapped around the urethra [8]. He theorized that unlike a fluid‐filled cuff, the tape would deliver an equal spring force around the urethra and thus decrease erosion risk [9, 10]. A combined hydraulic‐mechanical AUS was also created and could be implanted through a single incision [11]. Finally, the Tape Mechanical Occlusion Device [12] was a combination of these devices. This one‐piece device consisted of an occlusive tape that was turned on by a tension‐suture scrotal control mechanism and delivered radial pressure to the urethra. They underwent implementation in female canines and human cadavers with appropriate levels of intra‐urethral cuff pressures [13], but human trials were not pursued. Despite conceptual promise, these models fell out of favor as more advanced technologies emerged on the market.

NIDDK funding has also contributed to advances in pediatric urinary continence devices. Urinary incontinence in pediatric patients is caused by neurogenic sphincteric dysfunction, detrusor hyperactivity, and poor compliance [14, 15]. Conservative management options include anti‐cholinergic medications or intermittent catheterization. Other surgical treatments include bladder slings, urethral bulking, bladder neck reconstruction, and artificial urinary sphincters [15]. Less invasive options to address pediatric urinary incontinence are needed. One such design is a soft silicone urinary valve deployed at the bladder neck (NIH R43DK123908). The valve allows for continence when not in use and can open when the patient is catheterized or when pressure exceeds valve capacity. This technology is still in prototype form; therefore, additional funding is essential for further testing and innovations that are currently lacking in this area.

Looking to the Future

In 2015 the AUS Consensus group outlined features for an “ideal” AUS [16]. They suggested that this should have (1) easy manipulation and deactivation, (2) modifiable urethral cuff pressure following implantation, (3) adaptive cuff pressure, (4) simple robust design, (5) minimally invasive implantation, and (6) be cost effective. The advent of wireless technologies, including pressure sensors and Bluetooth capability, can advance the current AUS toward these ideals. One project funded by NIDDK explored implantable pressure sensors, wireless signaling technology, and shape memory alloy material to allow for real‐time measurement of bladder pressure (NIH R43DK069126), which then informs the user of the need to urinate. The device is designed to enable the user to use the control unit to signal the AUS to open and allow micturition. However, as can happen with research funding, no product appears to have yet resulted from this research investment.

Despite the durability and effectiveness of the AMS 800, challenges still remain. NIDDK funded research grants have provided support to innovations that may not always result in a marketable product but help pave the way toward a safer, reliable AUS. Continued funding is important to enable more patients to receive treatment with reduced risk of complications and need for revision. Emerging devices on the market demonstrate that this area remains an active area of research.

Urodynamics

Urodynamics is the general term used to describe any assessment of lower urinary tract function/dysfunction. These assessments can be performed in isolation or in conjunction and include non‐invasive tests, including uroflowmetry, ultrasound post‐void residual volume, and patch electromyography) and invasive tests, such as cystometry, measurement of abdominal pressure, needle electromyography, and catheterized post‐void residual volume [17]. Early work in the 19th and early 20th centuries laid the groundwork for better understanding bladder physiology and sparked interest in measuring various parameters in human patients [18]. In 1921, George Walker presented a means to measure static bladder pressure using a blood pressure manometer [19]. A few years later, in 1927, Dalton Keats Rose developed a mercury manometer which allowed for continuous monitoring with bladder filling and coined the term “cystometer” [18]. In 1948, Willard Drake developed the first uroflowmeter using gravimetric measurements to assess urinary flow [20].

The term “urodynamics” was not used in print until a publication in 1954 [21]. In subsequent decades (1960s–1990s) the practice of urodynamics was advanced and standardized largely by contributions from Frank Hinman, Emil Tanagho, Derek Griffiths, Werner Schäfer, Jerry Blaivas, Jack Lapides, Ed McGuire, Norm Zinner, Don Gleason, and others [18]. NIDDK research funding has been pivotal to advancing research related to urodynamics and development of improved urodynamics methods and technology.

In 1986 NIDDK provided funding for development of one of the first ambulatory urodynamic systems (NIH R43DK037731). The primary aim of this research was to expand on the use of urodynamics in the conventional office setting and to allow for continuous measurement beyond the office during daily activities over a prolonged period. The research grant from NIDDK also provided funding for automated analysis of the recorded data. In current practice, there are several commercial systems available that allow for wireless data collection and transmission, allowing for patients to ambulate and void beyond the constraints of typical in office urodynamic testing. It is worth noting that all these systems remain catheter‐based.

The idea of portable urodynamics was further expanded with the aid of NIDDK funding in 2005 when Dynaventions received NIDDK funding to study a catheter‐free urodynamics system (NIH R43DK070373). This system utilized an intravesical balloon which monitored urine volume, pH, temperature, and conductivity, as well as bladder pressure. An intravaginal pessary was described to measure intra‐abdominal pressure. The intravesical monitor was used in Phase 1 of the trial to determine the feasibility of using fluid volume conductance to measure bladder volume. This method was limited by variations in urine concentration and limited sensitivity with increasing bladder volume [22].

Although the previously mentioned system was not commercialized, through continued innovation and NIDDK funding, the Glean from Bright Uro, a commercially available wireless catheter‐free ambulatory urodynamics system, was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2025 (NIH R44DK131700). Initial clinical testing was conducted in a small series of patients with a non‐commercialized prototype [23]. The “urodynamics monitor” did not affect capacity, sensation, or urine flow and captured 98% of urodynamic events in the initial feasibility study which included adult females with overactive bladder symptoms [23]. Since the initial feasibility study, the system has been further refined and studied in a neurogenic bladder population, which demonstrated lower post void residual volume with the wireless device compared to catheter‐based urodynamics [24].

Support from NIDDK has not been limited to invasive urodynamics. UroGenie received funding to aid development of their home uroflowmetry device (NIH R43DK037731). Previously, uroflowmetry relied on a specific toilet in the clinical setting which then transmitted data to a paper print out. The UroGenie represents one example of a portable device that allows for uroflowmetry to be performed in any setting (clinic, home, etc.) and electronically transmits the data to an online server or the electronic medical record [25].

The field of urodynamics has evolved significantly due to funding from sources such as NIDDK. Both patients and clinicians have benefited from these advances. For example, patients in the near future will be able to provide urodynamic data from more comfortable settings, including from the comfort of their own home, with the Glean ambulatory urodynamics monitor and the at home uroflowmeter. Clinicians could then use this more physiologic data that can be collected over a longer period during normal activities rather than in isolation, to have better insight into a patient's condition and to guide therapy. Moving forward, NIDDK continues to fund innovation which will drive healthcare forward. For instance, NIDDK funded a team at Mayo Clinic aiming to obtain cystometric data non‐invasively. The team is utilizing quantitative ultrasound bladder vibrometry to record detrusor pressure with the goal of detecting detrusor overactivity and prognostically identifying BPH patients who would benefit from therapy (NIH R01DK131685). Continued funding from sponsors such as NIDDK will continue to drive innovation forward, improving urodynamics for both patients and physicians.

Neuromodulation

A set of preclinical and clinical investigations in the early 1970s by Blaine S. Nashold and Harry Friedman, using electrical stimulation of the spinal cord to control neurogenic bladder and resulting in bladder contractions and spontaneous voiding [26] sparked great interest from the NIH to restore bladder function in patients with spinal cord injuries [27]. Since then, NIDDK has been fundamental in the development and translation of neuromodulation for urology, especially for studies that have provided insights into electrophysiological and neuroanatomical targets controlling bladder function, parameter optimization, miniaturization of implantable devices, rechargeable and MRI‐safe capabilities, and remote programming of stimulation parameters. This support has resulted in the clinical use of sacral neuromodulation (SNM), and tibial nerve stimulation (TNS), as described below.

Sacral Nerve Neuromodulation

In the early 1970s, two groups independently pioneered sacral anterior root stimulation as a potential treatment for urinary dysfunction after spinal cord injury [28, 29]. The work by Tanagho et al., largely funded by the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS) and NIDDK, contributed to the development of SNM. Their research initially focused on stimulating the spinal cord and spinal anterior roots in dogs with spinal cord injuries, leading to bladder emptying [30, 31]. Human clinical trials followed in 1982, showing an increase in bladder capacity in participants with neurogenic bladder [32]. An incidental observation was that sacral root stimulation immediately inhibited detrusor overactivity [27], leading to the development of SNM to inhibit overactive bladder. As a result, Medtronic developed the first sacral neuromodulator, Interstim Therapy, receiving European CE approval in 1994 and FDA approval in October 1997 to treat urge urinary incontinence.

SNM has demonstrated approximately 67% long‐term success rate [33]. Since its approval, advances supported by NIDDK have centered around three areas: (1) Understanding the mechanisms of action, (2) developing new and improved devices, (3) refining and expanding therapeutic applications.

Understanding the Mechanisms of Action

The precise mechanism of action of SNM is not yet fully understood; however, it is widely accepted that it involves the multifactorial modulation of spinal and supraspinal reflexes via peripheral afferent pathways [34]. NIDDK has supported investigations in this area, in part through a variety of awards and mechanisms, including NIH R01DK037241, NIH R01DK049480, NIH R01DK053063, NIH K08DK065759, NIH R01DK094905, NIH R01DK102427, and NIH F32DK098904. Briefly, this research has determined that SNM inhibits bladder contraction by blocking afferent input from the pelvic viscera conveyed by the pelvic nerve; inhibits neural transmission of sensory signals from the bladder to the pontine micturition center, blocking the micturition reflex that initiates bladder contraction; blocks c‐fiber action potentials that may contribute to inflammation‐related detrusor overactivity; and may influence the supraspinal networks that are responsible for detrusor overactivity, bladder filling sensation, urge, and micturition timing [35, 36].

Developing New and Improved Devices

The transformation of SNM from a theoretical concept to a clinically valuable system has been heavily dependent on advances in device technology. Devices are now smaller, with longer battery‐life or rechargeable batteries, MRI‐safe, and can be programmed remotely via handsets. Lead design has advanced to deliver efficacious electrical stimulation through tined leads that prevent migration by self‐anchoring at the time of implantation [37]. In particular, support from NIDDK led to the development of a miniaturized, multi‐channel stimulator to improve the selectivity of sacral neuromodulation, implementing a radiofrequency communication link between the stimulator and external programmer (NIH R43DK052730, NIH R44DK052730).

Refining and Expanding Therapeutic Applications

SNM has improved by refining stimulation parameters, targets, and patient selection, supported in part by NIDDK. A transformative step was the development of implantation of SNM in two phases, a trial phase followed by a permanent implant phase, resulting in an 82% success rate for overactive bladder responders after 5 years, as well as 76% responders for urge incontinence and 71% responders for urge incontinence with frequency [33]. Animal models have been used to optimize stimulation parameters and neural targets stemming from identified anatomical variations resulting in differential bladder outcomes (NIH K01DK121866) [38] and computational modeling to optimize urinary outcomes (NIH R01DK133605) [39]. A recent project is currently attempting to optimize sacral lead placement using a photoacoustic guidance tool to ensure proximity to the target nerve, despite anatomical differences (NIH R43DK139914). NIDDK funding has contributed to the expansion of therapeutic indications, including nociceptive bladder overactivity and the treatment of fecal incontinence, including NIH U34DK109191, NIH R01DK121698, and NIH R01DK091253. As a direct result of NIDDK funding for the past 75 years, two FDA‐approved sacral neuromodulators for bladder and bowel dysfunction are clinically available in the U.S.: Interstim Therapy System by Medtronic and Axonics Therapy System recently acquired by Boston Scientific.

Tibial Nerve Stimulation

The first instance of TNS in the treatment of bladder overactivity was described in 1983 [40], drawing inspiration from acupuncture. Results in 15 patients with neurogenic bladders showed promise to reduce detrusor overactivity and symptoms of urgency. In 1999, Marshall L. Stoller described the use of percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation in 90 patients using a 34‐gauge needle [41]. The therapy consisted of weekly stimulation sessions of 20–30 min for 10 weeks, showing 81% responders with at least 50% reduction in urge symptoms. These results contributed to the FDA‐clearance in 2000 of the first TNS device for the treatment of overactive bladder. NIDDK has funded investigation into the mechanism of action of TNS, including NIH R01DK091253, NIH R01DK090006, and NIH R01DK094905. While the complete mechanism of action for TNS is not fully understood, inhibition of afferent nerve input to the sacral segments of the spinal cord is acknowledged as the primary mechanism [42, 43, 44]. NIDDK has supported the translation of TNS by funding development of new devices to achieve wireless, fully autonomous therapy (NIH R43DK101147 and NIH R44DK121578).

There are four non‐invasive (transcutaneous) or minimally invasive (percutaneous) TNS systems cleared by the FDA to date. In addition, there is a wireless, fully implantable, minimally invasive TNS system available. Even if the companies currently marketing these devices did not directly receive funding from NIDDK, the investment of public funding into investigation of mechanism of action, advanced device development and additional therapeutic applications, has greatly contributed to the broad range of neuromodulation systems available for patients today.

Conclusions

In conclusion, NIDDK funding has directly lead to development of improved methods of diagnosing and treating urologic conditions, and continues to do so. In addition, 75 years of NIDDK funding of basic and translational science contributes to future improvements to Urologic care through better understanding of urologic conditions. This paper has provided 3 examples which illustrate that, although not all funding leads directly to commercial products, the aggregate of funding overall has high value for improving knowledge and care. Only with continued support from NIDDK can urologic care continue to remain state of the art.
