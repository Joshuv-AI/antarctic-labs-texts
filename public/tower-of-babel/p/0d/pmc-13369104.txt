Overexpression of the BAHD Acyltransferase-Like Protein gene OsDCR Enhances Salt Tolerance in Rice
Authors: Xi Liu, Linqian Li, Mengjie Xu, Nan Lu, Yunlong Zhang, Xinhe Wang, Rui Shi, Fang Wang, Jianhui Ji, Qingsong Gao, Maodi Zhu, Haibo Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalt stress is a major environmental challenge for global rice production because it disrupts ionic balance and induces oxidative damage. We investigated the role of the rice BAHD-acyltransferase-like protein gene OsDCR in the salt stress response and found that OsDCR-overexpressing plants showed enhanced salt tolerance. In contrast, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated-knockout rice lines were sensitive to salt stress. OsDCR-OE plants also exhibited reduced oxidative stress and high ABA accumulation under 150 mM NaCl, and quantitative RT-PCR analysis revealed that the expression of OsDCR was induced by salt stress and high temperature. Additionally, we identified a proline-rich protein, OsPRP3, as an interaction partner of OsDCR by yeast two-hybrid, LCI and Co-IP assays. Similar to OsDCR, OsPRP3 also positively regulated rice salt tolerance and acted downstream of OsDCR. Taken together, our findings suggest that OsDCR functions as a positive regulator of salt tolerance by modulating ABA biosynthesis and interacting with OsPRP3. Our results regarding the molecular role of OsDCR provide novel insights for developing salt-tolerant rice cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-026-00918-4.Keywords: Oryza sativa L., Salt tolerance, OsPRP3, ABA biosynthesis, Positive regulation

Background

Soil salinization is one of the most serious abiotic stresses to crop growth and yield worldwide and has been responsible for significant adverse effects on food security (Choi et al. 2025; Sackey et al. 2025), as well as global agricultural production losses in excess of $20 billion annually. Among cereal crops, rice is the most sensitive to salt stress, especially during the germination and seedling stages (van Zelm et al. 2020; Saleem et al. 2025). High salt stress directly leads to decreased germination and emergence rates in rice. Complicating matters is the fact that salt tolerance is a complex quantitative trait controlled by multiple genes. More than 500 QTLs related to salt stress have already been isolated, but only a few QTL genes have been cloned (Li et al. 2021; Marè et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2024). Four major salt tolerance-related genes SKC1/OsHKT1;5, qSTS5/STG5, RST1, and OsWRKY53 were previously isolated through positional cloning and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in rice (Ren et al. 2005; Deng et al. 2022; Yu et al. 2023; Wei et al. 2024). For example, OsHKT1;5 encodes a Na+-selective transporter, and regulating K+/Na+ homeostasis under salt stress. OsHKT1;5 expression is controlled by a protein complex that consists of OsBAG4, OsMYB106, and OsSUVH7. qSTS5/STG5 and OsWRKY53 maintain Na+ /K+ homeostasis by regulating the OsHKT1;5 expression and can also be isolated by GWAS analysis (Wang et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2023). However, the main determining factors and genetic mechanisms of salt tolerance have yet to be elucidated in rice.

Abscisic acid (ABA) is a stress hormone in plants (Nambara and Marion-Poll 2003) that is widely involved in response to diverse abiotic stresses, including drought, high temperature, high salt, and low temperature, and plays a critical role in plants adaptation to adverse environments (Shu et al. 2016). The ABA biosynthesis pathway mainly involves three enzymes, zeaxanthin epoxidase OsABA1, Xanthoxin dehydrogenase OsABA2, and 9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase (NCED). OsABA1 and OsABA2 mutations lead to reduced ABA accumulation and abiotic stress-sensitive phenotypes (Agrawal et al. 2001; Liao et al. 2018; Xiong et al. 2025), and enhanced expression of NCEDs, the key rate-limiting enzyme of ABA biosynthesis, promotes ABA biosynthesis and confers the abiotic stress tolerance (Zhou et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2023).

Proline-rich protein (PRP) is a type of cell-wall-associated protein that plays important roles in drought and cold stress responses in plants (Gothandam et al. 2010; Gujjar et al. 2018; Li et al. 2019; Nawaz et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2023a, b). Proline accumulation helps a cell to maintain its water content and preserve normal cellular function under stress. Under drought stress, tomato proline-rich protein SIPRP rapidly decreases with proline accumulation (Gujjar et al. 2018). Down-regulation of PnPRPL1 in Panax notoginseng leaves has been shown to increase susceptibility to Fusarium solani, whereas overexpression of PnPRPL1 in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) has been found to enhance resistance to Fusarium solani (Su et al. 2024). In rice, OsPRP1 enhances cold tolerance by modulating antioxidants and maintaining cross talk through signaling pathways (Nawaz et al. 2019). Similar to OsPRP1, overexpression of OsPRP3 significantly enhances rice cold tolerance by improving the integrity of plant cell wall (Gothandam et al. 2010). In addition, OsPRP3 RNAi plants have significant defects in flower organs, suggesting that OsPRP3 may be essential for flower development. Gm8, a proline-rich protein, enhances the resistance of rice to gall midges, and researchers have developed a functional marker (PRP-del) for detecting Gm8 (Divya et al. 2018). However, the specific regulatory mechanisms of PRPs in salt stress signaling remain largely unclear.

In this study we identified a novel salt stress-associated gene, OsDCR, and investigated the functional interaction between OsDCR and OsPRP3. Our results reveal that OsDCR acts as a positive regulator of salt stress and directly interacts with OsPRP3, thus highlighting the vital role of OsDCR in modulating salt tolerance in rice.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials

Overexpression and knock-out of the OsDCR plants used in this study were generated as previously described (Liu et al. 2025). Rice materials were planted in the biotechnology park of Huaiyin Normal University, Huai’an City, Jiangsu Province from May to October in 2022–2024.

Salt Stress and Fluridone Treatments

The dry seeds of wild type, OsDCROE-1, OsDCROE-2, osdcra, and osdcrb rice were washed three times with sterile water and subjected to imbibition at 37 °C for 3 days in the dark. Then, consistently-germinated seeds were cultured in 96-well hydroponic boxes with Yoshida rice nutrient salts solution (NS1040, Coolaber, Beijing, China) under a cycle of 14-h light at 30 °C and 10-h dark at 25 °C. For salt treatment, 14-d-old seedlings of wild type, OsDCROE-1, OsDCROE-2, osdcra, and osdcrb were treated with 150 mM NaCl for 7 d followed by a 7 d recovery. For fluridone treatment, 14-d-old seedlings of wild type and OsDCROE-2 were treated with 150 mM NaCl and 10 µM fluridone (IF3480, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 7 d followed by a 7 d recovery.

Determination of Antioxidant, ABA Contents and Cell Wall Polysaccharides

To determine catalase activity (CAT) and malondialdehyde (MDA) content, 2-week-old seedlings were transferred to nutrient solution containing (or without) 150 mM NaCl for 3 days. A 0.1 g sample of the plants was ground into powder by liquid nitrogen, mixed with 1 mL of extraction buffer, and further homogenized. The subsequent procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions provided with the MDA and CAT assay kits (Nanjing Zhihui Biotechnology Co., Ltd).

For ABA content measurement, 1 g leaves of 2-week-old WT, OsDCROE-1, and OsDCROE-2 with 150 mM NaCl treatments for 3 days were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and ground to a dry powder. ABA was quantified by Pronetbio (https://www.bestofbest.top/, Nanjing, China) using the AB Sciex QTRAP6500 liquid chromatograph-MS/MS platform with three biological replicates. To measure cell wall polysaccharides, the 2-week-old seedlings of WT and OsDCROE-2 were collected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and ground into fine powder. Then, the samples were tested by Qingdao Future Co., Ltd (Kotake et al. 2011).

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR) Analysis

Total RNA was extracted and purified from rice tissues using a Polysaccharide Polyphenols Plant RNA Extraction Kit (YK1580, SenWeis, Huaian, China), and reverse transcription was performed using a PrimeScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TaKaRa). Next, qRT-PCR was performed using a SYBR Premix Ex TaqTM kit (Takara) and the CFX96 Touch Real-time PCR Detection System. The PCR reaction system consists of 8.8 µL cDNA, 1.2 µL primers, and 10 µL qPCR Mix.Three biological replicates were used, and the rice UBQ (LOC_Os03g13170) gene provided an internal control (Cai et al. 2025).

Yeast Two-Hybrid Analysis

The full length coding sequences (CDSs) of OsDCR and OsPRP3 were amplified with the WT cDNA. OsDCR and OsPRP3 were then inserted into the pGBKT7 and pGADT7 vectors, respectively. Yeast two-hybrid assay was then performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Clontech, Baoriyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). Briefly, these recombinations were co-transformed into the AH109 yeast strain on the SD/-Trp-Leu plates at 30 °C for 3 days and then transferred to SD/-Trp-Leu-Ade-His plates for 5 days. Yeast strain AH109 could grow normally on quadruple dropout medium, indicating an interaction between the proteins. Primers used for vector construct are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Luciferase Complementation Imaging (LCI) Assay

The full-length CDSs of OsPRP3 and OsDCR were amplified and cloned in-frame with the sequence encoding N-terminal and C-terminal half of firefly luciferase to form OsPRP3-nLUC and OsDCR-cLUC constructs, respectively. The above constructs were subsequently introduced into Agrobacterium strain EHA105, and four different combinations of EHA105 strains were co-infiltrated into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves as previously described (Zhou et al. 2023). After 48 h, leaves were harvested and incubated with 1 mM luciferin (E1601, Promega) for 3 min, and luciferase activities were measured using an imaging apparatus (NightShade LB 985, Berthold). Primers used for vector construct are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) Assays

The coding sequences of OsDCR and OsPRP3 were separately cloned into FLAG and GFP tagging plasmids, and the experiments were performed as previously described (Nam et al. 2023). Fusion constructs were introduced into the EHA105 strain and infiltrating strain combinations into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves for 36 ~ 48 h. Then, total leaf lysates were prepared in an ice-cold IP buffer (50 mM Tris‐MES, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.5 M sucrose, 10 mM EDTA, 5 mM DTT, 0.1% TritonX‐100, and 1× incubation mixture of protease inhibitors) and were incubated for 2 h at 4 °C with shaking. The proteins were then separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and detected by immunoblotting with anti-FLAG (AE121; ABclonal, Wuhan, China) and anti-GFP (AE078; ABclonal, Wuhan, China) antibodies.

RNA-seq Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from leaves of WT and OsDCROE-2 plants with 150 mM NaCl treatments with three biological replicates. RNA purity and quantity were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Gene libraries were then constructed and sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq™ 2500 (Gene denovo-Guangzhou). Finally, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were evaluated using p values < 0.05 and |log2 (FoldChange)| >1.

CRISPR/Cas9 Editing of OsPRP3

We used CRISPR/Cas9 technology to knockout the OsPRP3 gene in wild type Zhonghua 11 and OsDCROE-2, respectively. The OsPRP3 encoding region was selected as the knockout target, and the construct was cloned into the vector pCMBIA1305.1 before transformation into rice callus (Liu et al. 2021). Target identification primers (Supplementary Table 1) were designed to identify the knocked out lines using Primer 5.0 software.

Results

OsDCR Positively Regulates Rice Salt Tolerance

OsDCR has previously been identified as a key regulator of the GA and BR synthesis pathways, and mutations in OsDCR result in the altered expression of numerous stress-related genes (Liu et al. 2025). However, the role of OsDCR in regulating stress responses still remains unclear, however. To investigate this, the expression of OsDCR with heat and NaCl treatments was measured in japonica rice Dongjin (wild type, WT). The results showed that the expression level of OsDCR in the WT remarkably increased after heat and NaCl treatments (Fig. 1A). To analyze the impact of OsDCR on stress responses, OsDCR-OE-1 and OsDCR-OE-2 lines were also subjected to NaCl treatment. As shown in Fig. 1B-D, the survival rate of WT plants was 19%, whereas those of OsDCR-OE-1 and OsDCR-OE-2 were 41% and 60%, respectively. Consistently, the survival rates of knock-out of OsDCR (osdcra and osdcrb) lines were significantly lower than that of Nipponbare (NPB) (Figure S1). Under salt stress, Na+ concentration and the MDA content of the overexpression lines were also significantly lower than WT (Fig. 1E-F), and the CAT activity of the overexpression lines was significantly higher than WT (Fig. 1G).

OsDCR Overexpression Alters ABA Content During Salt Treatment

ABA plays an important role in signal regulation during a plant’s response to adverse stress (Ma et al. 2025). Accordingly, we measured ABA contents in wild-type and OsDCR overexpression plants under salt stress. These results showed that while ABA contents were similar among WT, OsDCR-OE-1, and OsDCR-OE-2 under normal conditions, ABA contents were significantly higher in OsDCR overexpression plants than in wild-type plants following salt stress treatment (Fig. 2A). Consistently, the expression levels of genes related to ABA biosynthesis (e.g. OsABA1, OsNCED1, OsNCED2) were significantly increased, and the expression levels of genes related to ABA degradation (OsABA8ox1 and OsABA8ox3) were repressed (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, we treated wild-type and OsDCR-OE-2 plants with fluridone, an ABA biosynthesis inhibitor. The results showed that the survival rate of OsDCR-OE-2 treated with fluridone was significantly decreased under salt stress (Fig. 2C–E).

OsDCR Interacts with OsPRP3

As mentioned above, OsDCR was found to be a positive regulator in salt stress response in rice. To explore the molecular mechanism by which OsDCR regulates rice salt tolerance, we next screened a rice cDNA library by the yeast-two hybrid using the CDS of OsDCR as the bait. We identified 34 potential interacting proteins of OsDCR (Supplementary Table S2). Among the multiple prey cDNAs obtained from the screen, one encoded a putative 28.9 kDa proline-rich protein (PRP) corresponding to the previously characterized OsPRP3 (Gothandam et al. 2010; Fig. 3A), which was therefore selected for further investigation.A further LCI assay was performed to validate this interaction between OsDCR and OsPRP3. When OsDCR and OsPRP3 were co-expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves, a strong luciferase signal was detected, but no fluorescent signal was observed in the negative control (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, we also performed Co-IP assay using Nicotiana benthamiana leaves co-expressing OsDCR-FLAG and OsPRP3-GFP, and the immunoblot analysis showed that OsDCR interacted with OsPRP3 in vivo (Fig. 3C).

OsPRP3 Positively Regulates Salt Tolerance in Rice

To gain an understanding of the role of OsPRP3 under salt stress, we first analyzed whether the expression level of OsPRP3 was induced by salt stress. Compared to that before salt treatment, the expression level of OsPRP3 significantly increased after salt treatment (Figure S2A). Subcellular localization indicated that OsPRP3 is a multi-localization protein (Figure S2B). Finally, we generated two OsPRP3 knock-out lines via CRISPR-Cas9 technology (Fig. 4), and under salt stress treatment the results showed that the survival rate of control plants (ZH11) under salt stress was 32% but that the survival rates of osprp3a and osprp3b were significantly reduced, at 5% and 7%, respectively (Fig. 4D).

OsPRP3 Functions Downstream of OsDCR

To understand the role of OsPRP3 in rice salt stress response, we generated OsPRP3 knockout lines in the OsDCR-OE-2 background (designated OsDCR-OE-2/osprp3). Homozygous lines were confirmed by sequencing (Fig. 5A). Fourteen-day-old seedlings of wild type, OsDCR-OE-2, and OsDCR-OE-2/osprp3 were then exposed to 150 mM NaCl for phenotypic analysis. The survival rate of OsDCR-OE-2 was significantly higher than that of the wild-type plants, but that of OsDCR-OE-2/osprp3 was reduced compared to that of the OsDCR-OE-2 plants (Fig. 5B, C).

Key Biological Pathways Involved in Transcriptomic Changes

To explore the molecular pathway of OsDCR regulating rice salt stress response, we focused on the link of gene expression change before and after salt stress. Before salt stress, we found that 603 up- and 640 down-regulated genes between wild type and OsDCR-OE-2, but after salt stress, 1088 up- and 932 down-regulated genes between wild type and OsDCR-OE-2 were identified (Fig. 6A). Additionally, before salt stress, metabolic pathways (ko01100) and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (ko01110) were significantly enriched by DEGs, whereas differentially expressed genes were mainly enriched in biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, metabolic pathways, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940), and plant hormone signal transduction (ko04075) with salt treatment (Fig. 6B, C).

142 up- and down-regulated genes were also detected simultaneously in the wild-type group and OsDCR-OE-2 group before and after salt treatment (Fig. 6A, Table S3). Among them, we found that 40 DEGs (e.g. salt stress related genes aquaporin protein OsPIP1; 3, euonymus lectin OSR40c1, and NAC transcriptional factor OsNAC10) were significantly elevated in both WT and OsDCROE-2, but their expression levels in OsDCROE-2 were significantly higher than those in wild type before and after salt stress treatment (Fig. 6D, Table S3). In addition, 19 DEGs (e.g. receptor-like kinase Os01g0113500, AP2 transcription factor RD2, and glutaredoxin OsGRX23) were significantly repressed in both WT and OsDCROE-2, but their expression levels in OsDCROE-2 were significantly lower than those in wild type both before and after salt stress treatment (Fig. 6D, Table S3).

Discussion

OsDCR Functions as a Positive Regulator to Modulate Rice Salt Stress Tolerance

Plants have developed a diverse array of defense mechanisms that enable them to endure adverse environmental conditions (e.g. salinity, drought, and cold) (Zhu 2016; Zhang et al. 2023a, b). High salinity in particular is a major limiting factor for crop production. Exploring the genes that confer salt tolerance and deciphering their molecular mechanisms can help to guide crop genetic improvement for developing salt stress-resistant and high-yielding varieties (Van Zelm et al. 2020; Yin et al. 2024). In Arabidopsis, a BAHD acyltransferase BAT1, acts as a novel gibberellin and brassinosteroid signaling crosstalk intermediate to regulate primary seed dormancy (Roh et al. 2012; Chong et al. 2025). In rice, the BAHD acyltransferase gene OsDCR/SLG controls grain size and leaf angle by modulating brassinosteroid and gibberellin homeostasis (Feng et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2025). Additionally, overexpression of SLG has been shown to enhance Cd tolerance and significantly reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation. Similarly, overexpression of SLG promotes the expression of the SA pathway genes (Zhang et al. 2025). In fact, our results showed that OsDCR directly confers salt stress tolerance in rice (Figs. 1 and S1). Overexpression of OsDCR significantly reduced MDA accumulation and increased the activity of CAT under salt stress (Fig. 1). Thus, future work will be of great interest to investigate whether OsDCR is also involved in regulating plant responses to biotic stress.

OsDCR-Mediated Salt Stress Tolerance Depends on ABA Production

ABA is known to act primarily by enhancing cellular dehydration tolerance and reducing water loss, thereby modulating plant adaptation to salt, drought, and cold stresses (Shu et al. 2016). In our study, OsDCR-overexpressing lines showed higher levels of ABA accumulation under salt stress conditions (Fig. 2A). Treatment with fluridone abolished the salt tolerance of OsDCR-OE plants, indicating that OsDCR enhances salt tolerance in rice by promoting ABA biosynthesis (Fig. 2C–E). Furthermore, 9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase (NCED) has been shown to contribute to increased ABA levels and abiotic stress tolerance in plants (Huang et al. 2018). In rice specifically, five OsNCED genes (OsNCED1 ~ 5) have already been isolated (Xiang et al. 2025). Among them, OsNCED1 and OsNCED3 are thought to be correlated with alkaline and heat stress-induced ABA biosynthesis (Feng et al. 2024; Xiang et al. 2025). In the present study, OsDCR-overexpressing plants had higher expression of genes involved in ABA biosynthesis than wild type under salt stress, especially the OsNCED1 gene (Fig. 2B). Thus, understanding how OsNCEDs are activated in response to salt stress by OsDCR overexpression is key to providing insights into the mechanisms that govern plant acclimation to abiotic stress.

OsDCR-OsPRP3 Interaction Implicates Cell Wall Modulation in Rice Salt Stress Tolerance

Plant cell walls provide structural support and determine cell shape as well as play a key role in defending against abiotic stresses such as salinity (Bacete and Mélida, 2023). Several studies have reported that defects in cell wall polysaccharide biosynthesis affect plant tolerance to salt stress. Arabidopsis cesa6 mutant displays enhanced root cell swelling under high salt conditions (Zhang et al. 2016), and salt stress induces GALS1 expression in roots, leading to the accumulation of β-1,4 galactan, a major component of pectins, which reduces root growth (Colin et al. 2023). In rice, the 1,4-β-D-xylan synthase gene OsCSLD4 mutant displays reduced salt tolerance, whereas the OsCSLD4 overexpression lines exhibit increased salt tolerance (Liu et al. 2022). In another study, a pectin methyltransferase OsTSD2-mediated cell wall modification was found to affect ion homeostasis and salt tolerance (Fang et al. 2019), and overexpression of UDP-galactose/glucose epimerase 3 (OsUGE3) has been found to promote the biosynthesis of cellulose and hemicellulose, which contributes to enhanced salt tolerance (Tang et al. 2022).

OsCESA9 mutation results in altered cell wall properties and increased salt tolerance (Ye et al. 2021). In our study, we found that OsPRP3 was the target gene of OsDCR (Fig. 4) and operated downstream of OsDCR (Fig. 5). Evidence shows that cold treatment induces the accumulation of the OsPRP3 protein, which enhances plant cell wall integrity (Gothandam et al. 2010). Previous proteomic analysis revealed that the abundance of OsPRP3 protein was significantly increased in OsDCR overexpression lines compared with the wild type, suggesting that the OsDCR-OsPRP3 interaction may contribute to maintaining OsPRP3 protein stability (Figure S3, Liu et al. 2025). Given that OsDCR is predicted to function as an acyltransferase, it is reasonable to speculate that OsDCR may acylate the proline-rich domain of OsPRP3, thereby affecting protein stability. Further biochemical assays will be performed to verify the acylation modification and elucidate the molecular mechanism of the OsDCR-OsPRP3 module. Additionally, the lignin content was significantly higher in OsDCR overexpressing plants compared to wild type (Figure S4C). These results suggest that OsDCR likely influences cell wall composition by interacting with OsPRP3, which in turn facilitates the response to salt stress.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that the biological function of OsDCR in responses to salt stress in rice. Plants overexpressing OsDCR were more tolerant to salt stress, whereas knockout lines were more sensitive to salt stress. Under salt stress, ABA biosynthesis was enhanced to confer tolerance. Moreover, OsDCR can interact with OsPRP3 in vivo and in vitro, implying a potential effect on salt stress resistance mediated by the cell wall. These findings provide a reference for further in-depth study of OsDCR and for the genetic improvement of salt tolerance in rice.
