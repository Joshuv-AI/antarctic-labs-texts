Scavenging Meets Reinforcement: A Dual‐Functional Electrolyte Additive Approach to Dendrite‐Free Lithium‐Metal All‐Solid‐State Batteries Under Low‐Pressure
Authors: Seong Gyu Lee, Kyu Seok Kim, Seihyun Shim, Dayoung Jun, Ji Eun Jung, Tae Eun Kim, Jeongmin Lee, Eunji Seo, Se Hwan Park, Yun Jung Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmong the challenges facing Li‐metal all‐solid‐state‐batteries (ASSBs), achieving stable low‐pressure operation remains a formidable task owing to limited interfacial contact and Li‐dendrite growth. In this study, a simple yet scalable approach is presented to address these issues via a dual‐functional additive strategy. Sulfide‐based solid electrolytes (SEs) are reformulated by incorporating mechanically robust and lithium‐scavenging Li4Ti5O12 (LTO) particles through powder mixing and cold pressing. Careful control of particle size localized smaller LTO particles at grain boundaries and pores without disrupting the bulk Li‐ion conduction network. The resulting LTO‐incorporated composite solid electrolyte (LTO‐CSE) simultaneously offers mechanical reinforcement and electrochemical scavenging/current homogenization via zero‐strain lithiation, without imposing mechanical stress within the SE matrix. The LTO‐CSE exhibits enhanced stability at high current densities even under low stack pressures, without requiring warm isostatic pressing, not in pouch cells but in custom‐built spring‐loaded cells. Notably, it raises the critical current density from 4.5 to 7.5 mA cm−2 at 10 MPa. Furthermore, full cells demonstrate over 900 stable cycles without short‐circuiting, delivering a high areal capacity of ≈3.5 mAh cm−2 under 10 MPa, and stable operation even at pressures as low as 2 MPa. This work establishes a generalizable design framework for next‐generation solid‐state batteries.Keywords: all‐solid‐state batteries, lithium, low‐pressure, scalability, solid electrolyte

Introduction

Non‐flammability and rigidity are hallmarks of solid‐electrolytes (SEs), endowing all‐solid‐state batteries (ASSBs) with enhanced thermal and mechanical stability.[ 1 , 2 ] In particular, the rigid solid nature of SE enables the utilization of lithium (Li) metal, with a high theoretical capacity of 3860 mAh g−1, as an anode material in ASSBs.[ 3 , 4 ] Among various types of SEs, sulfide‐based SEs have garnered particular attention due to their high ionic conductivity comparable to that of liquid electrolytes,[ 5 , 6 ] a Li+ transference number (tLi+) close to 1,[ 7 , 8 ] and partial ductility that facilitates cold press fabrication without requiring the high‐temperature sintering typically needed for oxide‐based SEs. However, ASSBs inherently suffer from interfacial instability stemming from the solid‐solid nature of all internal components. Insufficient physical contact results in elevated interfacial resistance, while the narrow electrochemical stability window of sulfide‐ and halide‐based SEs further exacerbates interfacial side reactions,[ 9 ] in contrast to oxide‐based SEs, which generally possess wider stability windows. However, despite their wider windows, oxide SEs typically suffer from high‐temperature sintering requirements, which limit their practical applicability compared to sulfide‐based SEs. During continuous Li plating and stripping, such non‐uniform contact induces localized current concentration, thereby promoting dendritic Li growth and ultimately leading to short‐circuit failure of the cell.[ 10 ]

These challenges are further exacerbated under low stack pressure, where insufficient contact and repeated electrochemical cycling induce mechanical instability such as delamination and volume fluctuations.[ 11 , 12 ] Under such conditions, the elevated local current density accelerates dendrite formation[ 13 ] and subsequent penetration into the SE matrix generates high internal stress.[ 12 ] This stress facilitates crack formation and its propagation within the SE, ultimately resulting in failure of the cell.[ 14 ] To mitigate these issues, most previous studies have operated ASSBs under high stack pressures ranging from 20 to 50 MPa,[ 15 , 16 , 17 ] conditions that are largely incompatible with practical and scalable battery systems. Furthermore, processes such as warm isostatic pressing (WIP) are often used to densify the SE and enhance interface uniformity.[ 18 , 19 ] However, WIP typically requires high pressures up to 4000 bar, combined with elevated temperatures, thereby substantially increasing both processing complexity and cost, and ultimately hindering its application for large‐scale manufacturing. In addition to such processing‐intensive approaches, material‐level approaches (e.g., electrolyte doping, coating, composite formation, and additive incorporation) and system‐level approaches (e.g., interfacial engineering, manufacturing processes, stack pressure control, and cell configuration) have been explored to mitigate anodic interfacial instability and suppress dendrite growth. Among them, the application of protective interfacial coatings between the SE and the anode has been widely employed. Nonetheless, this approach presents several inherent limitations, including its lack of bulk mechanical reinforcement for the SE and the process complexity associated with achieving uniform, conformal coatings. Accordingly, there is a pressing need for a design strategy that enables stable operation of ASSBs under low stack pressure, provides intrinsic mechanical strengthening of SE, and eliminates the reliance on additional post‐processing steps, thereby offering a fabrication process that is both simple and scalable. In this context, the reformulation of SE via the incorporation of functional additives represents a promising design direction.

While electrolyte additives have been extensively explored to stabilize Li metal anodes in liquid electrolyte systems, the vast majority of reported strategies rely exclusively on chemical stabilization mechanisms, such as surface film formation,[ 20 , 21 ] electrostatic shielding[ 22 , 23 ] or electrochemical scavenging[ 24 ] of intermediates. Physical blocking of lithium dendrite growth, on the other hand, has been primarily addressed through separator modifications[ 25 , 26 ] or solid‐state electrolytes, rather than by molecular or particulate additives dissolved or dispersed within liquid electrolytes. This is largely due to the inherent nature of liquid electrolytes, where dissolved additives do not intrinsically reinforce the bulk electrolyte matrix; instead, their contribution to dendrite suppression mainly relies on forming SEI (Solid Electrolyte Interphase) layers upon decomposition at the electrode surface. Consequently, truly dual‐functional additives capable of both electrochemical scavenging and mechanical blocking within liquid electrolyte systems remain extremely rare.

In contrast, the incorporation of mechanically rigid, electrochemically active particles into sulfide‐based SEs offers a promising opportunity to simultaneously achieve physical and electrochemical control over Li dendrite propagation. In this work, we introduce a dual‐functional additive approach by incorporating Li4Ti5O12 (LTO) particles into sulfide‐based solid electrolytes (Li6PS5Cl, LPSCl). The prior efforts in modifying sulfide SE have primarily focused on doping to improve ionic conductivity,[ 27 ] or on forming composites between sulfide and oxide SEs,[ 28 ] such as Li3PS4‐Li7La3Zr2O12 [ 29 ] systems, to synergistically combine the high conductivity of sulfide SEs with the interfacial stability of oxide SEs against Li metal. Our strategy for reformulating sulfide SEs adopts a fundamentally different approach, employing a zero‐strain, mechanically robust, and lithium‐scavenging additive (LTO). The LTO particles, owing to their small size relative to the electrolyte matrix, preferentially reside at grain boundaries and pores between larger sulfide SE particles, without compromising the continuity of the Li‐ion conduction network in the bulk SE. Concurrently, they provide mechanical resistance to dendrite penetration and electrochemically scavenging lithium via zero‐strain lithiation, thereby preserving interfacial stability without imposing mechanical stress within the electrolyte framework. This composite solid electrolyte (CSE) was fabricated by a simple yet scalable process involving powder mixing followed by cold pressing. The resulting LTO‐CSE exhibited outstanding cycling stability at elevated current densities even under low stack pressures, which is attributed to the effective suppression of dendritic Li growth through the SEs, achieved without applying WIP, not in pouch cells but in custom‐built spring‐loaded pressurized cells. Notably, the enhanced stability imparted by the LTO‐CSE raised the critical current density (CCD) from 4.5 cm−2 for the pristine LPSCl SE to 7.5 mA cm−2 at 10 MPa. Furthermore, full cells incorporating high‐loading cathodes achieved stable cycling over 900 cycles without short‐circuiting, delivering a high areal capacity of ≈3.5 mAh cm−2 under 10 MPa. The full cell with LTO‐CSE also exhibited stable operation even under significantly reduced pressure conditions, as low as 2 MPa. This work not only streamlines the fabrication process, but also offers a generalizable design platform essential for the scalable deployment of next‐generation solid‐state batteries.

Results and Discussion

Characteristics of Composite Solid Electrolyte Incorporated with LTO

The fundamental requirements for a dual‐functional additive capable of inhibiting dendritic Li growth into the SE are as follows: i) the ability to absorb Li or undergo lithiation, thereby serving as an electrochemical scavenger; ii) sufficient mechanical robustness to act as a physical barrier and to reinforce the SE mechanically. To ensure both structural enhancement and interfacial stability of the electrolyte layer without compromising inherent ionic functionality of the SE, several additional criteria must be met: i) minimal disruption to the ion‐conducting pathways within the SE matrix; ii) electronic insulation to prevent the formation of electronically conductive channels; iii) a zero‐strain characteristic, exhibiting negligible volume change upon lithiation, thereby minimizing mechanical stress evolution within the electrolyte matrix; iv) enhanced processability to enable facile and scalable fabrication without requiring complex or energy‐intensive processing steps.

LTO, a widely used anode material in conventional lithium‐ion batteries, was selected as the functional additive owing to its favorable electrochemical and mechanical characteristics. LTO reacts with Li ions at a potential below 1.57 V (vs Li/Li+), and has been reported to exhibit lithiophilicity upon initial lithiation,[ 15 ] suggesting its efficacy in absorbing Li and functioning as an electrochemical scavenger. Additionally, its inherent mechanical robustness, which originates not only from its oxide‐based nature but more importantly from its stable crystal structure, is expected to enhance structural stability and reinforce the mechanical integrity of the SE layer. Furthermore, LTO exhibits a zero‐strain property, arising from its unique Li+ insertion/extraction mechanism within the spinel structure, which results in negligible volume change (≈0.2%)[ 30 ] during the electrochemical charge/discharge process, and thus is anticipated to impose minimal mechanical stress when embedded within the SE matrix.

To fabricate CSE incorporating LTO as a functional additive, nanoscale LTO (300–500 nm, Figure 1B) was mixed with the micron‐sized sulfide‐based LPSCl SE (3–5 µm, Figure 1A). The two components were uniformly blended via high‐energy ball milling (HEBM) and pelletized through cold pressing at 380 MPa. The crystallographic structure of the LPSCl powder used was confirmed by X‐ray diffraction (XRD), as presented in Figure S1 (Supporting Information). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Figure 1C) and backscattered electron SEM (BSE‐SEM, Figure 1D) analyses revealed homogeneous dispersion of the LTO additive throughout the SE matrix. The resulting composite is hereafter referred to as the LTO‐CSE. The particle size ratio between LTO and LPSCl (300–500 nm vs 3–5 µm, respectively) was selected based on the Furnas model,[ 31 ] which provides a theoretical basis for achieving close‐packing in binary systems. According to this model, the incorporation of smaller particles into a matrix of larger particles effectively fills interstitial voids, thereby increasing bulk density while maintaining the structural continuity of the host matrix. A diameter ratio of ≈1:6 to 1:10 is considered optimal for this purpose. Therefore, the nanoscale LTO additives are expected to preferentially occupy interparticle voids within the LPSCl matrix, enhancing overall packing density without compromising the intrinsic ion‐conducting pathways of the SE.

XRD analysis of the LTO‐CSE (Figure 1E) confirmed the absence of secondary phase formation, indicating excellent chemical compatibility between LTO and LPSCl. To assess the influence of the LTO additive on the ionic conductivities of the composite electrolyte, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted using blocking cells (Figure S2A, Supporting Information), which were fabricated by cold pressing either pristine LPSCl SE or LTO‐CSE powders at 380 MPa and operated under a stacking pressure of 5 MPa. The ionic conductivities, extracted from the Nyquist plots (Figure 1F), are summarized in Figure 1G. The ionic conductivity of pristine LPSCl SE was measured as 1.573 mS cm−1, consistent with previously reported values.[ 32 , 33 ] Meanwhile, the LTO‐CSEs containing 10 and 20 wt.% LTO additives exhibited ionic conductivities of 1.494 and 1.473 mS cm−1, respectively. These values are marginally reduced yet comparable to those of pristine LPSCl SE, demonstrating that the carefully tailored additive size enables effective incorporation into SE without significantly disrupting the Li‐ion conduction pathways. Morphological characteristics and additional parameters relevant to the ionic conductivity calculations are provided in Figure S3 and Table S1 (Supporting Information).

Beyond the overall ionic conductivity, the effect of LTO incorporation on Li+ transport at particle boundaries was further examined using Distribution of Relaxation Time (DRT) analysis combined with EIS measurements (Figure S4, Supporting Information). The DRT spectra revealed that up to 20 wt.% LTO, the grain boundary responses in the low time‐constant region (10−7–10−5 s) remained similar to those of pristine LPSCl SE, indicating that Li+ conduction pathways across particle boundaries were preserved. However, at 30 wt.% LTO, a pronounced increase in the DRT intensity was observed in this region, suggesting that excessive LTO content disrupts the percolation of LPSCl and hinders ion transport. The EIS results (Figure S4B, Supporting Information) clearly show that the LTO‐CSE with 30 wt.% LTO exhibited significantly increased bulk and grain boundary resistances. In addition, the symmetric cell test revealed an instantaneous short circuit and a reduced CCD relative to the optimized 20 wt.% sample in Figure S5 (Supporting Information). Taken together, these analyses demonstrate that optimized LTO content enhances densification and mechanical reinforcement while still preserving efficient Li+ conduction across SE grain boundaries, thereby maintaining efficient ionic transport within the CSE matrix.

The electrochemical activity of the LTO additive was evaluated via cyclic voltammetry (CV) in an anode‐less half‐cell configuration (Figure S2C, Supporting Information) assembled with LTO‐CSE (20 wt.%) (Figure S6, Supporting Information). The main Li plating/stripping peak appeared near 0 V. The redox peaks associated with the lithiation/delithiation of LTO were substantially smaller than the dominant Li plating/stripping peaks. A redox peak at approximately 1.57 V (vs Li/Li+), as indicated by the region ② in Figure S6, (Supporting Information), corresponds to the Li4Ti5O12↔Li7Ti5O12 transition of LTO. Additional redox responses observed in the potential range of 0.1–0.4 V (region ① in Figure S6, Supporting Information) are attributed to the lithiation of LTO beyond its typical anode range (LixTi5O12, x > 7).[ 17 , 34 ] In our previous report, such low‐potential lithiation of LTO was demonstrated to occur below 0.5 V.[ 17 , 34 ] This CV measurement corroborates the ability of LTO to electrochemically scavenge Li via a lithiation mechanism. Although the lithiation potentials of LTO are higher than the onset potential of Li metal deposition, the LTO additives embedded within the bulk SE are initially electrically isolated from the anode. Consequently, lithiation of these isolated LTO particles does not occur until they become electrically connected through Li dendrites. When Li is plated at potentials near 0 V and Li dendrites begin to invade the SE, they can provide electrons to the LTO additives within the bulk of the SE, thereby triggering lithiation of previously isolated LTO additives, which actively scavenge Li flux otherwise destined for dendritic growth. On the other hand, the LTO additives located at the SE‐Li interface are in direct contact with the anode and thus undergo lithiation prior to the onset of Li metal deposition, as they receive a continuous supply of electrons even at potentials >0 V. These lithiation reactions redirect the current toward the LTO lithiation sites, thereby redistributing the local current densities and alleviating current inhomogeneity, particularly when the LTO additives are positioned at interfacial defects such as voids or gaps. This current redistribution behavior further stabilizes the interface and contributes to the suppression of dendritic Li growth.

The local current redistribution around the LTO located within voids at the SE‐Li interface was investigated using a finite‐element model in COMSOL Multiphysics (detailed in Experimental Section). The model domain is shown in Figure S7 (Supporting Information), and the current distribution near the voids with and without LTO is shown in Figure S8 (Supporting Information). In the pristine SE, current focusing occurs at the edge of the voids without LTO, where the local current density increases to 0.77 mA cm−2 compared to the applied current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 (Figure S9A; Figure S9C, Supporting Information). This is because voids at the SE surface block ion transport, thereby causing current focusing and increases increasing local current density. In contrast, Li ion transport through LTO mitigates current focusing with a maximum current of 0.58 mA cm−2 when the void is filled with LTO (Figure S9B,C, Supporting Information). This reduction in local current density at the LTO‐CSE surface is consistent with the proposed current redistribution mechanism, supporting the role of LTO in mitigating interfacial current inhomogeneity.

We further evaluated the influence of the LTO additive on the physical properties of the SE. Key physical characteristics, including pore size, porosity, and microhardness, critically affect the electrochemical performance and mechanical durability of SE during Li plating/stripping processes. Pores at the SE‐Li interface diminish the effective contact area, which leads to increased local current density. During cycling, Li deposits faster at these regions of elevated current density, resulting in non‐uniform plating/stripping behavior and accelerating interfacial side reactions.[ 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 ] In addition, dendritic Li growth, initiated by such current density inhomogeneities, induces substantial mechanical stress within the SE, potentially leading to crack propagation and subsequent cell failure via internal short‐circuiting.[ 39 , 40 ] The mechanically reinforced CSE, achieved through the incorporation of LTO additives, is anticipated to exhibit enhanced resistance to crack propagation.

The pore size of the pristine LPSCl SE and the LTO‐CSE was analyzed in Figure 2A–F. Following uniaxial cold pressing of the pristine LPSCl SE and LTO‐CSEs, their surface morphologies were characterized using SEM. For each sample, four randomly selected surface regions were examined, and the corresponding images were processed using ImageJ for thresholding and area quantification. As shown in Figure 2A,D, top‐view SEM images revealed a notable difference in surface morphology upon LTO incorporation. The pristine SE exhibited substantial surface porosity with large pores of ≈1.6 µm in diameter (Figure 2C). Given the particle size of pristine SE (3–5 µm), these pores are presumed to result from insufficient particle rearrangement and void formation intrinsic to uniaxial cold pressing, which imposes pressure along a single axis and frequently leads to incomplete densification. In contrast, the LTO‐CSE exhibited a markedly more uniform and densely packed surface morphology with pore sizes significantly reduced to 200–500 nm (Figure 2F), which closely matches the typical dimensions of the LTO additive. This observation suggests that the LTO additive effectively filled the pores and grain boundaries within the SE matrix, thereby reducing overall porosity. BSE‐SEM analyses in Figure S3 (Supporting Information) further corroborate these findings. BSE images of LTO‐CSEs containing 10 and 20 wt.% LTO additives revealed preferential positioning of the additive within pore regions, confirming its role in grain boundary and void filling. The reduction in pore size and improvement in surface morphology were further substantiated by quantitative porosity analysis. In Figure 2B,E image‐based pore identification revealed a distinct contrast in pore distribution between the pristine LPSCl SE and LTO‐CSE. The box plot in Figure 2G clearly demonstrates that the porosity of LTO‐CSEs is significantly lower than that of pristine LPSCl SE. For each sample, porosity measurements were conducted across four randomly selected regions to ensure statistical relevance. Comprehensive SEM images, pore size distributions, and porosity data for the pristine LPSCl SE and LTO‐CSE containing 20 wt.% LTO are provided in Figures S10 and S11 (Supporting Information).

The enhanced structural compactness upon LTO addition leads to the increased density and eventually fortifies CSEs. In Figure S3 (Supporting Information), a reduction in pellet thickness with increasing LTO content was observed. This indicates that LTO incorporation facilitates more efficient particle rearrangement during uniaxial pressing, resulting in enhanced structural compaction. Such void‐filling and compaction effects collectively contribute to the densification of the SE pellet and improved mechanical robustness. To evaluate the effect of this densification on mechanical robustness, micro‐indentation tests were performed on pristine LPSCl SE and LTO‐CSE with 20 wt.% LTO pellets under a 10 N applied load. Measurements obtained from three different locations revealed that the LTO‐CSE exhibited slightly higher microhardness compared to the pristine SE, as shown in Figure 2H. Despite the incorporation of 20 wt.% additive, the LTO‐CSE retained high ionic conductivity (Figure 1G) and exhibited comparable or improved mechanical strength, thereby validating the effectiveness of the particle size‐controlled additive strategy.

Demonstration of Interfacial Stability and Dendrite Suppression Enabled by the CSE

Enhanced Electrochemical Stability of LTO‐CSE

The elaborate design of functional LTO additives is expected to enhance the stability of repeated Li plating/stripping processes, thereby enabling prolonged operation under harsh conditions, such as low pressure and high current densities. The effect of LTO incorporation on electrochemical stability was evaluated using Li metal symmetric cells (Li‖LPSCl SE‖Li and Li‖LTO‐CSE‖Li, Figure S2B, Supporting Information) under a stack pressure of 5 MPa. Symmetric cell configuration is commonly employed to assess the interfacial and electrochemical stability of SEs against Li metal.

As shown in Figure 3A, symmetric cells were cycled at a current density of 2 mA cm−2 to an areal capacity of 2 mAh cm−2. Under these conditions, the cell employing pristine LPSCl SE underwent short‐circuiting after ≈200 h, indicating poor electrochemical durability. In contrast, the CSE containing 20 wt.% LTO exhibited stable operation for over 2900 h, with only a negligible increase in overpotential (15.3 mV at 500 h to 15.8 mV at 2900 h), suggesting markedly improved long‐term interfacial stability. As sulfide‐based SEs possess a narrow electrochemical stability window, side reactions and the formation of resistive interphases readily occur upon contact with Li, resulting in increased overpotential. The consistently low overpotential and significantly delayed short‐circuiting observed in the cells with LTO‐incorporated CSE indicate effective suppression of Li dendrite formation and robust interfacial stabilization. Notably, even with a reduced additive content of 10 wt.%, the CSE‐based symmetric cell maintained stable operation for over 2200 h (Figure S12, Supporting Information). This signifies that the beneficial effects of LTO incorporation are retained across a practical range of additive loading.

Electrochemical performance of Li metal symmetric cells employing pristine LPSCl SE and LTO‐CSE (20 wt.%). A) Long‐term cycling behavior of the Li metal symmetric cells. B) CCD test performed by stepwise current increases of 0.25 mA cm−2 per step, starting from 0.5 mA cm−2.

To further evaluate the robustness of the CSE under high current stress, we performed a CCD test, starting at 0.5 mA cm−2 and increasing in increments of 0.25 mA cm−2. As shown in Figure 3B, the CSE sustained stable cycling up to 4.75 mA cm−2, whereas the pristine LPSCl SE failed at a lower threshold of 3.5 mA cm−2, highlighting the superior current tolerance conferred by LTO incorporation. In addition, a CCD test was conducted in the constant‐current mode with stepwise increases of 0.25 mA cm−2 and a fixed plating/stripping duration of 30 min per cycle. As shown in Figure S13 (Supporting Information), the obtained CCD values were consistent with those measured under our spring‐loaded (constant‐pressure) setup, thereby confirming the reliability of our evaluation. Additionally, under a fixed high current density of 3 mA cm−2, the cell with pristine LPSCl SE failed within 150 h, while the CSE‐based cell maintained stable operation for over 1700 h (Figure S14, Supporting Information).

Importance of Electrochemical Scavenging and Zero Strain Behavior: Control Studies

The LTO additive was designed to be dual‐functional, exhibiting both electrochemical scavenging capability and physical reinforcement for the SE. To decouple these two effects and clarify the specific contribution of electrochemical scavenging, we conducted a control study using an oxide additive of similar size, but that is relatively inert to reaction with Li. The latter provides physical strengthening only, thereby serving as a control to isolate the physical effect and validate the additional contribution of electrochemical scavenging uniquely offered by the LTO additive. As a control oxide additive, SiO2 nanoparticles with a particle size of 300–500 nm, comparable to that of the LTO functional additive (Figure S15A, Supporting Information), were selected, as SiO2 is a relatively inert material to Li due to its well‐known ionic and electronic insulating properties (10−14–10−16 S cm−1 at 25 °C).[ 41 ] Although the reaction between SiO2 and Li is thermodynamically feasible, it is kinetically sluggish and often self‐limiting due to the formation of passivating Li2O layers.[ 42 ] Accordingly, SiO2 was considered as electrochemically inactive under our experimental conditions. This assumption was further supported by the cyclic voltammetry (CV) analysis, which revealed no discernible redox activity for the SiO2 under the applied testing conditions (Figure S16A, Supporting Information). The SiO2 particles were synthesized via a modified Stöber method and exhibited a uniform, spherical morphology. The pristine LPSCl SE was mixed with 20 wt.% SiO2 (SiO2‐CSE) under the same HEBM conditions as used for LTO‐CSE preparation. EIS measurements of this SiO2‐CSE (20 wt.%) in a blocking cell configuration revealed an ionic conductivity of 1.045 mS cm−1 (Figure S15B,C, Supporting Information), which was lower than that of both pristine SE (1.573 mS cm−1) and LTO‐CSE (20 wt.%, 1.473 mS cm−1). This result suggests that while SiO2 particles may physically fill pores and grain boundaries, their highly Li‐ion‐insulating nature slightly impedes the formation of efficient ion‐conducting pathways. In this regard, the better ionic conductivity of LTO likely contributes to the high retention of the intrinsic conductivity of LPSCl SE in LTO‐CSE.

The effect of SiO2 additive was further electrochemically assessed via CCD (Figure S15D, Supporting Information) and symmetric cell (Figure S15E, Supporting Information) tests under the same conditions applied to the LTO‐CSE. The SiO2‐CSE exhibited a CCD of 2.75 mA cm−2, which is comparatively lower than that of the pristine LPSCl SE (3.5 mA cm−2) and LTO‐CSE (20 wt.%, 4.75 mA cm−2) under the same conditions, demonstrating its inability to suppress Li dendrite growth at high currents. This relatively low CCD of SiO2‐CSE compared to that of pristine LPSCl SE originates from its lower ionic conductivity (1.045 mS cm−1) relative to that of the pristine LPSCl SE (1.573 mS cm−1). In the symmetric cell test, however, SiO2‐CSE maintained stable operation for 240 h–slightly longer than that of pristine LPSCl SE (200 h) but significantly lower than that of LTO‐CSE (2900 h). This result can be attributed to the lack of reactivity of SiO2 with Li. Unlike LTO, SiO2 does not react with Li under our test conditions, thus fails to redistribute local current–a process necessary for mitigating local current inhomogeneity–and to electrochemically absorb dendritic Li. However, the physical strengthening afforded by SiO2 additives enabled modest cycle life improvement compared to pristine LPSCl SE under the operation current (which is relatively lower than the CCDs of the SEs tested). These findings underscore that physical reinforcement alone, as provided by electrochemically inactive additives, may improve structural stability, but is insufficient to suppress dendritic Li growth, thereby highlighting the crucial role of electrochemical scavenging in stabilizing the SE‐Li interface within the CSE system.

The importance of zero‐strain behavior of the LTO additives was unveiled through comparison with a ZnO‐incorporated CSE. ZnO is known to undergo electrochemical reactions with Li‐ions, accompanied by significant volume changes, thereby lacking the zero‐strain nature required in a CSE system. The particle size of ZnO was in the range of 200–600 nm (Figure S17A, Supporting Information), comparable to that of the LTO additive. The pristine LPSCl SE was mixed with 20 wt.% ZnO (ZnO‐CSE) under the same HEBM conditions used for LTO‐CSE preparation. EIS measurement in a blocking cell configuration revealed an ionic conductivity of 1.316 mS cm−1 (Figure S17B,C, Supporting Information), slightly lower than that of LTO‐CSE. As evidenced by CV analysis (Figure S16B, Supporting Information), the ZnO‐CSE (20 wt.%) demonstrated multiple redox peaks, indicating active electrochemical interactions with Li.

In an electrochemical CCD test (Figure S17D, Supporting Information), ZnO‐CSE (20 wt.%) exhibited a CCD of 4.0 mA cm−2, which is higher than that of pristine LPSCl SE (3.5 mA cm−2), indicating that electrochemical scavenging and current redistribution enabled by reactive ZnO additives enhanced the resistance to dendrite. However, the symmetric cell test showed a relatively shorter cycling duration of ≈160 h, compared to 200 h for the pristine LPSCl SE, suggesting compromised long‐term stability. This decline might be attributed to the non‐zero‐strain nature of ZnO, which imposes mechanical stress within the SE matrix. Despite the electrochemical reactivity, the lack of structural tolerance in ZnO‐CSE resulted in inferior cycle life, highlighting the critical role of mechanical compatibility necessary for robust CSE performance.

To rule out the possibility of weakened physical reinforcement in the SiO2‐CSE and ZnO‐CSE compared with the LTO‐CSE, and to confirm the critical role of electrochemical reactivity with Li and the zero‐strain lithiation behavior of LTO, micro‐indentation tests were performed. As shown in Figure S18 (Supporting Information), the microhardness of all CSEs was higher than that of pristine LPSCl SE. Notably, the SiO2‐CSE exhibited the highest microhardness value among the CSEs, which can be attributed to the uniform size distribution of the synthesized SiO2 additives. In contrast, the LTO and ZnO additives, procured from commercial sources, showed a broader particle size distribution, leading to relatively lower microhardness values. These results indicate that the inferior electrochemical performance of the SiO2‐CSE and ZnO‐CSE cannot be ascribed to insufficient mechanical robustness. Instead, they underscore that the superior interfacial stability and dendrite suppression achieved in the LTO‐CSE arise from its combined effects of physical reinforcement, electrochemical scavenging, and current redistribution.

Overall, the zero‐strain nature of the additive, along with its capability to engage in redox reactions with Li both at the interface and within the bulk of the SE matrix, is pivotal in suppressing dendritic Li growth and mitigating internal stress accumulation within the SE matrix, thereby ensuring long‐term structural robustness of the electrolyte framework. Validation of Dendrite Suppression Capability in LTO‐CSE

To directly assess the dendrite‐suppressing capability of the LTO‐CSE, we conducted electrochemical Li deposition tests under stringent conditions using an anode‐less half‐cell configuration (Figure S2C, Supporting Information). The cells were operated at a stack pressure of 5 MPa and at a current density of 3 mA cm−2 to an areal capacity of 4.5 mAh cm−2. These conditions are notably aggressive, as the CCD determined in Figure 3B indicates that 3 mA cm−2 closely approaches the critical current threshold for the pristine LPSCl SE. In addition, the selected areal capacity is representative of practical loading levels for high‐energy‐density ASSB systems.

In Figure 4A, the cell incorporating pristine LPSCl SE exhibited a sudden overpotential drop after ≈2 mAh cm−2 of Li deposition, approaching the 0 V line (gray dashed), which is indicative of cell failure. We interpret this behavior as a soft short circuit[ 12 , 36 ] caused by Li dendrite growth through grain boundaries or pores within the SE, as further supported by post‐mortem SEM observations. Post‐mortem SEM analysis of the failed cell (Figure 4B–D) revealed dendritic Li threads extending through the SE, along with a large Li fragment embedded deep within the SE matrix. This observation underscores a fundamental limitation of sulfide‐based SEs fabricated by uniaxial cold pressing‐ namely, their tendency to form poor interfacial contact, particularly under low stack pressure. Under high current conditions, non‐uniform Li plating is further exacerbated by the limited ion replenishment rate, which is insufficient to correct the local morphological heterogeneities. As a result, Li preferentially penetrates along structural defects, such as pores or grain boundaries.[ 12 ] The inward growth of dendrites generates mechanical stress, which in turn accelerates crack initiation and propagation within the SE, ultimately leading to internal short‐circuiting.

To further investigate this degradation mechanism, we conducted FIB‐SEM analysis on a pristine LPSCl SE‐based anode‐less half‐cell after 10 cycles operated at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 to an areal capacity of 2 mAh cm−2, with Li stripping to 1 V. As shown in Figure S19A,B (Supporting Information), Li accumulation was observed in the central region of the SE, even under these low current density conditions. This result indicates that prolonged cycling alone can facilitate internal Li penetration and structural degradation. As schematically suggested in Figure S19C (Supporting Information), these results are likely driven by interfacial inhomogeneity, uneven Li deposition, and cumulative buildup of stress within the SE matrix. Additional SEM analysis using a cross‐sectional polisher was performed after 10 cycles of Li plating/stripping at 0.5 mA cm−2 followed by Li deposition of 2 mAh cm−2 during the 11th cycle (Figure S20, Supporting Information). The results revealed direct evidence of interfacial inhomogeneity, crack propagation, and Li dendrite penetration through the SE layer, further supporting that internal Li penetration is coupled with stress‐driven fracture propagation. Taken together with the direct evidence of Li dendrite growth in pristine LPSCl SE (Figure 4B–D), these findings clearly demonstrate that internal failure of the SE caused by dendritic Li growth can occur under low stack pressure, irrespective of current density.

In contrast, the cell incorporating LTO‐CSE demonstrated a remarkably stable voltage profile under the same harsh conditions (Figure 4E), even with 4.5 mAh cm−2 of Li plated. Post‐mortem SEM analysis (Figure 4F‐H) revealed a uniform Li deposition morphology (Figure 4F). In particular, the BSE image (Figure 4H) showed Li deposits interacting with functional additive particles along grain boundaries (highlighted by the green dashed circle), and further Li penetration was arrested at the LTO‐rich regions within the composite. In addition, cross‐sectional SEM analysis after 10 cycles (Figure S21, Supporting Information) confirmed that, unlike the pristine LPSCl SE showing severe internal degradation (Figures S19 and S20, Supporting Information), the LTO‐CSE maintained a dense and stable structure without any noticeable crack formation or Li intrusion. This suggests that the LTO particles suppress Li dendrite penetration by functioning either as localized dendrite scavengers, undergoing lithiation and thus intercepting the Li flux toward growing dendrites, or as physical barriers that mechanically block dendrite propagation. Together with the stable voltage response and SEM observations, these post‐mortem analyses confirm that the LTO‐CSE effectively mitigates Li dendrite propagation even under stringent electrochemical conditions through a dual mechanism of mechanical reinforcement and electrochemical absorption of Li.

Validation of Interfacial Stability Enabled by LTO‐CSE

The interfacial stabilization conferred by LTO‐CSE was assessed via 3D X‐ray microscopy (XRM) to visualize the Li|SE interface in a Li metal symmetric cell configuration, incorporating pristine LPSCl SE or LTO‐CSE (20 wt.%). Both cells were cycled for 10 cycles at a stack pressure of 5 MPa and a current density of 2 mA cm−2 to an areal capacity of 2 mAh cm−2, under the same conditions as those applied in the symmetric cells shown in Figure 3A.

After XRM measurement, the SE and Li deposit regions were segmented and reconstructed into 3D images (Figures S22 and S23, Supporting Information). In Figure 5A, the cell incorporating pristine LPSCl SE exhibited a non‐uniform interface, likely resulting from the combined effects of high surface porosity (Figure 2; Figure S10, Supporting Information), dendritic Li penetration (Figure 4), and crack formation observed in cross‐sectional SEM analyses (Figures S19 and S20, Supporting Information). In contrast, the cell with LTO‐CSE retained a highly uniform interface even after cycling, as shown in Figure 5B. This observation is further supported by µ‐CT images (Figure S23D,E, Supporting Information), which confirmed the presence of a stable, well‐defined Li‐CSE interface across both the Y‐Z and X‐Y planes. Uniform dispersion of the LTO additive was also confirmed in Figure S23C, (Supporting Information), corroborating the homogenous LTO dispersion observed in Figure 1C,D within SE matrix.

A,B) 3D XRM images showing the reconstructed Li|SE interfacial region after 10 cycles. Color‐mapped void intensity represents the degree of local delamination or interfacial heterogeneity, with higher intensity (blue) indicating regions of elevated void density. The SEs of the cycled anode‐less half‐cells were further examined via non‐contact surface profiler and cross‐sectional FIB‐SEM analysis. The surface profiler measurements revealed significantly higher surface roughness in the cycled pristine LPSCl SE (Figure S24A, Supporting Information) compared to that of the LTO‐CSE (Figure S24D, Supporting Information). FIB‐SEM images (Figure S24B,C, Supporting Information) confirmed microcrack formation within the SE and pronounced roughness at the Li|SE interface in the pristine LPSCl SE, whereas the LTO‐CSE retained a continuous and crack‐free interface (Figure S24E,F, Supporting Information) after cycling. These results further support that the LTO‐CSE maintains interfacial stability even after electrochemical cycling.

In parallel with these morphological observations, EIS analysis was conducted on the cells to monitor interfacial resistance evolution. As shown in Figure 5C,D, the cell incorporating pristine LPSCl SE showed a substantial increase in interfacial resistance, with Rinterface rising from 2.07 to 20.12 Ω after 10 cycles. In contrast, the cell incorporating LTO‐CSE exhibited a negligible increase, from 3.02 to 4.99 Ω. This increase in resistance might be attributed to the formation of dead Li and resistive interphases during cycling, both of which appear to be significantly suppressed upon introduction of the LTO‐CSE. Equivalent circuit and fitted resistance values before and after cycling are provided in Figure S25 (Supporting Information).

Low‐Pressure, High Current Operation of Full Cell Enabled by LTO‐CSE

The suppression of dendritic Li growth and the enhancement of interfacial stability afforded by LTO‐CSE are expected to enable stable operation of ASSBs under stringent conditions, such as low stack pressure and high current densities. To verify this, we fabricated Li metal ASSB full cells (configuration shown in Figure S2D, Supporting Information) employing a high Ni cathode material, LiNbO3‐coated LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (LNO‐NCM811), with a cathode loading of 17.3 mg cm−2 (1C = 4.276 mA cm−2). Cells were tested under varying stack pressures of 2, 5, and 10 MPa to evaluate pressure‐dependent electrochemical performance. Notably, unlike many previous studies on low‐pressure ASSB operation that utilize WIP followed by assembly into pouch‐type cells,[ 43 ] all full cells in this study were fabricated using simple custom‐built spring‐loaded or Swagelok‐type cells without any additional post‐treatment. A photo of the custom‐built spring‐loaded battery structure used in this study is shown in Figure S26 (Supporting Information). The latter configuration often requires pressure above 30 MPa for full cell operation owing to inherent pressure non‐uniformities and the resulting current inhomogeneity at the interfaces.[ 15 , 44 , 45 ] In this regard, our test pressures of 2, 5, and 10 MPa are believed to be substantially low. All full cells underwent initial formation cycles at 0.1 C prior to testing at the designated C‐rates.

Figure 6A presents cycling data under a 5 MPa stack pressure at 0.33 C (1.425 mA cm−2). The full cell incorporating the LTO‐CSE delivered an initial discharge capacity of ≈3.5 mAh cm−2 and maintained stable operation for ≈200 cycles, achieving 86.197% capacity retention and an average coulombic efficiency of 99.911%. In contrast, the full cell employing pristine LPSCl SE exhibited short‐circuit failure within 35 cycles, highlighting the crucial role of the LTO‐CSE in enabling stable operation under low stack pressure. Corresponding voltage profiles (Figure 6B) confirmed the retention of high capacity with minimal fading in the cell with LTO‐CSE. The performance at a lower pressure of 2 MPa is presented in Figure 6C,D. Cycling at 2 MPa was conducted at 0.2 C (0.855 mA cm−2). The full cell incorporating LTO‐CSE achieved a discharge capacity of ≈3.75 mAh cm−2 and retained 94.391% capacity over 50 cycles, with an average coulombic efficiency of 99.861%. Conversely, the full cell incorporating pristine LPSCl SE exhibited premature failure, manifested as short‐circuit behavior immediately after the formation cycles, as evidenced by the abnormal charge profiles in Figure S27 (Supporting Information).

To further confirm the robustness of the CSE under practical high‐rate conditions, we evaluated full cell performance at 10 MPa stack pressure. This pressure is still significantly lower than those typically required (20–50 MPa) for custom‐built spring‐loaded or Swagelok‐type pressure cells. As shown in Figure 7A, rate capability tests were conducted from 0.1 C to 1.5 C, with three cycles at each rate. The full cell incorporating LTO‐CSE maintained stable operation up to 1.5 C (6.414 mA cm−2), whereas the pristine LPSCl SE‐based cell short‐circuited at 0.5 C. Corresponding voltage profiles of the full cell with LTO‐CSE (Figure 7B) confirmed that even at 1.5 C, the full cell incorporating CSE delivered areal discharge capacity exceeding 2.75 mAh cm−2. To further support the electrochemical stability of LTO‐CSE under 10 MPa, we performed a CCD measurement in the Li metal symmetric cell configuration. The LTO‐CSE maintained stability up to 7.5 mA cm−2, while the pristine LPSCl SE failed at a significantly lower threshold (4.5 mA cm−2). Long‐term electrochemical cycling was performed at 0.5 C (2.138 mA cm−2), and the full cell incorporating LTO‐CSE demonstrated stable operation for over 900 cycles with a capacity retention of 80.088% at the 600th cycle and 76.531% at the 900th cycle, along with an average coulombic efficiency of 99.985% (Figure 7D). In contrast, the full cell with pristine LPSCl SE displayed abnormal operation just after formation cycles and failed after 50 cycles due to short‐circuiting. Additionally, to examine the LTO‐CSE under conventional high‐pressure conditions, we performed a full cell test at 30 MPa. Rate capability test (Figure S28A,B, Supporting Information) and cycling at 0.5 C (Figure S28C, Supporting Information) confirmed that the LTO‐CSE enabled stable operation up to 2 C (8.552 mA cm−2), which is considered a practically high rate. The 2 C discharge capacity reached ≈2.4 mAh cm−2, demonstrating excellent rate capability. In addition, while the pristine LPSCl SE full cell exhibited abnormal behavior and short‐circuited after 280 cycles, the full cell incorporating LTO‐CSE sustained over 550 cycles with an average coulombic efficiency of 99.907%.

To contextualize the performance of the LTO‐CSE relative to previously reported additive‐incorporated sulfide SEs, we constructed a 3D comparative plot (Figure S29, Supporting Information), mapping current density, areal capacity, and cycle number with stack pressure. Full comparative data and references are summarized in Table S3 (Supporting Information). Notably, most prior studies (red spheres) required high stack pressures (≥20 MPa) to achieve acceptable performance. In contrast, LTO‐CSE, denoted by purple and blue stars, demonstrated stable long‐term cycling (≥900 cycles) and high areal capacity (≈3.5 mAh cm−2) even under practically relevant low‐pressure conditions (10 MPa). This highlights the viability of functional additive‐based approaches for enabling low‐pressure, high‐performance ASSB integration.

Our results demonstrate that, while WIP is commonly employed to densify SEs and improve interfacial contact, the LTO‐CSE enables stable low‐pressure operation without the need for temperature‐ and pressure‐intensive post‐treatments. The LTO‐CSE achieves both electrochemical scavenging and mechanical reinforcement through a simple and scalable fabrication route involving only mechanical mixing and cold pressing. These findings suggest that the LTO‐CSE strategy may contribute to simplified processing and enhanced manufacturability in future ASSB production.

Conclusion

In this work, we presented a simple yet scalable strategy to enhance interfacial stability and suppress lithium dendrite growth in sulfide‐based ASSBs by incorporating a dual‐functional LTO additive into the LPSCl SE. The resulting LTO‐CSE, fabricated solely through mechanical mixing and cold pressing without any post‐treatment, provides both mechanical reinforcement of the SE framework and electrochemical stabilization through preferential, zero‐strain lithiation of the LTO additive. Electrochemical stabilization involves not only electrochemical Li scavenging near the growing dendrites but also interfacial current homogenization, as the additive undergoes lithiation prior to the onset of Li plating. Careful control over the additive particle size guided selective localization of smaller LTO particles at the grain boundaries and pores between larger LPSCl particles, thereby minimizing disruption to the Li‐ion conduction pathways within the bulk SE. Comprehensive electrochemical and morphological analyses revealed that the LTO‐engineered CSE effectively mitigates dendrite‐induced failure, enabling prolonged symmetric cell operation exceeding 2900 h and achieving a high CCD of 7.5 mA cm−2 under a stack pressure of 10 MPa. Full cells demonstrated stable cycling over 900 cycles with minimal capacity fading, high average coulombic efficiency (≈99.985%), and an areal capacity of ≈3.5 mAh cm−2. Notably, this performance was achieved without relying on WIP, and all tests were conducted not in pouch cells but in custom‐built spring‐loaded pressurized cells. Beyond demonstrating high performance under low‐pressure conditions, this study addresses a critical bottleneck in the practical deployment of sulfide‐based ASSBs, namely, the need for scalable fabrication and interfacial stabilization without thermal or pressure‐intensive treatments. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of additive‐guided SE engineering as a versatile platform for the development of high‐energy‐density solid‐state batteries capable of operating under industry‐relevant conditions.
