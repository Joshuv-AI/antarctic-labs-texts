Mitigating Mn‐Driven Interfacial Instability in LiMn0.5Fe0.5PO4 Cathodes for Lithium‐Ion Batteries via Surface‐Intensive Ta Doping
Authors: Hyeji Lee, Wonjun Lee, Ji‐Won Jung, Wonchang Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLiMnxFe1−xPO4 (LMFP) holds promise for next‐generation olivine‐type cathodes for Li‐ion batteries but suffers from sluggish Li+ diffusion along 1D [010] channels. Moreover, because Jahn‐Teller‐active Mn3+ species are involved, LMFP exhibits increased polarization and progressive performance degradation under high‐rate and low‐temperature conditions. Consequently, the Mn2+/Mn3+ redox becomes surface‐intensive at the near‐surface region, thereby triggering Mn dissolution and electrolyte side reactions. To address this problem, we use surface‐intensive Ta doping, exploiting the slow diffusion kinetics of Ta5+ to selectively enrich the near‐surface region of LiMn0.5Fe0.5PO4 particles in Ta. Ta doping strengthens the M─O framework and widens the Li─O diffusion pathways while increasing the near‐surface Mn2+ fraction from 53.9% to 59.7%, thereby suppressing Mn3+ accumulation. Electrochemically, Ta1@LMFP delivers 105.58 mAh g−1 at 20 C (71.1% of the 0.1 C capacity), outperforming Ta0@LMFP (82.27 mAh g−1, 54.8%). In addition, under high‐SOC storage conditions (60°C, 10d), Ta1@LMFP maintains 99.5% of its initial capacity, whereas Ta0@LMFP retains 83.1%, confirming that self‐discharge and interfacial degradation are suppressed at high temperature and high SOC. This work demonstrates that surface‐intensive Ta doping enables the wide‐temperature‐range and high‐power operation of LMFP‐based cathodes and provides compositional and structural design guidelines for developing robust cathode materials for Li‐ion batteries.Keywords: high‐rate capability, LiMn0.5Fe0.5PO4 , lithium‐ion battery, low‐temperature performance, Ta5+ surface doping

Introduction

Li‐ion batteries (LIBs) are the most widely used energy storage systems (ESSs), having numerous practical applications. In particular, high‐performance cathode materials providing high energy densities and long‐term cycling stabilities while maintaining low material and processing costs for large‐scale applications are highly sought after [1, 2].

The most widely used LIB cathode material is LiFePO4 (LFP) [2, 3]. LFP possesses an olivine structure stabilized by strong P─O bonding, which suppresses oxygen release and results in high thermal and chemical stability and excellent cyclability. In addition, LFP contains earth‐abundant elements and is relatively cheap, holding promise as a LIB cathode material for EVs and ESSs [4, 5].

The low Fe2+/Fe3+ redox potential of LFP (3.4 V) leads to low operating voltage and thus limits the achievable energy density and hinders compliance with the requirements of EVs (long driving range) and ESSs (large‐scale energy storage) [6, 7]. LiMnPO4 (LMP) has been proposed as an alternative cathode material to overcome this limitation, since the Mn2+/Mn3+ redox potential (∼4.1 V) enables an operating voltage (and hence, energy density) exceeding that of LFP [8, 9].

The Mn3+ ions formed during charge–discharge experience Jahn–Teller (JT) distortion and disproportionation (2Mn3+ → Mn2+ + Mn4+). The resulting Mn2+ ions dissolve into the electrolyte, leading to capacity fading and electrode–electrolyte interfacial instability [10]. In addition, the dissolved Mn species accumulate in the electrode–electrolyte interphase and increase interfacial resistance, further hindering Li+ transport and aggravating cell polarization [11]. LiMn x Fe1− x PO4 (LMFP) is expected to combine the high structural stability of LFP with the high energy density of LMP and thereby avoid the shortcomings of both materials. This material features voltage plateaus near 3.4 V (Fe2+/Fe3+) and 4.1 V (Mn2+/Mn3+) because of the coexistence of Fe‐ and Mn‐based redox reactions and is a promising next‐generation olivine cathode material suitable for EV and ESS applications owing to its multistep redox behavior and high structural stability and energy density [12, 13].

However, the commercialization of LMFP faces several obstacles. The introduction of Mn increases the energy density of LFP but distorts the 1D Li+ diffusion pathway along the [010] direction, hindering Li+ diffusion and leading to severe capacity loss under high‐rate and low‐temperature conditions [14, 15, 16]. To address these limitations, many studies used carbon coatings to construct conductive networks and enhance electronic conductivity [17]. For instance, Zhou et al. reported that an in situ CFx/glucose‐derived carbon coating on LMFP enabled a discharge capacity of 115.0 mAh g−1 at 5 C [18]. However, this approach cannot overcome the low ionic conductivity associated with the 1D Li+ diffusion pathway. Another limitation of LMFP arises from the Mn2+ → Mn3+ oxidation in the high‐voltage region around 4.1 V during charging. Given that electrochemical reactions in this potential range are mainly controlled by the electrode–electrolyte interface, Mn3+ tends to accumulate in the near‐surface region, where it induces JT distortion and promotes Mn2+ dissolution. The suppression of near‐surface Mn3+ accumulation and electrode–electrolyte interfacial degradation is therefore essential for the commercialization of LMFP‐based LIBs [19, 20, 21].

In this regard, many studies have focused on M2‐site doping, which expands LiO6 octahedra and widens the Li+ diffusion pathway, facilitating Li+ transport along the [010] direction [22, 23, 24]. Notably, representative M2‐site doping studies have reported that Zr4+ doped LiMn0.7Fe0.3PO4/C delivered 103.8 mAh g−1 at 20 C and exhibited 92.1% capacity retention after 200 cycles at 1 C, while Nb5+ modified LMFP/Nb@C‐4 achieved 134 mAh g−1 at 5 C together with 95.06% retention after 200 cycles at 1 C, collectively evidencing that high‐valence dopants can simultaneously improve high‐rate capability and cycling durability in bulk‐modified LMFP [23, 25].

Despite the abovementioned advantages, bulk doping, which primarily modifies the lattice throughout the bulk of the active material, has limited efficacy in alleviating Mn3+ instability, which predominantly arises in the surface region during cycling. Moreover, excessive doping across the entire bulk leads to pronounced lattice distortion and thereby reduces ionic conductivity [26]. Therefore, to more fundamentally enhance LMFP performance, one should focus on controlling the near‐surface regions where Mn‐related degradation and electrode–electrolyte interfacial resistance predominantly occur.

Herein, we use the surface‐intensive doping of LMFP with Ta5+ to stabilize the near‐surface region, extend the Li+ diffusion pathway, suppress surface side reactions, and decrease electrode–electrolyte interfacial resistance (Figure 1). The high oxidation state of Ta5+ enables effective charge compensation, while strong Ta─O bonds are expected to stabilize the lattice structure and modulate local LiO6 octahedra thereby suppressing structural instabilities and facilitating Li+ migration [23, 24, 25]. In contrast to bulk doping, which broadly modifies the bulk interior of active material particles, this strategy minimizes the unnecessary modification of the particle interior and instead enables precise control over the near‐surface region, compensating for the inherent limitations of bulk lattice modification [26, 27].

To achieve surface‐intensive Ta doping, we exploit the slow and limited diffusion of Ta compared with that of other LMFP constituents during calcination [28]. Ta possesses an atomic mass (180.95 g mol−1) notably exceeding those of light LMFP constituents, Li (6.94 g mol−1) and P (30.97 g mol−1), featuring slower diffusion and a shorter diffusion length. After mixing Li, P, and Ta sources with an Fe─Mn oxalate precursor and performing ball milling, preliminary calcination at 450°C is carried out to decompose the precursor and induce the nucleation and partial crystallization of olivine‐type LMFP [29, 30]. During the subsequent high‐temperature calcination at 700°C, the slower lattice diffusivity of Ta compared with those of other LMFP constituents keeps its migration largely confined to the near‐surface region rather than allowing uniform diffusion into the bulk of the preformed LMFP lattice, ultimately leading to surface‐intensive Ta doping.

In summary, a novel surface‐intensive Ta doping strategy is used to enhance the interfacial stability and ionic conductivity of LMFP and thus obtain cathodes with a longer cycle life, superior low‐temperature rate performance, and higher usable energy density at the cell level and thus accelerate the commercialization of LMFP cathodes for EVs and large‐scale ESSs.

As Ta5 + (0.64 Å), which possesses a smaller ionic radius and a higher valence state, partially substitutes for Fe2 + (0.78 Å) and Mn2 + (0.83 Å), a slight decrease in the unit cell volume is observed. This change is accompanied by a reduction in the average M─O bond length [34, 35]. The excess positive charge introduced into the lattice may induce a localized redistribution of the electronic structure to maintain overall charge neutrality of the system [36, 37]. Such electronic adjustments are likely accompanied by an increase in M─O bond rigidity and subtle modifications of the oxygen coordination environment. This interpretation is consistent with the observed increase in the Li─O bond length from 2.1222 Å for Ta0@LMFP to 2.1541 Å for Ta1@LMFP. This suggests the formation of a more open Li+ migration pathway along the primary [010] channel, which may consequently lead to a reduction in the activation energy for Li+ hopping [12, 13, 38]. The average P–O bond length decreased from 1.5498 Å in Ta0@LMFP to 1.5066 Å in Ta1@LMFP, increasing to 1.5302 Å in Ta2@LMFP. This behavior implied that excessive Ta doping induced local rearrangement in the PO4 framework, causing the re‐elongation of some P─O bonds and bonding‐strength nonuniformity [39]. The trend observed for P─O bond length was consistent with the results of FT‐IR (Figure S4), which revealed that the PO4 symmetric stretch near 990 cm−1 blue‐shifted upon going from Ta0@LMFP to Ta1@LMFP and then red‐shifted for Ta2@LMFP. Therefore, at an appropriate extent, Ta doping strengthened M─O bonding and tuned the Li─O diffusion channel while preserving high crystallinity, which provides a structural basis for the performance enhancements discussed later.

Fe, Mn, P, and O were uniformly distributed across the particles of Ta1@LMFP (Figure 3a) and Ta0@LMFP (Figure S5). The Ta signal was also uniformly distributed, and no Ta enriched regions or Ta‐rich impurity phases were observed, in line with the presence of single‐phase LMFP revealed by XRD. HR‐TEM image (Figure 3b) revealed that Ta1@LMFP particles were covered by a uniform carbon coating with a thickness of 2–3 nm. This coating was expected to provide electron transport pathways, reduce interparticle contact resistance, and improve electronic conductivity and interfacial stability [14, 39].

HR‐TEM image analysis confirmed that the same olivine structure was well formed in the particle interior (bulk, Region I) and in the near‐surface region (Region II). FFT analysis showed that the lattice spacing 0.429 nm in Region I and 0.411 nm in Region II) corresponded to the (101) plane in both region [40, 41]. The slight spacing difference between the near‐surface region and the bulk suggested that Ta doping may induce near‐surface residual stress. This lattice distortion can be interpreted as the reinforcement of the local M─O framework through strong bonding between the highly charged Ta5+ and surrounding O2− ions and is consistent with the reduction in the average M─O bond length and unit cell volume (Table 1; Table S1).

The TEM‐EDS line scan analysis performed across a single primary particle (Figure 3c) shows that the Ta signal exhibits higher intensity near the particle surface and gradually decreases toward the bulk region. In addition, SEM observations and BET analysis results (Figures S6 and S7), the average primary particle size of LMFP was determined to be approximately 393 nm. Considering this particle size, Ta doping can be interpreted as a surface‐intensive doping structure locally formed in the near‐surface region of most primary particles.

In the EELS analysis (Figure 3d), as the measurement point moves from the surface toward the bulk, the L3/L2 intensity ratio and peak positions of the Fe and Mn L23 edges gradually change. In particular, the slight shift of the L3 edge to lower energies indicated that the average oxidation states of Fe and Mn were lower near the surface [41, 42]. This indicates that Ta doping modulates the local electronic structure in a manner that lowers the average oxidation state of the transition metals near the particle surface.

This finding suggested that Ta doping modulated the local electronic structure, rearranged transition metal–O covalent hybridization (transition metal 3d–O 2p hybridization), and thereby suppressed unstable high oxidation states such as Mn3+ near the surface and preventing Mn‐induced dissolution and local structural collapse. Collectively, the HR‐TEM/FFT and EELS results indicate that Ta was mainly substituted at lattice sites near the particle surface, strengthened the local M─O framework through strong Ta─O bonding, and helped maintain the low average oxidation states of the transition metals near the surface.

XPS was used to identify the identities and valence states of constituent elements. The survey spectra of Ta0@LMFP and Ta1@LMFP (Figure 4a) revealed the presence of Fe, Mn, O, C, P, and Li. The corresponding C 1s spectra (Figure 4b) featured peaks at 284.60 eV (sp2 C─C), 286.77 eV (epoxy C─O), and 288.63 eV (carbonyl C═O), indicating the presence of a carbon coating [43, 44, 45].

(a) Survey, (b) C 1s, (c) Fe 2p, (d) Mn 2p, and (e) Ta 4f x‐ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) of Ta0@LMFP and Ta1@LMFP. (f) Fe2+/Fe3+ and Mn2+/Mn3+ ratios derived from deconvoluted spectra.

The Fe 2p spectra (Figure 4c) showed the Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2 peaks of Fe2+ (709.76 and 723.32 eV, respectively) and Fe3+ (711.57 and 725.25 eV, respectively), as well as satellite peaks at 715.12 and 729.65 eV. The Mn 2p spectra (Figure 4d) showed the Mn 2p3/2 and Mn 2p1/2 peaks of Mn2+ (641.12 and 652.89 eV, respectively) and Mn3+ (642.43 and 654.23 eV, respectively), as well as a satellite peak near 646.76 eV [24, 45]. The contributions of Mn3+ and Fe3+ indicated the occurrence of partial oxidation at the material surface. The Ta 4f spectra (Figure 4e) featured Ta 4f7/2 and Ta 4f5/2 peaks at 25.5 and 27.4 eV, respectively, for Ta1@LMFP, whereas no Ta signal was detected for Ta0@LMFP. Typically, Ta 4f7/2 and 4f5/2 peaks are reported for Ta5+, which confirmed successful Ta doping [43].

The doping effect was quantified by comparing the Fe2+/Fe3+ and Mn2+/Mn3+ ratios after fitting the spectra using the same baseline (Figure 4f). Upon going from Ta0@LMFP to Ta1@LMFP, the Fe2+ fraction increased from 53.8% to 58.3%, while the Mn2+ fraction increased from 53.9% to 59.7%, i.e., the average oxidation state of both metals decreased. These results were consistent with EELS data and demonstrated that Ta doping affected the surface oxidation state. Mn3+ is known to act as a JT‐active ion and induce local lattice distortion and structural instability in MnO6 octahedra [11, 12, 22]. Therefore, the increase in the Mn2+/Mn3+ ratio in Ta1@LMFP suggests the suppression of JT distortion and a possible increase in structural stability.

The abovementioned results suggested that surface‐intensive Ta doping could enhance the electrochemical performance of LMFP. To test this hypothesis, we tested the rate performances of different samples using galvanostatic charge–discharge tests (Figure 5). The charge–discharge potential profiles of Ta0@LMFP, Ta0.5@LMFP, Ta1@LMFP, and Ta2@LMFP (Figure 5a) showed that they exhibited nearly identical discharge capacities at 0.1 C (150.01, 148.25, 148.51, and 148.16 mAh g−1, respectively). All samples exhibited plateaus near 3.4 V (Fe2+/Fe3+) and 4.1 V (Mn2+/Mn3+), which indicated that Ta doping preserved the overall shape of the potential profiles [5, 39, 46]. The Mn2+/Mn3+ contribution for Ta1@LMFP increased to 44.1%, compared with 39.7% for Ta0@LMFP (Figure S9). Due to the kinetic limitations of Mn2+/Mn3+, strategies to enhance the reversibility of the Mn redox reaction are considered to be important for improving the electrochemical performance of LMFP [47]. Therefore, the increase in the Mn2+/Mn3+ contribution, together with the visually more extended Mn2+/Mn3+ discharge plateau at ∼4.1 V (Figure 5a), suggests that Ta doping can promote the manifestation of the Mn redox reaction [38, 48]. Figure 5b presents the rate capabilities obtained by increasing the C‐rate from 0.1 to 20 C in a stepwise manner.

(a) Charge–discharge potential profiles recorded at 0.1 C between 2.0 and 4.5 V, (b) rate capabilities at C‐rates of 0.1–20 C, and (c) discharge potential profiles recorded at different C‐rates for Tax@LMFP (x = 0, 0.5, 1, 2).

All samples showed the usual capacity decrease with increasing C‐rate, with Ta1@LMFP maintaining the highest discharge capacity over the entire range. For Ta1@LMFP, the discharge capacities at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20 C were 148.51, 142.44, 134.66, 127.66, 124.52, 118.99, 113.18, and 105.58 mAh g−1, respectively. The capacity retentions relative to 0.1 C were 95.9%, 90.7%, 86.0%, 83.8%, 80.1%, 76.2%, and 71.1% at 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20 C, respectively, revealing moderate capacity fading even at high rates. Under the same conditions, Ta0@LMFP featured a capacity retention of 54.8% at 20 C (82.27 mAh g−1), i.e., was inferior to Ta0.5@LMFP (63.2%, 93.72 mAh g−1) and Ta2@LMFP (60.9%, 90.24 mAh g−1).

Figure 5c shows the discharge potential profiles obtained while increasing the C‐rate from 0.1 to 20 C. With the increasing C‐rate, the plateaus tilted, and polarization increased for all samples [49]. At a given same C‐rate, Ta1@LMFP exhibited the flattest and longest plateaus with the smallest potential drop, thus delivering the lowest overpotential for the Fe2+/Fe3+ and Mn2+/Mn3+ redox processes and smoothest Li+ intercalation and deintercalation under high‐rate conditions. Collectively, these results indicated that rate capability was strongly dependent on the Ta doping level and that Ta1@LMFP showed the best high‐rate characteristics with the smallest extent of capacity fading. These improvements were ascribed not only to enhanced electronic conductivity but, more convincingly, to the mitigation of kinetic constraints along the Li+ diffusion pathway and the improved thermodynamic reversibility of the Mn2+/Mn3+ transition due to surface‐intensive Ta doping, which secured structural and interfacial stability during prolonged high‐rate operation.

To quantify the improvement in high‐rate performance, CV curves of Ta0@LMFP and Ta1@LMFP at 25°C and scan rates of 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mV s−1 in the potential range of 2.0–4.5 V after three formation cycles at 0.1 C. Each curve exhibited the peaks of two redox couples (Fe2+/Fe3+ and Mn2+/Mn3+) typical of the olivine structure (Figure 6a,b) [50, 51]. In particular, the Mn3+ → Mn2+ reduction peak of Ta1@LMFP near 3.9 V featured a larger current, narrower width, and smaller potential separation than that of Ta0@LMFP. This finding suggested that surface‐intensive Ta doping, consistent with XPS/EELS evidence for a reduced fraction of (JT‐active) Mn3+, alleviated Mn‐related issues and thus improved Mn redox reversibility, lowered charge transfer resistance, and enhanced Mn3+ → Mn2+ reduction kinetics, allowing Li+ intercalation and deintercalation at the particle surface to proceed in an energetically more favorable and more uniform manner and resulting in sharper and more symmetric CV peaks. To clarify the charge storage mechanism, the overall current response was expressed as a relationship between potential (V) and scan rate (v) [50, 53]. Accordingly, current (i) was separated into capacitive (k 1 v) and diffusion‐controlled (k 2 v 0.5) components according to Equation (2) (Figure 6e,f).

The contribution of the capacitive component increased with scan rate, equaling 79% for Ta0@LMFP and 90% for Ta1@LMFP at 1.0 mV s−1. These results suggest that surface‐intensive Ta doping enhanced the pseudocapacitive charge storage behavior. Previous studies reported that fast reversible charge transfer reactions at or near the surface are of key importance for high‐power and high‐rate battery design [50, 53, 54]. Meanwhile, LMFP‐based materials suffer from Jahn‐Teller distortion and Li+ diffusion limitations associated with the Mn redox reaction [47]. Owing to these diffusion‐limited characteristics, enhancing the pseudocapacitive contribution is considered an effective strategy to alleviate the limitations arising from sluggish diffusion kinetics in electrode materials [55, 56]. Therefore, the results of this study indicate that surface‐intensive Ta doping improved the reaction kinetics of LMFP by further increasing the pseudocapacitive contribution in LMFP systems.

Additional BET analysis (Figure S7) was conducted to examine whether the increase in capacitive contribution originated from an increase in surface reactions. The results confirmed that no significant difference in specific surface area was observed between the Ta1@LMFP and Ta0@LMFP. These findings suggest that the increase in capacitive contribution observed in this study is more likely associated with the enhancement of reversible electrochemical reactions included in pseudocapacitive contribution rather than with an increase in surface reaction [57, 58]. Consequently, surface‐intensive Ta doping acted as a key factor accelerating Li+ intercalation and deintercalation under fast charge–discharge conditions and enabled excellent high‐rate performance. Given that Li+ diffusion was restricted at low temperatures (≤ 0°C), the shift to surface‐dominated reactions was advantageous for maintaining the reaction rate. The high capacitive contribution and enhanced Li+ diffusivity observed herein are expected to favor high low‐temperature performance.

Figure S11 compares the potential–discharge capacity profiles of Ta0@LMFP and Ta1@LMFP recorded during discharge at 1 C and temperatures from 25°C to −30°C. Both samples showed reduced power output at low temperatures because of decelerated Li+ diffusion and charge transfer, although Ta1@LMFP maintained a higher discharge capacity than Ta0@LMFP in the entire temperature window. This behavior suggested that surface‐intensive Ta doping benefited Li+ transport at low temperatures. To quantify this effect, we performed GITT measurements involving 10 min of charge or discharge at 0.1 C followed by 60 min of rest (Figure 7) [56, 59]. In addition, it was confirmed that the potential change rate (ΔE/Δt) at the end of the relaxation period sufficiently approached a quasi‐equilibrium state [60]. The resulting potential–time profiles were processed to extract the potential change during the pulse (ΔEt ) and the quasi‐equilibrium potential change after rest (ΔEs); subsequently, D Li + was calculated using Fick's second law. As the Fe2+/Fe3+ and Mn2+/Mn3+ redox processes in the olivine structure occur in different potential regions, the Fe window was analyzed separately from the Mn window [50, 51]. At low temperature, the Mn2+/Mn3+ redox region of Ta1@LMFP is comparatively preserved and an additional plateau is observed, whereas the Mn plateau of Ta0@LMFP is severely distorted such that the effective reaction in this potential region is significantly limited [39]. Accordingly, the potential–time curves recorded during charge were presented in magnified form to more directly visualize polarization (Figure 7b–g).

The potential–capacity profiles obtained at 25°C (Figure 7a) showed two plateaus corresponding to Fe and Mn redox processes for both Ta0@LMFP and Ta1@LMFP. Ta1@LMFP exhibited a smaller IR drop after each pulse and faster relaxation that converged to the equilibrium potential during the rest period. As shown in the enlarged potential–time profiles of the Mn redox region in Figure 7b–d, Ta1@LMFP demonstrates a smaller ΔV under identical current pulse conditions, reflecting reduced overpotential, in good agreement with the enhanced DLi + values obtained from GITT analysis (Figure S12a,b; Table S6).

In particular, at −20°C, Ta1@LMFP exhibits an additional plateau‐like feature in the Mn2+/Mn3+ redox region, suggesting that the Mn redox reaction is not completely collapsed and remains partially preserved even under low‐temperature conditions (Figure 7e). In contrast, for Ta0@LMFP, the Mn plateau is severely distorted and the reaction within the Mn window does not sufficiently proceed, reflecting the simultaneous increase in interfacial and diffusion resistances at low temperatures [61]. As shown in Figure 7e–g, Ta0@LMFP showed a ΔV of 0.391–0.469 V, whereas Ta1@LMFP exhibited a ΔV of 0.265–0.359 V, i.e., Ta doping notably reduced overpotential at a given current pulse. Consequently, in the Fe window, D Li + increased from 4.88 × 10−11 cm2 s−1 to 2.41 × 10−10 cm2 s−1 (∼4.9‐fold) during charge and from 1.39 × 10−10 cm2 s−1 to 1.92 × 10−10 cm2 s−1 (∼1.4‐fold) during discharge (Figure S12c,d; Table S7). In the Mn window, the enhancement was larger, i.e., D Li + increased from 3.16 × 10−11 cm2 s−1 to 9.34 × 10−11 cm2 s−1 (∼3.0‐fold) during charge and from 3.67 × 10−12 cm2 s−1 to 1.81 × 10−11 cm2 s−1 (∼4.9‐fold) during discharge. These results quantitatively showed that surface‐intensive Ta doping accelerated the inherently sluggish Mn2+/Mn3+ reaction at −20°C.

The reduction in ΔV and relaxation span toward the equilibrium potential can be understood as a consequence of the structural changes induced by Ta doping. Ta5+ was selectively doped near the surface, the balance of M─O bonding was tuned, and the fraction of JT‐active Mn3+ at Mn sites decreased, which relieved the local distortion of MnO6 octahedra [62]. As a result, Li+ diffusion pathways were stabilized, and D Li + was especially enhanced in the Mn redox window, so that Ta doping effectively lowered the Li+ diffusion barrier and charge transfer resistance at low temperatures. Surface‐intensive Ta doping therefore suppressed the inevitable decline of Li+ diffusion and rise of electrode polarization at low temperatures and improved the power characteristics and capacity retention performance of LMFP.

To verify whether this structural and electronic regulation secured stability under long‐term cycling and high‐rate operation, we evaluated room‐temperature cycling performance at 1 C and 2/5 C. Figure 8a shows the results obtained for 200 cycles at 1 C over 2.0–4.5 V. Ta0@LMFP delivered an initial discharge capacity of 109.78 mAh g−1, which decreased to 87.03 mAh g−1 at the 200th cycle (capacity retention: 79.3%). Ta1@LMFP showed a higher initial capacity of 127.18 mAh g−1, which decreased to 121.69 mAh g−1 at the 200th cycle (capacity retention: 95.7%). Both samples reached a coulombic efficiency of nearly 100% after the first few cycles. The much smaller capacity decay slope of Ta1@LMFP indicated that surface‐intensive Ta doping markedly improved structural and interfacial stability under long‐term cycling at 1 C.

(c) Nyquist plots obtained after 1 C and high‐rate cycling. (d,e) dQ/dV profiles recorded at selected cycle numbers during 5 C cycling following 100 cycles at 2 C.

Durability under more demanding conditions was examined by performing 100 cycles at 2 C followed by 100 cycles at 5 C (Figure 8b). In both cases, fading at 2 C was faster than that at 1 C, with capacity retentions of 89.7% and 98.3% obtained for Ta0@LMFP and Ta1@LMFP, respectively. Subsequent cycling at 5 C accelerated degradation, with Ta0@LMFP and Ta1@LMFP showing capacity retentions of 84.6% and 92.6%, respectively, which indicated more stable high‐rate capacity. This difference is evident in the capacity retention curves in Figure S14. At 1, 2, and 5 C, Ta0@LMFP showed a pronounced capacity decrease upon cycling. Ta1@LMFP exhibited a higher initial capacity that decreased more slowly, and the per cycle capacity loss in the 5 C region was strongly suppressed. These results suggested that surface‐intensive Ta doping suppressed the loss of effective reaction volume and mitigated the structural collapse of the cathode during high‐rate and long‐term operation.

To quantify interfacial resistance changes before and after high‐rate cycling, we fitted Nyquist plots using an equivalent circuit to extract Rs (ohmic/solution resistance), R SEI (interphase film resistance), and R ct (charge‐transfer resistance) (Figure 8c, Table 3) [23]. After 200 cycles at 1 C, the R s, R SEI, and R ct of Ta0@LMFP were similar to those of Ta1@LMFP, i.e., the interfacial characteristics of the two electrodes did not markedly differ at this stage. By contrast, after high‐rate cycling, the two samples exhibited different increases in interfacial resistance. For Ta0@LMFP, Rs reached 2.349 Ω and changed only slightly, while R SEI rose to 11.09 Ω, R ct rose to 9.273 Ω. For Ta1@LMFP, R s remained at 1.896 Ω, while R SEI, and R ct remained at 5.548, and 6.569 Ω, respectively, under the same conditions, i.e., the absolute values and their increases were smaller than those for Ta0@LMFP. These results indicated that surface‐intensive Ta doping effectively suppressed the buildup of cathode‐electrolyte interfacial resistance and the accumulation of charge transfer resistance, thereby enabling a smaller increase and superior capacity retention [23, 39, 63].

Charge storage behavior over a series of consecutive 5 C cycles following 100 cycles at 2 C was probed using differential capacity (dQ/dV) analysis (Figure 8d,e). Both Ta0@LMFP and Ta1@LMFP showed redox peaks due to Fe2+/Fe3+ and Mn2+/Mn3+ transitions. For Ta0@LMFP, the Mn oxidation peak appeared near 4.23 V at the first cycle and gradually shifted to ∼4.30 V at the 100th cycle while experiencing broadening and rapid weakening (Figure S15). The reduction peak near 3.83 V rapidly weakened, essentially disappearing in later cycles. This shift toward higher potentials and large decreases in peak height and area indicated an increase in overpotential for Mn2+/Mn3+ transitions and the loss of active Mn redox sites, which led to the collapse of reversibility. For Ta1@LMFP, the Mn oxidation peak shifted from 4.21 to 4.25 V (by ∼40 mV), and the reduction peak shifted from 3.86 to 3.83 V. The Mn redox peaks of Ta1@LMFP retained their high intensity and sharpness throughout cycling and exhibited an area decrease notably lower than that observed for Ta0@LMFP (Figure 8e) [63]. Even in the initial cycles, the Mn peaks of Ta1@LMFP were larger and narrower than those of Ta0@LMFP, which reflected more active Mn sites and faster charge transfer in the former case. This advantage persisted up to the 100th cycle. Therefore, for Ta1@LMFP, the peak shift was limited, and the intensity loss was mitigated, i.e., the reversibility and kinetics of the Mn2+/Mn3+ transition were preserved under high‐rate cycling.

These dQ/dV trends were consistent with the rapid R ct and R SEI increases observed for Ta0@LMFP and the restrained growth of these resistances observed for Ta1@LMFP, suggesting that surface‐intensive Ta doping suppressed the structural collapse of MnO6 octahedra and accumulation of Mn3+ and thus retarded electrochemical degradation at high rates. This interfacial degradation behavior was further examined via metal (TM) dissolution behavior after high‐rate cycling, which was probed by ICP‐OES analysis (Figure S16). The amounts of Mn and Fe detected on the Li metal anode reflect the TM species dissolved from the cathode that migrated through the electrolyte and deposited on the Li surface. In all cases, the detected Fe and Mn were lower for Ta1@LMFP than for Ta0@LMFP [23]. Notably, the increase after the 2 and 5 C sequence was more pronounced for Ta0@LMFP. Thus, surface‐intensive Ta doping mitigated TM dissolution during high‐rate operation and the resulting interfacial contamination. This is in line with the smaller increase in interfacial resistance and preserved Mn redox peaks described above.

To elucidate degradation at the electrode–electrolyte interface under high‐rate cycling, we recorded the XPS of Ta0@LMFP and Ta1@LMFP after formation and high‐rate cycles (Figure 9). Given that XPS has an information depth of several nanometers, these spectra reflected the chemical state in the interfacial region rather than that in the bulk. The fractions of each component obtained using peak deconvolution are summarized in Figure S17. The C 1s spectra in Figure 9a,b and Figure S17a,b were used to compare the extent of organic interphase formation based on the relative intensities of the main and oxygenated carbon peaks. The main peak near 284.6 eV corresponds to the C─C/C─H moieties derived from the conductive carbon and binder skeleton, while C─O, C═O, and O─C═O peaks at higher binding energies correspond to organic decomposition products derived from the carbonate electrolyte and PVDF hydrolysis (e.g., ROLi, ROCO2Li, and Li2CO3). After high‐rate cycling, the peaks of oxygenated carbon moieties markedly intensified for Ta0@LMFP, whereas a much smaller increase was observed for Ta1@LMFP, which indicated the accumulation of a thicker carbonate‐derived and binder oxidation products on Ta0@LMFP and the suppressed overgrowth of the organic CEI on Ta1@LMFP [23, 64].

(a,b) C 1s, (c,d) F 1s, and (e,f) Mn 2p XPS of Ta0@LMFP and Ta1@LMFP electrodes recorded after (a,c,e) formation cycles and (b,d,f) high‐rate cycling.

The F 1s spectra (Figure 9c,d; FigureS17c,d) reflected the behavior of the inorganic F‐containing CEI produced through PVDF and LiPF6 decomposition. The higher binding energy peak was assigned to the CF2 units of the PVDF backbone, while the LiF/MFx peak at a lower binding energy and the LixPOyFz peak at a slightly higher binding energy were observed [23]. This indicates the decomposition of LiPF6 and suggests that dissolved transition metal ions can react with F− generated during electrolyte decomposition of form metal fluorides [65]. After high‐rate cycling, LiF/MFx and Li x PO y F z peak intensification was observed for both samples, being notably larger for Ta0@LMFP (i.e., the accumulation of LiPF6‐derived products was more pronounced in this case). Together with the C 1s data, these results indicated that Ta0@LMFP formed a thicker and more resistive CEI composed of organic decomposition products and LiPF6‐derived species, whereas Ta1@LMFP maintained thinner and more homogeneous organic and inorganic layers and thus showed improved interfacial stability. This interpretation is further supported by TEM observations and the increase in the carbon layer thickness observed after cycling is attributed to the accumulation of CEI (Figure S18) [66, 67].

Figure 9e,f and Figure S17e,f display Mn 2p spectra deconvoluted into Mn2+ and Mn3+ components. After high‐rate cycling, Ta0@LMFP exhibited a decrease in the Mn2+ fraction and increase in the Mn3+ fraction. This finding indicated the accumulation of the JT‐active Mn3+ at the interface and in the near‐surface lattice, which could promote the asymmetric distortion of MnO6 octahedra and Mn disproportionation and thus accelerate structural collapse and Mn dissolution [23]. Hence, surface‐intensive Ta doping in the near‐surface lattice tuned Mn─O bonding and the local electronic structure, suppressed Mn3+ formation and accumulation, and sustained reversible Mn2+/Mn3+ conversion. Post‐mortem XPS analysis therefore indicated that surface‐intensive Ta doping regulated CEI growth and blocked Mn3+ accumulation near the interface, fundamentally mitigating Mn‐based degradation pathways. Hence, this doping was concluded to be an effective design route for securing a high‐rate capability and long cycle life.

The abovementioned results suggested that surface‐intensive Ta doping strengthened the M─O framework at the LMFP particle surface and suppressed Mn dissolution and interfacial degradation. Hence, we verified whether this structural and interfacial stabilization was maintained under the high‐temperature and high‐State of Charge (SOC) operating conditions encountered in practical applications by performing ex situ EIS and other measurements on Ta0@LMFP and Ta1@LMFP charged to 4.5 V and stored at 60°C for 10 days (Figure 10). The open‐circuit voltage of both samples decreased during storage, more rapidly for Ta0@LMFP over the entire storage period (Figure 10a). This behavior indicated that (i) Ta0@LMFP experienced much more severe self‐discharge than Ta1@LMFP under the same high‐temperature and high‐SOC conditions and (ii) surface‐intensive Ta doping continuously (from the beginning to the end of storage) suppressed interfacial side reactions that contributed to self‐discharge [14, 68].

High‐temperature storage behaviors of Ta0@LMFP and Ta1@LMFP. (a) Self‐discharge profiles of samples charged to 4.5 V. (b,c) Galvanostatic discharge curves recorded before and after storage. (d,e) Nyquist plots from ex situ EIS measurements obtained at fixed time intervals during storage.

Ta0@LMFP showed a decrease in total discharge capacity to 83.1% of the initial value after storage at 60°C for 10 days, as follows from the galvanostatic discharge profiles recorded before and after storage (Figure 10b,c). This pronounced capacity loss was manifested by the high‐voltage Mn2+/Mn3+ plateau becoming notably shorter and more sloped. In contrast, Ta1@LMFP maintained a total discharge capacity of 99.5% of the initial value under the same conditions. Thus, Ta doping strongly affected the preservation degree of active Mn‐based redox sites and the amount of electrochemically available Li+ capable of participating in Mn redox reactions during high‐temperature storage. This trend was consistent with the results of the room‐temperature high‐rate cycling test, in which Ta1@LMFP showed high capacity retention without an increase in the polarization of the Mn redox reaction.

Additionally, a comparison of SEM images of the electrode surfaces before and after high‐temperature storage at 60°C revealed that Ta0@LMFP exhibited pronounced surface coverage by decomposition byproducts and increased surface roughness after storage, whereas Ta1@LMFP maintained a relatively smooth and compact surface morphology (Figure S19). This result indicates that Ta doping suppresses the cumulative growth of CEI/deposition layers associated with interfacial side reactions under high‐temperature storage conditions. Ex situ EIS analysis at fixed time intervals was performed to more directly examine the cumulative increase in interfacial impedance during high‐temperature storage (Figure 10d,e). The Nyquist plots were fitted using an equivalent circuit to extract Rs , R SEI, and R ct. The fitted parameters are summarized in Table S8. For Ta0@LMFP, the semicircles in the high‐ and medium‐frequency regions continuously expanded as the storage time increased. After storage at 60°C for 10 days, the Rct of Ta0@LMFP increased from 70.45 to 410.4 Ω. The RSEI also increased from 26.81 to 77.44 Ω, confirming that interfacial polarization cumulatively intensified during storage [23]. In contrast, Ta1@LMFP exhibited a much smaller increase in semicircle size over the same period compared with Ta0@LMFP. This suggests that interphase growth and the accumulation of side reactions were effectively suppressed during high‐temperature storage conditions. This behavior agreed with the interpretation that during storage at 60°C, the surface lattice strengthened by Ta─O bonding in Ta1@LMFP suppressed Mn‐driven degradation and effectively limited the cumulative increase in interfacial impedance.

In summary, surface‐intensive Ta doping can be interpreted as a strategy that simultaneously enhances reaction kinetics and interfacial stability based on Mn redox stabilization. As Ta is selectively introduced near the particle surface, the local structure is modulated and the Mn2+ fraction increases, which improves the reversibility of the Mn redox reaction during charge and discharge. Such Mn redox stabilization further enhances pseudocapacitive charge storage and promotes fast charge transfer kinetics. In addition, transition metal dissolution is effectively suppressed, which induces the formation of a thin and uniform CEI layer. Consequently, the Ta1@LMFP electrode delivers consistent high performance under high‐rate, low‐temperature, and high‐temperature conditions and provides an effective strategy for designing high‐power and durable LMFP‐based cathode materials.

Conclusion

Surface‐intensive Ta doping was used to simultaneously overcome the 1D [010] Li+ diffusion limitation of LMFP and surface‐dominated degradation associated with the JT‐active Mn3+. Rietveld refinement–based XRD, Cs‐STEM/EDS‐EELS, and XPS depth profiling analyses confirmed the successful realization of surface‐intensive doping exploiting the intrinsically slow lattice diffusion of Ta5+. Ta enriched at near‐surface M2‐sites broadened Li─O diffusion pathways while lowering the average oxidation states of Mn and Fe and thereby suppressing the formation and accumulation of the JT‐active Mn3+ ions. Ta1@LMFP delivered a discharge capacity of 148.5 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C and exhibited the smallest capacity loss at high C‐rates. Low‐temperature GITT measurements showed that Ta1@LMFP maintained well‐defined Fe/Mn plateaus with markedly reduced polarization. Furthermore, during high‐rate (5 C) cycling, Ta1@LMFP maintained 92.6% of its initial capacity, whereas Ta0@LMFP retained only 84.6%, indicating a pronounced improvement in capacity retention at high rates. Overall, the surface‐intensive Ta doping strategy proposed herein was found to be an effective route for finely tuning interfacial stability and ion transport kinetics. Surface‐intensive Ta doping simultaneously addressed the sluggish Li+ diffusion and surface‐dominated Mn degradation of LMFP, providing a promising design guideline for LMFP‐based cathodes capable of wide‐temperature high‐power operation in next‐generation LIBs for EVs and ESSs.

Experimental Section

Material Synthesis

Synthesis of Mn0.5Fe0.5C2O4·2H2O

(NH4)2C2O4·H2O was dissolved in deionized water (20 mol% excess relative to Fe + Mn) upon magnetic stirring at 60°C and 300 rpm to afford Solution 1. Solution 2 was prepared by dissolving FeSO4·7H2O and MnSO4·H2O and adding L‐ascorbic acid (10 mol% relative to Fe + Mn) as a reducing agent suppressing the oxidation of Fe2+ and Mn2+ during synthesis and thus enabling a uniform coprecipitation reaction. Solution 2 was dropwise added to Solution 1 at 60°C. The resulting suspension was aged upon stirring at 60°C and 500 rpm for 2 h to secure uniform crystal growth while suppressing agglomeration and thus obtain uniformly dispersed particles. The precipitate was washed with deionized water three times and ethanol three times to remove residual impurities and dried overnight at 80°C to yield Mn0.5Fe0.5C2O4·2H2O as a yellow solid.

Synthesis of Tax@LiMn0.5Fe0.5PO4/C (Tax@LMFP, x = 0, 0.5, 1, 2)

Ta‐doped LMFP (Tax@LMFP) samples were prepared using the same procedure but different Ta doping levels (x = 0, 0.5, 1, and 2 mol%). Mn0.5Fe0.5C2O4·2H2O, LiH2PO4, and Ta2O5 were mixed to afford a Li:(Mn0.5Fe0.5):Ta:P molar ratio of 1:(1 − x):x:1 (x = 0, 0.5, 1, 2). To compensate for Li loss during high‐temperature calcination, 5 mol% excess Li was added in the form of Li2CO3, and the mixture was ball‐milled using Zr balls (10 times the total powder weight) at 350 rpm for 4 h using acetone as a solvent. After ball milling, the powder was dried overnight at 80°C and calcined at 450°C for 5 h (5°C/min) under Ar to obtain LMFP. Next, glucose was added as a carbon source at LMFP:glucose = 1:0.15 (w/w). The mixture was supplemented with acetone and Zr balls (10 times the powder weight), ball‐milled at 350 rpm for 2 h, dried overnight at 80°C, and then calcined at 700°C for 7 h (5°C/min) under Ar to afford Tax@LMFP.

Material Characterization

The structure of LMFP was probed using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (INVENIO FT‐IR, BRUKER, Germany; KBr pellets, 350–8,000 cm−1) and x‐ray diffraction (XRD; SmartLab, Rigaku, Japan; Cu K α, λ = 1.5406 Å, 2θ = 10°–80°, 1°/min, Rietveld refinement with FullProf Suite). Thermogravimetric analysis (Discovery TGA 550, TA Instruments, USA; 25°C–650°C, 10°C/min in air) was conducted to determine the carbon contents of the composites. Morphologies and elemental distributions were examined by field‐emission scanning electron microscopy (FE‐SEM, SU8010, Hitachi High‐Tech, Japan) and spherical aberration–corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (Cs‐STEM; JEM‐ARM 200F, JEOL, Japan) coupled with energy‐dispersive x‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) and electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS; Model 965 GIF Quantum ER, Gatan). Local lattice structure was probed by subjecting CS‐STEM data to fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis (Digital Micrograph 3.8, Gatan, USA). Surface composition and oxidation states were characterized by x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; K‐Alpha, Thermo Scientific, UK), with spectra calibrated using the C 1s reference peak at 284.6 eV. Transition metal (Mn, Fe) dissolution into the electrolyte and deposition on Li metal were quantified by inductively coupled plasma‐optical emission spectrometry (ICP‐OES, Agilent 5110, USA).

Cell Assembly and Electrochemical Test

The electrochemical performance of Tax@LMFP was evaluated in CR2032‐type Li half‐cells. Cathodes were prepared by casting a slurry containing 80 wt.% Tax@LMFP, 10 wt.% Super P, and 10 wt.% polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) in N‐methyl‐2‐pyrrolidone (NMP) onto Al foil followed by vacuum drying at 80°C for 12 h and punching into 12 Φ disks (active material loading: 3–4 mg/cm2). Li metal discs (14 Φ) served as counter and reference electrodes, a polyethylene separator (18 Φ) was placed between the electrodes, and 1 m LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate (EC):dimethyl carbonate (DMC):diethyl carbonate (DEC) (3:4:5, v/v/v) was used as the electrolyte. Galvanostatic charge–discharge tests were carried out using a multichannel battery tester (WBCS 3000L, WonAtech, Republic of Korea) between 2.0 and 4.5 V (vs. Li/Li+) at 25°C to evaluate rate capability and cycling stability. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves were recorded in the 2.0–4.5 V range at 25°C and various scan rates. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis (SP‐300 potentiostat, Bio‐Logic Science Instruments) was performed in the frequency range of 10 µHz to 1 MHz at an amplitude of 7 mV. The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) procedure was applied between 25°C and −20°C using a 0.1 C current pulse of 10 min followed by a 60 min rest to determine near‐equilibrium Li+ diffusion coefficients (D Li + values). In high‐temperature storage tests, cells were charged to 4.5 V and stored at 60°C for 10 days in a temperature‐controlled chamber.
