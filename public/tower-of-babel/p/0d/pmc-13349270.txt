Headache in a Patient with Anti-Leucine-Rich Glioma Inactivated 1 Autoimmune Encephalitis Responding to Rituximab
Authors: Deepak D Danigondra, Pooja Mailankody, Bharath Ravindra, Rohan Mahale, Hansashree Padmanabha
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Autoimmune encephalitis (AE) is an inflammatory disorder of the nervous system due to a misguided immune system producing antibodies against self-antigens.[1] Anti-leucine-rich glioma inactivated 1 (LGI1) AE is the second most common type of AE.[1] The prodrome of headache has been reported in anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor AE.[234] However, migrainous headache in anti-LGI1 AE is hitherto unreported.

A previously healthy 33-year-old female presented with a headache of 1 week’s duration, which was holocranial, continuous, associated with vomiting and without photophobia or phonophobia, and relieved by analgesics. There was no history of episodic headaches in the past, and the patient had no family history of migraine. Later, she developed memory disturbances in the form of forgetting recent conversations, along with behavioral disturbances such as outbursts of anger, irritability, and seizures. On presentation, she was afebrile and had no focal neurological deficits. On evaluation, her cerebro spinal fluid (CSF) showed no cells and normal protein levels. Serum testing was positive for anti-LGI1 antibody (indirect immunofluorescence assay). Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed bilateral temporal and hippocampal hyperintensities [Figure 1]. Electroencephalogram (EEG) showed periodic lateralized epileptiform discharges in the temporal region [Figure 2]. Her paraneoplastic panel for malignancy screening was negative. She was treated with steroids and plasmapheresis, following which her headache, behavioral issues, and seizures resolved.

One month later, she presented with headache and memory impairment. Cognitive evaluation revealed disorientation and short-term memory loss, with a mini-mental state examination (MMSE) score of 25/30. She was treated with steroids and rituximab and received monthly steroid pulses for 4 months. Subsequently, she developed episodic headaches without aura in the occipital region, throbbing in character, occurring at a frequency of 3–4 times per month and lasting for 6–24 hours. The headaches were associated with nausea, photophobia, and phonophobia, fulfilling migrainous criteria (International Classification of Headache Disorders [ICHD]-3).[5] There was no behavioral disturbance or cognitive decline (MMSE 30/30). Her headaches failed to improve despite treatment with amitriptyline for 15 months; valproate was later added, and both medications have been continued to date. As she had a persistent, treatment-refractory headache, she was administered rituximab (1 g), following which she experienced resolution of the headache. She was continued on maintenance rituximab every 6 months and has remained asymptomatic since then [Figure 3].

We present a case of anti-LGI1 encephalitis presenting with headache, seizures, memory impairment, and behavioral disturbances. During follow-up, she developed recurrent headaches without other initial presenting complaints, which responded promptly to rituximab. Anti-LGI1 encephalitis is a distinct subtype of AE, typically occurring in older adults, with a mean age of 59 years.[1] Classic symptoms include short-term memory deficits, faciobrachial dystonic seizures, psychiatric disturbances, hyponatremia, and sleep abnormalities.[1] Our patient developed migrainous headaches during subsequent follow-up. To our knowledge, migrainous headache as a clinical feature of anti-LGI1 encephalitis has not been previously reported.

Pain in migraine can be explained by neurogenic inflammation and abnormal excitation of the trigeminocervical region.[6] Steroids, which act via immunosuppression, have a role in the treatment of migraine.[7] Although the pathophysiology of headache remains speculative, possible mechanisms include hyperexcitability of the brain due to antibodies or meningeal inflammation resulting from the intrathecal synthesis of antibodies.[2] A patient with anti-LGI1 AE and asymptomatic breast cancer had resolution of headache and seizures following treatment with steroids and rituximab.[8] Marasakatla et al.[9] described anti-LGI1 AE mimicking reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS), presenting with severe thunderclap headaches and showing a long-term response to azathioprine [Table 1]. Similarly, our patient, who had recurrent headaches during follow-up, has been free of headaches after treatment with rituximab. The improvement in headache can be attributed to a reduction in autoimmune-driven inflammation secondary to immunosuppressive therapy. Onset in a temporal relationship to the autoimmune process and improvement after treatment suggest that the headache in this patient is secondary, according to the ICHD-3 criteria.[5] Autoimmune disorders, such as anti-cardiolipin antibody syndrome and systemic lupus erythematosus, are included in ICHD-3 as causes of headache attributed to a metabolic or systemic disorder.[5] This list does not include anti-LGI1 AE. However, it is also possible that the patient had new-onset migraine triggered by the autoimmune process.

Monoclonal antibodies against Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide (CGRP) or its receptors (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab, and eptinezumab), which are used as preventive therapies for migraine, act by inhibiting trigeminovascular inflammation.[6] Rituximab, an anti-cluster of differentiation 20 (CD20) monoclonal antibody, acts by depleting B cells and suppressing the production of pathogenic autoantibodies.[10] Although their molecular targets differ, an indirect overlap may exist at the level of downstream neuroinflammation. The sustained resolution of the headache in our patient after rituximab supports an immune-mediated mechanism rather than primary migraine.

From a pathophysiological perspective, both migraine and AE are non-infective, sterile inflammatory conditions of the brain. Hence, it is possible that headache is more prevalent in AE and is under-reported because of other disabling symptoms. Future studies should include standardized assessment of headache, long-term follow-up, and response to immunosuppression in AE. A detailed elucidation of headache characteristics in AE will lead to a better understanding of the pathophysiology, particularly the role of immune-mediated mechanisms in the genesis of migraine.

Migrainous headache is an uncommon and under-reported symptom in anti-LGI1 AE. This case highlights neuroinflammation triggered by immune-mediated mechanisms as a possible cause of migrainous headache, as it responded promptly to immunosuppression. Hence, the workup for new-onset headache should include an autoimmune panel after common causes have been ruled out. Future classifications should also include anti-LGI1 AE as a cause of headache attributed to a systemic disorder. Future studies should systematically assess headache characteristics and their response to treatment in patients with AE.
