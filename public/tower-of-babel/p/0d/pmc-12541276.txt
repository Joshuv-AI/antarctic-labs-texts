Study of Types of Pathophysiologic Patterns of Chronic Constipation and Its Correlation With Clinical Features
Authors: Ajay Kumar Jain, Sudhanshu Yadav, Suchita Jain, Shohini Sircar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic constipation is common in India. Identifying its subtypes is essential not only to treat appropriately but also to improve quality of life.AimsTo study chronic constipation subtypes and assess their clinical characteristics.MethodsThis was a prospective observational study. All patients above 18 years with chronic constipation (as per ROME IV criteria) meeting inclusion and exclusion criteria were recruited. Their evaluation included colonic transit study, colonoscopy, anorectal manometry & balloon expulsion test and accordingly were classified into subtypes.ResultsThe mean age of the patient was 50.60 ± 15.61 years. Normal transit constipation was commonest observed in 64.0%, followed by dyssynergic defecation in 19.9%, slow transit in 14.3% and mixed (slow transit + dyssynergic) in 1.9%. Despite complaining of constipation, 70.2% of patients had 7–14 bowel movements per week, while 2.5% reported > 21 bowel movements per week. Only 3.1% reported bowel movements of less than 3 per week. The typical symptoms are sense of incomplete evacuation reported by 93.8%, straining by 88.8%, anorectal blockage by 13%, and manual evacuation by 14.3%. There was significant association between type of constipation, stool frequency and time spent in the toilet (p‐value < 0.001). In patient with dyssynergic defecation there was good correlation between digital rectal examination with manometry and balloon expulsion test (68.5% sensitivity and 96.8% specificity).ConclusionA sense of incomplete evacuation and not the number of bowel movements per week is the predominant symptom described by most patients with constipation in India. Chronic constipation should be subclassified into its subtypes for individualized treatment.

Introduction

Chronic constipation (CC) is a common gastrointestinal disorder, with global & Indian prevalence of 14% and 12%–17% [1, 2], respectively. Constipation results in millions of physician visits yearly, resulting in substantial healthcare utilization [3]. The definition of chronic constipation varies globally as the perception of what constitutes constipation varies with culture [4]. In India, constipation is identified as infrequent bowel movements, excessive straining, hard consistency of stools, a sense of incomplete evacuation, failed or lengthy attempts to defecate, use of digital maneuvers for evacuation of stool, and abdominal bloating [3]. Constipation amongst adults is often related to diet, physical inactivity, concurrent illness, or multiple medication use [2]. Whereas amongst children, multiple factors determine the pathophysiology of constipation; however, withholding of stools, starting after an experience of a hard, painful, or frightening bowel movement, is the most common cause found in children [5]. Chronic constipation, apart from significantly impacting the quality of life, also leads to hemorrhoids, rectal prolapse, diverticular disease, anal fissure, fecal impaction, etc. [2].

Recent advances in imaging and anorectal manometry have made it possible to subdivide chronic constipation on a pathophysiological basis into three subtypes, that is, normal transit, slow transit, and dyssynergic defecation. Treatment varies from dietary and behavioral approaches, pharmacologic therapy and non‐pharmacologic therapies (biofeedback), and surgical treatment depending upon the sub‐type of chronic constipation in selected patients [6]. Therefore, it's essential to identify the subtypes of chronic constipation and treat them accordingly to improve their symptoms, quality of life and decrease the economic burden. As data on subtypes of constipation is scarce from central India, we aimed to study the subtypes of chronic constipation in central India and its correlation with clinical characteristics, including presenting symptoms.

Materials and Methods

This observational study was done over a period of 21 months at a single center. All patients presenting with chronic constipation who fulfilled the inclusion and exclusion criteria were enrolled in this study. The inclusion criteria were patients above the age of 18 years presenting predominantly with symptoms of constipation for more than 6 months and fulfilled ROME IV criteria. All patients with secondary causes of constipation (like patients taking drugs such as acetaminophen, calcium supplements, diuretics, anticonvulsant, 5HT3 antagonist, NSAIDS which are known to cause constipation, or patients with comorbid condition of hypothyroidism, diabetes mellitus, previous open laparotomy, and bowel resection) were excluded from this study. During the study period, 237 patients presented with symptoms of constipation. Based on inclusion and exclusion criteria, 161 patients were recruited and 76 patients were excluded. All 161 patients, after detailed history and clinical examination, underwent colonic transit study, digital rectal examination (DRE), colonoscopy and blood investigations. Out of these 161 patients those who didn't respond to initial treatment (as per recommendation of Indian consensus on constipation which included lifestyle modification and bulk laxatives or osmotic laxatives or stool softeners) and in whom dyssynergic defecation was suspected clinically or by transit study (Ghosal protocol) underwent anorectal manometry and balloon expulsion test. High resting anal sphincter pressure, negative recto‐anal gradient, dyssynergic pattern of defecation and inability to evacuate balloon filled with 50 mL saline within 2 min was considered positive for dyssynergic defecation. Colonic transit time was studied using Ghosal Protocol [7, 8, 9]. According to this protocol 20 radio‐opaque markers filled in two capsules were administered at 0, 12, and 24 h and then abdominal radiographs obtained at 36 and 60 h. Retention of > 30 radio‐opaque markers at 36 h (sensitivity 90% and specificity 82%) and > 14 markers at 60 h (sensitivity 95% and specificity 100%) was quite accurate to detect slow transit constipation and fecal evacuation disorder (FED) as cause for chronic constipation. All patients, after proper evaluation as per protocol, were grouped into normal transit constipation (NTC), slow transit constipation (STC), dyssynergic defecation (DD), and mixed type, and their clinical, demographic and socioeconomic profile were analyzed. Descriptive statistics were performed. A comparison of responses between different age groups and gender was done using the chi‐square test; p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Two hundred and thirty‐seven (237) consecutive patients above 18 years of age presented with constipation. Only 161 patients fulfilled inclusion and exclusion criteria and were recruited for this study. The mean age of the study population was 50.6067 ± 15.61401 years (20–93 years), with the majority of 44/161 (27.3%) of the patients in the elderly age group (> 60 years). The male‐to‐female ratio of the study population was 1.8:1. Based on socioeconomic status (modified Kuppuswamy scale), 78/161 (48.4%) patients belonged to the lower middle class, followed by upper middle class 64/161 (39.8%). As most of the study population was vegetarian and could not recall the last 7 days' food intake, the effect of food on constipation was not studied. In our study, daily water intake of 2 or > 2 L was reported by 72/161 (44.7%) patients, while the remaining 89/161 (55.3%) reported water intake of < 2 L. National Institution of Nutrition, Hyderabad recommends at least 2 L of water intake per day for the Indian population [6]. A Dutch study has shown that at least 2 L of water per day increases the efficacy of dietary fiber in Chronic Constipation [10]. In this study, 82% (132/161) patients were engaged in physical activity of > 25 min/day. On comparing the types of toilets used by our study population, 57.1% (92/161) patients used Indian toilets and 42.9% (69/161) used western toilets. 83.8% (135/161) of patients had taken complementary and alternative medicines for constipation (Table 1).

In terms of frequency of defecation in our study group, most patients, despite complaining of constipation, had 1–2 bowel movements per day (113/161), and another 20/161 had bowel movements > 15/week. There were only 28 patients who had bowel movements < 6/week. Out of the three subtypes of constipation, NTC was the most common subtype, 64% (103/161), followed by DD 19.9% (32/161) and STC 14.3% (23/161). Mixed type, that is, slow transit with dyssynergic, was uncommon and was seen in 1.9% (3/161). In patients more than 60 years of age, normal transit constipation was seen in 13.6% of patients, with slow transit in 60.9% and dyssynergic defecation seen in 46.9%. There was a statistically significant association between age and the type of constipation (p value < 0.05). Post hoc analysis revealed that normal transit constipation was significantly less than expected amongst patients aged more than 60 years, whereas slow transit, DD, and mixed types of constipation were significantly more than expected amongst them.

In our study, commonly reported symptoms by patients were straining during defecation, sense of incomplete evacuation and hard stools (Bristol type I, II, III) with frequency of 88% (143/161), 93.7% (151/161) and 82% (132/161), respectively. Only a minority of patients reported a sense of anorectal blockage and manual evacuation. Straining was reported by 100% of patients with DD, 95.7% of patients with STC, while 83.5% of patients with NTC reported straining. Sense of incomplete evacuation was the predominant symptom reported by 95.1% and 95.7% of patients with NTC and STC, respectively (Table 1).

In our study, the association between constipation subtypes and stool frequency is significant (p value < 0.001). Most patients with NTC 90/103 (87.4%) and DD 21/32 (65.6%) had a stool frequency of 7–14/week. In comparison, most patients with slow transit constipation 17/23 (73.9%) had a stool frequency of 3–6/week, suggesting that patients with STC have lower stool frequency than those with normal transit and dyssynergic defecation. A statistically significant association was found between the type of constipation and time spent in the toilet (p‐value < 0.001). While 97.1% (100/103) of patients with NTC spend less than 20 min in the toilet, significantly fewer patients with DD and mixed type (slow transit + DD type) of constipation spend less than 20 min in the toilet (68.8% and 66.7%, respectively). DD, suspected clinically by DRE, had a good correlation with anorectal manometry and balloon expulsion test with a sensitivity of 68.5% and specificity of 96.8%. For males, sensitivity is 60% and specificity is 98.8%. For females, sensitivity is 80% and specificity is 93.02% (Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

Most patients with constipation take Complementary Alternative Medicine for years together for symptomatic relief. It is one symptom that affects the quality of life to such an extent that most of these patients visit multiple physicians without satisfaction and with considerable economic loss. It has been seen that if we can identify the subtype of constipation, treatment can be tailored. This study assessed subtypes of constipation with their clinical features. In our study of 161 patients, the majority are males, 64% (103 out of 161), compared to females, 36% (58 out of 161). Similar results were reported in a study by Shah et al. This contrasts with various studies showing that constipation is more common in females. The reason for this disparity may be higher health seeking behavior amongst males than females, as Indian society is male‐dominated [8].

In our study, 70% of patients had a stool frequency of 7–14/week. Whereas 3.1% had stool frequency < 3 stools/week. Similar results were found in a multicentric study by Ghosal et al. [9]. Several studies from India have reported that most people in India pass one or more stools per day, and patients with self‐perceived constipation report a median of two stools per day, which suggests clearly that less than three stools/week could not be used to define constipation in India as Indians have higher stool frequency than West [2]. In this study, 82% of patients have stool form of Bristol type I, II, III. Similar results were found in a study of 331 patients from eastern India [11]. In our study, the association between constipation subtypes and stool frequency is significant (p‐value < 0.001). Most patients with normal transit constipation (83.9%) had a stool frequency of 7–14/week, while patients with slow transit constipation (73.9%) had a stool frequency of 3–6/week, and 65.6% of patients with dyssynergic defecation had stool frequency 7–14/week. Similar results were reported in the past from India [8, 12]. This study also suggests that patients with slow transit constipation have lower stool frequency than normal transit and dyssynergic defecation.

Out of six symptoms of constipation described in ROME IV criteria, a sense of incomplete evacuation is the most common symptom reported by 93.8% (151) of patients, followed by straining, which was reported by 88.8% (143) of patients. 82% of the patients had hard stool (Bristol type I, II, III), whereas stool frequency < 3/week, manual evacuation of stool, and sense of anorectal blockage were reported by 3.1% (5), 14.3% (23), and 13% (21) patients respectively. In a review by Patimah et al. of the core symptoms of constipation in various Asian studies, commonly encountered symptoms were “straining,” “sense of incomplete evacuation,” “lumpy and hard stool” and “infrequent defecations” while “sensation of anorectal blockage” and “use of manual maneuver” to evacuate stools were less common symptoms [13]. In our study, the association between gender and constipation subtypes is insignificant (p > 0.5). In contrast, studies from the West have shown that fecal evacuation disorders are more common in females than males [14]. In the study by Ghosal et al., FED was more common in females than males [9]; no such disparity was seen in our study as most of the study population was male.

In this study, there is a statistically significant association between type of constipation and straining (p value < 0.05). In patients with normal transit constipation, straining is significantly less than in patients with constipation due to DD. In this study, there is no statistically significant association between the type of constipation and the sense of incomplete evacuation (p value > 0.05). In the study by Koch et al., feelings of incomplete evacuation, sense of anal blockage, and self‐digitation were associated with disordered defecation [15]. In an Indian study, prolonged straining (≥ 30 min), incomplete evacuation, and higher squeeze pressure were associated with FED [9]. A statistically significant association was found between the type of constipation and time spent in the toilet (p‐value < 0.001). While 97.1% of patients with normal transit constipation spend less than 20 min in the toilet, whereas 68.8% of patients with DD and 66.7% of patients with mixed type (slow transit + DD type) of constipation spend less than 20 min in the toilet. In their study, Ghosal et al. [9] suggested that straining for > 30 min, a sense of anorectal blockage, excessive straining, and manual evacuation of the stool are more common with dyssynergic defecation. Their study further showed sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and negative PV of DRE in detecting FED to be 69.7%, 81.5%, 82.1%, and 68.75%, respectively [9]. In our study, there is a statistically significant correlation between the findings of the digital rectal examination and manometry and balloon expulsion test for diagnosis of Dyssynergic defecation (p‐value < 0.001*) with a sensitivity of 68.5% and specificity of 96.8%. Another study reported 75% sensitivity and 87% specificity of DRE for identifying dyssynergia in patients with chronic constipation [16]. This suggests that the digital rectal examination test is a good screening tool for detecting dyssynergic defecation. Another important observation of this study was the use of complementary and alternative medications for constipation (83.9%). In a review, Peng et al. found that one out of three person suffering from constipation uses complementary and alternative medicines with herbal preparation [17]. The limitation of the study included that no attempt had been made to analyze the quality of life of patients with different types of constipation. Further research is needed to assess the impact of constipation on the quality of life of the Indian population.

Conclusion

Normal transit constipation is the most prevalent type of constipation among the population of central India.This study suggests that chronic constipation needs to be investigated appropriately to know the subtypes for proper and meaningful management.
