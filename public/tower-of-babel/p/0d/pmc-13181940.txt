Constructing and characterizing trillion-scale combinatorial chemical library
Authors: Jiaqi Su, Fu V. Song, Dawei Huang, Maofu Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe scale of screening within ultra-large chemical spaces plays a pivotal role in contemporary drug discovery, particularly during the initial stages of hit identification. Construction of such chemical spaces to allow their efficient exploration for compound discovery is a critical challenge. In this research, we have generated a novel combinational chemical library, through integration of a curated reaction set with over 1.8 million available building blocks, resulting in a chemical space with more than one trillion molecules theoretically.To characterize the structural diversity, novelty, and physicochemical properties of the compounds in this trillion-scale library, a randomly sampled subset as a surrogate for exploration, rather than an exhaustive search algorithm, was employed. Our results demonstrate that, at both the fragment-level and molecule-level chemical spaces, the constructed library encompasses broad physicochemical diversity and rich scaffold novelty, overlapping with but also extending beyond natural product and FDA-approved chemical spaces. Scaffold retrieval analyses indicated near-ideal structural diversity at scale. Following virtual screening, the hit compounds need to be chemically synthesized, which is often resource demanding. Leveraging the unique characteristics of the combinatorial chemical space and employing a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) model, we have developed a strategy to maximize the utilization of building blocks for chemical synthesis to generate a larger number of molecules with desired properties (drug-likeness, natural product-likeness, and structure similarity). Together, our work has established a theoretically trillion-scale combinatorial chemical library which can facilitates efficient virtual screening and hit identification and further developed a novel method for resource-optimized chemical synthesis.Scientific ContributionThis study presents a synthetically accessible trillion-scale combinatorial chemical library constructed from a curated reaction set and over 1.8 million building blocks, providing a highly diverse and scalable resource for ultra-large virtual screening. In addition, we develop a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO)-based strategy forresource-efficient compound synthesis, which maximizes building block utilization while generating molecules with desired properties. Together, this work establishes an integrated framework that bridges large-scale chemical space exploration with practical synthesis, enabling more efficient hit identification and downstream optimization in drug discovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13321-026-01185-7.

Introduction

For a long time, the classic paradigm of drug research has been using virtual screening to pick hits from chemical space, followed by biological activity experiments and subsequent progression to clinical trials [1]. Traditional chemical compound libraries are often categorized into natural product library [2], FDA library [3], drug-like compound library [4], and others. They mostly belong to the category of commercial compound resources [5]. However, the numbers of these libraries are limited. Currently, with the development of computation, AI-based drug screening methods and GPU-accelerated molecular docking can rapidly screen thousands to millions of molecules in several hours or days [6–12]. However, even when aggregating compound libraries from various commercial libraries, the total only reaches the billion-compound level [2, 3, 13, 14]. Traditional chemical libraries have fallen behind advanced computational methods. A larger and novel chemical space needs to be constructed and explored by scientists.

The advent of make-on-demand compound libraries has significantly expanded the accessible chemical space, enabling the exploration of virtual collections comprising billions of synthetically accessible molecules [15–19]. Chemists can synthesize these compounds through a few steps, ensuring high synthetic yield. Previous research results indicated that screening based on ultra-large chemical spaces was highly successful, and the picked molecules were synthesized in 3–4 weeks with a high success rate [20]. Recent reviews have emphasized that chemical space is extraordinarily vast, and that advances in computational software and hardware now enable systematic navigation of virtual chemical spaces that far exceed the scale of traditional physical libraries, spanning billions to trillions of compounds [16]. As a representative example, the Proximal Lilly Collection demonstrated systematic mapping and exploitation of feasible chemical space through virtual libraries focused on synthetically tractable structures [18]. This combinatorial chemical space encompasses hundreds of billions of chemical molecules and is highly scalable. Another advantage of combinatorial chemical space over traditional compound libraries is that it does not require scientists to enumerate all the molecules [21]. It only needs to store the building blocks (BBs), significantly reducing resource consumption. Given the vastness of this chemical space, full enumeration is impractical, necessitating specialized chemical software for collection, organization, and enumeration processes. In parallel, sustained efforts by the scientific community have led to the development and public release of multiple open-source combinatorial chemistry resources, which collectively highlight the remarkable diversity of chemical reactions and the vastness of synthetically accessible chemical space [22–24].

Recent developments in quantum computing have highlighted coherent Ising machines as a powerful approach for solving complex combinatorial optimization tasks [19, 20, 25]. These machines work by mapping an optimization task onto the Hamiltonian of a quantum system and using quantum evolution to find its ground state, which represents the optimal solution [27]. Simulating quantum annealing, Ising machines can efficiently solve combinatorial challenges such as MAX-CUT and maximum weighted clique problems [28]. Since the Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) and Ising models are mathematically equivalent, they can be interconverted and flexibly applied in both quantum and classical frameworks. QUBO aims to minimize a quadratic function over binary variables without constraints and is encoded into a Hamiltonian through qubit interactions. This approach shows strong potential in fields like combinatorial chemistry and drug discovery. Quantum Ising machines have already been applied in biological sciences, including RNA folding, molecular unfolding, and molecular docking tasks [29–31]. Despite these advances, their use in virtual screening of ultra-large compound libraries remains largely unexplored, with most current approaches still relying on conventional algorithms or AI models [20, 32–36].

To systematically construct and explore combinatorial chemical spaces, this work effectively designed a rapid, straightforward, and scalable program–SANQI (a traditional Chinese medicinal herb). A vast commercial chemical reagent library was collected and categorized according to the chemical reactions and the BBs. By incorporating these chemical reactions information, a chemical space consisting of hundreds of billions of molecules was formed. In subsequent chemical space representation, the physicochemical properties, structural diversity, and novelty of the chemical space are represented. Furthermore, this research developed a quantum strategy to explore this building blocks space and applied it to optimize molecule generation within selected chemical subspaces (Fig. 1). Results

We first compile and construct a chemical reaction library, followed by the collection of a building block library from commercial sources. By utilizing reaction fragment information from the chemical reaction library, we classify the building block library, thereby constructing a large combinatorial chemical library. Subsequently, a quantum optimization strategy is employed to search this chemical space.

Construction of the chemical reaction set and the building block set

The construction of the combinatorial chemistry library involves two main parts. The first part is to collect chemical reactions and store them in a standard format. Formatted and standardized chemical reaction data has always been difficult to collect. We reorganized the chemical reactions from this research [24]. These reactions, all related to organic synthesis, were stored in a computer-readable reaction SMARTS format, with graphical representations for verification. About 50% of these reactions were cyclization reactions, resulting in a high diversity and innovation of chemical scaffolds in the products. We recompiled 58 reactions from the paper and selected 39 available reactions. We exclude some reactions that could not be recognized by RDKit and some unbalanced reactions (the reasons can be found in the file within the Data Availability) [37]. Then, we formatted them into reaction SMILES (Fig. 2). These reactions follow the ‘A + B = C’ scheme, with 16 being cyclizations, which significantly increases the potential for generating novel molecular scaffolds.Fig. 2The Chemical Database Formation of SANQI. Further, we extracted reaction fragments from the reactants of these chemical reactions. These fragments help us select suitable reaction molecules from the building block space. The next step in the process is to categorize the BBs according to the reactions. We gathered compound libraries primarily from three companies: Enamine [38], ChemDiv [39], and LifeChemicals [40]. These compounds were then converted into SMILES format. After removing duplicates, the final building block library contained 1,581,956 unique entries from ChemDiv, 194,419 from Enamine, and 101,046 from LifeChemicals. These BBs were classified according to their reaction fragments through substructure matching. The total number of chemical molecules that can ultimately be synthesized from these fragments through these reactions is estimated to be around 1.094 trillion. The distribution of reaction products is as follows, with the top five reactions being: Reductive amination (70.65%), Sonogashira (15.05%), Heck terminal vinyl (2.63%), Schotten-Baumann amide (1.66%), and Pictet-Spengler (1.57%) (Figs. S1 and S5).

The physicochemical properties analysis

Given the immense scale of combinatorial chemical space, exhaustive enumeration is infeasible and limited sampling cannot faithfully capture its structural diversity. Because building blocks constitute the immutable fundamental units of this space, a comprehensive analysis of their physicochemical properties is therefore indispensable. We subsequently analyzed the building blocks chemical space. In parallel, we also analyzed other chemical spaces (Fig. S2). The FDA library is from DrugBank [3, 13] and the FDA fragment library, which is generated by applying the breaking of retrosynthetically interesting chemical substructures (BRICS) [41] method in RDKit to the FDA database. The natural product (NP) library is from the COCNUT [6] database, and the natural product fragment library is similarly generated by applying the BRICS method in RDKit. From an overall perspective, the chemical spaces investigated in this study can be classified into two major categories: fragment-level chemical spaces (BBs, NP Fragment, and FDA Fragment) and molecule-level chemical spaces (SANQI 1E6, NP, and FDA). Within fragment-level chemical spaces, the BBs (Fig. 3) occupy an intermediate regime between NP Fragment (Fig. S2d) and FDA Fragment (Fig. S2b). BBs exhibit a mean molecular weight of 370.05 Da, exceeding that of NP Fragment and FDA Fragment, with a range from 31.06 to 1675.93 Da, and approximately 75% of molecules below 442.52 Da. In terms of HAC, 75% of molecules contain no more than 31 heavy atoms. BBs maintain relatively constrained distributions in LogP, TPSA (75% of molecules below 85.69 Å2, ranging from 0 to 463.02 Å2), NHA (99.67% of molecules ≤ 10, and 99.76% with at least one acceptor), and NHD (almost all molecules ≤ 5, with 74.08% possessing at least one donor). BBs further exhibit moderate ring content (maximum observed 10 and 12, respectively; 55.67% of molecules with at least one aliphatic ring and 95.56% with at least one aromatic ring) and 98.44% of molecules have fewer than 10 rotatable bonds, reflecting a structurally controlled yet information-rich chemical space suitable for combinatorial expansion. In contrast, NP Fragment and FDA Fragment are characterized by lower molecular weights, fewer heavy atoms and rotatable bonds, and more compact property distributions, consistent with their fragment-level representation.

At the molecule-level chemical spaces, SANQI 1E6 (Fig. S2e), NP (Fig. S2c), and FDA (Fig. S2a) exhibit distinct profiles. SANQI 1E6 is enriched in larger and more complex molecules, with an average molecular weight of 772.61 Da, LogP of 7.05, HAC of 54.23, TPSA of 129.97 Å2, NHA of 10.00, NHD of 1.52, approximately 5 aromatic and 1.67 aliphatic rings, and 12.91 rotatable bonds. NP molecules occupy an intermediate regime in terms of size but display higher polarity and hydrogen-bonding capacity, whereas FDA-approved drugs are clustered within a narrower, more balanced chemical space with limited flexibility, reflecting optimization for oral bioavailability. Compared with BBs, SANQI 1E6 approximately doubles molecular weight, heavy atom count, TPSA, and number of rotatable bonds, while simultaneously increasing lipophilicity and ring complexity.

Overall, BBs offer a large-scale, chemically controlled, and synthetically tractable starting space with favorable properties, whereas SANQI 1E6 represents a molecule-level chemical space with greatly expanded size, complexity, and diversity, making it suitable for high-throughput virtual screening and hit discovery.

Diversity and novelty

To evaluate the structure novelty of this library, we compared the SANQI 1E6 space, the BBs space, the NP library, the NP Fragment library, the FDA library, and the FDA Fragment library. To reduce computational cost, we first extracted the Bemis-Murcko molecular scaffolds for each compound and calculated the corresponding ECFP6 fingerprints based on these scaffolds. The Bemis-Murcko scaffolds represent the full scaffold level, retaining all ring systems and linkers while removing side chains, thereby capturing the core structural features of each molecule. The ECFP6 fingerprints were then subjected to t-SNE dimensionality reduction to characterize the chemical space distributions of different molecular libraries (Fig. 4a). The results show that SANQI 1E6 and the BBs occupy a substantial region of the overall chemical space, covering most of the space represented by the FDA library and the FDA fragment library (Fig. S3 d,f). In comparison between BBs and the NP fragment library, BBs cover part of the NP fragment chemical space; however, a distinct region remains on the left side of the space for NP fragments, indicating structural features that differ from those of BBs (Fig. S3e). In comparison between SANQI 1E6 and the NP library, although BBs cover only a portion of the NP fragment chemical space, SANQI 1E6 spans a much larger fraction of the NP space (Fig. S3c). Nevertheless, a subset of NP molecules retains clear uniqueness, highlighting the intrinsic structural distinctiveness of natural products. At the same time, SANQI 1E6 contains a large number of molecules exhibiting NP-like structural features. In addition to the randomly generated 1E6 set, we further employed SANQI to generate a series of molecular spaces with 1E4 and 1E5 molecules, investigating their corresponding properties (Fig. 4c–e). The analysis indicates that the BBs space contains a larger scaffold repertoire than the NP Fragment and FDA Fragment libraries. Similarly, SANQI 1E6 contains more scaffolds than the NP library and FDA library. The absolute number of scaffolds in the SANQI-generated spaces (1E4, 1E5, 1E6) increases progressively with the number of sampled molecules (Fig. 4c). These results indicate that SANQI chemical space contains a broad range of distinctive molecular frameworks.

We also calculated two important drug optimization metrics: the quantitative estimate of drug-likeness (QED) [42] and the NP-likeness score [43]. For QED score, both the BBs and NP spaces remain among the top-ranking, and the SANQI 1E6 space also ranks highly, with QED scores increasing alongside sampling size (Fig. 4d). For NP-likeness scores, the BBs space outperforms SANQI 1E6, whereas the NP and NP fragment libraries exhibit the highest scores. Although SANQI 1E6 does not reach the topmost NP-likeness levels, it maintains a score above 0.6 and continues to show an upward trend (Fig. 4e). The results demonstrate that as the sampling size increased, the number of molecules achieving top QED scores and top NP-likeness scores also rose, suggesting that larger chemical spaces encompass a greater abundance of high-quality molecules with potential drug-like properties (Fig. 4d,e). Within the vast chemical space of SANQI, there are undoubtedly more molecules that conform to both QED and NP properties.

Case: Better BBs combinations solved by QUBO

In principle, computing and exhaustively exploring a chemical space of this magnitude is prohibitively expensive. A full traversal of the chemical space considered in this work would theoretically require over 1 million binary variables (bits), rendering both execution on practical quantum hardware and solution via classical QUBO solvers computationally prohibitive [26, 44]. Consequently, the envisioned application scenario of our approach is not to directly optimize the entire combinatorial chemical space, but rather to perform a secondary optimization using QUBO after an initial filtering step.

Specifically, we assume that a sufficiently large number of molecules has already been sampled from the combinatorial chemical space, and that the top-scoring molecules (top-n) have been identified based on predefined molecular properties. The building blocks (BBs) associated with these top-ranked molecules are then encoded into a QUBO formulation, followed by optimization step to identify optimal combinations of BBs. This strategy aims to select BB combinations that can generate a larger number of high-quality molecules. In this study, we use the previously sampled SANQI 1E6 chemical space (an initial filtering step) as the foundation and apply QUBO optimization to BBs derived from the top-n molecules ranked by three molecular properties. Subsequently, we employed three different scoring metrics-QED, NP, and Structure Similarity (SS)-to evaluate the molecular combinations (using CHOLESTEROL as the reference molecule for similarity comparisons). As shown in Fig. 5, the QUBO-based approach enables the selection of superior molecular combinations, defined here as achieving more efficient use of building blocks to generate a larger number of molecules.

Concretely, we first utilized the 1E6-molecule library generated by SANQI and selected the top 100 building block (BB) pairs according to specified molecular properties, including QED, NP, or SS. These selected pairs were then formulated as a QUBO problem. In this formulation, BBs are treated as nodes, and all feasible reactions between each pair of BBs are enumerated. The corresponding reaction products are generated, and the target objective functions (QED, NP, or SS) are evaluated for each product. The edge weight between two nodes is then defined as the average value of the objective function over all generated products. Under this framework, the optimization objective is to select 10 BBs from the top 100 BB pairs such that these BBs can collectively generate as many molecules as possible while satisfying predefined constraints. We impose the constraint that the highest-ranked molecule must be included in the selected set. Consequently, the QUBO optimization identifies an optimal combination of 10 BBs that maximizes molecular coverage and quality within the reduced search space. For comparison, we constructed two baseline strategies: one in which the same number of BBs was selected from the top 100 BB pairs according to score ranking (denoted as SAME BB), and another in which the same number of molecules was directly selected according to score ranking to generate molecules (denoted as SAME MOL). These two baseline strategies were selected from the 1E6-molecule library.

The results demonstrate that the QUBO-based selection strategy consistently outperforms conventional approaches (SAME BB and SAME MOL) across multiple scoring criteria, including QED, NP, and SS (Sup. Table 2). Under QED optimization, QUBO generated 49 molecules spanning 10 building blocks and 49 unique scaffolds (Fig. 5b), with the top 10 molecules achieving an average score of 0.908 and 1.111 molecules per building block (Sup. Table 3). In comparison, SAME BB produced only 5 molecules (using 10 building blocks), whereas SAME MOL yielded 49 molecules but required 95 building blocks and exhibited slightly lower scaffold coverage, with higher top 10 average score (0.942). For NP optimization, QUBO selected 26 compounds encompassing 25 unique scaffolds using only 10 building blocks (Fig. S4a), with the top 10 molecules scoring an average of 0.700 and 1.429 molecules per building block. In comparison, SAME BB produced 5 compounds and SAME MOL produced 26 compounds covering 20 scaffolds (using 42 building blocks), with a higher top 10 average score of 0.785 (Sup. Table 4). Even in SS-based selection, QUBO generates 45 molecules and 45 unique scaffolds from 10 building blocks (Fig S4b). The top 10 molecules achieved an average score of 0.452 with 1.0 molecules per building block, whereas SAME BB and SAME MOL either produced fewer unique scaffolds or required substantially more building blocks (Sup. Table 5). The Zipf [45] plot (Figs. 5c, S4c, d) further indicates that QUBO achieves substantially higher utilization of building blocks compared to baseline strategies (SAME BB and SAME MOL). Concurrently, the molecules produced by QUBO demonstrated substantial diversity, which is clearly reflected in the heatmap (Figs. 5d, S5e).

Although the top 10 average scores of QUBO-selected molecules were occasionally slightly lower, the significant gains in unique and synthesizable candidates underscore the method’s efficiency. The overall average scores of QUBO-selected molecules are lower, likely due to the inclusion of additional low-scoring but synthetically accessible combinations. Overall, the QUBO-based selection approach enables the synthesis of more molecules using fewer building blocks, thereby improving the efficiency of building block utilization.

Discussion

The results of this study illustrate the potential of the SANQI framework in expanding and exploring combinatorial chemical spaces for drug discovery. The proposed method demonstrates the ability to construct an ultra-large chemical space with notable structural novelty and diversity, allowing for the exploration of hundreds of billions of molecules. This capability addresses a crucial need in the field, particularly in the early stages of drug discovery and optimization.

For such an extensive chemical space, even with sample sizes reaching the million level, random sampling may introduce biases, indicating a need for more advanced statistical methods. Additionally, researchers can incorporate additional one-step chemical reactions to the SANQI to further broaden the chemical space. The in-house chemical reactions can facilitate subsequent experimental validation. Moreover, if these chemical reaction principles are integrated into generative models, the synthetic feasibility of AI-generated molecules can be significantly enhanced. And this study did not include mulit -step synthesis reactions, this presents an avenue for future optimization. Addressing multi-step synthesis may involve dimensionality reduction from HUBO (Higher-order Unconstrained Binary Optimization) to QUBO, representing a promising direction for further research.

To explore this combinatorial chemical space, this study employed the QUBO model to search the BBs space, yielding promising results. This approach enhances broader applicability in chemical discovery. Furthermore, the QUBO method offers great flexibility, allowing for optimization towards various goals, such as drug-like similarity, natural product characteristics, structure similarity, and more. It can be applied to different stages of chemical discovery, extending beyond small molecule drug design to other areas of synthetic chemistry and material design by simply adjusting the target score function in the QUBO model. Future research also could incorporate QUBO into deeper integration, for example in molecular docking (pose generation and sampling) and pharmacophore modeling (optimal selection of molecular features). Researchers could introduce further constraints-for instance, specifying particular reaction types or constraining edge weights in the network. Additionally, algorithmic parameters, including iteration settings, could be optimized to improve workflow efficiency and outcomes. Furthermore, the development of AI-based methods, including other heuristic exploration strategies, could effectively probe the combinatorial chemical space, providing a more practical starting point for subsequent QUBO optimization. And with the advancement of quantum computing, transforming this problem into a combinatorial optimization issue could enable faster exploration of the entire chemical space.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduced SANQI, a modular and extensible framework for the systematic generation and interrogation of an ultra-large combinatorial chemical space. By encoding 39 rigorously vetted organic synthesis reactions in SMARTS format and classifying 1.88 million commercially available building blocks via reaction-fragment matching, SANQI enumerated approximately 1 trillion virtual compounds. Comprehensive physicochemical profiling and scaffold-retrieval analyses confirmed that the resulting library encompassed a structurally diverse and novel domain of chemical matter relative to natural products and FDA-approved molecules. To address the combinatorial explosion in building-block selection, we recast the subset-selection task as a quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) problem and deployed coherent Ising machines to identify high-quality building-block ensembles. In this research, the quantum-inspired QUBO approach yielded a substantially greater number of synthesizable compounds exhibiting drug-likeness, natural-product likeness, and structure similarity metrics. These findings validate the integration of reaction-based virtual library construction with quantum-inspired combinatorial optimization as a robust strategy for rational compound design and lay the groundwork for future expansions to multi-step synthesis planning, generative model integration, and experimental lead validation.

Materials and methods

Building blocks space construction and random sampling

Building block libraries were collected from three commercial suppliers: Enamine, ChemDiv, and LifeChemicals. These compounds were then converted into SMILES representations and removed duplicates. No additional filtering criteria (e.g., molecular weight or functional group constraints) were applied, as these compounds originate from commercially available building block libraries. These BBs were then classified according to their reaction fragments through substructure matching using the HasSubstructMatch function implemented in the RDKit package, based on the fragment patterns defined in the ‘reaction.csv’ file. To explore the combinatorial chemical space generated by these BBs and reactions, a random sampling strategy was applied. In each iteration, 100 BBs were randomly selected from the library, and all possible BB pairs were evaluated against the defined reaction rules. When both BBs satisfied the fragment requirements of a reaction, the corresponding product was generated and added to a molecule set to avoid duplication. This process was repeated iteratively until the number of sampled molecules reached predefined thresholds (1E4, 1E5, or 1E6).

Chemical space visualization

RDKit is utilized to compute various physicochemical properties for the molecules set. The distributions of these properties were visualized using Matplotlib and Seaborn. For diversity analysis, RDKit is employed to determine the Bemis-Murcko molecular scaffolds, and the number of distinct scaffolds is quantified in each sampled dataset [37, 46]. The Bemis-Murcko scaffolds represent the full scaffold level, retaining all ring systems and linkers while removing side chains. Additionally, the ECFP6 fingerprint is calculated by RDKit (radius = 3, nBits = 2048) for the molecule set [47]. The t-SNE dimensionality reduction analysis is then conducted using scikit-learn, and visual representations are generated by Matplotlib.

The molecular database diversity

Previous studies have shown that CSR curves are a reliable method for quantifying scaffold diversity in chemical libraries [48]. In this work, CSR curves are constructed for each chemical library using scaffold data. The x-axis of the CSR curve represents the proportion of scaffolds, while the y-axis displays the percentage of compounds containing those scaffolds. From the CSR curves, two key metrics are derived: the Area Under the Curve (AUC) and F50 (the fraction of scaffolds required to retrieve 50% of the compounds). These metrics are used to assess and compare the scaffold diversity across different libraries. If each molecule in the database corresponds to a unique scaffold, the CSR curve will be diagonal, with an AUC of 0.5. The QUBO model

The workflow began by preprocessing the building block and reaction datasets, ensuring uniqueness and filtering for accepted reactions. A reaction graph is then constructed, with edge weights calculated based on molecular properties such as QED, NP-likeness, SS scores derived from SMILES strings. The QED score is calculated by RDKit. The NP-likeness score is calculated by the NP_score [43] (https://github.com/rdkit/rdkit/blob/master/Contrib/NP_Score). The SS score is calculated by first generating ECFP6 fingerprints (radius = 3, nBits = 2048) for the two molecules using RDKit and then evaluating their similarity via the Tanimoto coefficient. The QUBO model is formulated, incorporating constraints on node count, subgraph connectivity, and inclusion of top-weighted edges by the PyQUBO [44]. The QUBO model is solved using simulated annealing, and the resulting subgraph is validated to ensure compliance with the constraints. Post-processing involves extracting SMILES strings, calculating QED, NP-likeness, and SS scores, and comparing the results to molecules from the SANQI 1E6 dataset. The final output, including the selected subgraph and key metrics, is stored for further analysis.
