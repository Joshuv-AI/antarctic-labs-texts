Fish gut-water interface as a hotspot for the dissemination of antibiotic resistance genes across natural and aquaculture systems
Authors: Padma Dolkar, L. Themchuirin, Nongmaithem Sonia, Apoorva Atri, Pankaj Yadav, Sneha Siwach, Sonakshi Modeel, Ram Krishan Negi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Pristine river ecosystem hosts ESBL ARGs, like blaTEM and blaCTX-M.•The Yamuna River hosts multidrug, fluoroquinolone, fosfomycin, and β-lactam resistance genes.•Predominance of biofilm-forming bacteria and their co-occurrence with the ARGs.

Introduction

During the early 20th century, the advent of antibiotics marked a transformative milestone in modern medicine, providing an effective therapeutic intervention against bacterial pathogens that had previously resulted in high morbidity (Cook and Wright, 2022; Muteeb et al., 2023). This innovation substantially improved clinical outcomes, enabling both prophylaxis and treatment of infections once considered fatal. However, the irrational use of antibiotics has led to the worldwide dissemination of ARGs (Manyi-Loh et al., 2018; Salam et al., 2023). It is also predicted that by 2050, infections caused by antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) could eliminate around 10 million people per year, placing AMR among the top 10 global health concerns in 2024 (O’neill, 2014; Naghavi et al., 2024). Emphasising a combined approach to combat antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) by acknowledging the interdependent parts of environmental ecosystems, this "One Health" framework seeks to prevent cross-species spread of ARGs at the animal–environment–human interface (One Health Commission, 2018; McEwen et al., 2018). Further, the unmetabolized antibiotics accumulate in the aquatic ecosystem, resulting in selection pressure, dissemination, and the horizontal gene transfer (HGT) of ARGs to other organisms (Li et al., 2011; Huerta et al., 2013; Dolkar et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2024; Patra and Dubey, 2025). Horizontal gene transfer of ARGs is significantly enhanced by mobile genetic elements (MGEs), which accelerate the development and spread of AMR. The major MGEs involved in this process include plasmids, transposons, and Insertion Sequences (IS) elements (Tokuda and Shintani, 2024; Olsen and Riber, 2025). Furthermore, infections induced by resistant bacteria may be exacerbated by virulence factors (VFs) that increase bacterial pathogenicity (Beceiro et al., 2013; Qin et al., 2022). Intensive aquaculture sustains ARG reservoirs in fish gut microbiomes beyond antibiotic exposure and enriched clinically important plasmid-mediated resistance genes that facilitate HGT (Liu et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2024).

Large-scale catalogues of fish gut microbiomes for wild freshwater fish are emerging globally, particularly in East Asia through genome-resolved profiling frameworks (Yang and Wang, 2025; Tang et al., 2016), and in North American river catchments monitoring wastewater gradients (Rodriguez et al., 2025). Similarly, concentrations of different ARGs varying from 3.61 × 101 to 1.05 × 107 copies/mL were detected in rivers worldwide (Storteboom et al., 2010; Zainab et al., 2021). However, the spread of ARGs exhibits significant geographic variation, with Southeast Asia experiencing the highest prevalence, particularly due to lax regulation of antibiotic use (Amarasiri et al., 2020; Hanna et al., 2023; WHO, 2025). Numerous reports have documented the presence and dissemination of ARG in aquaculture, river waters, and sediments in Southeast countries (Nguyen et al., 2014; Hoa et al., 2011; Sharma et al., 2024; Thiang et al., 2021; Khurana et al., 2020; Dewi et al., 2022; Debnath et al., 2025; Banerjee et al., 2026). Regional studies have shown that anthropogenic pressure along the Yamuna affects not only the fish gut microbiota (Bharti et al., 2023) but also the sediments (Das et al., 2020) and water (Rahgav et al., 2024). Nevertheless, a substantial knowledge gap persists regarding their role as hotspots for the emergence and spread of AMR, mobilome interactions, and VFs, especially in the South Asian river system (EFSA Panel on Biological Hazards, 2021). Monitoring the incidence and mobilisation of virulence genes and ARGs is vital in assessing the risks they pose to community health (Zhang et al., 2022; Hossain and Chowdhury, 2024). Within a One Health framework, the Indus River (Ladakh, India) was selected as a relatively pristine system, and the Yamuna River as a highly polluted ecosystem (CPCB, 2018), both with the potential to act as reservoirs of ARGs (Mittal et al., 2019; Das et al., 2020). Given that aquaculture environments are recognised as a hotspot for the spread of ARGs, VF, and MGEs (Hemamalini et al., 2022; Deng et al., 2025), these sites were chosen to compare microbial community diversity and evaluate the roles of natural and managed aquatic ecosystems in the persistence and transmission of AMR. Further, to the best of our knowledge, economically and ecologically important indigenous wild species, particularly the vulnerable Himalayan snow trout Schizothorax plagiostomus and the indigenous fish of the Yamuna River, Labeo boggut, remain unrepresented in genome-resolved metagenomic repositories. Characterizing the gut resistomes and mobilomes of these native and farmed species across the Indus and Yamuna River systems provides the first comprehensive, culture-independent baseline of multi-class genomic resistance potential in these understudied host taxa. Despite gaining evidence, comprehensive shotgun metagenomic characterization of ARG resistomes in fish guts and river waters in this region remains scarce. This pioneering study integrates the fish gut and water metagenomics from these two rivers alongside aquaculture farms in India. By integrating samples from ecologically contrasting regions and employing high-resolution shotgun metagenomics with metagenomic-assembled genomes (MAGs), this research will comprehensively characterise microbial communities and elucidate the characteristics and mechanisms of ARGs, VFs, and MGEs in both the fish intestinal tract and aquatic ecosystems. Hence, present investigation aimed to identifying microbial diversity, resistome profiles, dominant ARG and VFs classes, and resistance mechanisms in both fish gut and water, to addresses the ecological processes underlying ARG transmission, including the role of plasmids, integrons, transposons, and bacterial taxa involved in ARG and VFs dissemination and to integrates comparative analyses, environmental drivers, and risk assessment, highlighting the broader significance of the findings.

Methods

About the sampling site

Originating from the Yamunotri glacier (31.01°N 78.45°E), the Yamuna River is a major tributary of the Ganges River system in India. It is regarded as a holy river, as it plays a significant cultural role in Indian mythology. It still provides water to almost 60 million people (Singhal et al., 2025). Human activities, however, have contaminated these rivers in recent times, polluting the ecosystem. The Yamuna has undergone significant ecological degradation due to continuous inputs of untreated urban wastewater, industrial effluents, harmful pesticides from the catchment areas, biomedical waste, and many other sources (CPCB, 2018).

The Indus River originates from an altitude of 5182 m from Lake Manasarovar (30°35′35″N 81°25′25″E), also known as Sindhu and Senge Tsangpo in the local dialect. It enters Demchok in Ladakh, formerly part of the erstwhile state of Jammu and Kashmir, India (Kumar and Srivastava, 2018; Namga and Nayak, 2024). Ladakh’s water resources, which are severely stressed due to its cold desert climate and the delicate ecosystem, are further endangered by tourism, population growth, labour migration, and the presence of armed forces (Dolma et al., 2020; Brian, 2021; Ali et al., 2024).

In 2022, the first trout fish farm was established in Ladakh to promote aquaculture and provide an alternative protein source and livelihood opportunities for communities residing in and around the region. In the present study, two trout fish farms in Chuchot, Leh, and Drass, Kargil, were selected for sampling fish and water.

Samples collection and preservation

Fish samples were collected from six geographically distinct sites, comprising Labeo boggut (Bata; LB; n = 70), an indigenous fish, and Cyprinus carpio (Chinese rohu; CR.; n = 25), an exotic fish, from Sur ghat and Sonia vihar, Yamuna River, Delhi, respectively; Schizothorax plagiostomus (Snow trout), an indigenous fish, from Shey (SF; n = 21), Indus River, Ladakh; and from Nimmo (NF; n = 33), Indus River, Ladakh; Oncorhynchus mykiss (Rainbow trout), an exotic fish and from Chuchot fish farm (CF; n = 20) and Kargil fish farm (KF; n = 25), Ladakh, respectively (Fig. S1; Table S1). Fish specimens were brought to the laboratory in dry ice and dissected in aseptic conditions. The GI tract of the individual fish specimens was dissected aseptically in the laminar flow, and the gut content was removed immediately and collected in a sterile microcentrifuge tube and kept at -80 °C until further use. Additionally, 5 litres of surface water samples were collected in a sterile container from four sampling locations: Sur ghat (DSgW) and Sonia vihar (DSvW) on the Yamuna River in Delhi; Shey (SW) on the Indus River in Ladakh; and Chuchot fish farm (CW) in Ladakh to represent the concentrated activity of the sampled fish and were brought to the laboratory. Water was then filtered using a vacuum pump via 0.22 μm Millipore membrane filter units.

DNA extraction

The fish gut contents and the filter paper were then subjected to DNA extraction using the PowerSoil® DNA Isolation Kit (MO BIO) according to the manual’s instructions, with slight modifications. The quality and quantity of genomic DNA were assessed using Nanodrop, and integrity was determined by visual inspection on 0.8% agarose gels. Genomic DNA was isolated from each of 194 individual fish specimens, and equal amounts of DNA from each specimen were pooled to ensure equal representation of all individuals within each fish species, resulting in six composite metagenomic samples based on sampling location. In addition, four water samples were also processed for the metagenomic analysis. It was then sent to BioNivid, Bangalore, India, for shotgun metagenomic sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) using 150-bp paired-end reads. Clean reads were received, and the parameters are mentioned in Table S2. The sequence data were then deposited in the NCBI Short Read Archive under the Bioproject accession no PRJNA1404649, and the BioSample IDs are listed in Table S2.

Metagenomic analysis

De novo assemblies were performed using Megahit on the clean reads (v1.2.9; https://www.l3-bioinfo.com/products/megahit.html; Li et al., 2015) with default settings. Prodigal was used to infer protein-coding genes from the clean-assembled contigs (v2.6.3; https://github.com/hyattpd/Prodigal; Hyatt et al., 2010), using sequences of at least 100 bp. Seqkit (v2.1.1; https://github.com/shenwei356/seqkit) was used to select contigs ≥ 300 bp for additional gene prediction (Shen et al., 2016). Nonredundant sequences were clustered using CD-HIT (v4.8.1; http://www.bioinformatics.org/cd-hit/; Fu et al., 2012).

Taxonomic annotation, ARGs identification, analysis of ARGs-MGEs, VFs

NCBI NR database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/refseq/about/nonredundantproteins; last update: July 2, 2024), The Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance (CARD) Database (v4.0.1; https://card.mcmaster.ca/; Alcock et al., 2023), Virulence Genes core database (http://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/; VFDB_set_A; last update: Sept 03, 2025; Liu et al., 2022), MGEs90 database (https://mobileogdb.flsi.cloud.vt.edu/; last update: April 09, 2025; Li et al., 2017) were used.

Nonredundant meaningful sequences were aligned to the NR database for taxonomic annotation, the CARD database, the VFDB core database, and the MGEs90 database for the identification of ARGs, VFs, and MGEs, respectively, using DIAMOND (v2.0.13; https://github.com/bbuchfink/diamond; BLASTP alignment option; Buchfink et al., 2021). Only genes with an E-value of 1e−10 and a 90% identification threshold were kept for analysis. The coverM (v0.7.0; Aroney et al., 2025) was used to quantify and normalise the abundance of ARGs, MGEs, and VF in Reads Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads (RPKM). The contigs carrying at least one ARG, MGE, or VF gene were identified as Antibiotic Resistance Contigs, Mobile Genetic Element Contigs, and Virulence Gene Contigs, respectively. The ARGs were further classified into intrinsic chromosomal efflux determinants and acquired resistance genes using CARD resistance mechanisms and AMR gene family annotations from Blair et al. (2015). The World Health Organisation (WHO) (https://apps.who.int/iris/bitstream/handle/10665/330420/9789240000193-eng.pdf) and a study by Zhang et al. (2021) were used to identify high-risk ARGs that pose a significant public health risk.

Metagenomic binning method

Three distinct tools, MaxBin2 (v2.2.7; Wu et al., 2016), MetaBAT2 (v2.17; Kang et al., 2019), and CONCOCT (v1.1.0; Alneberg et al., 2014), were used independently to perform genome binning of the non-redundant assembled contigs from individual samples (Uritskiy et al., 2018). The Benette (v1.2.0; Mainguy and Hoede, 2024) bin refinement and reassembly modules were used to extract the best draft metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). CheckM2 (v1.1.0) was used to evaluate draft MAGs for completeness (>50%) and contamination (<10%) (Parks et al., 2015).

Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) annotation and host identification

Using 120 bacterial marker genes, quality-checked MAGs were then categorised using the Genome Taxonomy Database toolkit (GTDB-Tk) (version 1.3.0; database release 226; accessed on August 12, 2025; Parks et al., 2018). ABRicate v1.0.0 (Seemann, n.d., https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) was used to screen MAGs for ARGs utilising a combination of the CARD (Alcock et al., 2023), NCBI AMR database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pathogens/antimicrobial-resistance/), Antibiotic Resistance Gene-ANNOTation (ARG-ANNOT; Gupta et al., 2014), and MEGARes 2.0 (Doster et al., 2020) database with default settings. Using the same tool, they were further run against the PLasmidFinder (doi:10.1128/AAC.02412–14; Carattoli et al., 2014) database and VFDB screen for the presence of plasmid replicons and VFs, respectively, with default parameters. In all cases, the E-value was 1e-10, and identities greater than 80% were used as a criterion for accepting significant hits during annotation.

To normalise the abundance of the annotated genes to transcripts per million reads (TPM), they were analysed with the tool Salmon (version 1.4.1; Patro et al., 2017) and subsequently used to calculate the relative abundance of ARGs, VFs, and the plasmid in MAGs (Seyler et al., 2021).

Statistical analysis and visualisation

All the statistical analyses were calculated in RStudio v.2025.09.0 (v4.5.1). The visualisation was performed using R Studio and Gephi (0/10.2).

Results

Taxonomic composition patterns in the fish gut and its surrounding habitat

We analysed ten metagenomic samples collected from two river systems, two aquaculture farms, and four fish species across six sampling sites to characterise the distribution of bacterial, archaeal, and viral communities using the NR database. It reveals a clear biological transition between the aquatic environment and fish gut microbiomes and demonstrates that the fish gut is a highly selective ecological niche, rather than just a reflection of water microbiota. Across all samples, the three dominant phyla, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, and Bacillota, make up over 90% of the total classified microbial community. However, their distribution is modulated by host-selective filtering and local environmental variables. Among the classified phyla, Pseudomonadota was predominant across all samples, with relative abundance ranging from 39.79 to 73.34% except in sample CF, where Bacillota dominated with 33.14% (Fig. 1A; Table S1). In the water samples, Bacteroidota was the highly abundant phylum, indicating typical freshwater microbial structures and organic nutrient inputs. For instance, Bacteriodota shows the highest abundance in DSvW (34.96%), followed by CW (17.19%) and DSgW (7.07%), indicating sewage and faecal contamination. Additionally, the viral phylum Uroviricota and Nucleocytoviricota were detected among the top 15 most dominant phyla. Uroviricota with a particularly high relative abundance in sample LB (3.29%), possibly suggesting bacteriophage-mediated predation. Archaea taxa and other viral phyla were present at comparatively low abundance and did not appear within the top 15 phyla; however, they were retained for further analysis. Sample-wise, the highest overall taxonomic abundance was observed in NF, followed by SF and LB.

Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in the fish gut and its surrounding habitat

To check whether host-mediated selection directly influences the resistomes, we further analyse variation in antibiotic resistance genes across samples. We identified 1108 ARG subtypes conferring resistance to 14 distinct antibiotic classes, based on CARD database analyses with their composition varying across samples along spatial gradients and in association with local anthropogenic selective pressures (Table S3). The structural diversity of the resistome is dominated by β-lactam resistance genes, which comprise 837 ARG subtypes. The second most diverse group was multidrug-resistant, with 74 genes, followed by 69 fluoroquinolone-resistance genes and 48 aminoglycoside-resistance genes. These patterns are further reflected in the relative abundance of ARG classes across the sample groups (Fig. 1B). β-lactam showed the greatest relative abundance in CW (97.34%), followed by DSgW (73.54%) and LB (72.34%). In contrast, multidrug resistance was mostly present in SW (95.42%), CF (89.27%), and CR (55.26%). The distribution of resistance mechanisms was predominantly driven by antibiotic efflux and antibiotic inactivation mechanisms, with the highest abundance in LB (Fig. 1C). In terms of gene-level diversity (Fig. 1D), NF exhibits the highest diversity of ARG subtypes (494), followed by SF (285) and LB (274). Conversely, CF shows the lowest number, with only two uniquely ARG subtypes present in this sample. However, the absolute abundance of resistance genes revealed the highest ARG concentration in LB, followed by NF, SF and KF (Fig. 1E). These findings highlight substantial ARG diversity and complex distribution patterns across diverse environments. Despite their geographical proximity, CR harboured a markedly lower ARG load compared to LB, suggesting site-specific or host-associated influences on ARG prevalence. Comparatively, water samples from the river Yamuna (DSgW and DSvW) exhibited higher ARG concentrations than the other water samples. To further assess ARG abundance and compositional variation across sites, principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was performed. It demonstrated clear clustering of ARG profiles in all samples, even though the overall dissimilarities were small (Fig. 1F). The Yamuna River samples formed a central group that partly overlapped with fish samples from the Indus River. This suggests that these river systems shared some resistome characteristics. The total variance accounting for PC1 and PC2 was 40.3%, and PERMANOVA yielded R2 = 0.221 (p = 0.397). Although the permutation-based p-value did not reach the usual significance level, likely due to the small sample size (n = 10 spatial composites), the R2 value shows that >22% of the variation in the resistome and microbiota is due to geographic location. The highest abundance and variation of MDR determinants were observed in the NF sample, followed by the SF and LB samples, with an overall 74 distinct ARG subtypes (Fig. 2A). Around 952 of the acquired ARGs and 156 of the intrinsic ARGs were distinguished from the overall 1108 ARGs, revealing direct clinical risk posed by environmental resistance pools (Fig. S2). Most of the multidrug resistance genes detected were intrinsic chromosomal efflux systems, representing members of the constitutive resistome of the Gram-negative gut microbiome. Although efflux pumps are ubiquitous in bacteria, their overexpression, often driven by genetic mutations, can significantly contribute to the emergence of multidrug resistance (MDR). Only oqxA, oqxB, qacE, qacdelta1 and qacL were confirmed as acquired mobile resistance determinants based on their plasmid/integron-borne genetic context and were comparatively more dominant in Yamuna water samples. A major concern is the high prevalence of acquired ARGs, especially first-generation cephalosporins and extended-spectrum β-lactamses (ESBLs). The ESBL genes blaTEM and blaCTX-M were detected in the Yamuna samples (CR and DSvW) and the Indus fish gut samples (NF and SF) (Table S3). This indicates active horizontal gene transfer and widespread environmental contamination. Along with it, the spatial distribution of carbapenem-resistance gene variants was also classified. Several clinically relevant ARGs were identified among these MDR determinants, including emrB, mdtE, TolC, mdtA, mdtG, and mdtM (Fig. 2B). The emrB gene was widely distributed in all fish samples except CF, and in the DSvW and SW, while mdtE was detected in LB, NF, and SF. In addition, acquired aminoglycoside resistance genes, such as APH(3″)-Ib, were identified in LB, DSvW, DSgW, and NF samples, while APH(6)-Id was identified in DSgW, DSvW, and NF samples. The broad distribution of plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance genes across the study sites indicates persistence of anthropogenic antibiotic pressure, facilitating the maintenance and dissemination of quinolone resistance determinants. The qnrB was detected in the Indus River snow trout (NF and SF), while the qnrS family was present in the Yamuna River (CR, LB, and DSvW), and QnrVC families in DSvW, suggesting both river basins act as the reservoir for the quinolone resistance genes. The samples from the Yamuna River were enriched in plasmids and integrons-associated ARGs, including cmlA variants, tet(A/B/C/G), and sul1, then other locations, reflecting strong anthropogenic selection pressure and an elevated potential for horizontal dissemination of antimicrobial resistance.

The abundance of ARGs in host bacteria

For the identification of potential taxonomic carriers of ARG classes, pairwise non-parametric Spearman rank correlation coefficients were conducted, and robust associations satisfying the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (BH-FDR < 0.05) and an absolute correlation coefficient |ρ| ≥ 0.4 (Fig. S3). A significant correlation was observed between the phylum Abditibacteriota, obligate endosymbiotic bacteria and bicyclomycin-like antibiotics, indicating that this phylum may function as an intrinsic reservoir for bicyclomycin-like antibiotics in aquatic and aquaculture environments. Pseudomonadota showed a positive correlation with β-lactam resistance genes (FDR < 0.05). As Pseudomonadota includes dominant aquatic Gram-negative pathogens, this correlation highlights their potential to resist specialized transcription-inhibiting antibiotics. All other phylum-antibiotic resistance gene pairs were non-significant after BH correction, indicating that most ARGs are broadly distributed across multiple phyla rather than confined to a single taxonomic group. To further validate these correlations, a co-occurrence analysis was performed between the most abundant bacterial taxa and the ARGs class to assess variations across sampling sites (Fig. 2C). Enterobacter cloacae was detected in all samples and predominantly harboured ARGs associated with β-lactam and multidrug resistance, with comparatively lower representation of other resistance classes except CF and CW. Enterobacter asburiae emerged as the second most abundant ARG-associated species, followed by Escherichia coli, which also carried a wide spectrum of ARGs. In addition, several clinically relevant pathogenic bacteria, including Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterococcus faecalis, were detected at varying abundance across different sampling sites and were associated with multiple ARGs, as illustrated in the heatmap. Among the 50 most abundant bacterial species, all belong to the phylum Pseudomonodota, except for Brumimicrobium oceani, which is a member of the phylum Bacteroidota. Notably, B. oceani exhibited high abundance in the samples SF, LB, and CR, and with the highest relative abundance observed in sample NF, indicating localized ecological conditions favouring Bacteroidata, emphasising spatial variation in microbial community structure that may influence resistome composition and ARG persistence.

Mobile genetic elements (MGEs) in the fish gut and its surrounding habitat

The ecological persistence and environmental transmission of ARGs are closely associated with mobile genetic elements (MGEs). MGEs’ profiles across samples reveal distinct stratification (Fig. 3A). Phage-associated MGEs (21.09–41.58%) and integration/excision (17.88–61.50%) represent the dominant functional categories across all samples. An assessment of host-associated fish gut samples exhibits a higher absolute abundance of MGEs than water samples. The predominance of MGE was highest in the LB of the Yamuna sample, followed by NF and SF (Fig. 3B). This indicates enhanced ARG transmission within the fish gut microbiome, characterised by microbial biofilms, high cellular contact rates, and continuous nutrient flow. This is further supported by the contig-level co-occurrence analysis, which demonstrates that the presence of ARGs and MGEs on a single assembled contig is higher in the fish gut samples. Virulence factor (VF) in the fish gut and its surrounding habitat

Zoonotic pathogens pose a substantial danger to both human and animal health, and their pathogenic potential is often enhanced by the virulence factors (VFs) they harbour. In the present investigation, we identified a total of 125 unique Type 2 VFs elements, which were further classified into 13 distinct functional categories, displaying marked spatial heterogeneity (Fig. 4A). Adherence-related VFs were predominant in DSgW (46.40%), whereas motility-associated functions were abundant in CR (33.08%) and in water samples DSvW (38.05%), CW (35.77%), and SW (28.00%). Regulatory VFs were dominant in LB (27.44%), while nutritional/metabolic factors were highly enriched in CF (89.21%) and NF (28.42%). Immune modulation-related VFs were most prevalent in fish sample KF (35.06%), whereas effector delivery systems in fish sample SF (23.70%) (Fig. 4B). At the gene level, the highest diversity and comparatively more exclusive VFs were detected from the Indus river, with 10 VF elements in NF (fish) and 9 VF elements in SW (water) samples (Fig. 4C). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) of the VF profile explained a 57.2% of the total variance (PC1 and PC2) (Fig. 4D). It revealed a cluster consisting of samples SW and CW, as well as most fish gut samples (KF, SF, NF, and LB), forming another cluster, indicating shared virulence-associated microbial communities. The abundance of VF in the bacterial host

Spearman rank correlation between VF functional category abundance and phylum level relative abundance identified nine statistically significant associations (|ρ| ≥ 0.4, BH-FDR < 0.05) (Fig. S5). Among the viral taxa, strong correlations were observed for phyla Uroviricota, particularly with virulence functions related to antimicrobial activity/competitive advantage, regulation, and effector delivery systems and Kitrinoviricota with other types of virulence, suggesting that phage-associated genetic elements may contribute to virulence gene dissemination and regulation in these fish gut communities. At the bacterial phylum level, Abditibacteriota showed a strong association with exoenzyme-related virulence factors specialized in extracellular tissue damaging enzymes and Minisyncoccota with others, suggesting co-abundance relationships between these virulence categories and their putative microbial and viral carriers. Pseudomonadota was strongly correlated with immune modulation, consistent with the well-characterised repertoire of immune evasion mechanisms in Pseudomonadota members. Likewise, Deinococcota shows a negative correlation with adherence and effector delivery systems. These findings suggest that these phyla may either harbour organisms encoding such virulence factors or co-occur with taxa carrying these functional traits. Alternatively, they may act as potential reservoirs or specific virulence functions within microbial communities. To further validate these associations and assess variation across samples, we also analysed the co-occurrence of microbial species and virulence factors on the same contigs.

At the genus level, the Indus River suggests that Escherichia, Enterobacter, and Pseudomonas were predominant genera harbouring VFs in fish samples (SF and NF) and water (SW), respectively (Fig. 5A). Samples from the Yamuna River exhibited a broader distribution of VFs across multiple genera, with varying levels of abundance among all four sample types. Similarly, fish farm samples exhibited considerable variability in both the diversity and relative abundance of VF-associated bacterial genera. Considering the patterns observed for ARGs, fish samples exhibited a higher abundance of VFs than corresponding water samples. Among the water samples, those from the Yamuna River showed comparatively higher VF abundance (Fig. 5B). The strong taxonomic and spatial correlation between virulence and resistance suggests coordinated ecological selection. Pearson correlation analysis reveals a highly unified virulome profile between the CR and DSvW (0.89) and SF and NF (0.97), highlighting how environmental connectivity drives similar virulence profiles (Fig. 5C). At the species level, a total of 166 bacterial species were associated with VFs, predominantly related to adherence, effective delivery systems, and nutritional/metabolic factors, along with other VFs level 2 categories (Fig. 5D). The most prominent VFs harbouring taxa included E. coli, particularly in the SF and NF, Aeromonas allosaccharophila in DSvW, CR and LB, Enterobacter cloacae in SF and NF and Enterobacter asburiae in NF, SF, and LB. Pathogenic Escherichia spp. (E. albertii and E. coli) together with multiple Enterobacter spp. (e.g., E. asburiae, E. cloacae, E. hormaechei, E. cancerogenus, E. bugandensis) were consistently detected across sampling sites. This widespread occurrence indicates that opportunistic members of the order Enterobacterales constitute a major environmental reservoir of combined virulence and antimicrobial resistance traits in these aquatic ecosystems. The genus Aeromonas, such as A. caviae, A. dhakensis, A. hydrophila, and A. veronii and several Klebsiella, like K. pneumoniae, K. oxytoca, and K. grimontii, were highly abundant and contributed substantially to the VF pool. Representative Pseudomonas species, including P. aeruginosa and other environmental Pseudomonads, were prevalent in water samples, consistent with well-documented environmental ubiquity and diverse virulence repertoires. MAGs: ARG, VFs and MGEs carrying host

Using the metagenomic binning approach, we recovered 52 MAGs with completeness >50% and contamination <10%. To assess the spatial distribution of ARGs, VFs, and plasmids, these MAGs were further analysed across sites. Nineteen MAGs carry at least one ARG, VF or plasmid, providing direct taxonomic linkages. Of these 19 MAGs, 18 harbour ARGs, 8 contained VFs, and 4 possessed plasmids (Fig. 6A; Table S6). A total of 147 ARGs were detected, out of which 94 were acquired ARGs, and the remaining 53 were intrinsic chromosomally linked ARGs (Fig. 6B). Taxonomic classification assigned one MAG to the family level, two to the genus level, and the remaining MAGs to the species level, spanning three phyla: Pseudomonadota, Bacillota, and Bacteroidota (Fig. 6C). Along with that, four MAGs were constructed from the gut microbiome of snow trout (SF) collected from the Indus River. The E. coli (Bin_1083) encoded the most extensive complement of adaptive traits, a genome harbouring 32 ARG subtypes, 25 VFs, and one p0111_1 plasmid replicon (Fig. 6D; 6E). This single genome carries a complex resistome dominated by 16 Resistance-nodulation-cell Division (RND) efflux pump repressors and seven Major Facilitator Superfamily (MFS) transporters, along with an aminoglycoside-resistance gene, 3 β-lactamases, a bicyclomycin-resistance gene, an MLS, and a peptide-resistance gene. The virulence-associated genes included those involved in adhesion and nutritional/metabolic processes, particularly iron acquisition. The presence of yagW/ecpD, yagV/ecpE, yagY/ecpB, yagX/ecpC, yagZ/ecpA, and ykgK/ecpR confirms the occurrence of ecp-encoded fimbrial associated with bacterial adhesion and colonisation. Similarly, the opportunistic pathogen, Klebsiella grimontii (bin_2251), also recovered from SF, contains β-lactamases, two peptide antibiotics, and 15 multidrug efflux pumps, including clinically relevant ARGs such as emrD and bacA. Furthermore, it also shows the presence of adherence factor yagZ/ecpA, along with two nutritional/metabolic factors (entA and entB) involved in iron acquisition. Another genome, Enterococcus hirae (bin_870), recovered from the SF fish gut microbiome, carried two plasmid replication (rep) groups: repUS15_2_repA(pNB2354p1) and rep11a_1_repA(pb82), indicating the co-existence of distinct plasmids within the same isolate. It also harboured the aminoglycoside resistance gene AAC(6′)-Iid. Another multidrug-resistance organism identified was Citrobacter portucalensis (bin_993), which harboured 14 ARGs, 10 MDR genes, two β-lactam resistance genes, a fosfomycin resistance gene, and a peptide antibiotic resistance gene and VFs associated with bacterial adherence (csgG). Whereas, two MAGs were recovered: E. coli (bin_3070) and Cronobacter turicensis (bin_1962), from the NF sample. The E. coli contained 23 ARG subtypes and 13 VFs, indicating substantial resistance and pathogenic potential carrying 12 RND family efflux, along with four MFS transporters and one ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter. Furthermore, β-lactam (PBP-2 and Ecol_ampC), Peptide (baeS, eptA and yojl) and MLS antibiotics (mphB) were also identified, contributing to multiple-drug resistance. Several virulence factors related to the effector immune system, nutritional/metabolic factors, particularly iron acquisition, and effector-like protein-encoding genes (esp) were also observed. The C. turicensis MAG harboured ARGs conferring resistance to β-lactam and fosfomycin, along with five MDR and the incompatibility (Inc) groups IncFIB(pCTU1)1_pCTU1 plasmid replicon was detected.

We also identified four MAGs from site CW, including Acinetobacter bohemicus (bin_11884), Pandoraea sputorum (bin_12859), g_Sphaerotilus (bin_13204) and Brucella pseudogrignonensis (bin_178) with only one or two ARGs per genome. The co-localization of qacG and an IncP1_2 plasmid replicon on the Sphaerotilus sp. (bin_13204) contigs identified it as a high-risk MGE with elevated HGT potential. Whereas, Brucella pseudogrignonensis and Pandoraea sputorum additionally encoded VF associated with immune modulation function (acpXL and pgm) and chemotaxis (cheW), respectively.

Additionally, Enterobacter cloacae (bin_9), an opportunistic fish-associated pathogen, was recovered from the gut of rainbow trout (KF). This genome encoded a diverse resistome carrying 26 ARGs, including four β-lactam genes, peptides, bicyclomycin, fosfomycin, and 18 multidrug efflux pumps together with the adherence-associated virulence genes csgG and ompA. In addition, two VF genes (csgG and ompA) associated with adherence were also detected. The co-occurrence of extensive resistance and adherence traits suggests a high adaptive potential for persistence and colonization within the fish gut.

Four MAGs were recovered from the Sur ghat water sample (DSgW), the Yamuna River, all belonging to the phylum Pseudomonadota, such as f_Burkholderiaceae (bin_21282), Acinetobacter sp003987695 (bin_22424), Acinetobacter junii (bin_12282) and Malikia (bin_11306). Each of these genomes harboured a single ARG associated with RND efflux pump multidrug resistance (MDR), except Malikia. The genus Malikia lacked detectable ARGs but encoded an effector delivery system along with two motility-associated VF genes, suggesting adaptation for environmental persistence and host interactions. Besides these, additionally, three MAGs were recovered from the Sonia vihar water sample (DSvW), Yamuna River, corresponding to Aeromonas salmonicida (bin_4291), g_Methylotenera (bin_4294) and Flavobacterium sp002422095 (bin_5171). In Flavobacterium sp002422095, two ARGs were encoded, comprising an MFS efflux pump-associated MDR gene and an MLS resistance gene. The genome affiliated with g__Methylotenera contained a β-lactamases gene and a fluoroquinolone resistance gene. Notably, Aeromonas salmonicida harboured a most diverse resistome encoding β-lactamases genes (IMI-3), fluoroquinolone (QnrVC1), SMR efflux pump MDR gene (qacK) and two MLS resistance genes (mphE and mphG), indicating a multidrug resistance genotype. From the fish gut microbiome of Labeo boggut (LB), we recovered Aeromonas veronii_A (bin_33), which harboured two β-lactam resistance genes (CEPH-A3 and OXA-12) along with one peptide resistance gene (ampS_1), further highlighting this species as an important reservoir of antimicrobial resistance within the fish gut microbiome.

Discussion

Global reports have highlighted rivers and aquaculture systems as important environmental reservoirs for the emergence and dissemination of clinically relevant resistance determinants (Jofré Bartholin et al., 2023). This highlights the urgent need to improve water quality and sanitation to limit AMR dissemination in line with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 6 (2015). A specific target of this goal is to substantially reduce hazardous waste inputs into aquatic ecosystems by 2030 to improve water quality (Alcamo, 2019). Despite this, antibiotic usage patterns and the occurrence, distribution and dissemination of ARGs in South Asian rivers and aquaculture systems remain adequately monitored and insufficiently characterised. Additionally, substantial gaps remain in our understanding of how these genetic determinants are structured, maintained, and propagated within the aquatic host-associated microbiome, particularly across heterogeneous environmental contexts. This integrative approach enabled a thorough assessment of taxonomic diversity and distribution of ARGs, VFs and MGEs within host-associated and environmental microbiomes. Furthermore, reconstruction of MAGs provided high-resolution insights into microbial population structure and functional potential across diverse geographical regions.

Pristine rivers have more diverse microbial communities

In agreement with previous reports, the aquatic microbiome in the present study was dominated by the phyla Pseudomonadota, Bacillota, and Bacteroidota, reflecting typical freshwater microbial structures and organic nutrient inputs (Krotman et al., 2020; Sylvain et al., 2020; Uren Webster et al., 2020). These phyla have consistently been shown to constitute up to 90% of bacterial communities in aquatic and fish-associated environments (Ghanbari et al., 2015; Minich et al., 2020). Notably, Bacillota emerged as the predominant phylum in the CF gut sample collected from the fish farm. Such divergence between gut and environmental microbial communities is commonly observed in managed aquaculture systems. It is largely driven by a high protein diet and intensive farming practices, which likely contribute to a specialized gut microbiome, in contrast to the surrounding water microbiome (Marmen et al., 2021; Sadeghi et al., 2023). The higher relative abundance of Bacteroidota in water samples is consistent with the previous studies suggesting that members of this phylum are an indicator of sewage and faecal contamination, likely reflecting the influence of untreated municipal wastewater discharges into the aquatic environment (Rajeev et al., 2021; Bharti et al., 2023; Xian et al., 2024). Predominance of Bacillota and Bacteroidota in the fish gut likely reflects their ecological and functional importance within the intestinal microbiome and these phyla facilitating the breakdown of carbohydrate metabolism, fatty acid absorption, fermentation, oligosaccharide degradation, immune system maturation and gut barrier integrity suggests that they play a pivotal role in preserving intestinal homeostasis and overall host health (Shin et al., 2024; Kanika et al., 2025). In contrast, the higher abundance of Pseudomonadota observed in the present study is consistent with their well-documented roles in the mineralisation of organic compounds and nutrient cycling in aquatic environments, thereby supporting fish growth and overall aquatic health (Kirchman et al., 2002). These taxonomic patterns showed that while water quality parameters may shape the transient microbial communities in the water column, host-specific physiological pressures and dietary requirements are the primary drivers of the host gut microbiota (Talwar et al., 2018; Kanika et al., 2025).

Polluted river fish contain more clinically relevant ARGs abundance

The widespread occurrence of antimicrobial resistance genes, mobile genetic and virulent factors across the studied aquatic ecosystems highlights that the fish-gut-water interface acts as an environmental reservoir for antibiotic resistance. The spatial variation in resistome composition suggests that local environmental conditions and anthropogenic pressures play a significant role in the distribution and persistence of resistance determinants. The relative abundance of multidrug, fluoroquinolone, fosfomycin, and rifamycin resistance genes, as well as β-lactam resistance genes in fish collected from the Yamuna River is consistent with severe anthropogenic pollution reported for this river (Parida et al., 2022; Mittal et al., 2019). Similarly, Das et al. (2020) reported widespread occurrence of β-lactam, aminoglycoside, macrolide, and tetracycline resistance genes from the Yamuna River sediments. These observations indicate that the Yamuna River represents an environmental hotspot for antimicrobial resistance. Furthermore, the enrichment of β-lactam and multidrug resistance in fish from Ladakh (SF, NF, and KF), many of which are considered intrinsic chromosomal ARGs, suggests that these resistance determinants are maintained within native environmental microbiota. These intrinsic ARGs are a natural component of the bacterial genome and may provide ecological advantages beyond antibiotic resistance. Nevertheless, their persistence and enrichment can also be influenced by anthropogenic activities such as agricultural practices and domestic wastewater inputs containing antibiotic residues into the aquatic environments (Lupo et al., 2018; Ruppé et al., 2015; Stachurová et al., 2021; Laborda et al., 2022). The dissemination of intrinsic resistance determinants within environmental bacterial communities is possible even in relatively less urbanized ecosystems (Stachurová et al., 2021; Laborda et al., 2022). The prevalence of multidrug resistance genes suggests that broad-spectrum resistance mechanisms constitute a major adaptive strategy within aquatic microbial communities exposed to diverse environmental stressors. The widespread occurrence of β-lactam resistance genes is also noteworthy, given the extensive use of β-lactam antibiotics in both clinical and veterinary medicines (Jung et al., 2023).

The detection of ESBL genes, such as blaTEM and blaCTX-M, in samples CR and DSgW from Yamuna and NF and SF from the Indus, together with widespread β-lactam resistance, highlights the role of aquatic ecosystems as a reservoir of clinically important antimicrobial resistance. These genes are often associated with mobile genetic elements that may facilitate horizontal gene transfer among environmental and pathogenic bacteria. Comparable results have been reported from the Yamuna River and other aquatic environments worldwide (Said et al., 2015; Caltagirone et al., 2017; Mittal et al., 2019; Despotovic et al., 2023; Osawa et al., 2026), highlighting the global dissemination of ESBL-mediated resistance in freshwater ecosystems. However, the detection of ESBL genes in the relatively pristine Indus River is a matter of concern that antimicrobial resistance can disseminate even in an aquatic ecosystem with limited anthropogenic pressure. These findings are in consistent with reports from the Tibetan Plateau and glacier ecosystems, where resistance genes have been detected despite low human population density (Sagawa et al., 2013; Sherpa et al., 2020; Mao et al., 2023; Gou et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025) and suggest that long-range transport through glacier meltwater, atmospheric deposition, and increasing tourism may facilitate the dissemination of ARGs into remote environments. Tsering et al. (2021) reported microplastic contamination in the same river despite minimal human activity. These microplastics can act as vectors for microbial colonization and horizontal transfer of ARGs, facilitating their long-range transport and persistence in aquatic environments, thereby posing significant ecological and public health risks (Liu et al., 2021; Ni et al., 2024). These findings collectively indicate that geographical isolation does not preclude the presence of clinically relevant antimicrobial resistance determinants. Additionally, the widespread occurrence of clinically relevant ARGs across all sampling sites highlights their extensive dissemination and persistence in the environment (Chen et al., 2021; Cui et al., 2022; Sati et al., 2025). Previous studies have reported sulfamethoxazole residues in the Yamuna River, supporting the environmental selection and persistence of sul1 (Akhter et al., 2023). Similarly, enrichment of Sul1 and Sul2 has been reported in the Haihe River, China (Luo et al., 2010), indicating that sulphonamide resistance is widespread in the environment. Sewage, faecal waste, agricultural runoff, and contaminated water bodies act as reservoirs of clinically relevant ARGs (Das et al., 2020). Hence, integrated surveillance under the "One-Health" framework is essential to monitoring and mitigating their ARGs environmental dissemination (Sassi et al., 2025).

In the present taxonomic analysis, greater diversity of ARG-associated taxa in the Yamuna River reflects the cumulative influence of heterogeneous urban landscapes, diverse pollution inputs, and complex environmental interactions, which create favourable conditions for the resistance determinants. Comparable patterns have been reported in similar environmental conditions, such as in the urban rivers of Japan (Sabar et al., 2023) and urban cats in China (Avellan-Llaguno et al., 2025). Based on taxonomic attribution of ARGs identified, Gammaproteobacteria, particularly Enterobacteriaceae, are a major reservoir of ARGs consistent with their recognized role in their acquisition and dissemination (Blair et al., 2015). The prevalence of E. cloacae carrying multidrug resistance determinants, including mdtC, mdtB, marA, bacA, blaCMH-X, and blaCTX-M, suggesting its role as an environmental reservoir of clinically relevant ARGs (Dong et al., 2022; Dwivedi et al., 2023). Similarly, widespread detection of E. coli harbouring ESBL variants and efflux-associated genes emrB and mdtA highlights the contribution of EmrAB-TolC and MdtABC-TolC systems to multidrug resistance and persistence in aquatic environments (Sivaraman et al., 2020). The enrichment of Escherichia, Citrobacter, and Pseudomonas suggests anthropogenic selection for bacteria carrying multidrug resistance determinants, which are associated with efflux pumps and horizontal gene transfer, highlighting their role in the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance in the aquatic environment (Puértolas-Balint et al., 2020; Okada and Murakami, 2022; Avatsingh et al., 2023).

MGE-associated signatures indicate multiple mobilisation pathways facilitating composite resistance–virulence assemblies (Talat et al., 2025). The co-occurrence of ARGs and MGEs on biofilm-forming taxa suggests that biofilm acts as a reservoir for sustained gene exchange, promoting the persistence and spread of antimicrobial resistance (Uruén et al., 2020; Rajangam and Narasimhan, 2024). MGE-associated signatures underscore the central role of horizontal gene transfer in the dissemination of resistance determinants and the formation of composite resistance–virulence assemblies (Talat et al., 2025). Predominance of ARG-MGEs associated with freshwater gut microbiome indicates enhanced horizontal gene transfer driven by diverse microbial communities and anthropogenic inputs in natural riverine systems. Additionally, hydrological connectivity may further accelerate the spread of resistance genes across the riverine ecosystem (Han et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2024).

Host gut microbiome possesses more virulent factors than its surrounding environment

The distribution of virulence-associated functions underlines the role of habitat-specific selective pressure in shaping the bacterial pathogenic potential. Fish-associated environments, interaction with the immune system and resident microbiota are expected to favour traits that enhance colonization and persistence, whereas environmental conditions are more likely to select for functions that promote survival and resource acquisition outside the host (Diwan et al., 2023; Suyamud et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). These outcomes support the view that virulence is an adaptive ecological trait influenced by host–microbe interactions and environmental selection, highlighting aquatic ecosystems as important reservoirs for the evolution and dissemination of bacterial virulence. Clinical studies have consistently strong association with VFs and microbial pathogenicity (Bajaj et al., 2021). The variation in VF composition indicates that virulence is shaped by local environmental and host-associated selective pressures rather than representing a uniform pathogenic repertoire. Such adaptive differences may enhance bacterial persistence, facilitate transmission and ultimately influence disease dynamics within aquatic ecosystems (Bhuiyan, 2025; Yang and Wang, 2025). The prevalence of capsule-associated virulence factors suggests that host-associated bacterial communities are primarily adapted for colonization and persistence in the fish gut. Capsules (VF0560) facilitate adhesion, biofilm formation, immune evasion and protection against phagocytosis (Pugazhendhi et al., 2022). Similarly, the enrichment of colonisation and invasion-related determinants, including nueB (VF0473), indicates a capacity for surface attachment, tissue colonisation and host-interaction. Such traits are characteristic of both environmental Gammaproteobacteria and pathogenic Aeromonas species, reflecting the selective pressures imposed by the gut environment that favour bacterial persistence and host adaptation (Ardissone et al., 2020; He et al., 2025). The predominance of enterobactin underlines the importance of iron acquisition for bacterial survival in the fish gut, indicating that iron availability is a key selective force shaping gut microbial communities (Amiri et al., 2025).

The frequent occurrence of the AcrAB efflux pump (VF0568) and type VI secretion system (T6SS; VF0480) indicates that microbial communities are shaped by ecological adaptation. The efflux pump AcrAB enhances tolerance to environmental stress and antimicrobial compounds, while T6SS promotes interbacterial competition and persistence through effector delivery (Cherrak et al., 2019; Yin et al., 2024). The presence of virulence factors in aquatic environments may facilitate the environmental survival and dissemination of opportunistic pathogens, potentially increasing human exposure through water, soil, and other environmental contaminants

Importantly, virulence determinants frequently co-occurred with antimicrobial resistance genes, particularly within Enterobacteriaceae, suggesting the co-selection of pathogenicity and resistance traits (Jackson et al., 2013; Madigan et al., 2015; Guilhen et al., 2019). The convergence of colonization, immune evasion, nutrient acquisition, and resistance-associated functions indicates that a subset of aquatic bacteria possesses enhanced ecological fitness and the potential for dissemination. These taxa may act as environmental reservoirs facilitating the dissemination of both virulence and antimicrobial resistance determinants across aquatic ecosystems.

Predominance of opportunistic pathogenic MAG in fish microbiome

Metagenomic assembly genome (MAG) improves the accuracy of ARG detection by reconstructing longer genome contigs, enabling more reliable assignment of ARGs, virulence factors, plasmid replicon, and their bacterial hosts (Yang et al., 2024). However, gene-level ARGs prediction from short-read metagenomes may be affected by non-specific read mapping. A recovery of MAG from most samples indicates sufficient genome resolution for downstream analyses. Predominance of multidrug, β-lactamase, and peptide resistance genes reflects strong antibiotic selection pressure in aquatic systems. The association with opportunistic bacteria highlights the potential public health risk in the aquatic ecosystem. Comparable with previous studies, the dominant role of Pseudomonadota as an ARG carrier reinforces its importance as a key reservoir and disseminator of antimicrobial resistance through its high genomic plasticity and capacity for horizontal gene transfer (Jimenez-Lopez et al., 2024). The recurrent co-occurrence of ARGs with virulence-associated traits, including adhesins, iron-acquisition systems, and effector-like genes, suggests that resistant bacteria may possess enhanced capacity for host colonisation and persistence (Silvester et al., 2025). However, detection of plasmid replicons in four MAGs further supports horizontal gene transfer as a mechanism facilitating ARG dissemination (Wang et al., 2025). In the present study, ARG plasmid co-localisation was confirmed in one MAG, most likely due to the limited resolution of short-read genome assemblies. While detection of ARGs alone does not directly indicate a human health risk (Abdulkadir et al., 2024), the occurrence of MGEs and VFs in pathogenic taxa highlights the capacity of the aquatic microbiome to act as reservoirs for the emergence and spread of clinically relevant antimicrobial resistance (Rizzo et al., 2013; Mulani et al., 2019; Peng et al., 2021; Maboni et al., 2022).

The genome-resolved data collectively confirm that the diverse microbial community of the Indus River acts as a highly integrated conduit, where clinically important pathogenicity factors and multi-class resistance are structurally consolidated within intact, viable bacterial lineages. This pattern likely reflects host-associated selection, local anthropogenic pressures and variability in sequencing depth and MAG recovery. Importantly, many ARG-carrying taxa are opportunistic pathogens and, such commensal and facultative anaerobic bacteria can function as reservoirs that facilitate HGT to clinically relevant bacteria, extending the environmental dimension of AMR beyond clinical settings (Yang et al., 2025).

Environmental implications and conclusion

The co-occurrence of mobilizable ARGs, MGEs and VFs across riverine and aquaculture systems identifies these environments as key reservoirs for the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance, underscoring the need for integrated One-Health surveillance under India’s National Action Plan 2.0. Our results indicate a striking occurrence of diverse acquired ARGs, particularly in the LB and water samples from the Yamuna River. It suggests that the microbial populations from these locations might act as reservoirs of ARGs and potentially disseminate them throughout the environment, compared to the Indus River samples, where most of the ARGs were intrinsic in nature. Furthermore, distinct MGEs and VFs in the samples, particularly in fish NF, CR and SF, indicates the potential role of microbial microcosms in the well-being of aquatic ecosystems, particularly in Enterobacteriaceae and biofilm-forming microbial taxa. It underscores the zoonotic risk and heightens public health concern posed by these aquatic ecosystems. The clustering of ARGs, plasmid replicons, and virulence determinants within a limited set of MAGs highlights priority targets for risk assessment. Mitigation will require high-resolution genomic surveillance, targeted monitoring of antibiotic residues and co-selecting pollutants, and management interventions that reduce sewage inputs, nutrient loading, and inappropriate antibiotic use to limit horizontal gene transfer.
