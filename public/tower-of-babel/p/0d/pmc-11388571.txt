Projecting phytochemical bacoside A anti-mucorale agent: An in-silico and in-vitro assessment
Authors: Komal Tilwani, Drashti Patel, Prachi Soni, Shyam Wadhwani, Gayatri Dave
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMucormycosis, a life-threatening fungal infection that primarily affects immunocompromised individuals.The protein family commonly observed in the fugus responsible for causing Mucormycosis. The attachment of spores to host cells surface, facilitated by a protein CotH, is a critical step for the invasion and progression of the disease. Therefore, CotH inhibitors have emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for treating mucormycosis.This study presents a novel therapeutic target and ligand for controlling the growth of Mucorales. First, to identify potential CotH inhibitors, we surveyed a library antifungal compounds elaborated in AYUSearch database. Next, using machine learning-based algorithms we screend 20 potentials ligands, followed by structure-based molecular modelling and molecular trajectory analysis to identify the three most promising chemical constituents. In-vitro tube assays on selected Mucorales determined the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) for screened chemotypes. The MIC assay revealed that Bacoside inhibits the growth and sporulation at 5 mg/ml concentrations, emerging as a probable CotH inhibitor. Further, the compound's toxicity was evaluated by adding it to the feed of C.elegans, and the finding suggests that the bacoside is reasonably safe at the studied concentration. The findings project bacoside A as a potential anti-mucorale lead compound that can be further validated with preclinical and clinical studies.Keywords: Bacoside, C.elegans, CotH, Machine learning, Mucormycosis

Introduction

Bacoside A is a bioactive compound copiously present in the plant Bacopa monnieri. Among native Indians, the plant is known as Brahmi or water hyssop, a traditional Ayurvedic herb used for centuries for its cognitive-enhancing and neuroprotective properties. Several other studies demonstrate the antifungal activity of crude Bacopa monnieri extract for the genus Alternaria and Fusarium, especially decoction enriched in Bacoside A and Bacoside B. The present research investigates its potential as an antifungal agent for Mucorales.

Mucormycosis, often known as black fungus, is a rare and fatal fungal infection that usually affects the sinuses, lungs, skin, and brain. The disease onset is mainly associated with inhalation or contact with the fungal spores belonging to order Mucorales, delimitate 55 genera and 260 species. The most prevalent genus causing mucormycosis is Rhizopus, followed by the genera Mucor, Lichtheimia, and Rhizomucor [1]. In general, immunocompromised people who have uncontrolled diabetes, bone marrow transplants, various types of metabolic acidosis, solid organ, malignant haematological illnesses, and deferoxamine medication in patients receiving dialysis have more frequent subjects of Mucormycosis [2]. Recently, during the COVID-19 pandemic, in developing countries like India, post-recovering from COVID-19 or during treatment, a rampant increase in Mucormycosis incidence was observed. Linked with the overuse of steroids and zinc during COVID-19 treatment, the use of industrial oxygen, frequent intubation, immunocompromised situations, and other comorbidities such as diabetes and ketoacidosis. Slowly, COVID-19 and Mucormycosis are sliding into history, but the acute drug crisis will always be reminisced [3]. The first-line treatment involves the intravenous application of liposomal Amphotericin B (AmB). AmB targets ergosterol in the cell membrane of fungi as its primary target, resulting in cell membrane disruption. AmB must traverse the fungus's rigid chitin and B-1,3-glucans cell wall to access the membrane. In many patients, nephrotoxicity is the dose-limiting factor, particularly when AmB is taken with other potentially nephrotoxic medications or when renal damage is highly concerning. Cell membrane lipid composition has been altered in most AmB-resistant fungi characterized [4]. Hamilton-Miller [5] isolated Candida albicans mutants with increased ergosterol content from AmB-resistant cells, whereas Athar and Winner found that AmB-resistant cells have reduced ergosterol content [6]. Therefore, there is a dire need for a novel agent that inhibits Mucorale's growth by a different mechanism.

Several factors influence the risk of Mucormycosis, including uncontrolled diabetes, neutropenia, acidosis, iron overload, and deferoxamine therapy. The mortality rate is 90–100 % in patients with disseminated illness, infections of the central nervous system, or persistent neutropenia [7]. Iron plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of mucormycosis as it is required for fungal growth and virulence. The interplay between the fungal spore coat protein (CotH), particularly CotH3 (isoform CotH), serves as the fungal ligands responsible for attaching to the 78-kDa cell-surface glucose-regulated protein GRP78 during the invasion of host cells. The process is a critical determinant of rhizopus's virulence. A GRP78, also known as BiP and HSPA5, is a molecular chaperone that regulates protein folding and is upregulated in response to cellular stress. The expression of GRP78 is increased on the surface of endothelial cells in response to hyperglycemia, free iron, and ketone bodies. CotH, particularly CotH3, recognizes and binds to GRP78, facilitating the attachment and invasion of spores on endothelial cell surfaces (Fig. 1A). This interaction has been identified as a critical mechanism of the pathogenesis of Mucormycosis8.

Mechanism of CotH and GRP78 in establishing intracellular infection by Rhizopus delemer.

Further, ketone bodies and free iron inhibit the immune response to infection (B), while sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3) neutralizes acidity and inhibits iron liberation from transferrin. The endothelial cells' response to the stress induced by hyperglycemia, free iron, and ketone bodies (C) increases glucose-regulator protein 78 (GRP78) expression on the cell surface. Moreover, glucose, free iron (transported via the high-affinity iron permease [Ftr1p]), and β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) upregulate CotH on the surface of fungal cells, leading to endothelial invasion and fungal growth [8].

With this review, we identified the need of anti-mucorale agent that can inhibit the attachment of fungle spores on the host surface.

Here, in the present study, we hypothesized that the inhibition of CotH can be an effective strategy to combat the infection of Mucorales as it is unreasonable to inhibit the multifunctional GRP78. First, each phytochemical from the chosen list was specifically subjected to activity prediction using a Naïve Bayesian model, Docking, and Molecular simulation. Then, the screened compounds were validated in-vitro under various growth conditions. The findings of this study will provide new perspectives for the development of CotH inhibitors. MATERIAL and methods

Materials

The axenic microbial cultures for Aspergillus niger (non-Mucorale member), Mucor rouxii, and Rhizopus delemar were obtained from the institute's culture repository, and the siderophore production in the chosen fungal strain was confirmed by Chromeazurol S assay (CAS). The microbial cultures were maintained on the Potato Dextrose Agar (Hi-media, India) for all the subsequent assays. The sugar glucose and beta-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) were purchased from Hi-media and Sigma (USA). Other phytochemicals, diosgenin, bacoside A, rutoside, and asiaticoside A (analytical grade), were obtained from Sigma (USA).

Target identification, phytochemical screening, and analysis of promising hits

Using the keywords "sporulation genes" and "fungi", we first identified 1952 reports with the direct or indirect association with the sporulations. Next, we surveyed the protein data bank for corresponding protein structures. We identified the related structures with resolutions up to 2.5 Å or less for further assays.

The crystal structure of CotH (PDB Id: 5jd9 (1.63 Å)) was obtained from the protein data bank (PDB). In this study, we employed a virtual screening approach to identify potential inhibitors of a fungal protein using an antifungal library from the AyuSearch database [9]. A hundred molecules were initially screened based on their known antifungal activity, and these ligands' 3D structures and Canonical SMILES were obtained. Next, we removed the duplicate entry of the chemical constituents. We generated the corresponding decoys(inactive compounds) using the online DUDE database [10] with a ratio of 4:1. Morgan fingerprints (radius 2507 bits) were generated using the RDKit package in Python [11,12], and then t-SNE (t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding) dimensionality reduction can be used to visualize the similar relationships between molecules based on their fingerprints and to identify clusters of identical molecules (Spiwok and Kříž,2020). To ensure that the subsequent machine learning models could model the data correctly, the diversity and spatial distribution of the dataset were investigated. The active and inactive datasets were randomly divided into a training set and a test set with a ratio of 4:1, as described in Supplementary Table 1. In both datasets, inhibitors were marked as "1″ and decoys as "0". Amphotericin B was used as the commercial-level standard for antifungal compounds.

Next, the best five docked complexes (based on binding energies) were chosen for further analysis. After that, we evaluated its stability in a natural cell environment by performing a molecular dynamic simulation of a protein-ligand complex. For that, the following steps were utilized.

a)Protein preparation: Chimera exported 3-dimensional (3D) models of apoproteins and protein-ligand complexes to.pdb format. Their dynamic behaviour was evaluated using molecular dynamic (MD) simulation with DESMOND (version 2022.2) [13].

Protein preparation: Chimera exported 3-dimensional (3D) models of apoproteins and protein-ligand complexes to.pdb format. Their dynamic behaviour was evaluated using molecular dynamic (MD) simulation with DESMOND (version 2022.2) [13].

b)System Setting: The complexes were inserted into the system after applying the force field. With periodic boundary conditions, they were solvated with the SPC water model [14] in a cubic box greater than 1 nm from the edge of the protein. Na + ions were added to neutralize the system, and energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm for 50,000 steps. The whole system was equilibrated by a 100-s NVT simulation at 300 K, followed by a 100-s NPT simulation. A leapfrog algorithm was used to couple protein, ligand, water molecules, and ions separately in the constant-temperature, constant-pressure (NPT) ensemble.

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentrations

Four phytochemicals have been identified by virtual screening and were further tested for their Minimum Inhibitory concentrations (MIC) for selected fungal cultures such as Mucor, Rhizopus, and Aspergillus niger. The stocks for each compound having the concentration of 1 mg/ml, 2 mg/ml, and 5 mg/ml and Bacopa crude extract (1 mg/ml, 10 mg/ml and 20 mg/ml) were prepared in dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) and distilled water. The microbial load was adjusted to 106 spores/ml, and 1 mL of the culture was inoculated at various concentrations of phytochemicals and incubated at 30 °C for 48 h. Amphotericin B was used as the standard antifungal agent in the study. The fungal growth inhibition was evaluated by withdrawing an aliquot (10 ml) and quantifying the fungus's dry weight at every 24-h interval.

Additionally, the phytochemicals' fungistatic or fungicidal activity was determined by intermittently removing the drug stress and observing the growth on fresh growth medium (detailed experimental strategy depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1).

Further investigation of fungal growth and bacoside's impact was conducted in the presence of growth-promoting factors such as glucose (5 gm%), beta-hydroxybutyrate (1 %), and free iron (10 mM).

Microscopic assays for sporangium enumeration

The block of potato dextrose agar was (1 mm) placed on a sterile glass slide impregnated with the individual phytochemicals at their respective inhibitory concentrations and 10 μL of spore suspension (106 spores/ml). Next, the slides were placed in empty petri plates and incubated at 30 °C for 96 h, having three sets for each treatment. At every, 24-h one unit was sacrificed and assayed microscopically for counting the sporangium in a defined region of view (ROV). Sporangium's size and other topological properties were evaluated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

In-vivo toxicity assay for bacoside

Caenorhabditis elegans is a popular model organism due to its ease of cultivation, transparent body, short life cycle, and resemblance to the human genome, often preliminary used to evaluate the toxicity of small chemical compounds. For that, the worm culture was prepared using 3g NaCl, 2.5g peptone, 1 ml MgSO4, 1 ml CaCl2, 1 ml of 5 mg/ml pre-autoclaved cholesterol, and 25 ml 1M KPO4 and 17g agar added in 1000 ml water. Next, the worms were co-inoculated with OP50 E.coli (106 cells/ml). Then, the plates were incubated at 20 °C. Further, the worm synchronization was conducted by plate washing with an M9 buffer [15]. The extracted buffer was centrifuged at 2000 RPM for 10 min, and worms were collected in the pellet. Five experimental sets are kept: bacoside, fungal treated and untreated, and control. The reactive oxidative species (ROS) levels were measured in each group to determine the possible toxic effect of bacoside A. The ROS were estimated by dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) staining [16].

Result and discussion

Machine learning (ML) model for screening of antifungal compounds

Based on the chemical space of the dataset and patterns of the active/inactive compounds, the t-SNE algorithm gave clusters for ligands (Fig. 3). The t-SNE algorithm was used to cluster active and passive compounds based on their molecular properties [17], revealing a clear separation between the two classes. The clustering of active compounds into different chemotypes suggests that they may target different biological pathways and mechanisms, which is worth further investigation.

t-SNE Visualization of clusters using the Morgan Fingerprint approach.

There are fifteen molecular descriptors were chosen to construct the Naïve Bayesian model. The model achieved 90 % accuracy in categorizing compounds as active or inactive based on a probability threshold of ≥0.9. The machine learning model ranks the compounds on their high probability score. The top ten ligands were screened using this model for further investigation, shown in Supplementary Table 2.

By projecting the ligands into a lower-dimensional representation, t-SNE (Fig. 3) has revealed the relationships between different ligand structures, enabling us to identify regions with potentially favourable chemical features. There are five clusters, each representing distinct chemical descriptors. This information is further utilized to decode ligands to proteins during molecular docking analysis.

Analysis of target-ligand complex

Molecular docking studies were conducted with the spore coat protein CotH as a target against ten scrutinized ligands (Supplementary Table 2). Five compounds have the highest binding affinity for CotH, bacoside, rutoside, and diosgenin, which have remarkable binding affinity for the target protein. According to docking analysis, Bacoside A and amphotericin B had the lowest binding energies at −9.0 kcal/mol (Fig. 4).

Mapping of various chemotypes for its binding affinity towards selected targets.

Further, the AmB and bacoside share 13 residues involved in binding with cotH through hydrogen bonds (Table 1), suggesting a solid attachment with spore surface protein. The images of the interaction and surface maps for docking complexes are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2.

To evaluate the stability of the CotH protein complexes, we performed molecular dynamic simulations and analyzed the RMSD and RMSF parameters. The results indicate that the CotH-bacoside and CotH-amphotericin B complexes exhibited stable behaviour during the 150ns simulation at 7 pH and 37 °C.

The RMSD analysis revealed that the protein-ligand complexes experienced minimal structural deviation (within the range of 1–3 Å) during the simulation for 150 ns. Fig. 5A shows that the CotH-bacoside complex had the highest fluctuation at 2.0 Å between 40 and 50ns, indicating that the complex underwent a conformational change and attained an equilibrium after 80 ns.

RMSD analysis of (A) CotH-Bacoside complex (B) CotH-Amphotericin B complex, where Cα represents (right Y axis) deviation in protein at a particular time frame, while Lig fit on Prot (left y-axis) illustrates how stable the ligand is for the protein and its binding pocket.

The CotH-amphotericin B complexes showed fluctuations of less than 2 Å, indicating very stable protein-ligand structures (Fig. 5B).

RMSF analysis of the protein residues provided insights into the flexibility and rigidity of the complexes. In the case of the CotH protein, the RMSF parameter showed that specific amino acid residues exhibited more significant fluctuations than others during the interaction with phytochemicals. The RMSF analysis of the bacoside-CotH complex showed in Fig. 6A that residues 20, 50, 150, 250, and 270 had higher fluctuations of around 3 Å. These residues are most likely the flexible side chains of the protein, while the alpha helix and beta sheets of proteins, which are rigid structural parts of proteins, showed fluctuations between 1-2Å. This suggests that the protein structure remained relatively stable during the interaction with bacoside in proximity.

RMSF analysis of (A) Protein-Bacoside complex (B) Protein-Amphotericin B complex.

In the case of the CotH-amphotericin B complex in Fig. 6B, the RMSF analysis showed higher fluctuations in residues 50 and 160, noted at 2.7 Å and 2.3 Å, respectively.

These fluctuations occurred between 0.5 and 1.5 Å, indicating the relatively more stable protein-ligand structure. Therefore, it can be concluded that the CotH protein maintained its structure and stability during the interaction with bacoside A and amphotericin B and can be established as a crucial cellular target for ligand binding. On the other hand, in comparison with AmB, bacoside A takes a long time to attain equilibrium (Fig. 5A) but still illustrates a stable interaction (RMSD 2 Å). Similar data analysis for rutoside and diosgenin is presented in supplementary images Supplementary Figs. 3A,3B,3C and 3D.

Additionally, the H-bond play a significant role in ligand binding. The H-bond has a strong influence on drug specificity, metabolization and adsorption. Bacoside has formed hydrogen bonds with residues Arg-45, Gly-46, ILE-144, ASN-228, TRP-245, and ASP-246 and maintained firm contact for over 70 % of the simulation duration.

Similarly, Amphotericin B has formed H-Bond with residues Arg-45, ALA-143, ASN -228, and ASP-246 and maintained the firm contact for over 70 % of the simulation time. Here, Arg-45, ASN-228 and ASP-246 have emerged as the common CotH residues involved in binding with the drug bacoside or AmphotericinB.

A statistically supervised experimental (N = 3) set-up determined the minimum growth inhibitory concentrations for bacoside, rutoside, and diosgenin using AmB as a reference drug (Table 2). The consortium indicates the mixture of spore suspension of all three fungi. The results depict that bacoside A inhibits the growth of all three fungi with about 80 % efficiency at 5 mg/ml concentrations. In vitro, bacoside exhibits a similar performance to AmB. Even the crude bacopa water decoction gave remarkable growth inhibition at 20 mg/ml concentration.

Assessment of anti-sporulation activity

It is hypothesized that the cotH gene product is present on spore coats, so its inhibition would likely affect sporulation. As part of our cultivation and growth inhibition assay and routine examination of agar plates, we observed that plates containing just a spot of bacoside resulted in a complete absence of sporulation (Supplementary Fig. 4). This promising observation prompted our subsequent assays. Fungal slide culture assays were conducted to verify bacoside's specific effects on sporulation by observing fungal sporulation and sporangium development under SEM and light microscopy. When followed for sub-lethal concentrations of bacoside (2 mg/ml), though there was sporangium, no spores were developed on the sporangium (Fig. 9A and B).

Conclusion

The mortality rate of mucormycosis remains unacceptable when treated with available antifungal monotherapies, especially in haematology patients, leading researchers to investigate novel antifungal combinations [18]. Due to the reliance on mono-drug therapy for mucormycosis, several problems have emerged, such as drug resistance to shortages. Also, combining antifungals can be more effective when multiple strains of fungus are involved in such aggressive infections. Recent studies have found Isavuconazole a promising anti-Mucorales agent due to its good pharmacokinetics and safety profile. Further, clinical trials are underway for new antifungal agents, including Manogepix (MGX), VT-1161, and statins with anti-Mucorales activity [19]. After the post-Covid era, the search for novel anti-Mucorals gained momentum. In this regard, the present research findings are very encouraging, with this bacoside A or even Bacopa monorie extract emerging as an excellent anti-Mucorale agent to complement the first-line drug. Though it may not kill the fungal cells completely, it inhibits the sporulations, halting the infection's progression.

Gebremariam (2016) [20] has reported that the mucorales CotH protein is a crucial virulence factor in establishing mucormycosis, binding to GRP78 receptors on the host epithelium to allow the fungi to invade. CotH is an invasive agent that attaches to the host cell GRP78 to drive pathogenic host-cell interactions.

We use laboratory and computational studies to identify phytochemicals as potential antifungal alternatives. In our attempt to identify cotH inhibitors, we discovered bacoside A as a potential antifungal compound. Indeed, we claim that it performs better than AmB in several in-vitro studies. When the AmB-treated culture was reseeded without AmB, the growth relapse was observed in the experimental sets, displaying the fungistatic activity against the spore cocktail. At the same time, bacoside A exhibited complete control. However, it is still too early to comment on its clinical efficacy. Still, the present study successfully establishes that it is the lead compound for further gene expression studies in mice. In addition, deferoxamine predisposes patients to mucormycosis by inappropriately supplying the fungus with iron and promoting its growth [21]. Even at elevated iron concentrations (in-vitro), bacoside A performs well, while AmB's efficacy is slightly compromised.

As another critical reflection, it was observed that R. oryzae exposed to lovastatin lost macroscopic melanin, was more susceptible to peroxide, and had diminished virulence in mouse and fly models of mucormycosis [22]. We also noted that melanin loss occurred upon exposure to Bacoside and significantly reduced sporangium numbers.

The present study successfully establishes bacoside A as the lead compound for further gene expression studies in mice and the candidate for subsequent clinical studies.
