Mesothelin as a biomarker and potential therapeutic target in rheumatoid arthritis bone destruction
Authors: Xiaohui Su, Qian Wang, Jingbo Wang, Panpan Zhu, Wanyi Guo, Jinghang Yang, Siyu Li, Fengyu Huang, Mingzhu Qi, Hui Li, Na Lin, Chao Lu, Xiangying Kong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryMesothelin (MSLN) is implicated in multiple biological processes, notably in modulating immune responses. However, the role of MSLN in osteoclastogenesis remains underexplored. Here we reveal that the level of MSLN was significantly elevated in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients, as well as in collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) animals. Pharmacological and genetic inhibition of MSLN significantly impair osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption. In the animal model, down-regulation of MSLN effectively alleviates bone destruction. Further investigation unravels that MSLN directly interacts with phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), resulting in the activation of the PI3K/protein kinase B (AKT) signaling pathway, which subsequently induces the expression and translocation of nuclear factor of activated T cells 1 (NFATc1), thereby promoting osteoclast differentiation. Overall, our findings indicate that MSLN could serve as a valuable prognostic marker for bone destruction in RA, and targeting MSLN may offer a therapeutic strategy for RA-related bone destruction.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a prevalent autoimmune disease with global impact, particularly on the adult workforce. The primary pathological characteristic of RA is the destruction of joint bones, one of the major causes of disability in affected individuals. The primary objective of RA treatment is to delay or prevent bone destruction.1,2 In the decades, the investigation of the underlying mechanisms of bone destruction has been the focus of research within the orthopedics field.

The overactivation of osteoclasts is a crucial phenomenon in the process of bone destruction.3 Accumulating evidence suggests that inflammatory responses play an important role in enhanced osteoclast activation and bone loss in RA.4 For example, inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, IL-17, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, highly expressed in the RA synovium, can induce the production of receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL) in synovial fibroblasts, thereby activating osteoclasts in the joints.5,6 Naturally, there is a balance between osteoclast-driven bone loss and osteoblast-driven bone formation. Once the balance is disrupted upon certain injury or inflammation and bone loss surpasses bone formation, RA bone erosion occurs.7,8 Thus, the modulation of osteoclast immunity emerges as a promising strategy for delaying bone destruction and represents a significant target for therapeutic interventions in the context of RA.

Mesothelin (MSLN), a membrane protein linked to tumor invasion, is highly expressed in diverse tumor tissues such as mesothelioma, ovarian, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma.9,10,11 Its significance as a potential target in tumor immunotherapy is underscored by its extensive investigation in preclinical studies and clinical trials, particularly in antibody-based medications such as chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapies.12,13 As a potential target, MSLN possesses the capacity to guide genetically modified immune cells toward cancer cells amid a heterogeneous population of cells and help to kill cancer cells.14 MSLN is also widely used as a diagnostic marker in the development of tumors.15,16 A high level of MSLN has been associated with tumor progression, increased staging, and decreased overall survival. Furthermore, serum MSLN has been used as an indicator of tumor progression in the routine clinical practice of malignant pleural mesothelioma treatment.17,18 The potential involvement of MSLN in the pathogenesis of RA, an autoimmune disorder disease, remains unexplored.

In this study, we demonstrate that MSLN contributes to the pathogenesis of RA through regulating systemic responses and bone destruction. Knockdown of MSLN by small interfering RNA (siRNA) significantly impacts osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption. In the collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) animals, down-regulation of MSLN effectively alleviated clinical symptoms and bone destruction. Mechanistically, MSLN could directly interact with phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) to influence the expression and translocation of nuclear factor of activated T cell 1 (NFATc1), thereby promoting osteoclast differentiation. Taken together, our findings indicate that MSLN could serve as a valuable prognostic marker and a therapeutic target for RA.

Results

MSLN is significantly expressed in the synovial tissues and plasma of RA patients

To investigate the specific role of MSLN in RA, synovial tissue and plasma samples were collected from patients with osteoarthritis (OA) and RA (Figure 1A), and the expression of MSLN in the synovium, plasma, and RA synovial fibroblasts (RASFs) were evaluated. The results of western blot showed that MSLN was abundantly expressed in synovium from RA patients compared to those with OA (Figures 1B and 1D). The up-regulation of MSLN in the joint synovial tissue with RA could be observed by immunohistochemical staining (Figures 1C and 1E). The predominant cell types in the synovial tissue are synovial fibroblasts and synovial macrophages. To characterize what cells in the synovium are expressing MSLN, we employed the immunofluorescence technique to label the cells for MSLN plus a specific marker for synovial fibroblasts or synovial macrophages in the synovial tissues from the patients with RA and OA. It is well established that the precursor protein undergoes proteolytic cleavage to generate two functionally distinct products: mature MSLN (40 kDa) and megakaryocyte-potentiating factor (MPF, 30 kDa), each exhibiting unique biological activities.19,20 To evaluate their clinical relevance, we measured circulating MPF and MSLN levels in bone fracture (BF) patients, OA patients, and RA patients using ELISA. The results indicated that the plasma MPF levels in OA or RA patients were comparable to those in BF patients (Figure 1H). Notably, MSLN levels were significantly elevated in RA patients compared to those with BF (Figure 1I). The expression of MSLN in RASFs was induced by TNF-α in a time-dependent manner as depicted in Figures 1J and 1K. Taken together, these data provide evidence of the positive correlation between MSLN expression and the pathogenesis of RA.

MSLN is upregulated in the joint tissues of rats and mice with CIA

Given that bone destruction is a key factor affecting the prognosis of patients with RA, we then aimed to investigate the potential involvement of MSLN in the RA-induced bone destruction in animal models. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining of joint tissues revealed significant joint destruction in CIA rats (Figures 2A and 2B). Additionally, immunohistochemical experiments were conducted to evaluate MSLN expression in destructive joints, which revealed an abnormal overexpression of MSLN in the synovial, cartilage, and epiphyseal plate tissues of CIA rats (Figure 2C). The level of MSLN in the joints of CIA rats with destructive arthritis was further assessed by western blot analyses. The results showed that the expression of MSLN was significantly increased in CIA rats with remarkable articular bone destruction, accompanied by up-regulation of NFATc1, matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), and cathepsin K (CTSK, Figures 2D–2H). Compared with normal rats, the levels of MSLN in the plasma of CIA rats were also remarkably higher (Figures 2I and 2J). To further investigate the relationship between MSLN and RA bone destruction, analogous experiments were performed on CIA mice, as depicted in Figure S1. The results demonstrated an up-regulation of MSLN in the joints of CIA mice, accompanied by a pronounced increase in the expression of NFATc1, MMP-9, and CTSK. Additionally, MSLN was found to co-localize with the macrophage marker CD68, the synovial fibroblast marker Vimentin, and the osteoclast marker CTSK within the joints of these mice. RNA sequencing revealed that Msln serves as a key regulator in osteoclast differentiation and RA-associated bone destruction

To systematically identify genes involved in osteoclast differentiation and RA-mediated bone destruction, we performed transcriptome-wide profiling comparing bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMMs) and differentiated osteoclasts. Our analysis identified 2,067 differentially expressed genes (DEGs, |log2FC| ≥ 1.2, p < 0.05), including 829 downregulated and 1,238 upregulated genes (Figure 3A). Notably, Msln emerged as one of the most significantly altered DEGs. To further delineate RA-specific transcriptional changes, we analyzed synovial tissue from CIA rats versus normal rats. This revealed 2,395 DEGs (1,258 upregulated and 1,137 downregulated; |log2FC| ≥ 1.2, p < 0.05; Figures 3C and 3D). Intersection analysis of the two DEG sets (osteoclasts vs. BMMs and CIA rats vs. normal rats) identified 300 overlapping genes (Figure 3E). Among these, Msln ranked second in significance when the top 20 genes were visualized by heatmap (Figure 3F). Pathway enrichment analysis implicated three key signaling cascades in osteoclast differentiation: PI3K/protein kinase B (AKT), nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), and Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signaling pathways (Figures 3G and 3H), suggesting their potential synergistic roles in bone-destructive processes.

Furthermore, the co-localization of MSLN and phalloidin, a fluorescent molecule that binds to filamentous actin, was observed during osteoclast differentiation. The results demonstrated the observation of a significant increase in the number of mature osteoclasts with multiple nuclei, and phalloidin-labeled actin rings, as well as MSLN protein expression, was also significantly increased in macrophage colony stimulating factor (M-CSF)-dependent BMMs after RANKL induction on day 5 (Figures 4E and 4F). Interfering with MSLN expression impacts osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption

To determine the role of MSLN in osteoclast differentiation, we knocked down MSLN in pre-osteoclasts by siRNA transfection and performed real-time qPCR to examine the expression of the osteoclast-specific genes. Interestingly, knockdown of MSLN resulted in down-regulation of Trap, Mmp-9, and Ctsk, implicating that they may be the downstream targets of MSLN (as shown in Figures 5A–5D). Most importantly, the numbers of TRAP-positive multinucleated cells (≥3 nuclei) were remarkably reduced in cells treated with MSLN siRNA compared to those in the cells treated with scramble siRNA. The observation was further demonstrated by F-actin staining. Accordingly, treatment with MSLN siRNA also led to a significant reduction in pit formation (Figures 5E–5J). Altogether, the results implicated a critical role of MSLN in osteoclast differentiation, activation, and function. To elucidate how MSLN affects osteoclast differentiation, we examined the factors associated with the event. The expressions of NFATc1, CTSK, and MMP-9 were assessed by immunofluorescence and western blot assays. As depicted in Figures 5K, 5L, and S2A–S2E, knockdown of MSLN resulted in a remarkable decrease in the expression of TRAF6, NFATc1, MMP-9, and CTSK. To further evaluate the involvement of MSLN in osteoclast differentiation, we overexpressed Msln in pre-osteoclasts by lentiviral vector-Msln (LV-MSLN). Apparently, MSLN overexpression enhanced the levels of osteoclast-specific factors (Figures 5M–5P) and inflammatory cytokinesis (Figures S3A–S3C). As expected, there was a notable increase in the number of TRAP-positive multinucleated cells (Figures 5Q and 5T). F-actin staining revealed that the actin ring area was significantly larger in the MSLN overexpression group, combined with a substantial increase in pit formation on bone slice (Figures 5R, 5S, 5U, and 5V). As shown in Figures 5W and S2F–S2J, MSLN overexpression also enhanced the expression of TRAF6, NFATc1, MMP-9, and CTSK. These findings collectively establish MSLN as a critical regulator of osteoclast differentiation, activation, and bone resorption function.

MSLN promotes osteoclast differentiation though direct interaction with PI3K

Knockdown of MSLN inhibited the JAK2/STAT3 and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways (Figures 6A and S4A–S4D), whereas MSLN overexpression enhanced their activities (Figures 6B and S4E–S4H), suggesting that MSLN mediates these pathways during osteoclast differentiation. To investigate if there is any direct interaction between MSLN and various signaling proteins, we performed a co-immunoprecipitation (coIP). Interestingly, MSLN was able to pull down PI3K, and vice versa (Figure 6C). Moreover, immunofluorescence labeling and stimulated emission depletion (STED) super-resolution microscopy showed that PI3K and MSLN were co-localized in the osteoclast cytoplasm (Figures 6D and 6F), further demonstrating their interaction inside a cell. In addition, knockdown of MSLN reduced the level of PI3K and weakened the interaction between PI3K and MSLN as observed with STED (Figure 6G). To theoretically decipher the possibility, we conducted the molecular docking analysis. As shown in Figure 6E, PI3K can mainly bind to the C-terminal structural domain of MSLN. The residues at the protein-protein interaction interface have close contact in a linear fashion. Among them, the amino group of Arg565 in MSLN protein forms a hydrogen bond with the carboxyl oxygen of Asp171 in PI3K. Overtly, His471 and Arg472 of PI3K are also solidly linked to Asp171. MSLN inhibition mitigated bone destruction in CIA rats and mouse models

Methotrexate (MTX) is a first-line drug for treating RA in the clinic, which can effectively impede disease progression in patients.1,21 In this study, we used MTX to alternatively verify the role of MSLN in RA. As shown in Figures 7A–7C and S5A–S5D, CIA rats showed severe bone destruction, which could be markedly inhibited after administration of MTX. MTX treatment could suppress the expression of MSLN and other proteins such as NFATc1, MMP-9, and CTSK (Figures 7D and S5E–S5G). (M) Western blot analysis was conducted on MSLN, TRAF6, PI3K, p-AKT, AKT, NFATc1, MMP-9, CTSK, and GAPDH after AAV transfection (n = 3).

The data presented in the figure are expressed as mean ± SEM, with statistical significance indicated by ##p < 0.01 compared with the control group, ∗∗p < 0.01 compared with the CIA group (C), and ∗∗p < 0.01 compared with the AAV-NC group (J–L).

To further confirm the role of MSLN in RA-induced bone destruction, we utilized an adeno-associated virus (AAV)-delivered short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting MSLN in the joints of CIA mice. Bioluminescent detection in the tissues showed that delivery of AAV to the joint tissues was completely achieved (Figure S6A). As illustrated in Figures 7E–7G, down-regulation of MSLN in the joints of CIA mice resulted in a reduction in foot swelling, a lower clinical arthritis score, and a decreased incidence rate. Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) analysis showed that MSLN suppression could effectively mitigate CIA-induced bone destruction (Figures 7H and S6C–S6G). H&E staining revealed that cartilage and bone destruction in the MSLN down-regulation group was significantly reduced compared with the AAV-NC group (Figures 7I and 7J). TRAP staining indicated a marked reduction in osteoclast differentiation in the AAV-MSLN treatment group (Figures 7K and 7L). Furthermore, western blot analysis revealed a reduction in MSLN expression levels in joint tissues after AAV-MSLN injection. Similar to the observations described earlier, the activation of the PI3K/AKT and TRAF6/NFATc1 signaling pathways was suppressed and the expression of osteoclast-specific factors MMP-9 and CTSK was significantly decreased (Figures 7M and S6H–S6N). Overall, these findings provide convincible evidence supporting the role of MSLN in osteoclast differentiation and arthritic bone destruction.

Discussion

Early diagnosis and early treatment are crucial for controlling the progression of RA and reducing disability.22,23 In particular, the early treatment can effectively alleviate their condition or even achieve complete remission in 90% of patients.24,25 In this study, we have demonstrated that MSLN not only can be used as a marker for early diagnosis of RA but also is a potent target for treating this disorder.

In the early stage of RA development, there are barely any clinical symptoms in patients. In the clinic, biomarkers such as rheumatic factor and anti-citrullinated protein antibodies have been employed for decades in RA diagnosis.26 Nevertheless, these existing diagnostic markers exhibit insufficient accuracy and sensitivity for early detection of RA.27 Hence, the identification of markers for early and accurate diagnosis of RA becomes an urgent need. In this study, we observed that there was a substantial elevation of MSLN in the plasma and synovial tissue of RA patients as compared to those with OA. Furthermore, we used CIA models to demonstrate the correlation of an increased level of MSLN in the plasma and joint with osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption. The findings suggest that MSLN has the potential as a diagnostic marker for early detection of RA.

The pathological characteristics of RA encompass cartilage degradation and bone erosion surrounding joints, which significantly contribute to disability in individuals with this condition. Osteoclasts, as the sole cells responsible for bone resorption, play a pivotal role in the process of RA-related bone erosion.28 Undoubtedly, impeding osteoclast differentiation and activation emerge as a crucial therapeutic target for mitigating RA-induced bone destruction.29 Studies in cancer biology have clearly elucidated the role of MSLN in apoptosis and drug resistance of diverse tumor cells.30,31 On the other hand, MSLN is involved in the secretion of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β by activating specific signaling pathways, including the PI3K/AKT, mitogen-activated protein kinase, and NF-κB pathways.32,33,34 Moreover, overexpression of MSLN also facilitates cell migration and invasion by inducing the activation and expression of MMP-7.35,36 Interestingly, these signaling pathways and target molecules are also critical factors contributing to bone destruction in RA. Therefore, MSLN may be a key target molecule for therapeutic treatment of RA.

MSLN is highly expressed in the joint of CIA animal models. Interestingly, the increased expression of MSLN during osteoclast differentiation was along with the activation of several signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT and JAK2/STAT3. Furthermore, the inhibition of MSLN by siRNA was found to suppress the PI3K/AKT and JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathways, whereas overexpression of MSLN could activate those pathways. The results imply that there is a correlation between MSLN and these signaling pathways. Indeed, our coIP experiments found that MSLN could directly interact with PI3K. This observation was confirmed by performing immunofluorescence staining to co-localize PI3K and MSLN in the cytoplasm of osteoclasts. Moreover, molecular docking analysis also provided strong evidence in support of the interaction between MSLN and PI3K. It is well known that activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway plays a critical role in osteoclasts differentiation. Therefore, our findings suggest that MSLN interacts with PI3K and subsequently affects osteoclast differentiation through regulating the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. However, the relationship between MSLN with the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway needs to be further investigated.

Studies have demonstrated that targeting MSLN represents a therapeutic approach for treating tumors37,38 because MSLN is highly expressed in various malignant tumor cells but has a very low level in normal tissues.39 In this study, we found that MTX, a first-line disease-modifying antirheumatic drug in the clinic, functions in reducing the symptoms of RA through suppressing the expression of MSLN. MTX is a chemotherapy agent and immune system suppressant, which is used to treat cancer and autoimmune diseases including RA. MTX in cancer treatment mainly inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, an enzyme that participates in the synthesis of DNA, RNA, thymidylates, and proteins. However, MTX affects RA by different pathways. Studies have shown that MTX inhibits the enzymes involved in purine metabolism, leading to the accumulation of adenosine; inhibition of T cell activation and suppression of intercellular adhesion molecule expression by T cells; selective down-regulation of B cells; inhibition of methyltransferase activity, leading to deactivation of enzyme activity relevant to immune system function; and the inhibition of the binding of IL-1β to its cell surface receptor. Most interestingly, our results indicate that the therapeutic role of MTX in RA may, at least in part, suppress MSLN, which provides a molecular mechanism for its pharmacological application in treating cancer and RA. For sure, further investigation of MSLN is needed to better elucidate its role in the pathogenesis of RA, particularly in osteoclast differentiation and bone destruction.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that MSLN contributes to osteoclast differentiation and bone destruction during the development of RA. Clearly, the role of MSLN in the pathogenesis of RA is through regulating the PI3K/AKT signaling, which impacts the expression of the genes associated with the RA progression. Thus, MSLN may serve as a promising prognostic indicator for bone damage in RA and, more importantly, as a key pathological target for treating this disorder. Targeting MSLN could potentially provide therapeutic opportunities for treatment of RA in the future.

Limitations of the study

Our study presents compelling evidence that MSLN promotes osteoclast differentiation and contributes to RA-associated bone destruction. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the clinical correlations are derived from a relatively small sample size of RA and OA samples. Future studies involving larger, multi-center cohorts are needed to validate the clinical relevance of MSLN. Second, although siRNA-mediated knockdown, AAV-shRNA, and overexpression approaches support a role for MSLN in osteoclastogenesis, potential off-target effects cannot be fully excluded. Thus, conditional knockout models would offer more definitive genetic evidence to establish causality. Third, although we demonstrate an interaction between MSLN and PI3K, our coIP and docking analyses cannot entirely rule out indirect interactions. More advanced biophysical methods are required for further validation. Finally, the study primarily focuses on osteoclasts, and the potential roles of MSLN in other synovial cell types or immune cells remain unexplored and merit further investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Acquisition of human samples and isolation of RASFs

Peripheral blood samples were collected from 17 patients with BF, 17 with OA, and 17 with RA. Synovial tissue samples were also obtained from RA and OA patients during total joint replacement or synovectomy. The cohort included both males and females, with an average age of 53.88 ± 30.12 years for BF patients, 68.00 ± 9.00 years for OA patients, and 57.52 ± 22.48 years for RA patients. All human samples were obtained from Xi’an Honghui Hospital for the measurement of MSLN protein expression in plasma and joint tissues. Individual participants in this study provided written informed consent, and all procedures involving human subjects were approved by the Ethics Committee of Xi’an Honghui Hospital and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (202303059).

Synovial tissues were rinsed five times with PBS containing 2% penicillin/streptomycin. After meticulous removal of extraneous tissues, particularly adipose tissue, the purified synovial tissues were minced into approximately 1 mm fragments. These tissue fragments were subsequently placed in a culture flask and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. RASFs were isolated and cultured in complete growth medium, consisting of DMEM supplemented with 15% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The medium was refreshed every three days. Upon reaching 80% confluence, the cells were passaged at a 1:3 split ratio. Cells from passages 3 to 6 were used for all subsequent experiments.

Animal experiments

The rat CIA model was established using male SD rats aged 6–7 weeks and weighing 160–180 g, which were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The CIA mouse model was induced in male DBA/1J mice aged 8–9 weeks, supplied by Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. All animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at the Institute of Chinese Materia Medica, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences. The housing environment was maintained at 22°C with a 12-h light/dark cycle, and food and water were provided ad libitum. All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of the Chinese Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (No. 201907060, 2020B122 and 2024B234).

Cell culture

C57BL/6J mice aged 6–8 weeks were sacrificed and immersed in 75% alcohol for 5–10 min. The femur and tibia of the bilateral hind limbs of the mice were aseptically removed. The bone marrow cavities at both ends were cut open to fully expose the bone marrow. Then the red blood cell lysate was added, mixed, washed with PBS, and the cells were resuspended in complete α-MEM containing 10% FBS for cell counting. The isolated cells were diluted with medium containing 20 ng/mL M-CSF and seeded in 6-well plates or 24-well plates. After 72 h of culture, 50 ng/mL RANKL was added for induction. Periodically observe the induction of osteoclasts under the microscope and perform fluid changes according to cell growth.

Virus

All lentiviral and adeno-associated viral vectors used in this study were purchased from GenePharma. The LV-MSLN was employed to overexpress MSLN in BMMs following 24 h of RANKL induction. For the downregulation of MSLN in the joints of CIA mice, AAV-MSLN was utilized.

Method details

TRAP staining

The round cell slices were subjected to sterilization using 75% alcohol before being seeded in 24-well plates containing complete α-MEM supplemented with 20 ng/mL M-CSF. Following 72 h of culture, induction was initiated by adding 50 ng/mL RANKL. After 6 days of induction, the culture medium was removed, and the cell tissue was fixed using a 4% fixative solution after cleaning with PBS for 15 min. The TRAP staining solution was added in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by the removal of the TRAP staining solution and subsequent rinsing of the cell slices with PBS three times. Hematoxylin was then added for a duration of 1 min, followed by tap water for 5 min. The cell slices were subsequently extracted, and images were captured using a microscope at a magnification of 200×.

Resorption pit assay

To assess the function of osteoclasts, BMMs were seeded onto bovine bone discs in 96-well plates at a concentration of 2×104 cells/well and subsequently exposed to M-CSF and RANKL for a duration of 7 days. Post-treatment, the adhered cells were meticulously removed by brushing, and resorption pits were visualized through toluidine blue staining using microscopy at 100× magnification. The percentage of resorbed areas was quantified using ImageJ software.

Real-time qPCR

BMMs were seeded in 6-well plates and induced to differentiate into osteoclasts with M-CSF and RANKL. Following the induction and culture of cells, the total RNA was extracted utilizing the TRIzoL reagent, and the reverse transcription (RT) reactions were performed in accordance with the Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit manual. A 40-fold dilution of cDNA was employed as a template for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification utilizing specific forward and reverse primers. PCR reactions were conducted with the following steps: initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s, and annealing for 1 min. The expressions of Msln, Mmp-9, Ctsk, Trap, and Traf6 were subsequently analyzed. For the Primer Sequences please refer to Table S1 in the supplementary materials.

Protein extraction and western blot

After induction and culture, RIPA was added to the cells for 30 min on ice, and the supernatant was centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 rpm at 4°C to extract the total protein. The Coomassie Brilliant Blue method was utilized for protein quantification. Following protein preparation, electrophoresis with a polyacrylamide gel was performed, and the resulting proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane via wet transfer. The membrane was subsequently blocked with 5% skim milk for a duration of 2 h. Primary antibodies were applied and incubated overnight, followed by the application of the secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. ECL reagent was administered, and the membrane was exposed in the gel imager.

Immunofluorescence assays

After induction and culture, the cells were washed twice with PBS and subsequently fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Following this, the cells were treated with a PBST solution containing 0.1% Triton for 15 min. An overnight incubation with primary antibodies for TRAP, NFATc1, MMP-9, and CTSK was carried out, followed by a 1 h incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies at a temperature of 37°C. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with a phalloidin staining solution at room temperature in the dark for 60 min, followed by Hoechst staining for 15 min. Finally, the cells were sealed with an anti-fluorescence quenching solution and immediately imaged after mounting.

ELISA assay

Levels of MSLN, MPF protein in the human plasma were detected with ELISA Kit according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The optical density of each well was determined after 30 min of incubation utilizing a microplate reader set at 450 nm. The assay sensitivity was 0.022 ng/mL, with a detection range of 0.2–10 ng/mL.

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

The total RNA of BMMs and osteoclasts was extracted and analyzed by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Differential gene analysis was performed based on the criteria of |log2 (FC)| ≥ 1.2, p value < 0.05. GraphPad Prism 8 software was used to create volcano plots and heat maps. Then, synovial tissue was randomly selected from three rats in the normal and CIA rats, and total RNA was isolated. RNA-seq was used to detect the differential genes between normal rats and CIA rats, screened based on the criteria of |log2 (FC)| ≥ 1.2 and p value <0.05. The DEGs of BMMs and osteoclasts, as well as the DEGs of normal and CIA rats, were intersected using the Weishengxin platform. 300 intersected genes were screened out, and the top 20 genes were selected to create a heatmap visualization. The DEGs of osteoclasts versus BMMs were submitted to the DAVID for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. The top 35 KEGG pathways and top 10 significantly enriched GO terms were visualized using the Weishengxin platform.

Transient transfection

M-CSF-dependent BMMs were seeded at a density of 3.0×105 cells/well in 24-well plates. Following 72 h of culture, the induction medium was replaced with a medium containing 20 ng/mL M-CSF and 50 ng/mL RANKL. After 24 h, the culture medium was substituted with Opti-MEM, and a mixture comprising 25 nM ON-TARGET plus siRNA targeting MSLN or control siRNA, Lipofectamine 3000 was prepared by diluting them in Opti-MEM for 5 min at room temperature. The diluted Lipofectamine 3000 was then combined with the diluted siRNA and incubated for 20 min. The mixture was then pipetted to the cells and incubated for 12 h, followed by the culture, the culture medium was replaced with induction medium for 48 h.

LV lentiviral vector

BMMs were seeded in 6-well plates at 1.5×106 cells/well and cultured for 72 h. Following this incubation, the medium was replaced with an induction medium containing 20 ng/mL M-CSF and 50 ng/mL RANKL. Lentiviral transfection was conducted 24 h later, with the addition of 2 μL Polybrene (final concentration of 5 μg/mL) was added to the induction medium and 10 μL of each of LV-NC and LV-MSLN (2×108 TU/mL). After 12 h of incubation, the medium was again replaced with induction medium, and RNA samples and protein samples were collected after 12 h and 48 h, respectively.

CIA induction in rats and mice

The animal studies were conducted in compliance with the guidelines and regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals as set forth by the National Institutes of Health (NIH). The CIA rat model was established using SD male rats. An emulsion with a concentration of 1 mg/mL was prepared by thoroughly emulsifying equal volumes of IFA and bovine type Ⅱ collagen solution. The primary immunization was administered via intradermal injection of 0.2 mL into the tail root of rats, followed by a booster injection of a 1 mg/mL emulsion one week later. DBA/1J male mice were utilized to establish a CIA mouse model. A 1 mg/mL emulsion was prepared by mixing equal volumes of CFA and bovine type II collagen and fully emulsifying the mixture. The primary immunization involved intradermal injection of 0.1 mL into the tail root of the mice. Three weeks later, the mice were boosted with a 1 mg/mL emulsion made from emulsifying CFA and bovine type II collagen solution.

Bone destruction assessment

The hind paw and knee joints were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 48 h and subsequently scanned using a Micro-CT system with a spatial resolution of 25 μm and a single scan time of 72 min. GE Healthcare Micro-View software was employed to analyze the dimensions of bone. Bone mineral density (BMD), bone surface/volume ratio (BS/BV), percent bone volume (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb. N) and trabecular thickness (Tb. Th) were measured and quantitatively analyzed. The hind paw and knee joints were fixed with 4% formaldehyde, decalcified, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4 μm sections. H&E and TRAP staining were applied to the sections for histological analysis. The evaluation was conducted in accordance with the Standardized Microscopic Arthritis Scoring of Histological sections (SMASH) criteria, focusing on bone erosion, synovial inflammation, and cartilage erosion, each graded from 0 to 3.

Immunohistochemical analysis

The knee joint’s paraffin sections underwent dewaxing and rehydration, followed by washing with PBS and incubation with 3% H2O2 for 10 min to eliminate the endogenous catalase activity. Subsequently, compound digestive solution was added (2–3 drops) for 20 min, followed by the addition of antigen repair solution for antigen repair. The sections were blocked with 5% BSA, and the primary antibody targeting MSLN was applied for overnight incubation. After PBS washing, a secondary antibody was incubated, and the DAB chromogenic solution was performed to reflect MSLN signals. After hematoxylin staining of the nuclei, the sections were dehydrated and sealed with neutral resin. Immunohistochemical images were captured using the OlymbusBX51 microscope.

CoIP

To extract cellular proteins, 1×lysis buffer was added, followed by thorough shaking and mixing. The mixture was then centrifuged at 4°C and 12000 rpm for 10 min to remove cellular debris. Protein G magnetic beads were added and incubated at 4°C on a shaking bed for 2 h. A magnetic rack was used for adsorption, and the mixture was left for 5 min to eliminate non-specific binding between the cell lysate and the magnetic beads. On the subsequent day, additional protein G magnetic beads were added and incubated at 4°C for 2 h. The mixture was then adsorbed on a magnetic rack and left for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the magnetic beads were re-suspended using NP-40 lysis buffer. SDS-PAGE protein buffer was added to elute the proteins.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was accomplished using the Schrödinger software package. The MSLN and PI3K protein molecules were selected from the Protein DataBank. Both were prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard module, such as hydrogenation, structure refinement and OPLS3 force field minimization. Finally, protein-protein docking was performed using the Biologics module.40 Protein hydrophobic surfaces were visualized using Chimera software. The 3D structure of the MTX active ingredient was downloaded using the PubChem website, the binding energies of proteins and small molecules were calculated with AutoDock Vina, and the results were visualized by PyMOL.

Adeno-associated virus vector

The efficacy of AAV-mediated MSLN shRNA delivery was assessed in DBA/1J mice CIA model through intra-articular injection. Following acclimatization, the mice were grouped and subjected to various treatments. The control group received no interventions, whereas mice in the AAV-NC and AAV-MSLN underwent intra-articular injections into the knee and ankle joint cavities at several predetermined time points: on the day of the initial immunization, on the day of the secondary immunization, and one week prior to sample collection. Specifically, the AAV-NC group received injections of a blank vector (2×1012 TU/mL, 8 μL), while the AAV-MSLN group was injected with the AAV-MSLN (2×1012 TU/mL, 8 μL). Sample collection for all mice was conducted on the 40th day post-primary immunization.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. The sample size (n) for each experiment is explicitly indicated in the corresponding figure legend. The results are expressed as mean ± SEM. Differences between two independent samples were evaluated using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Values of p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 were statistically significant.
