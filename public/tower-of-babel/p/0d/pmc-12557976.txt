Morphology and multigene phylogeny reveal a new species of Inosperma from Himalayas of Pakistan
Authors: Arooj Naseer, Rubab Khurshid, Yu-Guang Fan, Abdul Nasir Khalid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Inosperma kashmiranum from Himalayan forests of Azad Jammu and Kashmir is described as a new species based on morphological and molecular data. The phylogenetic placements of this species was inferred, based on molecular evidence from sequences of ITS, 28 S and RPB2. Phylogenetic analyses resolve placement of this species in section Cervicolores. Inosperma kashmiranum is characterized by purplish brown to grayish brown pileus, fibrillose, rimose to squamulose towards margin, smaller (12.1 × 6.9 μm) basidiospores and smaller (44.5 × 8.1 μm) cheilocystidia. Complete description and illustrations, including photographs and line drawings, are presented.

Introduction

The family Inocybaceae Jülich (Basidiomycota, Agaricales) is one of the most taxonomically diverse families of Agaricales. Its members are ectomycorrhizal, forming symbiotic associations with more than 23 families of vascular plants in tropical and temperate areas [1]. The family Inocybaceae was revised based on a six-loci phylogenetic analysis and divided into seven genera within the monophyletic family Inocybaceae i.e. Auritella Matheny & Bougher, Inocybe (Fr.) Fr., Inosperma (Kühner) Matheny & Esteve-Rav., Mallocybe (Kuyper) Matheny, Vizzini & Esteve-Rav., Nothocybe Matheny & K.P.D. Latha, Pseudosperma Matheny & Esteve-Rav. and Tubariomyces Esteve-Rav. & Matheny [1].

The genus Inosperma is characterized by a radially fibrillose and rimose or squamulose pileus; even or bulbous stipe base, context often reddening when bruised, phaseoliform basidiospores, hyaline or necropigmented basidia, cheilocystidia with cyanophilous contents in some species and absence of pleurocystidia. In addition, many species of Inosperma have distinct odours that may be fruity, pleasant honey, fishy, or pelargonium [1]. Multigene (ITS, nrLSU, RPB2, and mtSSU) molecular studies have confirmed that the genus is monophyletic and is classified into six major lineages, the Cervicolores clade, Maculatum clade, I. misakaense lineage, I. africanum lineage, and two Old World tropical clades [2–5].

Inosperma is represented by more than 70 known species that are distributed in Africa, Asia, Australasia, Europe, North America and Northern South America [1] and number is increasing [6–8]. From Asia, a few number of Inosperma species have been described as 12 species from China [5, 9, 10, 11) and 6 species from India as Inocybe in Inosperma clade [12, 13]. The only one species i.e. Inosperma shawarensis Aïgnon & Naseer, comb. nov. has been reported from oak forests of Pakistan [14].

During macrofungal survey from different parts of the Himalayan forests of Azad Jammu and Kashmir, Pakistan, a novel species of Inosperma was discovered. In this paper, we present our collections as a new species with brief diagnosis, an extensive description, and phylogenetic analyses based on combined ITS, LSU and RPB2 sequences data.

Materials and methods

All specimens were processed and deposited in Universitat Wien Herbarium, University of Vienna, Austria and LAH herbarium, Institute of Botany, University of the Punjab, Lahore, Pakistan. Macro-morphological descriptions were based on field notes and color photos of basidiomata. Color codes followed Munsell Soil Color Chart [15]. Micro-morphological structures are based on description of dried specimens [11, 16]. Line drawings were made with the aid of a drawing tube at 100X. Microscopic features (basidiospores, basidia, Cheilocystidia, Caulocystidia, Pileipellis, Stipitipellis, Clamp connections), measurements and drawings were obtained from slide preparations stained with Cotton Blue, Melzer’s reagent and 5% KOH solution. In the descriptions of basidiospores, we followed Naseer et al. methodology [17]. For DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and DNA sequencing, protocols of Naseer et al. (2023) have been followed [17]. Genomic DNA was extracted from portions of lamellae following a modified CTAB extraction method (Bruns 1995). Primers used for amplification of the ITS marker were ITS1F (Gardes & Bruns 1993) and ITS4 (White et al. 1990); For LSU, LR0R and LR7 (Vilgalys & Hester, 1990); for the RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (rpb2) region, bRPB2-6 F and bRPB2-7.1R were used (Matheny 2005). PCR was performed using a 10 µL reaction volume composed of 5 µL Biomed 2×Taq Plus PCR MasterMix (Biomed, Beijing, China), 3.9 µL ddH2O, 0.3 µL of each primer (10 µmol/ L), and 0.5 µL DNA template. Successful amplicons were purified using an enzymatic PCR cleanup (Werle et al. 1994) as described in Voglmayr & Jaklitsch (2008). DNA was cycle-sequenced using the ABI PRISM Big Dye Terminator Cycle Sequencing Ready Reaction Kit v. 3.1 (Applied Biosystems, Warrington, UK). Sequencing was performed on an automated DNA sequencer (ABI 3730xl Genetic Analyzer, Applied Biosystems). Chromatograms were checked and assembled using SeqmanII v.5.07 (Dnastar Inc.).

Generated sequences have been submitted in GenBank at NCBI. The following accession numbers have been obtained: ITS, LSU and RPB2 GenBank entry numbers for the holotype are PQ449774, PQ449776 and PQ584605 respectively, while the ITS GenBank accession number for the additional material examined is PQ452890. Phylogenetic analyses have been performed according to Li et al. [11]. For the tree reconstruction based on Bayesian inference, the program MrBayes 3.2.7 [18] was used with two parallel Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains with 10 million generations, saving every 1000th tree. The first 25% of the sampled trees was discarded as burn-in and 50% percent majority rule tree was generated along with posterior probabilities (PPs) of ≥ 0.95 by FigTree v. 1.4.2 [19].

Results

Phylogenetic analyses (Fig. 1)

The consensus sequences were obtained by assembling both forward and reverse primers using BioEdit 7.2.5 (Hall 1999). The consensus ITS sequences obtained from Pakistani collections were identical and showed 90.6% similarity with a sequence (MH930182) labeled “Inosperma cervicolor (Pers.) Matheny & Esteve-Rav. ”. The combined dataset included 2589 nucleotide sites with 830 bp ITS, 1194 bp nrLSU, and 565 bp RPB2 of 108 taxa, including gaps. The final dataset was deposited in TreeBase. The bestfit evolutionary models of three genes are all the same as the GTR + I + G model. The BI phylogenetic analysis was stopped after 5,400,000 generations, when the average standard deviation of split frequencies converged to 0.009874. The following models selected using the BIC were used for the ML analysis, which yielded a final log-likelihood value of -29640.629. The topography generated from BI and ML analysis were similar, and hence only the ML tree was provided (Fig. 1).

Etymology:—The specific epithet kashmiranum refers to type locality, Kashmir.

Diagnosis:— Inosperma kashmiranum is characterized by purplish brown to grayish brown pileus, fibrillose, rimose to squamulose towards margin, smaller (12.1 × 6.9 μm) basidiospores and smaller (44.5 × 8.1 μm) cheilocystidia.

Holotype:— Pakistan, Azad Jammu and Kashmir, Bagh District, Nari, at 2,125 m a.s.l., in mixed forest of Pinus wallichiana, Cedrus deodara on soil, solitary, 29 August 2021, Rubab Khurshid, N-277 (LAH38340), GenBank accession number for ITS: PQ449774, GenBank accession number for LSU: PQ449776.GenBank accession number for RPB2: PQ584605.

Description:—Basidiomata small sized. Pileus 2–2.5 cm in diam., convex, prominently depressed in the center, surface dry, dull, purplish brown (5.2YR4/4) to grayish brown (5YR4/2), dark brown (5YR3/4) scales covering the pileus, rimose to fine squamulose at margins, inflexed or slightly undulate margins; context moderately thick, purplish. Lamellae well defined, adnexed, margins even or entire, regular, crowded, purple to dark brownish. Discussion

Inosperma kashmiranum can be characterized by purplish brown to grayish brown pileus, fibrillose, rimose to squamulose towards margin, small (12.1 × 6.9 μm) basidiospores and small (44.5 × 8.1 μm) cheilocystidia.

The closely related species Inosperma mucidiolens (Grund & D.E. Stuntz) Matheny & Esteve-Rav. can be differentiated from Inosperma kashmiranum by size and color of pileus and stipe. Inosperma mucidiolens from America is characterized by its larger (10–40 mm), coffee brown to reddish brown pileus and longer stipe (90 mm) while I. kashmiranum has smaller (25 mm), purplish brown to grayish brown pileus and smaller (42 mm) stipe. Moreover, I. kashmiranum differs by its basidiospores size (12.1 × 6.9 μm). Both species share similar oblong to cylindrical basidiospore and share same habitat under conifers. Inosperma kashmiranum differs from Inosperma geraniodorum (J. Favre) Matheny & Esteve-Rav. in pileus morphology, basidiospores and cheilocystidia size. The pileus in Inosperma geraniodorum is dark brown with squamulose on entire pileus while Inosperma kashmiranum has purplish brown to grayish brown pileus that is rimose to squamulose only towards margin and having a central depression in pileus. Inosperma geraniodorum has larger (13.6 × 7.9 μm in Inosperma geraniodorum verses 12.1 × 6.9 μm in Inosperma kashmiranum) basidiospores and larger (51.3 × 13.8 μm) cheilocystidia.

Inosperma turietoense Pancorbo & Esteve-Rav., another closely related species, can be distinguished by Inosperma kashmiranum by larger pileus (up to 30 mm) and stipe (70 mm). Inosperma turietoense has been reported in mixed beech (Fagus sylvatica) and fir (Abies alba) forests, in the Buxo–Fagetum while Inosperma kashmiranum has been collected from pine dominating coniferous forests. Inosperma veliferum, a strictly alpine species differ in ITS sequence data (about 25 base pairs) and are phylogenetically distinct.

Inosperma longisporum can be differentiated by larger (upto 3.6 cm), oak brown to coffee brown pileus that has clamp scales in center, wooly fibrillose to coarsely fibrillose towards margins. Inosperma kashmiranum has smaller (2.5 cm), purplish brown to grayish brown pileus with rimose to squamulose scales towards margin. Stipe is unique in Inosperma longisporum with large (up to 8.5 cm) in size, few furfuraceous scales at apex, with some loose fibrils toward the base while Inosperma kashmiranum has short (up to 4.6 cm) stipe that is fibrillose at apex becoming coarsely squamulose to squarrose in lower half. Anatomically Inosperma longisporum has longer (13.3 μm) basidiospores and smaller (19–36 μm) cheilocystidia. On other hand, Inosperma kashmiranum has smaller (12.1 μm) basidiospores and larger (44.5 μm) cheilocystidia.

Inosperma kashmiranum differs morphologically from the phylogenetically (based on ITS data) related species I squamulosobrunneum that is characterized by oak brown to brown pileus with presence of erect conical squamules around the center, outward more upswept fibrils subsquarrose to squamulose. However, our species can be easily distinguished by purplish brown to grayish brown pileus, presence of central depression in pileus with rimose to squamulose towards margin. Furthermore, our species has larger (12.1 × 6.9 μm) basidiospores, larger (50.3 × 9.5 μm) basidia and larger (44.5 × 8.1 μm) cheilocystidia. Both species share same host i.e. Pine forests.

Inosperma calamistratum can be distinguished from I. kashmiranum in its pileus, which has dark brown squamules or recurvate scales at the center, contrasting with grayish brown background, larger basidiomata and longer spores (10.5– 12.3 × 5.3–5.9 μm) (Kuyper, 1986). Similarly, Inosperma cervicolor differs in its larger fruit bodies and spores (10.9–13.8 × 6.7–7.8 μm) (Kuyper, 1986). Inosperma squamulosohinnuleum recently described from China can be differentiated from I. kashmiranum by coarser, longer, granular and sparsely arranged scales in the pileus (Li et al. 2022).

The only one previously reported Inosperma species from Pakistan i.e. Inosperma shawarense can be easily distinguished by its convex pileus, gray gills, silvery lower stipe and dark gray upper stipe. Further it was collected from pure oak forests of Swat, Pakistan. With description of this species, total number of Inosperma species has been increased to two (See Fig. 4).
