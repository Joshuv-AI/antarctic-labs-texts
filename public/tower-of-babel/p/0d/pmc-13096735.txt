Long-term outcomes after septal reduction therapy for obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Andreas Schelldorfer, Rahel Laager, Laurent M Haegeli, Philipp Schuetz, Alexander Kutz, H Yakup Yakupoglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsWith cardiac myosin inhibitors emerging as a novel pharmacological option instead of septal reduction therapies (SRT) in obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), contemporary data on national long-term outcomes after SRT are needed.Methods and resultsIn this nationwide cohort study from 2015 to 2021, patients with obstructive HCM undergoing SRT (surgical myectomy or transcoronary ablation of septal hypertrophy) were 1:8 propensity score-matched with non-obstructive HCM patients or surgical controls undergoing appendectomy without heart disease in recent records. As assessed in time-to-event analyses, the primary outcome was a composite of all-cause mortality after discharge and rehospitalization for heart failure. After matching, 125 patients with obstructive HCM hospitalized for SRT were compared to 743 patients hospitalized with non-obstructive HCM. The incidence rate (IR) of the primary outcome was lower in the SRT group {12.62 vs. 74.86 per 1000 patient-years (py); hazard ratio (HR), 0.17 [95% confidence interval (CI), 0.07–0.42]; median follow-up 31 months}. In the second comparison, 126 patients with obstructive HCM undergoing SRT were matched to 560 surgical controls without heart disease undergoing laparoscopic appendectomy. The IR of the primary outcome after SRT was comparable to that of surgical controls without heart disease [12.61 vs. 8.09 per 1000 py; HR, 1.53 (95% CI, 0.56–4.18); median follow-up 41 months].ConclusionIn this nationwide cohort study, SRT was associated with a lower incidence of all-cause mortality after discharge and rehospitalization for heart failure compared to patients hospitalized with non-obstructive HCM, with an incidence comparable to surgical controls without heart disease.Keywords: Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Septal reduction therapy, Nationwide, Outcomes, Follow-up

Introduction

In patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) who remain symptomatic despite guideline-directed management and therapy, septal reduction therapy (SRT) to relieve left ventricular outflow tract obstruction is recommended in eligible patients performed at experienced centres.1,2 Ventricular surgical myectomy (Morrow procedure) is the most commonly performed surgical procedure to treat obstructive HCM.2,3 Transcoronary ablation of septal hypertrophy (TASH) and more specifically alcohol septal ablation (ASA) is the primary non-surgical alternative to myectomy and has even become the primary SRT modality in many centres, especially for patients who are not optimal candidates for surgical therapy.1–3 Alternative TASH techniques have been reported in small numbers of patients, but remain experimental.4–14 Both ASA and surgical myectomy are reported to have a high degree of safety and achieve comparable outcomes regarding improvement of symptoms, gradient reduction, and exercise capacity in experienced centres.2,15–18 While a systematic review and meta-analysis reports long-term mortality after ASA and myectomy to be similarly low,15 recent data suggest ASA to be associated with a higher long-term all-cause mortality compared with surgical myectomy.1,19,20 Of note, both the risk of pacemaker implantation and reoperation are higher after ASA.15–18,21 Importantly, the mentioned favourable outcomes after SRT are probably largely based on outcomes of high-volume HCM centres, since especially for surgical myectomy low-volume centres are reported to have a higher in-hospital mortality compared to high-volume centres.22–24

Recent data comparing long-term outcomes after SRT to control populations are limited. Patients after SRT are reported to have a survival exceeding non-operated symptomatic obstructive HCM patients.18,25 The long-term mortality of patients who underwent SRT is reported to be comparable to age- and sex-matched US populations.25,26 Further, SRT is associated with a reduced risk of a composite of acute cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality during the first year post-SRT compared to patients with HCM who never underwent SRT.27

While EXPLORER-HCM28 recently showed that cardiac myosin inhibitors are a promising pharmacological therapy for symptomatic obstructive HCM, VALOR-HCM29–33 further confirmed their effectiveness in even more symptomatic patients with an indication for SRT, providing an alternative to invasive septal reduction therapies in obstructive HCM. Contemporary national data on long-term outcomes after SRT compared to control populations are needed in order to allow an informed decision regarding pharmacological and invasive treatment of obstructive HCM in future practice.

We therefore sought to assess current long-term outcomes after SRT for obstructive HCM compared to controls with and without HCM in a retrospective population-based cohort study.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective population-based study was conducted using a claims-based dataset of hospitalizations in Switzerland between 2012 and 2021, provided by the Swiss Federal Statistical Office (Neuchâtel, Switzerland). The hospitalization data were sourced from the nationwide compulsory full census of Swiss hospitals. This dataset includes all Swiss inpatient discharge records from public as well as private acute care, general, and special clinics for both paediatric and adult patients. Individual-level data on demographics, hospital typology, healthcare utilization, medical diagnoses, diagnostics, clinical procedures, and in-hospital patient outcomes were provided. Since hospitalization records were linked to Swiss national death records, data on out-of-hospital deaths were also provided. A multi-step pseudonymization procedure and the usage of a unique patient identifier to ascertain rehospitalizations ensure patient confidentiality. Medical diagnoses were coded using the International Classification of Diseases version 10, German Modification (ICD-10-GM) codes. Diagnostic tests and clinical procedures were coded using the Swiss Operations Classification (Schweizerische Operationsklassifikation, CHOP), versions 2012-2021. The Institutional Review Board of Northwestern Switzerland approved this study and waived informed patient consent owing to the use of deidentified data (EKNZ Project-ID: Req-2021-01397). No written informed consent was needed as the data was unidentifiable due to pseudonymization. This study adheres to the ‘Strengthening The Reporting of OBservational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE)’ statement.34

Patient population and eligibility criteria

Our objective was to compare outcomes in patients with obstructive HCM who underwent SRT with those in a control group of patients with non-obstructive HCM who did not receive SRT, and with a second control group of individuals without any known heart disease. The second control group included patients after laparoscopic appendectomy with no heart disease in recent records. Patients who underwent laparoscopic appendectomy were selected as a control population because they constitute a large, relatively healthy cohort spanning all ages and both sexes who experienced an acute inpatient hospitalization.

Eligible patients in the intervention group had a first-time (i.e. first in the dataset) index acute care adult hospitalization for SRT (CHOP codes 35.35.10 for surgical myectomy and 00.66.35 for TASH) with obstructive HCM at any position in the discharge report (ICD-10-GM code I42.1). Patients eligible for the non-obstructive HCM control group had a first-time (index) adult hospitalization with non-obstructive HCM at any position (ICD-10-GM code I42.2) and no obstructive HCM during this hospitalization. Patients eligible for the surgical control group had a first-time (index) adult hospitalization for laparoscopic appendectomy (CHOP-code 47.01). In each of the two comparisons (i.e. SRT vs. non-obstructive controls and SRT vs. surgical controls), patients included in the intervention group were not eligible for the control group, respectively. The selection of ICD-10 codes was in line with previous studies.20,27,35–37

In a further step, we excluded patients who died during index hospitalization across all study groups. Because this dataset does not provide time information within a hospitalization, these patients could not be included in the time-to-event analysis. In-hospital mortality prior to the application of exclusion criteria was reported for all study groups in the Supplementary material. Next, we excluded those with <3 years of washout period prior to index hospitalization; patients with <1 month of observable post-discharge follow-up (i.e. patients with an index hospitalization in the last month of the observation period), thereby ensuring adequate time-at-risk; those who underwent SRT or unspecified selective embolization procedures of thoracic vessels within 3 years prior to the index hospitalization; and patients who experienced cardiac events included in the study endpoints (i.e. hospitalization for heart failure at primary or any position, cardiac arrest, heart transplant) within 3 months prior to the index hospitalization. In the non-obstructive HCM control group, we excluded patients who underwent SRT or unspecified selective embolization procedures of thoracic vessels during the index hospitalization and those who had a hospitalization with a diagnosis of obstructive HCM within 3 years prior to the index hospitalization. Furthermore, patients in the surgical control group who had a cardiac event during index hospitalization or within the 3 years prior were excluded (Supplementary material online, Table S1, Figure 1A and B and Supplementary material online, Figure S1A and B).

Baseline characteristics and outcomes

Administrative codes for patient characteristics extracted from the discharge report at baseline are detailed in Supplementary material online, Table S2. Supplementary information from the index hospitalization discharge report included the presence of selected cardiac devices at baseline as well as additional in-hospital outcomes, such as further characteristics of the index hospitalization and selected device implantations (administrative codes in Supplementary material online, Tables S2 and S3). Ancillary data on in-hospital mortality, collected prior to the application of study exclusion criteria and therefore not part of the primary analysis, were also reported.

The primary endpoint was a composite of all-cause mortality and rehospitalization for heart failure. Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality, rehospitalization for heart failure, rehospitalization with heart failure, rehospitalization with cardiac arrest, heart transplant, and all-cause rehospitalization (administrative codes in Supplementary material online, Table S4). Primary and secondary outcomes were assessed during follow-up (i.e. after discharge from the index hospitalization). Patients were followed until occurrence of the outcome of interest, death, or end of available data. Information on emigration was not available in the dataset. Therefore, individuals who permanently left Switzerland could not be identified.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for baseline patient characteristics. To account for the heterogeneity of the two control populations, we performed propensity score matching for each of the two comparisons (SRT vs. non-obstructive HCM controls and SRT vs. surgical controls), respectively.

After application of eligibility criteria, patients with obstructive HCM who underwent first-time SRT were 1:8 propensity score matched to eligible patients with an index hospitalization with non-obstructive HCM. Matching variables included age, sex, heart failure, ischaemic heart disease, atrial fibrillation or flutter, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, obesity, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral artery disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hepatopathy, thyroid disease, and the Elixhauser comorbidity score. Comorbidities with a clear causal link to the treatment were not used as matching variables. In a second comparison between patients after SRT and surgical controls after appendectomy with no known heart disease, we performed 1:8 propensity score matching using age and sex as matching variables.

This ‘one-to-many’ propensity score matching approach was chosen to increase precision [i.e. narrower confidence intervals (CI)] and generalizability while ensuring adequate covariate balance between the two groups in the first comparison (SRT vs. non-obstructive HCM controls).38 In the second comparison between patients after SRT and surgical controls, the same matching approach was used to ensure methodological consistency across both comparisons.

The propensity scores, which were separately estimated for each comparison (SRT vs. non-obstructive HCM controls and SRT vs. surgical controls, respectively), were used to match patients after SRT to respective nearest neighbour controls within a calliper width of 0.01 on the propensity scale.39 We assessed covariate balance before and after propensity score matching by computing absolute standardized differences. An absolute standardized difference of <0.10 was considered as an adequate balance between groups.40 For each outcome, incidence rates (IR) and incidence rate differences (IRD) per 1000 person-years (py), as well as hazard ratios (HR)—using Cox proportional hazard models—were calculated, with corresponding 95% CI, respectively. Kaplan–Meier failure estimates were used to illustrate differences in time to outcome, and log-rank tests to assess potential differences between the curves. The proportional hazards assumption for the primary endpoint was tested using scaled Schoenfeld residuals,41 whereby no violation could be detected.

To assess the robustness of our study’s results and to strengthen the conclusions, we conducted two sensitivity analyses. The first sensitivity analysis tested the reliability of our definition of an index SRT hospitalization. We explored the impact of potential misclassification with a broader definition and assessed the loss of external validity using a more restrictive patient selection. In this sensitivity analysis, we defined the intervention group more narrowly, restricting index SRT hospitalizations to first-time adult hospitalizations for SRT where obstructive HCM was the primary diagnosis, rather than appearing in any position on the discharge report as in the main analysis.

The second sensitivity analysis addressed the possibility that patients categorized as having non-obstructive HCM might have experienced episodes of obstruction prior to the index hospitalization (before the washout period) or could develop obstruction subsequently. In this sensitivity analysis, we applied stricter exclusion criteria for the non-obstructive HCM controls, eliminating any patient with a history of hospitalization for obstructive HCM at any time within the dataset (i.e. before or after the index hospitalization).

Both sensitivity analyses used the same propensity score matching parameters as the main analysis to maintain consistency in methodology.

All tests were two-sided, have not been adjusted for multiple testing, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA, version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC).

Results

Study population

The full dataset included 6 225 879 identifiable individual patients hospitalized in Switzerland between 1 January 2012 and 31 December 2021 (Figure 1A and B). After application of eligibility criteria and propensity score matching of patients after SRT with non-obstructive HCM controls, the study population included 125 patients who had a first-time hospitalization for SRT and obstructive HCM (median age of 65, 47.2% female) and 743 non-obstructive HCM controls (median age 66, 45.6% female). Baseline demographic variables and comorbidities included in the propensity score were well balanced after matching (Table 1). In the second comparison, the study population included 126 patients after SRT (median age 64, 47.6% female) and 560 surgical controls (median age 64, 48.6% female) after propensity score matching for age and sex (Table 2). Age- and sex-matched surgical controls had a lower overall comorbidity burden, resulting in a lower Elixhauser comorbidity score. Among SRT procedures, surgical myectomy and TASH were performed in roughly equal proportions (Tables 1 and 2). Approximately one-third of patients who underwent a surgical myectomy also had a concomitant mitral valve procedure (repair or replacement). Primary and secondary outcomes

In the first comparison, over a median follow-up of 31 months, the IR of the primary outcome was 12.62 per 1000 py for patients after SRT and 74.86 per 1000 py for non-obstructive HCM controls, corresponding to an IRD of −62.24 (95% CI, −78.38 to −46.10) per 1000 py and a HR of 0.17 (95% CI, 0.07–0.42). These findings were confirmed by the Kaplan–Meier failure estimate curve (see Supplementary material online, Table S11, Figure 2). Among secondary outcomes, the IR of all-cause mortality, rehospitalization for heart failure as primary diagnosis and at any position, as well as cardiac arrest, heart transplant, and all-cause rehospitalization were lower among patients after SRT compared to non-obstructive HCM controls, respectively (see Supplementary material online, Table S11).

Abbreviations: HR, hazard ratio; CI, confidence interval; SRT, septal reduction therapy; HCM, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

In the second comparison, over a median follow-up of 41 months, the IR of the primary outcome was 12.61/1000 py for patients after SRT and 8.09/1000 py for the surgical controls, with an IRD of 4.52 (95% CI, −7.22 to 16.27) and a HR of 1.53 (95% CI, 0.56–4.18). These findings were in line with the Kaplan-Meier failure estimate curve (see Supplementary material online, Table S12, Figure 3), which showed a comparable cumulative incidence over time [P-value (log-rank) 0.40]. Curves cross in the late follow-up, which may be attributed to low patient numbers in the last years of follow-up in the SRT group. No violation of the proportional hazards assumption for the primary endpoint was detected in both comparisons (SRT vs. non-obstructive HCM controls and SRT vs. surgical controls), respectively. Regarding secondary outcomes, we found a higher rate of rehospitalization with a diagnosis of heart failure at any position in the discharge report and a higher rate of all-cause rehospitalizations in patients after SRT compared to surgical controls (see Supplementary material online, Table S12). In all other secondary endpoints, outcomes between groups exhibited no significant differences, and notably, there were no heart transplants recorded in either group.

Sensitivity analyses

In the first sensitivity analysis, the intervention group was defined as patients with a first-time adult hospitalization for SRT with obstructive HCM as the primary diagnosis (instead of a diagnosis at any position in the discharge report) in both comparisons (SRT vs. non-obstructive HCM controls and SRT vs. surgical controls), respectively. The results remained consistent with the main analysis (see Supplementary material online, Tables S13–S16, Supplementary material online, Figures S2 and S3). In the first comparison between patients after SRT and non-obstructive HCM controls, primary endpoint outcomes did not relevantly differ from the main analysis. Propensity score matching was applied as in the main analysis to ensure consistency, but slight imbalances were observed, likely due to smaller sample sizes. In the second comparison between patients after SRT and surgical controls, the results remained similarly robust, with comparable rates of the primary endpoint between groups.

In the second sensitivity analysis, stricter exclusion criteria were applied to the non-obstructive HCM controls, excluding all patients with any prior or subsequent hospitalization with obstructive HCM in the dataset. The results remained consistent with those of the main analysis, with no relevant difference in the outcomes of the primary endpoint (see Supplementary material online, Tables S17 and S18, Supplementary material online, Figure S4).

Discussion

The present population-based cohort study provides contemporary nationwide data on long-term outcomes of patients who underwent SRT for obstructive HCM in Switzerland, compared to two control populations. Over a follow-up of up to 7 years, SRT patients showed a lower rate of all-cause mortality after discharge and rehospitalization for heart failure compared to patients hospitalized with non-obstructive HCM, with outcomes comparable to surgical controls without heart disease.

Cardiac myosin inhibitors provide a potent non-invasive treatment option for symptomatic obstructive HCM that may reduce the need for invasive SRT,42 despite the historically reported high safety and low early and long-term mortality for both myectomy and ASA.15,16,18,19,26,43 In this context, current guidelines and expert statements recommending that SRT be performed at high-volume HCM centres gain even more importance to maintain low complication rates both in the short- and long-term.1–3

This study included an equal distribution of myectomy and TASH. While in some studies TASH was reported to be less common than myectomy,18,19,26 a prospective registry reported similar baseline rates of myectomy (4.9%) and ASA (4.0%) among enrolled HCM patients,44 consistent with our findings.

In our study, before matching, 17% of patients who underwent surgical myectomy had a concomitant mitral valve repair, and 13% had a mitral valve replacement, whereas ∼70% of those patients underwent surgical myectomy alone. A study using US inpatient data from 2005 to 2020 reported that 13.2% of myectomies had a concomitant mitral valve repair, 18.4% had a concomitant mitral valve replacement, and 68.2% were myectomies performed without additional mitral valve procedures, in line with our findings.45

The individual components of the primary composite endpoint consistently showed lower rates of all-cause mortality and rehospitalization for heart failure after SRT compared to non-obstructive HCM controls, and similar rates to those observed in surgical controls with no heart disease in recent records.

We report an overall post-discharge all-cause mortality of 2.4% after SRT, with a median follow-up of 31 months (SRT vs. non-obstructive HCM controls) and 41 months (SRT vs. surgical controls), respectively. In line with these findings, a recent retrospective study with a similar design reported a mortality of 2.0% after ASA and 2.9% after myectomy over a comparable follow-up period.26 A systematic review and meta-analysis reported a long-term mortality of 1.5% per year after ASA and 1.4% per year after myectomy, aligning with our results.15 Similarly, a study from 2024 reported an absolute mortality rate of 0.6% per year after SRT in a cohort including paediatric patients.18 Notably, the present study excluded patients who died during the index hospitalization, inherent to its design as a propensity-score matched time-to-event analysis focusing on long-term outcomes. This may reduce comparability to other studies that include periprocedural mortality.

We observed a substantially lower post-discharge all-cause mortality after SRT compared to matched non-obstructive HCM controls (2.4% vs. 17.4%, respectively). However, two observational studies showed no significant difference in long-term survival when comparing patients after SRT to those with non-obstructive HCM.25,46 More consistent with our results, a study comparing patients who underwent SRT to patients with HCM who never underwent SRT found a reduced risk of a composite of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality during the first post-SRT year (OR 0.54 at 0–160 days and OR 0.33 at 181–360 days, respectively).27 Importantly, a significant distinction compared to the mentioned studies is that our study included a non-obstructive HCM group consisting of hospitalized patients diagnosed with non-obstructive HCM at any position in the discharge report, with ∼30% of them also diagnosed with heart failure before matching. Despite propensity score matching for key demographics and comorbidities, these patients were likely a sicker population, possibly in a more advanced stage of non-obstructive HCM, compared to the non-obstructive HCM patients in the aforementioned studies. This may, at least in part, explain the discrepancy regarding differences in mortality rates. Therefore, the reported effect sizes of outcomes after SRT compared to non-obstructive HCM controls should be interpreted in this context.

Post-discharge all-cause mortality after SRT was not different from that in an age- and sex-matched control population of patients after laparoscopic appendectomy with no heart disease in recent records. This finding is consistent with studies comparing outcomes after SRT to age- and sex-matched US control populations.25,26,47

Data on the burden of rehospitalizations for heart failure after SRT are limited. The large 2024 SHARE registry study18 on outcomes after SRT in paediatric and adult patients reported 13% of patients experiencing an advanced heart failure composite outcome (a composite of cardiac transplantation, implantation of a left ventricular assist device, LVEF <35% or new occurrence of NYHA class III or IV symptoms) over a median follow-up of 5 years, but increasing progressively from 1.5% at 1 year to 6% at 10 years.18 In the present study, 1.6% of patients who underwent SRT were rehospitalized for heart failure (primary diagnosis), though with a much shorter median follow-up. The comparability of these results is limited by differences in the chosen heart failure endpoints and follow-up duration.

We report lower rates of rehospitalizations for heart failure both as the primary diagnosis and as a diagnosis at any position in the discharge report after SRT compared to controls with non-obstructive HCM. A potential explanation is that the hospitalized non-obstructive HCM controls included in this study may represent a more advanced stage of non-obstructive HCM, whereas patients in the SRT group were likely hospitalized in an elective setting. While patients with non-obstructive HCM are reported to experience a mostly stable clinical course with relatively low risk for progressive heart failure, ∼10% of these patients may develop advanced heart failure refractory to drug therapy, with heart transplant being the only definitive treatment option.48,49

Rates of rehospitalization for heart failure as a primary diagnosis were similar to those of surgical controls with no heart disease in recent records. However, patients after SRT were more frequently rehospitalized with a diagnosis of heart failure at any position in the discharge report compared to surgical controls. These rehospitalizations may be attributed to patients with persistent chronic heart failure symptoms despite undergoing SRT, which may have been recorded as a secondary diagnosis of heart failure in future hospitalizations. Alternatively, asymptomatic patients post-SRT may have been misclassified based on heart failure symptoms documented prior to the procedure, resulting in overreporting of secondary heart failure diagnoses during follow-up. Therefore, we believe that rehospitalizations with heart failure as the primary diagnosis more accurately reflect the burden of heart failure rehospitalizations.

Rehospitalizations with cardiac arrest were rare, with no events after SRT compared to 1.7% in the non-obstructive HCM group and 0.2% in the surgical control group, respectively, which is consistent with previous data on (aborted) sudden cardiac death during long-term follow-up after SRT.15,26,46 The recent SHARE registry study18 reported 1.7% of patients after SRT to have experienced cardiac arrest, albeit over a much longer median follow-up, which limits comparability.18 The generally low rate of sudden cardiac death in patients who underwent SRT may be attributed to optimal risk stratification and the use of implantable cardioverter defibrillators in this population.

This study has limitations. First, using administrative claims data in cohort studies inherently carries the risk of misclassification and/or underreporting of diagnoses and medical procedures. Regarding ICD codes, we were able to draw comparison to population-based studies using similar codes and to validation studies of ICD codes.20,27,35–37 However, since population-based studies are rare in Switzerland, no validation studies of CHOP codes are currently available. Second, since CHOP codes are primarily designed for billing purposes and SRT are infrequent procedures, there are no specific codes for SRT. For this reason, we consulted with Swiss medical coding experts to determine how surgical myectomy and TASH are coded. To enhance the precision of our patient selection, only those who underwent SRT during a hospitalization with a diagnosis of obstructive HCM, a well-validated ICD-10 code, were eligible for the SRT groups. Furthermore, we conducted a sensitivity analysis applying a more restrictive definition of the intervention group, including only patients who underwent SRT with a primary diagnosis of obstructive HCM, which yielded consistent results. Based on these measures, we are confident that the risk of misclassification regarding SRT is low. Third, given that left ventricular outflow tract obstruction can either develop from initially non-obstructive HCM or improve, such as after medical therapy, some patients classified as non-obstructive in this analysis may have been diagnosed with obstructive HCM either before or after their index non-obstructive HCM hospitalization. To address this, we excluded patients with obstructive HCM hospitalizations within 3 years prior to the index hospitalization in the non-obstructive HCM control group. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with any obstructive HCM hospitalization within this dataset from the non-obstructive HCM control group produced consistent results. Fourth, although we applied propensity score matching to adjust for baseline characteristics in the first comparison (SRT vs. non-obstructive HCM controls) and for sex and age in the second comparison (SRT vs. surgical controls), residual confounding by unmeasured or not fully measured characteristics in claims data (e.g. vital signs or laboratory results) cannot be entirely excluded. Fifth, we acknowledge that the non-obstructive HCM control group may represent a sicker population compared to those described in previous studies, despite propensity-score matching. Accordingly, while we highlight the favourable outcomes observed after SRT relative to this group, the magnitude of the effect size should be interpreted within this specific context. Sixth, the employed washout period of 3 years to exclude a history of SRT in all groups, obstructive HCM in the non-obstructive HCM control group, and any heart disease in the surgical control group does not exclude the possibility of patients experiencing such events beyond the washout period. Nonetheless, we believe that further extending the washout period would not impact the interpretation of our results but would compromise external validity by necessitating stricter patient selection. Seventh, stratification by SRT procedure type was not performed due to sample size constraints. Eighth, information on emigration was not available in the dataset. Individuals who permanently left Switzerland during follow-up could therefore remain under observation without contributing further events, potentially leading to under-ascertainment of outcomes occurring abroad. However, given the advanced age and comorbidity burden of the matched study population, substantial emigration is unlikely. In addition, there is no indication that emigration would differ systematically between the matched study groups. Ninth, excluding patients who died during the index hospitalization may introduce survivor bias and underestimate early procedural risk, but it was necessary given the structure of the dataset. In-hospital mortality prior to application of study exclusion criteria was reported. Tenth, due to inherent limitations of claims data, granular clinical information (such as preprocedural NYHA classification, left ventricular outflow tract gradient, left ventricular function, or cardiac biomarkers) was not available, which may affect risk adjustment and the interpretation of outcomes. Finally, patients undergoing SRT were more frequently treated at university hospitals, likely reflecting referral patterns for specialized interventions and also guideline recommendations for treatment at experienced centres. This concentration may introduce centre-related bias and residual confounding; however, it also reflects real-world practice in Switzerland, where expertise in SRT is typically available at university hospitals and select larger private centres.

Conclusion

Over a follow-up period of up to 7 years, patients who underwent SRT were associated with a lower incidence of a composite of all-cause mortality after discharge and rehospitalization for heart failure compared to patients hospitalized with non-obstructive HCM. The incidence was comparable to that of surgical controls without heart disease. Our nationwide data suggest that contemporary SRT is a therapeutic option with favourable long-term outcomes. With the increasing use of cardiac myosin inhibitors and the expected decline in SRT volume, future studies should assess whether favourable SRT outcomes are maintained and how they compare with novel therapies.
