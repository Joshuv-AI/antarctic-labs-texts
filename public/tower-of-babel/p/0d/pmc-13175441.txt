A Psychometric Analysis of the Haemophilia Quality of Life Questionnaire for Adults Physical Health Subscale and Total Scores: Data From the Phase 3 XTEND‐1 Trial in Patients With Severe Haemophilia A
Authors: Jérôme Msihid, Alfonso Iorio, Amanda Wilson, Nana Kragh, Lauren Nelson, Shanshan Qin, Annemieke Willemze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionBleeding episodes can impair physical functioning and cause chronic pain in haemophilia A. The Haemophilia Quality of Life Questionnaire for Adults (Haem‐A‐QoL) was administered in XTEND‐1; the Physical Health (PH) subscale evaluated Physical Health/Functioning.AimTo evaluate psychometric properties of Haem‐A‐QoL PH and estimate clinically meaningful change thresholds using XTEND‐1 data (NCT04161495).MethodsPreviously treated patients (aged ≥12 years) with severe haemophilia A received once‐weekly efanesoctocog alfa prophylaxis (50 IU/kg) for 52 weeks (Arm A; pre‐study prophylaxis), or on‐demand efanesoctocog alfa (50 IU/kg) for 26 weeks followed by 26 weeks once‐weekly prophylaxis (Arm B; pre‐study on‐demand). Psychometric analyses used pooled data (Arms A and B) to confirm test‐retest reliability, construct validity, and responsiveness of the Haem‐A‐QoL PH and total scores. Clinically meaningful change thresholds were estimated using data from Arm A.ResultsThe Haem‐A‐QoL PH items were completed by all patients in the psychometric sample (N = 126) at baseline and 120 patients (95%) at Week 52. Haem‐A‐QoL PH and Haem‐A‐QoL total scores demonstrated high internal consistency reliability, construct validity, acceptable test‐retest reliability, responsiveness, and discriminated between known groups at baseline and Week 52. The meaningful within‐patient change (MWPC) threshold estimate was −10.0 (range −15.0 to −8.7) for Haem‐A‐QoL PH and −8.5 (−8.5 to −4.6) for Haem‐A‐QoL total scores (using PGIS‐Joint). Respective proposed meaningful within‐group improvement thresholds were −6.8 (range −4.8 to −6.8) and −5.8 (−5.8 to −3.5).ConclusionsThe Haem‐A‐QoL PH is a reliable, valid, and responsive measure to assess the impact of interventions in the treatment of haemophilia A.Keywords: haemophilia A, haemophilia quality of life questionnaire for adults, Haem‐A‐QoL, Haem‐A‐QoL PH, physical health, psychometric

Introduction

Haemophilia A is a rare genetic disorder characterised by deficient activity of clotting factor VIII (FVIII) [1, 2, 3]. People with haemophilia experience frequent, traumatic, and spontaneous bleeding episodes in the joints and muscles that can lead to pain, swelling, and restriction of movement. Repeated bleeding into the joints may cause chronic musculoskeletal damage, chronic pain, impaired physical functioning, and reduced health‐related quality of life (HRQoL), including reduced ability to engage in or avoidance of physical activities such as sports or daily living activities [4, 5, 6]. The association between pain, physical health (PH) and functioning and a reduction in HRQoL is well known in people with haemophilia [7, 8, 9].

The current standard of care for haemophilia is prophylaxis with either standard half‐life FVIII, extended half‐life FVIII concentrates, or non‐factor replacement therapy [10, 11, 12]. People with haemophilia who are treated prophylactically may still experience spontaneous bleeding and develop haemophilic arthropathy [13, 14, 15, 16]. Once‐weekly efanesoctocog alfa, a first‐in‐class high‐sustained FVIII replacement therapy [17, 18, 19], was approved in February 2023 by the United States Food and Drug Administration for adults and children with haemophilia A, based on the results of the Phase 3 XTEND‐1 clinical trial (NCT04161495) [20]. Efanesoctocog alfa was approved by the European Medicines Agency in June 2023 [21].

In XTEND‐1, adults with severe haemophilia received either once‐weekly prophylaxis with efanesoctocog alfa (50 IU/kg) for 52 weeks, after a pre‐study prophylaxis regimen (Arm A), or on‐demand efanesoctocog alfa (50 IU/kg) for 26 weeks followed by once‐weekly efanesoctocog alfa prophylaxis (50 IU/kg) for 26 weeks (Arm B) after a pre‐study on‐demand regimen [22]. Efanesoctocog alfa prophylaxis provided superior protection from bleeding versus pre‐study prophylaxis and normal to near‐normal FVIII activity [22]. Prophylaxis with efanesoctocog alfa for 52 weeks also resulted in statistically significant improvements in PH (Haemophilia Quality of Life Questionnaire for Adults [Haem‐A‐QoL] assessment, Pain Intensity (Patient‐Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System [PROMIS] Pain Intensity–Short Form (SF) 3a instrument) assessment, and Joint Health (Hemophilia Joint Health Score [HJHS] assessment) [22]. Supplementary Table 1 shows details of other patient‐reported outcomes (PRO) measures and Clinician‐reported outcomes (ClinRO).

Haem‐A‐QoL is a 46‐item patient‐completed questionnaire for measuring HRQoL impairments attributed to haemophilia in the past 4 weeks, across 10 domains [23, 24, 25]. It was developed for adults with haemophilia [23] but has been extended for use in individuals aged ≥17 years [26].

Although the full Haem‐A‐QoL was administered in XTEND‐1, the Haem‐A‐QoL PH subscale was the primary focus of this analysis. This includes five items pertaining to the frequency of haemophilia‐related pain and physical/mobility difficulties in the past 4 weeks: painful swelling, joint pain, ability to move without pain, ability to walk desired distance, and time needed to get ready. Items are scored on a 5‐point scale ranging from “never” (1) to “all the time” (5). Haem‐A‐QoL PH and total raw scores are computed as the sum of the item responses (for five and 46 items, respectively) and raw scores are transformed into a scale from 0 to 100, where higher values represent poorer HRQoL.

Evidence from the literature supports the content validity of the Haem‐A‐QoL PH and its use in patients with severe haemophilia A. Previously published responder thresholds, proposed to characterise improvement at the patient level, range from 8.9 to 10 points [26, 27].

Using data from XTEND‐1, psychometric analyses were conducted to confirm the reliability, validity, and ability to detect change, and to estimate meaningful within‐patient change (MWPC) and within‐group change (MWGC) thresholds for improvement for the Haem‐A‐QoL PH and total scores in patients on FVIII prophylaxis.

Materials and Methods

Psychometric Analysis

Haem‐A‐QoL evaluated patients’ HRQoL in relation to their haemophilia at baseline (Day 1), Week 26, and Week 52 of the XTEND‐1 study. The psychometric analysis sample included all participants who received ≥1 dose of the study intervention and had ≥1 evaluable Haem‐A‐QoL data point at baseline and Week 26 or Week 52 (end of study).

Details of PROs and ClinROs and their assessment timepoints, with annualised bleeding rate (ABR) and annualised joint bleeding rate (AJBR), included as supporting measures, are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Psychometric analyses used pooled data from Arms A and B of XTEND‐1. Clinically meaningful change thresholds were estimated using data from patients in Arm A.

Results

Descriptive Statistics

The psychometric analysis sample pooled from Arm A (N = 103) to Arm B (N = 23) included 126 adult patients aged ≥17 years. Patient‐level characteristics are presented in Table 2. The mean (standard deviation [SD]) age at study entry was 39.1 (13.37) years, with 99.2% (n = 125) males. Median age at the start of first prophylaxis regimen was 1 year old (range = 0–62 years).

At baseline, all patients completed the five PH items; at Week 52, 95% (n = 120; pooled Arm A and B) of participants had completed the PH items. Across baseline, Week 26, and Week 52, all patients with ≥1 response to the 46 items at corresponding timepoints completed the five PH items. For the total score, all patients with ≥1 response to the 46 items at corresponding timepoints completed more than 50% (23 out of 46) of all items.

No ceiling and floor effects were observed across the five PH items; the percentages of the best (score 1 “Never”) or worst (score 5, “All the time”) responses were less than the selected 40% cutoff (twice the expected probability for a 5‐point ordinal item level response of 20%). The mean (SD) PH score at baseline (pooled Arms A and B) was 38.9 (23.60). The full range of 0 (best) to 100 (worst) of the PH score was used, with a ceiling percentage of 95.5% and floor percentage of 0.8%, both less than the 15% cutoff. This indicated no problematic ceiling or floor effects at the domain level also. The mean (SD) change in Haem‐A‐QoL PH score from baseline to Week 52 was −10.3 (20.60), showing an overall trend of improving PH scores.

The mean (SD) Haem‐A‐QoL total score (pooled Arms A and B) at baseline was 32.6 (17.29). The ceiling and floor percentages were both less than the 15% cutoff, indicating no problematic ceiling or floor effects. By Week 52, the mean total score declined by −6.6 (SD = 12.57), also showing a trend of improvement on HRQoL related to haemophilia.

Inter‐Item and Item‐Domain Correlations (PH Domain)

All items showed strong correlations (absolute correlations >0.50) with most other items in the PH subscale at Week 26; all item‐domain correlations were strong, ranging from 0.55 to 0.79 at baseline. Similar results were observed at Week 52 (Supplementary Table 2).

Internal Consistency Reliability

Cronbach's alphas were 0.86 for the PH at both baseline and Week 52, within the 0.70–0.90 range demonstrating acceptable internal consistency reliability. Cronbach's alphas for the total score (based on using all 46 items) at baseline and Week 52 were 0.95 and 0.96, above the recommended range.

Test‐Retest Reliability

For the PH score, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) of 0.85 (95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 0.77 to 0.90 in the stable Patient Global Impression of Severity (PGIS)‐Joint subgroup, n = 80) and 0.78 (95% CI: 0.68 to 0.86 in the stable PGIS‐Activity subgroup, n = 72) exceeded 0.70, the recommended threshold for a multiple‐item scale, indicating acceptable test‐retest reliability. For the total score, the ICCs were 0.91 and 0.92, respectively, indicating high test‐retest reliability.

Construct Validity Evidence

There were strong (|r| ≥ 0.50) correlations between Haem‐A‐QoL PH and several supporting PROs at baseline and Week 52, including the EQ‐5D‐5L Pain/Discomfort scores (Supplementary Table 3). Strong correlations were observed between the Haem‐A‐QoL PH (higher scores reflect poorer PH) and the PROMIS‐SF Physical Function 6b T‐score, Haemophilia Activities List (HAL) total, and Euro Quality of Life Group's Visual Analog Scale (EQ‐VAS) scores (in all, lower scores reflect poorer PH) at baseline and Week 52. Moderate to strong (|r| ≥ 0.30) positive correlations were observed with Haemophilia Joint Health Score (HJHS) total score at baseline and Week 52, but as anticipated the magnitude of the correlations with this ClinRO were smaller than the correlations observed with PRO measures. Correlations with ABR and AJBR were weak.

Known‐Groups Validity Evidence

Construct validity was further demonstrated by the ability of the PH scores and total scores to discriminate between known groups defined by levels of PGIS and EQ‐5D‐5L items at baseline and Week 52 (Table 3). In each case, higher mean PH scores (reflecting poorer PH) were observed for subgroups with poorer PGIS‐Joint, PGIS‐Activity, EQ‐5D‐5L Mobility, Usual Activities, and Pain/Discomfort ratings. At both timepoints, between‐group differences were statistically significant for all omnibus tests (all P < 0.0001) and for most pairwise tests (P < 0.05, except for a few pairs of adjacent groups).

Responsiveness (Ability to Detect Change)

Correlations between the Haem‐A‐QoL PH change from baseline scores and the supporting measure change scores were computed at Week 26 and Week 52 (Supplementary Table 4). Moderate to strong (|r| ≥ 0.30) correlations were observed between change from baseline in Haem‐A‐QoL PH and change from baseline in PGIS‐Joint, PROMIS‐SF Pain Interference 6a T‐score, PROMIS‐SF Physical Function 6a T‐score, HAL total, and EQ‐5D‐5L Mobility, Usual Activities, Pain/Discomfort items at Week 26 and Week 52. At Week 52, moderate responsiveness correlations were also observed with the PGIS‐Activity change (r = 0.46) and PGIC‐Overall (r = 0.41). Weaker correlations were observed with ClinROs and clinical measures, as expected. A similar pattern of correlations was observed for the Haem‐A‐QoL total score (Supplementary Table 4).

Haem‐A‐QoL PH and total score change followed anticipated patterns across responsive subgroups (change in PGIS‐Joint, PGIS‐Activity, PGIC, and EQ‐5D‐5L items at Week 26 and Week 52) (Supplementary Table 5). The patterns of mean changes across groups were in the anticipated direction, showing greater improvement in mean Haem‐A‐QoL PH and total score change for groups with more improvement in these supporting measures. Between‐group differences on the Haem‐A‐QoL PH change scores were statistically significant for almost all omnibus tests (all P < 0.01, except for PGIS‐Activity at Week 26).

Most of the overall effect‐size estimates of changes (based on the SD at baseline and the SD of change) in XTEND‐1 were moderate (effect size range: −0.51 to −0.43).

Interpretation of Change in the Haem‐A‐QoL PH and Total (Arm A)

Based on their adequate correlations with the change from baseline in Haem‐A‐QoL PH score at Week 52 (criterion: correlation ≥0.371), the disease‐specific PGIS‐Joint and PGIS‐Activity (r = 0.49 and 0.41) were selected as the primary anchor measures, with the PGIC‐Overall (r = 0.34) as a supportive anchor measure. For the Haem‐A‐QoL total score, the corresponding correlations were 0.36, 0.30, and 0.43. Therefore, the same anchor measures were used for the total score. Given that the disease‐specific patient‐reported global measures were adequately related, estimates based on the generic EQ‐5D‐5L items were not needed.

MWPC Thresholds for Haem‐A‐QoL PH and Total Scores in Arm A

The median PH change score among participants in Arm A achieving 1‐point improvements on the PGIS‐Joint and PGIS‐Activity were −10.0 (mean = −15.00) and −7.5 (mean = −12.29), respectively (Table 4). The distribution‐based half‐SD estimate (unadjusted for measurement precision) for PH score was almost 12 points, and the maximum SEM value was almost 9 points (Table 4). The median Haem‐A‐QoL total scores, in the same respective groups of participants, were −8.5 (mean = −7.6) and −6.5 (median = −6.5) (Table 4). The half‐SD estimate for total score was 8.8 points and the maximum standard error of the measurement (SEM) was 4.6 points (Table 4).

Considering the median changes among participants with 1‐point improvements on the PGIS‐Joint and PGIS‐Activity, as well as the maximum SEM, the estimated MWPC threshold range for the Haem‐A‐QoL PH was −15.0 to −8.7, with a point estimate of −10.0. A 10‐point or more reduction was achieved by 66% of the participants in the PGIS‐Joint 1‐point improvement group (Figure 1A).

MWGC Thresholds for Haem‐A‐QoL PH and Total Scores in Arm A

Mean changes from baseline in Haem‐A‐QoL PH scores in key subgroups defined by the anchor measures are presented in Table 4. The upper limits of the 95% CI of mean changes in the Haem‐A‐QoL PH score were −6.8 and −2.8 for the 1‐point improvement on PGIS‐Joint and PGIS‐Activity subgroups, respectively. The 0.2 SD of the Haem‐A‐QoL PH score was 4.8. Given that the change in PGIS‐Joint anchor is more appropriately related to Haem‐A‐QoL PH change scores than is PGIS‐Activity (i.e., overlapping eCDF curves), the proposed MWGC threshold range for the Haem‐A‐QoL PH is −6.8 to −4.8, with a point estimate of −6.8.

The upper limits of the 95% CI of mean changes in the Haem‐A‐QoL total score in the 1‐point improvement on PGIS‐Joint and PGIS‐Activity subgroups, respectively, were −2.0 and −1.4. The upper limit of the 95% CI in the “much improved” PGIC‐Overall subgroup was −1.5; the lower limit of the 95% CI in the “minimally improved” PGIC‐Overall subgroup was −5.8, and the 0.2 SD of the total score was 3.5. The proposed MWGC threshold range for the Haem‐A‐QoL total score is −5.8 to −3.5, with a point estimate of −5.8.

Discussion

Using data from the Phase 3 XTEND‐1 trial of efanesoctocog alfa in patients with severe haemophilia A, the present analysis focused on the psychometric properties and clinically meaningful change thresholds for the Haem‐A‐QoL PH. The use of PH in this population was reliable, valid, and able to detect change in physical functioning, suggesting that it is a fit‐for‐purpose measure to assess the impact of interventions in the treatment of haemophilia A.

Internal consistency reliability and test‐retest reliability of this score were adequate. Moderate‐to‐strong correlations between PH score and supporting outcome measures confirmed construct validity. The discriminating ability of PH score was further demonstrated by the anticipated score patterns across groups of participants defined by the categories of PGIS and EQ‐5D‐5L items at baseline and Week 52, with statistically significant between‐group differences. The responsiveness of PH was demonstrated by moderate‐to‐strong responsiveness correlations with several supporting PROs (PGIS‐Joint, PROMIS‐SF Pain Interference 6a T‐score, PROMIS‐SF Physical Function 6a T‐score, HAL total, and EQ‐5D‐5L Mobility, Usual Activities, Pain/Discomfort items at Week 26 and Week 52), anticipated patterns of mean changes by various subgroups, and the approximately moderate effect size of overall change as well as moderate‐to‐large effect sizes of key group pairs.

Published responder thresholds in different study contexts have previously characterised improvement at the patient‐level (MWPC) for the PH domain as an 8.9 to 10‐point reduction in score in individual patients using distribution‐based methods [27] or generic EQ‐5D‐3L as anchor measures [26]. Similarly, a 6.3 to 7‐point reduction in total score has been characterised as indicative of HRQoL benefit [26]. Results presented here support a MWPC threshold estimate of −10.0 (range −15.0 to −8.7) to characterise improvement in PH scores, which is in line with the previously published thresholds [26, 27, 28]. There were no published estimates identified as MWGC thresholds for the PH. To facilitate the interpretation of the secondary efficacy endpoint based on change from baseline in PH score to Week 52 in Arm A, the proposed MWGC threshold estimate was −6.8 (range; −6.8 to −4.8). In XTEND‐1, the least squares (LS) mean change in PH score (−6.74) (95% CI: −10.13 to −3.36) from baseline to Week 52 in Arm A [22] was thus clinically meaningful, meeting the within‐group threshold ranges identified in the present analysis.

Limitations

The generalisability of our data was confined by the patient sample, which included only adult participants with severe haemophilia A currently treated with FVIII products. In addition, the study was not blinded, and participants knew that they were on efanesoctocog alfa. The meaningful thresholds were estimated using the XTEND‐1 dataset, which could be reason to challenge the use of these thresholds to interpret the results of the same study. Due to a small number of participants reporting worsening and a limited number of patients with improvement, as per the anchors, only improvement thresholds could be explored. Finally, the timeframe for defining stable patients for test‐retest reliability was longer than optimal and limited to 26 weeks based on PRO administration in the trials.

Conclusions

Our data support the reliability and validity of the Haem‐A‐QoL PH and suggest that it is a fit‐for‐purpose measure for the assessment of PH in people with severe haemophilia A. The computed MWPC threshold was in line with previous published estimates, suggesting that it is a reasonable threshold for use in clinical practice. The results from this analysis support the use of the Haem‐A‐QoL in future studies to evaluate the impact of interventions in the treatment of haemophilia A.
