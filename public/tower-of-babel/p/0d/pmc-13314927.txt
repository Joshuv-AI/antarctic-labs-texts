The effect of delayed timing to surgery on the likelihood of surgical site infection for open metacarpal and phalangeal fractures
Authors: Tyler Williamson, Jordan Carter, Brandon Driggs, Katerina Papanikolaou, Christina Brady, Jason Coffman, Ryan Rose, Brian Sager
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe level of risk for surgical site infection (SSI) and complications associated with delayed timing to surgery (> 24 h) for open metacarpal (OMC) and phalangeal (OPH) fractures has not been outlined. The purpose of this study is to assess the effects of delayed operative intervention on the rate of perioperative SSI following OMC and OPH fractures.MethodsThis is a prognostic study querying a national database from 2015 to 2021 for patients undergoing surgical management of OMCs or OPHs. Delayed surgical intervention for these fractures was defined as greater than 24 h after injury. Primary outcome was 30-day SSI. ANOVA and multivariable logistic regression were used to assess associations with odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI), accounting for age, sex, BMI, and ASA.ResultsA total of 1,513 patients underwent surgical intervention [OMC (n = 259), OPH (n = 1,254)]. Of those, 1,330 patients (87.9%) were operatively managed within 24 h (delayed: n = 183, 12.1%). The mean time-to-surgery for delayed patients was 1.3 ± 0.9 days. There was no difference in rates of SSI between those treated within 24 h and the delayed group (OR: 0.80, CI: [0.18–3.43]) or when assessing timing-to-surgery as a continuous variable. There was no difference in rates of any complication, readmission, or reoperation between groups.ConclusionDelayed surgical intervention for selected open metacarpal and phalangeal fractures was not associated with increased rates of 30-day surgical site infection, readmission, reoperation, or overall complications. These findings suggest that carefully selected patients may be safely managed with modest delays to surgery when appropriate antibiotic administration, wound care, and follow-up are ensured.Level of EvidenceIII, Prognostic study.Keywords: Open fracture, Hand, Metacarpal, Phalanx, Infection, Time to surgery

Introduction

Open fractures are relatively common orthopaedic injuries, occurring at a rate of 30.7/100,000 person-years [1]. These fractures are associated with higher risks of complications, including infection, even with timely intervention [2, 3]. There have been several advancements in open fracture care overall, with the most important being early administration of intravenous antibiotics [4, 5]. This is also in conjunction with initial bedside irrigation and debridement (I&D) followed by an operative I&D within 12–24 h and continuation of antibiotics for a certain duration [4]. This general protocol was originally given to open long bone fractures and later adopted for the treatment of all open appendicular fractures at most centers [4, 5]. However, this full protocol may not necessarily affect the outcomes of all open fractures.

Hand injuries are among the most common injuries presenting to the emergency departments in the United States. Metacarpal and phalangeal fractures, in particular, occur at a rate of 80/100,000 person-years and make up approximately one-third of all hand fractures [6, 7]. These fractures are considered open approximately 5% of the time and are often associated with significant damage to surrounding soft tissues, which may require additional procedures and rehabilitation, leading to increased cost [2, 8, 9].

Open fractures of the hand have a particularly low infection rate compared to other anatomic locations, likely due to their relatively abundant blood supply [9]. This low incidence of infection has led recent studies to call the urgency of surgical intervention for these fractures into question [10]. Several studies have demonstrated no relation between surgical site infection and timing to operative intervention, with some demonstrating that a bedside debridement alone may be adequate [8–12]. However, many of these studies have been limited by heterogeneity, bias, and low sample size.

Therefore, the goal of this study was to utilize the large sample within a national database to assess associations of surgical timing for open metacarpal (OMC) and phalangeal (OPH) fractures with the development of surgical site infection (SSI) and other outcomes within 30 days. This study aims to characterize the effect of delay of treatment, defined as over 24 h from the time of injury, and its potential association with outcomes within 30 days. We hypothesize that delay of treatment would not be significantly associated with increased rates of SSI or other relevant outcomes. We hope these findings will allow healthcare organizations to more effectively triage surgical cases that need urgent intervention within 24 h and efficiently manage resources to minimize adverse outcomes and save health-related costs.

Methods

Data source

The American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS-NSQIP) is a national database consisting of reports from approximately 700 hospitals nationwide. The data used in this study were retrieved from the 2015–2021 ACS-NSQIP participant use data files and have been utilized in previously published studies [13].

Patient selection

Patients with an open phalangeal (OPH) or metacarpal (OMC) fracture undergoing irrigation and debridement (I&D) and operative fixation were included in the study. Diagnosis codes were defined by the International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision (ICD-9-CM) and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10-CM), with confirmation of ‘open’ included. Surgical management codes were defined using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes, including percutaneous and open surgical interventions. Exclusion criteria included patients with ipsilateral upper extremity fractures, concurrent infection-related diagnoses, and those without all variables needed for statistical analysis.

Population characteristics and preoperative variables

Baseline population characteristics were recorded and included age, sex, and race. Comorbidities included: Independent function, diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder, congestive heart failure, smoking status, dyspnea, hypertension, disseminated cancer, chronic steroid use, weight loss, body mass index (BMI), ASA grade, and bleeding disorders. Frailty was measured by the 5-factor modified frailty index (mFI-5).

Outcomes and complications

The primary outcome of this study was 30-day surgical site infection (SSI) following surgical intervention for open phalangeal and metacarpal fractures. The primary predictor was delay in surgical debridement and fixation. This was defined both as a continuous variable (time of injury to time of surgery), as well as within and greater than 24 h. Secondary outcomes included: wound dehiscence, unplanned readmission, unplanned reoperation, and major complications within 30 days.

Statistical analysis

Baseline continuous demographic variables were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR). Preoperative comorbidities and 30-day outcome variables were reported as incidence. Initial chi-square and Student’s t-test comparisons were performed between those undergoing intervention before and after 24 h from the time of injury. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the associations between timing to surgery as a continuous variable and binary outcomes. Multivariate analysis was performed, accounting for age, sex, BMI, and ASA, to detect possible associations of operative timing with 30-day outcomes, reported as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI). In all testing, significance was established a priori for odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CI) exclusive of 1.0 and p < .05.

Results

After implementing inclusion criteria, a total of 1,513 patients underwent surgical debridement and fixation for open phalangeal and metacarpal fractures. There were 259 patients with open metacarpal fractures (OMC) and 1,254 with open phalangeal fractures (OPH). Of the total 1,513 patients, 1,330 patients (87.9%) were taken to the operating room within 24 h from time of injury, while 183 patients (12.1%) underwent delayed operative debridement and fixation. The mean time-to-surgery for delayed patients was 1.30 ± 0.90 days (range 1–7 days). Delayed patients were more often female with higher ASA, but were similar in age, BMI, and comorbidities (Table 1).

Comparison of within-24-hours and delayed patients

Multivariate analysis was performed, accounting for age, sex, BMI, and ASA, to assess associations between delayed surgical intervention and SSI, as well as secondary outcomes. When accounting for these variables, there was no difference in the likelihood of SSI between those treated within 24 h and the delayed group (OR: 0.80, CI: [0.18–3.43]). When assessing timing-to-surgery as a continuous variable, there was no association between increased surgical timing and likelihood of infection (OR: 1.05, CI: [0.55–2.01]). Regarding secondary outcomes, there was no difference in rates of any complication, readmission, or reoperation within 30 days between groups (all p>.4; Table 3). When assessing metacarpal and phalangeal fractures separately, delayed intervention was not significantly associated with SSI or any other outcome in these sub-cohorts (all p>.3).

Discussion

We investigated the timing of surgical intervention for open metacarpal and phalangeal fractures regarding surgical site infection development and other relevant outcomes within 30 days. In doing so, we found no association between increased infection rate and delayed operative management of open phalangeal or metacarpal fractures, and those who were managed operatively within 24 h. In addition, there was no difference in overall complications, readmission rates, or reoperation rates. Although prior single-center studies and systematic reviews have suggested that delayed operative intervention may not increase infection risk following open hand fractures, the current study provides external validation of these findings within a large contemporary multicenter national cohort. By evaluating over 1,500 surgically managed open metacarpal and phalangeal fractures, this study represents one of the largest analyses on this topic and supports the growing body of evidence suggesting that selected open hand fractures may not require emergent operative management.

The management of open fractures of the hand is controversial, with no agreed-upon treatment guidelines. There are several factors that may play a role in infection rates, including the extent of the soft tissue injury, type and degree of contamination, timing and type of antibiotic administration, and timing of operative intervention [3, 9]. Our findings are supported by previous studies demonstrating that delayed operative treatment of open hand fractures may not increase the rate of surgical site infection [3, 5, 9, 14]. Ng et al. found that early antibiotic administration in the emergency department is the most important factor in predicting postoperative infection in open fractures overall [5]. However, Sim et al. found no relation between time to antibiotics and infection rates specifically in open hand fractures [9]. Therefore, the recommendations of this authorship are to devise an individualized plan for each patient incorporating the patient’s history, injury characteristics, degree and type of contamination, reliability in follow-up, and shared decision making to guide timing of surgical intervention.

An important consideration is the relatively short duration of delay observed within our cohort. Although delayed treatment was defined as operative intervention beyond 24 h, the mean time-to-surgery among delayed patients was only 1.3 ± 0.9 days, indicating that most patients underwent surgery within approximately 48 h of injury. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as evidence supporting the safety of modest delays in appropriately selected patients rather than prolonged delays extending several days or weeks after injury. Additional investigation is necessary to determine whether substantially longer delays influence infection risk or other complications.

With this idea, selected open metacarpal and phalangeal fractures with limited contamination and without severe soft tissue compromise may potentially be temporarily stabilized in the emergency department, provided with a prescription of prophylactic oral antibiotics, and discharged if a capable surgeon is not readily available. Likewise, these principles may allow for more urgent inpatient procedures to be performed in a timely manner without forcing the hospital’s hand to delay those surgeries in order to manage open hand fractures. Additionally, this may allow for decreased direct transfers for non-emergent hand trauma. A recent study found that up to 66% of transfers for hand injuries to a Level I trauma center were deemed inappropriate for emergent transfer, costing certain centers over $225,000 [15, 16]. With appropriate emergency department management as noted previously, these patients may be allowed to follow up on an urgent basis, allowing for significant savings to the healthcare system as well as to the patient. Additionally, previous studies have demonstrated that hand surgical fellowship training can improve outcomes and decrease complications [17]. Therefore, delaying operative intervention until there is a hand fellowship-trained surgeon available may improve outcomes and allow for appropriate definitive management of these often-complex injuries.

This study has several limitations. First, this is a database study and as such, is susceptible to coding and data handling errors. Second, patients presenting to hospitals not participating in the NSQIP with complications would not have been captured, and this may underestimate the true incidence of complications. A relatively small number of patients received delayed surgical intervention, which may be due to several reasons not available to us in the database and may introduce an element of selection bias. Although this represents one of the largest multicenter cohorts evaluating this question, only 26 surgical site infections occurred during the study period. Consequently, confidence intervals surrounding the effect estimates remain relatively wide, and smaller differences in infection risk between groups cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant association should not be interpreted as definitive equivalence between treatment strategies. The NSQIP database does not provide information regarding Gustilo classification, contamination severity, fracture complexity, crush mechanisms, vascular injury, tendon injury, or the extent of soft tissue compromise. Consequently, we were unable to stratify patients by injury severity. It is possible that patients with more severe injuries were preferentially treated earlier, introducing selection bias. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted cautiously and may not be applicable to severe open hand injuries or heavily contaminated wounds. Lastly, this database only comments on 30-day outcomes, and there may be events outside of this perioperative window that may affect the recommendations and conclusions from this report.

Conclusion

Delayed surgical management of selected open metacarpal and phalangeal fractures beyond 24 h was not associated with increased rates of 30-day surgical site infection, complications, readmission, or reoperation. These findings suggest that carefully selected patients may be safely managed with modest delays to operative intervention when appropriate wound care, antibiotic administration, patient selection, and follow-up are ensured. However, these results should not be generalized to heavily contaminated injuries, severe soft tissue injuries, or fractures requiring emergent intervention.
