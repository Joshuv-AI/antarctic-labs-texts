Assessing the Benefits of Rapid Start Antiretroviral Therapy for Newly Diagnosed People with HIV in the United States
Authors: Patrick S Sullivan, Cillian Copeland, James Jarrett, Uche Mordi, Nikos Kotsopoulos, Rui Martins, Hansel E Tookes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInternational guidelines emphasize the need for earlier commencement of antiretroviral therapy (ART) among people with HIV (PWH). Reducing the time between HIV diagnosis and ART initiation can improve health outcomes, reduce healthcare utilization, and reduce HIV transmissions. This study evaluated the clinical and economic benefits associated with increasing uptake of rapid start ART among newly diagnosed PWH.MethodsA state transition disease model was developed in the United States setting to evaluate the benefits from earlier initiation of ART. The base case analysis compared two cohorts of 1000 newly diagnosed PWH: one following current patterns of ART initiation, and a counterfactual cohort where those receiving rapid start ART was doubled. Individuals were classified by different CD4 states at diagnosis and over time with viral suppression rates also being tracked. ART and CD4 state-specific healthcare costs were estimated over a 3-year time horizon. Averted HIV transmissions were calculated and used to estimate lifetime healthcare cost savings while CD4-specific mortality was also calculated. Several scenario analyses explored alternate assumptions related to the time at which PWH started ART after diagnosis.ResultsDoubling the proportion of newly diagnosed PWH receiving rapid start ART averted 7 HIV transmissions and 0.3 deaths per 1000 people, corresponding to numbers needed to treat of 141 and 3502, respectively. This leads to cost savings resulting from reduced healthcare resource use and lifetime cost savings from preventing new HIV transmissions.ConclusionReducing the time between HIV diagnosis and ART initiation can provide clinical and economic benefits by eliminating transmissions that might occur while individuals are viremic but not on treatment. The additional costs of providing ART required for this increase achieve high levels of return when considering the lifetime healthcare cost burden of onward HIV transmissions potentially averted by early ART start.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-025-03356-8.Keywords: HIV, Antiretroviral therapy, Rapid start ART, Economic evaluation, Viral suppression

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? International guidelines have emphasized the need for earlier initiation of antiretroviral therapy (ART) among people with HIV (PWH).Although earlier initiation of ART has been shown to improve health outcomes, reduce healthcare utilization, and reduce HIV transmissions, delays between HIV diagnosis and ART commencement remain an ongoing challenge.Using a state-transition disease model, this study evaluated the impact of increasing uptake of rapid start ART on healthcare costs, mortality, and onward HIV transmissions within the United States (US) context. What was learned from the study? Doubling the current rate of rapid start ART uptake among newly diagnosed PWH led to estimated reductions in HIV-related mortality and onward transmissions, with numbers needed to treat (NNT) to avoid 1 transmission and 1 death of 141 and 3502, respectively.This study extends existing research on the benefits of rapid start ART by demonstrating how earlier treatment initiation can generate both clinical and economic benefits within the US context.

Introduction

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) revolutionized management of the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, transforming a previously fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition [1–3]. Improved ART efficacy and access have led to substantial declines in AIDS-related deaths and new infections, contributing to improved health outcomes and public health benefits [3–5]. In the United States (US), ART treatment for people living with HIV has been central to national HIV strategies, playing a critical role in efforts to achieve the goals of the Ending the HIV Epidemic initiative [2, 6].

Advances in ART formulations have resulted in the availability of simplified regimens that offer improved tolerability, reduced pill burden and side effects, and ultimately enhanced adherence to therapy [7–9]. Newer ART options include single-tablet regimens, long-acting injectable therapies, and improved drug formulations that minimize the risk of developing resistance [10, 11]. Expanding access to user-friendly ART options is critical to ensuring newly diagnosed people with HIV (PWH) begin treatment as early as possible and are sustained on therapy, maximizing clinical and economic benefits [9].

International guidelines emphasize the benefits of rapid ART initiation and recommend that treatment be started as soon as possible after diagnosis due to demonstrable benefits in improving viral suppression, retention in care, long-term health outcomes, and survival [3, 12–15]. These recommendations reflect a growing evidence that rapid ART initiation confers individual and population-level benefits, reinforcing its role as a cornerstone of contemporary HIV management [13]. Intervals between HIV diagnosis and receipt of ART treatment have decreased steadily over recent decades, reflecting increasing compliance with guidelines [5, 16, 17].

Late initiation of ART, on the other hand, can carry significant clinical, economic, survival, and public health consequences. Delaying ART start allows the progressive deterioration of individuals’ immune systems, increasing the risk of opportunistic infections, hospitalizations, and premature death [18]. In contrast, starting ART in people with higher CD4 counts (> 350 cells/mm2) facilitates goals for rapid viral suppression [19]. From an economic perspective, progressive HIV disease, marked by lower CD4 counts, is associated with higher healthcare resource consumption and costs [20, 21]. Failure to achieve viral suppression has important public health consequences, increasing the likelihood of PWH transmitting HIV to their susceptible partners. Delayed access to ART predominately affects socioeconomically disadvantaged groups [22–25], including but not limited to those with inadequate social support systems, unstable housing, and mental health issues. Inadequate health care staffing and insufficient professional expertise to identify people at higher risk of screening can also delay access to ART [25]. In the US, access and reimbursement challenges have led to geographical disparities in entry to care and the interval to ART start [22, 25].

Rapid start ART leads to a range of health benefits, including improved medication adherence [26], higher retention in HIV care [4, 26], and improved clinical and economic outcomes [14, 21, 27]. Despite these recognized benefits, delayed ART initiation is common in several regions worldwide [28] and in the US [20, 21]. An analysis of real-world Medicaid data reported that 36% of individuals initiated ART over 1 year after diagnosis, with less that one in seven (13%) starting within 14 days of their HIV diagnosis [21]. Public health interventions can and should be implemented to mitigate the barriers to prompt ART start; such interventions include immediate start programs (treat all campaigns), targeted outreach/peer navigation programs addressing social barriers, and structural changes to improve insurance approvals and reduce waiting times [29].

Despite the growing evidence regarding the clinical benefits of rapid start ART, few studies have explored the associated economic impact of these benefits within the US context [14]. Thus, we developed an economic model to link the clinical outcomes of rapid start ART with relevant economic costs in the US setting. The results should inform clinicians, healthcare policymakers and relevant stakeholders of the impact of improving rapid start ART uptake, with the ultimate goal of optimizing health outcomes for PWH, and with the associated benefits to resource allocation within the US healthcare system.

Methods

We developed an economic model in Microsoft Excel® to assess the impact of commencing ART at different time points following HIV diagnosis. A cohort-based Markov model with half-cycle correction was developed to assess the cost consequences of rapid start ART in individuals with newly diagnosed HIV infection. A 1-week cycle length was adopted to provide the necessary granularity to differentiate between individuals starting ART at different timepoints, including those initiating therapy within one week of diagnosis (i.e., rapid start). A 3-year time horizon was adopted for the analysis, as the objective of this study relates to patterns of ART commencement following initial HIV diagnosis, rather than the long-term implications of HIV infection and ART treatment.

We compared two hypothetical cohorts of newly diagnosed PWH: one with rates of ART start reflecting current practice, and a second having increased rates of rapid start ART, consistent with current treatment recommendations [10]. Each cohort was assumed to contain 1000 individuals, in line with standard practice in health economic modeling. The primary objective of the model was to link changes in CD4 count and viral suppression over time with reductions in HIV-related mortality and onward transmission, respectively. These outcomes were then linked with accrued healthcare costs, ART costs and savings from averted onward HIV transmissions from PWH who are virally suppressed [30]. Because this model was populated with published data and does not include any individual patient-level data, no ethics approval was required.

The model structure was developed around health states defined by CD4 count and viral suppression status, which are key outcomes in HIV and align with previous disease modeling in this therapeutic area [31–33]. Each hypothetical cohort contained 1000 newly diagnosed PWH distributed across four ART initiation categories, reflecting time from HIV diagnosis to ART initiation. Initiation times were developed from published data [5]; these categories were rapid start ART (< 1 week), and ART commencement at 3 weeks, 8 weeks, and 12 weeks. Published literature indicates clear variation in the time from diagnosis to starting ART in the US, with notable differences observed across different states of diagnosis and individual funding sources (i.e., Medicaid vs. commercial insurance) [20, 21]. Inputs for the main analysis were taken from an analysis of outcomes from a city-wide rapid start ART initiative in San Francisco [5]. These data provide optimistic estimates of ART treatment timing, with all individuals starting within 12 weeks of diagnosis. PWH covered by Medicaid are known to experience a considerably longer lag between diagnosis and starting ART compared to PWH with other insurance types [21]. This is particularly relevant, because Medicaid is a publicly funded health insurance program that serves as the largest source of coverage for PWH in the US [34]. Therefore, a separate analysis evaluated the impact of rapid start ART using prevalent commencement patterns among Medicaid-covered PWH.

All individuals were assumed to be viremic (i.e., no viral suppression, defined as viral load < 50 copies/mL) before treatment and, once started on ART, viral suppression trends over time were applied using published clinical data [35, 36]. Depending on treatment status, individuals could transition between viral suppression or viremic, and independently between five CD4 states. Each CD4 state was assigned a corresponding cost and linked to a weekly probability of severity-specific excess HIV mortality [37]. Viral suppression rates were used to calculate HIV transmissions occurring among non-virally suppressed individuals (i.e., individuals with a viral load ≥ 50 copies/mL) over time, based on the transmission risk acts occurring per model cycle and per-act transmission probabilities [38, 39]. A range of clinical and economic inputs were used to populate the model, with further details provided in Supplementary Appendices 1 and 2.

The first modeled cohort was assumed to reflect current standard of care, with 16.2% of people receiving rapid start ART, and 38.2%, 25.7%, and 20.0% starting at 3 weeks, 8 weeks, and 12 weeks, respectively, in line with published US data [5]. For the second cohort, the rate of rapid start ART was doubled to 32.4%, with the remaining categories adjusted proportionately. The projected uptake of rapid start ART in the US is associated with some uncertainty, due to factors such as geographic variation in HIV infrastructure and differences in access to prompt treatment between subgroups [16]. As such, the rate of rapid start ART was doubled in the early start cohort to show the relationship between higher rates of rapid start ART and relevant clinical and economic outcomes, rather than attempting to accurately project uptake of rapid start ART. Results were compared between cohorts to evaluate the marginal benefit of this increase in rapid start ART.

The model was used to generate clinical and economic outcomes, including mortality, time spent receiving treatment for HIV infection and its clinical consequences, time spent in different CD4 count categories, and number of onward transmissions, over a 3-year time period. Economic results were generated by applying a weekly healthcare utilization cost for each CD4 category, reflecting costs such as opportunistic infection prophylaxis, inpatient care, and outpatient care [40]. ART costs were also included [41], with a one-off cost of a new HIV transmission, reflecting lifetime excess healthcare costs for a PWH versus an individual without HIV [41]. Costs and outcomes related to ART and healthcare utilization were captured within the 3-year time horizon while costs related to HIV transmission were applied as one-off values, reflecting the lifetime cost of new HIV transmissions. Costs were inflated to the present day using relevant US indices [42], and discounted at an annual rate of 3% [43, 44]. Further details on cost inputs are provided in Supplementary Appendix 1. Several scenarios were explored to test model assumptions related to rapid start ART uptake and cohort size.

Results

Results for Base Case Analysis

Doubling the rate of rapid start ART generated reductions in both HIV excess mortality and new HIV transmissions, leading to net savings compared to current ART initiation patterns. Over a 3-year period in the hypothetical early start cohort, 7 HIV transmissions were averted by increasing rapid start, corresponding to a number needed to treat (NNT) to avoid 1 HIV transmission of 141. Similarly, to avoid 1 death over this period, we estimated an NNT of 3502.

Increasing rapid start ART led to an increase in ART costs of US$792 per person, because more individuals spent time on treatment when they were assumed to start ART earlier. This increased cost for person-months on ART was offset by health state cost savings, which reflect better maintenance of health in the context of improved CD4 counts. Lower costs of HIV transmission in the early start cohort reflect that fewer onward HIV transmissions occur with higher rates of rapid start ART.

Discussion

We evaluated the clinical and economic benefits of reducing time from HIV diagnosis to commencement of ART. Increasing rapid ART initiation was estimated to lead to reduced HIV-related mortality and reduced HIV transmissions from viremic individuals, demonstrating that the benefits of rapid start ART substantially outweigh the costs at both individual and public health levels. Although increasing rapid start ART was estimated to lead to an additional $792,220 in ART costs over the 3-year model horizon, these costs were estimated to be offset by savings from reduced healthcare use associated with lower CD4 counts and from averted HIV infections, representing savings of $62,248 and $8,231,767, respectively, per 1000 newly diagnosed PWH. This corresponds to a benefit cost ratio of 10.5, indicating a significant long-term return on investment with a return of $10.50 for every $1 spent when considering lifetime cost savings. Scenario analyses exploring different assumptions relating to cohort size and uptake of rapid start ART support the conclusions of the base case analysis.

An important consideration when interpreting our results is the discrepancy observed between different populations from both a geographic and funding (i.e., commercially insured vs. Medicaid-covered PWH) perspective when it comes to starting ART. The base case analysis uses results from a rapid start initiative in San Francisco, which is an area that is likely to have greater supports for PWH, such as increased HIV care sites and access to ART, compared to other regions of the US [46]. This is particularly relevant considering the recognized variations in HIV policy, including ART access, by state, with Southern States experiencing a disproportionately high burden caused by gaps in knowledge of HIV status and socioeconomic factors [45]. A study among Medicaid-covered PWH also presented a notably different view of baseline ART uptake: 36% of Medicaid-covered PWH initiated ART more than a year after their HIV diagnosis [21]. Thus, our base case assumptions are likely to be highly conservative estimates of the cost savings from increasing rapid start uptake, as shown by the increased cost savings observed in the Medicaid-covered population. This scenario assumed the latest timepoint individuals start ART is 1 year post-diagnosis and that rapid start ART occurs within 2 weeks of diagnosis, although over one-third of Medicaid-covered PWH have not started ART within 1 year of diagnosis [21].

There are several limitations associated with this analysis. Inputs for this study were sourced from US studies in individual cities, including San Francisco. The high variability in ART access and HIV infrastructure across states may limit the generalizability of results to all the states. However, it is likely that implementing rapid start programs in traditionally underserved states could yield even greater cost savings. The model structure used in this analysis included a range of costs related to ART, general healthcare utilization and onward transmission; however, the cost of implementing new rapid start policies was not considered. The marginal costs associated with earlier start of ART might be significant, especially in areas that historically have had limited HIV infrastructure, although these areas may reap the greatest benefits from timely access to ART. Similarly, there are also further cost savings that have not been incorporated into the model, such as those related to subsequent lines of onward transmission propagated by newly infected people (e.g., second-generation onward transmissions). Due to limited available evidence on time from diagnosis to starting ART, the model was also developed around four timepoints obtained from a published US study [5]. In practice, individuals can commence ART at any time point from diagnosis; however, our base case for initiation provides conservative results given the assumption that all newly diagnosed PWH would begin ART within 12 weeks of diagnosis.

We implemented a novel approach to capture the clinical and economic benefits from increasing the use of rapid start ART on CD4 counts and viral suppression rates over time. This was achieved through a reproducible modeling framework in an area that has limited published research. The model structure facilitated comparisons of different scenarios, reflecting varying estimates of rapid start ART uptake and different cohort sizes and compositions. This structure captures cost benefits for the individual PWH receiving treatment and reflects the wider public health benefits of reducing HIV transmission during the critical phase following diagnosis.

International guidelines advocate for starting ART as soon as possible following diagnosis [3, 13]; however, delays still occur in the time between HIV diagnosis and ART commencement in the US [5]. Starting ART as close to diagnosis as possible is important: early start improves health outcomes for people living with HIV [47], and, according to our model, starting ART even several weeks earlier averts onward HIV transmissions. The availability of treatments that can be started without genotype testing, restrictions to viral load, or among those with Hepatitis B virus (HBV) coinfection, facilitates same-day ART initiation. In addition, when considering underserved areas where retention in care can be challenging, having diagnosis and ART initiation on the same day can improve retention and make effective use of the point of contact between the HIV clinic and PWH [48].

Rapid start ART represents an important component of broader HIV policy; however, it should be considered alongside other policy mechanisms such as those related to HIV prevention, screening and diagnosis. The US Ending the HIV Epidemic initiative has targeted a reduction in HIV incidence of 75% and 90% by 2025 and 2030, respectively [6]. Key challenges facing achievement of these policy targets include addressing underserved groups who experience disproportionately high rates of HIV and taking parallel steps to increase awareness of HIV status among PWH [6]. Focusing on both improving HIV diagnosis rates and increasing rapid start ART for individuals newly diagnosed with HIV could represent a significant step forward in reducing HIV transmission and progress towards US HIV policy targets and reducing societal costs of supporting the health of PWH.

Conclusions

Prompt treatment of HIV following diagnosis improves the health of people living with HIV, averts onward transmissions of the virus, and saves medical and societal costs. We used a novel approach to evaluate the clinical and economic benefits of increasing uptake of rapid start ART among newly diagnosed PWH. Although reducing time between diagnosis and starting ART results in more time spent on ART and more associated costs for treatment, considerable returns will be achieved in the health of people living with HIV, and by averting HIV transmissions that occur while PWH are not virally suppressed. As such, rapid start represents a critical and cost-saving component of HIV policy. Successfully scaling up earlier access to ART will require concerted efforts, particularly within the historically underserved areas of the US with high incidence.
