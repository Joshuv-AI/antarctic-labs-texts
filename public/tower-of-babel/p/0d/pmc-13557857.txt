Prognostic value of SUVpeak and prognostic nutritional index in patients with pleural mesothelioma treated with platinum-based chemotherapy
Authors: Yoshiki Kato, Kosuke Hashimoto, Hisao Imai, Kyoichi Kaira, Reina Ishii, Naohito Hashimoto, Ou Yamaguchi, Atsuto Mouri, Hiroshi Kagamu, Ichiei Kuji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPleural mesothelioma (PM) is an aggressive malignancy with poor prognosis, and reliable prognostic biomarkers are needed to better inform clinical management. We retrospectively investigated the prognostic value of the Glasgow prognostic score (GPS), prognostic nutritional index (PNI), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), body mass index (BMI), and tumor glucose metabolism measured by 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients with PM treated with first-line platinum-pemetrexed chemotherapy at our institution (April 2007–December 2023; data cutoff, December 2024) and evaluated the prognostic value of these markers and baseline FDG-PET parameters.ResultsAmong 79 patients diagnosed with PM, 51 who received first-line platinum-based chemotherapy with pemetrexed were analyzed. The median age was 69 (range, 42–81) years, 78% were male, and 67% had epithelioid histology. The objective response rate (ORR) was 27.5% [95% confidence interval (CI), 17.1–40.9%], and the disease control rate (DCR) was 72.5% (95% CI, 59.1–82.9%). The median progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were 5.8 (95% CI, 3.4–7.8) and 21.8 (95% CI, 13.4–33.5) months, respectively. Low PNI (≤45) was significantly associated with shorter OS than high PNI (>45) (18.8 vs. 28.5 months; P=0.04) and remained an independent prognostic factor in multivariable analysis (P=0.04). For 35 patients who underwent pretreatment 18F-FDG-PET, high peak standardized uptake value (SUVpeak) (>7.73) was significantly associated with worse OS than low SUVpeak (9.1 vs. 33.6 months; P<0.001) and remained an independent predictor in the multivariable analysis (P<0.001). None of the evaluated biomarkers significantly predicted PFS. GPS, NLR, and BMI showed no significant association with survival outcomes in the univariate analysis and were therefore not included in the multivariable models.ConclusionsLow PNI and high SUVpeak significantly predicted poor OS in patients with PM treated with platinum-based chemotherapy. These factors may serve as practical prognostic indicators in this population.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Low prognostic nutritional index (PNI) (≤45) was an independent prognostic factor for worse overall survival (OS) (hazard ratio: 2.04, P=0.04) in patients with pleural mesothelioma (PM) treated with platinum-pemetrexed chemotherapy.

• High peak standardized uptake value (SUVpeak) (>7.73) on baseline 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) was associated with worse OS. Both maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) and SUVpeak were prognostic on univariate analysis.

• Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) was the only independent predictor of progression-free survival, whereas PNI and SUVpeak predicted long-term survival but not early progression.

What is known and what is new?

• PNI and FDG-PET parameters have been individually evaluated as prognostic markers in various malignancies, but their concurrent assessment in patients with PM treated with platinum-pemetrexed chemotherapy has not been reported.

• To our knowledge, this study suggests that SUVpeak may serve as an independent prognostic biomarker in this setting, and that PNI and SUVpeak capture complementary biological dimensions—host systemic status and tumor metabolic activity.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• PNI, as a readily available blood-based marker, combined with SUVpeak from routine staging PET/computed tomography (CT), may provide clinicians with practical tools for risk stratification and personalized treatment planning.

• Integrating both systemic and imaging biomarkers may enable more precise prognostic assessment and may help identify risk groups that could be explored in future clinical trials.

Introduction

Pleural mesothelioma (PM) is a rare but aggressive malignancy primarily associated with asbestos exposure. It is typically diagnosed at an advanced stage, responds poorly to treatment, and consequently has an extremely poor prognosis; the median overall survival (OS) is often reported to be less than 1 year (1-4). The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors has expanded the therapeutic options, but reliable prognostic markers to guide treatment decisions and stratify patients in clinical practice remain lacking. Simple blood-based biomarkers such as the prognostic nutritional index (PNI), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and Glasgow prognostic score (GPS) have gained attention as indicators of host nutritional and inflammatory status in various malignancies (5-10). In parallel, imaging biomarkers derived from 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG-PET/CT), particularly the maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) and peak standardized uptake value (SUVpeak), have been investigated as predictors of tumor aggressiveness and clinical outcomes (11-17).

However, the prognostic significance of these parameters for PM remains unclear, and few studies have simultaneously assessed both systemic and imaging biomarkers in a single cohort (18). The relationship between host nutritional/inflammatory status and tumor glucose metabolism in mesothelioma has been understudied despite the potential clinical relevance of integrating systemic and imaging biomarkers for more comprehensive prognostic assessment. Moreover, the rarity of PM limits the availability of the large, homogeneous datasets needed to establish reliable prognostic markers, specifically for patients treated with chemotherapy.

Platinum-based chemotherapy combined with pemetrexed has been the standard first-line treatment for advanced PM for over a decade, with the addition of bevacizumab improving outcomes in selected patients (2-4,19). Despite the recent introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors as first-line therapy, platinum–pemetrexed remains widely used and represents a well-established treatment backbone for evaluating prognostic biomarkers. Moreover, the homogeneous nature of this regimen allows for a more reliable assessment of biomarker performance without the confounding effects of different treatment modalities.

The aim of this retrospective study was to assess the prognostic value of nutritional and inflammatory biomarkers as well as 18F-FDG-PET parameters in patients with PM who were treated with platinum- and pemetrexed-based chemotherapy to provide insights that may be applicable to contemporary treatment strategies. We present this article in accordance with the REMARK reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0393/rc).

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective, single-center cohort study was conducted at the International Medical Center of Saitama Medical University. We reviewed the medical records of patients who were pathologically diagnosed with PM between April 2007 and December 2023. Of the initial 79 identified patients, 28 were excluded (14 received first-line immune checkpoint inhibitors and 14 received other first-line treatments), and the data of the remaining 51 who received first-line platinum-based chemotherapy combined with pemetrexed were included in the analysis. The database was locked for analysis in December 2024, allowing an adequate follow-up duration for survival outcomes. Figure 1 illustrates the patient selection process. Seven patients had undergone surgery and three had received radiotherapy as curative-intent treatment for PM before the analyzed platinum-pemetrexed chemotherapy; none received palliative radiotherapy, and in these patients the analyzed regimen was given for post-treatment recurrence. Six of the seven surgical patients had also received intrapleural chemotherapy (pleural perfusion therapy). Patients with clinical stage I disease who did not undergo surgery were considered unresectable or unfit for surgery on the basis of performance status, comorbidity, or patient preference. A sensitivity analysis excluding these nine patients was performed (see section “Results”).

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Saitama Medical University (No. KOKU 2024-146) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Treatment and evaluation

Patients typically received cisplatin (75 mg/m2) or carboplatin [area under the curve (AUC) 5] in combination with pemetrexed (500 mg/m2) administered every 21 days. Bevacizumab (15 mg/kg) was additionally administered to selected patients without contraindications (2,3). Dose modifications were made and treatment was delayed at the discretion of the attending physician based on toxicity and patient performance status according to institutional guidelines. Treatment was continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, or patient refusal was observed.

Treatment response was assessed radiologically using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 (31). Adverse events were graded according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. Physical examinations and laboratory monitoring (complete blood count and chemistry) were performed by the attending physician before each cycle.

PET imaging and data analysis

18F-FDG-PET/CT imaging was performed using a Biograph Vision 600 scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Patients fasted for at least 6 h and received 3.7 MBq/kg of FDG. Scanning began 60 min post-injection at eight bed positions (2 min per bed). Images were reconstructed using an ordered subset expectation maximization method (OSEM) algorithm with point-spread function (PSF) correction (168×168 matrix, 2 mm slice thickness). Metabolic tumor volume (MTV) and total lesion glycolysis (TLG) were not analyzed because the diffuse, rind-like growth pattern of PM makes reproducible volume-of-interest delineation difficult and limits the reliability of these volumetric parameters.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact or the chi-squared test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were analyzed using Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Survival outcomes were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier curves, and differences were assessed using log-rank tests. Univariate analysis was followed by multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression for variables with P<0.10 or clinical relevance. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the duration from treatment initiation to radiographic progression or death, whichever occurred first; patients without an event were censored at the last disease assessment. OS was defined as the duration from the date of pathological diagnosis to death from any cause, and patients alive at the last follow-up were censored. The optimal cutoffs for SUVmax and SUVpeak were determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis with 2-year OS as the endpoint. Responders referred to patients achieving complete response (CR) or partial response (PR), and non-responders referred to those with stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD). Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 17.0.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Kaplan-Meier survival curves and graphical presentations were created using R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism version 7.0e (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Given the limited sample size, the number of events per variable (EPV) was considered when building the multivariable Cox models. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using scaled Schoenfeld residuals, and no clear violation was detected; the power of this diagnostic is limited in the small sample. To assess the stability of the hazard ratio (HR) for SUVpeak, we performed internal validation by bootstrap resampling with 1,000 replications. Because the optimal cutoffs for SUVmax and SUVpeak were derived by ROC analysis within the same cohort, they were regarded as exploratory, and no external validation cohort was available.

Results

Patient characteristics

Fifty-one patients with PM who received first-line platinum-based chemotherapy combined with pemetrexed were included in the study. The baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 69 (range, 42–81) years and 40 patients (78%) were male. The histological subtype was epithelioid in 34 patients (67%) and non-epithelioid in 17 patients (33%). The ECOG PS was 0 for 11 (22%), 1 for 37 (73%), and 2 for 3 (6%) patients. Pretreatment 18F-FDG-PET/CT images were available for 35 patients (69%).

Treatment outcomes

The median PFS and OS were 5.8 (95% CI, 3.4–7.8) (Figure 2A) and 21.8 (95% CI, 13.4–33.5) months (Figure 2B), respectively, after a median follow-up duration of 21.2 (range, 3.8–202.5) months. At the data cutoff date (December 2024), 44 of 51 patients had died, and 7 had survived. The objective response rate (ORR) was 27.5% (95% CI, 17.1–40.9%), including 1 CR and 13 PRs. The disease control rate (DCR) was 72.5% (95% CI, 59.1–82.9%) (Table 2).

Univariate analyses indicated associations between PFS and ECOG PS for both the entire cohort (P=0.004) and the PET/CT subgroup (P=0.009). Additionally, univariate analyses indicated associations between OS and ECOG PS (P=0.02), history of asbestos exposure (P=0.02), and PNI (P=0.04) in the entire cohort. In the PET/CT subgroup, OS was associated with sex (P=0.02), ECOG PS (P=0.03), SUVmax (P=0.02), and SUVpeak (P<0.001).

Multivariable analysis demonstrated that ECOG PS ≥2 was related to worse PFS for the entire cohort (HR for PS 0–1 vs. ≥2, 0.05; P=0.002) and the PET/CT subgroup (HR for PS 0–1 vs. ≥2, 0.10; P=0.03). The multivariable analyses also showed that low PNI (≤45) was correlated with worse OS (HR, 2.04; P=0.04) for the entire cohort, whereas high SUVpeak (>7.73) was associated with shorter OS in the PET/CT subgroup (HR for low vs. high SUVpeak, 0.08; 95% CI, 0.02–0.34; P<0.001), with eight patients in the high-SUVpeak group. The number of EPV was 11.0 for the overall-cohort OS model and 5.8 for the PET/CT-subgroup model. Scaled Schoenfeld residuals showed no clear violation of the proportional hazards assumption. On internal validation by bootstrap resampling (1,000 replications), the lower bound of the bootstrap HR distribution remained above unity, whereas the upper bound varied widely across resamples.

Survival curves analysis

PFS and OS curves were created using Kaplan-Meier analysis (Figures 4,5). Patients with a high SUVpeak (>7.73) had significantly shorter median OS (9.1 months) than those with a low SUVpeak (33.6 months; log-rank test, P<0.001; Figure 4A). Similarly, patients with high SUVmax (>7.31) had significantly shorter OS than those with low SUVmax (log-rank test, P=0.01; Figure 4B).

Patients with PNI ≤45 had shorter median OS (18.8 months) than those with PNI >45 (28.5 months; log-rank test, P=0.03; Figure 4C).

Patients with a high SUVpeak tended to have worse PFS, but the difference between the PFS of the high and low SUVpeak groups was not significant (P>0.05). ECOG PS was significantly associated with PFS differences, with patients with ECOG PS 0–1 having more prolonged median PFS (5.8 months) than those with ECOG PS 2 (1.2 months; log-rank test, P<0.001; Figure 5).

GPS, NLR, and BMI showed no significant association with PFS or OS in the univariate analysis and were therefore not included in the multivariable models. BMI was also excluded from the multivariable analysis because of its collinearity with PNI, as both reflect nutritional status.

Biomarker correlations with clinical features

Patients with higher PNI (>45) had significantly higher albumin concentrations, lymphocyte counts, and lower CRP concentrations than those with lower PNI (≤45) (Table 5), reflecting better nutritional and immune status. In contrast, SUVpeak was not significantly associated with baseline clinical characteristics, indicating that metabolic and systemic biomarkers can capture independent biological information.

Albumin and the PNI

Because the PNI incorporates serum albumin, we additionally examined albumin alone. Serum albumin was not significantly associated with OS, either as a continuous variable (HR, 0.68 per g/dL; 95% CI, 0.43–1.06; P=0.09) or when dichotomized at 3.5 g/dL (log-rank P=0.053), whereas low PNI remained significant. Albumin and PNI were strongly correlated (r=0.90), and when both were entered as continuous variables, neither retained significance.

Sensitivity analysis

In a sensitivity analysis excluding the nine patients who had undergone prior surgery or radiotherapy, low PNI remained associated with worse OS (HR, 2.07; 95% CI, 0.99–4.33; n=42), and high SUVpeak remained associated with worse OS (HR, 11.90; 95% CI, 3.16–44.78; n=31).

Comparison of patients with and without PET/CT

Pretreatment PET/CT was available for 35 of 51 patients. Compared with the 16 patients without PET/CT, the PET/CT group did not differ significantly in age, sex, ECOG PS, histology, clinical stage, PNI, albumin, or OS (log-rank test, P=0.24). The PET/CT group more often received immune checkpoint inhibitors (16/35 vs. 1/16; P=0.009), and 19 of 20 patients diagnosed in 2017 or later underwent PET/CT (P=0.001). High SUVpeak remained associated with worse OS after adjustment for immune checkpoint inhibitor use (HR, 8.84; 95% CI, 2.27–34.44; P=0.002).

Discussion

In this retrospective study involving 51 patients with PM uniformly treated with platinum-pemetrexed chemotherapy, we demonstrated that both the PNI and SUVpeak on 18F-FDG-PET/CT were independent prognostic factors for OS. Low PNI (≤45) was associated with a 2-fold increased risk of death (HR, 2.04; P=0.04), while high SUVpeak (>7.73) was associated with a markedly higher mortality risk (HR for low vs. high SUVpeak, 0.08; 95% CI, 0.02–0.34; P<0.001), although the magnitude of this estimate is imprecise given the small high-SUVpeak subgroup. These biomarkers predicted long-term survival but not PFS, whereas ECOG PS was the only factor associated with early progression. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to concurrently assess systemic nutritional status and tumor metabolic activity as complementary prognostic markers in a homogeneous cohort of chemotherapy-treated patients with PM.

The PNI incorporates serum albumin, but albumin alone was not significantly associated with OS in our cohort; albumin and the PNI were strongly correlated, and neither retained significance when entered together. This indicates that the PNI, which combines albumin with the lymphocyte count, captures prognostic information beyond that of albumin alone and does not merely act as a surrogate for hypoalbuminemia. This is consistent with a recent report in which the absence of albumin decline was associated with better outcomes of platinum-pemetrexed therapy in PM (32), and with an externally validated study showing that the PNI remained an independent predictor of mortality in lung cancer after accounting for its individual components (33).

Both SUVmax and SUVpeak were significantly associated with OS on univariate analysis. We did not perform a formal statistical comparison of their prognostic performance; SUVpeak was selected for the multivariable model to avoid collinearity between the two highly correlated parameters. Although SUVpeak is theoretically less susceptible to image noise and single-voxel variability than SUVmax (34,35), our data do not establish the superiority of either parameter. The PNI, calculated from serum albumin and lymphocyte counts, reflects both the nutritional status and immunological competence of the host.

Low PNI has been associated with poor outcomes across various malignancies, including thoracic cancers, largely due to its correlation with systemic inflammation and immunosuppression (36-38). Our findings are in line with these reports and reinforce the utility of the PNI as a practical, inexpensive, and accessible prognostic marker for PM.

Previous studies have evaluated the PNI in mesothelioma; however, most cohorts have included heterogeneous treatment modalities or surgical cases. Yao et al. first reported PNI as an independent prognostic factor in 121 patients with PM, using a cutoff value of 44.6 (39). However, their study included various treatment types, and only 48% of the patients received platinum-based chemotherapy. By restricting our analysis to patients treated with the same platinum-pemetrexed regimen, we found that a cutoff value of 45 was associated with significant OS discrimination (HR, 2.04, P=0.04). Performance status remains essential for determining chemotherapy eligibility, with ASCO guidelines recommending against chemotherapy for patients with ECOG PS of ≥3 due to low response rates and high toxicity (40). Clinical trials of mesothelioma typically require ECOG PS 0–1 for enrollment. However, nutritional status provides crucial additional prognostic information for eligible patients who qualify for treatment that performance status alone cannot provide. Previous studies have demonstrated that the PNI serves as an independent prognostic factor along with performance status in patients with advanced cancer receiving platinum-based chemotherapy (41,42). Our research extends these findings to PM, showing that the PNI functions as a complementary biomarker that reflects the complex interplay between immune function, nutritional reserves, and treatment tolerance. This is especially important in mesothelioma, because platinum-pemetrexed therapy is intensive and requires careful patient selection. Mechanistically, a low PNI may reflect impaired nutritional reserves, systemic inflammation, and reduced immune competence. In particular, hypoalbuminemia may increase the toxicity of highly protein-bound agents such as cisplatin through higher unbound drug fractions and altered pharmacokinetics, while a reduced lymphocyte count may impair antitumor immunity (43-45). Thus, the prognostic value of the PNI likely reflects a composite host condition rather than albumin alone. PNI and SUVpeak function independently as prognostic factors, highlighting different biological aspects. PNI reflects the capacity of the host to tolerate chemotherapy (46), whereas SUVpeak reflects tumor aggressiveness and potential resistance to antifolate therapy (47). Combining these markers may enable a more precise risk stratification, potentially defining four distinct prognostic groups for future clinical trials and personalized treatment strategies. Our data suggest that SUVpeak may serve as an independent prognostic biomarker for PM in the context of platinum-pemetrexed chemotherapy. Previous FDG-PET studies of mesothelioma have reported mixed results regarding SUV-based measurements and treatment responses. Zucali et al. showed that the baseline SUVmax could predict prognosis in 89 patients with PM treated with pemetrexed-based chemotherapy (14); however, their cohort included different platinum agents and did not test SUVpeak. Schaefer et al. found that changes in SUVmax during pemetrexed-platinum therapy did not predict survival, suggesting that changes in tumor size may be more informative than metabolic changes in response assessments (48). Our findings add to this literature by showing that baseline SUVpeak, rather than changes during treatment, is associated with OS (HR for low vs. high SUVpeak, 0.08; P<0.001). SUVmax and SUVpeak were both prognostic on univariate analysis. Because the two parameters were highly correlated, only one was entered into the multivariable model to prevent multicollinearity; we chose SUVpeak in view of its lower susceptibility to image noise and single-voxel statistical fluctuation (34,35). This choice reflects methodological considerations rather than a demonstrated prognostic superiority. A high SUVpeak may reflect increased tumor glucose metabolism and biological aggressiveness, which could partly explain its association with worse OS. However, our data do not establish a causal relationship between SUVpeak and treatment resistance. In contrast, PFS was not significantly associated with blood- or PET-derived biomarkers, whereas ECOG PS was associated with early progression, consistent with its established role as a determinant of immediate treatment tolerance (49,50). This suggests that PNI and SUVpeak may be more informative for long-term survival than for early treatment response (51,52). This study has some limitations. First, its retrospective design and single-center setting may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the sample size, particularly in the PET/CT subgroup (n=35), was limited. Third, some patients received immune checkpoint inhibitors during follow-up, which may have influenced OS outcomes. Fourth, the absence of an external validation cohort limited our ability to confirm the reproducibility of the cutoff values and prognostic associations. Fifth, blood glucose levels at the time of PET/CT were not available for retrospective analysis; however, all patients followed a standardized fasting protocol (≥6 hours), and hyperglycemia in patients with diabetes was managed according to institutional practice. Sixth, because the study period spanned 2007–2023, clinical staging was based on different editions of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification (6th–8th editions); stage was not significantly associated with OS, suggesting a limited impact on the main findings. Seventh, the optimal cutoff values were derived within the same cohort and should be regarded as exploratory; although internal validation supported the robustness of the prognostic direction of SUVpeak, the magnitude of its HR was unstable owing to the small high-SUVpeak subgroup, and external validation in an independent cohort is required. Finally, histological subtype was unknown in eight patients, predominantly older patients in whom only limited biopsy material was available; however, subtype was not included in the multivariable models and therefore did not affect the reported associations. Future multicenter, prospective studies with larger cohorts and standardized imaging protocols are warranted to validate our findings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that both SUVpeak on 18F-FDG-PET/CT and the PNI are independent prognostic factors for OS in patients with PM receiving platinum-based chemotherapy. These two parameters represent distinct biological dimensions—tumor metabolic activity and host systemic status—and may serve as complementary tools for risk stratification. The practical accessibility of the PNI as a blood-based marker, combined with the robust prognostic value of SUVpeak from routine staging PET/CT, provides clinicians with readily available tools for patient counseling and treatment planning. Incorporating both imaging- and blood-based biomarkers may facilitate personalized treatment strategies for the management of PM, pending validation in larger prospective studies.
