E.coli-derived CsgA-peptides stimulate cytokine production in microglia and lead to transient changes in neocortical Aβ-levels in pre-plaque APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 and wild type mice
Authors: Leda Mygind, Gabriel Jensen, Victoria Neesgaard, Katrine Tækker Krohn, Sergi Torres Puig, Anders Boysen, Sussanne Petersen, Kate Lykke Lambertsen, Athanasios Metaxas, Michael Kemp, Jakob Møller-Jensen, Bente Finsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpidemiological and pre-clinical data propose that infections can accelerate the cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease (AD) and other dementias. The implication of infectious agents, and especially the role of E.coli and other amyloid-peptide producing bacteria, on the development and progression of cerebral amyloidosis and neuroinflammation, both key neuropathological characteristics of AD, has only been studied to a limited extent.In this study, recombinant bacterial amyloid surface protein CsgA was injected intracisternally in pre-plaque 8-11-week-old APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice and age-matched wild type (WT) mice. Although less potent than bacterial lipopolysaccharide, CsgA significantly increased the gene expression of inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor, in the neocortex of both APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 and WT mice, and in cultured microglia. CsgA exposure also induced transient changes in neocortical amyloid-beta (Aβ) peptide levels, increasing the highly fibrillogenic Aβ42 in the guanidine-fraction in APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice and decreasing Aβ40 in the PBS-fraction in WT mice. The changes in Aβ levels had dissipated 24 h post-injection. In line with the only transient changes in Aβ levels and inflammatory gene expression, CsgA did not impact on long term spatial memory in pre-plaque APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice.Our findings highlight a contribution of bacterial amyloid proteins on neuroinflammation and a possible contribution in influencing Aβ-homeostasis during infections. However, findings need to be further elaborated in older APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice in which Aβ plaques are abundant and an inflammatory response already established. Also, the impact of CsgA and other bacterial amyloids should be examined after repeated and/or continuous administration and at different concentrations.Keywords: Amyloid-β, CsgA, Alzheimer's disease, APP/PS1 mice, Microglia, Neuroinflammation, Barnes maze

Introduction

Epidemiological studies have shown that infections can accelerate the cognitive decline in patients with sporadic Alzheimer's disease (AD) (Heneka et al., 2015; Perry et al., 2007; Holmes et al., 2009; Schmidt et al., 2002) and there appears to be a strong association between dementia and the overall accumulated burden of prior infections (Katan et al., 2013; Bu et al., 2015). Neuropathologically, AD is characterized by the occurrence of amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques, neurofibrillary tangles and chronic inflammation. The latter is reflected by chronic activation of the innate microglia, which are equipped with a broad range of pathogen-recognition receptors and immunity risk genes of AD (Perry et al., 2010; Kunkle et al., 2019; Ridge et al., 2017; Bellenguez et al., 2022). The role for infection in AD is in line with the fact that genetics accounts for only up to 53 % of the total phenotypic variance of the disease (Ridge et al., 2017; Bellenguez et al., 2022), with the remainder potentially explained by lifestyle and environmental factors, including infections and inflammation. Infections in elderly have been shown to elicit cognitive impairment that resides even after the infection has been cleared (Heneka et al., 2015; Perry et al., 2007; Holmes et al., 2009; Schmidt et al., 2002; Katan et al., 2013; Bu et al., 2015). Systemic inflammatory events and elevated peripheral tumor necrosis factor (TNF) have been associated with increased rate of cognitive decline during a 6-month-period in patients with mild AD and AD (Holmes et al., 2009). In fact, high baseline levels of TNF were associated with an increase in cognitive decline.

Urinary tract infections with curliated Escherichia coli (E.coli) are among the most frequently occurring bacterial infections in elderly (Rodriguez-Manas, 2020). In recent years, different proteins and genetic material from various infectious agents, including E.coli, have been detected in brain tissues of patients with AD while absent or less frequent in normal controls (Wozniak et al., 2007; Readhead et al., 2019; Hammond et al., 2010; Little et al., 2004, 2014; Zhan et al., 2016). Curli are surface proteins of E.coli that are formed by aggregation of the CsgA protein in self-seeding fibrillar assemblies. Although CsgA is not homologous to Aβ on the amino acid level, CsgA is like Aβ prone for aggregation adopting a β-sheet conformation (Zhou et al., 2012), and curli fibrils are morphologically identical to Aβ fibrils in AD (Barnhart and Chapman, 2006; Friedland and Chapman, 2017). Also similar to Aβ fibrils, which is a main constituents of the amyloid plaques in the brain of patients with AD and of transgenic mouse models of AD (Babcock et al., 2015; Heneka et al., 2015), curli acts as a ligand to Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) on innate immune cells including microglia (Tükel et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2012), instigating a chronic neuroinflammatory reaction. Furthermore, former in vivo studies show the ability of curli to elicit interspecies cross-seeding with other amyloidogenic proteins adopting a b-sheet conformation (Zhou et al., 2012). While the occurrence of curli has not so far been reported in the brain of patients with AD, curli has been detected in the stool in people with dementia as well as mild cognitive impairment (MCI) (Sanchez-Tapia et al., 2023).

The temporal lag between the first Aβ plaque formation and the onset of dementia in AD is believed to be at least a decade (Sperling et al., 2014; Villemagne et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2024). Thus, it is critical to better understand the preclinical stages of AD to determine if, when and how infections may either induce or accelerate the progression of pathology in AD. Due to the amyloidogenic and pro-inflammatory properties of bacterial curli, the aim of this study was to examine whether direct exposure of the brain to curli: 1) elicits a neuroinflammatory response, 2) increases Aβ levels and accelerates Aβ plaque formation in a pre-clinical AD mouse model, and/or 3) increases Aβ levels and induces de novo Aβ plaque formation in littermate wild type (WT) mice. Additionally, it was examined in the same AD model whether exposure to curli, elicits changes in long-term spatial memory (reference memory) which is a cognitive area that is affected even in patients with mild AD (Cammisuli et al., 2024). To achieve this, curli in the form of monomeric CsgA peptides, were injected into the cerebrospinal fluid-(CSF)-containing cisterna magna of young pre-plaque AD model mice. We chose to use the APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 model of AD which we have previously characterized in detail (Babcock et al., 2015), and to use an intracisternal (i.c.) administration to directly expose the brain, especially the neocortex in which the very first Aβ plaques can be observed at 3 months of age (Babcock et al., 2015), to high local concentrations of CsgA. The dose of CsgA was based on studies of CsgA's ability to stimulate microglial cytokine-expression in the neocortex of C57BL/6J mice without inducing too severe sickness-response, and on studies of CsgA's tendency to form fibrils and aggregates prior to injection, thereby preventing its injection.

Materials and methods

Animals

The APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 transgenic (Tg) mice express human APP carrying the Swedish double mutations (KM670/671NL) and PSEN1 lacking exon 9, under control of the mouse prion protein promoter (Jankowsky et al., 2004). Tg mice purchased from the Jackson Laboratories (MMRRC Stock No: 34832-JAX) were maintained as hemizygotes on a C57BL/6J background in the Biomedical Laboratory at the University of Southern Denmark (SDU) according to permission from the National Danish Animal Research Committee (J.no. 2016-15-0202-00037). A total of 181 mice were used.

(Tg, n = 72; littermate WT mice, n = 70; C57BL/6J, n = 39). All mice were examined with Q-PCR for cytokine and TLR2 mRNA levels, whereas a randomly selected majority subset of mice, had MSD done for Aβ42 and Aβ40 peptide levels in neocortical and hippocampal brain samples. Mice were housed under a 12:12 h dark-light cycle (light on: 7 a.m.) with food and water ad libitum. For experiments were used 8-11-week-old mice.

CsgA

Bacterial strains

E. coli strain LSR12 bearing plasmids pMC1 and pMC3 was used to produce CsgA recombinantly in the supernatant. This strain is a CsgA defective mutant and pMC plasmids are maintained with 100 μg/mL ampicillin and 30 μg/mL chloramphenicol and can be used to control overexpression of CsgA and CsgG using IPTG induction. This expression system was kindly provided by Matthew R. Chapman (Department of Molecular, Cellular, and Developmental Biology, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, MI, USA).

Expression and purification of CsgA monomers

Recombinant production of CsgA was performed as described in Chapman et al. (2002). Briefly, isolated colonies of LRS12 pMC1 pMC3 from LB agar plates containing ampicillin and chloramphenicol were propagated in LB overnight (O/N) at 37 °C and 200 rpm. Then, this starting culture was used to inoculate 3 L of LB with antibiotic selection and the OD 600 was monitored until it reached ∼0.9. At this growth stage, expression of CsgA was induced using 0.4 mM IPTG and cells were further cultured for 2–3 h. Then, cells were cooled on ice and spun at 5000×g for 20 min. Supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and 9 mL of TALON® Superflow™ resin were added to capture all His-tagged CsgA O/N with gentle agitation at 4 °C. The next day, TALON beads were recovered by filtration, resuspended in PBS +0.5 M NaCl and transferred to an empty gravity flow chromatography column (BioRad). Beads were washed with PBS, 0.5 M NaCl added 10 mM imidazole before eluting with PBS, 0.5 M NaCl added 150 mM imidazole. Eluate was concentrated using 3 kDa Amicon® Ultra-15 centrifugal spin units (Merck) in 1 M NaCl to prevent aggregation of purified CsgA monomers. Finally, concentrated CsgA was aliquoted in 120 μL aliquots and frozen in liquid nitrogen until further use. Concentration of CsgA was determined by liquid chromatography and Nanodrop.

SDS-PAGE and western blot of CsgA preparation

To verify the isoform of the injected CsgA, samples from batches of purified recombinant CsgA taken with 15 min interval were loaded into an SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to a Polyvinylidene Fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Merck) using Towbin buffer following standard procedures and probed with anti-6xHis antibody (Sigma) diluted 1:1000 in 5 % skimmed milk. CsgA was detected using an anti-mouse IgG HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Dako) and visualized using Immobilon Forte substrate (Merck).

Microglial cultures

Microglia were harvested from mixed glial cultures according to Krabbe et al. (2012). Brains from postnatal day 0–3 C57BL/6J pups were isolated in ice-cold Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) (Life Technologies) and cells were dissociated by trypsin digestion. To quench enzyme activity, DMEM, 10 % FBS, and 1 % Penicillin-Streptomycin-Glutamine (PSG), Life Technologies) were added. After centrifugation pellet was resuspended in DMEM and transferred to Poly-L-Lysine-coated (SigmaAldrich) filter-free T75 flasks (Orange Scientific) and incubated at 37 °C and 5 % CO2. After two days, cells were rinsed in Dulbecco's Phosphate-Buffered Saline (D-PBS) (Life Technologies) and provided with new DMEM. After 5 additional days, medium was replaced with DMEM and L929 fibroblast-conditioned medium which was repeated after two days. Next, microglia were harvested twice with a two-day interval, counted and plated in 24-well culture plates at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/mL. Twenty-four hours later cells were stimulated for 24 h with LPS (10 ng/mL, O111:B4) (Sigma-Aldrich), CsgA (0.5–2.0 μg/mL), or CsgA (1.0 μg/mL) in combination with LPS (10 ng/mL). Unstimulated cells served as control.

I.c. injection

For the i.c. injection, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. The mouse was placed on a stereotaxic frame, and the head was bent at an angle of 90° to the body. The skin of the neck was shaved and cleaned with 70 % ethanol. A needle that was bent at an angle of 55° was attached to a Hamilton syringe and used to perforate the posterior atlanto-occipital membrane at the base of the skull. A volume of 10 μL of purified CsgA (or sterile PBS) was injected into the CSF-filled cisterna magna over 60s. After the injection, the syringe was held for 60s to prevent backflow of CSF. Mice were sacrificed 3 h, 24 h or one-week post-injection. The procedure was approved by the National Danish Animal Research Committee (J. no. 2016-15-0201-00952).

Experimental design

CsgA-dose titration

C57BL/6J mice (n = 39; 33 females (F); 6 males (M)) received one injection with either PBS, purified CsgA (10, 50 or 100 μg/mL) or LPS (E.coli 0111:B4 Sigma) dissolved in sterile PBS (10 μg/μL) (Table 1). To ensure that the i.c. Injection had been performed with no parenchymal lesions that could impact brain cytokine expression, six naïve (untreated) mice were included. Mice were monitored for sickness behavior and killed 3 h post-injection (Table 1). Based on results it was decided to use CsgA dose of 50 μg/mL in the subsequent experiments.

Experimental design.

CsgA-injected Tg and WT mice 3 h post-injection

Tg (n = 26; 14 F/12 M) and age-matched WT mice were used (n = 36; 25 F/11 M). Tg mice were randomized into two treatment groups (PBS and CsgA 50 μg/mL) and WT mice were randomized into three groups (Naïve, PBS, and CsgA 50 μg/mL). Injections were performed once. Mice were monitored for sickness behavior and killed 3 h post-injection (Table 1).

CsgA-injected Tg and WT mice 24 h post-injection

Tg mice (n = 17; 11 F/6 M) and WT mice (n = 34; 28 F/6 M) received one injection of either CsgA (50 μg/mL), PBS or LPS (10 μg/μL) and were killed after 24 h (Table 1). All groups of mice were monitored for sickness behavior.

CsgA-injected Tg mice one-week post-injection

Tg mice (n = 8; 2 F/6 M) received one injection of CsgA (50 μg/mL) and were monitored for sickness behavior and weight loss until they were killed one-week post-injection (Table 1).

Impact of CsgA on BMT-behavior in Tg mice one-week post-injection

One injection of either CsgA (50 μg/mL) or PBS was given to 21 Tg mice (15 F/6 M) (Table 1). Spatial learning and memory were examined in the BMT under baseline conditions and long-term memory was examined one-week after CsgA or PBS administration, whereafter mice were killed. The workflow of the BMT as applied in this experiment is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1A.

Sickness behavior

Following i.c. injection, mice were single caged and monitored for sickness behavior by a blinded rater using a three-point scale based on Gandhi et al. (2017). Rated parameters included: piloerection (curled body posture), lethargy (diminished locomotion) and ptosis (drooping eyelids). One point was given for each symptom. The absence of symptoms was given 0 points. Scoring took place every 30 min for the first 3 h and thereafter once daily for experiments 2.5.4–5. The median scores were plotted against time and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. The weight of mice from 2.5.4 to 2.5.5 was registered daily before 10 a.m.

Behavior

The effect of i.c. CsgA on spatial long-term memory in the Tg mice was examined by use of the Barnes maze test (BMT) (Barnes, 1979). The BMT is an alternative to the Morris Water Maze Test with light (here 4 × 120 W) instead of water as the motivating factor (Barnes, 1979). The Barnes Maze (Panlab, Spain) and the BMT is described in detail in Mygind et al. (2021). Mice were habituated to the testing room in individual cages two weeks and to handling for 5 days prior to the BMT (See also Fig. 1A in Supplementary File). Tests were performed between 9 a.m. and 3 p.m. During the acquisition phase, mice received four training trials, each lasting 180s, on 3 consecutive days (D1-3). The memory-testing probe trials, during which the target box was removed, were conducted 24 h (D4) and 7 days (D11). Mice were randomized to receive one i.c. injection of CsgA or PBS after completing the probe trial on D4. On D11, long-term memory was assessed by repeating the probe trial from D4. All trials were recorded, and total latency, primary latency, target quadrant/block, and probe primary errors, e.i. the number of holes visited by a mouse on D4 and D11, before reaching the target box for the first time, were registered. The experimenter was blinded to the experimental groups. After each trial the maze and the escape box were cleaned with 70 % ethanol and water.

Tissue processing

Mice were killed by cervical dislocation, and brains were removed. One hemisphere was dissected into seven brain samples, including three neocortical and one hippocampal sample, and frozen on dry ice for RNA and protein isolation. The other hemisphere was frozen in isopentane on dry-ice and sectioned into 20 μm thick sagittal sections. Twelve series of sections were mounted onto glass slides, while three series were placed in Eppendorf tubes and for RNA and protein isolation.

Immunhistochemistry for Aβ

Sections were stained using biotinylated mouse 6E10 antibody recognizing residues 1–16 of human Aβ (Biolegend #39340) or biotinylated mouse 4G8 recognizing residues 17–24 of human and mouse Aβ (Biolegend #39200). The sections were fixated in 4 % paraformaldehyde O/N followed by 30 min demasking in 70 % formic acid. The staining was performed as described in Babcock et al. (2015). Sections from a 23-month-old Tg mouse were included in all stainings. Substitution controls with biotinylated mouse IgG1 isotype control (ThermoFisher, MG115) were devoid of Aβ-staining.

Quantitative real-time RT-PCR (Q-PCR)

Microglia

RNA extraction was performed using the RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen). Cells depleted of medium were rinsed 1× in D-PBS and then disrupted by pipetting Buffer RLT containing 1 % β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich). To obtain enough RNA, three wells were pooled per sample obtaining 4.5 × 10 5 cells in total. The RNA yield and concentration were measured on a NanoDrop One instrument (ThermoFisher). Next, RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA by using the HighCapacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, ThermoFisher), and samples were analyzed on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, ThermoFisher). Each sample contained nuclease-free water (ThermoFisher), 1× Maxima SYBR ® green/probe master mix (Thermo Fisher), and 500 nM forward and reverse primers (TNF (forward 5′-TGGCCTCCCTCTCATCAGTTC-3’; reverse 5′-ACTTGGTGGTTTGCTACGACG-3′), TLR2 (forward 5′-CTGGAGCATCCGAATTGCAT-3’; reverse 5′-GTTTGCTGAAGAGGACTGTTATGG-3′), HPRT-1 (forward 5′-AAACAGTACAGCCCCAAAATG-3’; reverse 5′-AAATCCAACAAAGTCTGG CCTGTA-3′) and 125 nM probes (TNF (FAM-probe: 5′-TGGCCCAGACCCTCACACTCAGATCATC-3′), HPRT-1 (FAM-probe: 5′-AGCTTGCTGGTGAAAAGGACCTCTCGAAGT-3′)) (TAG Copenhagen A/S). Finally, undiluted and 10× diluted cDNA for TNF and HPRT-1, respectively, were added to the respective samples. The primer sequences and procedures for interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-6 have been described before (Grønhøj et al., 2017). Data are calibrated to HPRT-1 from a pool of unstimulated microglia termed controls. The relative values are expressed as fold-changes compared to the mean value of mRNA levels from the controls.

Tissues

Neocortices were processed for mRNA extraction by the Trizol method (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA) and RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA as described above and analyzed on a Quant Studio 3 Real Time PCR system (Applied BiosystemsTM) using primer sequences described above. The standard curve was generated from cDNA from a spleen RNA sample of a control mouse. Data were normalized to the expression of HPRT-1 in the same samples. Results are reported as fold-changes compared to the mean value of mRNA levels in the naïve or PBS-treated mice. Data are shown on a log10 scale and values of 0 were set to 0.0001.

ELISA and mesoscale

TNF

The concentration of TNF protein in the supernatants from both harvests was measured in duplicates using the Mouse TNF-ɑ ELISA MAX TM Deluxe Set (BioLegend) following the manufacturer's instructions. Samples were analyzed undiluted except for samples obtained from microglia stimulated with CsgA (1.0 μg/mL and 2.0 μg/mL), LPS, and CsgA (1.0 μg/mL) in combination with LPS, which were diluted ×5. The absorbance was measured within 15 min on a Multiskan GO Microplate Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) at 450 nm and analyzed using SkanIt RE 6.0.2 (Thermo Scientific). Data are presented as pg/mL.

Aβ

Neocortical and hippocampal samples were sonicated in sterile PBS using an ULTRA-TURRAX Y25 basic homogenizer (Ika Werke). Samples were lysed with a mixture of protease inhibitors (Roche Diagnostics GmbH) on ice for 6s and next centrifuged at 9000×g for 20 min at 4 °C. Supernatants (PBS fractions) were collected and stored at −80 °C. Pellets were resuspended on ice for 6s in an 8-x volume of 5 M guanidine and 50 mM Tris-HCL buffer and solubilized by agitation for 3 h at RT. Finally, samples were spun at 20,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C and supernatants (guanidine fractions) were collected and stored at −80 °C. Total protein was measured using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific), whereas the quantification of Aβ42 and Aβ40 were performed using Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) V-Plex Aβ peptide panel 1 (4G8) kit (K15199E, MSD) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Mesoscale plates were analyzed in a SECTOR Imager 6000 and data evaluated by the MSD Workbench software. Results are presented as pg of Aβ per mg protein.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, LLC). Data are presented as median with interquartile range. Normality was tested with the Shapiro-Wilk test and Pearson test and outlier analysis using the ROUT with Q = 1 %. Analyses with and without outliers did not affect the statistical outcome and outliers were therefore removed from the data set. Comparison between two groups was performed using two-tailed Mann-Whitney or two-tailed, unpaired t-test and from three or more groups by Kruskal-Wallis followed by Dunn's multiple comparisons test, while Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test was used to compare the matched CsgA/LPS exposures of cultures. Two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni multiple comparison test was use for analysis of AUCs. Statistically significant differences are indicated as ∗/#p < 0.05; ∗∗/##p < 0.01; ∗∗∗/###p < 0.001; or ∗∗∗∗/####p < 0.0001, and non-significant as ns.

Results

CsgA dose correlates positively with cortical TNF mRNA levels

In the first in vivo experiment, CsgA was administered i.c. to C57BL/6J mice at different concentrations (10, 50 and 100 μg/mL) (Table 1). CsgA injection led to increased cortical levels of TNF mRNA reaching statistical significance at 50 and 100 μg/mL compared to vehicle PBS-injected [p < 0.0001, Kruskal-Wallis’ test] (Fig. 1A). LPS was used as a control due to its known potent pro-inflammatory properties. TNF mRNA levels in LPS-treated mice were 10-40-fold higher than the levels in CsgA-injected mice [two-tailed Mann-Whitney, LPS vs. PBS, and 10, 50 and 100 μg/mL CsgA; p < 0.01 or p < 0.001] (Fig. 1B).

Preceding injections, purified CsgA was maintained on monomeric form by storage on dry ice. The injection was performed with a microsyringe using a fixed volume of 10 μL for all injected compounds. During each experiment, which was performed at RT, samples from batches of purified CsgA were frozen of dry ice and analyzed with Western Blotting to ensure that the protein remained on a monomeric form. While CsgA at 100 μg/mL tended to immediately polymerize into fibrils (data not shown), CsgA 50 μg/mL (and concentrations below) remained as monomers (15.8 kDa as shown in Fig. 1C) for up to 180 min after thawing the CsgA (Morel and Conejero-Lara, 2019). To ensure that all mice were injected with monomeric CsgA, experiments were designed so that mice were injected with 180 min after thawing.

Murine microglia produce higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokine and TLR2 mRNA and secrete more TNF when stimulated with CsgA

Murine microglia expressed significantly higher levels of TNF, IL-1β and TLR2 mRNA after stimulation with CsgA at 1.0 and 2.0 μg/mL, however not 0.5 μg/mL, and after stimulation with CsgA at 1.0 μg/mL in combination with LPS (10 ng/mL) (Fig. 2A–C,D). The highest cytokine and TLR2 mRNA levels were observed after combined CsgA and LPS stimulation, which were not significantly different from stimulation with LPS alone (Fig. 2A–C,D). Comparison of the cytokine mRNA levels for the microglia stimulated with the highest dose of CsgA (2.0 μg/mL) and LPS (10 ng/mL), showed that levels were significantly higher in the LPS-stimulated microglia (Fig. 2A–C), whereas the TLR2 mRNA levels were comparable in CsgA- and LPS-stimulated microglia (Fig. 2D). The TNF protein concentration in the supernatants of the same cultures, mirrored the changes in TNF mRNA in the microglia (Fig. 2A and B).

Assessment of sickness behavior after i.c. administration of CsgA, LPS and PBS

Mice treated with CsgA (50 μg/mL), PBS, or LPS (10 μg/mL) were assessed for the development of sickness behavior (Table 1, Experiments 2.5.1, 2.5.2, 2.5.3 and 2.5.4). Mice generally tolerated i.c. Injection well. PBS-injected C57BL/6, WT and Tg mice exhibited no sickness behavior and are therefore not depicted in Fig. 3A. Compared to LPS-injected mice, which showed moderate symptoms, symptoms in CsgA-injected Tg and WT mice were mild. In all groups of mice sickness score peaked after 0.5–1.5 h post-injection and returned to baseline 2.0–2.5 h post-injection (Fig. 3A). Sickness behavior was also quantified as AUC for each mouse from baseline to 24 h post-injection. Total AUC was highest for LPS-injected WT mice [AUC: 2.800 LPS WT versus 1.031 CsgA Tg versus 0.682 CsgA WT (Fig. 3B). Two-way ANOVA showed significant effects of time [F(7,880) = 42, p < 0.0001] and of treatment on sickness scores [F(4,880) = 19, p < 0.0001], and time × treatment interactions [F(28,880) = 5, p < 0.0001], with post-hoc Bonferroni test showing significant differences between CsgA-injected Tg and WT mice [∗p < 0.05], and LPS- and CsgA-injected WT mice 0.5–1.5 h after injection [##p < 0.01] (Fig. 3A and B).

CsgA induces transient increases in neocortical expression of cytokine and TLR2 mRNA after i.c. administration in Tg and WT mice

Based on the results in the preceeding experiments (3.1–3.3), it was decided to inject CsgA at a dose of 50 μg/mL in all subsequent experiments, and with the maximal duration of experiments being <180 min. Treatment with CsgA (50 μg/mL) transiently increased neocortical mRNA levels of TNF and IL-1β as well as TLR2 in both Tg and WT mice when compared with PBS [two-tailed Mann-Whitney, p < 0.01; p < 0.001; p < 0.0001] (Fig. 4). Overall, Tg and WT mice thereby showed comparable pro-inflammatory responses to CsgA.

CsgA leads to transiently decreased levels of Aβ40 in WT mice

To investigate putative effects of CsgA on endogenous Aβ levels, we also analyzed Aβ-levels in WT mice (Table 1, Experiments 2.5.2 and 2.5.3). The MSD-results shown for the WT mice represent 4G8+ Aβ measured in the PBS-fraction of the neocortex. CsgA- and PBS-treated Tg mice show no differences in long-term memory

The effect of CsgA on long-term memory in Tg mice was examined using the BMT (Table 1, Experiment 2.5.5, Fig. 7). There were no learning differences between experimental groups in the acquisition phase, under physiological conditions, and no differences between groups in target-block time, primary latency, and primary errors at day 4 before administrated PBS or CsgA (Supplementary Fig. 1B and C and Fig. 1D,E,F left), thereby emphasizing a successful randomization. A single CsgA-administration thereby had no effect on long-term memory in Tg mice using the BMT.

Discussion

The main results of this explorative study showed that i.c. injection of young pre-plaque APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 Tg mice with purified E.coli-derived CsgA peptides directly into the CSF-containing cisterna magna, leads to a transient neuroinflammatory response, a transient increase in the level of the highly fibrillogenic Aβ42, and an overall increase in the percentage of mice with the highly fibrillogenic Aβ42 in the guanidine fractions of the neocortex. While the pro-inflammatory effect of CsgA was very clear, both after i.c. Injection and in the microglial cultures, the alterations in Aβ42 did not reflect in the formation of more plaques and had no effect on long-term memory in APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice.

Like Aβ42 which is associated with brain inflammation, bacterial CsgA peptides have been implicated in the induction of host inflammatory responses by stimulating TLR2, evidenced by induction of Nos2 expression (Tükel et al., 2009). In our study, TLR2 gene expression was significantly increased in CsgA-treated APPswe/PS1ΔE9 and WT mice, complementing former studies that have shown elevated microglial expression of CD14-TLR2 in animal models of AD and in AD patients (Tükel et al., 2009). CD14-TLR2 mediates microglias pro-inflammatory response to Aβ and bacterial CsgA (Letiembre et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2012). In line with this, we found that i.c. injected CsgA lead to a significant increase in TNF and IL-1β gene expression lasting up to 24 h in both WT and APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice. While we did not measure TNF and IL-1β protein in the same samples, the finding of a significant increase of TNF protein in the supernatant of the CsgA-stimulated microglia, indicates that the production of TNF protein might also be increased by the microglia in situ, at least transiently, after CsgA injection, possibly supplemented by macrophages in the CSF-filled Robin-Virchow and perivascular spaces.

Our results showed higher percentage overall of CsgA-treated APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice with the more fibrillogenic Aβ42 in the guanidine-fraction compared to PBS-treated mice. While this shows that CsgA induced changes at the biochemical level, these changes were clearly not sufficient to lead to an increased plaque formation in these young mice. When comparing the levels of the two Aβ-isoforms detected in the present study with the levels detected in older APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice (von Linstow et al., 2017) it is clear, that the Aβ-levels are very low which may especially affect the ratios.

The presently used mouse model of AD (APPSWE/PS1ΔE9) show elevated levels of Aβ42 and Aβ40 and develop Aβ plaques in the neocortex and hippocampus by 3–4 months of age (Jankowsky et al., 2004; Babcock et al., 2015). The development of plaques is age-dependent and increases until 18–24 months of age where it reaches a plateau (Babcock et al., 2015). We have not previously monitored the Aβ peptide levels in the guanidine-fraction in 3-month-old APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice. However, in von Linstow et al. (2017), the maximum total Aβ peptide levels in the guanidine-fraction were 2000 pg/mg protein in 6-month-old and 3000 pg/mg protein in 9-month-old APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice. In the PBS-fraction the total Aβ levels were reported as 200 pg/mg protein at 6 months increasing to 600 pg/mg protein at 9 months of age. Eight-11-week-old APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice are just at the onset of developing Aβ plaques and, as shown in the present study, the total Aβ levels rarely exceeded 1 pg/mg protein in the guanidine fraction and 100 pg/mg protein in the PBS-fraction in PBS-treated APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice.

The finding of an increased percentage of CsgA-treated APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice with fibrillogenic Aβ42 in the guanidine-fraction therefore emphasizes the relevance to follow with further studies allowing APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice a longer post-injection survival, possibly several months and possibly administrating a higher dose - or repeated numbers of injections.

In our WT mice that lack the human APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 transgene any detected Aβ is murine Aβ. Interestingly, the acute effect of CsgA on Aβ was exclusively seen on the more soluble Aβ40 isoform, while no significant changes were observed in the level of Aβ42. At 24-h post-injection there were accordingly no differences in the Aβ levels, and immunohistochemical stainings for murine Aβ were devoid of staining regardless, whether mice had received CsgA or PBS. There was also no accumulation of Aβ in relation to the vasculature, around which Aβ40 typically accumulates in AD (Brenowitz et al., 2015; Blennow and Zetterberg, 2019). The temporarily decreased levels of Aβ40 in CsgA injected mice at 3 h, may reflect better and more efficient clearance mechanisms in non-susceptible, non-transgenic mice in comparison to APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice were the levels of Aβ were either increased or at the same level. Nevertheless, we cannot definitively state this since protein levels are generally very low in these young mice regardless of genotype.

Whether CsgA cross-seeds human or murine Aβ is still unknown. However, CsgA appears to be able to accelerate Aβ aggregation in vitro (Hartman et al., 2013; Perov et al., 2019). Also, cross-seeding has been shown to take place between CsgA and other amyloidogenic proteins in several previous studies (Lundmark et al., 2005; Friedland and Chapman, 2017; Zhou et al., 2012). In 2005, Lundmark et al. demonstrated the ability of bacterial curli to seed and accelerate fibril formation in an amyloid AA reactive amyloidosis mouse model characterized by the buildup of amyloid protein A fibrils. It has also been shown that curli can accelerate aggregation of α-synuclein (which is a pathogenic amyloid protein in PD). Aged Fischer 344 rats have been described to have aggregated α-synuclein in the intestinal submucosal plexus. These rats were orally exposed to curli-producing E.coli and expressed significantly higher levels of α-synuclein deposition in the gut and brain in comparison to vehicle-treated rats and to rats that were exposed to mutant bacteria unable to synthesize curli (Chen et al., 2016). Recently, these results have been corroborated in a leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) transgenic mouse model of PD (Liang et al., 2023). Similarly, others have shown that both fiber-deprived diets as well as exposure to bacterial curli, exacerbate disease in the gut and brain and enhance motor abnormalities in transgenic Thy1-Syn14/Thy1-Syn-α mouse models of PD that overexpress human α-synuclein but not in non-susceptible mice (Sampson et al., 2020; Schmit et al., 2023). These observations suggest that microbial triggers are not sufficient but also require predisposing factors to facilitate disease progression.

Behavioral studies by others have found that long-term (reference) memory is impaired from 6 months of age, as amyloid burden worsens (Trinchese et al., 2004), while in other studies it appears that reference memory remains largely intact even as APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice age (Arendash et al., 2001; Pompl et al., 1999). In the present study, we asked whether i.c. CsgA injection increased the susceptibility of 3-month-old pre-plaque APPSWE/PS1ΔE9 mice to impairments in long-term spatial memory one-week after a single CsgA-administration and found no evidence that this should be the case in the performed experiment. However, seeing that symptoms of sickness dissipated in all mice 2–2.5 h post-injection, future studies exploring other aspects of cognitive functioning following CsgA-administration, e.g. working or short-term memory which is generally the first cognitive domain to be impaired in AD, are warranted.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, we are the first to examine the effect of direct exposure to bacterial CsgA on Aβ levels and neuroinflammation in an AD mouse model and in WT mice. Although in our study CsgA exposure did not induce amyloid pathology which in part may be explained by the very young age of the pre-plaque AD mouse model, our findings point at the possible contribution of bacterial amyloid proteins in triggering neuroinflammatory responses and altering Aβ-homeostasis during infections. Testing whether curli accentuates amyloid pathology shall be done in older mice where Aβ plaques as well as inflammation is already established. Additionally, future studies should examine the impact of CsgA (and other bacterial amyloids) when administered repeatedly and/or continuously at different concentrations.
