Degradable piezoelectric KNN/PLLA nanofibers for promoting osteogenesis and angiogenesis in bone regeneration
Authors: Qiuyi Zhu, Ziteng Zhao, Anlin Liu, Yunbo Guan, Yun Yang, Yang Xia, Yao Shu, Yantao Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReplicating the endogenous electromechanical microenvironment of bone remains a significant challenge in regenerative medicine. This study aims to develop a promising scaffold by integrating piezoelectric K0.5Na0.5NbO3/poly (KNN) nanoparticles into poly (L-lactic acid) (PLLA) nanofibers to promote bone healing.MethodsKNN/PLLA nanofibers were electrospun and verified via X-ray diffraction (XRD). Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) was used to characterize morphology and assess biocompatibility on 9 wt% KNN/PLLA. The distribution of KNN was analyzed via energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The mechanical properties were evaluated through Universal Testing Machine (UTM). Piezoelectric properties were quantified using an electrostatic voltmeter and Piezoresponse Force Microscopy (PFM), while Niobium (Nb) ion release was measured via inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis. Osteogenic differentiation was evaluated through cell proliferation, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) for osteogenic markers osteocalcin (OCN) and runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and Alizarin Red S (ARS) assays for osteogenesis, and tube formation for angiogenesis.ResultsXRD confirmed successful KNN loading. Tensile tests showed that KNN incorporation enhanced mechanical properties. ICP analysis detected Nb release, reflecting the degradation. Increasing KNN content reduced fiber diameter and enhanced piezoelectricity. SEM verified biocompatibility via cell growth on 9 wt% KNN. Notably, KNN loading dose dependently upregulated OCN and RUNX2 expression, enhanced ALP activity and ARS staining, and promoted angiogenesis.ConclusionThe 9 wt% KNN/PLLA nanofibers exhibited superior physicochemical and mechanical properties, a sevenfold increase in piezoelectric output. The nanofibers significantly enhanced bone regeneration, evidenced by upregulated osteogenic markers (OCN/RUNX2) and markedly improved ALP activity (60%) and ARS mineralization (70%). Coupled with favorable degradation and enhanced angiogenesis, the nanofibers demonstrate high potential for functional bone tissue engineering.Keywords: Piezoelectricity, Nanofibers, Osteogenesis, Angiogenesis, Bone regeneration

Introduction

Bone defects resulting from trauma, congenital abnormalities, or tumor resections often lead to significant functional impairment, decreased quality of life, and long-term disability [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), as the global population continues to age, the prevalence of bone fragility increases, with millions of new cases of bone defects emerging every year. This growing burden has become a major global health concern [2, 3]. Current treatment strategies, including autografts, allografts, and biomaterials, have shown some success but also notable drawbacks. Osteogenic differentiation is essential for the success of bone tissue regeneration. However, both autografts and allografts are constrained by their availability [4]. While biomaterials offer a promising alternative, they often lack the biological signals required for optimal bone regeneration, resulting in suboptimal healing. These challenges highlight the urgent need to develop novel biomaterials. Current research has identified electrical signals as a key factor in promoting bone regeneration [5]. Mechanistically, it is suggested that piezoelectric scaffolds influence the electrophysiological state of the cell membrane, specifically by modulating membrane potential and regulating voltage-gated Ca2+ ion channel activity [6]. This biophysical stimulation effectively activates downstream intracellular signaling cascades, such as the TGF-pathway, which plays a pivotal role in regulating osteoblast differentiation and bone matrix mineralization [6, 7]. Angiogenesis, the formation of new blood vessels, is indispensable for bone regeneration because it ensures adequate delivery of oxygen, nutrients, and signaling molecules to regenerating tissues [8]. A study suggested that electrical stimulation could enhance FGF2 secretion, which might subsequently activate the MAPK/ERK pathway, potentially promoting angiogenesis [9]. Some studies have employed direct electrical stimulation to enhance osteogenesis and have achieved positive results [10]. However, the safety concerns and challenges associated with in vivo application of direct electrical stimulation limit the widespread use of this treatment approach. Natural bone has the characteristic ability to generate electrical signals in response to mechanical stress. Walking and muscle activities simulate this characteristic, thereby promoting bone growth [11, 12]. Piezoelectric materials are a novel class of biomaterials that generate electrical signals in response to mechanical forces. Piezoelectric materials can effectively compensate for the loss of bioelectric signals caused by bone loss, making them a focal point of recent research on bone defect treatment [13].

Existing studies have shown that while piezoelectric materials have the potential to promote osteogenesis and angiogenesis in bone tissue repair, most research has focused on single materials, with limited in-depth exploration of combinations of novel piezoelectric materials [14]. PLLA is a commonly used biopolymer in bone tissue engineering owing to its outstanding biocompatibility, biodegradability, mechanical properties, and its capacity to promote cell adhesion and proliferation [15]. As a synthetic polymer, PLLA effectively mimics the natural osteogenic extracellular matrix (ECM) microenvironment, providing an ideal environment for osteoblast growth and differentiation, thereby promoting bone regeneration [16]. Despite its promising characteristics, PLLA exhibits relatively low piezoelectric properties, which limit its ability to enhance bioelectric signals [17]. To address this limitation, the integration of piezoelectric materials, particularly KNN, into PLLA is explored in this study, aiming to improve both piezoelectric performance and biological functions. Electrospinning is a highly effective method for producing nanofibers scaffolds by applying a high-voltage electric field to a polymer solution, resulting in the formation of fine, continuous fibers. Such precise control over fiber diameter and uniformity is essential for replicating the structural characteristics of the ECM, which plays a fundamental role in regulating cell adhesion, migration, and differentiation [18]. This process can promote alignment of polymer chains and dispersion of nanoparticles within the PLLA matrix, thereby increasing crystallinity in the nanofibers [19]. By reinforcing the nanofiber network structure, both the piezoelectric response and mechanical properties are enhanced. The improved alignment and connectivity of the nanofibers contribute to better stress distribution, thereby increasing the material’s ability to generate electrical signals and enhancing its mechanical strength [20]. Therefore, we intend to integrate piezoelectric materials through electrospinning to enhance their piezoelectric functions. KNN, a lead-free piezoelectric ceramic, was introduced as a potential enhancement to PLLA. KNN is known for its excellent piezoelectric properties and biocompatibility, with no environmental or biological risks, making it a better alternative to lead-based piezoelectric materials [21]. In a study conducted on KNN particles, the injection of these particles into rats showed no adverse effects on heart, liver, or kidney function, confirming the safety of KNN in orthopedic applications [22]. Moreover, the osteogenic response of KNN piezoelectric bioceramics has been shown to be significantly enhanced by surface polarization charges and dynamic electrical stimulation, which accelerate osteoblast differentiation and bone formation. This integration of KNN improves cellular bioactivity, positioning it as a promising candidate for bone tissue engineering applications [23]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that the piezoelectric coefficient (d33) of single-crystal KNN can reach up to 392 pC/N [24], providing a substantial enhancement to PLLA’s piezoelectric effect. Specifically, despite the challenges in achieving independent control, the electromechanical coupling coefficient (kp) of KNN can reach over 56% [25]. In comparison, Zinc oxide (ZnO) typically exhibits d33​value lower than 50 pC/N, indicating a lower coupling efficiency. This means that KNN generates a stronger piezoelectric field under small deformations, such as those induced by cellular activities or external ultrasound, making it more effective in stimulating osteoblast differentiation. Although Barium titanate (BaTiO₃) exhibits good piezoelectric properties similar to KNN, its cytotoxicity and non-biodegradability limit its applications. Ultrasound-excited PVDF-TrFE/BNNT nanofiber piezoelectric films significantly enhanced osteoblast differentiation [26]. In a similar context, Khare et al. demonstrated that PVDF-KNN composites, fabricated via electrospinning and subsequent polarization, exhibit enhanced piezoelectric properties, significantly promoting neuronal growth and extension in peripheral nerve regeneration [27]. Existing studies primarily focus on KNN/PLLA composites for neural repair, such as the work by Zhang et al., which utilizes their piezoelectric properties to promote nerve regeneration after spinal cord injury [28]. In contrast, this study innovatively applies KNN/PLLA composites to bone formation and angiogenesis. Unlike previous research, our material not only supports bone regeneration but also promotes angiogenesis through electrical stimulation, addressing blood supply issues in bone defects. This multifunctional design offers broader clinical applications in tissue engineering.

Given these findings, we will use low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) to activate the KNN/PLLA nanofibers and provide a stable electrical signal for stimulation, thereby improving the activation efficiency. When KNN particles are incorporated into the PLLA matrix, they may enhance the piezoelectric performance and mechanical strength of the nanofibers while maintaining the polymer’s biodegradability. These characteristics make the KNN/PLLA nanofibers an ideal candidate for bone tissue repair.

The primary objective of this study is to systematically explore the impact of varying KNN content on the osteogenic and angiogenic potential of piezoelectric PLLA nanofibers. Unlike previous research that primarily focuses on mechanical enhancement or isolated biological functions, this study investigates the synergy between piezoelectric performance and dual-lineage bioactivities. Initially, KNN/PLLA nanofibers were fabricated and characterized for their biocompatibility and electrical output. This approach evaluates how piezoelectric stimulation can simultaneously promote osteogenic differentiation and vascularization, both of which are essential for functional bone healing. As shown in Scheme 1, our experimental design seeks to identify the optimal composition for bone repair. These findings contribute to the development of "active" regenerative materials, providing a strategic bridge between piezoelectricity and bone regeneration.

Results

Physical and chemical characterization of KNN/PLLA nanofibers

To investigate the effect of KNN content on the morphology and diameter of the KNN/PLLA nanofibers, SEM imaging and diameter distribution analysis were performed. Furthermore, XRD was conducted to verify the surface chemical composition and crystalline structure of the composite fibers, respectively. To evaluate the degradation behavior of the KNN particles within the polymer matrix, the release profile of Nb ions was monitored via ICP. The morphology and elemental composition of the KNN/PLLA nanofibers were characterized using SEM and EDS. To investigate the effect of varying KNN content on the nanofiber structure, SEM images were first obtained (Fig. 1a). It was observed that a high density of KNN particles was present on the fiber surfaces, confirming the successful incorporation of the piezoelectric phase. Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Fig. 1b) further revealed that the KNN particles were uniformly distributed within the fibers, although more noticeable aggregation on the surface occurred as the KNN content increased. Furthermore, the impact of KNN content on the fiber dimensions was quantified through diameter analysis (Fig. 1c). As the KNN content increased, the fiber diameter gradually decreased. The pure PLLA sample (0 wt% KNN) was relatively uniform, with most fibers ranging between 900 and 950 nm. At KNN contents of 3 wt% and 6 wt%, the diameters slightly decreased, with a higher proportion of fibers shifting to the 800–850 nm range. Notably, the 9 wt% KNN sample exhibited the smallest fibers, predominantly in the 750–800 nm range. These observations demonstrate that adjusting the KNN content allows for the effective modulation of both the chemical distribution and the physical dimensions of the nanofibers, offering significant potential for optimizing bone tissue repair materials.

As shown in Fig. 1d, the XRD pattern of the 9 wt% KNN/PLLA composite reveals characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ = 16.5° and 18.8°, corresponding to the (110)/(200) and (203) planes of PLLA [29]. Additionally, two prominent peaks at 2θ = 45° are observed, corresponding to the (022) and (200) planes of KNN, confirming the incorporation of KNN into the composite [30, 31].

The cumulative release of Nb from the KNN/PLLA nanofibers exhibited a time-dependent increase over the 7-day in vitro degradation period (Fig. 1e). Specifically, the Nb release reached approximately 0.15 μg/g by day 3. As degradation continued, the cumulative release further increased, reaching approximately 0.25 μg/g by day 7. This sustained release suggests that the KNN particles were steadily exposed and ionized.

The mechanical properties of the PLLA and KNN/PLLA nanofibers were evaluated via tensile testing. The representative stress–strain curves (Fig. 1f) indicate that the incorporation of KNN particles effectively reinforces the PLLA matrix, as evidenced by the upward shift of the curves with increasing KNN content. Specifically, the tensile strength (Fig. 1g) exhibited a concentration-dependent increase, rising from 2.15 MPa for pure PLLA to a maximum of 2.69 MPa for the 9 wt% KNN/PLLA group. Statistical analysis reveals that while the initial addition of 3 wt% KNN resulted in a marginal improvement, a significant enhancement in tensile strength was observed as the KNN content reached 6 wt%. This trend suggests that the KNN particles act as effective reinforcing agents, likely due to efficient interfacial stress transfer between the inorganic fillers and the polymer matrix, thereby enhancing the overall structural integrity of the nanofibers.

Enhancement of piezoelectric properties

Thereupon, we evaluated the piezoelectric performance of the KNN/PLLA nanofibers with varying concentrations using an electrostatic meter. Our results show that as the KNN content increases, both the output voltage and current significantly improve (Fig. 2a, b). The pure PLLA fibers exhibited minimal piezoelectric performance, with a voltage of approximately 50 mV and a current of approximately 5 nA. When the KNN content was increased to 3 wt%, the output voltage and current increased to 100 mV and 15 nA, respectively. Further increasing the KNN content to 6 wt% resulted in even higher output voltage and current, reaching approximately 150 mV and 35 nA, respectively. The 9 wt% KNN/PLLA nanofibers exhibited the strongest response, with a peak output voltage of 350 mV and a current of 50 nA, representing a sevenfold increase in voltage and a ten-fold increase in current compared to the pure PLLA sample. PFM analysis further confirmed these findings, with the 9 wt% KNN/PLLA nanofibers tested, revealing a typical “butterfly-shaped” amplitude–voltage curve and a phase curve with a clear inversion (Fig. 2c, d), indicating a substantial piezoelectric effect at the microscale. These results demonstrate the enhanced piezoelectric performance of the KNN/PLLA nanofibers with increasing KNN content.

In vitro biocompatibility

We then assessed the influence of different KNN contents on BMSCs proliferation using three primary methods: CCK-8 assay, Live/Dead cell staining, and SEM morphological observation. BMSCs were cultured for 1, 3, and 7 days with ultrasound stimulation (10 min/day, power: 1 W, sound intensity: 0.5 W/cm2). The CCK-8 assay results (Fig. 3c) demonstrated a concentration-dependent increase in BMSCs metabolic activity as the KNN loading increased. On day 1, the metabolic activity of all KNN-containing groups (3, 6, and 9 wt%) was already significantly higher than that of the pure PLLA group. This became more pronounced by day 3, with the 9 wt% KNN/PLLA group exhibiting a significantly higher OD value compared to the 6 wt% group. By day 7, the trend of increased OD values proportional to KNN content became even more remarkable; the 9 wt% KNN/PLLA group reached the highest proliferation level, significantly outperforming all other groups. These findings were further corroborated by Live/Dead cell staining and quantitative viable cell density analysis (Fig. 3a-b). To accurately evaluate the proliferation capacity, the number of live cells was normalized to the surface area. The viable cell density (cells/mm2) increased progressively with higher KNN content across all time points. On days 3 and 7, nearly all cells across these groups were viable, with the 9 wt% KNN/PLLA group exhibiting the highest cell density, reaching approximately 600 cells/mm2 by day 7 (Fig. 3b). This concentration-dependent increase in density confirms that the piezoelectric effect from the KNN/PLLA nanofibers significantly promotes BMSCs expansion in a density-dependent manner. This suggests that the piezoelectric effect generated by the higher KNN content under ultrasonic stimulation significantly promotes BMSCs proliferation and survival.

SEM images (Fig. 3d) illustrate the morphological development of BMSCs on 9 wt% KNN/PLLA nanofibers at days 1, 3, and 7. On day 1, the cells exhibited stable initial attachment, maintaining a predominantly round morphology with localized contact with the fiber surface. By day 3, the BMSCs showed enhanced spreading, characterized by the extension of individual filopodia that facilitated physical interaction with the underlying nanofiber network. By day 7, the cells demonstrated a significant increase in surface area and achieved superior spreading compared to earlier time points. At this stage, the secretion of extracellular matrix (ECM) was clearly observed, appearing as a thin biological coating that partially encapsulated the nanofibers and smoothed the scaffold topography, indicating active cellular functionalization.

Enhanced osteogenic differentiation level

The osteogenic induction capacity of KNN/PLLA nanofibers under LIPUS stimulation was systematically evaluated through qualitative and quantitative assays. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, a pivotal early marker of osteoblastic differentiation, was first examined after 7 days of culture. As shown in the representative images (Fig. 4a), the neat PLLA group exhibited only sparse, faint staining, indicating a low baseline of osteogenic induction. In stark contrast, the incorporation of KNN particles led to a progressive intensification of ALP expression. The 3 wt% KNN/PLLA group showed a noticeable expansion in staining area, while the 6 wt% KNN/PLLA and 9 wt% KNN/PLLA groups displayed robust, dark purple-blue staining, suggesting a high degree of early-stage differentiation. These qualitative findings were firmly supported by quantitative analysis (Fig. 4c), which revealed a significant, dose-dependent increase in intracellular ALP levels. Notably, the 9 wt% KNN/PLLA group achieved the highest enzymatic activity, which was significantly greater than both the PLLA control and the lower-concentration composite groups, confirming the potent stimulatory effect of higher KNN content.

To further evaluate late-stage osteogenic maturation, Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining was performed after 21 days to visualize mineralized nodule formation. In line with the ALP trends, the ARS results (Fig. 4b) demonstrated that calcium deposition was substantially enhanced in the presence of KNN. While the PLLA group exhibited only mineralized nodules, the composite scaffolds, particularly the 9 wt% KNN/PLLA group, were characterized by large, dense, and uniformly distributed mineralized areas. Quantitative assessment of the relative mineralization levels (Fig. 4d) indicated that the 6 wt% KNN/PLLA and 9 wt% KNN/PLLA groups exhibited significantly higher calcium deposition compared to the PLLA and 3 wt% KNN/PLLA groups. Specifically, the mineralization level of the 9 wt% KNN/PLLA group was the most pronounced, reflecting a mature and extensive calcified matrix. Collectively, these multi-timeframe results provide cohesive evidence that the KNN/PLLA nanofibers, through their concentration-dependent piezoelectric response, significantly accelerate the entire process of BMSCs differentiation and mineralization under ultrasound stimulation.

To further investigate the effect of these piezoelectric nanofibers on cell behavior, the expression of key osteogenic markers in BMSCs was quantified after 7 days of induction. Both OCN and RUNX2 mRNA levels displayed a clear upward trend with increasing KNN content. As shown in Fig. 4e, the expression of OCN in the 6 wt% KNN/PLLA and 9 wt% KNN PLLA groups was significantly higher than that in the pure PLLA group. Similarly, RUNX2 expression in the 9 wt% KNN/PLLA group exhibited a more than threefold increase relative to the pure PLLA control (Fig. 4f). These results indicate that the integration of KNN particles provides a potent electromechanical microenvironment that significantly promotes the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.

Increased tubular formation capacity

Finally, to assess the angiogenic potential of KNN/PLLA nanofibers with different KNN contents, we investigated their effects on endothelial cell tube formation, which is a key process in angiogenesis. A tube formation assay was conducted using HUVECs, seeded on a Matrigel substrate, and subjected to ultrasound stimulation of the KNN/PLLA nanofibers. As the KNN content varied, endothelial cell tube formation was observed. The pure PLLA group exhibited less angiogenic activity, characterized by fewer tubular structures, limited junction formation, and shorter tubes, indicating low-level angiogenesis. Then, 3 wt% KNN was incorporated into PLLA, and after 8 h of culture, significant tubular structure formation and angiogenesis were observed, surpassing the results of pure PLLA. In contrast, the 6 wt% and 9 wt% KNN/PLLA groups formed more complete branched tubular structures with longer tubes and more junctions, which exhibited stronger angiogenic activity (Fig. 5a). These quantitative analyses further confirmed these observations, revealing significant increases in key tube formation indicators. In the analysis of these angiogenesis indicators, the 9 wt% KNN/PLLA groups exhibited a greater number of junctions and branches, as well as increased total tube and branch length compared to the other groups (Fig. 5b). These results demonstrate that higher KNN content, coupled with ultrasound stimulation, promotes endothelial cell tube formation, highlighting the beneficial role of the piezoelectric effect.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that increasing the KNN content substantially enhanced the nanofibers’ piezoelectric output, thereby positively influencing cellular behaviors crucial for bone regeneration. These findings highlight the significant role of piezoelectricity induced by higher KNN content in modulating cell proliferation, osteogenesis, and angiogenesis, while the improved fiber morphology provides a favorable microenvironment for cellular interactions.

The incorporation of KNN particles into PLLA polymer significantly affected the fiber morphology. The KNN/PLLA nanofibers exhibited a uniform structure and favorable alignment, likely due to electrostatic interactions between KNN and PLLA chains [32]. Interestingly, increasing the KNN content reduced fiber diameter, which contrasts with the typical trend in pure polymer electrospinning systems, where higher polymer concentrations usually result in thicker fibers [33]. This may be attributed to the increased electrical conductivity of the solution, which elevates surface charge density and enhances electrostatic repulsion, leading to further stretching of the fibers under the electric field [34]. As a result, the fibers stretched further under the electric field, producing thinner, more uniform fibers. XRD analysis revealed KNN-specific peaks at 2θ = 45°, confirming the successful incorporation of KNN into the PLLA matrix. The higher intensity of the (110) peak compared to the (100) peak indicates the piezoelectric properties of KNN [35]. Furthermore, the degradation behavior of the KNN was elucidated by the Nb ion release profile measured via ICP. Due to the inherently slow hydrolysis rate of high-molecular-weight PLLA, a complete degradation analysis of the PLLA matrix was not conducted in this study. In physiological environments, complete degradation of PLLA typically takes several months to two years [36]. The incorporation of KNN nanoparticles effectively reinforced the PLLA matrix via efficient stress transfer between the rigid fillers and polymer chains [37]. This concentration-dependent enhancement, driven by strong interfacial adhesion, significantly improved the structural integrity and load bearing capacity of the nanofibers. The KNN content in the PLLA nanofibers was selected at 0, 3, 6, and 9 wt% to optimize the balance between piezoelectric performance and structural integrity. Pure PLLA served as a control to isolate the effects of the piezoelectric phase. The upper limit of 9 wt% was established primarily due to processing constraints. Specifically, as the KNN content exceeded 9 wt%, the excessive viscosity of the spinning solution led to jet instability and poor fiber uniformity. Given that 9 wt% KNN already elicited robust biological responses, higher concentrations were not explored to avoid further degradation of the scaffold stability.

As the KNN content increased, the distribution of KNN particles within the PLLA matrix progressively improved, indicating successful integration into the nanofibers. This is crucial because the piezoelectric properties of KNN are transferred to the fibers, enhancing their ability to generate electrical signals under mechanical stress. Increasing the KNN content amplified the piezoelectric response, optimizing the conversion of mechanical stimuli into electrical signals, which is essential for cellular behavior in tissue engineering applications. A clear positive correlation between KNN content and piezoelectric output was observed, confirmed by electrostatic measurements and PFM. This enhancement is attributed to KNN’s high d₃₃ (392 pC/N) [24]. Similar to KNN, BaTiO3 also exhibits excellent piezoelectric performance. Previous studies have shown that increasing the concentration of BaTiO3 in silicone rubber nanofibers enhances voltage output [38]. The 9 wt% KNN/PLLA generated strong electrical signals (350 mV, 50 nA), which play a critical role in modulating cellular behavior, particularly in bone regeneration, where continuous electrical signals regulate various cellular processes [39]. The study by Wang et al. confirmed that scaffolds doped with the piezoelectric-responsive material PWH promote osteogenesis and simultaneously enhance angiogenesis [40]. The results from CCK-8 and Live/Dead assays demonstrated that higher KNN content significantly promoted BMSCs proliferation. The 9 wt% KNN/PLLA group exhibited the highest proliferation rate and excellent cell viability, highlighting the role of piezoelectric stimulation in promoting osteoblast proliferation and growth, potentially mimicking the electrical signals cells experience in physiological conditions. Consistent with these quantitative results, the observed superior spreading morphology and visible ECM encapsulation by day 7 provide compelling morphological evidence of the scaffold's excellent cytocompatibility. These findings confirm that piezoelectric nanofibers can create a favorable microenvironment for BMSCs proliferation and growth under piezoelectric stimulation.

Higher KNN content in KNN/PLLA nanofibers enhanced BMSCs osteogenic differentiation and promoted mineralization. The piezoelectricity of KNN upregulates the expression of RUNX2 and OCN, which act as pivotal molecular switches to initiate and sustain osteogenesis [41]. This genetic activation directly leads to elevated ALP activity, which subsequently primes the extracellular matrix for calcification. The process ultimately culminates in enhanced calcium deposition, as confirmed by the dense mineralized nodules in ARS staining, particularly in the 9 wt% KNN/PLLA group. These findings suggest that piezoelectric materials, by mimicking the in vivo electric field, create an electrical microenvironment that influences osteogenesis and bone matrix formation. The electric field generated by KNN/PLLA nanofibers plays a crucial role in promoting cell adhesion and mineralization, enhancing osteoblast activity and accelerating bone matrix formation [42]. These findings, consistent with existing literature, demonstrate that piezoelectric materials regulate osteogenic behavior through electrical signaling at the cellular level. While the precise molecular pathways through which these electrical signals modulate cellular activity remain to be fully elucidated, this study provides significant insights into the potential of piezoelectric scaffolds for bone regeneration applications.

In this context, the angiogenesis tube formation assay was used to evaluate the angiogenic potential of KNN/PLLA nanofibers. The results demonstrated that, with increasing KNN content, angiogenic activity became more pronounced, resulting in a more branched tubular network and longer tubes. These findings suggest that increased KNN content and the resulting enhancement of piezoelectric properties effectively modulate endothelial cell behavior, favoring vascular network formation. The piezoelectric KNN/PLLA nanofibers promote endothelial cell tube formation, consistent with reports that piezoelectric materials generate microelectrical cues that influence key steps in angiogenesis [43]. In this study, the positive correlation between KNN content and tube formation paralleled the increase in voltage output, indicating a close link between the nanofibers’ piezoelectric performance and their pro-angiogenic effects.

Although the results of this research are encouraging, there are still some limitations. The lack of in vivo validation limits the generalizability of our findings to clinical scenarios, as the static in vitro environment cannot replicate systemic complexities such as the host immune response, dynamic mechanical loading, and metabolic degradation processes. Consequently, our conclusion that KNN/PLLA nanofibers promote bone regeneration is currently restricted to the cellular level rather than affirming organismal therapeutic efficacy. However, the robust concentration-dependent increase in ALP activity and mineralization, combined with the high statistical significance observed, provides a strong proof. This confirms that the 9 wt% KNN/PLLA system possesses the intrinsic bioactive potential required to modulate cell behavior via piezoelectric signals, establishing a solid scientific foundation for future animal studies. In addition, the current evaluations were conducted with a relatively limited sample size (n = 3), which may not fully capture the biological variability of BMSCs. While the observed concentration-dependent trends and high statistical significance provide an encouraging basis for our findings, we recognize the need for more robust validation. We plan to expand the number of replicates to ensure the broader reliability. While the current study lacks independent controls without LIPUS stimulation, the significant concentration-dependent increase in ALP activity and calcium deposition across groups under identical US parameters strongly indicates that the piezoelectric effect is the dominant driver of the observed osteogenic enhancement. This aligns with previous findings by Wang et al. [40], who demonstrated that the piezoelectric effect under US stimulation plays a far more critical role in cellular modulation than ultrasound or material surface properties alone. Although future studies with isolated control groups are required to explore these factors, the current design provides meaningful insights into the synergistic application of piezoelectricity and ultrasound in bone tissue engineering. The ultrasound parameters (intensity, frequency, and exposure time) in this experiment have not been fully optimized, which may affect the stability of the piezoelectric effect in KNN/PLLA nanofibers and influence cell growth and differentiation. Different ultrasound parameters may impact the piezoelectric effect and the reliability of bone repair outcomes. Thus, future studies should focus on optimizing ultrasound parameters and their combination with KNN content to ensure stability and reproducibility.

This study explored the effects of varying KNN content on the piezoelectric performance of KNN/PLLA nanofibers and their potential to promote osteogenic proliferation, differentiation, mineralization, and endothelial cell tube formation, highlighting the significant impact of the enhanced piezoelectric effect on osteogenesis and angiogenesis. Future research should focus on understanding the underlying mechanisms of the piezoelectric effect, including the optimal stimulation levels for cells, activated signaling pathways, and their effects. Additionally, the response of different bone cell types to piezoelectric stimulation requires further investigation. These insights will help optimize bone tissue regeneration and develop effective strategies for future tissue engineering applications.

Conclusions

In summary, this study successfully developed advanced KNN/PLLA nanofibers that effectively mimic the electro biological microenvironment of natural bone. The results demonstrate that KNN incorporation significantly enhances the piezoelectric properties and structural stability of the nanofibers. Bioassays reveal that these nanofibers favorably influence cell behavior, promoting both osteogenesis and angiogenesis. The integration of piezoelectric stimulation makes KNN/PLLA nanofibers a promising platform for enhancing bone tissue regeneration. These findings highlight the potential of functionalized materials in bone and vascular repair, offering a novel approach for future clinical applications.

Nanofiber fabrication

The electrospinning solution for KNN/PLLA nanofibers materials was prepared as follows: First, PLLA was dissolved in HFIP to create a 15% (w/v) solution. KNN was then added to the PLLA solution at different contents (0 wt%, 3 wt%, 6 wt%, and 9 wt%), and the mixture was stirred using a magnetic stirrer (1000 rpm) for 6 h. The prepared solution was loaded into a 20 mL syringe equipped with a 23G stainless-steel needle and installed on the electrospinning apparatus. The electrospinning parameters were as follows: needle at + 20 kV and collector at -1 kV, spinning distance of 18 cm, extrusion rate of 3 mm/min, temperature of 25 °C, and collector roller speed of 3500 rpm. After electrospinning, the nanofibers were naturally dried in a fume hood for 2 h, then transferred to a vacuum drying oven, where they were further dried at room temperature for 24 h until completely dry and stable.

Fiber morphology observation

To investigate the effect of different KNN content on the morphology of the KNN/PLLA nanofibers, SEM (Zeiss GeminiSEM 300, Germany) was used. High-resolution SEM images enabled us to characterize the fibers' morphology, including diameter, shape distribution, and surface texture. Thirty fibers from different regions of each image were analyzed for each sample. To accurately determine the average diameter of the fibers, all images were processed and analyzed using Image J (2.3.0). The software automatically calculated the fiber diameter distribution for the selected region and performed a statistical analysis to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the measurements. By comparing samples with different KNN content, the effect of KNN content on the fiber diameter and morphology was revealed.

XRD analysis

To examine the crystallinity and phase composition of the KNN/PLLA nanofibers, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed (Bruker D8 Advance, Germany). The samples were scanned using CuKα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at room temperature, with a scanning speed set to 0.02°/min. XRD patterns were obtained and analyzed using Jade 6.5 software for phase identification. The positions of the crystalline peaks were examined and compared with the PDF-#77-0038 database to identify the material phases.

Nb ion release measurement

The release of Nb ions from KNN/PLLA nanofibers was monitored using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy/Mass Spectrometry (ICP-OES/MS, Agilent 5110, USA). To simulate physiological degradation, nanofiber samples were immersed in deionized water and incubated at 37 °C with constant agitation on a shaker. At predetermined intervals (0, 3, and 7 days), the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove fiber debris. The Nb ion concentration was then quantified via ICP-OES/MS against a standard calibration curve. Cumulative release was calculated based on the measured concentrations to establish the time-dependent release profile.

Mechanical characterization

The mechanical properties of PLLA and KNN/PLLA nanofibers were evaluated using a universal testing machine (AGS X 50N, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Samples were cut into rectangular strips, and their thickness was measured with a digital micrometer. Tensile tests were conducted at a constant speed of 10 mm per minute at room temperature. The stress–strain curves were recorded, and the tensile strength was calculated by dividing the maximum load by the initial cross sectional area. Three independent specimens were tested per group, with results expressed as mean plus or minus standard deviation.

Piezoelectric performance testing

To evaluate the effect of different KNN contents on the piezoelectric performance of KNN/PLLA nanofibers, an electrostatic meter (Keithley 6517B) was used for testing. Ultrasound (Fuxin, BH200) with a sound intensity of 0.5W/cm2 and power of 1 W was applied as mechanical stimulation to generate an electric field in the piezoelectric materials, allowing us to record their voltage response. During the experiment, the nanofibers materials were subjected to mechanical pressure, and ultrasound was applied to them. The voltage and current outputs of the samples with different KNN contents were monitored in real time using an electrostatic meter. Through data analysis, we investigated the effect of KNN content on piezoelectric performance and explored their correlation. In addition, we used PFM (Bruker Dimension Icon, Germany) for further piezoelectric performance testing. These tests helped us understand how piezoelectric materials regulate cell behavior via electrical signals during bone repair.

Cell culture and proliferation

Rat bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were used for cell proliferation experiments. The cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. To evaluate the effects of different KNN contents under ultrasound on BMSCs proliferation, the CCK-8 assay was employed, 2 × 104 cells were seeded into a well of a 24-well plate, three replicates were set up for each group. Specifically, CCK-8 reagent was added to the cell cultures on days 1, 3 and 7. Cell proliferation was assessed by measuring absorbance at 450 nm (OD values) at the respective time points. To further assess cell viability, a Live/Dead staining assay was performed. In this assay, live cells were stained green and dead cells were stained red, and cell viability and death were visualized under a fluorescence microscope. The stained cells were visualized under a fluorescence microscope, and live cell density was quantified by counting the number of live cells in various fields of view, which illustrates the correlation between KNN content and cell viability. By evaluating the effects of varying KNN content on cell proliferation and viability, we systematically assessed the impact of piezoelectric performance on BMSCs growth.

SEM characterization of cell morphology

To visualize BMSCs attachment and spreading, cell-seeded 9 wt% KNN/PLLA nanofibers were collected at days 1, 3, and 7. The samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 4 h and rinsed with PBS. Dehydration was performed through a graded series of ethanol (30–100% v/v, 15 min each), followed by natural air drying at room temperature. Finally, the specimens were gold-sputtered and observed using a Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM, SU8020; Hitachi, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV.

Osteogenic differentiation and mineralization assays

To evaluate the osteogenic potential of KNN/PLLA nanofibers under ultrasound stimulation, rat BMSCs were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well and cultured in osteogenic induction medium. The progression of differentiation was monitored through alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and extracellular matrix mineralization.

Qualitative staining

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained using an ALP staining kit. The distribution and intensity of the purple-blue reaction products were observed and imaged via an optical microscope.

Quantitative measurement

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer and quantified with a BCA protein assay kit. Specific ALP activity was determined via the diethanolamine (DEA) method, measuring the hydrolysis rate of p-nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP) at 405 nm. Results were normalized to total protein content and expressed as × 10–3 DEA U/mg protein.

Qualitative staining

Extracellular matrix mineralization was visualized using Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining. Fixed cells were incubated with 1% ARS solution to identify calcium-rich nodules, which were then imaged to assess the distribution of mineralized crystals.

Quantitative measurement

To quantify calcium deposition, the ARS-stained mineralized matrix was desorbed using 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC) for 1 h. The absorbance of the extracted solution was measured at 550 nm using a microplate reader.

Osteogenic gene expression

The relative mRNA expression of OCN and RUNX2 was quantified using qRT-PCR. After 7 days induction period, total RNA was extracted from BMSCs using a total RNA isolation kit. The isolated RNA was then reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a prime script RT reagent kit. The amplification was performed using a SYBR Green qPCR master mix on a real-time PCR system. Target gene expression was normalized to glyceraldehyde 3 phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method. Results were expressed as the fold change relative to the pure PLLA group. The specific primer sequences, synthesized by servicebio (Wuhan, China), were as follows: GAPDH, forward CTGGAGAAACCTGCCAAGTATG, reverse GGTGGAAGAATGGGAGTTGCT; OCN (Bglap), forward TGACAAAGCCTTCATGTCCAA, reverse CTCCAAGTCCATTGTTGAGGTAG; RUNX2, forward CAGTATGAGAGTAGGTGTCCCGC, reverse AAGAGGGGTAAGACTGGTCATAGG. The annealing temperature for all primers was 60 °C.

Tube formation assay

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were used to evaluate tube formation ability. As a commonly used cell line in vascular biology research, HUVECs can form vascular-like structures in three-dimensional matrices, such as Matrigel. In the experiment, cells were seeded onto a Matrigel matrix, 6 × 104 cells were seeded into a well of a 24-well plate, and three replicates were set up for each group, and incubated under serum-free ECM culture conditions for 8 h. After live cell fluorescence staining, tube formation was observed under a fluorescence microscope, with a focus on the formation of vascular-like tubular structures. This experiment evaluated the effects of different KNN contents on ultrasound endothelial cell tube formation. After completing the endothelial tube formation assay, quantitative analysis was performed using image analysis software to assess parameters such as the number of niobium junctions, branch distribution, and total branch length. This further revealed the potential role of different piezoelectric effects generated by varying KNN content in promoting angiogenesis.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) (n = 3). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2). For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. Results with p > 0.05 were considered not statistically significant (ns).
