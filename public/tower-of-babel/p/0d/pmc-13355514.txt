Seroprevalence of Q-fever among domestic ruminants, humans and ticks in Ethiopia: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yihenew Getahun Ambaw, Mequanent Dessie Bitewa, Smegnew Gichew Wondie, Tadele Derbew Kassie, Zinie Abita Mengie, Simegnew Adugna Kallu, Berhanu Ayana Tafere, Tesfaye Shumet Mekonnen, Mikias Getahun Molla, Daniel Mulat Eshetu, Thomas Kidanemariam Yewodiaw, Ambachew Motbaynor Wubaye, Birhanu Feleke Shitu, Amare Mebrat Delie, Aysheshim Belaineh Haimanot, Yezbie Wagaw Ayicheh, Adugna Mengesha Mulugeta, Anley Shiferaw Enawgaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQ fever is a neglected zoonotic disease that poses a significant threat to the economy, animal health, and public health sectors worldwide, particularly in developing countries such as Ethiopia. In Ethiopia, where livestock production is the primary source of livelihood and food security for many communities, the implications of Q fever are particularly concerning. Despite its importance as a health issue for both animals and humans, there is insufficient national-level consolidated evidence regarding the burden of Q fever in ticks, domestic ruminants, and humans in Ethiopia. Therefore, this meta-analysis aims to assess the pooled prevalence of Q fever among ticks, ruminants, and humans in the country. A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines, using common databases such as Scopus, ScienceDirect, PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. The literature search was conducted from January 21 to January 30, 2026, to identify articles published in Ethiopia between January 1, 1990, and January 30, 2026. To evaluate the heterogeneity among studies, a random-effects model was employed to explore the pooled prevalence of Q fever in ticks, ruminants, and humans. Subgroup analysis, meta-regression, and sensitivity analysis were conducted to identify sources of variation between studies. Funnel plots, along with Egger’s and Begg’s correlation tests, were used to assess publication bias. The overall prevalence of Q fever across ticks, ruminants, and humans was 27.0% (95% CI: 20.0–34.0). The pooled prevalence of Q fever was 9% (95% CI: 2.0–16.0) in ticks, 29% (95% CI: 21.0–38.0) in ruminants, and 20% (95% CI: 5.0–36.0) in humans. The prevalence of Q fever was highest in Eastern Ethiopia at 41.0% (95% CI: 22.0–59.0) and lowest in Southwestern Ethiopia at 7.0% (95% CI: 2.0–13.0). Camels exhibited the highest exposure rate at 48.0% (95% CI: 0.0–1.0), while cattle had the lowest at 12.0% (95% CI: 4.0–21.0). The meta-regression analysis revealed that camel and goat species, along with the geographical regions of Eastern and Central Ethiopia, significantly affected the prevalence of Q fever. The findings highlight that Q fever continues to pose a significant threat to ticks, ruminants, and humans in Ethiopia. Therefore, collaboration among environmental, veterinary, and public health professionals is essential for designing and implementing effective prevention and control programs for Q fever in the context of a One Health approach.Keywords: Ethiopia, Humans, Meta-analysis, Prevalence, Q fever, Ruminants, Ticks

Introduction

Q fever is a neglected zoonotic disease caused by the gram-negative, intracellular, spore-forming bacteria, Coxiella burnetii (Bwatota et al., 2022). This bacterium causes Q fever in humans and coxiellosis in animals (Robi et al., 2023). The disease affects a wide range of hosts, including domestic animals, humans, wild animals, ticks, reptiles, fish, and birds (WOAH, 2019). Q fever is considered a vector-borne disease, but the role of ticks in its transmission remains unclear. Although C. burnetii is excreted in the faeces and saliva of ticks, the significance of these findings and their epidemiological context are still debated (Yessinou et al., 2022). Although Q fever in ruminants is often subclinical (Bwatota et al., 2022), it can lead to serious complications during the later stages of pregnancy, such as abortion, perinatal death, premature birth, reduced birth weight, and impaired fertility. These issues ultimately result in decreased productivity and substantial economic losses for farmers, especially in low-resource settings (Agerholm, 2013; Robi et al., 2023).

Several factors, including host characteristics, environmental conditions, and management practices, can influence the occurrence of C. burnetii infection in animals (Bwatota et al., 2022). Infected ruminants serve as the primary reservoirs for the pathogen, making them important sources of infection for humans (Bwatota et al., 2022; Robi et al., 2023). Ruminants shed the bacteria during birth or late-term abortion, leading to environmental contamination and increased exposure for other animals and humans (Bwatota et al., 2022). The principal source of human infection is the consumption of contaminated milk and milk products, although transmission can also occur via aerosols and dust generated during livestock operations (Robi et al., 2023). Vaccination and biosecurity measures are effective strategies for managing C. burnetii infection in animals (Bwatota et al., 2022; Robi et al., 2023).

The first report of Q fever in Ethiopia was published in 1966 by Philip et al. (1966) who identified the disease in ticks among cattle. A subsequent report in 1990 by Abebe (1990) documented Q fever in humans in the Addis Ababa abattoir. Recently, Ethiopia has prioritized Q fever in its national health strategies through a One Health approach, recognizing it as a second-tier disease in the national One Health strategic plan (2018–2022) (FDRE, 2018). These initiatives are part of Ethiopia's broader commitment to the global health security agenda, which aims to prevent and respond to various health threats through enhanced surveillance and biosecurity measures to control the spread of zoonotic diseases like Q fever (Nyokabi et al., 2023).

Research on Q fever in animals and humans in Ethiopia has primarily utilized serological methods (ELISA, IFA, CFT) and PCR in ticks. Recent advancements in diagnostic technologies have significantly improved the ability to identify C. burnetii in both animals and humans. Currently, advanced molecular methods, such as PCR, immune-chromatographic tests, and nano-electrochemical biosensors, provide highly sensitive and specific diagnoses (Sedky et al., 2025). In the context of advancing research and clinical practices in the future, developing nations, including Ethiopia, are urged to implement and utilize advanced diagnostic methodologies. These approaches aim to enhance the identification and detection of strain diversity and species hybridization between animals and humans. Furthermore, the adoption of predictive modelling is suggested to ascertain the potential progression of diseases, thus contributing significantly to public health efforts and disease management strategies in these regions (Kallu et al., 2026).

In Latin America, the prevalence of Q fever ranges from 1.7% to 61.0% in humans and from 0.2% to 60.6% in ruminants (de França, 2025). The pooled prevalence of C. burnetii in ticks ranges from 4.8% to 12.5% in the Middle East, 2.5% to 10.5% in Europe, 0.0% to 100.0% in Latin America and the Caribbean (Epelboin et al., 2023) and 2.9% to 14.0% in Africa (Yessinou et al., 2022). In Ethiopia, the seroprevalence of Q fever among cattle in the Tigray region is documented at 0.0%, while in camels in the Oromia and Somali regions is markedly elevated at 90.0%. Among humans, the seroprevalence rates exhibit significant variation, ranging from 6.5% in Addis Ababa (Abebe, 1990) to 27.2% in the Afar region (Oaklay et al., 2024).

Examining the epidemiology of Q fever in ticks, animals, and humans is essential for understanding how the disease spreads within the One Health framework. This integrated approach is vital for tackling Q fever in animals, humans, and the surrounding environment in Ethiopia. A thorough understanding of the interactions among these factors is necessary for developing and implementing targeted intervention strategies that align with One Health principles (Islam et al., 2025). The ultimate goal is to reduce the incidence of the disease to an acceptable level within the Ethiopian context.

Although Q fever is a significant zoonotic disease, its burden and distribution in developing countries, especially Ethiopia, are not well understood (Robi et al., 2023). Ethiopia lacks comprehensive national-level evidence on the pooled prevalence of Q fever in ticks, ruminants, and humans, despite some existing, fragmented, studies. To design and implement effective prevention and control programs for Q fever in Ethiopia within the context of a One Health framework, comprehensive national-level pooled data are required. This gap emphasizes the need for consolidated evidence to inform effective prevention and control programs within the One Health framework. To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies published over the past 35 years, from 1990 to 2025. Thus, synthesizing data across different geographic regions and production systems is essential to understand the scale of the problem, its drivers, and to support evidence-based disease management decisions.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

Literature searches were performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) recommendations (Parums, 2021). Electronic databases and search engines, such as Google Scholar, Scopus, ScienceDirect, PubMed, and Web of Science, were employed to identify relevant articles published between 1990 and 2025. The comprehensive searching strategy used condition, context, and population (CoCoPop) (Munn et al., 2015), formulating questions and searching terms, including prevalence, seroprevalence, incidence, Q fever, Query fever, coxiellosis, animals, ruminants, cattle, sheep, goat, camel, human, tick, epidemiology and Ethiopia. Boolean operators such as “OR” and “AND” were appropriately used to combine search terms to fit the advanced searching of literature based on the formulation of the research questions. Additionally, the protocol was officially registered with PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) before the start of data collection.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

In this study, all articles conducted by a cross-sectional study design in Ethiopia about the prevalence of Q fever and published in peer-reviewed journals in the English language were eligible for inclusion. All articles reported about knowledge, attitudes, and practices of Q fever, studies with limited records on outcomes, personal opinions, correspondence, letters to the editor, proceedings, and reviews were excluded from the study.

Study selection and quality assessment

The qualities of the incorporated articles were evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute's critical appraisal tool, intended to assess prevalence studies (Munn et al., 2015). The tool has nine items, each measured with four alternatives: “yes,” “no,” “not applicable”, and “unclear,” which assess study quality, including bias, confounding, the validity of exposure and outcome measurements, and the validity of analysis methods. The evaluation of the included articles was performed by two independent teams: Team one (YGA, MDB, and SGW) and Team two (TDK, ZAM, and SAK). By using this critical appraisal tool, studies were appraised, and those studies which had a score of >75% were considered as high quality, whereas those articles having 50–75% were assigned good quality. Any disagreements between the two group reviewers were resolved through both group discussions. Ultimately, studies of good and high quality were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome of the study was prevalence of Q fever in domestic ruminants, ticks and humans in Ethiopia. Moreover, the study explores factors associated with Q fever.

Data collection

Data from primary articles were extracted independently by two research teams: Team one (BAT, TSM, and MGM) and Team two (DME, TKY, and AME) by using a predefined data extraction sheet in Microsoft Excel thirteen spread sheet. The extracted data were study population, species of domestic ruminants in which the study was conducted, year of publication, the name of the first author, geographic region where the study was conducted, sample size, diagnostic test employed, and sex of the study population.

Data management and statistical analysis

Data management, visualization, and analysis were conducted using R Studio version 4.4.2. (R Core Team, 2025) using the meta package. By considering the significant heterogeneity observed across studies, with significant between-study variability indicated by I² values of 98.7% for Q fever, a random-effects meta-analysis model was employed.

DerSimonian-Laird (DL) method of estimating was used to estimate between-study variance (DerSimonian & Laird, 2015). To enhance uncertainty quantification, confidence intervals were adjusted via the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) procedure (Christodoulou et al., 2023). The pooled prevalence of Q fever was graphically demonstrated by using a forest plot to display individual study estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CI) (IntHout et al., 2016). Between-study heterogeneity, attributable to methodological variation, was quantified using the Cochrane Q-test (Cochran, 1954), and the I² statistic (proportion of total variability due to heterogeneity rather than sampling error) (Higgins & Thompson, 2002).

In addition, to explore the heterogeneity observed in the pooled estimate derived from the random-effects model, sensitivity analysis, Baujat diagnostic plot (Baujat et al., 2002), and meta-regression analysis were performed. Furthermore, to explore heterogeneity among studies, both univariable and multivariable meta-regression models were conducted for moderators such as species of studied population, diagnostic test, sample size, publication year, sex, and geographical region of the studies. Eventually, publication bias was also assessed by using graphical and statistical methods. Graphically, the symmetry of the funnel plots was assessed, and statistically, Egger et al. (1997) and Begg & Mazumdar, 1994) rank correlation tests were conducted. A significant test result (p < 0.05) indicated the presence of significant publication bias or small-study effects in the meta-analysis.

Result

Eligible studies from literature search

Among the 776 studies initially retrieved through electronic searches and manual screening of reference lists for the prevalence of Q fever, 329 articles were excluded due to duplication. Following the review of titles and abstracts, 273 articles were excluded because their content was unrelated. Subsequently, the full texts of 174 articles were evaluated against the eligibility criteria. Ultimately, 155 studies were excluded for various reasons, including study design (3), studies conducted outside of Ethiopia (85), articles that did not report on the target population (19), absence of full information (25) and review articles (23). After conducting a quality assessment of the methodological approaches, 19 articles (covering 37 Multiple species) were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis on the prevalence of Q fever in domestic ruminants, ticks and humans in Ethiopia (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram for the selected eligible studies about Q fever in Ethiopia.

Characteristics of the included studies

The studies were conducted from 1990 to 2025 and the review included data from 19 articles published in Ethiopia. Among the 37 species-specific studies, the distribution by study population was 10 for cattle, 9 for goat, 7 for sheep, 4 for camel, 3 for tick and 4 for human. In terms of the species of study ruminant, the seroprevalence of Q fever varies from 48.0% in camel to 12.0% in cattle. Regarding geographical region, studies were distributed as follows: 9 in Eastern Ethiopia, 8 in Central Ethiopia, 6 in Northern Ethiopia, 5 in South Eastern Ethiopia and 3 in South Western Ethiopia (Table 1, Table 2). The sample sizes ranged from 35 to 1564 in the multi-species study population, with apparent seroprevalence varying from 7.0% in South Western Ethiopia to 41.0% in Eastern Ethiopia (Table 1). Across all studies, the total sample comprised 13,071 ruminants and humans, of which 3096 (23.7%) tested seropositive for Q fever (Table 1). Regarding diagnostic tests, most of the included studies employed ELISA (n = 28), followed by IFA (n = 5), PCR (n = 3) and CFT (n = 1). Most of the included studies were published between 2021 and 2025 (n = 20), whereas the remaining studies were published between 1990 and 2020 (n = 17).

Pooled and subgroup analysis of Q fever in domestic ruminants and humans in Ethopia

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, the pooled prevalence of Q fever was 28.0% (95% CI: 21.0–36.0) (Fig. 2), demonstrating a significant heterogeneity among pooled individual studies (I2 = 98.7%, Q33 = 2524.7, p < 0.001). This high heterogeneity was also supported by a wide predictive interval (0.0–71.0%), indicating a significant variation among the included studies for the prevalence of Q fever. Due to this high heterogeneity, a random effects model was employed to compute the pooled prevalence of Q fever in ruminants and humans.

Overall seroprevalence of Q fever among domestic ruminants and humans in Ethiopia (n = 13071), by excluding studies on ticks.

A subgroup analysis was also employed to evaluate the influence of categorical variables such as study population, species of ruminant, geographic region, diagnostic test, publication year, and sample size for the prevalence of Q fever in ruminants and humans (Table 2). Interms of the ruminant species, camels were more exposed to Q fever with the prevalence of 48.0% (95% CI: 0.0–100.0), followed by goats 43.0% (95% CI: 30.0–57.0) and sheep 25.0% (95% CI: 14.0–37.0). Cattle and human were less exposed with the seroprevalence of 12.0% (95% CI: 4.0–21.0) and 20.0% (95% CI: 5.0–36.0), respectively. This seroprevalence was statistically significant among the species (Q4 = 22.79, p < 0.001) (Table 2 and Fig. 3).

Subgroup analysis of Q fever among domestic animals and humans by species (n = 13071), by excluding studies related to ticks.

Regarding geographical region, the prevalence of Q fever was higher in Eastern Ethiopia 41.0%, (95% CI: 22.0–59.0), followed by Southern Ethiopia 30.0% (95% CI: 18.0–41.0), Central part Ethiopia 27.0% (95% CI: 6.0–47.0), Northern Ethiopia 25.0%, (95% CI: 4.0–46.0), and lowest in South Western Ethiopia 7.0% (95% CI: 2.0–13.0) with a significant statistical difference between geographical regions (Q5 = 48.94, p < 0.001) (Table 2 and Fig. 4).

Subgroup analysis of Q fever among domestic animals and humans by geographic region (n = 13071), by excluding studies related to ticks.

The prevalence of Q fever was also evaluated using the employed diagnostic tests, resulting in a higher prevalence of Q fever when using ELISA, 28.0% (95% CI: 20.0–37.0), compared to IFA and CFT, revealing statistically significant variation between diagnostic tests (Q2 = 34.9, p < 0.001) (Table 3). Regarding publication year, studies conducted from 1990 to 2020 37.0% (95% CI: 23.0–51.0) had a higher prevalence of Q fever relative to studies published from 2021 to 2025 22.0% (95% CI: 14.0–30.0) with a statistically significant difference (Q2 = 4.08, p = 0.043) (Table 3).

Publication bias

The effect of publication bias was explored through funnel plots (Fig. 6) graphically and Begg’s and Egger’s tests statistically. An asymmetrical distribution of effect size on the funnel plot indicates the presence of publication bias among Q fever studies in ruminants and humans. The graphical result was also supported by the statistical tests with a p < 0.05; Begg’s (Kendall's score = 262.00, z = 3.84, p = 0.0001) and Egger’s (β = 9.24, z = 5.54, p < 0.0001) demonstrating the presence of significant publication bias. Considering Duval and Tweedie’s trim-and-fill analysis technique, 14 additional studies were missed (Fig. 7). To address the presence of publication bias, we had imputed those 14 missed studies. As a result, the pooled prevalence of Q fever in ruminants and humans declined from 28.0% (95% CI: 21.0–36.0) to 12.6% (95% CI: 3.3–21.8) with a prediction interval of (0.0-76.8), demonstrating that the finding was influenced by the presence of publication biases.

To explore the presence of temporal trend over time for the prevalence of Q fever among ruminants and humans between studies, a random effects meta-regression model was fitted. The prevalence of Q fever demonstrated an insignificant, gently declining temporal trend over time (β = −0.0035, t = −0.607, p = 0.548) (Fig. 10). More specifically, for every one-year increment, the average seroprevalence of Q fever decreased by 0.35%.

Meta-regression models for the prevalence of Q fever in farm animals and humans

Univariable and multivariable meta-regression models were conducted to further explore the sources of variability among the included studies for the prevalence of Q fever. In both univariable and multivariable analysis, moderators such as publication year and sample size were treated as continuous predictors, while the study's geographical region, species, diagnostic test, sex and study quality score were analyzed as categorical variables within a mixed-effects framework. The predictors were evaluated for linear associations with the prevalence of Q fever in ruminants and humans. Species of the study population and geographic region were the strongest explanatory factors for the prevalence of Q fever studies, accounting for 32.65% and 19.49% of total heterogeneity (R² = 32.65% and R² = 19.49%), respectively, in the univariable regression analysis. The multivariable model accounted 43.22% (R² = 43.22%) of the total heterogeneity observed in this analysis (Table 3). The multivariable meta-regression model indicates that the species of study participants and the geographic region are significant risk factors associated with the prevalence of Q fever in animals and humans in Ethiopia. The likelihood of getting Q fever antibody in camels serum increase by 34.00% compared to cattle. For goats, the seroprevalence of Q fever was 1.35 times more likely compared to cattle, by holding all other variables in the model constant. Additionally, the seroprevalence of Q fever increases by 16.00% in sheep compared to cattle. In central Ethiopia, study participants show a 16.00% lower seroprevalence of Q fever compared to those in eastern Ethiopia. The likelihood of getting Q fever antibody was reduced by 22.00% for study participants living in mixed geographic regions compared to those residents in eastern Ethiopia. The seroprevalence of Q fever is 23.00% lower in participants from northern Ethiopia compared to those from eastern Ethiopia. For study participants found in south-west Ethiopia, the seroprevalence of Q fever decreases by 16.00% compared to those found in eastern Ethiopia by holding other variables in the model constant (Table 3).

Relation between sex and the prevalence of Q fever in humans and ruminants

Among the total 34 included studies, 9 reported on the association of sex and the prevalence of Q fever in ruminants and humans. Thus, the odds of developing Q fever in females were >83% compared to males (OR = 1.83, 95% CI: 1.28–2.60) with a significant statistical difference between these groups (Fig. 11).

Discussion

Q fever is a significant neglected zoonotic disease that poses severe risks to public health and the veterinary sector. It has a considerable socioeconomic impact on the livestock industry, leading to issues such as abortion, stillbirth, premature birth, and infertility. To effectively prevent and control Q fever in both humans and animals, it is essential to have a comprehensive understanding of its transmission routes and sources of infection, especially in developing countries like Ethiopia (Celina & Cerný, 2022). This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to explore the pooled prevalence of Q fever in ticks, farm animals, and humans in Ethiopia. The objective is to provide consolidated evidence that can help to develop effective prevention and control strategies, ultimately reducing the public health and veterinary burdens while benefiting the country’s economy and community well-being. The findings will assist stakeholders in implementing targeted interventions in the One Health framework.

Combining different host populations, such as ticks, humans, and animals, in a meta-analysis is a powerful technique for increasing sample size and statistical power. However, this approach inherently introduces significant heterogeneity (Choi & Kang, 2025). Rather than viewing heterogeneity as inherently good or bad, researchers should recognize it as a crucial aspect of systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Choi & Kang, 2025).

The extreme heterogeneity observed in this meta-analysis may be driven by different ecological, biological, and geographical contexts in which C. burnetii occurs. This high level of variability presents substantial challenges to the interpretation, validity and application of findings, effectively limiting the usefulness of a single pooled estimate (Christodoulou et al., 2023). Key limitations of blending different hosts include high heterogeneity driven by differences in host genetics, immunology, and environmental factors, as well as an increased risk of confounding variables that may obscure true effect sizes. Therefore, a careful assessment of heterogeneity is essential for improving the reliability, applicability, and overall impact of pooled findings (IntHout et al., 2016). This is typically achieved through the use of random-effects models, subgroup analyses, prediction intervals, sensitivity analyses, influence analyses, and meta-regression models to ensure that the results are robust (Choi & Kang, 2025).

The overall prevalence of Q fever among ticks, ruminants and humans in the current meta-analysis was 27.0% (95% CI: 20.0–34.0). This finding was higher than the meta-analysis in Pakistan, 15.0% (Munir et al., 2025). This higher prevalence of Q fever in Ethiopia indicates the presence of a significant economic impact on livestock farmers, resulting from issues such as abortion, stillbirths, reduced body condition, decreased milk production, and compromised milk quality (Robi et al., 2023). Additionally, Q fever poses an occupational hazard, primarily affecting individuals such as veterinarians, milkers, artificial inseminators, animal attendants, and those who consume unpasteurized or inadequately boiled milk, putting them at a greater risk of infection (Christodoulou et al., 2023). Furthermore, the higher prevalence of Q fever in Ethiopia may be due to a lack of awareness and inadequate veterinary and medical support. As a result, many cases in both humans and animals could go undiagnosed or misidentified as fevers of unknown origin. Therefore, it is essential to prioritize prevention and control measures targeting Q fever in different regions of Ethiopia.

In addition, efforts to reduce Q fever in Ethiopia have been hindered by the negligence of policymakers' and the expansion of illegal livestock trade with neighboring countries. In East African countries, over 95% of cross-border trade was conducted through illegal routes. The illegal movement of livestock from Ethiopia to Kenya, Djibouti, and Somalia generates between $250 million and $300 million annually (Pavanello, 2010). This illegal trade contributes to the spread of zoonotic diseases like Q fever across borders. However, the link between zoonotic diseases and illegal animal trade has not yet been thoroughly explored (Hikal et al., 2023). To mitigate the impact of zoonotic diseases, such as Q fever, on both animals and humans, it is recommended that East African countries, including Ethiopia, adopt and enforce the existing One Health policies proposed by the WOAH (WOAH, 2019; Ambaw et al., 2025).

Subgroup analysis by the study population demonstrated that higher prevalence of Q fever was found in ruminants followed by humans and ticks. The present pooled prevalence (29.0%) of Q fever in ruminant was comparable with existed reports in the Eastern Mediterranean region 22.6% (Ahmadinezhad et al., 2022). However, this finding was higher than the reports from South Asian Countries 11.9% (Islam et al., 2025). This variation in prevalence of Q fever may be attributed to differences in the management conditions of animals, the level of health service and environmental conditions in different settings.

The subgroup analysis by geographic region also demonstrated that the prevalence of Q fever in ruminant was higher in Eastern (41.0%) and South Eastern (30.0%) Ethiopia and lower in South Western Ethiopia (7.0%). However, the number of studies included in these geographic regions of Ethiopia remains low (9 in Eastern Ethiopia, 5 in South-Eastern Ethiopia and 3 in South-Western Ethiopia), indicating the need for further studies to generate more robust evidence. This higher prevalence of Q fever can be attributed to the fact that almost all parts of Somali and some portions of the Afar region are primarily found in the Eastern part of Ethiopia which is dominated by arid and semi-arid areas where the environmental conditions favor the survival and transmission of C. burnetii, with frequent exposure of ruminant to sandstorms and dust. In addition, in this geographic region, the pastoral communities mostly reared camels and goats, which are mostly exposed to Q fever infection in this study.

Besides, both ruminants and humans are more exposed to Q fever in droughts areas could be the bacteria’s capacity to survive in the dry environment. The bacteria that cause Q fever are resistant to dehydration and UV radiation. Coxiella burnetii spores are very resilient and able to survive in soil or dust for many years. This also helps the bacteria spread: it can attach to dust and travel 10 km or more on winds (Celina & Cerný, 2022; WOAH, 2019; ). The current finding underscore the influence of environmental and geographical factors on the transmission of Q fever in ruminants, highlighting the need for control strategies that are tailored to specific regions. Additionally, lack of awareness among pastoral communities about zoonotic transmission, close contact with infected animals, and the consumption of raw milk contribute to the persistence and spread of Q fever. The extensive pastoral lifestyle, characterized by high animal movement and the mixing of herds, further facilitates transmission. Furthermore, occupational exposure and unsafe handling of dystocia cases in animals increase the risk for animals and human in the pastoral areas (Tan et al., 2023).

Regarding to the species of ruminants, camel (48.0%) had been found higher prevalence of Q fever followed by goats (43.0%), sheep (25.0%) and cattle (12.0%). The result demonstrated that the prevalence of Q fever in camel and goat was higher than in sheep and cattle. The higher prevalence of Q fever in camels and goats might be attributable to camels and goats are predominantly reared in arid and semi-arid areas where environmental conditions favor the survival and transmission of C. burnetii with frequent exposure of the animals to sandstorms and dust. Additionally, camel’s broader-ranging behaviors increases their exposure to tick infested areas and contact with wildlife that may serve as reservoirs (Celina & Cerný, 2022). Goats are the most commonly marketed animals in the region, exposing them repeatedly to diseased animals at marketplaces and collection sites (Kichamu et al., 2024). Furthermore, goats are more prone to frequent kidding and have high placental shedding of C. burnetii during abortion events, which is a major source of environmental contamination and infection spread (Muleme et al., 2017).

Besides, camel and goat are often raised in extensive management systems or co-graze with other species, which increases the opportunity for the disease to spread among different herds and even wildlife. In contrast, cattle are more often kept in more confined, intensive systems with better biosecurity, which can limit exposure to Q fever. In addition, camel is particularly susceptible to heavy tick burdens, especially from species like Hyalomma ticks, which are significant vectors for C. burnetii transmission. Higher tick burdens in camel herds correlate with increased Q fever seroprevalence (Celina & Cerný, 2022).

Regarding diagnostic methods, the prevalence of Q fever was higher in study participants diagnosed by IFA (30.0%) than ELISA (28.0%) and CFT (6.0%), with a significant statistical difference. The majority of the included studies used ELISA followed by IFA as a detection method, which is in line with other previous reports (Ahaduzzaman & Reza, 2024; Bwatota et al., 2022; Wegdam-Blans et al., 2012). This is due to IFA detects a higher prevalence of C. burnetii antibodies (has higher sensitivity) compared to the ELISA and the CFT, especially in follow-up samples. This higher prevalence of Q fever in IFA diagnostic test could be it is the reference standard for Q fever diagnosis due to its high sensitivity and ability to differentiate between acute and chronic infections by detecting phase I and phase II antibodies especially in humans. Human studies show that IFA detects more positive cases than the other methods, particularly in follow-up sera (Wegdam-Blans et al., 2012). Most of the analyzed studies used ELISA as a diagnostic test, which detects antibodies indicating prior exposure to C. burnetii, rather than confirming an active infection or clinical Q fever. Therefore, it’s important to distinguish between historical exposure and active cases among study participants.

Bias from diagnostic differences in studies assessing Q fever prevalence can significantly affect the validity of meta-analytic findings (Ahmadinezhad et al., 2022). Factors such as diagnostic methods, test sensitivity and specificity, and population characteristics can lead to varying estimates of prevalence. This variability complicates the aggregation of data and may result in overestimation or underestimation of prevalence. To address this issue, standardizing diagnostic criteria and conducting sensitivity analyses are essential (Choi & Kang, 2025). By recognizing and mitigating these biases, researchers can improve the accuracy of prevalence estimates and support effective public health responses to Q fever.

Subgroup analysis by years of publication revealed that the prevalence of Q fever was declining from 37.0% in 1990–2020 to 22.0% from 2021–2025, with a significant statistical difference. However, most of the included studies were found in 2021–2025 compared to 1990–2020, which may create some biases in this report. This decline in prevalence was supported by the trend analysis, which demonstrated that Q fever prevalence was slightly decreasing over time, but the reduction in trend analysis was not statistically significant. This result was comparable with a recent global and regional systematic review and meta-analysis in small ruminants (Ahaduzzaman & Reza, 2024). On the other hand, Ethiopia has implemented several national initiatives to combat zoonotic diseases in animals and humans, such as awareness campaigns, veterinary services, mass drug administration, integrated control strategies, and research collaborations, which may reduce the diseases to some level (FDRE, 2018).

In the multivariable meta-regression model, geographic region and species of the study population were significant factors. The prevalence of Q fever was higher in Eastern, South and Central Ethiopia than in South Western and Northern Ethiopia. The higher prevalence of Q fever in Eastern Ethiopia could be due to the large pastoralist populations, rural communities, and the practices of mixing herds and sharing watering resources, which increase transmission between animals and to humans. In these communities, risky behaviors are common, including handling birth fluids and consumption of raw milk (Oakley et al., 2024).

The pooled prevalence of Q fever in humans was 20.0% (95% CI: 5.0–36.0). The high prevalence of Q fever in humans in this meta-analysis may indicate the strong link between humans and ruminants in the pastoralist areas of Eastern Ethiopia. The finding was in line with reports in the Eastern Mediterranean region, 25.5% (Ahmadinezhad et al., 2022), in Iran, 26.3% (Mohabbati Mobarez et al., 2017), in South Asian Countries in general, 9.2% (Islam et al., 2025) and specifically in Bangladesh, 4.2%, in India 12.0% and in Pakistan, 18.8% (Islam et al., 2025) and in Guinea, 24.5% (Mediannikov et al., 2010). This variation in human Q fever prevalence may be associated with different agro-ecology in different countries, the employed diagnostic tests to identify the diseases, the level of human and veterinary health services and the management of reservoir hosts (Hussen et al., 2023).

On the other hand, this elevated rate of Q fever infection in humans may be attributed to the transmission from infected animals and ticks. This scenario poses significant health risks for livestock workers and animal handlers, particularly in rural communities (Clark & Soares Magalhaes, 2018). Furthermore, empirical evidence indicates a high prevalence of raw milk consumption within livestock-rearing pastoral regions of Ethiopia. Such practices significantly increase the risk of zoonotic disease transmission from animals to humans (Tschopp et al., 2021). This phenomenon may be influenced by the cultural beliefs prevalent among pastoral societies, which often regard raw animal milk as superior in taste and associate it with various health benefits, including aphrodisiac properties (Njenga et al., 2020).

To effectively address the issue of Q fever in animals and humans, it is crucial to implement various targeted interventions. These include public awareness campaigns, the use of protective clothing for handling aborted animals, and the pasteurization of milk. Additional measures should involve animal vaccination, proper hygiene practices, quarantine protocols, and serological testing to identify infected animals. It is also essential to strengthen collaboration between public health and veterinary sectors as a One Health framework (Almuzaini et al., 2025).

The pooled prevalence of Q fever in ticks was 9.0% (2.0, 16.0). The finding aligns with reports from Pakistan, which also showed a prevalence of 9.0% (Munir et al., 2025), Guinea at 5.4% (Camara et al., 2026) and 7.2% (Ohlopkova et al., 2023), and Europe at 4.8% (Körner et al., 2021). Conversely, this result is lower than those reported in the Eastern Mediterranean region, which had a prevalence of 17.5% (Ahmadinezhad et al., 2022), and higher than the report from Africa, which recorded a prevalence of 0.0% (Cossu et al., 2023). This high prevalence of C. burnetii suggests that ticks may act as potential reservoirs and vectors, playing a crucial role in maintaining the natural transmission cycle of this pathogen. On the other hand, the vector competence of ticks in transmitting C. burnetii remains a subject of ongoing scientific debate (Camara et al., 2026).

Importantly, all studies conducted in Ethiopia about ticks did not include genotyping of C. burnetii, relying solely on an unspecified qPCR assay targeting the IS1111 gene. The observed prevalence rate of Q fever in ticks falls within the range of 0.0% to 37.6% reported for C. burnetii detection in ticks collected from domestic animals in Senegal (Mediannikov et al., 2010). This variability in Q fever prevalence may stem from regional differences in tick distribution, which could be influenced by the composition of tick species, host reservoirs, or various environmental factors (Camara et al., 2026). For instance, the study conducted in Guinea included both genotyping and strain isolation of C. burnetii (Camara et al., 2026), in contrast to the studies carried out in Ethiopia. The detection of C. burnetii MST 6 in ticks, as well as in animal and human samples in Africa, specifically in Senegal and Guinea, emphasizes the need for broader genetic diversity studies of C. burnetii among animals and humans, particularly in rural communities. In these areas, the genetic diversity of C. burnetii is significant. Such evidence could aid in predicting and potentially preventing Q fever outbreaks. The identification of novel MST groups and alleles in the Republic of Guinea may reflect local evolutionary dynamics or the introduction of diverse strains due to animal movement from neighboring countries (Camara et al., 2026).

Among the included studies in this meta-analysis, only nine reports examine the relationship between sex and the prevalence of Q fever. Four studies (Alemayehu et al., 2026; Ibrahim et al., 2021; Marami et al., 2025) revealed that the prevalence of Q fever was significantly higher in females compared to males. While the remaining five studies showed absence of significant difference between female and male participants. The current meta-analysis of all nine included studies demonstrated that females were more likely to be positive for C. burnetii infection than males. This report was in agreement with a recent meta-analysis on the global and regional seroprevalence of Q fever in small ruminants (Ahaduzzaman & Reza, 2024), in Africa among domestic ruminants (Bwatota et al., 2022) and the Eastern Mediterranean region (Ahmadinezhad et al., 2022), where the prevalence of Q fever was significantly higher in females than males. Similarly, several individual studies reported a higher prevalence of Q fever in female study participants (Mangena et al., 2021; Sakhaee & Khalili, 2010; Siengsanan-Lamont et al., 2023). This higher prevalence of Q fever in the female study population could be attributed to C. burnetii’s greater affinity to reproductive organs such as the uterus, mammary glands, and placenta (Roest et al., 2020; Sobotta et al., 2017), from which it may be shed in subsequent parturitions and lactations. In addition, females are kept in production systems for longer periods for breeding and production, resulting in prolonged exposure to contaminated environments, while males, on the other hand, are frequently sold at an early age or raised separately, lowering their infection risk (Tschopp et al., 2022). However, other studies reported that female and male participants had similar prevalence of Q fever (Gebretensay et al., 2019; Getachew et al., 2024; Haile et al., 2023; Ibrahim et al., 2021; Islam et al., 2025).

In Ethiopia, there is a notable correlation between Q fever prevalence in tick, animal and human populations, particularly in the eastern regions, which exhibit the highest rates. However, the presence of only four studies among humans limits the ability to draw strong conclusions. This prevalence poses a significant public health concern and highlights the importance of the One Health framework, which focuses on the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health. Understanding this shared prevalence is crucial for developing effective strategies to reduce the impact of Q fever on humans and animals in Ethiopia (Kallu et al., 2026).

The significant prevalence of Q fever in ticks underscores their role as important vectors for the disease. Ticks can harbor C. burnetii, the etiological agent of Q fever, and could transmit this pathogen to livestock and potentially to humans through bites or contaminated environments (Yessinou et al., 2022). The observed correlation between tick prevalence and animal infection rates suggests that regions with higher tick populations may experience increased transmission rates among livestock, thereby elevating the risk of human infection. Goats and camels exhibited high rates of exposure to Q fever due to their proximity to human populations in rural areas, where they were commonly raised for milk and meat production. This close association increased the likelihood of human exposure to pathogens carried by these animals, particularly in pastoral communities (Bwatota et al., 2022; Robi et al., 2023). Furthermore, inadequate biosecurity measures on dairy farms and the nomadic movements of herds during dry seasons lead to the mixing of animals with different health statuses, facilitating disease transmission. In Ethiopia, the common practice of consuming unpasteurized dairy products provides another route for infection in humans (Robi et al., 2023). These high prevalence rates in ticks and specific livestock types serve as critical indicators of potential human exposure and risk. Therefore, addressing these interconnections through targeted One Health strategies will be vital in mitigating the impact of Q fever in affected regions such as Ethiopia (Bwatota et al., 2022; WOAH, 2019). Further investigation may be necessary to explore the ecological factors influencing these patterns and to develop effective interventions tailored to local contexts.

Limitation of the study

The first limitation of this meta-analysis is the presence of inadequate reporting in some regions, which may lead to a biased estimation of the national-level prevalence of Q fever among the study populations. Additionally, all studies conducted in ruminants and humans relied on serological tests, including I-ELISA, IFA, and CFT, which can yield false-negative or false-positive results. As a result, these findings may not accurately represent the true burden of Q fever among ruminants and humans. ELISA detects antibodies that indicate past exposure to pathogens. However, this can lead to an overestimation of the pathogen’s active or clinical burden. Furthermore, in large-scale epidemiological studies, ELISA may produce inflated estimates of Q fever seroprevalence due to cross-reactivity with other bacterial species. Furthermore, the exclusion of studies published in non-English-language literature is another limitation. This meta-analysis also did not account for various biological and management-related factors that could influence the prevalence of Q fever, due to the limited available evidence. Consequently, differences in ecological and animal management practices may not be adequately represented. The final limitation may be the combination of different host populations, which could complicate the interpretation of results and the validity of the conclusions.

Conclusion

In Ethiopia, the prevalence of Q fever among ticks, ruminants, and humans is remarkably high. Significant risk factors include being a camel and goat, residing in Eastern Ethiopia, and being female. Consequently, policymakers, as well as environmental, veterinary, and public health professionals, are encouraged to prioritize evidence-based control programs that target high-burden regions and susceptible ruminant species. These programs should be aligned with the One Health framework to effectively combat the spread of the disease. It is essential to implement targeted interventions for female ruminants that are more susceptible to Q fever, along with improving access to veterinary health services in arid and semi-arid regions. Additionally, further research is needed to assess the economic impact of Q fever and to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions aimed at addressing this neglected zoonotic disease in Ethiopia.
