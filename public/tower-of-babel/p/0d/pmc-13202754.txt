Assessment of collum angle and alveolar bone plate thickness relative to tooth inclination in central incisors using CBCT: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Saeed Jameie, Zahra Dalili Kajan, Yasamin Babaee Hemmati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding the relationship between incisor inclination, Collum angle, and alveolar bone thickness is crucial for accurate orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning. This study is one of the first to assess the Collum angle and buccal and lingual bone plate thickness of maxillary and mandibular central incisors with tooth inclination on cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans.Materials and methodsIn this cross-sectional study, 112 CBCT scans of the maxilla and 112 CBCT scans of the mandible were three-dimensionally reconstructed using Ez3D-I version 4.1 software. The Collum angle and the labial and lingual bone plate thickness were measured on CBCT scans. The U1-palatal plane angle and the incisor mandibular plane angle (IMPA) were used to determine the inclination of maxillary and mandibular central incisors, respectively. Accordingly, the patients were assigned to three groups of low, normal, and high inclination. Data were analyzed by ANOVA, and the Kruskal-Wallis and Bonferroni tests (α = 0.05).ResultsThe mean value of the Collum angle was significantly different among the three groups in both the maxilla and mandible (P < 0.001). The smallest value was recorded in the high inclination group, and the largest value was recorded in the low inclination group. The difference in labial and lingual bone plate thickness was significant among the three groups at the apex of maxillary central incisors, and at the apex and mid-root of mandibular central incisors (P < 0.05).ConclusionPatients with highly inclined maxillary and mandibular central incisors had a significantly smaller Collum angle and vice versa. Tooth inclination had a significant effect on labial and lingual bone plate thickness at the apex of maxillary central incisors, and at the apex and mid-root of mandibular central incisors. Orthodontists should carefully evaluate these parameters during treatment planning to minimize the risk of complications such as root resorption and cortical bone perforation.

Background

In the process of growth and development, tooth morphology is influenced by a number of factors such as the environment, genetics, oral habits, and medication intake [1, 2]. Variations in tooth morphology can serve as an important factor in achieving optimal esthetics, function, and occlusion through orthodontic treatment [3–5]. Maxillary incisors are the most visible teeth in dental arch. Therefore, their incorrect position, even slightly, is highly important for patients. Morphological variations of incisors can change the treatment plan and affect the outcome of orthodontic treatment [4–6].

The Collum angle, which is the supplementary angle of the crown-root angle is an important morphological characteristic of incisor teeth [7]. This angle is formed at the intersection of the longitudinal axis of the crown and root [7]. Sufficient labial or lingual inclination of incisors plays an important role in creation of an ideal anterior occlusion and esthetics [7, 8].

Root angulation is another important morphological characteristic of incisor teeth [9, 10]. Proximity of the incisor root to the buccal or lingual bone plate, and root angulation relative to the bone plate are among the parameters that can restrict tooth movements; this distance can be visualized and measured on radiographs, and particularly cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans [9–11]. Contact of the incisor root with the cortical bone in the process of orthodontic tooth movement can cause external root resorption [12, 13]. This angle is measured by measuring the Frankfurt mandibular incisor angle, incisor mandibular plane angle (IMPA), U1-palatal plane, and U1-sella-nasion (U1-SN) [12, 13].

Traditional two-dimensional (2D) dental imaging modalities like lateral cephalograms and panoramic radiographs are widely used in orthodontics and implantology for evaluating anatomical structures. However, these 2D techniques face several limitations: superimposition of anatomical structures, distortion, and the absence of three-dimensional (3D) information. These factors can compromise the accuracy of important measurements such as alveolar bone thickness and the Collum angle (the angle between the long axis of the tooth and its root) [14–16].

Several studies have evaluated the inclination of teeth and its association with different malocclusions [17–20]. Feres et al. [21] measured the Collum angle of maxillary central incisors on CBCT scans of 48 Class I, Class II division 1, and Class II division 2 patients, and reported that the mean value of the Collum angle was larger in Class I and Class I division 2 patients compared with Class II division 1 patients. Shailaja et al. [17] measured the Collum angle of maxillary central incisors on 799 lateral cephalograms of patients in 4 groups of Class I, Class II division 1, Class II division 2, and Class III patients, and found the largest mean value of the Collum angle in Class II, and the smallest value in Class III patients. These studies have focused on the relationship between the Collum angle and skeletal malocclusions, primarily in the maxillary incisors [17, 21]. Likewise, existing research on alveolar bone thickness has mainly investigated its association with different malocclusion classes [17–20]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has simultaneously explored these parameters using CBCT, which allows accurate three-dimensional assessment of root angulation and surrounding bone structures.

Understanding how tooth inclination affects the Collum angle and alveolar bone thickness is crucial in orthodontic treatment planning. Improper evaluation of these parameters may increase the risk of adverse outcomes, such as root resorption, cortical plate perforation, and compromised tooth movement. Therefore, this cross-sectional study aimed to assess the relationship between tooth inclination, Collum angle, and buccal and lingual bone plate thickness of maxillary and mandibular central incisors using CBCT as a three-dimensional imaging tool. The null hypothesis of the study was that no significant difference would be found between the Collum angle and buccal and lingual bone plate thickness of maxillary and mandibular central incisors with tooth inclination.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted on CBCT scans of the maxilla and/or mandible retrieved from the archives of the Oral Radiology Department of School of Dentistry, Guilan University of Medical Sciences. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Guilan University of Medical Sciences (IR.GUMS.REC.1402.526). As the study was retrospective and utilized archived CBCT scans without any direct patient involvement or additional radiation exposure, and all patient data were fully anonymized, the requirement for informed consent was formally waived by the Ethics Committee in accordance with the national ethical guidelines for biomedical research.

All CBCT scans were acquired using a Vatech Pax-i3D scanner (Vatech Co., Gyeonggi-do, Korea) with either an 8 × 8 inch or 12 × 12 inch field of view, depending on the clinical indication. The scans were obtained with a voxel size of 0.3 mm. The tube current (mA) and tube potential (kV) were automatically adjusted by the scanner based on the default scout view for each patient. Image reconstruction and morphometric measurements were performed using Ez3D-I version 4.1 software (Gyeonggi-do, Korea) on a medical-grade monitor (EIZO Co., Japan). The inclusion criteria were complete dentition with fully-developed roots and posterior stop, sound incisors with no extensive restoration or dental prosthesis, age between 15 and 40 years, systemically healthy individuals with no systemic or periodontal disease, no history of orthodontic treatment or orthognathic surgery [17, 19, 21]. Participants aged between 15 and 40 years were selected because younger individuals (under 15 years) may present incomplete root formation, which can significantly influence accurate measurement of the Collum angle and alveolar bone morphology. Conversely, subjects older than 40 years were excluded due to common age-related periodontal and alveolar bone changes such as reduced bone density, alveolar bone resorption, periodontal attachment loss, and tooth wear, all of which may adversely affect the validity and precision of measurements. The exclusion criteria were syndromic patients, those with cleft lip/palate, or oral habits, and poor-quality CBCT scans with low resolution and/or motion or metal artifacts [17, 22, 23].

The sample size was calculated using PASS 11 software through ANOVA and considering the Collum angle as the main variable. Considering the effect size of 0.5868, a statistical power of 0.95, and a significance level of 0.05, the minimum sample size required for each group was determined to be 16 [21]. CBCT scans were retrospectively retrieved from the archives of the Oral Radiology Department of the School of Dentistry, Guilan University of Medical Sciences, covering the period from January 2019 to January 2024. The sample size was determined by prior calculation, and the number of included cases exceeded the minimum required. Cases were consecutively included based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure validity and reliability. No randomization was performed, as the study utilized existing radiographic data.

Cross-sectional slices were obtained from the central incisors in buccolingual direction with a slice thickness and slice interval of 1 mm. Of different sections, the one showing the highest crown and root length was selected for the measurements. The cementoenamel junction (CEJ) of each tooth was identified on the cross-sectional image. The superior incisor landmark was identified as point I, the root apex was identified as point A, and the labial and lingual CEJ were identified and their mid-point was marked as point M. The straight IM line indicated the longitudinal axis of the crown, and the straight AM line indicated the longitudinal axis of the root. The Collum angle was defined as the acute angle formed between the IM and AM lines (Fig. 1). This value would be positive if the IM line is located labial to the AM line. Otherwise, this value would be negative. The angle value was measured and reported to one decimal place.

The alveolar bone thickness was measured at the level of the apex and mid-root in millimeters. The mid-root was considered as the mid-point of the AM line, and was marked as M2. Two lines were drawn perpendicular to the longitudinal root axis at these points. The intersection of the vertical line passing from the root apex and labial cortical plate was identified as point F, while the intersection of this line with the lingual cortical plate was identified as point G (Fig. 2). The intersection of the vertical line passing from the mid-root with the labial cortical plate was identified as point B, while the intersection of this line with the lingual cortical plate was identified as point E. The intersection of the vertical line passing from the MT with the root surface was identified as point C in the labial, and point D in the lingual surface. The length of the AF and AG lines indicated the labial and lingual bone plate thickness at the level of the root apex, respectively. The length of the BC and DE lines indicated the labial and lingual bone plate thickness at the level of the mid-root, respectively (Fig. 2).

All CBCT scans were evaluated by two observers. Prior to data collection, the two examiners were calibrated. Calibration was performed by jointly reviewing and measuring a set of representative CBCT scans (not included in the main analysis), and any discrepancies were resolved by discussion and consensus. This process was intended to standardize the measurement technique and minimize observer bias. To assess intra-examiner reliability, each observer re-evaluated 20% of the CBCT scans after a 4-week interval under the same conditions. The inter-examiner reliability was determined by comparing the initial measurements of both observers. The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was calculated for all measured variables, and the results indicated good to excellent reliability, with ICC values exceeding 0.75 for all measurements. The mid-sagittal CBCT sections of patients were used to generate lateral cephalograms three-dimensionally. Accordingly, the patients were assigned to three groups of low, normal, and high inclination according to their IMPA and U1-palatal plane angle.

The IMPA angle is the angle formed at the intersection of the longitudinal axis of mandibular central incisor and the mandibular plane. The mandibular plane is the line connecting the Gonion and Menton. To measure the IMPA angle, three-dimensional lateral sagittal images of the patients were used. In the software, the maximum intensity projection feature was selected, and three-dimensional images were reconstructed by using the sagittal sections (Fig. 3).

Fig. 3Measurement of the incisor mandibular plane angle (IMPA) on a reconstructed lateral cephalometric image. The angle is formed between the long axis of the mandibular central incisor (L1 inclination) and the mandibular plane (line connecting Gonion and Menton)

Measurement of the incisor mandibular plane angle (IMPA) on a reconstructed lateral cephalometric image. The angle is formed between the long axis of the mandibular central incisor (L1 inclination) and the mandibular plane (line connecting Gonion and Menton)

The U1-palatal plane angle is the obtuse angle formed at the intersection of the longitudinal axis of maxillary central incisors and the lingual bone plate axis. The lingual bone plate axis is the line drawn between the two points of anterior nasal spine and posterior nasal spine. The reconstructed mid-sagittal section was used to measure the U1-palatal plane angle. This section best visualized the middle part of the maxillary central incisors. The palatal plane was selected to increase the number of available CBCT images (Fig. 4).

Measurement of the U1-palatal plane angle (U1-PP) on a sagittal CBCT section. The angle is formed between the long axis of the maxillary central incisor and the palatal plane (line connecting anterior nasal spine [ANS] and posterior nasal spine [PNS])

It is well established that the normal range of incisor inclination exhibits variability across different populations. Due to the absence of similar studies conducted in the Iranian population, the present study adopted reference values based on previously published literature [7–9, 24–28]. Accordingly, Patients with 89 < IMPA < 94 degrees were assigned to the normal inclination group, those with IMPA < 89 degrees were assigned to the low inclination group, and those with 94 < IMPA were assigned to the high inclination group. For the U1-PP angle, Patients with 108 < U1-palatal plane angle < 112 degrees were assigned to the normal inclination group, those with U1-palatal plane angle > 112 degrees were assigned to the high inclination group, and those with U1-palatal plane angle < 108 degrees were assigned to the low inclination group.

Normal distribution of data was analyzed by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Homogeneity of the variances was assessed by the Levene test. Accordingly, ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test with Bonferroni adjustment for pairwise comparisons were applied. All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS version 24 at 0.05 level of significance.

Results

In this study, 112 CBCT scans of the maxilla and 112 CBCT scans of the mandible were evaluated. Of the CBCT scans of the maxilla, 48.2% (n = 54) belonged to males and 51.8% (n = 58) belonged to females. Of the CBCT scans of the mandible, 53.6% (n = 60) belonged to males and 46.4% (n = 52) belonged to females. The mean age of the patients with CBCT scans of the maxilla was 32.38 ± 6.01 years. The mean age of patients with CBCT scans of the mandible was 31.43 ± 6.25 years. The mean value of the U1-palatal plane angle was 107.98 ± 6.53 degrees, and the mean value of the IMPA was 93.41 ± 6.18 degrees.

Table 1 presents the mean value of the Collum angle in the three tooth inclination groups. In the maxilla, the Kruskal-Wallis test showed that the mean value of the Collum angle was significantly different among the three groups (P < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons by the Bonferroni test showed significant differences in the Collum angle value between the low inclination group with the normal (P < 0.001) and high (P < 0.001) inclination groups. The lowest mean value of the Collum angle was recorded in the high inclination group, and the highest mean value of the Collum angle was recorded in the low inclination group. In the mandible, the Kruskal-Wallis test showed a significant difference in the mean value of the Collum angle among the three inclination groups (P < 0.001). The smallest mean Collum angle was recorded in the high inclination group, and the highest mean Collum angle was recorded in the low inclination group. Pairwise comparisons by the Bonferroni test revealed that the low inclination group had significant differences with the normal (P = 0.002) and high (P < 0.001) inclination groups regarding the Collum angle value. Moreover, the mean value of the Collum angle in the normal inclination group was significantly higher than that in the high inclination group (P < 0.001).

According to ANOVA, the mean labial and lingual bone plate thickness at the mid-root of maxillary central incisors was not significantly different among the three tooth inclination groups. However, the mean labial and lingual bone plate thickness at the mid-root of mandibular central incisors was significantly different among the three tooth inclination groups (P < 0.001). The mean labial bone plate thickness at the mid-root of mandibular central incisors was higher in the low inclination group compared with the other two groups. The mean lingual bone plate thickness at the mid-root of mandibular central incisors was lower in the low inclination group than the other two groups (Fig. 5).

In summary, the statistical analysis revealed that the Collum angle and bone plate thickness differed significantly among inclination groups. The low inclination group exhibited the highest Collum angle, while the high inclination group had the lowest. The most pronounced differences in bone thickness were observed at the apical level, where retroclined incisors tended to have the thickest lingual and the thinnest labial bone plates, whereas the opposite pattern was seen in proclined incisors. At the mid-root level, significant differences were mainly found in the mandible. These results highlight the close association between tooth inclination and variations in both Collum angle and alveolar bone morphology.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to evaluate the relationship between tooth inclination and both the Collum angle and alveolar bone plate thickness in maxillary and mandibular central incisors using CBCT. Importantly, by assessing these parameters in both jaws, the present study also addressed methodological gaps found in previous research.

According to the results, the Collum angle had significant inverse associations with the U1-palatal plane angle and IMPA, such that in both the maxilla and mandible, the mean value of the Collum angle decreased by an increase in tooth inclination (U1-palatal plane and IMPA, respectively). In both the maxilla and mandible, the highest mean Collum angle was recorded in the low inclination group, and the lowest mean Collum angle was recorded in the high inclination group. Thus, the null hypothesis of the study was rejected.

In line with the present results, the mean value of the Collum angle in the normal inclination group was greater than that in the high inclination group. Feres et al. [21] stated that the mean value of the Collum angle in Class I and Class II division 2 patients was higher than that in Class II division 1 patients. Moreover, Shailaja et al. [17] stated that the largest mean value of the Collum angle was found in Class II patients and the smallest in Class III patients. Arvind et al. [20] reported that the mean value of the Collum angle in Class I and Class II division 2 patients was higher than that in Class II division 1 patients. Li et al. [29] measured the Collum angle in low-angle, average-angle, and high-angle patients (with normal anteroposterior jaw relationship) and reported similar results. They also demonstrated that vertical jaw relation had no significant effect on the Collum angle, and no significant difference was found in this regard among the groups. With respect to Class II division 2 patients, it should be mentioned that the crown of maxillary central incisors has a lingual bending relative to the root, and therefore, they have a high Collum angle. This unique morphology of maxillary central incisors contributes to deep bite development in Class II division 2 patients. It is not known whether this bending is dictated by genetics or occurs due to physical parameters in the process of tooth development. This question is difficult to answer. Either way, severe retrusion of maxillary central incisor crown is not only due to its incorrect position in the maxillary arch, but also due to the abnormal angulation of the crown relative to the root. This fact may probably cause complications in Class II division 2 patients. In case of severe inclination of crown and root, risk of root contact with the lingual bone plate in the process of orthodontic tooth movement towards the lingual should be taken into account [30–37]. Moreover, the present study found that the low inclination group exhibited a significantly higher Collum angle compared to the high inclination group. This result suggests that retroclined maxillary and mandibular central incisors tend to have a more pronounced discrepancy between the long axis of the crown and the root, with the root deviating further towards the lingual aspect. Anatomically, this may reflect an adaptive mechanism to maintain root position within the alveolar bone contour. Clinically, such findings highlight the importance of considering the initial Collum angle and tooth inclination during orthodontic treatment planning. Excessive proclination of retroclined incisors with a high Collum angle may increase the risk of lingual cortical plate perforation, root resorption, or unfavorable periodontal outcomes, as the root apex may move outside the alveolar housing. Therefore, individualized biomechanics and careful monitoring are recommended when attempting to change the inclination of incisors with large Collum angles [38].

In the present study, the mean value of the Collum angle in the low and normal inclination groups was positive in both the maxilla and mandible, which means that the IM line was labial to the AM line. The mean Collum angle value was negative in the high inclination group, which means that the IM line was lingual to the AM line. The mean Collum angle value was positive in studies by Feres et al. [21], Shailaja et al. [17], and Arvind et al. [20]. The results of previous studies on Class II division 1 patients were different from the present findings because in the present study, the mean Collum angle was negative in the high inclination group while it was positive in Class II division 1 patients in studies by Feres et al. [21], Shailaja et al. [17], and Arvind et al. [20], (-0.78 versus 0.1, 6.12, and 0.64 degrees). This difference may be attributed to different classification of patients since in the current study, the patients were assigned to high, normal, and low inclination groups while the abovementioned studies classified patients according to the Angle’s classification of malocclusion [17, 20, 21].

According to the present results, by an increase in tooth inclination in the maxilla, the mean bone thickness at the apex significantly increased in the labial, and decreased in the lingual plate. Also, by an increase in tooth inclination in the maxilla, the mean bone thickness at the mid-root decreased in the labial plate, and increased in the lingual plate; although this change did not reach statistical significance. This finding can be justified scientifically. By an increase in tooth inclination, the maxillary central incisor root distances from the buccal plate, and the mid-root and cervical part of the root approximate the buccal plate. Thus, by an increase in dental inclination, the mean buccal plate thickness increases at the apex and decreases at the mid-root [19, 25, 39, 40].

According to the present results, by an increase in tooth inclination in the mandible, the mean bone thickness at the apex significantly increased in the labial, and significantly decreased in the lingual plate. Also, the results indicated that by an increase in tooth inclination in the mandible, the mean bone thickness at the mid-root significantly decreased in the labial, and significantly increased in the lingual surface. This finding can be scientifically justified as follows: by an increase in tooth inclination, the mandibular central incisor root distances from the buccal plate while the mid-root and cervical part of the root approximate the buccal plate. Therefore, by an increase in tooth inclination, the mean buccal plate thickness increases at the apex and decreases at the mid-root [19, 25, 39, 40]. However, different results were reported by Andrews et al. [19], which may be due to differences in study populations, ethnicity, and race. Also, CBCT scans were used in the current study; while, Andrews et al. [19]. used lateral cephalograms. Andrews et al. [19]. evaluated patients with ideal normal occlusion and skeletal Class II patients while the CBCT scans of patients were included in the current study according to the inclination of incisors.

Gracco et al. [41]. reported that the distance between the root apex of maxillary central incisors and the lingual cortical plate in short-face patients was greater than that in long-face patients. Similarly, the present study indicated that by an increase in inclination of maxillary and mandibular central incisors, the bone thickness at the apex decreased in the lingual and increased in the buccal plate. Although the association of tooth inclination and bone thickness with facial vertical pattern of patients was not directly evaluated in the present study, the current results confirm the findings of Gracco et al. [41], that the distance between the maxillary central incisor root apex and lingual cortical plate in long-face patients is smaller than that in short-face patients.

In the present study, the difference at the mid-root of maxillary central incisors in both the buccal and lingual plates was not significant among the three inclination groups. This finding was in agreement with the results of Andrews et al. [19], and Gracco et al. [41], who showed that the mid-root region experiences the least change following positive or negative alterations in tooth inclination as a result of buccolingual movement of maxillary central incisors due to environmental factors during tooth eruption or orthodontic treatment after tooth eruption. These findings can serve as a cornerstone for diagnosis and treatment planning and indicate that, if necessary, optimal root position may be compromised to compensate for the uncorrected discrepancies in the anteroposterior position of the jaw or improvement of facial profile esthetics. Nonetheless, it should be noted that this compromise may be associated with some risks for the integrity of the supporting structures or root [42, 43].

This study has several limitations. First, bone thickness was evaluated only at the apex and mid-root levels; assessment at additional points along the root axis may yield different results. Second, vertical facial pattern and its potential association with the Collum angle were not analyzed. Third, the retrospective cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Lastly, the sample was restricted to a specific population, which may affect generalizability. Future research should address these issues and further explore the relationship between tooth inclination, Collum angle, and facial pattern using prospective, multi-center designs.

Conclusion

Tooth inclination significantly affects the Collum angle and buccal and lingual bone plate thickness of maxillary and mandibular central incisors, such that in both the maxilla and mandible, by an increase in tooth inclination, the mean value of the Collum angle decreased and the mean labial and lingual bone plate thickness at the apex of maxillary and mandibular central incisors significantly decreased. These findings suggest that orthodontists should carefully consider the inclination of central incisors during treatment planning, as the observed relationships provide valuable insights into the potential risk of bone resorption and related complications. Although direct prediction of such complications requires further longitudinal research, our results may help guide clinical decision-making to minimize these risks. Additionally, orthodontists should take the Collum angle into account, since changes in tooth inclination may alter the Collum angle and affect the proximity of the root to adjacent anatomical structures during orthodontic tooth movement.
