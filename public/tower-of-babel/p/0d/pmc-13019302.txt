Breaking the stoichiometric limit of padlock probe ligation via catalytic hairpin assembly-and-cyclization
Authors: Fanming Meng, Yuting Ma, Mengqing Sun, Bin Tian, Zheyuan Zhou, Zhuxin Dong, Bo Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRolling circle amplification (RCA) is a powerful isothermal nucleic acid amplification technique prized for its robustness and simplicity. However, conventional RCA-based detection is fundamentally limited by the stoichiometry of padlock probe ligation, wherein each target molecule ideally yields only one circular template. Existing strategies to improve ligation efficiency often sacrifice key benefits of RCA or focus on specificity rather than catalytic turnover. Herein, we developed catalytic hairpin assembly-and-cyclization (CHAC), a homogeneous cascade that integrates catalytic hairpin assembly with enzymatic ligation, enabling each target to initiate multiple circularizations and breaking the 1:1 stoichiometric limit. As the target serves only to initiate catalytic assembly without acting as the substrate for ligation or the primer for RCA, CHAC overcomes constraints on target identity and topology. Applied to detection of the mpox (monkeypox) virus E9L gene using a single-tube format (incorporating detection probe-modified magnetic nanoparticles) with real-time optomagnetic sensing, CHAC-RCA achieved detection limits of 0.3–2 fM (depending on amplification duration) within a total assay time of ~2–2.5 h, representing at least a 100-fold improvement over conventional ligation-RCA. The assay showed high specificity, robustness, and clinical concordance with quantitative PCR, establishing CHAC-RCA as a versatile and efficient platform for ultrasensitive nucleic acid detection.

Introduction

Rolling circle amplification (RCA) stands as a cornerstone technique in isothermal nucleic acid amplification, valued for its simplicity, specificity, and versatility [1–3], and has been widely applied in fields such as biochemistry, biomaterials, and molecular biology [4–6]. In nucleic acid detection, the utility of RCA often relies on a padlock probe (PLP) ligation process, where the 5′ and 3′ ends of the PLP hybridize adjacently on a splint strand (usually the target molecule) and are covalently joined by DNA ligase to form a circular template for RCA [7]. Theoretically, this process exhibits exceptional sequence specificity, as DNA ligases efficiently catalyze nick sealing only when the terminal bases are perfectly matched [8, 9], making it a powerful tool for discriminating single-nucleotide variants, nucleic acid sequencing, and genomic mapping [10–13].

A fundamental limitation of the PLP ligation approach lies in its stoichiometric nature, i.e. one splint target molecule can ideally produce only one circular template. This 1:1 ratio inherently restricts the amplification efficiency and ultimate sensitivity of RCA-based assays. Although extensive research efforts have been devoted to improving RCA amplification efficiency, leading to variants such as nicking-enhanced RCA [14–16], branched RCA [17, 18], hyperbranched RCA [19, 20], and circle-to-circle amplification [21, 22], relatively few studies have focused on enhancing the efficiency of the ligation step itself. Consequently, the detection workflow in most RCA-based assays continues to rely on conventional PLP ligation that has remained largely unchanged since the 1990s.

Recent efforts to enhance ligation efficiency have followed distinct paths with inherent limitations. One strategy introduces thermal cycling during ligation to promote splint reuse [23–25], which, although conceptually viable, fundamentally compromises the isothermal advantage by requiring precise thermal control systems. Moreover, practical constraints, such as suboptimal turnover efficiency and enzyme destabilization under repeated heating cycles, further limit the reaction yield. Alternatively, architectural modifications to the PLP design, e.g. dumbbell and terminal hairpin structures, have been explored to reduce intermolecular interactions and favor circularization [26–28]. Although beneficial for suppressing background noise, these PLP modifications primarily enhance reaction specificity rather than catalytic turnover and still operate within the fundamental stoichiometric constraint.

Moreover, neither thermal cycling splint nor PLP architectural modifications alter the target’s inherent role as the splint, thus failing to address the intrinsic constraints of traditional PLP ligation-RCA assays with respect to splint identity and topology. When an RNA molecule serves as the splint, the ligation efficiency is considerably lower due to the strong substrate preference of most DNA ligases for DNA over RNA [29]. Furthermore, conventional PLP ligation requires the splint strand to possess free 3′ or 5′ ends proximal to the hybridization region to permit efficient RCA initiation, which restricts target selection and may introduce additional sample pre-treatment requirements [30].

A more promising route involves the integration of enzyme-free catalytic circuits, such as catalytic hairpin assembly (CHA), with ligation reactions. For a typical CHA circuit, a target molecule cyclically triggers enthalpy-driven hairpin hybridization, generating numerous stable duplex complexes and amplifying signals 50–100 fold within a timeframe [31, 32]. Song et al. demonstrated the feasibility of using a target molecule to catalyze the assembly of hairpin probes into a circular structure suitable for DNA ligation and subsequent RCA [33]. However, they employed hairpin structures functionally decoupled from the ligation site, which allows target-independent ligation, leading to elevated background signals and consequently a suboptimal sub-picomolar detection limit (while conventional PLP ligation-RCA assay offers picomolar detection limit) [33]. In addition to the inferior hairpin design, they did not further explore or validate the significance of decoupling target properties from the ligation and priming processes. Our group adapted the CHA mechanism in a different context, developing a catalytic hairpin assembly-and-cyclization (CHAC) aptasensing strategy for AND-logic detection of nucleic acid and protein targets within ribonucleoprotein complexes [34]. However, in this CHAC aptasensing platform for logical operation, catalytic turnover was intentionally suppressed through low-temperature incubation (16°C) to prioritize absolute fidelity over amplification efficiency, highlighting a critical trade-off in the design of catalytic nucleic acid circuits [34]. These cases underscore that the ineffective suppression of nonspecific ligation remains a major bottleneck to achieving efficient catalytic cycling in nucleic acid amplification systems.

Herein, we present a reconstructed CHAC strategy with substantially enhanced efficiency and specificity to overcome the stoichiometric limitation of conventional PLP ligation. A key design element is the introduction of stable hairpin structures at the termini of both the PLP and the splint strand, which effectively sequester the complementary regions and ensure that ligation becomes strictly dependent on toehold-mediated strand displacement initiated by the target molecule. Benefiting from these specially designed/optimized hairpins and toehold regions, CHAC harnesses the target-recycling capability of CHA to prepare multiple circular templates for RCA.

The homogeneous CHAC-RCA cascade is performed in a single-tube format with detection probe (DP)-functionalized magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs), enabling real-time optomagnetic monitoring of the RCA process. This biosensing platform was applied to detect the mpox (monkeypox), a disease historically prevalent in West and Central Africa [35, 36]. Since 2022, a global mpox virus (MPXV) outbreak has emerged [37], prompting the World Health Organization to declare mpox a Public Health Emergency of International Concern in 2024. Targeting the mpox virus E9L gene, the CHAC-RCA biosensor achieved detection limits of 0.3–2 fM (depending on amplification duration) within a total assay time of ~2–2.5 h, representing at least a 100-fold improvement over conventional PLP ligation-RCA. This study establishes CHAC not only as a conceptual advance but also as a practical pre-amplification module that improves the sensitivity of ligation-based diagnostics.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and synthesized oligos

Phi29 DNA polymerase, phi29 buffer (10×), T4 DNA ligase, T4 ligation buffer (10×), and SYBR Gold nucleic acid gel stain were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Waltham, MA). Ampligase and its ligation buffer were purchased from Biosearch Technologies, Inc. (Hoddesdon, U.K.). DNA exonuclease I and III were obtained from New England BioLabs (Ipswich, MA). Bovine serum albumin (BSA), salmon sperm DNA, and dNTPs were sourced from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Agarose was acquired from Hispanagar S. A. (Burgos, Spain). Tris–HCl buffer (1 M, pH 8.0), Tris-borate-EDTA (TBE) buffer, and Tween-20 were supplied by Applygen Technologies Inc. (Beijing, China). Streptavidin-coated cross-linked starch iron oxide composite particles (100 nm sized MNP, product code 10-19-102) were purchased from Micromod Partikeltechnologie GmbH (Rostock, Germany). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was procured from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). All nucleic acid sequences (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2) were synthesized and HPLC-purified by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Unless indicated otherwise, a working buffer composed of 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) supplemented with 0.05% Tween-20 was used throughout the experiments.

Functionalization of magnetic nanoparticles

Prior to conjugation with biotinylated DPs (DP1 and DP2), the streptavidin-coated MNPs were subjected to two washing steps and resuspended. Biotinylated DP1 and DP2 were conjugated with the MNPs separately to prepare DP1–MNP and DP2–MNP complexes, respectively. The concentrations of DP and MNPs in the conjugation mixture were adjusted to 3.6 µM and 2 mg/mL, respectively. This mixture was then incubated at 37°C for 30 min, washed three times to eliminate unconjugated DPs, resuspended to a final MNP concentration of 1 mg/mL, and stored at 4°C until further use.

CHAC and conventional PLP ligation

For CHAC, the padlock-hairpin strand (H1) and the splint-hairpin strand (H2), each at 1 μM, were separately denatured at 95°C for 5 min and then gradually cooled to 25°C at a rate of 1°C/min to facilitate hairpin formation. The as-prepared hairpin strands were stored at −20°C before use. CHAC was performed at 37°C for 55 min in a reaction mixture containing target molecule, H1 (1 nM), H2 (10 nM), T4 DNA ligase (50 mU/μL), T4 ligation buffer (1×), phi29 buffer (1×), and BSA (0.2 mg/mL). For conventional PLP ligation, a ligation mixture containing H1 (20 nM), RCA-splint (60 nM), T4 DNA ligase (50 mU/μL), T4 ligation buffer (1×), phi29 buffer (1×), and BSA (0.2 mg/mL) was incubated at 37°C for 30 min.

RCA, magnetically actuated incubation, and optomagnetic sensing

The ligation mixtures, prepared by either CHAC or conventional PLP ligation, were mixed with the RCA mixture at a 1:1 volume ratio. The RCA reaction mixture consisted of phi29 polymerase (0.33 U/μL), phi29 buffer (1×), dNTPs (0.66 mM), BSA (0.2 mg/mL), DP1–MNP (0.1 mg/mL), and DP2–MNP (0.1 mg/mL). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tubes containing RCA suspensions (minimum volume of 40 μL for measurement) were mounted in heating blocks, incubated at 37°C under magnetic actuation, and monitored optomagnetically in real-time. For each optomagnetic measurement, an oscillating magnetic field (2 mT, 0.3–100 Hz) was applied to actuate the MNPs, generating optical modulation signals for optomagnetic particle spectroscopy-based analysis. Between measurements, 12 cycles of magnetically actuated incubation (each comprising 6 s of a 2 mT/0.25 Hz rotating magnetic field followed by 4 s of 0 mT) were automatically performed to accelerate the reaction.

Feasibility tests

For agarose gel electrophoresis (2.5%, 1× TBE buffer), 15 μL of sample was mixed with 3 μL of loading buffer (containing 0.1 μL of SYBR gold stain) and loaded onto the gel. Electrophoresis was carried out at a constant voltage of 100 V for 45 min. Subsequently, the gels were visualized using an OI 100 gel imaging system (BIO-OI Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). Ferromagnetic resonance (FMR) spectra were acquired with a Bruker Magnettech ESR5000 spectrometer (Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA), while hydrodynamic size measurements were performed with dynamic light scattering (DLS) on a BeNano 90 particle size analyzer (Bettersize Instruments Ltd., Dandong, China). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) imaging was conducted to characterize the morphology of RCA amplicon-clustered MNPs, using an Asylum Research MFP-3D Bio system (Oxford Instruments plc, Abingdon, U.K.) equipped with AC240TS-R3 silicon probes under tapping mode in air.

Clinical sample validation

Fifteen clinical skin swab samples were collected. Nucleic acids were extracted with the StarSpin Fast Virus DNA/RNA kit (GenStar, Beijing, China). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) targeting the mpox virus F3L gene was performed using a fluorescent qPCR kit (TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) on a QuantStudio 1 real-time PCR instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of School of Basic Medical Sciences, Central South University (No. 2024-KT163). All information and patient identifiers were kept anonymous to protect patient confidentiality.

Results and discussion

Biosensing principle

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the CHAC-RCA strategy consists of two sequential steps: CHAC-mediated ligation and RCA. By comparing 100 MPXV whole-genome sequences from NCBI, we selected a highly conserved 25 nt region of the E9L gene (NC_003310.1, nucleotides 54 720–54 744) as the target sequence. The presence of the target triggers the formation of an H2-splinted H1 complex via CHA, a process driven by both enthalpy change and ATP hydrolysis due to the involvement of T4 DNA ligase. Specifically, the target molecule hybridizes to the toehold region of H1, inducing the unfolding of the 3′-terminal hairpin structure of H1 through toehold-mediated strand displacement. This exposes a previously sequestered domain that initiates the unfolding of H2 via an analogous toehold-mediated strand displacement mechanism, leading to the assembly of H1 and H2. The hairpin assembly process triggers the release of the target molecule, which can then be recycled to initiate additional assembly events. Simultaneously, the 5′-terminal of H2 anneals complementary to the 5′-terminal of H1, thereby forming a splinted structure that presents a nick site for T4 DNA ligase, resulting in H1 circularization. After CHAC, phi29 polymerase, dNTPs, and DP–MNPs are introduced to initiate RCA, with H2 serving directly as the primer. During RCA, the resulting amplicons hybridize with the DPs, leading to changes in the hydrodynamic size and shape anisotropy of the MNP labels [38], which can be monitored in real time using a low-cost optomagnetic setup (Fig. 1b). In the absence of the target, the complementary 3′-terminal regions between H1 and H2 remain sequestered within their highly stable stem structures, which are designed to prevent nonspecific interactions. The optomagnetic sensing mechanism and the lab-built setup are detailed in Supplementary Section S1 and supported by previous studies [39–41].

Schematic illustration of CHAC-RCA-based optomagnetic MPXV detection. (a) Target nucleic acid hybridization with H1 initiates H1–H2 formation, concurrently enabling H1 circularization and target release. The circularized H1 is enzymatically ligated and serves as the RCA template. The released target molecule can be recycled to induce more hairpin assembly events. (b) RCA products are labelled and measured in real time by a lab-built optomagnetic sensing system.

Feasibility tests

Coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations (Fig. 2a) were performed using oxDNA at 37°C with ion concentrations matching experimental conditions [42, 43], confirming structures of the two hairpins, the target-induced H1 unfolding, and the H2-splinted H1 complex. Synthetic DNA sequences were employed in feasibility, optimization, and dose-response tests. First, the molecular design of CHAC-RCA was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. To isolate the hybridization driven solely by the 3′-terminal hairpin assembly (i.e. the CHA mechanism) from the interference of the 5′-terminal complementarity, we truncated the 5′-terminal complementary regions of H1 and H2, effectively converting the CHAC system into a standard CHA system for gel electrophoresis analysis. The truncated H1 contained an 8-nt-long toehold region for target binding (H1*-8), while the truncated H2 contained a 7-nt-long toehold region for H1* binding (H2*-7). As shown in Fig. 2b, the presence of 20 nM target molecule triggers the assembly of H1*-H2* (formed by 200 nM each of H1* and H2*, 37°C, 1 h), demonstrated by the newly generated band in Lane 5.

For CHAC validation using full-length H1 and H2, DNA oligos and complexes are schematically indicated in the gel image (Fig. 2c). Similar to CHA, the presence of 20 nM target molecule induced the assembly of H1 and H2 (0.2 μM each, 37°C, 1 h) into a complex (Lane 10). The subsequent addition of T4 DNA ligase and ATP significantly altered the intensity distribution within the multiband pattern (compare Lanes 10 and 11), which can be attributed to the generation of circular H1 (since the 8-nt complementary regions at the 5′-terminals of H1 and H2 are not sufficiently stable under electrophoresis conditions) and/or to the introduction of ATP-driven enzymatic kinetics. Treatment of ligation products with a combination of exonuclease I and III confirmed the formation of circular DNA (Lane 12). For RCA (using ligation products prepared by 0.5 nM of target, 5 nM each of H1 and H2, 37°C, 1 h), a strong band indicates successful CHAC-RCA appeared when the target was present (Lane 14). Whereas no RCA product can be observed in the absence of target DNA (CHAC-RCA without target molecule, Lane 13). Details of the gel electrophoresis analysis are given in Supplementary Section S2.

The hybridization of DP–MNPs with RCA products was confirmed using DLS, FMR, and AFM. For comparison, a specifically designed splint oligo was employed to circularize H1 and initiate RCA independently of CHAC. Exposing DP–MNPs to either RCA or CHAC-RCA products induced a significant increase in the hydrodynamic size of MNPs, confirming hybridization between amplicon coils and DPs (Fig. 2d). Multivalent DNA binding prevented MNP chaining and led to reduced rotational flexibility as well as lowered net magnetic anisotropy of amplicon-bound DP–MNPs, which was reflected by right-shifted FMR spectra [44, 45], as shown in Fig. 2e. AFM imaging further elucidated morphological features of DNA-entangled individual and clustered MNPs (Fig. 2f), consistent with the proposed strategy and corroborating the DLS and FMR results. Endpoint optomagnetic measurements of CHAC-RCA and control groups showed significantly attenuated signals in samples lacking target, ligase, H1, or H2 relative to the complete CHAC-RCA system (Fig. 2g). Collectively, these analyses confirmed nucleic acid amplification and demonstrated amplicon-induced physical alterations in magnetic labels amenable to optomagnetic monitoring.

Optimization studies

To enhance amplification efficiency while minimizing nonspecific background amplification (caused by nonspecific hairpin assembly, i.e. leakage), systematic optimizations were performed focusing on the length of toehold regions (at the 3′-terminals of the hairpins), the length of complementary regions (at the 5′-terminals of the hairpins), the CHAC reaction temperature, the ratio between the two hairpins, and the CHAC reaction duration. DNA oligos used in these experiments are listed in Supplementary Table S2. For general design principles for H1 and H2 to facilitate experimental reproducibility, refer to Supplementary Section S3.

The influence of toehold length (located at the 3′-terminals of both hairpins) was evaluated using truncated variants denoted H1*-n and H2*-m, where n and m represent the number of nucleotides in the toehold regions of H1* and H2*, respectively. The CHA reaction temperature was set to 37°C based on electrophoretic analysis (Supplementary Fig. S2) and to maintain compatibility with the ligase activity required in subsequent CHAC reactions. Toehold length significantly influences strand displacement kinetics: overly short toeholds hinder hybridization, while excessively long toeholds can impede target recycling [46]. Furthermore, the toehold length for the target binding determines the overall specificity of the circuit. By testing H1*-n with n ranging 6–12 (reacted with H2*-7 at 37°C for 2 h), an optimal toehold length of 8 nucleotides was identified, which yielded the highest reaction rate (Fig. 3a). To determine the optimal toehold length for H2*, gradient target concentrations (from 0.1 nM to 0.2 μM) were tested with H1*-8 and H2*-m, where m ranged 7–10 (Fig. 3b). The electrophoresis tests showed that 6 nM of target molecule could exhaust 200 nM each of hairpins in 2 h, suggesting a target recycling rate of 17 h–1 under these unoptimized CHA conditions. Grayscale intensity analysis of the electrophoretic bands corresponding to products and reactants indicated that a toehold length of 9 nucleotides for H2* provided the highest sensitivity, based on rates of product formation and reactant consumption (Fig. 3c).

In the CHAC system, the 5′-terminal complementary regions of H1 and H2 facilitate formation of the H2-splinted H1 complex. This design induces spatial confinement between H1 and H2, enhancing their collision probability and effective local concentration [47]. While this proximity effect accelerates reaction kinetics, it promotes both specific and nonspecific assembly events, necessitating optimization of the complementary region length to balance specificity and reactivity. To investigate the temperature dependence of CHAC, we performed CHAC-RCA assays using two distinct ligases: T4 DNA ligase over 22°C–37°C, and Ampligase (a thermostable ligase) over 37°C–52°C. For these experiments, H2 variants with 5′-terminal complementary regions of 5–9 nucleotides were tested, alongside a control H2 strand lacking any complementary region (denoted as H2-c0, formerly H2*-9). As shown in Fig. 3d, for T4 ligase-mediated CHAC, lower reaction temperatures led to elevated background signals in blank samples due to enhanced proximity-driven interactions, as well as reduced target response due to diminished ligase activity.

High-temperature reactions revealed a critical difference between CHAC and conventional CHA. In conventional CHA, elevated temperatures typically cause substantial leakage. In contrast, Ampligase-mediated CHAC retained excellent specificity (Fig. 3e). However, target response gradually decreased with increasing temperature (37°C–52°C), attributed to thermal melting of H1 and H2’s 5′-terminal complementary regions. This melting weakens H2-splinted H1 complex formation, suppressing both specific target-dependent ligation and nonspecific off-target reactions. Collectively, these results identify 37°C as the optimal temperature for CHAC, balancing T4 DNA ligase activity and H1-H2 complementary interaction stability. At this temperature, a 7-nucleotide 5′-terminal complementary region was ideal, maximizing target response while minimizing leakage.

Toehold length optimizations via gel electrophoresis were conducted under relatively high H1/H2 concentrations for better visualization. To confirm that this optimization remains valid at the working concentrations (consistent with picomolar target), we performed orthogonal optomagnetic screening of H1 and H2 toehold lengths using 5 pM of target and 5 nM each of H1 and H2 (Fig. 3f; 1 h of CHAC at 37°C followed by 1 h of RCA at 37°C). As shown in Fig. 4g, the CHAC-RCA assays exhibited dynamic detection ranges spanning four orders of magnitude, with limits of detection (LODs, determined as the intersection of a cutoff line with the corresponding trendline) of 0.3 fM and 2 fM at the 90 min and 55 min end points, respectively. In contrast, the conventional PLP ligation-RCA assay showed an inferior LOD of ~0.2 pM, consistent with previous studies [38, 48]. This 100- to 1000-fold improvement in LOD stems from the CHAC mechanism, which provided an effective catalytic turnover number of >100 within the assay timeframe, thereby breaking the 1:1 stoichiometric limit inherent to conventional PLP ligation.

Notably, by decoupling the target properties from ligation/priming processes, the CHAC strategy not only overcomes the stoichiometric constraint but also addresses two longstanding limitations inherent to traditional PLP ligation-RCA assays. First, CHAC circumvents the challenge of RNA-splinted DNA ligation [29] by employing the DNA hairpin H2 as the invariant splint, which ensures robust efficiency regardless of whether the target is DNA or RNA. We validated this versatility experimentally through the detection of a truncated RNA target. Since RNA:DNA duplexes exhibit inherent stability, we truncated the RNA sequence to preclude the need for re-optimizing the toehold length of H1. An LOD of 1.5 fM with 55 min of RCA was achieved for truncated RNA target (Supplementary Fig. S3), closely matching the 2 fM LOD achieved for DNA target detection under the same conditions. Second, CHAC fundamentally eliminates the strict requirement for proximal 3′ or 5′ ends on the target strand [30], as H2 rather than a highly variable target strand serves as the splint as well as the RCA primer. This inherent advantage is indirectly corroborated by our subsequent validation with clinical viral DNA samples, which lack well-defined proximal ends yet exhibited efficient detection.

The CHAC-RCA assay employs stepwise homogeneous reactions within a single tube without requiring washing or separation steps, underscoring its operational simplicity, user-friendliness, and high repeatability. The average coefficients of variation (calculated across target concentrations from 1 fM to 100 pM) were 3.41% and 3.78% for CHAC followed by 90 min RCA and 55 min RCA, respectively, confirming excellent repeatability for both the molecular strategy and the optomagnetic sensing platform. Furthermore, the observed dependence of the LOD on RCA amplification time suggests that real-time signal kinetics carry quantitative information related to target concentration. This implies that for applications requiring rapid results, early changes in the optomagnetic signal could be used for quantification, potentially further reducing the total assay time, particularly for samples with higher target loads. To comprehensively evaluate the performance of CHAC-RCA as a nucleic acid detection platform, we present a comparative analysis with representative cascade RCA-based optomagnetic biosensors, excluding interference from different detection mechanisms (Supplementary Table S3).

Practicability validations

Synthetic DNA sequences were employed for specificity tests. To test the resistance of the proposed system against background DNA, salmon sperm DNA (sheared and denatured, with an average size of around 150 nt that can be regarded as background random DNA) samples containing 0, 20 nM, 200 nM, and 2 µM of salmon sperm DNA were spiked with 10 pM of target DNA for CHAC-RCA detection. As shown in Fig. 5a, a high concentration of background random DNA resulted in a decrease of positive signals due to the competition-based polymerase inhibition [49]. Blank controls with random background DNA exhibited minor signal change, implying the specificity of CHAC reaction.

To further verify the specificity of the system, target sequences containing 1–4 nucleotide mutations (Supplementary Table S1), including the most homologous sequences from horsepox virus and smallpox virus, were tested (Fig. 5b). The results indicated moderate discriminatory power for single-nucleotide variations but strong differentiation of multiple mutations. This level of specificity is sufficient for clinical MPXV detection, given that horsepox virus rarely infects humans and other homologous poxvirus sequences contain significant distinctions from the MPXV target region (Supplementary Table S4). In addition, by assaying mutant targets carrying single-nucleotide mutations at distinct positions (Supplementary Fig. S4), we observed that the ability to discriminate single-nucleotide variations was highly dependent on the position of the mutation relative to H1. Mutant target sequences harboring a mutation within the region complementary to the H1 toehold (e.g. M1) generated a stronger signal than those with a mutation within the region complementary to the H1 hairpin domain (e.g. horsepox). This suggests that probe specificity can be maximized by strategically placing the most variable and pathogen-specific nucleotide sequences within the regions recognized by the stem domain of the hairpin strand. In contrast, the toehold region can be reserved for initial binding and catalyst recycling.

Although clinical MPXV samples (skin swabs) typically contain negligible or no serum, spiked serum samples were tested to evaluate potential matrix effects (Fig. 5c). The biosensor successfully detected the target molecule spiked in serially diluted FBS, demonstrating high robustness and confirming its suitability for complex biological matrices. For clinical validation, DNA extracts from 15 clinical skin swab specimens were analyzed using both our optomagnetic biosensor and reference qPCR (Fig. 5d). To assess the storage stability of the DNA hairpins, H1 and H2 were stored separately in working buffer at −20°C, 4°C, and 37°C. After storage periods of 5 and 10 days, H1 and H2 were used in CHAC-RCA assays containing either 10 pM target (positive control) or no target (blank control). As shown in Fig. 6, signals from positive controls remained largely unchanged over time, showing an inter-day coefficient of variation of 3.68%, which indicates high assay reproducibility. In contrast, blank controls exhibited a clear increase in background signal that correlated with both storage time and temperature, suggesting gradual accumulation of spontaneously opened hairpins. These results underscore the necessity of storage at −20°C to minimize undesired leakage amplification. To facilitate distribution and storage without cold-chain dependence, future efforts will focus on developing lyophilized formulations of the reagents.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed CHAC-RCA, a catalytic sensing platform that breaks the fundamental 1:1 stoichiometric limitation of conventional PLP ligation while simultaneously overcoming its inherent constraints on target identity and topology. When integrated with a low-cost optomagnetic readout system, the CHAC-RCA biosensor achieved detection limits ranging from subfemtomolar to femtomolar levels (depending on RCA amplification time) within a total assay time of 2–2.5 h. The practical utility of the biosensor was rigorously demonstrated through its resistance to background DNA, discrimination of homologous sequences, tolerance to serum matrix, and high concordance with qPCR using clinical skin swab specimens. With its homogeneous, one-tube format and minimal instrumentation requirements, CHAC-RCA represents a promising alternative to PCR-based methods for the rapid, sensitive, and accurate diagnosis of mpox and other infectious diseases in both laboratory and point-of-care settings.
