History of Presentation

A 61-year-old man with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) and hyperlipidemia presented with new chest pain while playing pickleball that was associated with fatigue and shortness of breath. The discomfort was nonradiating and resolved with rest. He had recurrence of pain later that day when he went out to walk the dog and while he was painting his house. His wife was concerned about his symptoms and urged him to go to the emergency department later that same day.

On arrival to our emergency department, he was afebrile, normotensive with a normal heart rate, and had normal oxygen saturation on room air. He appeared in no acute distress and had clear lungs on auscultation, no jugular venous distension, no edema, and warm extremities.

Past Medical History

The patient was diagnosed with sigmoid-variant HCM in 2023 with a cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) then showing septal thickness of 2.3 cm and no significant obstruction on past transthoracic echocardiograms. He tested positive for the MYBPC3 mutation. He has been followed regularly at our HCM clinic with NYHA class I symptoms and was on rosuvastatin 20 mg daily for his hyperlipidemia.

Investigations

The initial high-sensitivity troponin was also the peak troponin of 29,000 ng/L, with subsequent repeat values trending down. Initial electrocardiogram revealed left ventricular hypertrophy that was unchanged compared with the previous with no acute changes or signs of ischemia. He was admitted for non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.

Transthoracic echocardiogram revealed a normal ejection fraction of 59% with stable asymmetrical septal hypertrophy (1.9 cm) and no significant left ventricular outflow tract obstruction or systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve. Coronary angiogram revealed a long segment of intramyocardial bridge in the mid-distal left anterior descending (LAD) artery with obliteration of flow in systole but only mild coronary artery disease (Figure 1). Given his presentation with myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA), a cardiac MRI was performed in the hospital to initiate the workup and revealed basal septal-variant HCM with an ejection fraction of 56% with midanteroseptal late gadolinium enhancement at the area of maximum hypertrophy, similar to the previous cardiac MRI from 2023, without edema or inflammation.

Management

During his hospital stay, he had no recurrence of chest discomfort. After multidisciplinary discussion, the plan was to discharge the patient with close follow-up and schedule him for coronary function testing (CFT) to evaluate his MINOCA and assess the severity of the LAD myocardial bridge.

He underwent CFT 2 months postdischarge that documented the severe mid-distal LAD myocardial bridge but also showed severe epicardial coronary vasospasm with high-dose 100 μg acetylcholine that resolved with intracoronary nitroglycerine as well as severe endothelial-dependent (vasoconstriction to mid-dose acetylcholine) and endothelial-independent microvascular dysfunction with reduced CFR of 2.1 (Figure 1). The LAD bridge was assessed with pressure wire measurements. Both the Pd/Pa and resting full-cycle ratio proximal to the bridge were normal (0.92). When the wire was placed distal to the bridge, it surprisingly captured the left ventricular pressures with distal pressures 40 to 50 mm Hg greater than the aortic pressure, a unique “reverse Pd/Pa.” Therefore, the Pd/Pa was unreliable because Pd was greater than Pa, resulting in a number >1.0, as was the fractional flow reserve (FFR). Because it is a diastolic measure, the resting full-cycle ratio was reliable at 0.64, indicating the bridge was physiologically significant and dobutamine was not required. Intravascular ultrasound revealed a small distal LAD artery (1.49 × 1.52 mm, area of 1.76 mm2) with mild coronary artery disease and confirmed the significant myocardial bridge (Figure 2). He was started on amlodipine 5 mg for the severe vasospasm, and ezetimibe 10 mg was added for better lipid control. He remained symptom-free 3 months later at follow-up.

Discussion

MINOCA occurs in 5% to 6% of patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and 5% to 10% in non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and requires further workup to determine the etiology that is quite heterogenous.1, 2, 3 Based on the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology definition, MINOCA includes plaque disruption, coronary emboli or thrombus that resolved, spontaneous coronary artery dissection, epicardial coronary vasospasm, and coronary microvascular dysfunction.1,3 The American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology guidelines also include a separate category of “MINOCA mimickers” that includes myocarditis, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, and cardiomyopathies, whereas the European Society of Cardiology guidelines include both as MINOCA.3 Although baseline troponin elevation can often be observed in HCM because of microvascular dysfunction, myocyte disarray, and increased wall stress from hypertrophied myocardium, this patient at baseline had normal troponin. The MINOCA guidelines recommend initially excluding clinically overt nonischemic causes of myocardial injury (such as sepsis, pulmonary embolism, cardiac contusion) followed by a cardiac MRI within 72 hours to diagnose myocarditis, Takotsubo syndrome, cardiomyopathies, and the presence of myocardial infarction in a specific coronary distribution.1, 2, 3, 4 The finding of myocardial infarction in a specific coronary territory then requires careful review of the angiogram for spontaneous coronary artery dissection and evidence of plaque erosion or ulceration, thrombus, or coronary embolism.1 If the etiology remains unknown, CFT becomes a crucial part of the workup to determine the presence of coronary vasospasm and/or coronary microvascular dysfunction.5

Myocardial bridges are common, with a prevalence estimated up to 42% on autopsies and 13% of coronary angiograms.6 Myocardial bridges cause compression of the coronary artery, most commonly the LAD artery, during systole that can lead to inducible ischemia with exercise or stress, including pharmacologic stress with dobutamine infusion.6 In a study by Allan et al,6 30 patients with angina with myocardial bridges were referred for coronary unroofing at the University of Chicago and underwent CFT, which showed that 50% did not have a hemodynamically significant bridge and 77% had another cause of angina, including endothelium-independent coronary microvascular dysfunction, microvascular spasm, and epicardial spasm.6 Therefore, in patients with MINOCA or angina with nonobstructive coronary arteries and myocardial bridging, physiological assessment of the myocardial bridge is important to determine its contribution to symptoms and to exclude other causes. Our patient underwent CFT, which confirmed severe epicardial coronary vasospasm, coronary microvascular dysfunction, and mild atherosclerosis in addition to the clinically significant myocardial bridge with a unique reverse Pd/Pa waveform.

Intracoronary functional evaluation of a myocardial bridge can be challenging because its pathophysiology results in inaccurate FFR measurements.7 This is because the FFR is an average of systolic and diastolic pressures, and in a myocardial bridge the compression of the coronary artery in the tunneled segment causes a systolic pressure overshoot and negative pressure gradient.7 Instead, physiological assessment with diastolic indices is more accurate for assessing the functional significance of myocardial bridges and including dobutamine infusion if needed.7

A study by Tarantini et al7 compared FFR measurements against the instantaneous wave-free ratio, a diastolic-only index, to evaluate myocardial bridges in 20 patients at rest and hyperemia (adenosine infusion for FFR measurements and dobutamine infusion for instantaneous wave-free ratio measurements).7 These patients had angina and abnormal noninvasive testing suggestive of ischemia. They found that FFR was negative in all patients at rest and did not significantly change with hyperemia. On the other hand, the instantaneous wave-free ratio was abnormal in 65% of patients at rest and dropped with dobutamine infusion in all patients, confirming better concordance with ischemia.

Conclusions

We present a unique case in a patient HCM with MINOCA and multiple severe etiologies including a severe myocardial bridge, severe epicardial coronary artery vasospasm, and endothelial-dependent and -independent microvascular dysfunction with abnormal coronary flow reserve who had a unique reverse Pd/Pa pullback waveform recording left ventricular pressures distal to the bridge, suggesting a deep myocardial bridge. Therefore, the specific etiology of the elevated troponins was likely multifactorial. The patient has subsequently remained asymptomatic on CFT-guided therapy.
