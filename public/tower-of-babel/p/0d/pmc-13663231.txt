Home-based palliative care provisions in Kerala, India: do government-run and community-owned centers differ in their operations and efficacy?
Authors: S Gowri, E P Abdul Azeez, Manoj Mathew, K P Farsana, K Sarath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPalliative care provisions have been operative in Kerala, India, predominantly through a home-based model for the last three decades. Over time, the community- and home-based model has evolved into a widely recognized and sustainable model, providing almost universal palliative care access to people in need in Kerala through community-owned and government-run centers. However, a significant gap exists in explaining how these models' efficacy and operations vary. In this context, this study aims to compare the nature of operations and the effectiveness of interventions at government-run palliative care centers (GPCs) and community-owned palliative care centers (CPCs) in Kerala, India.MethodsWe employed a qualitative research approach, guided by a comparative case study design. We have chosen ten palliative care centers, five each from government-run and community-owned centers, for the comparison. We have conducted in-depth interviews with key service providers and beneficiaries of the palliative care services.ResultsWe have identified 8 dimensions for comparison. The medical services offered by both categories of palliative care were similar. However, the results revealed significant differences in community participation, socioeconomic and psychosocial support provided, and sustainability measures adopted across the centers. CPCs demonstrate a broader, more holistic approach by integrating services, resulting in better operations and greater effectiveness than GPC initiatives.ConclusionThe findings of the study emphasizes that community-based healthcare models grounded in organic civic networks offer a unique advantage in delivering services more effectively, while government-run frameworks operate with minimal community participation. Replicating Kerala’s community- and home-based palliative care model in resource-constrained and low- and middle-income contexts requires considering the state's unique socio-political, cultural, and policy context for successful translation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12904-026-02232-8.Keywords: Home-based palliative care, Community-based palliative care, Kerala model of palliative care, India

Introduction

The growing prevalence of chronic and non-communicable diseases, coupled with population aging, has significantly increased demand for long-term care [1]. The need for patient-centered, holistic care has driven the demand for palliative care. Palliative care is a holistic, interdisciplinary approach to healthcare that has emerged as an essential component of comprehensive healthcare systems worldwide [2]. Palliative care improves the quality of life of people with life-threatening chronic or serious illnesses, as well as their families, through early identification, holistic assessment, and the management of pain and other symptoms [3]. The focus of palliative care is on the physical, psychological, and spiritual well-being of the patients under care and their families. Despite its importance and global recognition, access to palliative care remains limited, especially in low- and middle-income countries, where services are unevenly distributed [4]. However, palliative care services extend beyond hospital settings and are delivered across multiple settings, including palliative care clinics, hospice centers, community-based centers, and home-based care. While the traditional and conventional forms of health care delivery through hospitals and hospice centers have their own merit in terms of their quality and multidisciplinary care, community and home-based care have emerged as a preferred and convenient form of care for most patients and their families [5, 6].

Home-based palliative care is operational in different regions, countries, and contexts, with varied characteristics. Scholars have described several core features of home-based palliative care. Home-based palliative care models foster trust and safety among health care providers, patients, and their families, ensuring the continuity of care [7]. Family involvement in care delivery is important, and providing training, awareness, and assistance to them also plays an important role in home care [8]. Access to quality care and effective coordination are also quintessential in home care to prevent unnecessary hospitalization and reduce healthcare costs [9]. Palliative care achieves these objectives through a holistic, multidisciplinary approach, with a team that includes healthcare professionals, psychologists, social workers, and volunteers, who can address the patient's physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs [10, 11]. One of the globally acclaimed community and home-based palliative care models is that of Kerala. Access to palliative care in India is very limited, and only a small proportion of those who require it receive it [12, 13]. The situation is different in Kerala, where palliative care services are available and accessible to all through its palliative care policy and grassroots-level community and home-based palliative care provisions [14].

In Kerala, community-based and home-based palliative care services were an initiative of the neighborhood network of palliative care [15]. The initiative was later implemented and replicated at the grassroots level by many community-based organizations and non-governmental organizations. The widespread collaboration, participation, and support from the community led to the introduction of a palliative care policy in the state, the first of its kind in Asia [16]. WHO recognized the Kerala model of palliative care for its sustainability measures, which incorporated local self-governance and NGO collaboration into the delivery of palliative care services at the grassroots level [17]. The model was recognized for its community participation, volunteer involvement, and integration with local self-government. Over time, the model evolved into a decentralized system that emphasized home-based care, social support, and community ownership, parallel to institution-centered palliative care services [18]. Home-based palliative care, with the help of a dedicated team, is a coordinated service provided within the patient's residence. The multidisciplinary team, including doctors, palliative care nurses, social workers, or community volunteers, and sometimes physiotherapists, together addresses the health and socioeconomic needs of patients and their families [19]. The initiative has emerged as the preferred form of care, addressing gaps in conventional health systems [5].

In line with community-based palliative care initiatives, the Government of Kerala institutionalized palliative care within the primary health system by reforming the palliative care policy. The palliative care policy of Kerala, 2008, focused on integrating palliative care into the primary healthcare system and mandated the establishment of palliative care units in government healthcare facilities [20]. The government palliative care program was later extended to home-based services through a multidisciplinary team. Interestingly, Kerala hosts both government-run services delivered through public health infrastructure and community-based organizations operating through volunteer networks, community funding, and sometimes in partnership with local governance [21]. Kerala, along with government- and community-based initiatives, sets a replicable model for sustainable palliative care services. However, there is a gap in scholarly research comparing government-run and community-based palliative care services in terms of their operations and efficacy. This study, therefore, aims to address this gap by comparing service delivery, funding mechanisms, psychosocial support initiatives, and sustainability measures across government-run and community-owned palliative care centers in Kerala. The study examined the prevailing Kerala model and critically assessed whether palliative care functions are uniform or differ significantly across these institutional types. The study is crucial for developing a sustainable palliative care model for low- and middle-income countries.

The term community, in the community-based palliative care model of Kerala, does not merely denote a specific geographical location or the demographic they serve. However, this comprises an active network of local people who leverage social proximity and mobilize resources from their localities (and beyond) to provide services to people in need. As we conceptualize community in this term, and the very historical development of the community-based palliative care model of Kerala is rooted in community participation and social networks, we ground our theoretical lens in Putnam's [22] social capital theory. The available literature underscores the crucial role of community participation in the success of Kerala's community-based palliative care model. Hence, by employing the social capital theoretical framework, we critically analyze how networks of civic engagement, norms of generalized reciprocity, and social trust manifest across government-run and community-owned palliative care centers in Kerala. This framework allows us to examine whether community-owned centers sustain their operations effectively by mobilizing social capital into resources and providing healthcare and psychosocial support. Furthermore, this framework helps us evaluate whether government-run programs can successfully deliver palliative care services by leveraging social capital and volunteer cooperation. The core question this framework allows us to uncover is whether state-led programs can formally leverage social capital within the community, or whether they must remain rooted in community dynamics to sustain community-led initiatives.

Methods

Research approach and design

We employed a qualitative research approach guided by a comparative case study design. Similar to single case studies, the comparative case study provides a thick description of cases and examines both the context and the features of specific phenomena [23]. In the context of the present study, a comparative case study is most appropriate, since we were interested in comparing the home-based palliative care models of community-run and government-managed initiatives. Goodrick [24] suggests that comparative case studies can be useful for identifying and synthesizing the similarities and differences among programs and policy initiatives. We have adopted the comparative case study framework proposed by Bartlett & Vavrus [25]. This framework involves: 1. identifying the phenomenon, 2. exploring the different axes of the cases, 3. collecting rich data through interviewing and analyzing documents, 4. analyzing the data to answer the what and why, and 5. reporting.

Bartlett & Vavrus [25] criticize the traditional case studies being 'bounded' and highlight the need for a comparative perspective that goes beyond by viewing cases from different axes, including the horizontal that focuses on differences/similarities based on location or context, vertical that focuses on levels of influences, and transversal that focuses on the historical trajectory of the case. These three axes are relevant in the context of the current study: the horizontal axis compares the community-run and government-managed initiatives; the vertical axis examines the influence of government policy measures; and the transversal axis examines the evolution of these home-based palliative care models.

Study locality and participants

This study was conducted in selected palliative care units from Palakkad and Malappuram districts of Kerala, India. The magnitude of palliative care interventions is promising in the northern parts of Kerala. We have chosen 5 palliative care units, each from government-managed and community-run initiatives. The selection of palliative care units was based on predefined inclusion criteria. First, palliative care units that had been functioning for at least 5 years were considered. Second, only palliative care units with active home-based care services were considered. We have prepared a list of available palliative care services from the two administrative blocks of these districts, both government- and community-run separately. Furthermore, from this list, we have chosen five palliative care units (5 from community-run and 5 from government-managed) with the highest years of experience.

We have also chosen 16 beneficiaries from these palliative care centers, 8 from each community-run and government-managed center. Of these, 4 were suffering from age-related diseases, 3 were diagnosed with different types of cancer, 2 were diagnosed with chronic kidney diseases, 2 were diagnosed with cardiovascular diseases, 2 had Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease, 2 with stroke-related paralysis, and 1 was diagnosed with multiple sclerosis. The selection of beneficiaries was purposive and based on the active patient list in the palliative care units' patient registry. We have followed the inclusion criteria to select appropriate participants. Patients who had been receiving palliative care services for at least 1 year were selected. It is common in Kerala for patients to receive palliative care for many years, as the services are not only rendered to the terminally ill patients but also to the chronically ill patients. Patients who are seriously ill and have received very few visits (fewer than 3 in the last six months) from the palliative care team were excluded. The patients and their caregivers or family members were interviewed.

Data collection, analysis, and rigor

We have employed multiple strategies to collect data and used various sources for this study. First, we conducted in-depth interviews with key palliative care personnel and interacted with the palliative care team, including nurses, social workers, managers, and volunteers, using a semi-structured interview schedule (see supplementary file 1). Second, the second and fifth authors of this paper participated in home-based palliative care visits to observe the nature and extent of services offered by the team. Third, the research team had access to a few official documents, including the patient registry and the socioeconomic services offered by the palliative care centers. Fourth, we have conducted interviews with patients and their caregivers to understand their perspectives on palliative care services. All interviews with key palliative care personnel and caregivers were audio-recorded, while interactions with staff and patients were field-noted. The interviews with palliative care personnel were conducted at palliative care centers, while interviews with patients/caregivers were conducted at their homes. All interviews were conducted in Malayalam, and the second and fifth authors conducted them all.

The analysis of data was grounded in the comparative case studies model, specifically focusing on the three axes: horizontal, vertical, and transversal. These three dimensions have been particularly explored in the context of comparing the palliative care models. An iterative approach was adopted to analyze the data and gain deeper insights into the phenomenon under study. The major focus of analysis was ‘tracing’, ‘comparing’, and ‘situating’, with tracing focusing on the various actors of the palliative care model under consideration. The tracing component helped us identify the core features to compare between the models. Furthermore, the comparison component was employed to identify key differences in these features and their probable explanations. Finally, the identified differences were situated in the socioeconomic, policy, and global context. The analyses were presented based on the identified features of these models, comparing them with evidence from data collected across different sources.

In accordance with the comparative case study method, we have adopted three measures to ensure the study's rigor. First, we have adopted a thick description and comparison of the studied phenomenon, featuring the core aspects of both models and providing contextual pathways for each. This enables comparability of the models across a number of domains, including operations and efficiency. Second, to ensure rigor, we conducted persistent and extended observation, because in a comparative case study, mere data collection may not be enough to understand the phenomenon. The second and fifth authors of the paper spent a significant amount of time in the field, interacting with participants (patients/caregivers, palliative care staff, volunteers, and officials) and accompanying the palliative care home care teams to observe the extent of services they rendered. Finally, we employed member check, in which we returned to 4 palliative care officials, two each from community-run and government-owned palliative care, to assess whether the results we derived resembled the experiences they shared with us. During this stage, one participant shared suggestions and new insights, and we have incorporated them into the results. Through this exercise, we could ensure the rigor of the analysis and results we reported.

Ethical considerations

The Research Ethical Committee (REC) clearance of Kalinga University, reference number REC/2025/15, has been obtained for conducting this study. All participants were informed of the study's purpose before their participation. They were encouraged to ask questions about the study, which were answered satisfactorily. Data were collected only from participants who voluntarily consented. Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason. Participants' anonymity is maintained when reporting the results. The data is kept confidential and shall be used only for research purposes. The study followed the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki throughout.

Results

Home-based palliative care services were introduced to reduce the burden on patients and their families in accessing medical care, to shorten hospital stays, and to address the difficulty of travel for chronically ill patients. The 'care at home' initiative has been perceived as an important service by patients and their families. Notably, home-based palliative care provision is one of the most important features of the Kerala model of palliative care. We have identified the key features of the model for comparing community-owned and government-managed centers.

Medical services

The palliative home care team provides essential medical services to patients who would otherwise have difficulty accessing necessary health support due to cost, travel, or a lack of caregivers. The narratives of patients' family members and service providers provide an in-depth understanding of the differences in home care delivery between community-owned and Government-run Palliative Care (GPC) centers in Kerala.

A grandson of a 76-year-old patient seeking medical care from a community-based palliative care (CPC) center mentioned that:

"There were wounds and fluids around his body and legs. They (PC team) care for this as it is one of the important needs. The nurse dresses those wounds with utmost care and affection. Moreover, whenever the services are required, they are a phone call away". (CPC beneficiary).

"When they come home, they bring equipment to check vitals, like checking sugar and pressure. But in the case of medicine, his (patient's) medicines are available only at Coimbatore, so we purchase from there. As we are financially stable, we do not request medicines. They provide a tube, pulse machine, and other things" (CPC beneficiary).

The wife of a patient availing service from community-owned palliative care said:

"When we shared our financial difficulties of taking treatment with the palliative care team, they told us that they would arrange medicines for us, and they also offered physiotherapy free of cost" (CPC beneficiary).

Medical support and care delivery ranged from basic wound dressing to fetching medicines and providing other medical aids for the patient. Home-based palliative care is patient-centered, tailoring care to each patient's needs. Experiences of community palliative care beneficiaries highlight the multifaceted work CPC does to ensure patients have easy access to medical care from home. Reduced stress from treatment and travel costs adds to the benefits of home-based palliative care.

A daughter of a 56-year-old palliative care patient under the government palliative care program said that:

"We have a medicine list given by Kozhikode Medical College. We give the list to the palliative team, and they provide whatever medicines they have; we buy the remaining medicines from outside. The water-bed was received from community -owned care" (GPC beneficiary).

"Once, an Ayurvedic doctor also visited. They examined him and gave a powder for dressing the wound. Also, Pariraksha (Government team) provides urine covers and other medical equipment. Initially, we used to buy urine covers from outside. Now, we do not buy them from outside; either the Pariraksha team or the Community-owned palliative care team provides them" (GPC beneficiary).

The experiences of patients and families seeking palliative care at community-owned and government-run centers are similar in terms of service delivery. However, beneficiaries of government-run palliative care centers (PCC) tend to seek additional medical support from community-based palliative care centers for treatment and medical aids, such as water/air beds. Notably, patients and families receiving medical care from community-based palliative care centers rely exclusively on these services. Even though both palliative care centers offered similar medical services, a substantial difference existed between the number of beneficiaries receiving medical care and the number of medical aids provided. The average number of beneficiaries served monthly for the studied GPC is 178, while the average for CPC is 260. Similarly, when GPC distributed 76 medical aids in the last quarter (the last 3 months of December 2025), CPC delivered 235 in the same period. Family involvement in care

The structure of home-based palliative care ensures that all enrolled patients and families receive care at regular intervals, typically weekly or monthly, depending on the patient's condition and needs. However, the palliative care team ensures continuity of care by involving family members in care delivery. A family member of a community-owned palliative care patient states that:

"They taught me how to care for my father, including the ways to move his arms and legs, and what to keep in mind during physiotherapy" (CPC beneficiary).

"He (patient) needed a urine tube. It was provided by palliative care. They change it once a month and told us it should be changed every 28 days. They (Palliative care team) instructed us to inform them if we see a yellow shade or thickness, as that is a warning to change it. They taught us how to care for him, how to bathe him, clean his teeth, turn him to one side, put a sheet on him, clean and repeat on the other side, and cut his nails very softly. His teeth are not strong, so we brush them with a small brush" (CPC beneficiary).

A beneficiary of a government-run palliative care program shares:

"They often tell me about the things I need to take care of for my father. For example, helping him stand up, sit down, and brush his teeth. They have even explained how to brush his teeth. Father hesitates to brush his teeth, so I make him sit and place a basin near him" (GPC beneficiary).

The involvement of family members in care delivery is similar across both government- and community-based PC programs. The palliative care team provides training to family members in dressing wounds, changing urine tubes and covers, and maintaining patients' hygiene, especially those who are bedridden. Training and family involvement are essential for maintaining quality care. Notably, government palliative care centers also encourage family participation, considering their limited availability and busy schedules.

Socioeconomic support

Palliative care is a holistic approach that aims to reduce the physical, psychological, and socioeconomic burden on patients and their families. One of the key aspects of home-based palliative care is the reduced financial burden on families and the support from palliative care in meeting their healthcare costs. Beneficiaries of community-based PCCs highlighted the socioeconomic support they receive from the team, in addition to medical care.

"We have received a lot of support from Palliative Care. We received food kits and other financial assistance. They were the ones who helped us during the time of the COVID-19 pandemic" (CPC beneficiary).

"My father's friend was volunteering with the palliative care team. They used to come to take my father to the physiotherapy center. That is a great help available to us from the palliative care" (CPC beneficiary).

Another family member of a patient mentioned that:

"The driver of the palliative home care team asked us if we were facing any financial difficulties with expenses. He even went into the kitchen to see if we had enough groceries. When a need arises, we can communicate it to them. The volunteers often tell us to let them know if we need groceries, and they have helped us whenever we have needed support" (CPC beneficiary).

A volunteer from the community-owned palliative care narrated that:

“We are very much connected to the patients and their families and try to understand the socioeconomic situations. Whenever necessary and within our budget, we provide various types of support to needy families. For instance, we have supported a few families for the education of their children.”

A nurse from a government-run palliative care unit responded to our question on how they render socioeconomic support:

“Many patients and families require support other than medical and psychosocial, and they approach us, but we don’t have any such provisions to provide support. Often, we try to connect them with community-owned palliative care because they have a better support system in these aspects.”

Community-based palliative care centers provide financial support to families in need to cover household and medical expenses. The team provides socioeconomic support ranging from food kit distribution (groceries and rations) to fetching medicines for patients and arranging transportation for patients who need to travel for services such as physiotherapy as part of their healthcare plan (Fig. 1). The PC team also assists patients in reaching healthcare centers for services offered there. However, the government-run PCC offered only medical care.

Psychosocial care and family support

As Saunders [26] points out, palliative care is not just about pain and symptom management; it emphasizes the physical, psychological, social, and spiritual health of patients and their families. The Kerala model of community palliative care prioritizes patients' mental health and well-being alongside medical care. Figure 2 illustrates the extent of psychosocial support provided by the community-based palliative care in Kerala. A family member of a patient discusses the psychological support provided by the palliative care team.

"Palliative support has helped us reduce our tension about his disease. We can always call them if anything comes up. I talk to the palliative care staff about family-related issues. When I had a personal issue and was mentally weak, the palliative care coordinator helped me a lot to overcome that situation" (CPC beneficiary).

Psychosocial care services of community palliative care

A grandson of a patient mentions the role that palliative care volunteers play in bringing joy and enhancing the patient's well-being through patient-centric interventions. Community participation and engagement of young adults in service delivery, through their presence and psychological support, have created a sense of belonging among patients and families. The feeling of being cared for and not neglected by the community and the people around them has contributed to the patients' well-being.

"It is a huge relief when the PC team comes, especially when student volunteers come. Often, we see he (patient) feels happier with them (PC team) than with us. Students ask about his well-being and engage with him. I have seen him smiling while talking to them. I feel good about that. The family also feels happy to see my grandfather joyful with these volunteers, as he has been bedridden for a long time. There is nothing new in our talks; we talk about the same things every day, but when volunteers come, they discuss something different. We've seen a smile on his face when they come, and it makes us happy too." (CPC beneficiary).

Brother of a 48-year-old bedridden patient receiving government palliative care support mentions that:

"They talk more about the symptoms and medicines. They know that everything is out of hand now, and there is nothing much left. Also, when someone gets a disease like this (referring to cancer), everyone knows that it is beyond our control, or 'it is all over. So they come and check the physical condition and go.” (GPC beneficiary).

The government palliative care team focuses primarily on the patient's physical health. Family members have shared that the government-run palliative care team has limited availability, and therefore, time spent with the family is dedicated to medical interventions and documentation. The rigid work schedule restricts them from addressing the non-medical needs of the patients and families. Also, we have observed that volunteer participation in the five government-run palliative care centers is meager. Mostly, it is run by trained nurses and home-care unit drivers, with occasional involvement of doctors/physicians.

Another major aspect discussed by the palliative care team and families concerns the palliative care team's recreational programs to reduce stress, build networks among beneficiaries of palliative care, and enhance the well-being of patients and families. Recreational programs offered by community palliative care centers range from family get-togethers and excursions to games, outings, training, and workshops to strengthen families. Whereas, government-run palliative care centers limit their recreational activities to training for family and Rogi-bendhu sangamam (patient-family meetups).

A center in charge of community-based palliative care shared its experiences of hosting recreational programs over the years for the patients and their families:

"We regularly conduct recreational programs for the patients and their families. I think we have been conducting family meets for the last 6 years, and hundreds of patients and their families have participated. We also did an excursion to the beach last year with those patients who could travel. Many volunteers played a crucial role, and it is a great comfort to the patients" (Center-in-charge, CPC).

A caregiver of a patient narrated that:

“Since my husband has been bedridden for the last 6 years due to an accident, it is difficult for him to move anywhere out of his room. He is always confined to the room. Over the last three years, the palliative care team has organized excursions to different places for patients and their families. This year we went to the beach. It was such a relief for him” (Caregiver of a patient who receives support CPC).

It is evident that a multitude of psychosocial support systems are actively delivering services to patients and their families, mainly through the community-owned palliative care systems. The involvement of government-run palliative care centers in such activities was limited. The reasons cited by the palliative care professionals include a lack of funding and initiatives from bureaucratic systems.

Human resources

The backbone of the home-based palliative care program is its team. Palliative care envisages a multidisciplinary team that ensures the varied needs of patients and families are met. We found differences in team composition and available human resources between government-run and community-owned palliative care. The availability of human resources, including professionals essential to delivering different services, is crucial for the effective delivery of palliative care and for addressing patients' and their families' concerns.

"When the home care team comes, there will be two volunteers, a nurse, and a driver. Once they arrive, they first ask about our well-being. Then, they inquire about the parents' health and other things. The volunteers, nurses, etc., ask and understand things. Sometimes, the volunteers might be students" (CPC beneficiary).

"The palliative care team visits us twice every month. They usually come with a driver, a nurse, and one or two volunteers. Once or twice, a doctor was also there. When they arrive, they fill out a form. Then, they ask about the patient's condition and try to understand the details. They ask for information regarding the patient's doctor visits." (CPC beneficiary).

The family receiving community-based palliative care mentions that, generally, the home visit team comprises palliative care-trained nurses, volunteers (mostly students or community members), a driver, and doctors (twice or thrice a month). While the nurse provides medical care, the volunteers provide psychological support to the patient and family. The ambulance/van driver from the CPC team, who is usually a community volunteer, participates in the process by cross-checking with the family on their socioeconomic needs (food, groceries, medicines, etc.) and by arranging resources.

"Usually, when they come, there is one or two nurses and a driver. Our main requirement is the wound dressing. We also need help changing the bed sheets and changing my father's clothes. Nurse assists us with these tasks" (GPC beneficiary).

A nurse, serving with the community-owned palliative care center, mentioned that:

"The major difference we see is that government palliative care programs are run by nurses and other employees and not by the community. Community involvement is minimal" (Nurse in CPC).

The government-run palliative care team also has a similar home visit team structure. Beneficiaries of the government palliative care program mentioned that, apart from student volunteers, ward members of the Panchayat (elected representatives) and Accredited Social Health Activist (ASHA) workers also accompanied the team as volunteers, though very occasionally. However, the team's engagement in providing social and economic support is limited, as their primary focus is on medical care. Likewise, the community's involvement in government-run palliative care programs is meager. Notably, while community palliative care centers have an average of 43 active volunteers, government centers have only 9 (Table 2). The average number of registered volunteers in CPC was 176, and in GPC, 75. We have observed that some community-owned palliative care centers also employ social workers, counselors, and physiotherapists to provide services to patients. However, the government-run model relies on a more medicalized approach to service delivery by nurses, with limited involvement of doctors. On the other hand, most of the community-owned palliative care centers did not have regular services from doctors/physicians. Doctors' services were available only once a month or fortnightly.

Accessibility and geographical coverage

A distinct feature of home-based palliative care is its accessibility and geographical coverage. The care provisions of both community-owned and government-run palliative care resulted in almost universal access to palliative care in Kerala. Home-based palliative care was initiated to increase the accessibility and affordability of healthcare services for people suffering from serious health challenges and chronic illnesses. One of the remarkable dimensions of community-based palliative care is its accessibility and on-call availability. Community-owned palliative care beneficiaries have mentioned that, whenever needed, their services were readily available. Certain families, those who require long-term care, also shared that they rely entirely on home-based care from CPC and have stopped consulting elsewhere.

"They usually come about twice a month. When they arrive, they check my father's sugar and pressure. Then, they ask him to move his hands and legs. They make him do physiotherapy-related tasks. If we call them when needed, they always come" (CPC beneficiary).

"When we called them and told them about our situation, they came immediately to inquire. Usually, they visit home once every week. Initially, it was monthly, but as the condition became severe, they started weekly visits" (CPC beneficiary).

Another family member of a patient receiving community-based palliative care added:

"Usually, there is no need to call them because they come once a week properly.

The doctor comes once a month. We usually seek a doctor's visit only if needed. Other than palliative care, we prefer nothing now because he is already old. The basic things are provided by palliative care, like catheterization. They taught us how to do the basic care. So, we do not feel the need to consult anywhere else" (CPC beneficiary).

In contrast, access to government-run palliative care is inadequate. Though the team generally visits once every week or month, depending on the severity of the patient's medical condition, they are often not available on call. The geographical coverage of the palliative care team also differs between community-owned and government-run settings. Each community-based PCC covers one or more panchayats/municipalities, while government palliative care centers are in every Panchayat, with their operations limited to the Panchayat. The difference is also evident in the average monthly home visits: CPC conducts an average of 274, whereas GPC conducts only 126 among the studied palliative care patients. "They are the only ones who have to cover this entire Panchayat. So, we call them when needed. Then there are the community palliative care people, too. Also, when he has wounds, Mom is the one who does the dressing now. When they do not come, we have to do the dressing ourselves. They have a problem with coming to us daily, and the nurse cannot manage it all. They must have so much workload. They have many houses to complete. This is one of the Panchayats with the largest population. There are a lot of bedridden people and others here" (GPC beneficiary).

A GPC nurse narrated that:

“We try to cover as many patients as possible every day and week. But we have limitations. Sometimes we cannot reach every needy patient because of our busy schedule, and sometimes we might be assigned to some other work, so we cannot go for home visits.”

Beneficiaries of government palliative care services have shared their experiences of having been waiting for the palliative care team to visit, due to the team's tight schedule and operating hours. A few nurses in government-run palliative care reported that their department sometimes assigns them other duties, even though these are meant for palliative care services. This sometimes disrupts the service delivery in GPCs. Families have equipped themselves to meet the need (e.g., wound dressings or tube changes) when the palliative care team is not available. Notably, the contribution of community-owned palliative care teams to families receiving both government and community palliative care services is also lower, as such families are enrolled in and receive services from both institutions.

Community participation

A prominent distinguishing factor between government- and community-owned palliative care is community participation, as the name suggests. Community-based palliative care centers are operated by community members themselves. However, the government's palliative care program is merely an extension of its healthcare programs, operated by government staff with minimal volunteer participation. Though there are registered volunteers in both PC programs, community involvement and cooperation are very limited in government PC services.

A nurse from a government-run palliative care unit responded to the question on ‘what difference she sees between the two models’:

“I have earlier worked with a community-owned palliative care for 3 years, and after my marriage, I have started working here. See, both have many similarities in the services provided and the approach. But there are differences also. As you can see here, many people from the community take part in the day-to-day activities of the palliative care as volunteers and supporters. In government palliative care, such participation is very limited to some elected representatives or ASHA workers, and that is very rare.”

"The backbone of community palliative care is the participation of people from the community. We have people from all walks of life, women, students, retired employees, and working people. That is what helps us to take this forward" (Centre -in-Charge of a Community-owned palliative care).

Interestingly, family members of people receiving care from community-based palliative care units also participate in and volunteer to provide care to other palliative care patients. Families and communities have formed a network of volunteers to connect and deliver care within the locality.

"People in the local community contribute a lot to our palliative care. They take many roles. Some days, as a van driver; other days, just accompanying the home care team to sit and talk with the patients; and sometimes helping the nurses with medical procedures. Their service is very important because we cannot afford to have many staff members" (Managing Committee Member, a Community-owned palliative care).

Community participation is not limited to volunteering with the team. Community-owned palliative care organizations are run by the people in the locality, who mobilize funds and resources and take on various roles and responsibilities, from drivers to accompanying volunteers to office staff, depending on need. A member of a community-owned palliative care center mentioned how difficult it is to sustain full-time staff, but through the active and dedicated participation of community members who volunteered for any role, operations and service delivery were never hindered. The volunteers are not just students or young adults, but also retired and working professionals. During our fieldwork in palliative care centers and patients’ residences, we have observed that volunteers and local community members offer support to the palliative home care team. Some in the form of helping nurses extend nursing care to patients, and some in carrying necessary medical equipment to patients' residences.

Sustainability

Sustainability plays a vital role in any initiative. In home-based palliative programs, it is crucial, as the continuity of operations and care delivery depends on the model's sustainability. The government funds the government palliative care service to adhere to the Palliative Care Policy of Kerala. However, sustainability measures pose a challenge for community-based palliative care operations, as they must build from scratch and maintain their operations effectively. Patients and their families, as well as service providers in community-based palliative care programs, discussed the measures the team has adopted to ensure the sustainability of these initiatives.

"We offered some money to the PC team for their service. But they rejected it and said, if we want to give money, we can put it in the palliative fund collection box, or they can give us a box to collect money monthly or every two months" (CPC beneficiary).

The community-owned palliative care, managed and operated by the community, raised funds and mobilized resources from within the community. As a result, all services offered by palliative care are free for patients and their families. They have relied on individual donations and have initiated measures such as collection boxes, monthly donations/subscriptions, and micro-enterprises to raise funds. The donation is completely voluntary. Individuals and businesses can donate whatever they can afford. They also collaborate with the government and other organizations to mobilize resources.

"For the last 16 years, the center has been running with the generous support of the community and well-wishers. We collect donations from people and businesses, and people working in Gulf countries generously support us. Over the years, we have not faced any financial constraints. We also received funds from the government, though very minimal" (Director, CPC).

"You see this building, which is 2500 square feet. We have constructed this by mobilizing funds from the community. A generous person donated this land. We have many ways of fundraising, including small donations and event-based fundraising" (Volunteer, CPC).

The contributions of the people are not limited to cash but also include in-kind contributions. Service providers at palliative care centers have shared stories of people donating their land and other resources to the cause. The community volunteers coordinate, collaborate, and work together to sustain the model. We have observed that the palliative care units (CPCs) studied primarily focus on microdonations through box collections. These boxes are placed in hotels, shops, and other places where people visit frequently and attract small contributions, such as rupees 10 to 100. Palliative care personnel shared that such contributions play a vital role in the financial sustainability of palliative care centers.

The palliative care team not only sustained its operations but also supported families by setting up microenterprises, which helped them manage their expenses. The team offers support in initiation, functioning, restocking materials, and marketing and distribution of the commodities.

"They help us in various ways. We run a small convenience store in front of our house. Once, when they visited, our store was out of supplies. So, they called one of the students with them, asked them what supplies were needed, made a list, and left. That very day, they bought the supplies and filled our store" (CPC beneficiary).

"Over the years, we have been looking for new ways to raise funds for the day-to-day operations of our center. We have initiated stitching units for products such as women's nightdresses. The workers are mostly family members of patients. We sell the products across the region. This helps families get an income as well as the centers" (Director, CPC).

These grassroots initiatives by the community and people contribute to the sustainability of the Kerala model of Palliative care. This sustainability model is circular and ensures the continuity of care through and by the people within the community. Percived quality of care

We have explored the perceived quality of care in the two palliative care models because patients’ satisfaction plays a crucial role in care provision. Though providing care in patients' residences has limitations compared to those in a healthcare setting, home-based palliative care aims to ensure the quality of care is not compromised. However, there are distinguishing features of government-run and community-owned palliative care programs regarding the quality of care they provide.

"When palliative care is available, we feel a sense of peace. If someone feels that there are people to support those who have nobody, that alone brings peace to the mind, right?" (CPC beneficiary).

"They do all their work neatly, with proper protection like gloves. Another thing is, they do not receive any financial gifts even if we try to give them. Initially, we did not know it was a service-oriented work as the nurse, driver, and volunteers were putting so much effort" (CPC beneficiary).

“Palliative care is a blessing for us. My father is suffering from a chronic wound. The doctors at the medical college said that treatment options are very limited and that we should consider palliative care. For the last two years, we have been getting their service. Fortnightly, they come and dress the wound, and they taught us how to do it at home. Just imagine what would happen if this service were not available. We have to take him to the hospital twice a week. They are very good and provide all the support” (CPC beneficiary).

“My mother is bedridden, and she is on catheter tube. Monthly, it needs to be changed. Initially, we did it from the hospital, and later brought in a nurse from a private hospital. Both were costly and time-consuming affairs. For many months now, the palliative care team has been doing this. They do it very nicely and come whenever we require their services. This is great help, and we are very much satisfied with their services” (GPC beneficiary).

The quality of care was ensured through the delivery of care by trained palliative care staff equipped with medical aids to meet patient needs. Another important aspect to highlight is the dedication of community palliative care staff to maintaining hygiene and ensuring procedures are followed accurately, even though most work voluntarily, underscoring the importance of quality in health care delivery. It is also important to note that, in home-based palliative care, the patient's dignity and the right to access quality care are protected.

"Even though there are no major changes, some slight changes are visible. He was unable to move to his sides. When they (PC team) come, they help him with some physical movements, and we have seen some change. One important change we can see on his face is the joy when volunteers come. Often, he asks, 'Won't they come? Will they come this week?” (CPC beneficiary).

“Initially, they had explained to Umma (Mother) how to dress the wound. Now, umma usually does it herself, not the sister. It is not that the sister (nurse) does not dress the wound or do the tasks; it is often that they cannot reach us. So, umma or any other has to do it. So, there is a conflict between our need for care and their (Pariraksha) availability” (GPC beneficiary).

The delivery of medical care does not merely measure the quality of care. It also incorporates psychological and mental health care. Community-owned palliative care programs, as perceived by patients and their families, are found to be effective through their volunteers, offering families strength and emotional support. Among the studied palliative care units, only one CPC unit had inpatient facilities to address patients' critical healthcare needs. Although the government palliative care team also contributes to the quality of medical care, time and resource constraints hinder the delivery of holistic, high-quality care.

Discussion

The present study compared the operation and efficacy of home-based palliative care services provided by community-owned and government-run palliative care centers in Kerala, India. The dimensions of comparison included medical care, psychosocial and socioeconomic support, family and community participation, sustainability measures adopted, and the quality of care offered by the centers. The study findings highlight notable differences in operations and efficacy between the two types of palliative care. The study results show that even when the medical care services offered by government-run and community-owned palliative care centers are similar, there is a significant difference in their service reach, with community-owned palliative care centers having higher coverage of home visits and distribution of medical aids than GPC. The existing literature on the community-based palliative care model in Kerala supports this finding, as the model relies on community volunteers and local networks to expand service reach and mobilize resources for home-based palliative care [27]. A study on the neighborhood network of palliative care highlights a decentralized, community-based model that promotes broader coverage of medical services at regular intervals [15]. Another study conducted among palliative care patients and stakeholders in Kerala also indicates that CPC services were more readily accessible and responsive [28]. Notably, another study contradicts this study’s findings, highlighting the limitations of community-based models for providing specialist medical supervision with limited doctor involvement, which affects clinical decision-making and advanced symptom management in cases of chronic illness [29]. While government services maintain a standardized medical care approach, the literature identifies limitations in workforce or community participation, time constraints, and a lack of resource mobilization, which restrict the frequency of home visits, accessibility, and the large-scale distribution of medical aids [30]. In contrast, a study among patients and stakeholders of government-run and community-owned palliative care programs found that Government palliative care programs had a higher number of new patient registrations and home visits than community-based palliative care programs [31].

A major distinction observed between the two palliative care systems was the socioeconomic support they provided to patients and families. Results highlighted that community-owned palliative care offered a wide range of socioeconomic support, including ration and food kit distribution, educational assistance for children, transportation and bystander services, and distribution of medicines for patients and their families. Previous studies indicate that addressing socioeconomic factors affecting patients and caregivers, such as financial support and caregiver support, would improve the overall health of patients and caregivers [32–34]. The results align with a previous study that identified the Kerala model of palliative care as a social movement rather than merely a medical initiative [35]. A conceptual analysis of studies from 12 countries and a realist review indicate that community volunteers collectively mobilize resources to help patients and families address their social needs, taking into account their health conditions [36, 37]. Also, the results of this study show that the socioeconomic support provided by government-run initiatives was limited to medicine and medical equipment distribution alone. However, some studies indicate that, despite community volunteers' efforts, the financial needs of patients and families remain unmet, suggesting that socioeconomic support is limited by available community resources [38, 39].

The study results indicate that community palliative care offered a broader range of psychosocial interventions, including emotional support, referrals to mental health programs, and recreational activities, to improve the psychological well-being of patients and caregivers, compared to the government-run palliative care program. These supports, rendered by community palliative care in Kerala, resemble the core features of PC evident in other parts of the world, namely the extension of services to families and patients to address emotional aspects at the end of life [40, 41]. Interestingly, studies also highlight how volunteers build rapport with patients and families, providing emotional and social support alongside medical care [28, 42]. A study conducted in Norway among volunteers found that palliative care volunteers receive training to identify psychosocial problems and chart interventions through community networks [43]. Likewise, community participation emerged as a defining characteristic of CPC services relative to GPC in this study's findings. Available evidence indicates that the Kerala model of community-based palliative care is largely sustained by the active involvement of the community, with volunteers playing a crucial role in service delivery and patient support [14]. A systematic review highlights that community participation has enhanced service reach and fostered a collective responsibility for patient care within the community [44]. However, the results of this study indicate that GPC programs operate within a formal institutional framework and lack community participation in their operations. Scholars have identified that the Kerala model of home-based palliative care has emerged globally as an example of community participation in health care [45]. Notably, studies examining volunteer motivation found that community participation is guided by altruism, personal experiences, and social responsibility, especially in healthcare initiatives [42, 46].

The sustainability of the home-based palliative care initiative represents a significant distinction between the two systems in the study’s findings. GPC has benefited from the funding and institutional stability of a government project. However, CPC relies on various community fundraising campaigns, micro-enterprise initiatives, and sustainable strategies for its long-term. Results from a multi-country study and an Ethiopian study indicate that community involvement helps sustain programs in resource-limited settings by mobilizing resources from local support networks [47, 48]. However, a study based on German survey data on volunteering indicates that the sustainability of any organization remains a concern, as volunteer engagement is unstable and fluctuates over time [49]. In terms of overall quality of care, the study results highlight that CPC services appear to adopt a more holistic approach by integrating medical, psychosocial, and socioeconomic support for patients and families, whereas GPC services tend to focus primarily on clinical aspects of care. Supporting the findings, a previous study found that the holistic approach of CPC aligns with the broader philosophy of palliative care, which emphasizes the physical, emotional, social, and spiritual needs of patients and their families [7].

This study's results underscore that community-owned palliative care programs operate and deliver services more effectively than government-run centers across many aspects of service delivery, validating Putnam's social capital theory, which we used in this study as a theoretical framework. This finding emphasizes that community-based healthcare models grounded in organic civic networks offer a unique advantage in delivering services more effectively. At the same time, although government-run programs are also useful for patients and people in need, services are largely delivered through formal bureaucratic frameworks with little or no community engagement. The community-owned centers effectively mobilize civic engagement networks, a central aspect of Putnam's theory, to address the medical, psychosocial, and socioeconomic needs of patients and their families. It is evident that norms of generalized reciprocity and deeply rooted social trust are at work, with neighborhood and local communities actively investing their time, resources, and skills in collective action to better the lives of fellow beings. Most importantly, the results of the study highlight that while the state-sponsored home-based palliative care programs in Kerala enjoy consistent budget allocation, making them economically sustainable, they struggle to deliver an effective, holistic service compared to community-owned initiatives, where the community participation, mobilization, and utilization of social capital are very much evident in translating sustainability and delivering a holistic and compassionate care in an accessible manner.

When discussing Kerala’s model of community- and home-based palliative care, one of the core questions is its replicability. Efforts have been made to replicate the model, especially in resource-poor settings, as this model is a good fit for such contexts. One such effort to translate this model to West Bengal, India, shows that the momentum, especially in terms of community participation and solidarity, is not evident, indicating the limitations of translating the palliative care delivery to the same level as in Kerala [50]. Efforts are ongoing to adopt this model in other parts of the country, but evidence of successful replication is not yet available. It is a very debated topic what accounts for Kerala’s social development and health landscape, including the palliative care interventions. Kerala has a unique context compared to other states, marked by better health and social development metrics, a long history of mass mobilization, political sensitivity, progressive public policies, social solidarity, and a long tradition of mutual aid. These factors might have been highly contextual in shaping organic civil networks and the exceptional case of Kerala's palliative care model. However, more robust studies are required to examine the socioeconomic, religious, political, and cultural context of the palliative care movement in Kerala. Efforts to replicate Kerala’s model in other contexts should consider the state's unique socio-cultural background, especially its social solidarity, community participation, and conducive public policy that nurture community-based healthcare interventions.

Limitations

The study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the study primarily relies on dimensions observed in the literature for comparison and on services reported by the centers, which may be subject to reporting bias. Second, the study is based on a comparative assessment of community-owned and government-run palliative care services within a specific regional context and therefore does not address dimensions of service delivery outside Kerala, where the health system, care delivery structure, and family and community participation might differ. Finally, with some patients (four participants) enrolled in both CPC and GPC programs, there was duplication of care services, which interfered with the clear distinction between the operations and might have affected the accuracy of the comparison. Finally, a more robust assessment is required to compare the efficacy and satisfaction of services provided by two different models based on patients’ and their families' experiences.

Conclusion

This study highlights key differences between community-owned and government-run palliative care programs in Kerala, India, in terms of their operations and efficacy. While both models provide essential medical services, CPC demonstrates a broader, more holistic approach by integrating socioeconomic support, psychosocial care, community participation, and sustainability measures. The findings underscore the significant role of community engagement in expanding the scope and reach of palliative care services. CPC services driven by volunteer networks, community resource mobilization, and local sustainability measures not only address patients' medical needs but also the social and emotional challenges they and their families face. Despite the strengths of the community-based palliative care model, the limited involvement and availability of physicians pose a challenge. The duplication of services between CPC and GPC highlights the need for better coordination and integration between the institutional types. Strengthening coordinated services and collaboration between community-owned and government-run models could help optimize resource utilization, improve continuity of care, and enhance overall service quality. CPC and GPC should be viewed as two components of a comprehensive palliative care system that can fill the healthcare gaps and ensure universal access to palliative care. Policies that promote collaboration, integration, and mutual support among these approaches should be formulated to deliver holistic palliative care more effectively and sustainably, particularly in resource-constrained settings or low- and middle-income countries.
