Successful Delayed Endovascular Therapy for Multiple Lower Nerve Palsy Caused by Spontaneous Internal Carotid Artery Dissection
Authors: Nobuhiko ARAI, Kazunari YACHI, Ryutaro ISHIHARA, Takao FUKUSHIMA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Spontaneous internal carotid artery dissection is a relatively common cause of stroke in younger patients. The occurrence of spontaneous internal carotid artery dissection with lower cranial nerve palsy is quite rare. However, few studies have reported its natural course or an effective treatment policy. We received a 42-year-old man with spontaneous internal carotid artery dissection presenting with multiple lower cranial nerve palsies. He exhibited difficulty in swallowing, dysarthria, and tongue deviation. After 2 months of conservative therapy, stent placement with coils was performed because the symptoms did not improve. The patient was discharged 4 days after the operation without any complications and showed evident improvement in symptoms. We reviewed cases of multiple lower cranial nerve palsies reported in the literature up to January 2025 and discussed the appropriate period of observation and the effectiveness of endovascular therapy for spontaneous internal carotid artery dissection with multiple lower cranial nerve palsies.

Introduction

Spontaneous internal carotid artery dissection (SICAD) is a relatively common stroke-inducing disease that accounts for approximately 15% of all ischemic strokes in younger patients.1) For SICAD without any neurological deficit or ischemic signs and presenting only head or neck pain, conservative treatment with blood pressure control is a standardized therapy. Meanwhile, for rare subtypes of SICAD accompanied by cranial nerve deficits, a standardized treatment policy remains unclear. In a literature review, most patients underwent conservative therapy and achieved relatively acceptable clinical results.2) However, recent reports have demonstrated the effectiveness of endovascular intervention in terms of palsy recovery rate or period.3,4) Additionally, it is unknown whether conservative therapy should be continued or whether surgical therapy, such as stent placement, can be attempted. We present a case of SICAD with multiple lower nerve palsies treated by stenting with coiling as long as 2 months after onset and emphasize the potential of endovascular therapy for SICAD with lower nerve palsy.

Case Report

A 42-year-old man presented with a 3-week history of dysphagia and dysarthria. He had experienced an upper airway infection 1 month prior. He had never experienced any pain in the head, neck, or shoulder in the past few months. The patient had no remarkable medical history. The blood pressure was elevated (196/145 mmHg). The patient showed a left deviation of the tongue (Fig. 1a) and weak movement of the left pharynx, as indicated by the curtain sign. No hoarseness of voice or muscle weakness in the bilateral shoulders was observed throughout the observational period. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed no ischemic signs in the brainstem but showed a caliber-changed extracranial internal carotid artery (ICA) with an intimal flap. The diameter of the ICA pseudoaneurysm was 19.2 mm × 9.6 mm, including the mural thrombus (Fig. 2). The patient was diagnosed with SICAD with pseudoaneurysm formation. Glossopharyngeal and hypoglossal nerve deficits were presumed to be caused by compression from the pseudoaneurysm and/or occlusion of the feeding artery to nerves from the dissected ICA. Considering the relatively favorable outcome of oculomotor nerve deficits due to an aneurysm reported previously, the initial treatment policy was conservative, with the prescription of vitamin B12 agents. However, 1 month after the start of treatment, the extent of cranial nerve palsy did not improve. Based on the stable clinical course and previously reported successful treatment of a pseudoaneurysm from ICA dissection, we presumed that the cranial nerve palsy was caused by pulsating compression from the pseudoaneurysm with a mural thrombus and attempted to perform endovascular intervention. We could not exclude the possibility of occlusion of the feeding arteries to the nerves, in which case, stenting with a coil might not be effective. However, we observed the patient for 1 month to confirm the results of conservative treatment. To achieve a correct and smooth flow, LVIS stents (MicroVention TERUMO, Tustin, CA, USA) were placed with coil filling, which significantly decreased blood flow into the pseudoaneurysm (Fig. 3a-c). Dual antiplatelet therapy was started 2 weeks before the operation. The patient was discharged without any additional neurological symptoms on the 4th day after surgery. A follow-up MRI (Fig. 3d) showed a reduction in pseudoaneurysm size, and the symptoms had almost disappeared 1 month (Fig. 1b) after stenting with the coil.

Discussion

Here, we report the safe and successful endovascular treatment of SICAD with multiple lower nerve palsies. In this case, 2 important findings were identified. First, clinicians must consider SICAD and perform imaging studies to exclude brainstem ischemia when examining isolated lower cranial nerve deficits. Second, delayed stenting for pseudoaneurysms with multiple cranial nerve palsies was safe and effective in rapidly improving nerve palsy.

SICAD is diagnosed 2.5-3 per 100,000 individuals annually,5) and is a rare entity for which a standardized treatment policy with firm evidence has never been established. In practical medical settings, conservative therapy is primarily chosen to control blood pressure and can lead to relatively favorable outcomes.2,6) Among patients with SICAD, cranial nerves are involved in 8%-16% of cases, with the lower cranial nerves 9-12 the most commonly affected.1) To the best of our knowledge, this is the first literature review to identify cases of multiple lower nerve palsy (excluding isolated cranial nerve palsy cases) and compare the outcomes of conservative therapy and surgical treatment.

A total of 30 cases of SICAD with multiple-nerve palsy have been reported as of 2025 (Table 1).7-30) In addition, we classified the 30 cases as syndrome-associated laryngeal paralysis to the extent possible (Table 2). Our case showed the paralysis of only the IX and XII nerves. Although this is an extremely rare pattern of ICA dissection associated with multiple lower nerve palsies, our case could be a subtype of Tapia syndrome or an unnamed syndrome 1, expressing IX, X, and XII nerve palsies. To date, 10 cases (33%) have been reported as syndromes. Generally, the clinical symptoms of vagal nerve palsy include voice hoarseness and/or deviation of the soft palate (curtain sign). Our case might show signs of incomplete vagal palsy and/or glossopharyngeal palsy.

Among these cases, only 1 was a woman, showing a significant sex difference. The mean age was 47.9 years, and the standard deviation of the groups was 6.9. Notably, as many as 7 cases (24%), including our case, presented with no pain at all, even though the usual artery dissection usually causes some pain in the body. Considering the background of the reported patients, that is, middle-aged males showing isolated lower nerve deficits such as tongue deviation or dysphagia, clinicians should exclude SICAD even if the patient reports no neck or head pain.

Endovascular therapy was performed in 4 cases, including our case. Of the 19 patients who received conservative therapy (6 cases were excluded from this analysis because of unavailable outcome data, and 1 case was excluded because the patient underwent bypass surgery), 13 (68.4%) showed improvement in nerve palsy. The mean duration of the spontaneous recovery period was 3.1 ± 3.5 months. Meanwhile, all 4 patients who underwent endovascular therapy improved without any complications, only 16.9 ± 9.4 days after the surgery, which was a significantly shorter period than in conservative cases (p = 0.01, Brunner-Munzel Test). We sincerely want to emphasize that surgical intervention is not the first-choice treatment for symptomatic SICAD. Considering the general complication rates related to endovascular therapy (8.4%-12.3%)31) and the good clinical outcome of conservative therapy, as shown in chart 1 (52% of cases improved without surgical intervention), medical treatment should be the first choice for SICAD with multiple lower nerve palsies. However, among the previously reported cases, 6 patients (29%) had permanent lower nerve palsy, which may have warranted earlier surgical treatment trials. Currently, due to a shortage of evidence related to SICAD with multiple lower nerve palsies, the ideal observation period before surgical intervention has not been determined. Among the reported cases of spontaneous recovery, eight (67%) improved within 2 months after onset. Our case showed a favorable clinical outcome with delayed surgical intervention, which was performed 2 months after onset. The other 3 reported surgical cases underwent stent placement within 2 months of onset.20,22) After the longest observation period prior to surgical intervention, the present case still showed good clinical outcomes. Therefore, we suggest that for SICAD with multiple lower nerve palsies, 1-2 months of conservative therapy should be performed initially, and stenting with a coil for pseudoaneurysm of SICAD should be attempted if no improvement is observed after this period. Flow diverters (FD) are currently an effective endovascular option for pseudoaneurysm treatment.31) A recent report showed that 69.7% of ICA aneurysms associated with cranial nerve palsy in patients who underwent FD stenting resolved or improved their symptoms after 1 year of treatment.32) The factor for symptom improvement was associated with the time from symptom onset to treatment (improved group; 197.1 days vs non-improved group; 800 days, p = 0.023). To the best of our knowledge, few studies 20 has reported FD stent placement for ICAD with lower nerve palsy; however, FD may be a promising treatment option for aneurysms with nerve palsy. However, the differences between dissected and undissected true aneurysms must be noted. The former can resolve spontaneously in 1-2 months. Therefore, we believe that true aneurysm-associated cranial nerve palsy should be treated surgically. Meanwhile, flow-diverter stents are associated with certain risks of ischemic events and bleeding complications caused by dual antiplatelet agents, similar to stenting with a coil. One possible mechanism of cranial nerve palsy is occlusion of the feeding arteries, which differs greatly from true aneurysms. For dissecting aneurysm-related cranial palsy, medical treatment should be provided for a certain period. Although we performed coiling in this case, considering that the tight packing of coils into pseudoaneurysms may have a mass effect compressing cranial nerves, coiling is not always effective and may even exacerbate symptoms in some cases. A recent meta-analysis revealed FD superiority over stent-assisted coil for retreatment rate, although the difference was not statistically significant.31) Thus, the first-step treatment policy should be conservative, and a flow-diverter stent without coiling can be a subsequent method instead of coiling with a stent if symptoms do not improve after a 1-2 month observational period. The limitations of this literature review include the small number of subjects and publication bias. There are several unreported cases of SICAD with lower nerve palsy for which endovascular therapy has been unsuccessful. In addition, the recovery in our case could have followed a natural course. However, a surgical option for relieving nerve compression is reasonable. This option could be considered for refractory medical treatments.

Conclusions

We report a successful stent placement with coiling for SICAD with multiple lower cranial nerve palsies. Endovascular therapy for intractable cases can be effective after 2 months of conservative therapy.
