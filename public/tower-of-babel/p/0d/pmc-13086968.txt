Comparative evaluation of deep learning models for cardiovascular disease diagnosis and classification
Authors: Iman Bhia, Soroush Soltanizadeh, Wasswa Shafik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide, highlighting the need for accurate and computationally efficient diagnostic tools. Electrocardiography (ECG) is widely used for the diagnosis of CVD and is a noninvasive, cost-effective method. However, manual interpretation requires substantial time and can lead to human error. In this study, we propose and systematically compare low-complexity Artificial Intelligence (AI)–driven Deep Learning (DL) models for multi-class ECG-based CVD diagnosis and classification. Using the publicly available PTB Diagnostic ECG (PTB-ECG) dataset, which comprises multiple CVD categories, we evaluate CNN, LSTM, MLP, CNN-MLP, and ConvLSTM architectures, focusing on balancing diagnostic performance and computational complexity. Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT)–based feature extraction and data-level imbalance handling are employed to enhance efficiency and robustness. Experimental results show that the LSTM model achieves the best overall performance, with an accuracy of 99.98%, an F1 score of 98%, and a precision of 100%, while maintaining low computational complexity, measured in Real Multiplications Per Symbol (RMpS). These findings demonstrate that high diagnostic accuracy can be achieved without high computational complexity, supporting the feasibility of real-time and resource-constrained clinical deployment.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Deep learning, Artificial intelligence, Diagnosis, ClassificationSubject terms: Cardiology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) arise from a complex interplay of medical conditions, including abnormal heart rhythms, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and other metabolic disorders, as well as lifestyle-related risk factors such as smoking, unhealthy diet, and chronic stress1. Despite continuous progress in medical science, heart attacks, or myocardial infarctions, remain the primary cause of heart-related fatalities. CVDs are estimated to be responsible for around 17.9 million deaths annually worldwide, accounting for roughly 32% of all mortalities2. Of these, approximately 85% result from heart attacks. Early identification of CVDs could preserve countless lives, thereby underscoring the urgent need for prompt and accurate diagnosis. Ultimately, improving early AI-detection strategies holds the potential to reduce the global burden of CVDs3. Traditionally, the diagnosis and assessment of CVD rely on clinical examinations supported by diagnostic tools such as electrocardiography (ECG), cardiac imaging modalities including Computed Tomography (CT) and Cardiac Magnetic Resonance imaging (CMR), Cardiac MRI, and laboratory tests4, 5. Among these modalities, ECG is particularly prominent due to its noninvasive nature, low cost, wide availability, and ability to provide real-time insights into cardiac electrical activity. However, despite its clinical value, manual ECG interpretation is time-consuming, highly dependent on clinician expertise, and susceptible to inter-observer variability, especially in high-volume clinical environments and long-term monitoring scenarios, which increases the risk of error6, 7. These limitations highlight the need for more efficient and reliable diagnostic support systems.

In parallel, the growing adoption of real-time health monitoring technologies has enabled continuous tracking of physiological signals, allowing clinicians to identify subtle abnormalities, such as arrhythmias or early hemodynamic changes, before they progress to critical events8, 9, 10. Continuous data streams not only facilitate timely medical intervention but also promote patient engagement and collaborative care between patients and healthcare providers11, 12. However, the increasing volume and complexity of such data exceed the practical limits of manual analysis, further motivating the adoption of automated and intelligent diagnostic approaches. Recently, Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) methods, has become a valuable complement to the diagnosis and classification of CVDs. By learning complex, non-linear patterns from large-scale ECG datasets, AI-driven models can uncover diagnostically relevant features that may not be readily discernible through traditional analysis7, 13, 14. In recent years, many studies have used AI and DL frameworks that demonstrate impressive diagnostic accuracy. However, these often require substantial computational resources, limiting their use in real-time, embedded, and resource-limited clinical environments15. In practice, improvements in model accuracy often come at the cost of higher computational complexity, yet many studies overlook this critical trade-off.

To address this challenge, the present study focuses on the development and comparative evaluation of low-complexity AI-driven DL architectures for multi-class ECG-based CVD diagnosis and classification. Specifically, we investigate Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), Long-Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), and their combination (i.e., ConvLSTM and CNN-MLP) models, to diagnose and classify CVDs using the PTB Diagnostic ECG (PTB-ECG) dataset. The proposed framework aims to achieve high diagnostic accuracy while explicitly minimizing computational complexity, thereby enhancing feasibility for real-time and resource-limited clinical deployment.

Related literature

In recent years, several studies have investigated the application of ML and DL techniques for CVDs diagnosis and classification, with ECG data serving as a common foundation across many studies16, 17. In7, the authors utilized traditional ML algorithms, hybrid frameworks, and advanced DL architectures to enhance diagnostic accuracy, particularly to overcome challenges such as class imbalance, noisy signal data, and the presence of irrelevant artifacts. A key strategy for addressing data imbalance is the use of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), which generate synthetic ECG samples to improve training robustness. Hybrid models combining GANs with LSTM networks have demonstrated superior predictive capability, achieving an F1 score of 0.987, Area Under the Curve (AUC) values up to 0.984, and accuracies of 0.992 on the MIT-BIH dataset. Additionally, the model achieved higher performance on the PTB-ECG dataset, with an accuracy of 0.994, F1 score of 0.993, and AUC of 0.995, outperforming all other baseline models.

In a different study, the authors have introduced signal preprocessing techniques, such as the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT), to forecast cardiac conditions and reduce noise. This is followed by classifiers, including Nonlinear Vector Decomposed Neural Networks (NVDN) and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), to enhance interpretability and generalizability using clinical data from the UCI and PhysioNet repositories. The model showed 92.0% sensitivity, 89.33% specificity, and a general accuracy of 90.67%15.

In a study by Rath et al., a method was developed to detect coronary artery disease using ECG signals. The authors developed four DL models: an autoencoder (AE), a Radial Basis Function Network (RBFN), a self-organizing map (SOM), and a Restricted Boltzmann Machine (RBM). The models were trained using the MIT-BIH and PTB-ECG datasets. The AE model performed well when tested, with accuracy, F1 score, and AUC values of 0.974, 0.932, and 0.922 on the MIT-BIH dataset and 0.984, 0.967, and 0.932 on the PTB-ECG dataset. Moreover, performance improved further when AE was integrated with a SOM in an ensemble model, which surpassed the AE model alone in all key metrics18.

In another study19, researchers utilized DL techniques to classify four cardiac conditions, which include myocardial infarction, history of myocardial infarction, irregular heartbeat, and normal rhythm. Utilized a publicly available dataset of ECG images and started by exploring transfer learning with well-known pre-trained architectures, such as SqueezeNet and AlexNet. Additionally, the study introduced a novel CNN explicitly designed for this predictive task. The proposed CNN model demonstrated superior performance compared to existing models, achieving accuracy, recall, precision, and F1 scores of 98.23%, 98.22%, 98.31%, and 98.21%, respectively. When combined with feature extraction via a Naïve Bayes classifier, the model’s effectiveness further improved, attaining a performance rating of 99.79%.

In14, the authors presented the DLECG-CVD framework, an automated DL system aimed at analyzing one-dimensional biomedical ECG signals for diagnosing CVD. This framework comprises a structured pipeline that includes data preprocessing, feature extraction using a Deep Belief Network (DBN), hyperparameter optimization via the Improved Swallow Swarm Optimization (ISSO) algorithm, and final classification with the XGBoost technique. Validation experiments with the PTB-XL dataset revealed notable enhancements in crucial diagnostic metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed methodology.

A study carried out by Wang et al. developed an automated ECG classification method that combines the Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) with a CNN model. CWT decomposed ECG signals into time-frequency representations and converted them into 2D scalograms for CNN-based feature extraction. Four RR interval features, capturing the region around the R peak for arrhythmia diagnosis, were integrated with CNN features and processed through a fully connected layer for classification. Using the MIT-BIH arrhythmia dataset, the method achieved a positive predictive value of 70.75%, a sensitivity of 67.47%, an F1 score of 68.76%, and an overall accuracy of 98.74%. This outperformed existing techniques, improving F1 scores by 4.75–16.85%, demonstrating strong potential as an accurate and efficient clinical decision support tool20.

In21, a comparative analysis was performed using various ML and DL algorithms on the UCI Machine Learning Heart Disease dataset, which contains 14 essential clinical attributes. The research included preprocessing steps such as normalization and the use of the Isolation Forest algorithm to remove irrelevant features, thereby improving model performance. The classification results were assessed using accuracy metrics and confusion matrix. Among the methods evaluated, the deep learning model attained the highest accuracy at 94.2%.

In a separate study22, the authors developed a CNN model featuring three layers of an adaptive version of 1D convolutional layers. This network was trained using the MIT-BIH arrhythmia dataset to classify lengthy ECG data streams. They obtained accuracy rates of 99% for identifying ventricular ectopic beats and 97.6% for classifying supraventricular ectopic beats.

The research in23, the authors proposed a CNN architecture featuring three 1D convolutional layers, followed by three max-pooling layers, a fully connected layer, and a SoftMax output. The first and second convolutional layers utilized filters of size 5, and a stride of 2 was applied in the initial two max-pooling operations. Using the MIT-BIH arrhythmia dataset, the model achieved a classification accuracy of 92.7% for ECG heartbeat recognition, among other studies as depicted in [24–31].

Pal et al. conducted a study that employed a deep CNN transfer learning method, using a pre-trained DenseNet to classify arrhythmias from ECG signals. The authors used the PTB and MIT-BIH arrhythmia datasets, which were converted into 2D images. Due to the dataset’s imbalance, a data augmentation method was implemented. The DenseNet model was selected to address the vanishing gradient issue in deep networks through dense layer connections. Their proposed CardioNet model achieved the precision, recall, and F1 scores of 98.62%, 98.68%, and 98.65%, respectively32. Our paper presented a solution for diagnosing and classifying CVD by applying deep learning models while ensuring low computational complexity.

Materials and methods

In this section, we explored various DL architectures for the diagnosis and classification of CVDs, including CNN, LSTM, MLP, and their combination ConvLSTM and CNN-MLP models. These approaches were evaluated and compared based on their performance in diagnosing and classifying CVDs from the PTB-ECG dataset.

Dataset

Our study utilized the PTB-ECG dataset, consisting of 549 records collected from 290 subjects aged 17 to 87 years, to diagnose and classify CVDs. This dataset included a clinical summary encompassing relevant information, such as gender, age, diagnosis, medical history, prescribed medications, interventions, coronary artery pathology, ventriculography, echocardiography, and hemodynamic assessments. The dataset encompasses various CVDs, including myocardial infarction, bundle branch block, heart failure, dysrhythmia, valvular heart disease, myocardial hypertrophy, myocarditis, and miscellaneous33. Long short-term memory (LSTM)

The LSTM architecture is widely used in areas such as Natural Language Processing (NLP), computer vision, and speech recognition due to its ability to maintain and utilize information over long periods. CNN-MLP

The CNN-MLP architecture features two sub-model components: a CNN sub-model for feature extraction and an MLP sub-model for classification. The CNN component processes the input data by applying a convolutional layer followed by a max-pooling layer, which reduces the feature dimensions. A dense layer is subsequently applied to further refine the feature representations. The convolutional layer uses the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) as its activation function. Once features are extracted, the output is flattened before passing it to the MLP sub-model, which contains two hidden layers and an output layer. The process of flattening turns the feature maps’ output two-dimensional arrays into a single linear vector. The hidden layers also use the ReLU activation function, while the output layer applies the SoftMax activation function (Fig. 6). ConvLSTM

The ConvLSTM model combines CNN and LSTM submodels, leveraging the strengths of both architectures. In this setup, the CNN segment focuses on extracting relevant features from the input, while the LSTM component performs classification. Initially, a convolutional layer is applied, followed by a max-pooling layer to reduce the spatial dimensions of the extracted features. The feature representations are then further improved by applying a dense layer. The convolutional layer uses the ReLU activation function. Once feature extraction is complete, the resulting data is flattened before being fed into the LSTM network. The LSTM model portion consists of a hidden layer using ReLU activation and a final output layer that applies the SoftMax function for multi-class classification (Fig. 7). Result

A comparative analysis of several DL algorithms for diagnosing and classifying CVDs was conducted. The study aims to develop DL models that achieve a balance between accuracy and computational complexity. We employed AI-driven DL techniques, specifically CNN, LSTM, MLP, and hybrid CNN-MLP and ConvLSTM models. We utilized Python (3.12.3) along with TensorFlow (2.17.0) and Keras (3.3.3) libraries to develop the proposed models. This study employed the PTB-ECG dataset33, a widely used dataset for CVD studies. We measured accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score metrics to assess the models’ performance. While also evaluating the computational complexity through Real Multiplications per Symbol (RMpS)35.

The preprocessing phase and certain modifications of the ECG signal are necessary. To improve data quality, several established techniques were applied, including handling missing values, applying a high-pass filter with a 0.5 Hz cutoff to eliminate low-frequency noise, and downsampling the ECG signals to 250 Hz. Then, feature extraction was performed using the DWT, specifically the db3 wavelet15, followed by min–max normalization to standardize the extracted features. Importantly, the extracted DWT features were integrated with the raw ECG signal representations, rather than being used independently. To address class imbalance, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied exclusively to the training set after the train–test split, thereby preventing data leakage and ensuring an unbiased evaluation of model performance.

This study performs multi-class classification across eight cardiovascular disease categories, as described in this paper. We also utilized the confusion matrix to demonstrate the model’s classification performance. After preprocessing, we randomly split the dataset into 80% for training and 20% for testing. To ensure data independence, all ECG records from each subject were assigned to either the training or the testing set, but not both. The input to all models consisted of ECG signals rather than images. To account for variability and ensure statistical robustness, we repeated this 80/20 split five times using different random seeds and report the mean and standard deviation of all performance metrics. We trained our models for 50 epochs, using early stopping to prevent overfitting and underfitting. Among the evaluated architectures, the LSTM model provides the most favorable balance between accuracy and computational complexity, achieving a computational complexity of approximately RMpS while maintaining an accuracy of 99.98%, an F1 score of 98%, and a precision of 100%. Importantly, these results indicate that near-optimal classification performance can be achieved at the lowest complexity level investigated, highlighting the efficiency of temporal modeling when combined with compact feature representations. While the CNN, MLP, CNN-MLP, and ConvLSTM models also demonstrate competitive accuracy, their performance gains are accompanied by higher computational demands, yielding F1 scores of 91%, 93%, 94%, and 96%, respectively. These results further reinforce that increasing architectural complexity does not necessarily translate into proportional performance improvements. Overall, the findings confirm that the proposed framework enables low-complexity multi-class ECG-based CVD diagnosis and classification, with the LSTM model emerging as the most suitable candidate for deployment in real-time, embedded, and wearable healthcare applications.

Confusion matrix

Figure 8 demonstrates the confusion matrix commonly utilized in our analysis. Here, samples are classified into three main categories: MI (A), Heart Failure (B), and Healthy (C). The terms “True Positive (TP)”, “False Positive (FP)”, “True Negative (TN)”, and “False Negative (FN)” pertain to the classification test’s accuracy. The confusion matrix allows for the calculation of precision and recall metrics. Precision measures the ratio of TP predictions among all positive predictions made by the model and is calculated as the ratio of TP to the total of TP and FP (). Recall denotes the proportion of true positive predictions compared to all actual positive cases, calculated as the ratio of TP to the total of TP and FN (). Accuracy, the simplest performance metric, indicates the proportion of observations that are correctly classified out of the total observations. It is best suited for scenarios where class distribution is balanced, and the consequences of false positives and false negatives are comparable. However, it may not be an effective measure in unbalanced datasets where positive and negative classes vary significantly in their instances. Therefore, alternative metrics are needed for performance assessment. An optimal classifier aspires to achieve equality between precision and recall, which suggests the absence of false positives and false negatives. Therefore, a metric that combines both precision and recall is essential. The F1 score incorporates these dimensions, defined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall . This measure provides a balanced evaluation of precision and recall, making it a more thorough metric for assessing classification models. Discussion

In this study, we developed and implemented advanced AI-driven DL models to classify CVDs into distinct conditions, each representing unique pathological effects on cardiac function. Through a comprehensive analysis, our approach demonstrated strong diagnostic capabilities, aligning with previous research that highlights the effectiveness of DL techniques in medical imaging and CVD diagnosis. These findings not only confirm the robustness of our models but also highlight the increasing role of AI-based systems in improving clinical decision-making and enhancing early detection and treatment strategies for cardiovascular disorders. The findings of our research support previous studies that demonstrate the value of ML and DL models in diagnosing CVD. Notably, in a reference study36,37, the CNN model achieved an accuracy of 97.70% and an F1 score of 97%. A prior study reference18, indicated that the SOM-AE model reached 98.2% accuracy and an F1 score of 97%. In contrast, our proposed model significantly outperforms these existing methods, achieving an accuracy of 99.98%, an F1 score of 98%, and a perfect precision rate of 100%, with a computational complexity of roughly RMpS. This remarkable performance highlights the effectiveness of our DL approach in diagnosing and classifying CVDs, providing enhanced diagnostic reliability. By improving both accuracy and F1 score, our model highlights its potential for practical clinical applications, where accurate and dependable classification is crucial for effective patient care. Furthermore, the results suggest that hybrid architectures, such as CNN-MLP, can achieve competitive diagnostic performance without the substantial computational complexity associated with deeper standalone models.

Several limitations should be noted. The PTB-ECG dataset suffers from class imbalance and limited samples for certain disease categories; although SMOTE was applied, synthetic data may not fully reflect real physiological variability, leading to unstable per-class metrics. Consequently, this study focuses on comparative model evaluation rather than disease-specific performance. In addition, misclassifications mainly occurred between clinically similar conditions, and real-time deployment may introduce further challenges related to noise and latency. However, the results presented here can guide future efforts to use these models to evaluate condition-specific metrics, such as AUROC, AUPRC, and calibration scores.

Conclusion

This study evaluated multiple DL architectures for multi-class ECG-based CVD diagnosis and classification, with a primary focus on achieving high diagnostic performance while maintaining low computational complexity. The results demonstrate that accurate CVD classification can be achieved without reliance on computationally intensive models. Among the evaluated approaches, the LSTM model achieved the most favorable accuracy–complexity trade-off, attaining 99.98% accuracy, a 98% F1 score, and 100% precision while operating at a computational complexity on the order of RMpS. The consistent performance observed across all evaluated models highlights the effectiveness of combining DWT–based feature extraction with compact deep learning architectures. This strategy enabled a three-order-of-magnitude reduction in computational complexity while preserving clinically relevant diagnostic information, thereby supporting the suitability of the PTB-ECG dataset for efficiency-oriented AI-driven CVD diagnostics.

Future work will focus on validating the proposed framework on larger, more diverse datasets, exploring transformer-based temporal models, and further optimizing feature engineering to enhance robustness while maintaining low computational complexity. Overall, the proposed low-complexity DL framework provides a practical foundation for real-time ECG-based cardiovascular screening and decision support in resource-constrained clinical and wearable healthcare settings.
