The impact of loneliness on sleep quality in college students: a chained mediation model involving perceived stress and expressive suppression
Authors: Qiancheng Dong, Jiazhao Li, Wenjuan Wang, Shujuan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Previous studies have indicated a close association between loneliness and sleep quality. Nonetheless, the underlying mechanism that link these constructs, especially the potential intermediary roles of expressive suppression and perceived stress is not well established. The current study employed a chained mediation model to explore the association between loneliness and sleep quality among college students, focusing on the mediating roles of expressive suppression and perceived stress. This study contributed to a nuanced theoretical understanding by highlighting expressive suppression and perceived stress as mediating factors in college students’ sleep studies. This current study utilized a cross-sectional design to administer questionnaires to 1,065 students randomly selected from the university town of Bengbu, Anhui Province, China from April to October 2024, using the following four instruments, namely the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), the Russell Loneliness Scale (UCLA), the Emotion Regulation Scale (ERQ), and the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10). The Questionnaires were rigorously screened with Mahalanobis distance and 1040 questionnaires were ultimately selected out for analysis while 25 were identified as haphazardly completely questionnaires and were subsequently excluded. This research employed Mplus for data analysis. (1) The sleep quality indices of college students showed a substantial and positive correlation with feelings of loneliness, perceived stress, and expressive suppression. (2) expressive suppression and perceived stress act as chain mediators between loneliness and sleep quality. This research found that expressive suppression and perceived stress serve as mediators in the relationship between loneliness and sleep quality among college students. Our findings suggest tailored interventions targeting perceived stress and expressive suppression should be developed to address college students’ sleep problems, and priorities should be accorded to strategies aimed at managing perceived stress.

Introduction

A significant section of the general population suffers from poor sleep quality, which is a fairly prevalent issue. Globally, an estimated 852 million people meet the clinical criteria for insomnia, accounting for 16.2% of the global adult population. Of these, 415 million individuals (7.9%) are affected by severe insomnia1. Similarly, according to the China Sleep Study, the per capita sleep index for Chinese residents in 2023 is to reach 62.61 points, a decrease of 5.16 from 2022 to 2.17 from 2021. A recent meta-analysis has shown that 19% of 376,824 Chinese reported poor sleep quality2, which underscores that sleep has developed into a pressing and urgent concern in China’s society.

Sleep is an inherent behavioral activity of human beings and serves as an important regulator of the cardiovascular system, the neuroendocrine system and the immune system, exerting a direct impact on human’s health3. Chronical exposure to poor sleep quality is reported to trigger the onset and development of brain and cardiovascular diseases4. Additionally, chronic sleep deprivation has been associated with a significant risk for cardiovascular diseases and metabolic disorder, as well as impairment of human emotion functioning4–6. Sleep quality is closely related with individual’s quality of life, and a study involving 1279 university students revealed that participants with poor sleep quality experienced worse physical and mental quality of life7.Besides, it is particularly concerning that college students, who undergo the significant transition from adolescence to adulthood and from campus to society, are one of the most sleep-deprived groups. A study involving 112,939 Chinese university students revealed that 24.1% suffered from sleep disturbances, 20.3% reported dissatisfied with sleep quality and 23.6% were affected by insomnia, indicating a prevailing sleep problem among this group in China8. Recent study involving 1040 college students revealed that around 46.8% reported poor subjective sleep quality9. Moreover, a strikingly high prevalence of sleep disorder (82.3%) was observed among medical students after the pandemic10. Taken together the high prevalence and significant health implications, it is therefore urgent and imperative to identify the factors that contribute to poor sleep quality and to clarify their relevant mechanisms.

A recent scoping review has identified social factors, including social relations as one of the four determinants of sleep quality in college students11. A systematic analysis by Gordon et al., suggested that college students with higher relationship quality indices exhibited relatively better sleep quality12. However, the selected 52 relationship researches are not evenly sampled across culture backgrounds, with 70% originating from North America and Europe, and only 3 studies from East Asia. Consequently, localized measurement and analysis are necessary to investigate sleep quality among college students.

It is widely accepted that loneliness is an unpleasant feeling that frequently arises when the need to belong in is not satisfied13. Loneliness is considered a global public health problem, considering its adverse effects on physical and mental health14. Based on WHO data, 5 to 15% of adolescents feel lonely, and this number is likely to be underestimated15. A recent scoping view has suggested a prevalence of loneliness among emerging adulthood (approximately 18–25)14. A research that involve 689 college students has reported that 32.4% of students report moderate loneliness, and in addition, 3.2% of students report they have severe loneliness16. Therefore, we posit that loneliness has become a non-negligible factor affecting the mental health level of college students. Moreover, there are studies showing that loneliness affects the soundness and continuity of sleep17, and correlates with bedtime procrastination18. Cacioppo’s study of college students reveled that loneliness is related to objectively measured sleep efficiency, indicating that loneliness is a significant predictor of a decline in subjective sleep quality19–22. Therefore, loneliness may directly impact the quality of sleep, but the mechanism of action needs to be further explored. Poor sleep quality has been reported related to perceived stress23,24, and loneliness has been viewed as a potential stressor25. Existing researches have provided robust evidences suggesting its positive association with loneliness26 and their interdependence27. Besides, research has confirmed an indirect relationship between loneliness and sleep quality28,29. Specifically, research has argued that loneliness has indirect effects on sleep quality via prior day daily stress and hassles partly, grounded in Cacioppo et al.’s hypothesis that lonely people may perceive the world more intimidating30. It is reasonable to assume that lonely people may exhibit high level of perceived stress, which further impact their sleep quality. Therefore, we propose Hypothesis 1 of this research: Perceived stress mediates the effect of loneliness on sleep quality.

According to the process model of emotion regulation by Gross et al., individual differences in emotion regulation may be a key factor in explaining loneliness31. The process through which people feel and communicate their feelings is known as emotion regulation, and it mostly comprises of expressive suppression and cognitive reappraisal32. Cognitive reappraisal is an emotion regulation strategy that achieves emotion regulation effects by changing cognition33. Expressive suppression is an emotion regulation strategy that focuses on adapting to maladaptive emotions, and it alters the effects of situations on emotions by suppressing expressive emotional behaviors34. In accordance with emotion regulation theory, the process of emotion regulation demonstrates specificity, as unique emotional processing tendencies are observed across different emotional states31. Compared to cognitive reappraisal, which is often associated with positive emotions, expressive suppression is frequently linked to negative emotions. Research by Cacioppo et al. indicates that lonely individuals maintain heightened vigilance toward social threats. Consequently individuals experiencing loneliness tend to hold more negative expectations and employ actions characterized by expressive suppression—such as active rejection or withdrawal—to achieve emotional regulation35. Additionally, research indicates that expressive suppression may play a significant role in stress-related experiences, exerting an influence far greater than cognitive reappraisal36, making expressive suppression a better pathway than cognitive reappraisal for interpretation. Taken together the specificity of emotion regulation and the findings of loneliness studies, loneliness can be linked to perceived stress through the lens of expressive suppression within emotional regulation strategies. Moreover, the poor sleep quality has been reported to correlate with the use of expressive suppression among young adults37,38. Considering the established association between loneliness and sleep quality, it is reasonable to investigate the mediating role of expressive suppression in the relationship between loneliness and sleep quality. In the framework of Process model of Emotion Regulation, valuation system shows that the second-valuation system will target the emotion instantiated in the first valuation system39. Emotion regulation can occur at different stages of emotion generation, including situational selection, situational modification, attentional deployment, cognitive reappraisal, and response modulation. Expressive suppression falls under the category of response modulation, meaning that after an emotion has already arisen, it is regulated by controlling behavioral expression. This inhibitory behavior itself then creates a new context, causing the individual to experience additional stress and thereby creating a cyclical process. In this ongoing consumption of limited psychological resources, the subjective experience of stress is amplified31. Stress can be viewed as an experiential component, while expressive suppression is a behavioral component40. This process can be understood as an interaction between behavior and experiential component: behavioral expressive suppression leads to increased experiential stress, and this heightened stress may in turn influence subsequent behavioral choices and emotional regulation strategies. Therefore, we propose Hypothesis 2: Expressive suppression mediates the effect of loneliness on sleep quality.

In summary, the complex and intertwined influences between loneliness, perceived stress, and emotion regulation work together to influence sleep quality in college student populations. The interactions between these four variables have not yet been thoroughly investigated in researches. The majority of previous studies have exclusively focused on one or two of the variables separately, without fully investigating their interactions and combined effects. Therefore, we propose Hypothesis 3: Expressive suppression and perceived stress exert a chained mediating effect in the influence of loneliness on sleep quality.

These four variables constitute a chain-mediated effects model, elucidating the mechanism through which loneliness affects sleep quality. The model seeks to give a more thorough analytical framework to better understand the various factors that affect sleep quality and to identify possible pathways of relationship between loneliness and sleep quality by integrating a number of relevant variables. Methods

Participants

Data were collected from a convenience sample of 1,065 college students enrolled between April to October 2024. Participants were recruited via an online convenience sampling method and questionnaires were distributed in the university town of Bengbu, Anhui Province. After applying the Multivariate outlier test to exclude outliers and remove haphazardly completed questionnaires,1040 questionnaires (valid questionnaire: 97.6%) were retained for subsequent data analysis. Informed consent was acquired from all participants. Participants’ ages ranged from sixteen to thirty-three (M = 19.56, SD = 1.44); 432 (41.54%) were male and 608 (58.46%) were female; 219 (21.06%) were only child and 821 (78.94%) were non-only child. There were 214 freshmen (20.58%), 400 sophomores (38.46%), 192 juniors (18.46%), 166 seniors (15.96%), 44 medical students of the fifth year (4.23%), and 24 postgraduate students (2.31%). In addition, 5.48% of the students were current smokers or had a past history of smoking, 28.46% were current drinkers or had a past history of drinking, and 75.87% were single.

Univariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) has revealed lower division undergraduates (freshmen, sophomores and juniors) experienced better sleep quality compared to upper division undergraduates (medical students of the fifth year). Independent samples t-tests revealed no statistically significant differences in sleep quality scores with regard to gender, major, and being an only child, while significant differences were observed with regard to smoking history, drinking history, and relationship history, demonstrating that those with smoking experience, drinking experience, and relationship experience had relatively lower sleep quality. Given the significant variability observed among demographic variables, we include these variables as covariates in the analysis process.

Prior to data collection, all participants received an explanation of the research objectives, measures, procedures, and privacy protection measures and each provided a written informed consent. Relevant ethical guidelines were strictly adhered to throughout the research, ensuring the confidentiality of participants’ personal information and their responses. Measures

A general situational questionnaire was created based on our research objectives and interviews with several college students, of which the demographic information includes gender, age, grade, major, being an only child or with siblings past history of smoking, past history of drinking, and relationship status.

Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI)

The Pittsburgh Sleep Index is a self-administered questionnaire developed by Buysse et al.41 used to assess the subject’s sleep status over the last month. The scale comprises 19 self-assessment items and 5 other assessment items. The total score is calculated based on 18 items out of the 19 self-assessment items. These 18 items are categorized into seven main components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, daytime dysfunction, and sleep medication frequency. Scores for each component range from 0 (no difficulty) to 3 (severe difficulty), and the total scores varies from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate lower sleep quality, with a total score greater than 5 suggesting sleep impairment. The Chinese version of the scale has been widely used in Chinese population for localized sleep quality assessments and has exhibited good reliability and validity42. Moreover, in this study, the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.799, indicating a good internal consistency and thereby supporting the robustness of this scale.

UCLA loneliness scale (UCLA-LS)

Loneliness was measured in this research using the Loneliness Scale developed by Prof. Russell et al.43 at the University of California, Los Angeles. The scale contains a total of 20 self-assessment items, which consist of 10 positively worded sentences and 10 negatively worded sentences. The score for each item ranges from 1 (never) to 4 (always), and the individual items are summed to produce a total score. This scale is commonly used to measure loneliness, and the Chinese version of the scale has been widely used to measure loneliness in other parts of China with good reliability and validity44. In our research, the scale showed good reliability with the Cronbach’s alpha of 0.843.

Emotion regulation questionnaire (ERQ)

The Emotion Regulation Scale, originally developed by Gross and John45 was one of the most well-known instruments to investigate individual’s emotion regulation strategies and it has been subsequently revised by Wang et al.46 for a localized use. It comprises two dimensions: cognitive reappraisal (CR) and expressive suppression (ES).CR refers to regulating one’s emotions through cognition, and consists of a total of 6 items.ES refers to controlling one’s emotions through suppression of self-expression, and consists of a total of 4 items. The present study utilized the ES dimension of the scale, excluding the CR part, given our research focus of the ES instead of CR. The scoring method used a 7-point scale, with valid scores for ES ranging from 4 to 28. A higher score for the ES dimension indicates a greater tendency for the individual to use the strategy. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha for ERQ was 0.876.

Perceived stress scale (PSS-10)

The Perceived Stress Scale was developed by Cohen et al.47 in 1983, aimed to measure the extent to which a subject’s stress level reaches over the course of a month. The scale comprises 10 questions, each scored on a 5-point scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (very common). The scores of each question are summed to produce the total score which ranges from 0 to 40. Higher scores indicates a higher level of stress. The Chinese version of the questionnaire used in this research was revised by Wang et al.48. and has demonstrated good reliability and validity. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha for PSS-10 was 0.788.

Procedure

The survey was administered online between April and October of 2024. Prior to commencing the study, the questionnaire were distributed to several teachers with relevant academic background as well as some undergraduates from non-sample schools for pretest and feedback. Their opinions were collected regarding the layout. Then, guided by online distribution method, the questionnaire was distributed using the online surveying tool ‘Questionnaire Star’ and computed on computers or mobile devices. The questionnaires were forwarded to the class by teachers via a link or QR code, and students are informed of their right to voluntarily participate in the survey. They were provided an explanation of the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality, duration, and data retention. Informed consent was received from participants for this study in accordance with the guidelines set forth by the Ethics Committee of Bengbu Medical University (2024 − 411). After obtaining the informed consent from participants, the questionnaires will be accessible for completion. Additionally, during the questionnaire completion process, our researchers were available online to respond to inquiries, ensuring the seamless operation of the survey procedure.

Data analysis

Data collation was performed using Excel, while subsequent descriptive statistics, correlation analysis and regression analysis were executed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 and M-plus 8.3. Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient for normally distributed data and Spearman’s correlation coefficient for non-normally distributed data. In this research, regression analyses of multiple linear were conducted using M plus to assess the interlocking mediating role between loneliness and sleep quality. A bootstrap test with 5,000 samples was used to compute 95% confidence intervals. A significant mediation effect is indicated by a confidence interval that excludes 049. The threshold for statistical significance was P < 0.0550.

In addition, a Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess the presence of common method bias. Through principal component analysis, we identified 10 components with eigenvalues greater than 1. The first common factor explained 21.45% of the total variance, which was below the required criterion of 40%, suggesting that the data in this research was not seriously biased by common method variance51.

Discussion

Loneliness among college students is a prevalent problem and is associated with sleep quality. The current study confirmed that loneliness is linked to sleep quality both directly and indirectly. The direct effect accounted for 42.15% of the total effect in this research, indicating that loneliness is a strong predictor of sleep quality. This observation is consistent with recent evidence that higher level of loneliness was associated with decreased sleep quality28,52 and aligns with earlier reports that loneliness increases sleep fragmentation and that perceived loneliness prospectively predicts less salubrious sleep53,54. Theoretically, it fits with the classical model of loneliness proposes, which suggests that heightened vulnerability and hypervigilance of environment threat were unhelpful for sound sleep, and associated with pre-sleep arousal, and morning tiredness, thereby impeding restorative sleep20,28,55.

The indirect effect comprises three paths and together accounts for over half (58.33%) of the total effect. Expressive suppression and perceived stress significantly mediate the relationship between loneliness and sleep quality among college students. These three paths are respectively: L → PS → SQ; L → ES → SQ; L → ES → PS → SQ. Among the three mediating pathways, the single mediating path “L→PS→SQ” has the largest effect size, accounting for 45.04% of the total effect. This indicates that the effect of loneliness on sleep quality is primarily mediated by perceived stress. It is consistent with the loneliness model and a recent study by O’Connor & Rogerson28, who experimentally showed that loneliness has indirect effects on sleep quality through stress. Within the model of loneliness, loneliness is associated with stress. Lonely individuals are likely to suffer from a self-reinforcing loneliness loop as they tend to expect negative social interaction, keep distance from others and view social distance beyond control55. Loneliness is also reported linked with lower social support which hinders stress recovery56,57. Our findings of the correlation between loneliness and stress is in line with studies by Cacioppo et al.30 and Kang et al.57. Finally, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis has suggested a moderate and comparable effect size for sleep quality and stress among college undergraduates58. Therefore, it is plausible that loneliness is associated with poor sleep quality through the mediating role of high perceived stress.

The path “ L → ES → SQ ” suggests that loneliness is associated with frequent use of expressive suppression, which in turn is related with poorer sleep quality. In line with the model of Loneliness55, individuals who feel lonely tend to distance themselves from social partners, and are characterized by a high use of expressive suppression59–61. Moreover, expressive suppression—the inhibition of ongoing emotion-expressive behaviors—is maladaptive and is associated with negative emotions and adverse outcomes45. Consistently, the results showed that expressive suppression was associated with sleep quality, which aligns with findings that poorer sleep quality is correlated with increased use of expressive suppression38,62,63. Therefore, loneliness can be associated with a high use of expressive suppression, which further links with poorer sleep quality.

With regard to the combined chain-mediated pathways “ L → ES → PS → SQ ”, the chain model holds, though the effect size is comparatively small. This indicates that loneliness (L) is associated with sleep quality (SQ) through the mediating roles of emotional stress (ES) and perceived stress (PS). The relation between expressive suppression and perceived stress is consistent with findings by Caramanica et al.64. Considering the valuation system in Gross’s Emotion Regulation Process, the expressive suppression starts at the last stage — the modulation stage, which means that the inhibitory effort imposes on the physiological and cognitive demands, creating a new while more challenging context to cope39. Moreover, the cognitive demands of expressive suppression deplete resources39, and depleted resources without replenishment contribute to stress65. Empirical studies by Moore et al.36 and Öztekin et al.66 suggest that expressive suppression is related to self-reported stress symptoms and stress perception. Besides, expressive suppression, which is considered maladaptive, acts on the behavioral expression of emotion rather than the emotional experience itself. It has been reported as unhelpful in preventing the accumulation of negative emotions, which is associated with stress experiences67,68. Additionally, use of expressive suppression have been found to be associated with lower social support and worse interpersonal functioning45,69, which are associated with perceived stress57. Taken together, the cognitive load, impaired social relations, and accumulation of negative emotions associated with expressive suppression plausibly translate into heightened stress.

Regarding covariates, our research found that grade differences, smoking or drinking habits, and relationship status all significantly impact college students’ sleep quality, consistent with previous research70–73. Although inconsistent with a previous study that indicated no significant differences in poor sleep quality between different grade levels of medical school students in China74, our data revealed a poorer sleep quality of the senior students, aligning with findings by Hu, et al.75, which corroborates that fourth grade or above in college has been reported as a risk factor for sleep quality75. The inconsistency may be explained by an unprecedented intense and harsh competition in job market, elevated requirement for academic degree and abilities and postgraduate exams in the post-Covid 19 era76. Senior grade students experienced increased employment-related or academic stress in the post-Covid 19 era77. High employment stress and high academic stress both are highly correlated with poor sleep quality78. At the same time, senior college students tend to take courses that are more difficult than those taken junior college students. Moreover, senior college students also face challenges like internships and the preparation of graduation theses, which possibly generates more negative emotions and affects the quality of sleep.

Sleep quality disparity between smokers and non-smokers, alcohol consumers and non-alcohol consumers can be attributed to the correlation between sleep hygiene or health-promoting style and sleep quality to some extent. Bad sleep hygiene, including drinking alcohol and smoking is believed to linked with sleep problems79; Besides, poor health-promoting lifestyle, which includes drinking, has been argued as a risk factor for sleep disorders among medical university students80. Notably, individuals who ingest nicotine, a neuroactive drug that significantly increase cardiovascular and central nervous system activity81, tend to be more awake82, making them more susceptible to sleep disorders. With regard to the significant difference in sleep quality between drinkers and non-drinkers, one of the possible factors is the use of alcohol. Specifically, it has been shown that uptake of adenosine (AD) by alcohol increases extracellular AD83, which inhibits the action of the basal forebrain (BF) on neurons, suggesting that alcohol has the potential to disrupt sleep homeostasis and leads to poorer sleep quality in individuals.

Additionally, our research data indicates that college students in romantic relationships score lower on sleep quality metrics than their single counterparts, suggesting that individuals in relationships experience poorer sleep quality compared to those who are single. This finding is consistent with prior researches12,75,84, which suggests that the poorer sleep quality among individuals in romantic relationships can be attributed to physiological changes due to relationship distress. Relationship distress (such as conflict) significantly elevates daily cortisol secretion levels. High cortisol levels enhance central nervous system excitability, disrupting sleep depth and continuity, and thereby diminishing overall sleep quality12,75,84. Moreover, combination of academic pressure and “romantic distress” (such as emotional entanglements or heartbreak) is associated with a marked decline in sleep quality75. This phenomenon was particularly pronounced within East Asian cultural contexts85. Compared to Western countries like the United States, students in East Asian nations such as China and South Korea generally endure higher academic pressures. Within East Asian societies, romantic relationships are more likely to trigger guilt, anxiety, and family conflicts, which in turn negatively impact academic performance. Conversely, in Western cultures, dating is viewed as a normal developmental stage with minimal or neutral effects on studies. Thus, under this culturally imposed burden, the combination of high academic pressure and the inherent emotional fluctuations and maintenance demands of romantic relationships form chronic stressors that severely disrupt students’ sleep quality. Consequently, Western students often experience less significant impacts on their sleep quality.

The results of this research can help to apply in the following aspects. First, loneliness is an important influence on sleep quality in college students, and a healthy psychological state plays a very important role in sleep quality. Second, expressive suppression and perceived stress play a role between loneliness and sleep quality. When the regulation of sleep quality by loneliness is inhibited, the goal of improving sleep quality can be achieved by regulating expressive suppression and perceived stress. When regulating sleep quality in college students, multiple factors should be considered and comprehensive measures should be taken. This includes encouraging schools, communities and families to foster a more relaxing, supportive environment, and recommending professionals and institutions to promote emotional regulatory tools to help reduce individual expressive suppression, and improve individual mental health, etc. At the same time, it is also necessary to correctly guide college students to establish good and effective living habits to avoid the deterioration of sleep quality. Besides, given the poorer sleep quality of high grade levels, specific attention should be directed toward this group and appropriate social supports should be readily provided when necessary.

Limitations

The present research has certain limitations, The cross-sectional design limits our ability to accurately infer causal relationships between loneliness, expressive suppression, perceived stress and sleep quality. Furthermore, this research did not fully account for the impact of factors such as sample size and data distribution on the results. The model lacks potential key confounding variables (e.g., depression, anxiety, social support, physical health status, etc.). Sleep hygiene indicators such as sleep schedules and caffeine intake lack precise measurement, potentially introducing confounding bias. In order to deepen our understanding of this phenomenon and optimize the theoretical model, future research should consider incorporating longitudinal research methods as well as in-depth exploration of potential mediators.

Second, all participants in this research were randomly selected from the university town in Bengbu City, Anhui Province, and a sample from a single geographic source may weaken the external validity of the findings considering the significant regional cultural differences among Chinese college students. Future research should expand the geographic scope to enhance the generalizability of the discoveries. Furthermore, the sample collection exhibited a significant disparity in the proportion of fifth-year medical students versus lower-year medical students. This imbalance in distribution may negatively impact the validity of grade-based statistical analyses, and such sample composition bias should be considered in future research. Finally, this research only focused on the role of loneliness, expressive suppression, and perceived stress on college students’ sleep quality and did not cover other potential influences. In order to broaden the research horizons, future research could explore the possible effects of other domains on sleep quality by increasing the sample size to encompass a wider range of participants.

Conclusion

In summary, a direct relationship between loneliness and sleep quality, as well as indirect effects on sleep quality mediated by expressive suppression and perceived stress. Specifically, lower loneliness, expressive suppression, and perceived stress are associated with better sleep quality. Conversely, alleviated loneliness as well as decreased use of expressive suppression can reduce the perceived stress. These findings provide a theoretical basis and foundation for developing interventions to improve sleep quality among college students.
