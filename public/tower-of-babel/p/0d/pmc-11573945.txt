Survival Outcomes in US Medicare Patients with Non-Cystic Fibrosis Bronchiectasis by Rate of Baseline Exacerbations
Authors: Joseph Feliciano, Benjamin Lewing, Maitreyee Mohanty, Melanie Lauterio, Sebastian Fucile, Joseph Tkacz, Alan F. Barker
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThere are limited real-world data on outcomes in patients with non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis (NCFBE). This study assessed clinical characteristics and survival in patients with NCFBE by baseline exacerbation rate.MethodsPatients with bronchiectasis (≥ 1 inpatient or ≥ 2 outpatient claims with a bronchiectasis diagnosis code, or one outpatient claim with bronchiectasis code and a chest computed tomography scan) were from the 100% Medicare Fee-for-Service database (Jan 2014–Dec 2020). Patients had continuous enrollment ≥ 12 months pre-index (baseline) and post-index (follow-up), with index a random bronchiectasis claim preceded by ≥ 12 months bronchiectasis history. Patients with cystic fibrosis were excluded. Patients were stratified by exacerbations during baseline (0, 1, or ≥ 2). Follow-up exacerbation rate and all-cause mortality were assessed. Controls were identified using a multistep direct matching approach. Time to death from index was estimated by Kaplan–Meier analyses.ResultsExacerbation analysis included 92,529 patients with NCFBE and 92,529 matched controls. Exacerbations were common (43% had ≥ 1 exacerbation), with patients with more baseline exacerbations more likely to have ≥ 2 exacerbations during follow-up (11.4%, 24.2%, and 46.8% of patients with 0, 1, and ≥ 2 baseline exacerbations, respectively). Survival analysis included 110,298 patients with NCFBE and 110,298 controls. Time to death was shorter in patients with more baseline exacerbations (P < 0.0001). Five-year survival was 55.3%, 62.6%, and 65.4% for patients with ≥ 2, 1, and 0 baseline exacerbations, respectively, compared with 64.1% for controls.ConclusionsIn these patients with NCFBE, exacerbations were common. History of exacerbations was associated with future exacerbations and increased all-cause mortality.

Introduction

Non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis (NCFBE) is a chronic respiratory condition characterized by permanent bronchial dilation, persistent cough and sputum production, and recurrent respiratory infections [1, 2]. Recurrent exacerbations, which are defined as a worsening of usual respiratory symptoms, are common in patients with NCFBE [1, 3].

The prevalence of NCFBE has been increasing globally, affecting an estimated 52 people per 100,000 in the USA in 2001 and 139 people per 100,000 in 2013 [4–6]. Reported all-cause mortality rates for patients with NCFBE range from 9% to 20% over 3–6 years of follow-up and up to 30% at 1 year after a severe exacerbation [2, 7–10]. In a recent study of over 14,000 patients from the Korean National Health Insurance Service database, all-cause mortality was 1.15-fold higher in patients with NCFBE compared with a matched control cohort [11]. However, recent real-world data on survival among patients with NCFBE in the USA are limited. NCFBE also impacts health-related quality of life and has a substantial economic burden, reported to be approximately US $13,000–26,000 per patient per year in the USA in 2001–2020 [6, 12–14]. Exacerbations have been reported to drive an increase in the economic burden up to US $37,000 per patient per year in the USA in 2008 to 2011, largely due to increased rates of hospitalization [12, 13].

The causes of exacerbations are varied and may include bacterial or viral infection and environmental effects [15]. However, certain factors are associated with an increased risk of exacerbations, including a history of previous exacerbations [1, 15]. For instance, one study of 2572 patients across 10 European centers found incident-rate ratios for future exacerbations of 1.73, 3.14, and 5.97 for patients with one, two, and three exacerbations per year at baseline, respectively [1]. Other risk factors for frequent exacerbations include infection with Pseudomonas aeruginosa and comorbidities, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and immunodeficiency [15]. The occurrence of exacerbations is linked to poorer outcomes for patients, with a greater frequency of exacerbations (≥ 2 per year) being associated with declining lung function and increased all-cause mortality [1, 16]. There is also a clear association between exacerbation frequency and all-cause mortality, with a “frequent exacerbator” phenotype (defined as patients who have three or more exacerbations per year) being associated with greater long-term disease severity and increased mortality risk [1].

Treatment options for patients with NCFBE are limited, with none currently approved by the US Food and Drug Administration or the European Medicines Agency [2]. Therapeutic approaches are aimed at reducing symptoms, preventing exacerbations, improving quality of life, and limiting disease progression [2].

Despite its association with mortality, high healthcare burden, and its increasing prevalence, there is a paucity of real-world data on outcomes in patients with NCFBE [11, 17, 18]. The objective of this real-world study was to assess differences in clinical characteristics and survival among patients with NCFBE, stratified by number of baseline exacerbations.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This was a longitudinal, observational, retrospective study of patients in the 100% Medicare Fee‑for‑Service (FFS) database (parts A, B, and D) with a bronchiectasis diagnosis from Jan 2014 to Dec 2020 (Fig. 1). Access to the 100% Medicare FFS database was provided under a research data agreement between Inovalon and the Centers for Medicare Services.Fig. 1Study design. NCFBE non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis

Study design. NCFBE non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis

Study Population: Patients with NCFBE

Patients were included if they had ≥ 1 inpatient or ≥ 2 outpatient claims with an International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9/ICD-10 diagnosis code for bronchiectasis (J47*) that were > 30 and < 365 days apart, or one outpatient claim with a bronchiectasis diagnosis code and chest computed tomography (CT) scan.

Patients were excluded if they presented ≥ 1 claim for pulmonary fibrosis, cystic fibrosis, interstitial lung disease, sarcoidosis, or acute bronchitis during the pre- and post-index periods. Patients included in the exacerbation analyses had continuous enrollment ≥ 12 months pre-index (baseline period) and ≥ 12 months post-index (follow-up period). Patients included in the survival analysis had continuous enrollment ≥ 12 months pre-index (baseline period). To avoid immortal time bias, there were no post-index enrollment requirements in this analysis.

Patients with NCFBE were stratified by number of exacerbations during the baseline period (0, 1, or ≥ 2). A randomly selected bronchiectasis claim after > 1 year of NCFBE disease history was set as the index date to ensure a prevalent sample.

Study Population: Controls Without a Claim for Bronchiectasis

Patients without a bronchiectasis diagnosis were eligible as controls. Apart from the NCFBE diagnostic criteria, all other criteria for the control cohort were the same as for the NCFBE cohort. Controls without a claim for bronchiectasis were matched to patients with NCFBE using a multistep direct matching approach based on age, sex, length of enrollment in the Medicare FFS database, COPD status, Deyo–Charlson comorbidity index (DCI) score, and whether or not they appeared within the database following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Following the match, the date of the claim closest to the index date of their matched patient with NCFBE was selected as their index date. In instances where multiple controls matched to a single patient with NCFBE, the control patient with the index date closest to their matched patient with NCFBE was selected.

Study Endpoints: Demographics

Patient demographics including gender, age, race/ethnicity, and geographic region were assessed on the index date. Clinical characteristics including the DCI, baseline levels of disease exacerbations, comorbidities, and prior respiratory treatments were assessed during the 12-month baseline period.

Study Endpoints: Exacerbation Analysis

Bronchiectasis exacerbations were assessed using a previously published Medicare claims-based definition in which an exacerbation was identified and defined as either a severe exacerbation (where there was an inpatient claim with a primary/admitting diagnosis of bronchiectasis, or an outpatient claim with a bronchiectasis diagnosis code in any position followed by an intravenous antibiotic claim within 7 days) or a moderate exacerbation (where there was an outpatient claim with a bronchiectasis diagnosis code in any position followed by a prescription for an antibiotic filled within 7 days) [19]. Rate of exacerbations (per year) was assessed. Time to exacerbation was assessed from the index date.

Study Endpoints: Mortality

All-cause mortality was determined by the presence of a validated death record after the index date from the Master Beneficiary Summary file, which is part of the Medicare FFS database. Patients without a death date or disenrollment date were assumed alive and censored as of the end of enrollment or end of study period.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics were presented for patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and overall survival, including means with SD and medians for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Between-group differences were tested for significance using Student t test for normally distributed continuous variables, Mann–Whitney U tests for skewed continuous variables, and chi-square tests for categorical data. Time to death from index was estimated with Kaplan–Meier analyses. Analyses were conducted in SAS (Cary, NC) and the critical alpha level set to 0.05.

Ethical Approval

This study was verified as exempt from an ethical approval by Solutions IRB according to the Code of Federal Regulations under 45 CFR 46 section 101 (b)(4) as the study used previously existing, anonymized, and deidentified patient data. For this type of study, informed consent is waived/not applicable.

Results

Patient Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 92,529 patients with NCFBE and 92,529 matched controls were included in the exacerbation analysis. Patients with NCFBE had similar demographics as controls, as reported previously [14, 20]. Mean (SD) age was 76.6 (8.8) years for the control cohort and 76.7 (8.8) years for the NCFBE cohort; most patients in each cohort were female (72.3%) and White (87.6% in the control cohort and 88.0% in the NCFBE cohort).

Overall, patient health was similar between the NCFBE and control cohorts, with mean (SD) DCI scores of 3.2 (2.4) and 3.3 (2.4), respectively. Respiratory comorbidities were more prevalent in the NCFBE cohort than the control cohort. The most prevalent respiratory comorbidities were asthma (55.1% vs 40.1% of patients in the NCFBE vs control cohorts), COPD (45.9% vs 38.1%), and chronic cough (42.1% vs 18.8%).

In total, 110,298 patients with NCFBE and 110,298 matched controls were included in the survival analyses, with similar demographics at baseline (Table 1). As in the exacerbation analysis cohorts, there was a lower proportion of Black patients in the NCFBE cohort (4.0%) than in the control cohort (7.3%), and a higher proportion of Asian patients (3.5% vs 1.7%). Exacerbation Analysis

Bronchiectasis exacerbations were relatively common during the 1-year follow-up period: 21.6% of patients had ≥ 2 exacerbations during follow-up, 21.4% had 1 exacerbation, and 57.1% had 0 exacerbations. A greater proportion of patients with exacerbations during baseline presented with exacerbations during the follow‑up period (Fig. 2). Of patients with 0 exacerbations at baseline, the majority (69.6%) also presented with 0 exacerbations during follow-up; 18.9% had 1 exacerbation during follow-up, and 11.4% had ≥ 2 exacerbations during follow-up. There was a trend towards decreasing 5-year survival with increasing number of exacerbations at baseline, from 65.4% in patients with 0 exacerbations at baseline, to 62.6% and 55.3% in patients with 1 exacerbation and ≥ 2 exacerbations at baseline, respectively (Table 3). Discussion

Real-world data on outcomes for patients with NCFBE are limited [11, 17, 18]. This study provides real-world evidence on survival and exacerbations in patients with NCFBE in a US cohort of > 90,000 patients. Among these patients, 5-year survival was poorer in those with ≥ 2 baseline exacerbations (55.3%) compared with all patients with NCFBE (62.5%) and controls without NCFBE (64.1%).

The trend for higher mortality rates in patients with NCFBE compared with controls seen in this large-scale database study in the USA is consistent with previous trends seen in Europe, South America, and Asia. These indicate that mortality rates have risen over the past decade, possibly influenced by greater rates of NCFBE diagnosis due to increased awareness and improved diagnostics [7–11]. Several single-center studies have reported all-cause mortality rates ranging from 11% to 20% over 3–6 years [7–10]. These estimates are lower than seen in our study, which may be attributable to the differences in the study population, such as different country-specific life expectancies, or a higher proportion of patients with respiratory comorbidities in this study in both NCFBE and control groups [7–9]. A larger single-center study from 2005 to 2016 of over 18,000 patients with NCFBE and 90,000 non-NCFBE controls in Seoul National University Hospital found that 9% of patients in the NCFBE group died compared with 0.8% in a control group over a follow-up of 5.9 and 5.4 years, respectively, in a younger population with fewer comorbidities than our study [10].

Large-scale retrospective database studies, similar to our study in a US population, have been carried out in Korean and UK populations [11, 21]. Choi et al. found that in Korea, 10-year all-cause mortality was 1.15-fold higher in patients with NCFBE compared with matched controls (2505/100,000 person-years vs 2142/100,000 person-years, respectively) [11]. In comparison, 5-year all-cause mortality in our study was approximately 1.08-fold higher in patients with NCFBE compared with matched controls (21.9% vs 20.2%). In a population-based UK cohort study, Quint et al. reported age-adjusted all-cause mortality rates from 2004 to 2013 of 1438/100,000 for women and 1915/100,000 for men, both more than twice the mortality rates in the general population [21]. The latter study was based on primary care data, and different selection criteria were used for the control population, which might explain the higher mortality rate vs controls compared with the present study. Alternatively, these may reflect genuine differences between these populations.

In this study, exacerbations were relatively common in patients with NCFBE, with over 40% of patients experiencing exacerbations during the 1-year follow-up period. A history of exacerbations was associated with future exacerbations, and a higher exacerbation rate was associated with increased all-cause mortality. The observed link between NCFBE severity and poorer outcomes is also consistent with previous studies [1, 8, 16]. In a multicenter study of 10 clinics in Europe and Israel, Chalmers et al. showed that patients with frequent exacerbations at baseline were more likely to have frequent exacerbations during the next 4 years [1]. They found an association between number of exacerbations and survival over the following 5 years (Cox hazard ratio for all-cause mortality for patients with ≥ 3 exacerbations per year vs patients with 0 exacerbations per year of 1.86, 95% CI 1.30–2.66) [1]. Similarly, results from a prospective cohort study in the UK (2008–2012) showed increased NCFBE severity, based on a “bronchiectasis severity index,” to be a predictor for all-cause mortality, hospitalizations, and reduced quality of life [16]. The study found ≥ 3 exacerbations in the year before the study to be an independent predictor of 4-year mortality [16]. In a single-center prospective study of 120 patients with NCFBE in Brazil from 2017 to 2020, all-cause mortality at 41 months was 10.8%, and the researchers identified exacerbations among several factors associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality [8].

Our study highlights the high healthcare burden of NCFBE in the USA. In accordance with previous studies from other countries, we saw a strong association between bronchiectasis exacerbations and negative health outcomes, including all-cause death.

Strengths and Limitations

As a result of the large sample sizes collected in the study, some between-cohort differences may be statistically significant but not clinically meaningful. However, as the data in the study are derived from the Medicare FFS database, which includes all patients with NCFBE who have traditional Medicare coverage, a large (> 10,000 patients) and geographically diverse selection of patients was included in the study. In addition, the Medicare database includes a validated date of death and race/ethnicity data, which are often sparsely populated in other data sources. As direct measures of disease severity were not available, the definition of exacerbations was limited to the information captured in the database (e.g., diagnoses, healthcare settings). There is currently no consensus definition of a bronchiectasis exacerbation for real-world studies, which may lead to underestimates in the incidence of exacerbations in this population and limits comparability to other studies. However, the definition in this study has been published previously in a similar study with a claims-based approach to identify exacerbations in patients with NCFBE from a Medicare database [19]. As with any study based on administrative claims, it should be noted that these data are potentially subject to data-coding limitations, data entry error, and a lack of some clinical test results such as sputum culture, which may result in missing or erroneous data [22]. While patients were identified using claims data and the presence of a diagnosis code for bronchiectasis, the criteria to identify patients with NCFBE (which included the requirement for a chest CT scan for patients with only 1 outpatient claim with a diagnosis code for bronchiectasis) have been utilized in prior studies [4, 5, 23]. The control cohort was matched using a multistep direct matching approach taking into account a variety of key demographic and clinical characteristics; however, as claims data lack the clinical and possible demographic detail of other data sources (e.g., electronic health records), the matching variables included in the current study were based on convenience/availability. Finally, the study is limited to patients with Medicare insurance and may not be generalizable to uninsured patients or patients with other insurance.

Conclusions

In this retrospective US claims study, patients with NCFBE had reduced survival compared with matched controls. Exacerbations were relatively common in patients with NCFBE, and a history of exacerbations was associated with future exacerbations, as well as poorer survival. Patients with NCFBE may benefit from the addition of new therapeutics to the treatment armamentarium to prevent pulmonary exacerbations and improve outcomes.
