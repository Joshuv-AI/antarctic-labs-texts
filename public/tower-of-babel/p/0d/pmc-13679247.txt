Lower fibrinogen and fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio in migraine with white matter hyperintensities: implications for microvascular vulnerability
Authors: Aslıhan Baran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhite matter hyperintensities (WMH) are increasingly recognized as imaging markers of migraine-related cerebral small-vessel injury. However, the biological correlates underlying WMH in migraine remain poorly defined. The relationship between WMH and the fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR), a composite hemostatic marker, has not yet been evaluated.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 69 patients with migraine were included after exclusion of systemic inflammatory and vascular confounders. WMH presence was assessed using brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Plasma fibrinogen and albumin levels were measured during the interictal period, and FAR was calculated. C-reactive protein (CRP) and inflammatory indices derived from complete blood counts were also analyzed. Multivariable regression analyses were performed with adjustment for age and sex.ResultsPatients with WMH had significantly lower fibrinogen levels and FAR compared to those without WMH (p = 0.003 and p = 0.004, respectively). After adjustment for age and sex, WMH remained independently associated with lower fibrinogen levels and FAR (p = 0.002 for both). No differences were observed in CRP or other systemic inflammatory markers.ConclusionsWMH in migraine were independently associated with lower fibrinogen levels and FAR. These findings suggest that WMH in migraine may reflect microvascular vulnerability associated with altered hemostatic balance rather than systemic inflammation. Further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms and clinical implications.

Background

Migraine is increasingly regarded not merely as an episodic pain condition but as a neurovascular disorder associated with vascular vulnerability. Accumulating evidence suggests that migraine may be linked to long-term vascular and microvascular consequences, with endothelial activation and dysfunction playing a central role in this association [1].

Endothelial dysfunction can contribute to alterations in cerebral hemodynamics, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, increased oxidative stress, and a shift toward a prothrombotic milieu. Importantly, these vascular alterations are not confined to ictal periods but have also been demonstrated during the interictal phase [2, 3].

Hemostatic markers provide important insights into the vascular biology associated with migraine. Although fibrinogen is traditionally considered a positive acute-phase reactant, findings regarding fibrinogen levels in migraine populations are inconsistent. While some studies—particularly in migraine with aura—have reported elevated fibrinogen levels, others have found normal or even reduced concentrations [4–7].

Albumin also plays a central role in vascular homeostasis through its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and endothelial-protective properties [8]. However, plasma albumin concentration alone may not fully reflect its functional antioxidant capacity, as albumin can undergo oxidative and structural modifications that impair its biological activity [8, 9]. Therefore, composite indices integrating fibrinogen and albumin may more sensitively reflect the balance between hemostatic activity and antioxidant defense than isolated biomarkers [10].

White matter hyperintensities (WMH), frequently detected on brain magnetic resonance imaging in patients with migraine, are increasingly considered imaging markers of chronic microvascular injury and endothelial dysfunction rather than incidental or purely age-related findings [11]. The presence of these lesions suggests that migraine may represent a neurovascular condition characterized by cumulative vascular and microvascular stress over time. In particular, the reported increase in WMH burden associated with migraine with aura, longer disease duration, and advancing age supports a role for chronic endothelial dysfunction and impaired microcirculatory regulation in migraine pathophysiology [11, 12].

Despite growing evidence linking migraine to endothelial dysfunction and WMH, the biological correlates underlying WMH formation in migraine remain incompletely understood. In particular, the extent to which subtle alterations in hemostatic balance contribute to WMH development has not been clearly defined.

We hypothesized that WMH in migraine may be associated with subtle alterations in vascular homeostasis, reflected by fibrinogen levels and the fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR), a composite biomarker integrating hemostatic activity and antioxidant defense.

Methods

Study population

This prospective cross-sectional study included 69 patients with migraine (49 women and 20 men) who presented to the Neurology outpatient clinic between September 2023 and March 2024. The sample size was determined based on the available eligible patients during the study period. Migraine diagnosis were established by an experienced neurologist according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) criteria [13].

To minimize potential confounding related to systemic inflammation, vascular risk, and altered hemostatic states, individuals were excluded if they were current smokers, regular alcohol consumers, had substance abuse, used oral contraceptives (women), had an acute infection at the time of evaluation, systemic inflammatory or autoimmune diseases, malignancy, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia (defined as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥ 160 mg/dL), arterial hypertension, coronary artery disease, chronic organ failure (renal, hepatic, or cardiac), other known vascular risk factors, or known inflammatory or hematological disorders.

Participants with medication-overuse headache related to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) underwent medication withdrawal prior to inclusion. Blood sampling in these individuals was performed one month after successful discontinuation of NSAID use.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging and WMH evaluation

All brain MRI examinations were performed using a 1.5-T MAGNETOM Altea BioMatrix MR scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 12-channel head matrix coil. All participants were imaged using the same standardized MRI protocol, including axial T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences. The standard FLAIR protocol included a repetition time (TR) of 4340 ms, echo time (TE) of 80 ms, inversion time (TI) of 1631.7 ms, a field of view (FOV) of 230 × 230 mm, and a slice thickness of 5.5 mm.

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) were defined as hyperintense lesions within the cerebral white matter on FLAIR images that were clearly distinguishable from cerebrospinal fluid signal. Brain MRI examinations were initially interpreted by experienced neuroradiologists as part of routine clinical practice. For the purposes of the present study, all MRI examinations were subsequently reviewed by the author, who was blinded to the participants’ clinical characteristics and laboratory findings. WMH were assessed by visual inspection and classified dichotomously as present or absent.

Laboratory assessments

Venous blood samples were collected under standardized conditions during the interictal period, defined as at least 3 days after and more than 3 days before a migraine attack. Blood samples were obtained in the morning, and routine laboratory analyses were performed on the same day.

Plasma fibrinogen levels were measured using standard coagulation assays. Plasma albumin and C-reactive protein (CRP) concentrations were determined using automated biochemical methods. Complete blood count parameters—including platelet count, mean platelet volume, and differential leukocyte counts—were measured using an automated hematology analyzer.

Derived inflammatory indices—including neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII)—were calculated using established formulas. The fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR) was calculated by dividing plasma fibrinogen levels by plasma albumin concentrations.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences). Continuous variables were assessed for normality and are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t tests or Welch’s t tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on expected cell counts.

Multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted to evaluate independent associations between WMH and laboratory parameters after adjustment for age and sex. Given the exploratory nature of the study and the relatively small sample size, correction for multiple comparisons was not applied, and the findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics

There was no significant difference in sex distribution between participants with and without white matter hyperintensities (WMH) (χ²(1) = 0.00, p = 0.994). Participants with WMH tended to be older than those without WMH (38.1 ± 8.1 vs. 35.3 ± 4.3 years); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Welch’s t-test, p = 0.088) (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics

Migraine with aura was more frequent among participants with WMH than among those without (16.1% vs. 5.3%); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.230).

The prevalence of chronic migraine also did not differ significantly between the two groups (35.5% vs. 21.1%; χ²(1) = 1.78, p = 0.182).

Hemostatic and inflammatory markers

Participants with WMH exhibited significantly lower fibrinogen levels and fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR) compared with those without WMH (p = 0.003 and p = 0.004, respectively). In contrast, plasma albumin and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels did not differ between groups. Other hematological and inflammatory indices, including platelet count, NLR, LMR, PLR and SII, were comparable between participants with and without WMH (Table 2).

Multivariable linear regression analyses

In multivariable linear regression analysis adjusted for age and sex, the presence of WMH was independently associated with lower fibrinogen levels (β = −0.374, p = 0.002). Age was not significantly associated with fibrinogen levels (p = 0.188), whereas sex showed a borderline association (p = 0.068). The overall model was statistically significant (adjusted R² = 0.148, p = 0.004) (Table 3).

In a separate multivariable model, WMH presence remained independently associated with a lower fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR) after adjustment for age and sex (β = −0.365, p = 0.002). Discussion

In this study, we examined the relationship between white matter hyperintensities (WMH) and hemostatic–inflammatory markers in patients with migraine, with particular focus on fibrinogen and the fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR). Patients with WMH exhibited significantly lower fibrinogen levels and FAR compared with those without WMH, whereas albumin and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels did not differ. Similarly, classical hematological and inflammatory indices—including NLR, LMR, PLR, and SII—were comparable between groups. Importantly, after adjustment for age and sex, WMH presence remained independently associated with lower fibrinogen levels and FAR.

Although fibrinogen is traditionally regarded as a positive acute-phase reactant, the observation of lower fibrinogen levels in WMH-positive patients may support a model of chronic microvascular stress rather than acute inflammatory activation. Beyond its inflammatory role, fibrinogen participates in the coagulation–fibrinolysis cycle, and sustained endothelial dysfunction may be associated with subtle alterations in coagulation and fibrinolytic pathways, potentially contributing to relatively lower circulating fibrinogen concentrations [2]. Within this framework, reduced fibrinogen may be compatible with chronic low-grade hemostatic activation and consumption dynamics, although this interpretation remains speculative in the absence of direct markers of thrombin generation or fibrin turnover (Fig. 1).

This interpretation is supported by previous clinical and genetic studies demonstrating that migraine susceptibility—particularly migraine with aura—is associated with alterations in coagulation pathways independent of classical systemic inflammatory markers. Elevated levels of factor VIII, von Willebrand factor, and fibrinopeptide A, accompanied by reduced fibrinogen levels, may be compatible with altered hemostatic regulation involving both activation and consumption pathways. This pattern points toward endothelial-driven hemostatic remodeling rather than a predominantly proinflammatory state [4, 6]. Accordingly, the lower fibrinogen levels observed in WMH-positive migraine patients in our study may represent a biological reflection of subtle alterations in hemostatic balance associated with chronic vascular vulnerability. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as direct markers of thrombin generation or fibrin turnover were not measured.

While fibrinogen alone reflects hemostatic activity, it is influenced by multiple biological processes, including inflammation and consumption dynamics [14]. FAR integrates fibrinogen with albumin, a multifunctional protein with antioxidant and endothelial-protective properties that contributes to vascular homeostasis [9, 15]. Even in the absence of differences in albumin levels, this composite index may provide a more integrated reflection of hemostatic load relative to vascular buffering capacity. In our cohort, FAR was reduced in parallel with fibrinogen and showed a similar association in multivariable models. However, its incremental contribution beyond fibrinogen alone appeared modest, suggesting that FAR may be better conceptualized as a complementary composite index rather than a superior biomarker.

The findings of the present study are consistent with recent population-based analyses demonstrating no significant associations between migraine and commonly used systemic inflammatory markers, including NLR, PLR, SII, and SIRI [16]. In contrast, only selective indices such as the lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR) have shown modest associations with migraine risk. This pattern further supports the notion that vascular alterations associated with WMH in migraine may be involve mechanisms beyond classical systemic inflammation, pointing instead toward a more nuanced interplay between endothelial function and hemostatic regulation. Future studies incorporating dynamic markers of thrombin generation and fibrin turnover are needed to clarify the biological mechanisms underlying these findings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the relatively modest sample size may limit statistical power and generalizability. In addition, a formal a priori power calculation was not performed because the sample size was based on the number of eligible patients available during the study period. WMH were evaluated dichotomously (present/absent) without semi-quantitative grading such as the Fazekas scale. In addition, the 5.5-mm FLAIR slice thickness may have reduced the sensitivity for detecting very small WMH lesions, potentially leading to underestimation of lesion burden. Detailed migraine-related clinical variables, including disease duration, attack frequency, and migraine subtype, were also not systematically available for all participants. However, the homogeneity of the cohort and the rigorous exclusion of inflammatory and vascular confounders likely reduced biological variability and enhanced the interpretability of the observed findings. Future studies incorporating quantitative WMH burden, detailed clinical phenotyping, and dynamic hemostatic markers may provide a more comprehensive understanding of these associations.

Conclusion

WMH in patients with migraine were associated with lower plasma fibrinogen levels and FAR, in the absence of differences in conventional systemic inflammatory markers. Contrary to the expected proinflammatory and procoagulant profile, these findings suggest that WMH in migraine may reflect increased microvascular vulnerability associated with alterations in endothelial–hemostatic regulation rather than overt systemic inflammation. Further longitudinal studies incorporating quantitative WMH assessment and dynamic hemostatic markers are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms and clinical implications of these findings.
