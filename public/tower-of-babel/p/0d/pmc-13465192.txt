Cultivating collaborative solutions: co-developing a preventative intervention for burnout among doctors working in a Namibian public hospital’s maternity care unit
Authors: Tanya Y. Brückner, E. V. Syurina, T. Endjala, T. van. den Akker, S. Heemelaar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBurnout among healthcare workers, especially in maternity units, is prevalent globally and exacerbated in resource-constrained contexts, contributing to decreased quality of care. Doctors in a maternity care unit (MCU) in a large Namibian public hospital complex were found to be at high risk of burnout, indicating need for intervention. Existing burnout interventions are predominantly individually-focused, created in a top-down manner and devised in high-resource settings. A dearth of research exists on burnout interventions that are contextually-fitted to low- and middle-resource settings in sub-Saharan Africa. This research aimed to explore how a burnout intervention could be co-created with local stakeholders in a Namibian public hospital MCU through an Interactive Learning and Action (ILA) approach.MethodsA research team comprising doctors of varying clinical levels was recruited and senior management figures were consulted throughout the ILA process. The iterative action-research process consisted of workshops with specific objectives, a training session, dialogue sessions and evaluative interviews.ResultsBased on a needs assessment, the research team devised ideas for six interventions. Priority setting of these interventions selected a mentorship programme to be fully developed and implemented. Outputs of each co-creation session created inputs for the next. After initial implementation of the intervention, adaptations were made based on input from end-users. End-evaluation showed the intervention’s potential to prevent and mitigate burnout by stimulating increased feelings of support, strengthening social relationships, and building trust. Barriers to uptake included distant mentorship style, lack of accountability, and perception of the intervention as an increased workload. Facilitators encompassed enthusiastic and personal mentorship style and positive perceptions of the intervention as helpful with matters like case management.ConclusionsCo-creating and implementing a context-specific intervention to address healthcare worker burnout appeared feasible in a sub-Saharan African context without funding. By leveraging local knowledge and skills, co-researchers collaboratively devised solutions addressing the needs of various levels of staff, created ownership of the intervention, and proactively integrated accommodations to contextual constraints into its design and implementation. Embracing local- and user-led co-creation in intervention development offers a promising avenue for addressing complex challenges.

Background

Healthcare workers (HCWs) are at high risk of burnout globally due to emotionally demanding work, heavy workloads, and limited support [1, 2]. HCW burnout is associated with adverse personal and organisational effects, including depression, anxiety, high staff turnover, unprofessional behaviour, reduced quality of provided care, and even suicidality among staff [3–9]. While most research is from high-income countries (HICs) in the Global North, emerging evidence from sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) also indicates high burnout rates among doctors, nurses, and health professional students [2, 10, 11]. In SSA, key contributors include inadequate staffing, excessive workloads, emotional distress, low social support, and interpersonal or professional conflict [2].

Within this broader trend, HCWs in maternity units are especially at risk. They report some of the highest levels of burnout among different specialty disciplines [12], likely exacerbated by frequent exposure to adverse events like maternal and perinatal deaths [13, 14]. This exposure, combined with physically and emotionally demanding conditions like heavy night shifts, high-acuity care, and work in high-risk settings, further compounds the risk of burnout [2].

In addition to clinical and emotional demands, structural contributors to burnout, such as staffing and resource shortages, are pronounced in many SSA health systems [15, 16]. In Namibia, a SSA country, the health system is split between public and private sectors. While the private sector is well-resourced, it remains financially inaccessible to most due to entrenched socio-economic inequities [17–19]. The public sector, which serves over 80% of the population, suffers from inadequate financial support and a critical shortage of human resources [20]. Fewer than 30% of Namibian doctors work in the public system, despite its overwhelming patient burden. The gap between available and needed healthcare personnel places heavy and sustained demands on public sector staff, significantly increasing their vulnerability to burnout [2, 21, 22].

In Namibia, the incidence of maternal and perinatal mortality is higher than expected for an upper-middle income country [23]. A recent national audit revealed that the majority of these deaths were potentially preventable with the provision of better care, and were largely attributed to avoidable delays within the healthcare facility. In particular, severe staff shortages frequently left junior staff without adequate supervision, forcing them to make high-stakes clinical decisions beyond their level of competency and compromising the quality of care [24]. Such conditions not only affect patient outcomes but also contribute to staff demotivation and exacerbate already high turnover rates [25–27]. Notably, the audit identified improved support for staff mental wellbeing as a key recommendation for addressing poor maternal and perinatal outcomes [24].

Several interventions have been developed to improve maternal and perinatal outcomes worldwide, with many focusing on enhancing the knowledge and skills of healthcare staff [28–30]. While such approaches are necessary, the importance of addressing burnout among maternity care unit (MCU) HCWs should not be overlooked. Doing so could improve staff retention and the quality of care provided [7, 9]. However, beyond these practical benefits, prioritising the mental health and wellbeing of HCWs is equally important as a matter of ethical and professional responsibility. The World Health Organisation recognises burnout as an occupational phenomenon, emphasising the responsibility of health systems to protect the wellbeing of their workers [31, 32].

Therefore, this study aimed to explore how a burnout intervention could be developed for HCW in MCUs. The study took place at a hospital complex, comprising two of the largest public hospitals in Namibia. This facility records the highest number of maternal and perinatal deaths in the country [24]. Recent research conducted at the identified hospital complex underscores the urgency of addressing burnout among HCWs in the MCU. One study reported high levels of burnout symptoms among midwives [33], while our previous research found that doctors in the same department faced significant burnout risk due to high exposure to maternal and perinatal deaths and excessive workloads. Additional contributing factors included a culture of blame, limited trust between colleagues, and a lack of perceived institutional and interpersonal support [11].

Preventing and mitigating burnout requires a comprehensive approach, given its complex and multi-layered nature. Systemic drivers like inadequate resource and workforce allocation must be addressed through policy level change, while interventions at organisational and individual levels are critical in managing occupational stress. However, individual-level interventions alone are often insufficient [34].

Despite global recognition of the need to address HCW burnout, many interventions are designed and implemented through top-down approaches, often originating from research institutions or organisational leadership in Global North HICs [35–41]. Replicating such interventions in SSA low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) without adapting them to the local context risks failure, reinforces global health inequities, and disregards the diversity of knowledge, practice and experience [42–44]. Tailoring interventions to local contexts and needs is essential for their success.

Co-creation offers a participatory and democratic framework for developing contextually relevant and culturally sensitive interventions. It engages local stakeholders as active contributors in identifying needs and shaping solutions, promoting shared ownership and mutual understanding [45–50]. This approach is particularly well-suited to complex problems like burnout, which arise from interrelated individual, organisational, and systemic factors. Burnout requires solutions grounded in the lived experiences of those affected as it is deeply influenced by workplace culture, relational dynamics, and structural pressures [2, 51]. Co-creation’s multi-directional approach to problem-solving allows for the integration of diverse perspectives, enabling the development of holistic and adaptable interventions. Grounding solutions in local knowledge and lived experience enhances their feasibility, acceptability, and sustainability, particularly in dynamic and resource-constrained health systems [45, 47].

Despite the potential of co-creation to improve health outcomes through inclusive and context-sensitive approaches, studies on co-creation in public healthcare settings in LMICs remain scarce [45, 52–54]. This gap is especially evident in SSA, where burnout among HCWs is high and contextually-tailored interventions are lacking. In response, this study aimed to explore how a burnout intervention could be co-created in a Namibian public hospital complex. Specifically, we examined the role of co-creation in developing an intervention to prevent and mitigate burnout among doctors working in the MCU of the large hospital complex identified above. The research team included doctors from the obstetrics and gynaecology department, who participated as co-developers throughout the process. The overarching goal was to investigate how co-creative methods could support intervention development in a dynamic and resource-constrained public healthcare setting.

Methodology

Approach

This case study used co-creation methodologies, including workshops, semi-structured interviews, and informal discussions - see the research process below for further details. To guide co-creation, we followed the Interactive Learning and Action (ILA) design, an iterative and reflexive form of co-creation. This involved action-research cycles of planning, action, observation, and reflection, where each phase informed the next, allowing for adaptive problem solving [55]. Reflection entailed assessing experiences of the burnout intervention, including its functionality and practicalities. By embedding co-creation, shared decision-making, and iterative learning into the process, the research team and MCU doctors worked together to develop a contextually-fitted intervention. Recognising that power imbalances could shape collaboration, both within the research team and among the doctors involved, we actively incorporated reflexive strategies to promote equitable participation.

Author positionality

The primary researcher (T.Y.B.) is a white Namibian woman affiliated with a Dutch university. Aware that her identity and affiliation could shape interactions and findings, she engaged in reflexive practices like peer debriefing, respondent validation, and participatory research practices to mitigate power imbalances. While her background in global health, psychology, and anthropology informed the study, the co-creation approach prioritized the lived experiences and expertise of MCU doctors in shaping the intervention.

Both S.H. and T.v.d.A are Dutch obstetricians and gynaecologists with experience in maternal health research in Namibia, with S.H. having worked in the MCU for several years. T.E., a former midwife in the MCU and now head of the community health department at the University of Namibia’s School of Nursing and Public Health, played a key role in aligning the research with local healthcare priorities. E.V.S., affiliated with a Dutch university, contributed expertise in global mental health and co-creating health interventions.

In line with the ILA approach, the research process was iterative and reflexive, emphasising shared decision making, feedback loops, and equitable knowledge exchange. This ensured the intervention was not externally imposed but co-created with Namibian healthcare professionals, embedding their insights into both the research process and outcomes.

Setting

Both hospitals that form the hospital complex are state hospitals in the capital city, Windhoek. One of the hospitals is a national referral hospital. The hospital complex has an academic function, providing medical education and training to medical interns and to students from an affiliated medical school. Doctors working in the obstetrics and gynaecology department are responsible for care in both hospitals, which have independent gynaecology wards and maternity units. Each day, doctors are divided into teams and provide either obstetric or gynaecological care in one of the two hospitals. However, sometimes they have to travel between hospitals, as the blood bank or intensive care is only available at one hospital, or because there are not enough doctors to cover each hospital. The number of doctors in the MCU varies depending on availability of funds from the Ministry of Health and Social Services to create or fill vacancies.

In preparation of the current study, we performed a mixed-methods needs assessment among doctors in the same department with the aim to explore the drivers, experiences, and impact of burnout symptoms. This study, further referred to as our ‘previous’ research and the ‘preceding’ study, found a high prevalence of burnout risk among doctors in the obstetrics and gynaecology department where, in a department of 54 doctors, seven out of 18 participants were at very high risk for burnout and three were at risk [11]. A lack of support emerged as a key contributor to burnout. Systemic contributors encompassed inadequate resource availability and allocation leading to understaffing and high workloads, and subsequently elevated stress levels. Personal and occupational drivers entailed high exposure to adverse events and a lack of trust among colleagues. Doctors experienced low moods and work dissatisfaction resulting from a pervasive blame culture, while academic support from senior colleagues and peer support facilitated feelings of well-being. Variables included gender and occupational position. The burnout risk was similar between Medical Interns and Medical Officers, while gender qualitatively impacted burnout-related experiences.

Participants and recruitment

Stakeholders included representatives of six hierarchical occupational groups: the Head of Department (HOD), Specialist Gynaecologists and Obstetricians (specialists), Senior Medical Officers (SMOs), Medical Officers (MOs), Medical Intern Team Leaders, and Medical Interns (interns). For the sake of anonymity, stakeholders in this study are clustered into three groups: specialists, MOs, and interns.

Interns are doctors who recently completed medical school and are required to work under strict supervision of MOs and specialists in eight different departments for two years before becoming MOs themselves. Every four months, a new group of interns joins the MCU. MOs receive daily supervision from specialists for admitted patients and work independently during on calls with a specialist on standby. Midwives conduct low-risk births. If pregnancy or birth complications arise, interns are called to assess the patient first. If they need further help, they should contact the MO on call. Interns are trained to perform caesarean sections, always under supervision of an MO. See our previous paper for more information [11]. At the time of study, there were 54 doctors working in the MCU: 27 interns, 18 MOs, and nine specialists.

The research team was recruited through purposive and snowball sampling and consisted of eleven key stakeholders who were representative of the occupation groups outlined above: there were six interns, three MOS, and two specialists. Research process

This study consisted of five phases in accordance with the ILA and was conducted between February and June 2021. Table 1 details an overview of each phase. Phase 1 consisted of participant observations in the department and a mixed-methods needs assessment. It is reported in a previous article [11] and summarised above in the setting description.

For this article, phases 2–5 are discussed. The duration of each workshop or session in each phase ranged from 90 to 120 minutes, depending on participant availability. Phase 2 entailed a discussion of preliminary results from phase 1 in an unstructured brainstorming workshop with the research team, allowing for a wider range of ideas to be generated. The workshop assisted in analysing problem perceptions and ideas.

In phase 3 we conducted a prioritisation exercise where we identified the top three choices of interventions. The options presented were based on the outcomes of phase 2. The exercise was conducted among all doctors working and present in the department that day and the research team. Each participant indicated their top three choices. A co-creation workshop ensued where the top three identified interventions were further conceptualised and developed by the research team. With a prioritisation exercise, further detailed in results, one intervention was selected.

Phase 4 encompassed project formulation and implementation of the selected intervention, where the plan developed in phase three was actioned. Here, there was an iterative process of observation, reflection, and re-planning of the intervention as challenges or room for improvement arose. This phase consisted of several elements such as a mentor training session, evaluative interviews and informal dialogue sessions.

Phase 5 consisted of in-depth, semi-structured interviews evaluating how the intervention was perceived and experienced by stakeholders and identifying avenues for improvement.

Data analysis

The co-creation sessions were designed to produce practical outputs to guide intervention development. These included collaboratively generated materials like annotated flipcharts, prioritisation matrices, and brainstorming posters. See Fig. 1 for an overview of the outputs. Rather than conducting formal qualitative analysis of recorded discussions, the focus was on synthesising the tangible outputs created during each session. Key ideas and priorities were summarised by the primary researcher and used to inform the next project phase.Fig. 1Visualisation of the study overview

Visualisation of the study overview

The analysis was pragmatic and action-oriented, drawing on principles from participatory action research and co-design. Outputs were reviewed to identify recurring themes or concerns, which were grouped into broad categories to support collaborative decision-making. Summaries were shared with participants for member-checking - who confirmed that the interpretations were correct - and the findings are presented alongside each phase in the results section.

Data from the prioritisation exercise were analysed using descriptive statistics in IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26). Preferences were summarised using frequency distributions and compared across occupational groups to explore variation in priorities.

The semi-structured evaluative interviews were voice-recorded and manually transcribed. Thematic analysis was conducted in ATLAS.ti 8 using an inductive approach. Codes were developed from the data and iteratively grouped into broader themes based on conceptual patterns and recurring ideas.

Results

The results chronologically present the processes and outputs of each phase of the study, highlighting how each phase informed the next and ultimately contributed differently to the final intervention design and implementation. Figure 1 visualises the study overview.

Phase 2: Brainstorming possible interventions

Process: To begin, a brainstorming session occurred to develop ideas for interventions, informed by the outcomes of the needs assessment [11]. The primary researcher (T.Y.B) presented the outcomes of the preceding study with particular focus on the ideas for support. These included team building to build trust and better relationships among colleagues, improving communication between colleagues, workshops from mental health professionals, individual counselling sessions with a mental health professional, a mentorship programme between interns and MOs, and debriefings that focus on providing psychological support.

Output: An open-floor discussion ensued where team members corroborated the experiences and ideas and jointly decided to focus on six interventions. Each one addressed different forms of support identified as needed. These interventions were: a mentorship programme, debriefings after adverse events, team building, peer support groups, workshops from mental health professionals, and the establishment of a support line to mental health professionals. The team jointly brainstormed to conceptualise each intervention. Phase 3: Priority setting and co-creation of the intervention

Priority setting process: One week later, a prioritisation exercise was conducted during a departmental handover meeting to establish an intervention priority list. The intervention ideas developed in phase 2 were presented and circulated to all members of the department. Participants were not given pre-defined criteria for ranking the interventions; instead, the exercise aimed to capture participants’ unprompted preferences, without influencing them through externally imposed value frameworks.

Priority setting output: Overall, the top three choices were: the mentorship programme (35.6% preference), team building (21.1%), and debriefings (19.4%). However, the preferred interventions differed according to occupation group. Interns and MOs had the same top three choices, which were in line with the overall results, while specialists’ top choices were debriefings (27.5%), the mentorship programme (22.5%), and workshops from mental health professionals (17.5%). Co-creation workshop process: The prioritisation exercise was done by the research team the following day. The team was instructed not to interrupt the presentations and instead to note any questions or comments about another group’s presentation to ask once they were done. This allowed each group a fair chance to present their ideas, and to account for established power dynamics where more senior staff members might interrupt, speak over or question their junior colleagues.

Co-creation workshop output: The mentorship programme was jointly selected as first-choice intervention. Practicalities of the mentorship programme

Interns would be the mentees, MOs would be the mentors. Becoming a mentor would be voluntary, as not all MOs would like to be a mentor. A specialist would be allocated to each mentor group to provide guidance to the MO if needed, and for interns to contact if their allocated MO was unavailable.

For each MO, there would be 3–4 interns allocated. More interns could be allocated to each group if necessary.

Action-planning workshop output: The mentorship programme involved pairing one MO with three-to-four interns. Mentors would be selected on a voluntary basis and the mentor group would meet once every two weeks. The group provided a point of contact for interns to ask academic questions, speak about issues that arise at work, and to have someone who could provide them with social support after exposure to an adverse event. Table 3 outlines the details.

Approaches to addressing potential barriers included the following: if MOs were not providing adequate mentorship, interns could speak to the allocated specialist to their mentor group; if an MO were to be absent, the allocated specialist could fill in as a mentor; and with each intern rotation, new interns would be allocated to mentors by the SMO.

Dialogue session process: The mentor training session for MOs was further developed through dialogue sessions between T.Y.B., a research team member, and a psychologist working at the hospital complex.

Dialogue session output: The mentor training session outline included what it means to be a mentor, roles and responsibilities, the purpose of the mentoring programme, and how to deliver criticism constructively. It also detailed a protocol for mentors to follow with teaching mentees how to manage expectations, cope with stress, handle criticism, progress with their academic knowledge, learn necessary skills, and learn about the daily ongoing processes in the department.

Phase 4: Enactment of the intervention implementation

Process of intervention implementation: The training session for MOs was conducted online and was jointly presented by T.Y.B. and the research team member involved in the session development. Ten MOs, the selected mentors, attended. After the session, a complete summary was distributed to all departmental MOs to ensure they had access to the information. The following day, T.Y.B. and the research team member presented the mentor programme to the department to clarify the purpose, structure, expectations and responsibilities of different stakeholders in the programme.

The mentor programme ran for ten days before the intern group changed to other departments. These ten days were used as a pilot to identify initial challenges with implementation. The possibility to implement it again with a new group of interns 10 days later provided an opportunity to strengthen implementation and gain further insight into other challenges that might arise. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with two MOs about their experiences of the programme, suggestions for improvement, and ideas on how to address the problem of beginning the programme with the start of a new group of interns. An informal dialogue session was conducted with the HoD where the same topics were discussed.

Shortly after the new mentor groups were formed for the next intern rotation, a research team member, together with the primary researcher, presented the mentor programme to the interns at a handover meeting. Additionally, the primary researcher met with the Chief Medical Superintendent of the hospital complex to discuss the preliminary research findings, the intervention developed, and recommendations based on these.

Mid-cycle evaluation output: Due to the limited intervention running period for the first group of interns, the MOs stated that they could not provide extensive insight into experiences of the programme yet. However, they confirmed that most of the implementation practicalities were working, and provided valuable insight into troubleshooting the first implementation cycle. Based on these insights, responsibility was transferred from the SMO to the intern coordinator (an MO) for kickstarting the programme with each new group of interns as the SMO already had a heavy task load; see Table 3.

The Chief Medical Superintendent agreed that it was necessary to address burnout in the department, and supported the idea of a mentorship programme. They anticipated a risk of a high time-investment required by the mentorship programme negatively affecting the intervention’s sustainability.

Phase 5: Evaluation of the intervention

Process: The second implementation cycle ran for approximately four weeks before we conducted semi-structured key-informant interviews.

Output (results)

Perceived benefits: Interns reported that the programme provided good academic support if the mentors were active. Some mentors helped interns complete procedures required to pass the rotation. Generally, the academic support offered clarity on procedures and general orientation in the department. The programme made the internship less stressful as there was not only academic support, but also “somebody to lean on”. Interns compared their current experience to that in their first rotation in the department the previous year and said one of the biggest benefits was that they now knew who to turn to if they had questions or needed advice:It’s really helpful. […] I started my internship last year and my first rotation was obs and gynae. […] We didn’t have any mentorship […] and we struggled. […] My very first day I was already on call, I didn’t know what to expect, I didn’t know who to contact, I didn’t know anybody, but then these guys that joined now with the mentorship programme, it’s really helping (Intern1).

Another benefit of the programme was the strengthening of relationships within the group and between mentors and mentees. The programme allowed participants in the groups to interact with each other outside of the official working hours and environment. It removed social barriers, which reportedly built a sense of trust, rapport, and guidance. Some relationships developed in the programme between interns and their mentors reportedly continued after the rotation changed, showcasing the potential of the intervention to improve trust and social support after participants have left the department.

Perceived barriers: Barriers to the intervention’s success included poor existing relationships between mentees, leading to unwillingness to work together in a mentee group; mentees not seeking contact with mentors, and mentors’ maintenance of hierarchical distance from mentees. Some mentors had not reached out to their groups, which participants attributed to several factors: some did not seem interested in the programme and in teaching; mentees had different schedules, making it difficult to coordinate meeting times; and there was no sense of accountability if group members did not engage in the programme. An intern described their perception of reasons for interns’ resistance to engaging in the programme: “The meet ups, they’re not being treated as an opportunity to study, as an opportunity to learn. It seems almost like an additional task […] that no one has really been putting effort into and […] it feels like there’s no consequence if you don’t do it.” (Intern 2).

Perceived facilitators: The biggest facilitator was a mentor’s initiative and enthusiasm about the programme, alongside their leadership style. Some mentors drove the relationship and frequently contacted their mentees to discuss academic content and to build personal relationships, investing the limited time they had available. They provided guidance on department orientation and knowledge application. An intern described a shift in the mentors’ attitudes throughout the programme as moving from hesitancy to enthusiasm:I feel like in the beginning, the first few days […] they felt it was a burden, but after they got into it - when you start something and it feels like a lot, but when you get your footing - it seems as if they were really, really happy to help. Genuinely, they were excited, they were involved, they would be asking like, “why didn’t you ask me? I was here, you should ask if you don’t understand.”(Intern1)

Other facilitators included mentees’ interest in the programme and being receptive to seeking mentorship. For example, some interns found it helpful to have a specific person who could provide guidance on expectations from the department and wider hospital, and translate academic knowledge to skills. An intern described that their experience of case management was improved by having a mentor as it assisted them in navigating and clarifying different expectations.

Conditions (reflections) for sustainability: Participants’ ideas were to integrate the mentorship programme into the medical education curriculum and internship training as a means to institutionalise it, believing that the programme would be a beneficial addition: “… it will go a long way in improving the quality of doctors.” (Specialist). Institutionalising it would ensure continuity of the programme, independent of staff turnover among MOs and specialists. Efforts to raise awareness of the benefits of a medical mentorship programme through academic publications and presentations to hospital management were encouraged to build broader support and institutional buy-in.

Additional reflections on sustainability included ensuring flexibility in scheduling of mentor group meet-ups. Some groups decided to meet at the weekends to account for scheduling issues, which reportedly successfully improved meeting attendance. Other ideas were to assign two MOs to each group in case one was temporarily unavailable or if mentees felt uncomfortable approaching one of their mentors; to have one-on-one meetings instead of group meetings to counteract scheduling issues; and to provide incentives for participation such as prizes for the most improved member of each group.

Discussion

This study addresses a research gap by co-creating and piloting a contextually relevant intervention to prevent and mitigate burnout in a public healthcare setting in Namibia. Using an iterative action-research approach, a diverse group of doctors, ranging from interns to the department head, collaborated in the design and implementation of a structured mentorship programme. The co-creation process was coordinated and facilitated by the primary researcher, supporting shared decision-making and maintaining engagement across hierarchical levels. The collaborative process built ownership, grounded the intervention in everyday clinical realities, and increased its relevance and feasibility. This discussion reflects on the outcomes of the intervention, the factors that contributed to its implementation, and potential implications for similar contexts.

This study demonstrated that a co-created mentorship intervention can effectively address key drivers of burnout in a high-pressure maternity care setting. Despite running for a limited time and without formal post-intervention burnout assessment, the programme led to meaningful shifts in participants’ workplace experiences. Doctors reported feeling more supported during the intervention, an important change given earlier reports linking lack of support to emotional exhaustion, poor mental health, and reduced quality of care [11, 56–58]. The mentorship programme created protected time for reflection, strengthened professional and personal connections across hierarchy levels, and provided junior staff with trusted points of contact, contributing to a more supportive work environment.

Notably, the intervention helped disrupt entrenched patterns of blame culture and a lack of positive social relationships - two central contributors to burnout identified in this setting [11]. Blame culture is often deeply entrenched in healthcare systems and is challenging to address, particularly in hierarchical systems where staff fear repercussions for raising concerns [59–61]. Through co-created processes and regular mentoring interactions, the intervention created space for open dialogue and mutual trust. By the end of the study, participants reported improved collegial relationships, increased trust, and a stronger sense of psychological safety, sharply contrasting with earlier accounts of mistrust and isolation. These outcomes are significant given the well-documented links between workplace isolation, distress, and poor mental health [62–66]. Strengthened relationships and perceived support, both cultivated by the intervention, are recognised protective factors against burnout and may contribute to greater individual resilience [58, 67].

In addition to enhancing social connections, the intervention contributed to interns’ professional development. While this study did not directly measure training outcomes, participants noted increased self-confidence and support in meeting rotation requirements - pointing toward professional development and improved confidence in clinical tasks. Literature associates mentoring with improved training, higher confidence, fewer errors, and better job satisfaction - all factors linked to reduced burnout risk [68–70]. Improved training and supervision may also lead to improved patient outcomes, such as reduced surgical complications and missed diagnoses [71, 72], indicating the intervention’s broader implications for healthcare delivery.

Several factors contributed to the sustained engagement observed throughout the study and to the perceived relevance of the intervention. These outcomes were likely influenced by a combination of structural and relational enablers that supported ongoing participation and ensured the process remained meaningful to those involved. Structurally, the intervention was supported by a deliberately inclusive process that aimed to navigate traditional hierarchies and cultivate shared ownership. With senior staff traditionally being the decision-makers in the department, we anticipated some complexity in including shared decision-making in the study. Hierarchy is ingrained in healthcare culture and is difficult to change [73]. Embedding shared leadership principles and engagement guidelines into the structure and code of conduct enabled wider engagement throughout the study - these important facilitators to co-creation are echoed across literature [74–76]. Including key figures throughout the process, like the HoD, SMOs and intern team leaders, was critical for legitimacy, support, and encouraging engagement in the study [77, 78].

In parallel, relational dynamics and intrinsic motivation also played a central role in sustaining participation in the co-creation process and reinforcing the intervention’s relevance. The co-creation approach built a safe and respectful environment for sharing experiences and perspectives, which contributed to creating a shared vision and strong interpersonal engagement - elements recognised in literature as essential to effective co-creation [76, 78]. A clearly defined yet flexible structure further supported collaboration by reducing uncertainty around roles and expectations [76]. Our preceding study helped align the intervention with identification of local challenges, pointing out the urgent need to address burnout. This all contributed to highly motivated participants and ensuring a deep knowledge of the context [78]. Interns viewed the intervention as both a learning opportunity and a source of positive social interaction, and many expressed a shared sense of social purpose, reflecting common motivators for engagement in co-creation processes [79]. These factors - psychological safety, social purpose, and peer connection - help explain the consistently high level of engagement, even in the absence of funding.

Despite these strengths, the co-creation process was not without its challenges. The significant time and effort required for co-creation should be carefully considered in planning future research. These demands have been cited as challenges in literature, with some co-creation processes spanning years [80]. In this study, meetings often occurred outside of working hours, and additional time was required from intervention participants who already had long working days. This posed a barrier to adoption for some and may affect sustainability. Due to time limitations, the study could not explore how the intervention might be integrated long term. Future research should examine how such initiatives could be sustained over time, such as through embedding and institutionalising a mentorship programme into medical curricula and training [81, 82].

While the intervention showed promise, acknowledging its limitations is essential to strengthening future efforts and aligning with the iterative, adaptive nature of co-creation. Challenges encountered during the process should not be viewed as failures, but as opportunities for reflection and redesign, consistent with the ILA approach [55]. Co-creation is not a universal solution to complex problems, but rather a process that demands flexibility and continuous engagement with diverse perspectives. Recognising and responding to emerging barriers is central to ensuring interventions remain relevant, responsive, and effective over time.

Strengths and limitations

Limitations include a limited project timeline and lack of funding, resulting in insufficient time for further iterations of implementation and subsequent adaptations. A longer time-frame could have facilitated better integration of the intervention into departmental processes and allowed for more actions to address encountered barriers effectively. The absence of long-term follow-up limits the study’s ability to assess sustained impacts over time, such as the impact on quality of provided care or on maternal and perinatal outcomes. Limited evaluative interviews and an absence of evaluation among mentors posed a further limitation to the study. Including mentors’ perspectives and experiences could have provided valuable insights into the intervention functioning. No quantitative measure of effectiveness was collected either, which could have strengthened qualitative evaluations. Nonetheless, the presence of any evaluation is acknowledged as a strength, given the lack of research on similar studies that include evaluations. Furthermore, we were informed by the research team that the mentorship programme was still running at the time of submission of this paper (April 2025), indicating a successful implementation in the department.

Strengths of this approach notably include the process being entirely participant-led. Through this, the research team effectively tailored the co-creation process to local needs, benefitting from reflection on lived experiences leading to the pre-identification of potential barriers and facilitators within the study context. The inclusion of diverse stakeholders in the research team ensured that the co-creation process represented a wide range of perspectives, particularly regarding hierarchical occupation levels. Additionally, regular consultations with the HoD ensured support from senior decision-makers in the department, enhancing the study’s success in terms of acceptability and adoption.

Conclusion

This study explored the role of co-creation in developing a contextually-grounded response to the complex challenge of HCW burnout in SSA, through a case study in a large Namibian public hospital. The findings demonstrate that it is feasible to co-create and implement a relevant, locally driven intervention with promising potential to prevent burnout and improve mental wellbeing, even in the absence of external funding. While systemic initiatives to address burnout are crucial, such as policy changes to increase departmental resources, this study demonstrates how co-creation can generate tailored, locally driven solutions within a specific healthcare context. By leveraging local knowledge and experience, co-researchers collaboratively devised nuanced solutions that responded to the realities and needs across hierarchical staff levels and created ownership of the intervention. The intervention contributed to both individual resilience and improved working conditions, demonstrating the creation of a multifaceted solution to a complex problem. Not only so, but co-researchers also proactively integrated accommodations to contextual constraints and enablers into its design and implementation, such as the timing of meetings and identification of key actors for implementation. Exploring the possibility of locally- and user-led research agendas offers encouraging avenues for the development of effective interventions and equitable research partnerships in similar contexts elsewhere. Ultimately, embracing co-creation in intervention development facilitates inclusive decision-making processes and cultivates ecosystems conducive to meaningful change.
