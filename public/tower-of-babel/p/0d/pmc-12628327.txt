Cellulose–Silver and Cellulose–Gold Bioactive Nanocomposites Obtained Using SCOBY Purified Membranes
Authors: Violeta Dediu, Mariana Buşilă, Claudia Ungureanu, Leontina Grigore-Gurgu, Mihaela Cotârleṭ, Cosmin Romanitan, Vasilica Tucureanu, Oana Brincoveanu, Bogdan Stefan Vasile, Gabriela Elena Bahrim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The green synthesis of bioactive nanomaterials is becoming more attractive in various fields like biotechnology, pharmaceuticals, cosmeceuticals, etc. In this study, bacterial cellulose–silver and bacterial cellulose–gold bionanocomposites were obtained through an environmentally friendly and low-cost method without using additional reducing agents. In the first step, the bacterial cellulose, a byproduct from kombucha production using a symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast (SCOBY), was purified using an alkaline solution. In the second step, the purified bacterial cellulose (SBC) was used to obtain silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) and gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) on the bacterial cellulose matrix, starting only from metal precursors in different media: water, black tea, and kombucha. Gold and silver nanoparticles were obtained on cellulose fibrils in all media, even in water, indicating the reducing role of cellulose. The morphology and structural features of the noble metal nanoparticles/bacterial cellulose nanocomposites (AgNPs/SBC and AuNPs/SBC) were investigated. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images show nanoparticles with an irregular shape with dimensions ranging from a few nanometers up to 70 nm, depending on the synthesis medium. TEM analysis revealed mostly quasi-spherical nanoparticles distributed along the surface of the cellulose fibers or within the interfibrillar pores. All nanoparticles are well crystallized and generally formed from more than two nanocrystallites, except AuNPs obtained in kombucha which are monocrystalline. XRD analysis shows the characteristic diffractograms of Iβ cellulose allomorphs and confirms the formation of crystalline AgNPs and AuNPs. The antioxidant capacity tests determined that the best activity was registered for the AgNPs/SBC composites obtained in kombucha and fresh black tea. The antimicrobial potential was evaluated against the Gram-negative bacteria Escherichia coli and the Gram-positive bacteria Staphylococcus aureus and Listeria monocytogenes. Cellulose–silver and cellulose–gold nanocomposites showed increased antimicrobial activity compared with raw SBC, especially in the case of kombucha medium for green synthesis. The highest antioxidant activity, determined by DPPH and ABTS assays, was obtained for AgNPs/SBC produced in kombucha and fresh black tea. Based on the results, cellulose–silver and cellulose–gold nanocomposites could be considered as bioactive materials for multiple practical applications, such as the medical field and food packaging.

Introduction

The increased demand for eco-friendly and sustainable cellulose-based materials leads to more assiduous research in the development of new sustainable alternatives than synthesis from biomass. The need for cellulose is increasing, while the production cannot keep up with the demand. The production of cellulose from the biosynthesis of various microorganism metabolism may provide a viable alternative to the classic production process, being able to be applied on a large scale at a low-cost level. Some specialized bacteria build an extracellular cellulose matrix as a shield against desiccation and UV radiation. Cellulose-producing bacteria belong to the genera Acetobacter, Gluconobacter, Gluconacetobacter, and Komagateibacter, which are able to transform sugar or alcohols to acetic acid in an aerobic environment. These bacteria produce cellulose in the interior of bacterial cells through an enzymatic process, and then it is spun out through some openings like nozzles. The resulting polymer is formed of linear (1 → 4) β-glycosidic-linked glucose units, where every unit is corkscrewed 180° with respect to the next one, forming the basic structures called fibrils with cross-contributional dimensions in the nm range. In a cellulose linear chain, one end glucose group is a reducing hemiacetal unit and another one has nonreducing functionality (a pendant hydroxyl group). These nanofibrils can form highly ordered (crystalline) and disordered (amorphous-like) regions. This variation in crystalline arrangements is related to the intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bonds between free hydroxyl groups present in the cellulose macromolecules and van der Waals forces established between the elementary nanofibrils, leading to their aggregation in microfibrils with lengths of up to several micrometers, their size being 100 times smaller than plant cellulose fibers. These microfibrils can form a three-dimensional network structure that can allow the passage of gases or can be used as a support for loading various substances that have a functional role.

Cellulose has unique characteristics in terms of its chemical stability, molecular structure, and mechanical strength. Bacterial cellulose has some advantages compared to plant cellulose: high purity (no impurities of hemicelluloses and lignin), a higher degree of polymerization, higher crystallinity (60–90%), and better mechanical strength. The bacterial cellulose has a nanofiber architecture with crystalline microfibrils 100 times smaller than in plant cellulose. This fibrous biomaterial offers advantages like low cost, large specific surface area, high strength, softness, breathability, moisture-wicking properties, thermal stability, good biodegradability, and biocompatibility, which make it suitable for applications like wound dressing, drug delivery, biosensors, food and additives, tissue engineering, etc.

Among the many types of bacterial cellulose, the one resulting from kombucha beverage preparation, using symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast (SCOBY) membranes as an artisanal starter culture, has gained increasing attention. During fermentation of the medium based on tea infusion and sugar, the acetic bacteria from multiple consortia of SCOBY start to produce cellulose as a floating membrane that encapsulated the microbial cells (lactic acid bacteria, acetic acid bacteria, and yeasts). The bacterial cellulose yield is influenced by intrinsic factors such as substrate composition, inoculum concentration and quality, fermentation parameters, or extrinsic factors such as environmental factors. Regarding the microorganism’s variability in SCOBY microbiota, the predominant are acetic acid bacteria (Gluconobacter spp., Acetobacter spp.), lactic acid bacteria, and the most frequently isolated yeast species are Zygosaccharomyces spp., Saccharomyces spp., Saccharomycodes spp., and Schizosaccharomyces spp. The microbial cells contained in the nascent gel-like pellicle can be inactivated by immersion in a dilute alkaline solution.

SCOBY cellulose membrane resulting from the self-assembling of secreted fibrils into nanostructured biomaterial has been considered as the strongest synthesized biopolymer, with high specific surface area, low density, biocompatibility, and hydrophilicity. The large number of active hydroxyl groups on the nanocellulose chains are suitable for chemical modification, which significantly expands the range of applications for SBC. These −OH can provide active sites for metal ion adsorption and in situ synthesis of different well-dispersed nanoparticles in nanocellulose matrix. SCOBY nanocellulose has significant potential to be used as a multifunctional food ingredient, environmental biotechnology, packing material, substitute for cotton, etc. SCOBY membranes still have application limitations (e.g., low processability), and research needs to be done to counteract the tendency of this material to absorb water and deform. The very porous structure and permeability to liquids make SBC a very competitive biomaterial for chronic wounds healing because it maintains a proper moisture level and offers solid protection from microbial contamination and other external factors throughout the healing treatment.

Although SBC does not intrinsically possess antimicrobial properties, it can be used as a loading medium for nano-antimicrobial agents because of its large surface area and great mechanical properties. The combination of nano-antibacterial agents and bacterial nanocellulose to directly inhibit bacterial growth has been a research hotspot. In 2008, Maneerung et al. obtained silver nanoparticles impregnated into bacterial cellulose obtaining an antimicrobial wound dressing material. Other researchers have proposed different combinations of bacterial nanocellulose and silver nanoparticles, − and in most of them, premade silver nanoparticles were used or external reducing agents were added to the reaction medium. Comparatively, few papers reported the incorporation of gold nanoparticles in the SCOBY nanocellulose matrix. −

In this study, a simple eco-friendly method for cellulose–silver and cellulose–gold bioactive nanocomposites synthesis was performed by using purified nanocellulose from SCOBY pellicle resulting from kombucha production, where the nanocellulose acts as the reducing agent and stabilizer for the noble metal nanoparticles formation. The syntheses took place in different media: water, black tea, and kombucha, to investigate the role of nanocellulose in the synthesis of silver and gold nanoparticles as well as the influence of the compounds present in these beverages on the morphology, structure, and bioactive properties of the resulting composites. The antioxidant and antimicrobial properties of the obtained silver- and gold-nanoparticle-loaded bacterial nanocellulose were investigated to target the new and challenging applications based on their functional characteristics.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Microorganisms

Analytical chemical reagents, AgNO3, HAuCl4·3H2O, NaOH, ABTS- 2,20 azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt, and DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Louis, MO). Brain Heart Infusion Broth medium (BHI) was purchased from Millipore, Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt; Luria–Bertani Broth medium from Sigma, Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany; and Muller-Hinton agar media from Scharlau, Scharlab S. L., Gato Perez, Spain. All chemicals were used directly without any further purification. Black tea leaves (Azekay Buket brand) were purchased from a local store.

The fresh SBCs, purchased from a private household from the Republic of Moldova, were multiplied via successive cultivation in a black tea infusion (0.5%, w/v) and sugar 5% (w/v)-based medium. The strains Listeria monocytogenes Scott A, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, and Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, preserved in the Microbial Cultures Collection of the “Dunărea de Jos” University of Galati, Romania (affiliated at MIRRI, www.mirri.org), were used as indicator microorganisms for the antibacterial activity assay. The bacterial stock cultures were stored at −80 °C.

SCOBY Nanocellulose Preparation (SBC)

The production of bacterial cellulose was carried out by the fermentation of black tea using native SCOBY membranes. The growth medium based on 5 g of black dry tea leaves which were infused for 10 min into 1 L of boiling water was then filtered into sterilized glass bottles, and 50 g of sugar was added. When the temperature decreased to 20 °C, 3% of fresh SBCs was inoculated into the prepared fermentation medium, and the mouth of the glass jars was converted with a woven cotton cloth to protect from contamination as well as allow the small amount of oxygen to pass through for the growth of SBCs. The mixture was incubated for 14 days at room temperature in the dark. During fermentation, a new SBC was formed at the air–water interface. At the final stage of fermentation, the newly formed pellicle is carefully removed and kept in a small volume of fresh fermentation medium at low temperatures (4–10 °C) for future use. The SBCs collected were washed several times with distilled water, boiled in distilled water at 90 °C for 2 h, and then bleached with 2 wt % NaOH solution in a 90 °C water bath for 15 min to remove impurities (melanoidins, proteins, saccharides, amino acids, etc.). The resulting purified samples were freeze-dried at 10 mbar and −42 °C, for 48 h, using a freeze-dryer α 1–4 LD (Martin Christ, GmbH, Osterode am Harz, Germany). The dried SBC was ground to obtain a fine powder called and used in the next step: the synthesis of gold and silver nanoparticles.

Synthesis AgNPs/SBC and AuNPs/SBC Nanocomposites

For the synthesis of AgNPs, 40 mg of purified SBC powder was added in 10 mL of tap water (samples coded SW), fresh sweet black tea (samples coded ST), and kombucha beverage (samples coded SK), respectively, in which 5 mM AgNO3 was dissolved. In the case of AuNPs synthesis, the same protocol was used using 5 mM HAuCl4·3H2O instead. Simultaneously, a control setup was established with only purified nanocellulose powder under similar conditions. All samples were maintained in an orbital shaker at 120 rpm, at room temperature, in the dark conditions, for 24 h. The color change of the liquid samples was noted by visual evaluation confirming the nanoparticles synthesis. Characterization Methods

The morphology of the obtained materials was observed using a Quanta Inspect F (FEI Company) scanning electron microscope (SEM), and their chemical composition was analyzed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). All of the samples were analyzed before and after being sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold. Bright-field (BFTEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images were acquired using an aberration-corrected 80–200 kV Titan Themis transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific), operating at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. Sample preparation involved placing a small amount of the dried material into a 100-mesh, shell-like copper grid. The analysis was performed on the as-prepared dried samples without any additional treatment before imaging. TEM images were collected at the edges of the nanocomposite in regions thin enough to ensure electron transparency. Additionally, crystallographic information was acquired through the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) module, which is integrated into the TEM system. X-ray diffraction analysis was performed using a 9 kW Rigaku-SmartLab diffractometer (Rigaku Corporation, Osaka, Japan) that provides a monochromatic X-ray beam with λCu Kα1 = 0.154 nm. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) investigations were performed in θ/2θ mode with a speed of 5°/min. The infrared spectral analysis of the obtained cellulose materials was performed through FTIR Spectroscopy using a Bruker Tensor 27 FTIR spectrometer (Bruker Optics, Karlsruhe, Germany), in Attenuated Total Reflectance (Platinum ATR) mode in the wavelength range between 4000 and 400 cm–1, the number of scans was 64, and the resolution was 4 cm–1. All obtained results were assessed with OPUS software from Bruker (Bruker Optics, Karlsruhe, Germany).

Antioxidant Activity

The antioxidant activity of the nanoparticles was quantified by measuring the concentration of unreacted ABTS•+ (2,2′-azino-bis­(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonate)) or DPPH• (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl free radical) in the system using spectrophotometric measurements to monitor changes in both radicals. First, the samples were extracted using ultrapure water at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v) for 15 min in an ultrasonic water bath at 40 °C. Then, the samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for further analysis. In ABTS radical technique, equal amounts of 7 mM ABTS stock solution were mixed with 2.45 mM potassium persulfate (K2S2O8), and the resulting mixture was kept in the dark for 16 h before being used. To obtain an absorbance of 0.70 at 734 nm, 1 mL aliquots of the ABTS+ solution were further diluted with 35 mL of 96% ethanol 96%. The absorbance at 734 nm was measured after volumes of 1.980 mL of the ABTS+ solution and 0.20 mL of the extract were allowed to react for 7 min in the dark. In the DPPH radical scavenging assay, 3.9 mL of DPPH (4 mg of DPPH diluted in 100 mL methanol) was mixed with 0.1 mL of sample, homogenized, and then placed in the dark for 30 min. The color of DPPH is deep purple and shows a strong absorption band in the wavelength range of 515–520 nm. Absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 515 nm. Methanol was used as a control sample. The concentration of the unreacted ABTS+ and DPPH• after reaction with the antioxidant molecules was estimated using eq 1 I(%)=Ac−AsAc×100 where A c is the absorbance for the control sample and A s is the absorbance for the test sample.

Antibacterial Activity

The in vitro antibacterial activity of the obtained materials was assessed by agar well diffusion method, against the bacterial strains L. monocytogenes Scott A, S. aureus ATCC 25923, and E. coli ATCC 25922, respectively. Briefly, to obtain the overnight cultures, L. monocytogenes and S. aureus were grown for 18 h at 37 °C in Brain Heart Infusion Broth medium, and E. coli in Luria–Bertani Broth medium, respectively. An amount of 100 μL from each sample (50 mg/mL) was placed into the agar wells (8 mm in diameter) created in the double layers of the Muller-Hinton agar. The second layer was inoculated with each bacterial strain at the final concentration of 107 CFU/plate. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the inhibition zones’ diameter observed around the wells was measured.

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) were assessed. The MIC against the indicator strains was determined by the microdilution method in the Muller-Hinton broth media. Precisely, in each well of a 96-well plate, 100 μL of bacterial culture, 100 μL of the sample with concentrations ranging from 25 to 0.048 mg/mL, and 10 μL of 1.25 mg/mL resazurin were homogenized. The MIC test was assessed at a final bacteria concentration of ∼5 × 105 CFU/mL. The positive control was considered erythromycin (10 mg/mL) for S. aureus and L. monocytogenes, and ampicillin (10 mg/mL) for E. coli, respectively. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of the sample that inhibited the bacterial growth, as evidenced by the blue color obtained in the well. The MBC, the lowest concentration of the nanoparticle sample that totally inhibited the indicator bacteria growth, was determined according to Li et al. Briefly, an amount of 100 μL of sample mixture from every well was taken, diluted 1/10 in BHI media, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Further, 10 μL were aliquoted, spotted on the BHI agar plates, and incubated under the same conditions at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the agar plates were analyzed for the presence or absence of bacterial biomass around the spots.

Statistical Analysis

Experimental data were obtained from triplicate measurements. The data was analyzed using the statistical software OriginPro 8 (OriginLab Corporation). Size distribution analysis of nanofibers and nanoparticles in the samples was obtained from SEM images by measuring around 200 nanofibers and nanoparticles using ImageJ software. The histograms were best fitted with the Gauss function. Statistical analysis was performed on the diameters of the inhibition zones (mm), reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent replicates for each strain × sample combination (L. monocytogenes, S. aureus, E. coli × Control, ST1, SK1, SK2). Overall differences were assessed by two-factor ANOVA (Strain and Sample), and when the interaction was significant, one-factor ANOVAs were performed within each strain. Homogeneity of variances was checked using Levene’s test. For point comparisons (especially with the Control) and/or when homogeneity was doubtful, the Welch t test, two-sided, with Holm correction (Holm–Bonferroni) was used to control for type I error in multiple comparisons. In the figures, asterisks indicate significance vs Control: p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***); “ns” = not significant.

Results and Discussion

Surface Morphology and Chemical Structures

In Figure , the production of cellulose nanofibers in SCOBY membranes can be observed. The protofibrils of the glucan chains are produced by bacteria and excreted through the pores present in the cell wall (Figure a). The SEM images of the obtained samples emphasized a 3D cellulose fiber network developed through the fermentation process, with random arrangements of the fibers having different diameters. The nanofibers come together in different areas to form larger fibers (ribbons) that are included in crystalline domains. The appearance of the cellulose network changes very little between these samples, and the fibers’ density decreases from the SWO sample to the SK0 sample. Additionally, in SEM images of SW0, ST0, and SK0 samples, some small particles can be seen, indicating the presence of melanoidins, probably due to an incomplete purification through this alkaline method. Further, SEM analysis proved the successful synthesis of AgNPs and AuNPs in the bacterial nanocellulose network (Figure d–j). In these syntheses, the hydroxyl groups (−OH) in cellulose donate electrons to metal ions, reducing them to their metallic form, which aggregates to form nanoparticles embedded within the cellulose matrix. The proposed overall reactions can be written as 2 cellulose−OH+Ag++e−→cellulose=O+Ag0+H+ 3 cellulose−OH+Au3++3e−→cellulose=O+Au0+3H+

Nanoparticles formation occurred in all tested media (tap water, black tea, and kombucha) in the presence of nanocellulose derived from SCOBY, indicating its role as a reducing agent. Upon addition of Ag and Au precursors to the synthesis media, an immediate color change was observed (Figure S1), suggesting also chemical interactions facilitated by the rich content of polyphenols and antioxidants in tea and kombucha. The obtained AuNPs and AgNPs were polymorphic shapes, mostly of quasi-spherical morphology on the surface of the fibers or in the pores formed between fibers. Some of the nanoparticles are assembled in agglomerates with sizes of up to 1.5 μm. The elemental composition of cellulose loaded with silver or gold included carbon, oxygen, silver or gold, and small amounts of nitrogen, probably due to the presence of melanoidins. In composite samples, the silver atom content ranges from 3.4 to 8.0%, while the gold content ranges from 1.8 to 4.4%, as can be seen from the data plotted in Figure b.

TEM micrographs (Figures a–c and a–c) revealed the fibrous morphology of the nanocellulose, highlighting the individual fibrils and their networked arrangement. Additionally, the gold–silver nanoparticles were distinctly visualized as dark, well-defined spots distributed along or within the nanocellulose matrix. TEM analysis further confirmed the successful incorporation of AgNPs and AuNPs within the bacterial nanocellulose network, supporting the morphological observations obtained from the SEM analyses. Consistent with the SEM results, HRTEM images (Figures d–f and d–f) showed that the nanoparticles exhibited a polymorphic nature, predominantly adopting quasi-spherical shapes. These particles were distributed both along the surface of the cellulose fibers and within the interfibrillar pores. Also, the histograms (Figures g–i and g–i) display a monomodal size distribution of the nanoparticles.

TEM data highlight the important influence of the synthesis medium on nanoparticle size and uniformity. Samples synthesized using kombucha generally exhibited larger nanoparticles (Figures c and c), which may be attributed to the medium’s rich organic content and biochemical complexity. In contrast, black tea and tap water facilitated the formation of smaller and more uniform nanoparticles (Figures a,b and a,b), particularly for AuNPs. Furthermore, AgNPs tended to be larger than AuNPs under similar synthesis conditions, with the notable exception of the tea-based samples, where AgNPs displayed smaller average sizes.

The crystalline nature of the synthesized AgNPs and AuNPs was confirmed by SAED patterns (Figure a,b), which exhibited diffraction patterns characteristic of polycrystalline materials, with concentric rings. The observed diffraction rings were indexed to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) Miller indices orientations of the face-centered cubic (FCC) crystal structure of both silver and gold. Further investigation at the single-particle level revealed well-defined lattice fringes, indicating high crystallinity. As shown in Figure d–f, two sets of lattice spacings were resolved, measuring 0.24 and 0.20 nm, corresponding to the (111) and (200) crystallographic planes of Ag, respectively. Similarly, HRTEM images of AuNPs embedded in the SBC matrix (Figure d–f) displayed fringe spacings of 0.24 and 0.20 nm, consistent with the (111) and (200) planes of FCC gold. These TEM-based crystallographic observations are in good agreement with the XRD analysis, further confirming the polycrystalline FCC structure of the synthesized nanoparticles. Examining the HRTEM images, it is evident that the nanoparticles are polycrystalline.

It can be observed that different synthesis procedures led to different mean crystallite sizes, the crystallite size is larger for the AgNPs/SBC than AuNPs/SBC, indicating a more effective growth of gold crystals. The crystallite size varies from ∼2.1 nm for SK1 to ∼40 nm for the SK2 sample. Comparing the mean size of the nanoparticles with the crystallite value, it is observed that the crystallite is generally much smaller than the size of the particles, suggesting particles of a polycrystalline nature. Only for the gold nanoparticles obtained in kombucha fermented medium is a small difference registered between the crystallite and the average size of the nanoparticles, indicating a good crystallization process.

To evaluate the crystallinity of cellulose before and after the Au and Ag nanoparticle synthesis, the pseudo-Voigt analysis against the experimental data was performed. Figure d shows a careful investigation of the cellulose main diffraction peaks for samples coded SK0, SK1, and SK2. The degree of crystallinity (Cr.I) was evaluated using the empirical equation proposed by Segal et al. through the following equation 4 Cr.I=I200−IamI200×100 where I 200 represents the peak intensity corresponding to the crystalline domain and I am to the minimum intensity between (110) and (200) located at ∼18°. Accordingly, a degree of crystallinity of 64% was obtained before nanoparticles immobilization. After that, the crystallinity of cellulose decreased to ∼50%. At the same time, the mean crystallite size of the cellulose decreased from ∼4.2 to ∼3.7 nm. These results suggest that the crystal quality of the cellulose was slightly altered during the synthesis of noble metal nanoparticles.

The molecular structure of SBCs and their corresponding composites was analyzed through FTIR spectroscopy. The obtained spectra are presented in Figure , while the vibrational assignments for these spectra are summarized in Table . As can be seen, small differences in the intensity of the spectral bands are registered between the unmodified cellulose and the cellulose loaded with gold or silver nanoparticles, probably as a result of the modification of the hydrogen and glycosidic bonds during the nanoparticle synthesis process, which translates into the densification of the cellulosic matrix around the metal nanoparticles. The broad bands centered at around 3300 cm–1 can be associated with the stretching vibration of the hydroxyl groups from both primary and secondary alcohols in cellulose, and this band includes inter- and intramolecular hydrogen bond vibrations in the polymer chains bonds that bind the cellulose fibers together keeping them compact and structured. The bands from the fingerprint region of the spectra, appearing in the spectral range of 1500–800 cm–1, confirm the coexistence of amorphous and crystalline domains in the obtained nanocellulose materials. The presence of C–H bonds from functional groups associated with cellulose is also confirmed by the appearance of bands centered at around 2890 and 1314 cm–1. The crystalline fraction of the obtained cellulosic materials was estimated through the ratio between the absolute intensity of the band centered at ∼1427 cm–1 (crystalline phase) and the band intensity at ∼897 cm–1 (amorphous phase) and shows values between 0.80 and 1.29 (Table ). This ratio varies between the samples and depends on the synthesis medium; the highest crystallinity being registered for the samples where tea was used compared to tap water and kombucha. However, the formation of Ag and Au nanoparticles led to small changes in the arrangement of the nanofibers when tap water and kombucha beverages were used. In tea, the highest crystallinity is shown by sample ST2 (1.29) and decreases to 1.14 for sample ST1. These results suggest some new interactions between the synthesis medium and the cellulose structure and changes in the ordered arrangement of cellulose fibers after the synthesis of noble metal nanoparticles.

The bands in the regions 1300–900 cm–1 can be associated with the elongation of the C–O–C, C–OH, and C–C ring, while the peak centered at around 1100 cm–1 can be due to the vibration mode of the C–O bonds involved in polyhydroxyl groups from glucose. The C–O–C bonds, whose presence is associated with the peaks at about 1055 cm–1, are of a glycosidic nature involved in the polymeric binding of glucose units to form cellulose. The connection of the OH group to these bonds is also supported by the existence of a band at about 1335 cm–1. The peak at 993 cm–1 could be associated with a ring stretching mode.

The appearance in the spectra of the peaks from around 3200 cm–1 indicates the possibility of the overlap of some characteristic bands of hydroxyl groups with bands specific to bonds in amines. The existence of protein groups can also be associated with the appearance of bands centered at 1645 cm–1 (amide I) due to the vibration mode of the CO bond and 1542 cm–1 (amide II) associated with the coupling between CN and NH bonds. These functional groups are related to the presence of colored compounds with high molecular weight in the nanocellulose derived from SBC membranes called melanoidins resulting from the interaction between protein or peptide amino groups and carbohydrates. Several studies have also documented the presence of residual proteins and associated functional groupssuch as amino and amide groupsin bacterial cellulose from culture solutions, residual bacterial bodies, and proteins generated during the bacterial culturing process. , Also, the residual proteins or peptides can be incorporated within the cellulose matrix in NaOH-based treatment applied for bacterial cellulose purification. , In the case of the samples coded ST0 and ST1, the disappearance of the specific amide II band can be associated with an increase in the amount of water absorbed in the cellulose network, which makes the band centered at around 1630 cm–1 be associated with the deformation mode of the O–H bonds. The FTIR spectra also confirm the purity of nanocellulose-based samples proved by the absence of the bands centered at around 2925, 1250, 929, and 811 cm–1, and the lack of the band at around 1390 cm–1 (associated with NO3 –) from the spectra of AgNPs/SBC composites.

Antioxidant Activity

The antioxidant activity of the samples was evaluated by DPPH• and ABTS+ assays and proved the presence of compounds that can protect a biological system from the potentially damaging effects of reactions or processes involving reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (Table ). The bacterial cellulose purification process improves the antioxidant activity by exposing functional groups to protective effects. The antioxidant activity values through ABTS+ scavenging assay were significantly higher for all analyzed samples, and the best activity was registered for the AuNPs/SBC composites obtained in kombucha, followed by the samples obtained in fresh black tea. As can be seen from the results, the incorporation of noble metal nanoparticles in the cellulose matrix significantly enhanced the ABTS+ scavenging activity, and this effect is more pronounced when kombucha was used. AgNPs/SBC composites have the highest antioxidant activity compared to AuNPs/SBC composites. The highest ABTS+ scavenging activity were 44.81 ± 7.20% for SK1, 34.55 ± 1.99% for ST1 and, 15.92 ± 1.02% for SW1, respectively. For the DPPH• scavenging activity, the trend was similar to that of the ABTS+ scavenging activity. The DPPH free radical scavenging activity of the composites was significantly higher compared with SBC. This enhancement is more pronounced for SK1 because AgNPs in this case are the biggest nanoparticles (average size 46 nm) and are polycrystalline with a crystallite size of 2.1 nm therefore can dissolve more easily in the aqueous solution. Silver ions released in the medium react with DPPH free radicals, resulting in silver oxide. The nanocomposites with gold nanoparticles exhibited antioxidant activity superior to that of the SBC because of the intrinsic antioxidant activity of the AuNPs. The best DPPH free radical scavenging proved to have sample SK2.

The large active surface of the nanoparticles and the high surface energy allow efficient adsorption of bioactive molecules, keeping them active for a longer period. The nanoparticles synthesized in the presence of the SBCs and the compounds from black tea and postbiotics from kombucha beverage possess enhanced catalytic properties that accelerate the neutralization of reactive oxygen species (ROS), reducing oxidative stress and contributing to increased antioxidant activity and restoring the balance of antioxidants in cells. Another hypothesis for this antioxidant activity is the presence of polyphenolic compounds and melanoidins on the surface of nanocellulose derived from the SBC membrane. The purification process can affect the structure of the cellulose derived from the SBC membrane, influencing its adsorption capacity. It may also enhance antioxidant activity by removing impurities and noncellulosic components (such as proteins, saccharides, amino acids, and melanoidins) that could interfere with its functional properties. Purification exposes more hydroxyl groups on the cellulose surface, which can participate in antioxidant reactions by donating hydrogen atoms to neutralize free radicals. SEM, EDS, and FTIR analyses of the samples synthesized in unfermented black tea and fermented beverage (kombucha) media revealed the presence of melanoidin crystals with proved antioxidant properties. The purification process increases the crystallinity of cellulose, as seen from SEM and XRD analysis, which can stabilize the structure and improve its functional properties, including antioxidant activity.

Antibacterial Activity

The preliminary test for the antimicrobial activity of all the samples was carried out considering their diffusion potential in the agar media, previously inoculated with the pathogenic strain. The size of the inhibition zone indicated that the SK1, SK2, and ST1 composites were effective in inhibiting the activity of the pathogenic strains, regardless of their cell wall structural properties (Figure ). No relevant antimicrobial activity was observed for the other samples at the tested concentration. SK1 and SK2 composites exhibited better antimicrobial properties compared to the SK0 sample, which showed no bacterial inhibitory activity at the tested concentration of 50 mg/mL. Further, the antimicrobial activity of the ST1 composite was observed only against S. aureus and E. coli strains, being lower compared to those of the SK1 and SK2 composites. A large inhibition zone was measured for the SK2 sample against the E. coli (18.5 ± 0.7 mm), followed by L. monocytogenes (16.0 ± 0.56 mm) and S. aureus (15.0 ± 0.28 mm) strains, respectively. Additionally, the SK1 sample affected the growth of S. aureus, showing an inhibition zone diameter of 14.5 ± 0.14 mm. Based on the results of the inhibition zone diameters, the SK1 and SK2 samples were further tested for their minimum inhibitory and bactericidal concentrations. Even though the inhibition zone for SK2 was larger in the case of E. coli and L. monocytogenes compared to the SK1 sample, the MIC values indicated that SK1, with silver nanoparticles, was more effective. Among the currently accepted antimicrobial mechanisms of NPs, the interaction between the metal ions or metal nanoparticles and bacterial cells plays an important role. Also, the kinetics of such interactions depend on the nanoparticles’ surface properties. The results can be explained by the fact that the AuNPs might be more stable and diffuse more easily, while the surface charge of AgNPs can make them more prone to aggregation in agar media, which might hinder their diffusion. Furthermore, the AgNPs tend to have higher reactivity and form bonds with different components in the medium. This can cause AgNPs to clump together or bind to other molecules, potentially limiting their ability to diffuse efficiently. AuNPs, on the other hand, are generally more chemically stable and may not exhibit the same level of reactivity, which could allow them to diffuse more freely. , In our case the interactions between silver or gold and the biological compounds in the kombucha beverage known to be rich in metabolites (proteins, polyphenols, catechins, theophylline, various organic acids, vitamins, carotenoids, and other compounds with antimicrobial properties) could change the physicochemical properties of the silver and gold nanoparticles, leading to a strong interaction with the bacterial cell wall. These nanocomposites can also fight bacteria through the generation of ROS, inhibition of enzyme activity, DNA replication, etc.

The higher susceptibility of E. coli compared to S. aureus cells to nanosilver has been previously reported to be correlated with the physical characteristics of the nanoparticles, including their size, shape, surface charge, as well as the specific properties of the Gram-negative cell wall structure. ,

Conclusions

Cellulose–silver and cellulose–gold bioactive nanocomposites were synthesized using a green and cost-effective method employing alkaline-purified SCOBY nanocellulose. Noble metal nanoparticles were formed on the cellulose surface in different reaction media: tap water, black tea, and kombucha beverage using only metal precursor, proving the involvement of cellulose reducing ends in the synthesis process. AgNPs and AuNPs are quasi-spherical and generally formed from more than two nanocrystallites distributed along the surface of the cellulose fibers or within the interfibrillar pores. The synthesis media influenced the size of the nanoparticles. AgNPs tended to be larger than AuNPs under similar synthesis conditions. Kombucha medium contributed to the formation of the largest nanoparticles, while those produced in black tea had a narrower size dispersion. Furthermore, the bioactivity of the cellulose/gold and cellulose/silver nanocomposites was influenced by their origin, with the highest antioxidant and antimicrobial activities being observed in those produced from kombucha and black tea. The nanocomposites containing silver have antioxidant activity higher than that of the cellulose/gold nanocomposites. Kombucha medium favored the obtaining of nanocomposites that are effective in inhibiting the activity of pathogenic strains, regardless of their cell wall structural properties. This research presents a green synthesis technique for cellulose/silver and cellulose/gold nanocomposites, which could be used in medical applications and food packaging.
