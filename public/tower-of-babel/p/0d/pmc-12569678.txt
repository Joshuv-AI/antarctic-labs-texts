Turing COF Membranes with Tunable Patterns for Antibiotic Desalination
Authors: Jingcheng Du, Qian Sun, Dong Cao, Ayan Yao, Weiwang Lim, Ji Ma, Ali A. Al-Thuraya, Yumo Fan, Pengjia Dou, Jian Guan, Jiangtao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tailorable membrane surface architectures are crucial for efficient and precise separation. The nanoemulsion regulation strategy, an emerging technique for constructing nanostructured membranes with tunable architectures, has been scarcely explored for the fabrication of covalent organic framework (COF) membranes. In this work, inspired by the vesicle storage, transport, and release mechanism in human cells and the natural self-assembly of emulsions at interfaces, we proposed a nanoemulsion-directed interfacial polymerization (NDIP) strategy to precisely construct COF membranes with tunable Turing patterns. The introduction of these Turing structures leads to COF membranes with fewer defects and an enlarged surface area, attributable to the nanoemulsion templating effect and the controlled monomer transport mechanism. Short-range van der Waals forces and electrostatic interactions between the nanoemulsions and monomers were elucidated through molecular dynamics simulations and experimental results. Moreover, we systematically explored the effects of variable nanoemulsification space, different internal microenvironments, and emulsifier chain length on membrane separation performance. The designed Turing COF membranes exhibit excellent antibiotic separation ability with a molecular weight cutoff of 289 g/mol and ultrahigh antibiotic/salt selectivity (124.1 for TC/NaCl). This study provides a new design perspective for patterned regulation in COF membranes and highlights the potential of emulsion-guided strategies for advanced molecular separations.

Covalent organic frameworks (COF), featuring well-defined porous network structures formed by robust covalent bonds, have emerged as a promising class of materials for porous membranes. They offer distinct advantages such as tunable composition and structure, high specific surface area, significant porosity, predesigned architectures, and easy functionalization. As a result, COF have found broad applications in separation, heterogeneous catalysis, energy storage, sensing, drug delivery, optoelectronics, and various other fields. However, a large portion of COF is still used as powders or is incorporated into membranes riddled with defects, which poses major challenges for recycling and practical use. This limitation is mainly due to (i) the fact that nearly all COF are synthesized via conventional solvothermal methods under harsh conditions; (ii) the limited solubility of the monomers in typical solvent systems; and (iii) the slow or uneven mass transfer of monomers during synthesis. Collectively, these issues significantly hinder the broader application of COF-based membranes in separation science and technology.

To achieve optimal COF membranes for high-efficiency separation, researchers have developed innovative strategies based on both chemical and physical regulation. Chemical regulation involves introducing functional molecules or polymers to fine-tune the reaction’s thermodynamics and kinetics, thereby synthesizing defect-free COF membranes. For example, the dual-activation interfacial polymerization (IP) method employs Brønsted acids and bases to reduce interfacial barriers, while the dual acid-mediated IP approach uses organic acids to form dense membranes. Additionally, a polymer (PVP)-assisted crystallization strategy precisely controls the growth of COF nanosheets for ultraselective separation, and a polyelectrolyte-mediated assembly (PMA, PEI) technique bridges ultralarge COF nanosheets to create gas separation membranes. Physical regulation methodssuch as light, electrochemical, and thermal controlshave also been successfully applied. Examples include a phototailored strategy for synthesizing heterocrystalline COF membranes, a phase-switching approach that leverages thermal transformation for compact, highly crystalline membranes, and a layer-by-layer stacking assembly via electrophoretic deposition to produce ultrathin, homogeneous COF membranes. These advances underscore the critical role of rational regulation in fabricating defect-free COF membranes. Inspired by these strategies, our group engineered a molecular bridge at the oil/water interface to accelerate uniform molecular transfer, successfully producing a soft 2D COF membrane. Recently, various advanced COF membranes have been developed through different synthetic strategies. − Nonetheless, while these methods yield smooth surfaces with reduced liquid contact areas, precisely controlling the membrane’s surface architecture to further enhance separation performance remains a significant synthetic challenge.

Surface architectures of powder materials have been extensively engineered by using both hard and soft templates. Hard templates, such as silica nanostructures and metal oxides, often suffer from inflexible pore structures and cumbersome, complex removal processes, limiting their practical applications. In contrast, soft templatesincluding surfactants, copolymers, and ionic liquidsoffer significant advantages in tailoring morphologies, optimizing pore sizes and structures, and integrating hierarchical porosity to enhance material properties. Among soft templates, nanoemulsion-guided growth is particularly attractive for synthesizing nanomaterials with diverse architectures, although its application in optimizing the COF membrane structures has been limited. Nanoemulsions are dispersions of two immiscible liquids stabilized by surfactants, which reduce the surface tension. An oil/water nanoemulsion system not only provides a dynamic interface for self-assembly but also serves as an effective carrier for monomers. Critically, the self-assembly process is driven by the synergistic interplay of various noncovalent interactionsincluding hydrogen bonding, π-π stacking, electrostatic forces, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals forcesthat ensure effective interaction between the nanoemulsions and monomers. By leveraging both the carrier and templating roles of nanoemulsions, this strategy promotes the heterogeneous self-assembly of the COF, paving the way for COF membranes with novel architectures, such as Turing patterns.

Inspired by vesicles in human living systems, which possess distinct hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions, it facilitates the transport of both water-soluble and water-insoluble molecules to specific targets (Figure a). Vesicular transport is a fundamental mechanism for the trafficking of proteins, lipids, ions, and signaling molecules. The process involves five main steps: (i) vesicle formation mediated by coat proteins such as clathrin, COPI, and COPII; (ii) cargo selection regulated by adaptor proteins and signal sequences; (iii) vesicle transport along the cytoskeleton via motor proteins such as kinesin, dynein, and myosin; (iv) tethering and docking at the target membrane facilitated by Rab GTPases and tethering factors; and (v) membrane fusion driven by specific interactions between v-SNAREs and t-SNAREs. Together, these processes ensure precise intra- and intercellular transport. Besides, we also take advantage of the natural tendency of emulsions to self-assemble at interfaces. In our approach, we utilize two types of nanoemulsions, oil-in-water and water-in-oil, to carry water-soluble monomers and oil-soluble monomers, respectively, from the bulk phase to the targeted interfacial region (Figure b). The oil phase composition (toluene, hexane, and isooctane) and the surfactant chain length in the oil-in-water nanoemulsion can be systematically adjusted based on their unique chemical properties (Figure c). Subsequently, amphiphilic TAPA monomers or hydrophilic GA monomers, transported and directed via the nanoemulsions, react at the oil/water interface, resulting in the formation of a 2D COF membrane with a Turing structure (Figure d,e). The self-assembly process of a single nanoemulsion system for COF particles reveals that various COF architectures, such as plicated, bowl-shaped, caky, and foldable structures, can evolve, indicating successful self-assembly. To combine self-assembly with IP, we designed three distinct NDIP (nanoemulsion-directed interfacial polymerization) systems (Figure e): (i) Top NDIP system consisting of toluene/hexane/isooctane-in-water nanoemulsions as the top layer and DCM as the bottom layer, labeled TT-NDIP, TH-NDIP, and TI-NDIP, respectively; (ii) Down NDIP system with water as the top layer and water-in-DCM nanoemulsions as the bottom layer, labeled DD-NDIP; (iii) Double-layer NDIP system, with toluene-in-water nanoemulsions on top and water-in-DCM nanoemulsions beneath, labeled DL-NDIP. Compared with traditional IP systems, the NDIP strategy facilitates the formation of asymmetric COF membranes with tunable Turing patterns (Figure f). The proposed mechanism was confirmed by MD simulation and rigorous experimental results, mainly including monomers storage via van der Waals interaction and electrostatic interaction, monomers transport, nanoemulsions close-packed array, monomers release, nanoemulsions template effect, and template removal (Figure f–h). Finally, owing to the pore sizes lying between those of molecules and salts, the resulting membranes demonstrate excellent dye and antibiotic separation performance with low salt rejection, achieving an ultrahigh antibiotics/salt selectivity of 124.1. This systematic study elucidates the role of different nanoemulsion systems, clarifies the influence of self-assembly on NDIP, and highlights the significance of optimizing the architecture of COF membranes for advanced applications in water purification and pharmaceutical separations.

Results and Discussion

Nanoemulsions and NDIP System Construction

The key to achieving transport of TAPA and GA monomers and the templating effect for self-standing COF membranes with adjustable Turing structures lies in the rational design of nanoemulsions within specific microscopic environments. Here, we developed six nanoemulsions via the procedure consisting of four steps: (i) mixing oil (toluene, n-hexane, or isooctane) with deionized water and surfactants; (ii) subjecting the mixture to ultrasonication for 0.5 h; (iii) stirring magnetically for 0.5 h; and (iv) obtaining a stable nanoemulsion. They were designed as follows: (i) oil-in-water nanoemulsions with varying inner microenvironments (toluene-in-water (SDS), hexane-in-water (SDS), isooctane-in-water (SDS)); (ii) oil-in-water nanoemulsions with different surfactant lengths (toluene-in-water (SHS), toluene-in-water (SNHS)); and (iii) water-in-oil nanoemulsions (dichloromethane, DCM) (Scheme S1). These initially transparent oil/water mixtures turned into milky white, well-dispersed, spherical nanoemulsions. The six nanoemulsions exhibited distinct emulsification behaviors, with samples water-in-DCM (S80), toluene-in-water (SDS), isooctane-in-water (SDS), toluene-in-water (SHS), and toluene-in-water (SNHS) showing higher degrees of emulsification, whereas sample hexane-in-water (SDS) displayed slightly weaker emulsification (Figure S1). Furthermore, spherical nanoemulsions with varying sizes were observed by an optical microscope. Specifically, the toluene-in-water (SDS) and hexane-in-water (SDS) nanoemulsions exhibited larger particle sizes compared with the water-in-DCM (S80) and isooctane-in-water (SDS) systems, demonstrating that the emulsification environment exerts a significant effect on nanoemulsion size (Figure S2). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) results showed that the particle sizes of the isooctane-in-water (SDS), toluene-in-water (SDS), and hexane-in-water (SDS) nanoemulsions were approximately 350, 600, and 800 nm, respectively. Furthermore, as the surfactant chain length increased, the size of the corresponding nanoemulsions decreasedachieving sizes of 2800, 600, and 150 nm for the toluene-in-water nanoemulsions. The water-in-oil nanoemulsions, in contrast, exhibited the smallest size, about 30 nm (Figure S3). All oil-in-water nanoemulsions displayed a strong electronegativity (Figure S4). Lastly, GA and TAPA monomers were incorporated into the oil-in-water and water-in-oil nanoemulsions to derive TAPA or GA-in-nanoemulsions, which were then used in the COF self-assembly and NDIP processes (Figure S5).

Nanoemulsions have proven valuable in the synthesis of single-crystal COFs and COF powders with adjustable pore sizes by using amphiphilic molecules, an aspect that previously received limited attention. − However, the influence of the nanoemulsion microenvironment on the COF formation remains unclear. To investigate the relationship between the self-assembly of COF and the microenvironment of nanoemulsions, we employed the obtained nanoemulsions for spatial division of monomers. A schematic illustration of the nanoemulsion-directed assembly process is shown in Scheme S2. The formation mechanism of COF powders within the IP system follows a four-step process: (i) Nanoemulsions adsorb GA monomers via van der Waals forces, resulting in GA-loaded nanoemulsions; (ii) The negatively charged nanoemulsions adsorb positively charged TAPA monomers through electrostatic interactions; (iii) Partitioned TAPA and GA monomers are localized at the interface of the nanoemulsions and undergo a Schiff base reaction under room temperature and atmospheric pressure via spatial division; (iv) COFs with unique morphologies are synthesized after washing to remove the nanoemulsions. Notably, in the NDIP system, spherical COF powders (D = 0.35 μm, Figure S6) were transformed into a bowl shape (Figure S7). The internal microenvironment of the nanoemulsion also significantly impacted COF self-assembly. When the inner microenvironment was toluene, a half-bowl-shaped COF with a diameter of ∼5.5 μm was formed (Figure S7). In contrast, when the microenvironment was hexane or isooctane, the bowl-shaped COFs became larger and the walls thinner (Figure S8). Conversely, when the microenvironment was a water-in-DCM nanoemulsion, the morphology of the COF powders resembled a cake with a diameter of approximately 1.3 μm (Figure S9). In the TT-NDIP system, increasing the length of the surfactants resulted in a coarser hemispherical COF shape (D = 1.1 μm) with some small particles (Figure S10). This was likely due to longer surfactants, which elongate the monomer transfer duration and create uneven reactions.

Self-assembly enables the formation of COF powders with diverse morphologies that hold tremendous potential for catalysis and adsorption, thanks to their high specific surface area and unique geometries. , However, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood, and linking the self-assembly of COF powders to the fabrication of self-standing COF membranesthereby incorporating diverse COF morphologies into membranes with adjustable Turing structuresremains largely unexplored. We observed that emulsions naturally self-assemble at interfaces to reduce the interfacial tension and maximize the space utilization (Figure a), a phenomenon that is crucial for preparing Turing-patterned COF membranes. Capitalizing on this behavior, we devised the NDIP (nanoemulsion-directed interfacial polymerization) strategy to couple self-assembly with interfacial polymerization (IP). In this system, monomers encapsulated in static nanoemulsions act as dynamic nanotransporters, delivering them from the bulk phase to the interface in a controlled manner, thereby enabling the formation of self-standing COF membranes with designed surface morphologies. Within the NDIP system, surfactants existed both as part of the nanoemulsion and in free molecular form. These surfactants are uniformly arranged at the interface, promoting rapid and even monomer transfer, which accelerated the formation of defect-free COF membranes. , Beyond acting as nanotransporters, the nanoemulsions serve as templates: the encapsulated monomers were released at the interface to react with monomers from the opposite phase, leading to the uniform formation of self-assembled bowl-shaped structures. These patterned morphologies greatly enhanced the specific surface area, favoring efficient molecular sieving. In contrast, traditional IP systems yielded self-standing COF membranes with smooth top and bottom surfaces that exhibited inherent defects and resulted in low separation efficiency for dyes and antibiotic molecules (Figures b­(i) and S11). In our TT-NDIP system, however, well-defined Turing patterns emerged on the membrane surfacefor example, a honeycomb structure was observed on the down surface of a TAPB-GA membrane, while a uniform honeycomb with embedded bowl-shaped structures appeared on the top surface of a TAPA-GA membrane (Figures b­(ii),c,d and S12). Notably, the opposite surfaces of these COF membranes remained dense and smooth (Figures e and S14), exhibiting a typical asymmetric state that reflected a nonthermodynamic equilibriuma rare characteristic in COF membranes. This observation further confirmed the dual role of nanoemulsions as both transporters and templates. Moreover, when the surfactant concentration in the TT-NDIP system was increased, the Turing structures gradually diminished and the membranes became superhydrophilic (Figure S13). In the DD-NDIP system, the TAPA-GA COF membrane also exhibited an asymmetric structure, with the top surface covered by numerous small particles, whereas the bottom surface remained smooth (Figure b­(iii),f,g). This phenomenon indicated that the dominant reaction zone was located at the top surface (aqueous side) rather than the down surface. This localization arose from the solubility and diffusion behavior of the monomers, where nanoemulsions effectively transported amphiphilic TAPA monomers from the organic phase into the aqueous phase for Schiff base reaction with GA, thereby inducing pattern formation at the top surface of the COF membrane. , In contrast, when the amine monomer was soluble only in the organic phase, as in the case of TAPB, the reaction preferentially occurred on the organic side of the interface, leading to Turing structures localized on the bottom surface of the membrane. Furthermore, the DL-NDIP system, which employed dual-phase nanoemulsions, yielded a dark brown-yellow COF membrane with an asymmetric structure: the top surface exhibited a “ridge-valley” morphology, while the down surface showed a regular honeycomb and bowl-shaped pattern (Figure b­(iv),h,i). Elemental analysis confirmed a uniform distribution of C and N in these membranes (Figure S14). Additionally, reducing the surfactant chain length in the toluene-in-water (SHS) NDIP system resulted in a vesicle-like Turing structure on the top surface, with numerous small COF particles on the down surface (Figure S15), whereas extending the surfactant length led to an ordered bowl-like structure on the down surface and a relatively smooth top surface (Figure S16). In summary, the Turing structures of COF membranes can be selectively regulated by adjusting emulsification space (water phase emulsification, oil phase emulsification, and dual-phase emulsification) and chemical environments (chain length and concentration of surfactants), providing technical support for tailored membrane architectures.

Furthermore, these self-standing COF membranes can be easily transferred to various substrates, such as PES, PAN, AAO, silicon wafers, or copper mesh, for subsequent characterization and application. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) analysis revealed well-defined lattice microregions with a lattice spacing of 0.3 nm, matching the <001> facet (Figure j). As shown in Figure k,l, the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface areas of the self-standing TAPA-GA COF membranes synthesized via (i) traditional IP, (ii) TT-NDIP, (iii) DD-IP, and (iv) DL-IP systems were 32.23, 47.65, 53.78, and 108.95 m2/g, respectively. This demonstrated that the nanoemulsion strategy significantly enhanced the surface area of the COF membranes, with the DL-NDIP system producing the largest surface area. Additionally, the pore size distributions of TAPA-GA COF membranes synthesized via (i) traditional IP, (ii) TT-NDIP, (iii) DD-IP, and (iv) DL-IP systems were centered at 1.04, 0.957, 0.875, and 0.860 nm, respectively, indicating that the nanoemulsion strategy effectively enhanced the pore size optimization in the COF membranes. Thus, nanoemulsions not only improved the surface area of the COF membranes but also reduced the prominent pore size, further optimizing functional properties

The chemical composition of the TAPA-GA COF membranes was determined by using Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy and 13C solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. The FT-IR spectrum of the TAPA-GA COF membranes (Figure S17) displayed the disappearance of the C = O peak (1694 cm–1) of the GA monomer and the -NH2 peak (3320–3430 cm–1) of the TAPA monomer. At the same time, a new C = N peak (1607 cm–1) emerged, confirming the successful formation of the imine bond. Additionally, the 13C solid-state NMR spectrum (Figure m) showed a peak at 144.1 ppm assigned to C = N, further validating the condensation between the aldehyde and amine groups. Chemical composition and structural analysis of the TAPA-GA COF membranes from different systems were also conducted through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Figure S18), which revealed an N 1s peak at 398.5 eV, corresponding to the C = N bonds in the COF skeleton. These findings, along with the chemical and structural analysis results, confirmed the successful synthesis of the self-standing COF membranes. Surface charge analysis indicated that the COF membranes present negative charge in a neutral environment (Figure S19). Furthermore, thermogravimetric analysis showed excellent thermal stability with membrane integrity maintained up to 300 °C in an air atmosphere (Figure S20).

To deeply probe the transport and template function of nanoemulsions, underlying mechanisms including the interaction force between molecules and nanoemulsions and the distribution and mass transfer process of molecules and nanoemulsions in the NDIP system should be revealed at a molecular level. Therefore, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of TT-NDIP and DD-NDIP systems were performed according to experimental conditions. The steepest descent method was applied to minimize the initial energy for each system with a force tolerance of 1 kJ·mol–1·nm–1 and a maximum step size of 0.002 ps before MD simulations. In all three directions, periodic boundary conditions were imposed. Leapfrog algorithm was used to integrate the Newtonian equation of motion. In NPT simulations, the pressure was maintained at 1 bar by the Berendsen barostat in a semi-isotropic manner and the temperature was maintained by the V-rescale thermostat at 298.15 K. The particle-mesh Ewald (PME) with a fourth-order interpolation was used to evaluate the electrostatic interactions, and the grid spacing is 1.0 Å, whereas a cutoff of 1.0 nm was employed to calculate the short-range van der Waals interactions.

The constructed nanoemulsions and optimized molecules were introduced into a molecular dynamics (MD) simulated NDIP system (Figure a,c,e). In the simulation, SDS molecules self-assemble at the DCM/H2O interface due to their hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tail. The toluene-in-water nanoemulsions in the bulk phase quickly moved toward the interface, driven by short-range van der Waals interactions (−4.883 kJ/mol) and electrostatic interactions (−0.905 kJ/mol) between the nanoemulsions and TAPA monomers, yielding a total mean interaction energy of −5.789 kJ/mol (Figures a,b,g, and S21a). Similarly, S80 molecules self-assemble at the DCM/H2O interface, and water-in-DCM nanoemulsions gradually moved toward the interface, primarily driven by van der Waals forces (−99.156 kJ/mol) and electrostatic interactions (−13.539 kJ/mol) between the nanoemulsions and GA, with a total mean interaction energy of −112.696 kJ/mol (Figures c,d,i, and S21b). Therefore, the dominant driving forces behind the nanoemulsion’s migration toward the interface were the van der Waals interactions between the nanoemulsions and the phase monomers. Simulations and experimental data further confirmed that the nanoemulsions can carry monomers. Once the system reached equilibrium, individual nanoemulsions were analyzed in depth. As shown in Figure g and S22, a large quantity of GA monomers was embedded within the toluene-in-water nanoemulsion via molecular interactions (−738.002 kJ/mol), including van der Waals forces (−705.101 kJ/mol) and electrostatic interactions (−32.900 kJ/mol). Local analysis revealed that the interaction distances between SDS and GA molecules in the nanoemulsion were d1 = 4.27 Å and d2 = 4.43 Å, facilitating monomer storage in the nanoemulsion due to the strong affinity (Figures f and S23). In comparison, the distribution of TAPA monomers in water-in-DCM nanoemulsions was studied, showing that the water-in-DCM nanoemulsions could carry TAPA monomers via electrostatic interactions (−5.677 kJ/mol) and van der Waals forces (−151.021 kJ/mol), with van der Waals forces remaining the dominant force (Figures i and S24). Local analysis showed that the interaction distances between S80 and TAPA molecules in the nanoemulsion were d1 = 3.95 Å and d2 = 3.54 Å, indicating strong interactions between S80 nanoemulsions and TAPA molecules at the molecular level (Figures h and S25). Furthermore, filtration experiments with monomers/nanoemulsions confirmed the nanoemulsions’ ability to carry monomers, as evidenced by the reduction in monomer concentration in the filtrate of the nanoemulsion system (Figures S26 and S27). Experimental calculations showed that the concentration of GA and TAPA monomers in the nanoemulsions was 11 and 3.8 times higher than that in the bulk phase, respectively (Figure S28a,b), indicating a significant aggregation of monomers in the microenvironment of the nanoemulsions. Therefore, nanoemulsions can carry TAPA and GA monomers, functioning as templates to alter the surface microstructure of COF membranes.

The density distribution along the Z-axis in the TT-NDIP system, with an interfacial width of approximately 1 nm, revealed that TAPA, GA, toluene-in-water nanoemulsions, and SDS molecules coexisted at the interface (Figures j and S29). In the DD-NDIP system, TAPA, GA, water-in-DCM nanoemulsions, and S80 molecules were present at the interface with a broader width of ∼1.5 nm due to the longer S80 surfactants, which suggested higher accumulation of monomers and nanoemulsions in the polymerization reaction zone (Figures k and S30). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations over a 0–20 ns period showed that in the TT-NDIP system, the diffusion coefficients of TAPA and toluene-in-water nanoemulsions were 0.8443 × 10–5 cm2/s and 0.1157 × 10–5 cm2/s, respectively (Figure l­(i)). During the final 1 ns, the diffusion coefficients achieved 1.3379 × 10–5 cm2/s for TAPA and 0.3815 × 10–5 cm2/s for the nanoemulsions (Figure S31), reflecting the faster diffusion of TAPA due to its smaller molecular weight. Similarly, in the DD-NDIP system, the diffusion coefficients of TAPA and water-in-DCM nanoemulsions during the entire 0–20 ns period were 0.9361 × 10–5 cm2/s and 0.1984 × 10–5 cm2/s, respectively (Figure l­(ii)). During the final 1 ns, these coefficients increased to 1.2888 × 10–5 cm2/s for TAPA and 0.4898 × 10–5 cm2/s for the nanoemulsions (Figure S32), further confirming faster diffusion of TAPA. To further validate the role of nanoemulsions in enhancing monomer transfer, UV spectrophotometry was used in four monomer transfer experiments (Figure m). In a near-real IP system without nanoemulsions, TAPA molecules crossed the water/DCM interface by diffusion, resulting in a moderate absorbance reduction of ΔA1 = 0.5726 over 30 min (Figure n). However, in the presence of toluene-in-water nanoemulsions, the transfer of TAPA monomers was accelerated by short-range van der Waals and electrostatic interactions, leading to a significant absorbance reduction of ΔA2 = 2.2126 within the same time framenearly a 4-fold increase (Figure o). Similarly, for GA monomers, the absorbance reduction in the water-in-DCM nanoemulsion system (ΔA4 = 0.0302) was almost double the decrease observed in the traditional system (ΔA3 = 0.0171), demonstrating an effective enhancement of mass transfer (Figures p,q). Together, these experimental and simulation results provided strong evidence that nanoemulsions successfully facilitated monomer transfer through intermolecular interactions, thereby enabling the formation of self-standing COF membranes with tunable surface structures.

Separation processes in the chemical industry account for roughly 15% of global energy consumption, primarily due to traditional equilibrium techniques like distillation that rely on energy-intensive phase transitions. , In contrast, membrane technologies based on size screening offer significant advantages in compactness, energy efficiency, scalability, and environmental sustainability. In particular, nanofiltration (NF) membraneswith their nanoscale sieving channelscan precisely separate molecules or ions, making them highly effective for water purification, pharmaceutical refinement, and organic compound separation. − This capability not only supports environmental goals but also enables the production of high-purity drugs that are essential for health.

A critical application of these methods is antibiotic desalination. Antibiotics (e.g., ciprofloxacin (CF), tetracycline (TC), rifampicin (RFP), and vitamin B12 (VB12)) are typically produced via fermentation or chemical synthesis, during which large amounts of inorganic salts are introduced to regulate osmotic pressure, sustain microbial growth, or stabilize the products. As a result, the crude antibiotic solutions often contain high concentrations of salts (e.g., chlorides, sulfates, or phosphates), which compromise product purity, hinder downstream crystallization and drying processes, and may lead to safety concerns if excessive ions remain in the final formulations. Therefore, efficient desalination is a critical step in antibiotic purification and refinement. Conventional approaches, such as activated carbon adsorption, solvent extraction, and recrystallization, are often limited by low selectivity, high energy consumption, or yield loss. In recent years, membrane-based separation technologies have attracted increasing attention. By tailoring the pore size and interfacial chemistry, such membranes enable effective ion removal while maintaining high antibiotic retention, thereby providing a green and sustainable pathway for antibiotic desalination. To tackle this, a dead-end filtration device was designed (Figures a and S33), employing a COF membrane that selectively blocked larger antibiotic molecules while allowing smaller salt ions to pass through (Figure b). The membrane’s precise pore size was crucial for this selective separation. By using various probe molecules (detailed information in Table S1), the TT-NDIP membrane was shown to achieve a molecular weight cutoff as low as 289 g·mol–1, with an excellent rejection rate of 98.33% and a permeance of approximately 3 L·m–2 ·h–1 ·bar–1 (Figures c and S34,S35). Traditional COF membranes exhibited poor separation performance for RG and RB with higher permeance (Figures S36 and S37). Moreover, the DD-NDIP and DL-NDIP membranes also exhibited high rejection rates of 96.63% and 92.91%, respectively, along with slightly higher permeance values (3.5 and 4.0 L·m–2 ·h–1 ·bar–1) when tested with Congo red (Figures S38–S41). These results underscored the significant impact that the nanoemulsion system had on optimizing the pore size and overall separation performance of the COF membranes.

Beyond comparing different space emulsification IP systems, we also studied how the internal environment of nanoemulsions affects membrane separation. By incorporating hexane and isooctane into the nanoemulsions, we produced hexane-in-water and isooctane-in-water systems for membrane formation. The results (Figures e and S42–S45) indicated that isooctane-in-water nanoemulsions favor the formation of denser membranes, thereby enhancing molecular separation. Moreover, altering the emulsifier length to change the nanoemulsion size adversely affected the membrane performance. If the surfactant was too short or too long, the interface became unstable, disrupting the uniform transfer of monomers and leading to membrane defects (Figures f and S46–S48).

Based on the molecular sieving results, the TT-NDIP membrane demonstrated superior separation performance and was selected as a candidate for antibiotic desalination studies. Four antibiotic molecules (CF, TC, RFP, and VB12) and four inorganic salts (Na2SO4, MgSO4, KCl, and NaCl) were used as model species (detailed information in Table S3). As shown in Figures g and S49–S50, the TT-NDIP membrane effectively separated antibiotic molecules, achieving a retention rate of over 99% with excellent permeance. In contrast, the membrane exhibited low rejection (<5%) for all inorganic salts due to their hydrated radii being smaller than the COF’s aperture size and negligible ion-channel interactions. For practical demonstration, mixture solutions of TC/NaCl (20 mg/L + 1000 ppm) and CF/NaCl (20 mg/L + 1000 ppm) were processed. As shown in Figure h, the TT-NDIP membrane maintained high antibiotic rejections of 99.21% (TC) and 98.13% (CF), while achieving extremely low salt rejections of 2.0% and 1.6%, respectively. Consequently, the antibiotic/salt selectivity of the TT-NDIP membrane was outstanding124.1 for TC/NaCl and 52.6 for CF/NaClfar exceeding that of state-of-the-art membranes (Figure i). Furthermore, both the water permeance and the rejection rates for TC and NaCl remained stable over 7 days of continuous operation (Figure j), demonstrating excellent long-term stability and durability.

Conclusions

In summary, Turing COF membranes with tunable patterns for antibiotic desalination were successfully prepared via nanoemulsion-directed IP. The internal microenvironment and size of the nanoemulsions were precisely adjusted to achieve COF powders with diverse architectures, which were obtained through controlled monomer aggregation and spatial segregation within static nanoemulsions. By coupling self-assembly with IP, the NDIP strategy was designed to leverage both the transport function and the templating effect of nanoemulsions for the fabrication of COF membranes exhibiting tunable Turing patterns. As a result, the molecular separation efficiency and antibiotic/salt selectivity were dramatically enhanced. Moreover, by variation of emulsification conditions, COF membranes with higher surface areas, fewer defects, and adjustable pore sizes were enabled, thereby reinforcing separation performance. The interactions between dynamic nanoemulsions and homogeneous and heterogeneous monomers, nanoemulsion transmission efficiency, and TAPA/GA monomer transfer rates in NDIP systems were systematically elucidated through MD simulations and experimental results. New avenues for the development of COF membranes with tunable Turing structures via IP have been opened, and versatile platforms for next-generation membrane technology have been established.
