From innovation to implementation: Addressing the AI adoption gap in cardiac surgery
Authors: Dabeluchi Ngwu, Fahd Hamadi, Oluranti Akinyemi, Chinwe Wokocha, Niket Arora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile artificial intelligence (AI) is advancing rapidly across cardiovascular medicine, its translation into cardiac surgery remains limited. Algorithms show promise in diagnostics, perioperative risk prediction, and workflow optimization, yet most applications remain confined to research environments. A focused synthesis is needed to clarify validated clinical value and persistent implementation barriers.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review following PRISMA 2020 guidelines, searching PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science for studies published between January 2015 and September 2024. We included 45 primary studies (2019–2024) and eight foundational studies (2015–2018) reporting original data or validated AI models relevant to any stage of cardiac surgical care. Findings were synthesized across five domains: diagnostic support, personalized treatment planning, intraoperative decision support, operational efficiency, and equitable access to care.ResultsAI demonstrated strong performance in echocardiographic interpretation, outcome prediction, and perioperative resource planning, often surpassing conventional risk models. Computer-vision platforms supported surgical phase recognition and enhanced intraoperative imaging workflows, while operational tools improved scheduling accuracy, transfusion forecasting, and bed allocation. Evidence for AI-driven improvements in equitable care delivery was emerging but limited. Most studies were retrospective, single-center, and lacked external validation or clinical integration.ConclusionAI is positioned to augment cardiac surgery, with the most mature applications in imaging and operational logistics. Adoption remains constrained by heterogeneous data, limited interpretability, regulatory uncertainty, and poor workflow integration. Progress will require multicenter data collaboratives, strong validation frameworks, and clinician-centered implementation strategies that position AI as an augmentative partner, enhancing precision, judgment, and system efficiency.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly shaping cardiovascular medicine by enabling clinicians to analyse large datasets, detect subtle patterns, and support decisions that were once guided primarily by experience and intuition. In cardiology, AI-based systems now match or exceed human performance in imaging interpretation, arrhythmia detection, and risk prediction [13], [14], [15], [17]. Cardiac surgery, however, has adopted these tools more cautiously, not due to resistance, but because the surgical environment is uniquely dynamic. Procedures generate heterogeneous and rapidly evolving data streams, ranging from real-time physiology and surgical video to instrument telemetry, making algorithm development, validation, and safe clinical integration especially challenging [4], [5], [16], [46].

Recent advances have also introduced the Internet of Things (IoT) into cardiac surgical practice. Connected monitors, infusion devices, imaging systems, and operating room sensors now enable continuous physiologic monitoring and real-time data acquisition across the perioperative continuum [51]. When paired with AI analytics, these infrastructures can enhance patient surveillance, support earlier detection of complications, and improve workflow efficiency. At the same time, the volume and velocity of such data increase system complexity, reinforcing the need for robust validation, interoperability standards, and governance frameworks before widespread adoption. In this way, IoT both enables richer AI capabilities and illustrates the integration challenges that underlie the AI adoption gap.

Between 2015 and 2018, foundational work by Deo, Rumsfeld, Taggart, Biswas, Krittanawong, Litjens, and others established the conceptual underpinnings of surgical AI [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. Early studies explored perioperative risk prediction, image classification, and big data-driven decision support [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]. Allyn et al. demonstrated that machine-learning models could outperform EuroSCORE II for mortality prediction [11], while Kitaguchi et al. emphasized the importance of transparent reporting standards for surgical computer-vision systems [12]. Together, these contributions defined the methodological architecture upon which subsequent algorithms were built.

Since 2019, advances in deep learning, imaging automation, and computational modeling have accelerated progress across both cardiology and cardiac surgery [13], [14], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]. Parallel developments in thoracic surgery further illustrate the expanding role of AI across cardiothoracic disciplines. Studies describing AI-assisted imaging interpretation, video-based surgical analytics, and risk stratification for lung resection and minimally invasive thoracic procedures highlight shared technical and implementation challenges across cardiac and thoracic surgery [52], [53]. Despite this momentum, only a limited number of AI systems have achieved sustained use in routine clinical practice in either domain.

Persistent barriers include fragmented data pipelines, limited external validation, challenges integrating tools into existing workflows, evolving regulatory expectations, and ongoing clinician scepticism [14], [15], [47], [48], [49], [50]. The result is a clear AI adoption gap—the difference between algorithmic performance demonstrated in controlled research settings and the value delivered in everyday surgical care [14], [15], [46].

Bridging this gap will require surgeon leadership, multidisciplinary collaboration, and clear evidence of clinical benefit [10], [16], [46]. By synthesizing the literature from 2015 to 2024 across five domains—diagnostic support, personalized treatment planning, intraoperative decision support, operational efficiency, and equity in care delivery—this review examines where AI currently adds value in cardiac surgery and where important limitations and implementation challenges remain.

Methods

Search strategy

A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science to identify studies published between January 1, 2015, and September 15, 2024 (date of last search). Search strategies combined standardized AI-related terms, including “artificial intelligence,” “machine learning,” “deep learning,” “neural networks,” “natural language processing,” and “computer vision,” with cardiac surgery–related keywords such as “cardiac surgery,” “cardiothoracic surgery,” “cardiac procedures,” and “operative outcomes.”

Database-specific Boolean search strings were developed iteratively and are reported in Appendix A to ensure transparency and reproducibility. Searches were limited to English-language, peer-reviewed studies involving human subjects. Reference lists of eligible articles and relevant prior reviews were manually screened to identify additional studies not captured through database searching.

Primary analyses emphasized studies published between 2019 and 2024, reflecting contemporary AI methodologies, validation practices, and implementation considerations. Earlier foundational studies published between 2015 and 2018 were retained selectively to provide historical and methodological context (summarized in Table 2). Search conduct and reporting followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines [1].

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they reported original data or validated AI models relevant to one or more stages of cardiac surgical care, presented quantitative outcomes (including discrimination and/or calibration metrics, workflow measures, complication rates, or mortality), and were published in indexed, peer-reviewed journals.

Studies were excluded if they focused exclusively on nonsurgical cardiovascular care; used animal, cadaveric, or purely simulated datasets; were editorials, commentaries, narrative reviews, or viewpoints without extractable outcome data; were conference abstracts, preprints, or non–peer-reviewed reports; or described hybrid analytical approaches in which AI-specific performance could not be clearly distinguished from traditional statistical modeling.

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility, followed by a full-text review of potentially relevant studies. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

A total of 1288 records were identified across PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. After removal of 258 duplicates, 1030 unique records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 890 were excluded as unrelated to AI applications in cardiac surgery. One hundred forty full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 95 studies were excluded, including 62 with no relevant surgical AI application and 33 with insufficient validation detail or outcome reporting. The final synthesis included 45 primary studies published between 2019 and 2024, with eight pre-2019 studies retained as foundational references. Data extraction

Data were extracted using a standardized template capturing author and year; AI technique or model type; study design and sample size; clinical application domain; input data modality (e.g., imaging, electronic health records, or multimodal data); validation strategy (internal, external, or temporal); and primary outcomes and performance metrics. Each study was categorized into one of five predefined domains: diagnostic support, personalized treatment planning, intraoperative decision support, operational efficiency, or equity in care delivery.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality and risk of bias were evaluated using adapted domain-based criteria informed by AMSTAR-2 and ROBIS. These domains focused on the clarity and completeness of reporting, the rigor of model validation (including external or temporal testing), the representativeness of training and test datasets, the degree of clinical integration or simulation, and transparency regarding model reproducibility and availability [2], [3]. Assessments were conducted independently by two reviewers, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Each study was assessed across these domains and assigned an overall confidence rating of high, moderate, or low based on the combined judgments. High-confidence studies demonstrated transparent methodology, appropriate validation, and clinically relevant outcomes; moderate-confidence studies reported key elements incompletely; and low-confidence studies showed substantial limitations in validation, reporting, or reproducibility. Synthesis approach

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Because of substantial heterogeneity in study design, AI methodologies, clinical endpoints, and validation strategies, a quantitative meta-analysis was not performed. Instead, findings were synthesized narratively, emphasizing model performance, validation rigor, workflow integration, and readiness for real-world implementation.

Results and emerging domains

Diagnostic support

AI has reshaped diagnostic imaging in cardiac surgery through automated interpretation of echocardiography, cardiac computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Deep learning models, most notably EchoNet-Dynamic, estimate left ventricular ejection fraction with near-expert consistency, demonstrating consistently high diagnostic performance in controlled settings and strong agreement with expert measurements across external validation cohorts and imaging platforms [23], [24], [25]. A randomized clinical trial showed that AI-assisted ejection fraction estimation matched sonographer performance while reducing interobserver variability [24]. More recent vision–language models extend these capabilities by maintaining task-level performance across heterogeneous acoustic windows and imaging conditions [26].

Computer-vision systems have likewise demonstrated consistently high diagnostic performance in controlled settings for specific image-based tasks, including transesophageal echocardiography view classification, with reproducible accuracy across adult and pediatric populations [27], [28]. In structural heart disease, deep-learning–based reconstruction techniques support transcatheter aortic valve implantation planning by accelerating segmentation and reducing contrast requirements [29]. Complementary computational modeling approaches further support procedural planning and decision-making for valve interventions [30], [31].

Despite these advances, most diagnostic AI systems remain deployed as stand-alone tools rather than integrated components of picture archiving and communication systems or routine clinical workflows. Broader adoption will require multicenter validation, harmonized imaging standards, and clearly defined regulatory pathways to support safe and reliable clinical deployment [12], [13], [14], [47], [48], [49], [50].

Personalized treatment planning

AI increasingly supports procedural planning and perioperative risk stratification in cardiac surgery. Gradient-boosting and interpretable machine-learning frameworks consistently outperform traditional risk models such as EuroSCORE II and demonstrate improved calibration when supplied with nonlinear clinical inputs [6], [11], [35], [36]. Multimodal models integrating imaging with electronic health record variables typically report AUCs of approximately 0.75–0.88 for identifying patients at elevated risk of mortality, complications, or readmission following cardiac surgery [22], [35], [36], [37].

Beyond perioperative risk estimation, deep learning analysis of resting electrocardiograms enables prediction of future atrial fibrillation, thereby complementing findings from population-scale digital health screening initiatives [32], [33]. Together, these approaches may refine patient selection for surgery, inform decisions between coronary artery bypass grafting and percutaneous strategies, and support shared decision-making in complex scenarios [5], [6], [22], [31].

However, most treatment-planning models remain retrospective. Few are embedded within real-time clinical systems, and only a limited number have undergone prospective or multicenter evaluation, constraining conclusions regarding sustained real-world effectiveness [22], [35], [36], [37], [46].

Intraoperative decision support

AI is beginning to influence intraoperative care through enhanced situational awareness and workflow analysis. Computer-vision platforms trained on operative video can recognize surgical phases and nontechnical team behaviors with strong performance in validation studies, supporting workflow tracking, automated documentation, and early detection of deviations during complex procedures [16], [38], [39]. These systems offer structured insight into technical performance and team dynamics, with potential value for quality improvement and training.

AI-enhanced echocardiography also supports real-time interpretation during valve and aortic procedures, building on advances in automated view classification and image analysis [23], [24], [25], [27], [45]. In parallel, recurrent neural networks and other time-series models trained on cardiopulmonary bypass parameters and hemodynamic data can predict perfusion instability and hypotension several minutes before onset [40], [41], enabling earlier anticipation and response by perfusionists and anaesthesiologists.

Integration challenges remain substantial, including hardware compatibility, multimodal data synchronization, medicolegal considerations, and the need for continuous human oversight during high-stakes intraoperative decision-making [12], [15], [16]. Operational efficiency

Operational applications are among the most mature domains of AI use in cardiac surgery. Machine-learning models predict case duration more accurately than surgeon estimates, with reported mean absolute error reductions of approximately 15–30%, supporting improved operating room scheduling and resource allocation [18]. Transfusion-prediction models demonstrate moderate-to-high discrimination in validation cohorts across coronary artery bypass grafting, valve surgery, and mixed cardiac surgical populations [19], [20], [21].

Predictive tools for postoperative length of stay and discharge readiness also show moderate-to-high predictive performance in validation cohorts. They may support intensive care unit bed planning and ward capacity management [10], [42]. Other perioperative models identify patients at elevated risk for adverse outcomes using intraoperative and early postoperative data, enabling earlier opportunities for targeted monitoring or intervention [22], [35], [36], [37], [41].

Despite promising accuracy, adoption remains limited because many operational tools function as stand-alone analytics rather than embedded components of hospital information systems. Integration into electronic health records, perioperative dashboards, and hospital command centers will be essential for routine clinical use [10], [18], [19], [20], [21], [42], [46].

Equity and access

AI-enabled echocardiography shows promise for expanding access to diagnostic services in low- and middle-income countries. Mobile AI systems detect rheumatic and degenerative valvular disease with near-expert sensitivity, offering scalable solutions where specialist availability is limited [43]. The World Heart Federation has endorsed AI-augmented echocardiographic screening as one strategy to improve early detection in resource-limited environments [44].

AI guidance can also enable novice operators to acquire diagnostically adequate transthoracic echocardiographic images, reducing reliance on on-site specialists and facilitating decentralization of imaging services [45]. These approaches may mitigate geographic and workforce-related disparities.

However, real-world deployment faces persistent challenges, including inconsistent connectivity, limited local infrastructure, and the risk that models trained predominantly on high-income populations may perform less reliably across diverse demographic and clinical settings [13], [14], [15], [43], [44].

Overall trends

Across the past decade, the field has shifted from algorithm development toward implementation feasibility and clinical relevance. Diagnostic models have advanced to prospective and randomized evaluations [23], [24], [25], [26], [28], [29], [30], [31], while operational tools have demonstrated reproducible improvements in scheduling accuracy, bed-flow prediction, and transfusion optimization [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [42]. Global health applications further highlight AI's potential to improve equity by expanding access to imaging and enabling earlier disease detection [43], [44], [45]. Across domains, interpretability, workflow integration, and clinician trust—rather than accuracy alone—consistently shape adoption [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [35], [36], [46]. Systems co-designed with clinical teams and embedded directly into workflows show the greatest likelihood of sustained use. Nevertheless, performance reporting remains heterogeneous, with limited calibration assessment, inconsistent external validation, and underreporting of subgroup performance across studies.

Discussion

The findings of this review suggest that the barriers to AI adoption in cardiac surgery are less technical than organizational, regulatory, and human factors. Although AI has progressed from conceptual development to early clinical presence across diagnostic imaging, perioperative risk prediction, intraoperative support, and operational logistics, most systems continue to demonstrate their strongest performance in controlled research environments rather than in routine clinical practice [13], [16], [18], [23], [24], [25], [35], [36], [37], [46]. This persistent disconnect between laboratory performance and bedside usefulness defines the AI adoption gap in cardiac surgery [14], [15], [46].

To interpret progress in this field more clearly, it is helpful to distinguish between three related but distinct concepts. Technical maturity refers to model performance under validation conditions, including discrimination and robustness across datasets. Implementation maturity reflects the extent to which systems are embedded within clinical workflows and supported by governance structures, monitoring processes, and maintenance pathways. True clinical adoption requires sustained routine use in practice, with defined accountability and demonstrable impact on care delivery. Many AI tools in cardiac surgery have achieved technical maturity without yet progressing to implementation maturity or durable clinical adoption.

Several factors contribute to this gap. Cardiac surgery generates heterogeneous and rapidly evolving data streams, including high-frequency perfusion signals, multimodal imaging, operative video, and continuous physiologic monitoring [10], [16], [40], [41]. These data are difficult to standardize, synchronize, and annotate across institutions, limiting reproducibility and generalizability. Even when algorithmic performance is strong, adoption depends heavily on surgeon trust. Interpretability, workflow integration, and clarity of accountability often influence uptake more than accuracy metrics alone [12], [14], [15], [35], [36].

Progress is nonetheless evident. Diagnostic AI systems, particularly deep-learning echocardiography models, have advanced into prospective and randomized evaluations [23], [24], [25], [26], [28]. Machine-learning–based risk prediction tools increasingly match or exceed conventional surgical risk scores, although calibration assessment is reported inconsistently across studies [6], [11], [22], [35], [36], [37]. Operational applications, including case-duration prediction, transfusion forecasting, and bed-flow management, appear among the most mature and closest to clinical deployment [18], [19], [20], [21], [42]. Together, these developments suggest a gradual shift from feasibility demonstrations toward clearer identification of where AI can deliver reliable clinical value [14], [16], [46]. This trajectory mirrors prior transitions in cardiac surgery, in which early innovation was followed by normalization through validation, standardization, and clinician-led integration.

Across the literature, several principles help explain why some systems progress toward adoption while others stall. Successful models are supported by data infrastructures that match clinical ambition, with diverse and well-annotated datasets enabling generalization across institutions and surgical contexts [4], [5], [35], [36], [37]. Early and sustained clinician involvement is equally important. Tools co-designed with surgeons, perfusionists, anesthesiologists, and nursing staff are more likely to align with workflow demands, safety expectations, and time-critical decision-making [10], [12], [16], [38], [39]. Transparency also plays a central role, as systems that provide interpretable outputs, through feature attribution, explainable architectures, or explicit calibration reporting, are more readily trusted and more amenable to prospective validation [11], [15], [22], [35], [36]. When these elements align, AI functions not as a replacement for clinical judgment but as a supportive partner that augments decision-making, improves consistency, and reduces cognitive and operational burden [14], [15], [16], [18], [46].

Interpretation of findings

AI adoption in cardiac surgery appears to be advancing along two parallel pathways: clinical intelligence and operational intelligence. Clinical applications, including deep-learning echocardiography, multimodal perioperative risk prediction, and structural-heart simulation, offer opportunities to enhance diagnosis and preoperative planning [23], [24], [25], [30], [31], [32], [33], [35], [36], [37]. Parallel advances in thoracic surgery provide additional context. AI-assisted imaging interpretation, video-based surgical analytics, and risk stratification for lung resection and minimally invasive thoracic procedures highlight shared technical challenges and implementation barriers across cardiothoracic specialties [52], [53].

While clinical models demonstrate increasing accuracy, durability across institutions, workflows, and patient populations remains a central challenge [6], [11], [22], [35], [36], [37], [46]. Operational applications have progressed more rapidly. Predictive analytics for case duration, transfusion requirements, intensive care unit bed flow, and postoperative length of stay show consistent performance and appear better positioned for near-term implementation [10], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [35], [36], [37], [42]. Even in these domains, adoption faces non-technical barriers, including the cost and complexity of electronic health record integration, the risk of alert fatigue, and the need for information-technology governance to manage updates, downtime, and accountability.

Intraoperative AI remains in early development. Computer-vision systems capable of recognizing surgical phases or instrument movements show promise for documentation, training, and early error detection, but widespread use will require device integration, prospective validation, and clearly defined governance frameworks [16], [38], [39], [40], [41]. More broadly, integration failures often reflect a mismatch between technical design and clinical reality. Algorithms developed without early clinician input frequently overlook workflow variability, cognitive load, and time-critical decision pathways. In contrast, successful co-design involves clinicians in defining the problem, selecting meaningful outputs, and determining how recommendations are presented and acted upon. Overall, the evidence reflects a maturing field moving toward careful, incremental implementation rather than disruptive transformation [14], [15], [16], [46].

Ethical, regulatory, and human factors

Ethical and regulatory considerations remain central to the safe adoption of artificial intelligence in cardiac surgery. Models trained on narrow or homogeneous datasets risk reproducing or amplifying existing disparities, particularly when deployed across diverse populations and care settings [13], [14], [15], [43]. Evidence from rheumatic heart disease screening and global echocardiography initiatives demonstrates how inclusive dataset design can extend diagnostic capability to underserved populations [43], [44], [45]. However, while AI-enabled screening and diagnostic tools have shown equity-related benefits in selected contexts, evidence supporting downstream improvements in cardiac surgical outcomes remains limited, and many proposed advantages remain hypothesized and require prospective validation. These observations underscore equity not only as an ethical obligation but also as a core requirement for design and evaluation.

Regulatory frameworks are evolving in parallel with these ethical concerns. The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act introduces a risk-based classification system for medical AI, while the United States Food and Drug Administration continues to refine regulatory pathways for adaptive and continuously learning algorithms [47], [48], [49], [50]. Despite this progress, substantial challenges persist, including governance of model updates, monitoring of performance drift, and delineation of responsibility when algorithms influence clinical decisions. In semi-autonomous decision-support settings, unresolved questions remain regarding liability allocation among surgeons, healthcare institutions, and AI vendors, particularly when recommendations inform but do not dictate operative management.

The literature increasingly emphasizes the need for practical governance structures to translate ethical principles into operational safeguards. Proposed approaches include pre-deployment audits evaluating bias, fairness, and dataset representativeness; implementation protocols defining clinician responsibilities, escalation pathways, and override conditions; and post-deployment monitoring strategies that incorporate routine performance evaluation, calibration assessment, and drift detection. Such frameworks aim to ensure that AI systems remain safe, reliable, and aligned with clinical priorities throughout their lifecycle.

Human factors are equally influential in determining adoption. Surgeons value precision and efficiency but remain cautious when automation obscures reasoning, disrupts workflow, or threatens situational awareness. Prior analyses of medical AI highlight explainability, calibrated trust, and appropriate scepticism as prerequisites for safe and sustained use [14], [15], [17]. Multidisciplinary oversight committees, comprising surgeons, anesthesiologists, perfusionists, nursing staff, and informatics specialists, may further support the evaluation of the fairness, transparency, and long-term performance of deployed systems [10], [16], [47], [48], [49], [50]. In current clinical practice, AI tools function as decision-support systems, with ultimate responsibility remaining with the operating surgeon. Implications for practice

Near-term opportunities for clinical impact are most feasible in settings where data are structured, outcomes are measurable, and implementation risk is relatively low. Perioperative risk prediction, transfusion forecasting, scheduling accuracy, and postoperative bed planning exemplify domains in which AI can augment decision-making without fundamentally altering clinical workflows [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [35], [36], [37], [42].

Surgeon involvement remains essential. Systems co-designed with clinicians are more likely to address meaningful problems, align with workflow rhythms, and achieve sustained adoption [10], [12], [16], [38], [39]. Integration matters more than invention; tools must be embedded within existing infrastructures, including electronic health records, scheduling platforms, and hospital command centers, to ensure consistent use [10], [18], [19], [20], [21], [42], [46].

Limitations of the evidence

The reviewed literature shares several important limitations. Most studies are retrospective, include limited external validation, and demonstrate performance degradation when applied to new populations. Reporting of missing data, class imbalance, and calibration is inconsistent, and outcome definitions and evaluation metrics vary substantially across studies [6], [11], [13], [14], [15], [22], [35], [36], [37], [46]. Together, these factors precluded quantitative meta-analysis and necessitated a descriptive synthesis.

Many studies also rely primarily on discrimination metrics, particularly AUROC, when evaluating imbalanced but clinically critical outcomes such as mortality. While AUROC provides useful information on ranking performance, it does not capture absolute risk estimation. Calibration metrics were inconsistently reported or absent in most studies, limiting assessment of real-world clinical interpretability. Alternative measures, such as the F1-score or area under the precision–recall curve, may offer more informative insights into clinical utility in these settings.

Workflow and training implications

Cardiac surgery generates rich but underutilized data streams. Early AI systems can recognize operative phases, anticipate procedure duration, and flag early signs of hemodynamic instability [16], [18], [38], [39], [40], [41]. Video-based analytics and simulation platforms may soon offer objective assessments of technical and nontechnical performance [16], [38], [39]. Incorporating foundational AI literacy into surgical training may help future surgeons interpret outputs responsibly and recognize model failure modes [14], [15], [17], [46].

Future directions

Future progress will depend on collaborative data ecosystems, including multicenter networks and privacy-preserving approaches to data sharing [4], [5], [32], [37]. Advances will also require standardization through transparent reporting, shared benchmarks, and adherence to emerging surgical computer-vision guidelines [9], [12], [16], [38]. Governance frameworks incorporating bias audits, explainability testing, and drift monitoring will be essential to preserve safety and trust [15], [47], [48], [49], [50]. Explicit attention to equity is required to ensure representation of populations affected by rheumatic and infectious cardiac disease in training and validation datasets [43], [44], [45]. Long-term success will depend less on algorithmic novelty and more on reliable validation, clinician co-design, and governance structures that support safe clinical integration [14], [15], [16], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50]. Conclusion

Artificial intelligence has progressed from conceptual exploration to increasingly mature applications in cardiac surgery, particularly in diagnostic imaging, perioperative risk prediction, intraoperative monitoring, and operational management. Despite strong task-level performance in many domains, sustained adoption in routine clinical practice remains limited.

This adoption gap reflects challenges beyond algorithmic accuracy. Heterogeneous data environments, limited external validation, inconsistent reporting of calibration, workflow integration barriers, and evolving regulatory expectations continue to constrain real-world implementation. Many systems demonstrate technical maturity without having achieved implementation maturity or durable clinical adoption.

Bridging this gap will require reproducible validation frameworks, transparent reporting, and governance structures that clarify accountability and support ongoing performance monitoring. Equally important is clinician-led co-design to ensure alignment with surgical workflows, safety priorities, and the demands of decision-making. When implemented thoughtfully, AI has the potential to augment surgical judgment, improve efficiency, and support more consistent care delivery, while preserving the human expertise central to cardiac surgery.
