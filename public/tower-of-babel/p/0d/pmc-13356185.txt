Bimetallic nano mixtures enable rapid ultrasensitive detection of high risk HPV for early cervical cancer diagnosis
Authors: Mahsa Kiaee, Maliheh Entezari, Ali Jebali, Mehrdad Hashemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, particularly HPV16 and HPV18, is the primary etiological factor in cervical cancer development. The oncogenic potential of these viruses stems from the action of viral E6 and E7 oncoproteins, which drive malignant transformation by degrading tumor suppressor proteins p53 and pRb, disrupting cell cycle regulation, and inducing genomic instability [1, 2]. These mechanistic insights underscore the critical need for sensitive, rapid, and reliable diagnostic tools suitable for point-of-care settings to enable early detection and intervention.MethodsWe designed, synthesized, and evaluated bimetallic nano mixture-based lateral flow assays (LFAs) employing Au–Cu, Ag–Cu, and Ag–Au nano mixtures for detection of HPV16 L1 and HPV18 L1 capsid proteins. Nanoparticles were characterized by dynamic light scattering (DLS), zeta potential analysis, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, UV–Vis spectroscopy, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) before and after functionalization with CTAB and monoclonal antibodies. Analytical performance including linearity, limit of detection (LOD), limit of quantification (LOQ), accuracy, precision, specificity, and stability was systematically validated. Clinical evaluation was conducted using 100 cervical samples, with performance parameters calculated against PCR as reference standard. Additional experiments were performed to assess early infection detection capability, correlation with E6/E7 oncoprotein expression, and specificity in the presence of p53 and pRb tumor suppressor proteins.ResultsPhysicochemical characterization revealed successful antibody conjugation across all formulations, with predictable increases in hydrodynamic size (5–7 nm) and shifts toward less negative zeta potentials (−30 to −35 mV). Among monometallic systems, Au nanoparticles demonstrated the highest analytical sensitivity (LOD = 1 ng/mL), while Cu nanoparticles showed the poorest performance (LOD = 12 ng/mL). Remarkably, bimetallic nano mixtures dramatically outperformed monometallic counterparts, with Ag–Au nano mixtures achieving the lowest LOD (0.15 ng/mL), highest linearity (R2 = 0.998), and most rapid response time (10 min). Time-course experiments revealed that Ag–Au LFA detected infection as early as day 3 post-infection, 4 days before detection of integrated viral DNA. Among 20 PCR-positive clinical samples, 18 (90%) showed detectable E6/E7 oncoprotein expression, with strong correlations between LFA signal intensity and both E6 (r = 0.82) and E7 (r = 0.79) levels. No cross-reactivity was observed with p53, pRb, or E6/E7 oncoproteins. Clinical evaluation demonstrated that Ag–Au formulations achieved superior clinical performance (95% sensitivity, 91–92% specificity for both HPV targets), 96% positive predictive value (PPV), and 89–90% negative predictive value (NPV).ConclusionBimetallic nano mixture-based LFAs, particularly the Ag–Au formulation, represent a highly sensitive, specific, and rapid point-of-care diagnostic platform for HPV detection. By enabling early identification of high-risk HPV infection—the critical initiating event in cervical carcinogenesis driven by E6/E7-mediated disruption of p53 and pRb tumor suppressor pathways—this platform addresses a crucial need in cervical cancer prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04804-2.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, particularly with oncogenic genotypes 16 and 18, represents one of the leading etiological factors underlying cervical cancer on a global scale [1]. Cervical cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women in low- and middle-income countries, where limited access to screening and diagnostic services contributes to late-stage presentation and poor clinical outcomes [2]. Prompt identification of these high-risk HPV strains is essential for early clinical management and therapeutic intervention, thereby significantly improving patient prognosis [2].

Understanding the oncogenic mechanisms of high-risk HPV is fundamental to appreciating the clinical importance of early viral detection. Structurally, HPV is a small, non-enveloped virus exhibiting an icosahedral morphology with an approximate diameter of 55 nm. Its capsid is composed of 72 pentameric capsomers predominantly formed by the major capsid protein L1, with contributions from the minor capsid protein L2 [3]. Enclosed within this protein shell is a circular double-stranded DNA genome of approximately 8 kilobases, which encodes viral proteins critical for replication and cellular transformation. Among these, the E6 and E7 oncoproteins play a central role in malignant progression, particularly in high-risk HPV types such as HPV-16 and HPV-18 [1, 4].

The oncogenic potential of high-risk HPV genotypes is directly attributable to the functions of the E6 and E7 proteins, which cooperatively subvert host cell cycle control mechanisms to create a cellular environment permissive for viral replication [4]. The E7 oncoprotein binds to and promotes the proteasomal degradation of the retinoblastoma tumor suppressor protein (pRb), a master regulator of the G1/S cell cycle transition [5]. Under normal conditions, hypophosphorylated pRb sequesters E2F transcription factors, preventing expression of S-phase genes. E7-mediated pRb degradation liberates E2F, forcing quiescent differentiated epithelial cells—the natural host cells for HPV—into an inappropriate S-phase state that supports viral genome amplification [6]. Concurrently, the E6 oncoprotein targets the p53 tumor suppressor protein for ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation through its association with the E6-associated protein (E6AP) ubiquitin ligase [7]. p53, a critical mediator of DNA damage response and apoptosis, is normally stabilized in response to aberrant proliferative signals. By eliminating p53, E6 abrogates this protective response, allowing cells with accumulated DNA damage to evade apoptosis and continue proliferating [8]. The combined action of E6 and E7 thus disrupts two fundamental tumor suppressor pathways, leading to genomic instability, accumulation of mutations, and progressive cellular transformation [9]. This mechanistic understanding underscores why persistent infection with high-risk HPV types represents the necessary initiating event in cervical carcinogenesis, and why early detection of viral presence—whether through nucleic acid amplification or capsid protein detection—is clinically paramount for cancer prevention [10].

Although conventional diagnostic approaches such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) demonstrate excellent analytical sensitivity and specificity, their reliance on sophisticated instrumentation, extended processing times, and laboratory-based infrastructure limits their applicability in point-of-care and resource-constrained environments [11]. These limitations have stimulated considerable efforts toward the development of rapid, cost-effective, and user-friendly diagnostic platforms suitable for decentralized testing. Lateral flow assays (LFAs) have emerged as particularly attractive platforms for point-of-care diagnostics due to their simplicity, rapid time-to-result, and minimal infrastructure requirements [12, 13]. However, conventional LFAs often suffer from limited sensitivity, which restricts their utility for detecting low-abundance biomarkers such as HPV antigens in clinical specimens [13]. Recent advances in nanoparticle engineering have demonstrated that tailoring the physical parameters of noble metal nanoparticles—including size, shape, composition, and external structure—can significantly enhance the sensitivity of LFAs through improved optical, plasmonic, and catalytic properties [14].

Recent advances in nanotechnology have enabled the design of biosensing platforms based on nanostructured materials, offering new opportunities to overcome the shortcomings of conventional diagnostics. Nanomaterials such as gold nanoparticles (Au NPs), silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs), and copper nanoparticles (Cu NPs) have demonstrated significant potential for sensitive HPV detection [15, 16]. These nanoparticles exhibit distinctive surface plasmon resonance (SPR) phenomena, characterized by intense and tunable optical absorption and scattering properties that can be exploited for signal generation [17]. In the context of HPV detection, L1 capsid proteins—whether present on intact virions or as isolated antigens—can interact with functionalized nanoparticles, triggering aggregation and producing discernible colorimetric responses [15]. Various nanoparticle-based approaches for HPV detection have been reported, including Cys-Sso7d/AuNP systems integrated with PCR [18], label-free AuNP-based colorimetric assays [19], and magnetic nanowires with gold shells for enhanced sensitivity [20]. Despite these impressive analytical performances, many of these platforms remain dependent on sophisticated fabrication procedures, specialized equipment, or trained personnel, limiting their accessibility for widespread point-of-care implementation [21].

In recent years, bimetallic nano mixtures—combinations of two distinct metal nanoparticles such as Au–Cu, Ag–Cu, and Ag–Au—have emerged as promising alternatives to conventional monometallic nanoparticles, offering unique synergistic properties that arise from their mixed composition [17]. Link et al. demonstrated that alloy formation in gold-silver nanoparticles creates hybrid electronic structures that modify surface plasmon resonance properties, potentially enhancing optical signal generation compared to monometallic systems [17]. Unlike single-metal nanoparticles, these bimetallic nano mixtures can be engineered to simultaneously optimize multiple performance characteristics; for instance, while gold nanoparticles provide excellent biocompatibility and optical properties, the incorporation of silver enhances signal intensity, and copper offers the advantage of significantly lower material costs [22].

This study aimed to design, synthesize, and evaluate nano mixture-based lateral flow assays employing Au–Cu, Ag–Cu, and Ag–Au nano mixtures for colorimetric detection of HPV16 L1 and HPV18 L1 proteins. We systematically investigated the physicochemical characteristics of these bimetallic formulations before and after antibody conjugation, comprehensively validated their analytical performance, and evaluated clinical performance using patient cervical samples. Additionally, we performed mechanistic studies to establish the biological relevance of our detection platform, including assessment of early infection detection capability, correlation with E6/E7 oncoprotein expression, and specificity in the presence of p53 and pRb tumor suppressor proteins.

Materials and methods

Materials

We purchased Au, Ag, and Cu NPs (20–45 nm, colloidal, citrate stabilized) from Nanobio Paya Company of Iran. Hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), Tween® 80, and bovine serum albumin (BSA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Tween-20, anti-HPV16-E6/18-E6 antibody (sc-460), and anti-HPV16-E7 antibody (sc-6981) were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA). The recombinant proteins HPV16-L1 (ab119880), HPV16-E6 (ab226447), HPV16-E7 (ab237790), HPV18-L1 (ab119881), HPV18-E7 (ab236931), the monoclonal antibodies anti-HPV16-L1 (ab234305), anti-HPV18-L1 (ab267404), anti-HPV18-E7 (ab100953), recombinant HPV18-E6 protein (DAGF-097), recombinant proteins HPV6-L1 (HPV6), HPV11-L1 (HPV11), HPV33-L1 (HPV33), HPV45-L1 (HPV45), recombinant human p53 protein (ab166915), recombinant human pRb protein (ab168806), and SARS-CoV-2 B.1.1.529 (Omicron) Spike RBD were purchased from Abcam, Ltd. (Cambridge, UK). HPV16-infected keratinocyte cell lines (SIHA and CaSki) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA).

Monoclonal antibodies targeting the major capsid protein L1 were selected for this study because L1 is the most abundant and accessible viral antigen on the surface of intact HPV virions [3]. Importantly, detection of L1 protein enables identification of active viral infection prior to viral genome integration and expression of E6/E7 oncoproteins—the critical initiating events in cervical carcinogenesis [1, 4]. While the E6 and E7 oncoproteins themselves represent the direct mediators of malignant transformation through p53 degradation and pRb inactivation, their low abundance and intracellular localization make them less accessible for point-of-care antigen detection compared to the highly expressed capsid protein L1 [5]. Thus, L1 detection serves as an effective surrogate marker for the presence of high-risk HPV types capable of driving E6/E7-mediated oncogenesis.

Preparation of bimetallic nano mixtures

To prepare the bimetallic nano mixtures, equal volumes of the respective monometallic nanoparticle suspensions (1 µg/mL) were mixed at a 1:1 volumetric ratio. Three distinct bimetallic formulations were prepared for subsequent analyses: Au–Cu nano mixtures, Ag–Cu nano mixtures, and Ag–Au nano mixtures.

Stabilization and biofunctionalization of nano mixtures

For the selective detection of HPV capsid proteins, the prepared nano mixtures were first stabilized and then conjugated with monoclonal antibodies specific to HPV16 L1 and HPV18 L1 antigens.

Surfactant-Based Stabilization: To enhance colloidal stability, 1000 nL of each bimetallic nano mixture suspension was incubated with 20 nL of Tween® 80 (10% w/v) and vortexed for 10 s. The mixture was allowed to incubate for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the suspension was centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of ultrapure water. The sample was vortexed for 10 s and sonicated in an ultrasonic bath for 10 min to ensure complete dispersion. This washing step was repeated once more under identical centrifugation conditions.

Surface Charge Modification with CTAB: Following the washing steps, the nanoparticle pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, 1.0 mM) solution. The suspension was vortexed for 10 s and incubated for 10 min. To ensure thorough surface coating, the sample was vortexed for an additional 10 min and then allowed to stand undisturbed at room temperature for 30 min. The nanoparticles were then collected by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant containing excess CTAB was removed. The resulting CTAB-stabilized nanoparticle pellet was finally resuspended in 1 mL of ultrapure water.

Antibody Conjugation via EDC/NHS Covalent Coupling: For covalent immobilization of monoclonal antibodies onto the bimetallic nano mixtures, a carbodiimide-based crosslinking chemistry was employed using EDC (1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide) and NHS (N-hydroxysuccinimide). This method generates stable amide bonds between carboxylic acid groups on the CTAB-coated nanoparticle surface and primary amine groups on the antibody molecules. First, CTAB-stabilized bimetallic nano mixtures (1 mL, 1 µg/mL) were washed twice with 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid (MES) buffer (0.1 M, pH 5.5) by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove excess CTAB and adjust the pH for optimal EDC/NHS activity. The pellet was then resuspended in 1 mL of MES buffer (0.1 M, pH 5.5) containing 2 mM EDC and 5 mM NHS. The mixture was incubated for 30 min at room temperature with gentle shaking to activate the carboxylic acid groups on the nanoparticle surface, forming a semi-stable NHS-ester intermediate. Following activation, the nanoparticles were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.01 M, pH 7.4) by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove excess EDC and NHS. The activated nanoparticles were then resuspended in 1 mL of PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4) containing 10 µL of anti-HPV16-L1 or anti-HPV18-L1 monoclonal antibody (stock concentration: 1 mg/mL). The mixture was incubated for 2 h at room temperature with gentle shaking to allow covalent amide bond formation between the NHS-ester groups and primary amine groups (lysine residues) of the antibody molecules. To quench any remaining activated carboxyl groups and prevent non-specific binding, the reaction mixture was incubated with 20 µL of ethanolamine (1 M, pH 8.5) for 15 min at room temperature. The antibody-conjugated nanoparticles were then washed three times with PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4) containing 0.05% (v/v) Tween 20 by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove unbound antibodies and quenching reagents. The final pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of storage buffer (PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20, 1% bovine serum albumin, and 0.02% sodium azide) and stored at 4 °C until further use. The success of covalent conjugation was confirmed by measuring the antibody loading efficiency using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay on the supernatant collected after the final wash. The amount of immobilized antibody was calculated as the difference between the initial antibody added and the unbound antibody recovered in the combined wash supernatants.

Stability assessment of antibody-nanoparticle conjugates

To evaluate the stability of the antibody-nanoparticle conjugates under different environmental conditions, the prepared nano bioconjugates were subjected to various pH and temperature treatments, and their hydrodynamic size was monitored over time using dynamic light scattering (DLS). Briefly, aliquots of the final antibody-conjugated nano mixtures were separately adjusted to pH 5.0, 7.4, and 9.0 using appropriate buffers (acetate buffer for pH 5.0, phosphate-buffered saline for pH 7.4, and bicarbonate buffer for pH 9.0), and each pH-adjusted sample was then incubated at three different temperatures (4 °C, 25 °C, and 37 °C) for up to 24 h. At predetermined time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h), 100 µL aliquots were withdrawn from each sample, and the hydrodynamic diameter (Z-average) and polydispersity index (PDI) were measured in triplicate using a DLS instrument equipped with temperature control. As a control, unconjugated CTAB-coated nanoparticles without antibodies were incubated under identical conditions (pH 7.4, 25 °C) to assess the effect of the buffer itself on nanoparticle stability. A significant increase in hydrodynamic size or PDI compared to the initial measurement was interpreted as aggregation indicative of antibody desorption and conjugate instability, while minimal size changes confirmed stable conjugation under the tested conditions.

Fabrication of lateral flow assay (LFA) strips

The lateral flow assay strips were constructed using Whatman paper as the absorbent pad. The paper was cut into strips measuring 1 cm in width and 10 cm in length. For the preparation of the conjugate pad, 10 µL of the antibody-conjugated nano mixture was dispensed onto the center of each Whatman strip and allowed to dry at room temperature. Separately, nitrocellulose membranes were coated with 10 µL of capture antibodies (anti-HPV16-L1 or anti-HPV18-L1) at a concentration of 1 mg/mL to form the test line. The coated membranes were dried at 37 °C for 60 min. Once dried, the nitrocellulose membrane was carefully affixed to the Whatman paper strip, ensuring proper overlap for continuous capillary flow.

HPV detection assay

To perform the detection, 50 µL of sample (containing either HPV16 L1 or HPV18 L1 antigen at various concentrations) was loaded onto the sample pad of the prepared LFA strips. The results were interpreted visually after 5–10 min, and quantitative analysis was performed by measuring the intensity of the test line using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Assessment of early infection detection capability

To evaluate whether the developed LFA could detect HPV infection prior to viral genome integration—the critical step that enables sustained E6/E7 expression and malignant transformation—we performed time-course experiments using HPV16-infected keratinocyte cell lines (SIHA and CaSki) at different stages of infection. Cells were harvested at days 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 14 post-infection, and lysates were prepared by three freeze–thaw cycles followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min to remove cellular debris. L1 protein concentration in each lysate was measured using our Ag-Au nano mixture-based LFA and compared with quantitative PCR detection of HPV16 DNA (targeting E6/E7 genes) as reference standard. The ability to detect L1 protein before detection of integrated viral DNA was considered indicative of early infection diagnosis capability. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Evaluation of E6/E7 oncoprotein expression in clinical samples

To establish a direct link between our LFA results (detecting L1 capsid protein) and the oncogenic potential of detected HPV infections (mediated by E6/E7 oncoproteins), we performed additional analyses on a subset of clinical samples. From the 100 cervical samples, 20 PCR-positive samples (10 HPV16-positive and 10 HPV18-positive) and 10 PCR-negative samples were randomly selected for E6/E7 oncoprotein expression analysis. Total protein was extracted from cervical cell pellets using RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors, and concentrations of HPV16 E6, HPV16 E7, HPV18 E6, and HPV18 E7 proteins were measured using commercial ELISA kits (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) according to manufacturer's instructions. The correlation between LFA signal intensity (based on L1 detection) and E6/E7 oncoprotein levels was analyzed using Pearson correlation coefficient. Samples with detectable E6/E7 proteins were considered to represent infections with active oncogenic potential, as E6/E7 expression is required for malignant transformation through p53 degradation and pRb inactivation [4, 7].

Evaluation of interference from tumor suppressor proteins

To ensure that our LFA specifically detects HPV L1 protein without interference from host cellular proteins involved in the p53 and pRb tumor suppressor pathways—which are targeted by HPV E6 and E7 oncoproteins during carcinogenesis—we evaluated potential cross-reactivity with recombinant human p53 and pRb proteins (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Ag-Au nano mixture-based LFAs were tested with p53 protein (1 ng/mL and 10 ng/mL), pRb protein (1 ng/mL and 10 ng/mL), and a mixture of HPV16 L1 (1 ng/mL) with p53 and pRb proteins (each at 10 ng/mL). Test line intensities were measured and compared with control samples containing only HPV16 L1. Absence of test line signal in p53-only and pRb-only samples, and comparable signal intensity in mixed samples versus L1-only samples, confirmed the specificity of our assay for viral capsid protein in the presence of host tumor suppressor proteins.

Control experiments with recombinant E6 and E7 oncoproteins

Although our LFA targets L1 capsid protein and not the E6/E7 oncoproteins, we included recombinant HPV16 E6, HPV16 E7, HPV18 E6, and HPV18 E7 proteins in our selectivity panel to confirm that these oncoproteins—which are the direct mediators of p53 degradation and pRb inactivation—do not cross-react with the anti-L1 antibodies used in our assay. Each oncoprotein was tested at concentrations of 1 ng/mL and 10 ng/mL, and test line intensity was measured. Absence of signal confirmed that our assay specifically detects the L1 capsid protein and not the intracellular oncoproteins, ensuring that positive results indicate presence of intact virions rather than accidental detection of host cells expressing viral oncogenes.

Analytical validation of the nanoparticle-based assay

The analytical performance of the colorimetric assay was systematically validated by assessing key parameters, including linearity, sensitivity, precision, accuracy, and selectivity.

Linearity assessment: Linearity was evaluated by generating calibration curves across defined concentration ranges for each HPV genotype. For the detection of HPV16-L1 and HPV18-L1, recombinant protein concentrations spanning 0.4–1.0 ng/mL were employed. Three independent calibration curves were generated on separate days, each measured in duplicate, to ensure reproducibility. Finally, both linearity (R2) and linear range were measured.

Sensitivity assessment: The analytical sensitivity of the nanoparticle-based colorimetric assay was evaluated by determining the limit of blank (LoB) and limit of detection (LoD). To calculate LoB, calibration curves were generated at low concentrations of recombinant HPV16-L1 and HPV18-L1 proteins (0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8 ng/mL). The blank response was estimated by extrapolating these data points to zero concentration to obtain the mean blank signal (mean_blank). The standard deviation at zero concentration (SD_blank) was similarly obtained from the extrapolated standard deviation versus concentration plot. All sensitivity determinations were performed using three independent calibration curves on separate days, with duplicate measurements for each data point. The LoB and LoD were then calculated using standard formulas.

Precision evaluation: Precision of the assay was assessed by repeated measurement of three recombinant protein concentrations: 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2 ng/mL. For each concentration, HPV16-L1 and HPV18-L1 proteins were added to the nano mixture suspension to reach the desired level. Each concentration was measured five times per experiment across three separate days using calibration curves generated between 0.5 and 2.0 ng/mL. Intra-assay and inter-assay precision were quantified by calculating the relative standard deviation (RSD).

Accuracy evaluation: Accuracy was determined by comparing the mean measured concentration of recombinant HPV16-L1 protein to the nominal reference values (0.7, 1.0, and 1.5 ng/mL). Statistical analysis was performed by calculating the observed t-value (t_obs). The assay was considered accurate if t_obs < t_(n-1) at a significance level of 0.05.

Selectivity assessment: The selectivity of the nanoparticle-based assay was investigated by evaluating its ability to discriminate the target HPV protein from structurally or functionally related proteins. Nano mixtures were exposed to various recombinant proteins, including HPV16-E6, HPV16-E7, HPV18-E6, HPV18-E7, HPV16-L1, HPV18-L1, HPV6-L1, HPV11-L1, HPV33-L1, HPV45-L1, SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, bovine serum albumin (BSA), p53, and pRb, each at a concentration of 1 ng/mL. Following a 1-h incubation, five independent replicates were performed for each protein to ensure reproducibility of selectivity measurements.

Limit of blank (LOB), limit of detection (LOD), and limit of quantification (LOQ) calculation

The limit of blank (LOB), limit of detection (LOD), and limit of quantification (LOQ) were determined according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) EP17-A2 guidelines.

First, blank samples (zero concentration of recombinant HPV16-L1 and HPV18-L1 proteins in assay buffer) were measured in 20 replicates to establish the mean blank signal (Mean_blank) and standard deviation of the blank (SD_blank). The LOB was then calculated using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ {\text{LOB }} = {\text{ Mean}}\_{\text{blank }} + { 1}.{645 } \times {\text{ SD}}\_{\mathrm{blank}} $$\end{document}

The factor 1.645 corresponds to the 95th percentile of a normal distribution, ensuring that 95% of blank measurements fall below the LOB.

To determine the LOD, low-concentration samples (prepared at concentrations near the expected detection limit: 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4 ng/mL) were measured in 20 replicates each. The standard deviation of the low-concentration samples (SD_low) was calculated for each concentration level. The LOD was then calculated using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ {\text{LOD }} = {\text{ LOB }} + { 1}.{645 } \times {\text{ SD}}\_{\mathrm{low}} $$\end{document}where SD_low represents the standard deviation of the lowest concentration sample that consistently produced a detectable signal above the LOB. The factor 1.645 maintains the 95% confidence level.

For the LOQ, two approaches were applied. First, the LOQ was estimated as the lowest concentration at which the relative standard deviation (RSD) was ≤ 20% and the signal-to-noise ratio was ≥ 10:1. Second, the LOQ was calculated using the following formula based on the calibration curve:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ {\text{LOQ }} = 10{\mkern 1mu} \times {\text{ SD}}\_{\mathrm{blank}}/{\mathrm{S}} $$\end{document}where S is the slope of the calibration curve obtained from linear regression analysis of test line intensity versus protein concentration. The more conservative (higher) value from both approaches was reported as the final LOQ. All calculations were performed using three independent calibration curves generated on separate days, with 20 replicate measurements for each data point. Results are presented as mean ± SD.

Stability evaluation

The temporal stability of the nanoparticle-based assay was assessed over 90 days. Nanoparticles functionalized with anti-HPV16-L1 antibodies were stored at 4 °C, and both particle size and absorbance at 590 nm were monitored at defined intervals: days 0, 1, 5, and every five days thereafter until day 90. To assess retention of assay functionality over time, the detection capability was periodically evaluated by measuring the absorbance following addition of 2 ng/mL recombinant HPV16-L1 and HPV18-L1 proteins.

Long-term stability assessment of nanoparticle-based LFAs

The long-term colloidal and functional stability of the antibody-conjugated mono- and bimetallic nanoparticles was evaluated over a period of 90 days under controlled storage conditions. All nanoparticle formulations (Au NPs, Ag NPs, Cu NPs, Au-Cu, Ag–Cu, and Ag-Au nano mixtures) were conjugated with anti-HPV16 L1 and anti-HPV18 L1 antibodies as described and stored at 4 °C in ultrapure water containing 0.05% (v/v) Tween 20. At predetermined time intervals (days 0, 1, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 55, 60, 65, 70, 75, 80, 85, and 90), aliquots were withdrawn from each stored sample for analysis. Colloidal stability was assessed by measuring hydrodynamic size (Z-average) and polydispersity index (PDI) using dynamic light scattering (DLS) at 25 °C. Functional stability was evaluated by assessing the antigen detection capability of the stored antibody-conjugated nanoparticles at each time point. Additionally, visual inspection of the colloidal suspension color and absence of visible precipitates were recorded at each time point as qualitative indicators of stability.

Clinical evaluation of the nanoparticle-based assay

Clinical performance was tested using 100 cervical samples obtained from different hospitals in Iran. Cervical cells were collected via cytobrush sampling while patients were positioned on an examination table. The brushes were subsequently immersed in 15 mL of 1% Tween 20 solution, gently homogenized, left at room temperature for 20 min, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. For assay application, 10 nL of the sample was added to each designed LFA.

Clinical specificity, sensitivity, negative predictive value (NPV), and positive predictive value (PPV) were calculated in comparison with polymerase chain reaction (PCR) results obtained by an external laboratory using samples collected concurrently. Test outcomes were classified as follows: true negatives (TN) corresponded to samples negative by both PCR and nanoparticle assay; false positives (FP) were negative by PCR but positive by nanoparticle assay; true positives (TP) were positive by both methods; and false negatives (FN) were positive by PCR but negative by nanoparticle assay.

All experiments on human samples were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations (Helsinki Declaration) and approved by the Ethics Committee of TMS.C., Islamic Azad University, Tehran, Iran (Ethical code: IR.IAU.PS.REC.1403.525). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and confidentiality and anonymization of patient data were strictly maintained.

Statistical analysis

Normality of datasets was assessed using the D'Agostino-K2 test. Comparisons between two groups were conducted with paired-sample t-tests, whereas comparisons involving more than two groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post-hoc test. To investigate the relationship between LFA signal intensity and viral oncogenic potential, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between LFA signal intensity, HPV DNA copy number, and E6/E7 oncoprotein levels in the subset of 30 clinical samples. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Physicochemical characterization of mono- and bimetallic nanoparticles before and after antibody conjugation

FTIR spectroscopic analysis of functionalized nanoparticles before and after antibody conjugation

The successful surface modification and antibody conjugation of mono- and bimetallic nanoparticles were further confirmed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. FTIR spectra were recorded for all formulations in both unconjugated and conjugated states to identify functional groups and verify the presence of surfactant coatings and antibody molecules (Supplementary 1).

For unconjugated nanoparticles (Au NPs, Ag NPs, Cu NPs, and all bimetallic mixtures), the FTIR spectra exhibited three common features: a broad absorption band at 3420–3450 cm⁻1 corresponding to O–H stretching vibrations from surface hydroxyl groups and adsorbed water molecules, a medium intensity band at 1630–1640 cm⁻1 attributed to O–H bending of adsorbed water, and a weak band at approximately 1380 cm⁻1 associated with C–O stretching vibrations from the citrate capping agent used during nanoparticle synthesis. In the case of copper-containing nanoparticles (Cu NPs, Au-Cu, and Ag–Cu nano mixtures), an additional weak and broad absorption band was observed at 600–650 cm⁻1, corresponding to Cu–O stretching vibrations, which indicates the presence of a thin surface oxide layer on copper nanoparticles, consistent with their higher susceptibility to oxidation.

Following functionalization with CTAB and conjugation with anti-HPV monoclonal antibodies, significant changes in the FTIR spectra were observed across all formulations, confirming successful surface modification. The most prominent evidence of successful CTAB coating was the appearance of two sharp absorption bands at 2925 cm⁻1 and 2854 cm⁻1, corresponding to asymmetric and symmetric C-H stretching vibrations of the long alkyl chains present in CTAB molecules. These bands were observed in all conjugated samples with medium to strong intensity, confirming uniform surfactant coating on nanoparticle surfaces. Additionally, a weak to medium band at 1465–1470 cm⁻1, attributed to C-H bending vibrations of CTAB methylene groups, further supported the presence of the surfactant layer.

Crucially, the successful immobilization of antibodies was confirmed by the appearance of two characteristic amide bands in all conjugated samples. The amide I band, observed at 1650–1660 cm⁻1, arises from C=O stretching vibrations of the peptide bonds in antibody molecules, while the amide II band at 1540–1550 cm⁻1 corresponds to N–H bending coupled with C-N stretching. The presence of these amide bands exclusively in conjugated samples, and their absence in unconjugated controls, provides direct evidence of successful antibody adsorption onto the nanoparticle surfaces.

Notably, the intensity and resolution of amide bands varied among different formulations, suggesting differences in antibody loading efficiency. Ag-Au nano mixtures exhibited the strongest and most well-resolved amide I and amide II bands among all bimetallic formulations, indicating higher antibody loading capacity and more uniform antibody orientation. In contrast, copper-containing nanoparticles (Cu NPs, Au-Cu, and Ag–Cu) showed relatively weaker amide bands, which may reflect lower antibody adsorption efficiency or partial desorption during sample preparation, consistent with their lower colloidal stability observed in DLS measurements and stability assessment.

The O–H stretching band at 3420–3450 cm⁻1 became broader in conjugated samples due to overlapping contributions from N–H stretching vibrations of antibody amino groups, further confirming protein adsorption. The citrate-related band at 1380 cm⁻1 decreased in intensity after conjugation, suggesting partial displacement of citrate molecules by CTAB and antibodies during the functionalization process. Overall, the FTIR spectroscopic analysis provides conclusive evidence for the successful stepwise functionalization of mono- and bimetallic nanoparticles with CTAB surfactant and anti-HPV antibodies, with Ag-Au nano mixtures demonstrating the most efficient antibody immobilization.

XPS analysis of nanoparticle conjugates

The surface chemical composition and electronic states of monometallic and bimetallic nanoparticle conjugates were characterized using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The binding energies, full width at half maximum (FWHM) values, atomic percentages, and chemical states are summarized in Supplementary 2.

For monometallic nanoparticles, the Au 4f7/2 spectrum of Au NPs exhibited a single peak at 84.0 ± 0.1 eV with a FWHM of 1.1 ± 0.1 eV, corresponding to metallic gold (Au⁰). Similarly, Ag NPs showed the Ag 3d5/2 peak at 368.3 ± 0.1 eV (FWHM: 1.2 ± 0.1 eV), confirming the presence of metallic silver (Ag⁰). The Cu 2p3/2 spectrum of Cu NPs revealed a peak at 932.5 ± 0.2 eV (FWHM: 1.8 ± 0.2 eV), indicating a mixed chemical state consisting of approximately 70% metallic copper (Cu⁰) and 30% oxidized copper (Cu2+), as evidenced by characteristic satellite peaks.

For bimetallic nano mixtures, the Au-Cu sample displayed Au 4f7/2 at 84.1 ± 0.1 eV and Cu 2p3/2 at 932.6 ± 0.2 eV, with atomic compositions of 42 ± 3% Au and 58 ± 4% Cu. The Ag–Cu sample exhibited Ag 3d5/2 at 368.4 ± 0.1 eV and Cu 2p3/2 at 932.7 ± 0.2 eV, with atomic ratios of 45 ± 3% Ag and 55 ± 4% Cu. The Ag-Au sample showed Ag 3d5/2 at 368.2 ± 0.1 eV and Au 4f7/2 at 83.9 ± 0.1 eV, with nearly equal atomic percentages of 52 ± 3% Ag and 48 ± 3% Au.

Notably, no significant binding energy shifts were observed between monometallic and bimetallic formulations, suggesting the absence of strong electronic interactions or alloy formation at the interface. The presence of Cu2+ species in all copper-containing samples (both monometallic and bimetallic) is attributed to surface oxidation upon exposure to ambient conditions, which is consistent with previous reports on copper-based nanomaterials.

UV–Vis spectroscopic analysis of mono- and bimetallic nanoparticles before and after antibody conjugation

The optical properties of mono- and bimetallic nanoparticles were characterized by UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy before and after antibody conjugation, with surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak wavelengths, absorbance values, and spectral shifts summarized in Supplementary 3. UV–Vis analysis provides valuable insights into nanoparticle size, dispersion state, and surface modifications, as the SPR band is highly sensitive to changes in local refractive index caused by biomolecule adsorption.

Unconjugated Au NPs exhibited a characteristic SPR peak at 520–525 nm with absorbance of 0.85 ± 0.05 and narrow peak width, confirming the presence of well-dispersed spherical nanoparticles with uniform size distribution, consistent with DLS measurements (Table 2). Similarly, unconjugated Ag NPs showed their characteristic SPR peak at 400–410 nm with absorbance of 0.82 ± 0.06, typical for silver nanoparticles of 20–35 nm diameter. In contrast, unconjugated Cu NPs displayed a broader and weaker SPR peak at 560–580 nm with absorbance of only 0.62 ± 0.08, reflecting their larger size distribution, higher polydispersity, and partial surface oxidation, which dampens the plasmonic response.

For bimetallic nano mixtures, the UV–Vis spectra exhibited bimodal SPR features characteristic of their dual-metal composition. Unconjugated Au-Cu nano mixtures showed a primary SPR peak at 525–530 nm corresponding to the gold component, with a secondary shoulder at 560–575 nm attributable to copper nanoparticles. Similarly, Ag–Cu nano mixtures displayed two distinct SPR features: a sharp peak at 405–415 nm from silver nanoparticles and a broader shoulder at 560–575 nm from copper. Notably, Ag-Au nano mixtures exhibited the most well-resolved bimodal spectrum, with distinct SPR peaks at 410–420 nm (silver) and 525–535 nm (gold), indicating minimal electronic interaction or alloy formation between the two metals and preservation of their individual plasmonic properties.

Following antibody conjugation, all nanoparticle formulations exhibited red shifts in their SPR peak positions, confirming successful surface modification through changes in local refractive index around the nanoparticles. Au NPs showed a moderate red shift of approximately 5 nm (from 520–525 to 525–530 nm) with a slight decrease in absorbance (0.85 to 0.78) and minor peak broadening, indicating uniform antibody coating without significant aggregation. Ag NPs exhibited a slightly larger red shift of 8 nm (from 400–410 to 408–418 nm), consistent with their higher refractive index sensitivity compared to gold.

Cu NPs displayed the most pronounced spectral changes after conjugation, with a broad red shift of 10–15 nm and significant peak broadening, accompanied by a substantial decrease in absorbance (from 0.62 to 0.55). These changes indicate partial aggregation during conjugation, consistent with the lower colloidal stability of copper nanoparticles observed in DLS and stability assessments (Tables 2 and 6). The bimetallic nano mixtures showed intermediate behavior: Au-Cu and Ag–Cu formulations exhibited red shifts of 5–12 nm with moderate peak broadening, while maintaining their bimodal spectral features, indicating successful conjugation of both metal components.

Remarkably, Ag-Au nano mixtures demonstrated the most favorable optical characteristics after conjugation, with well-resolved bimodal SPR peaks red-shifted by 8–10 nm (silver component from 410–420 to 418–428 nm; gold component from 525–535 to 532–542 nm) while maintaining excellent peak resolution and minimal broadening. The absorbance decreased only slightly (from 0.80 to 0.72), and the distinct bimodal nature of the spectrum was fully preserved, indicating uniform antibody coating on both metal surfaces without inducing aggregation or inter-particle coupling. This exceptional optical stability suggests that Ag-Au nano mixtures provide an optimal surface for antibody immobilization while maintaining colloidal integrity, which likely contributes to their superior analytical and clinical performance.

Stability assessment of antibody-conjugated nanoparticles under different pH and temperature conditions

The colloidal stability of antibody-conjugated mono- and bimetallic nanoparticles was systematically evaluated under various pH and temperature conditions to simulate potential environmental stresses during storage and application. The hydrodynamic size and polydispersity index (PDI) of each formulation were monitored over 24 h at pH 5.0, 7.4, and 9.0, combined with temperatures of 4 °C, 25 °C, and 37 °C (Supplementary 4).

As expected, all nanoparticle formulations exhibited optimal stability at physiological pH (7.4) and refrigeration temperature (4 °C), with minimal size increases (< 5%) and negligible PDI changes over 24 h. Under these conditions, Au NPs, Ag NPs, Au-Cu nano mixtures, and Ag–Cu nano mixtures maintained their initial size of 32 ± 2 nm with PDI values of 0.25–0.26, while Ag-Au nano mixtures showed exceptional stability with only 2.5% size increase (from 40 ± 2 to 41 ± 2 nm) and unchanged PDI (0.35 ± 0.02). Cu NPs, despite their larger initial size (40 ± 3 nm) and higher PDI (0.30 ± 0.02), also demonstrated acceptable stability at pH 7.4 and 4 °C with only 5% size increase.

Increasing the temperature to 25 °C at physiological pH resulted in mild size increases (6.3–12.5%) for most formulations, with Cu NPs showing the highest sensitivity (12.5% increase) and Ag-Au nano mixtures the lowest (5.0% increase). At 37 °C and pH 7.4, all copper-containing nanoparticles (Cu NPs, Au-Cu, and Ag–Cu) exhibited moderate to significant aggregation, with size increases of 25–35%, while Ag-Au nano mixtures maintained remarkable stability with only 15% size increase and PDI change from 0.35 to 0.39.

The most challenging conditions were observed at acidic pH (5.0) combined with elevated temperature (37 °C), where severe aggregation occurred in several formulations. Cu NPs showed the poorest stability under these conditions, with size increasing by 70% (from 40 ± 3 to 68 ± 6 nm) and PDI reaching 0.55 ± 0.05, indicating extensive aggregation and probable antibody desorption. Au-Cu and Ag–Cu nano mixtures also exhibited significant instability at pH 5.0 and 37 °C, with size increases of 50% and 56.3%, respectively. In contrast, Ag-Au nano mixtures demonstrated superior acid tolerance, with only 30% size increase under the same harsh conditions, representing the best stability among all tested formulations at low pH and high temperature.

At alkaline pH (9.0) and 37 °C, a similar trend was observed, with copper-containing nanoparticles showing pronounced aggregation (43.8–62.5% size increase), while Ag-Au nano mixtures maintained moderate stability with 25% size increase. Control experiments using unconjugated CTAB-coated nanoparticles (without antibodies) at pH 7.4 and 25 °C showed less than 5% size change over 24 h, confirming that the observed aggregation in conjugated samples resulted from antibody desorption or conformational changes rather than inherent nanoparticle instability.

Overall, the stability assessment revealed a clear hierarchy of robustness among the tested formulations: Ag-Au nano mixtures > Au NPs ≈ Ag NPs > Au-Cu nano mixtures ≈ Ag–Cu nano mixtures > Cu NPs. The superior stability of Ag-Au nano mixtures across all pH and temperature conditions can be attributed to their optimized surface chemistry, uniform antibody coating, and the synergistic stabilizing effects of both gold and silver components. These findings confirm that all bimetallic formulations, particularly Ag-Au nano mixtures, possess adequate colloidal stability for practical diagnostic applications under typical storage (4 °C, pH 7.4) and operating conditions (25 °C, pH 7.4).

Long-term stability of antibody-conjugated mono- and bimetallic nanoparticles

The long-term colloidal and functional stability of antibody-conjugated mono- and bimetallic nanoparticles was evaluated over 90 days of storage at 4 °C. All nanoparticle formulations maintained acceptable colloidal stability throughout the 90-day storage period, with varying degrees of size increase and PDI changes. Ag-Au nano mixtures demonstrated exceptional stability, exhibiting minimal size increase from 40 ± 2 nm at day 0 to 41 ± 2 nm at day 90 (only + 2.5% change), with PDI remaining virtually unchanged (0.35 ± 0.02 to 0.36 ± 0.02). Au NPs and Ag NPs showed good stability with moderate size increases of 6.3% and 9.4%, respectively, after 90 days. Au–Cu and Ag–Cu nano mixtures demonstrated intermediate stability, with size increases of 9.4% and 12.5%, respectively. Cu NPs exhibited the poorest stability, with a size increase of 15.0% and PDI increasing from 0.30 to 0.35. Functional stability assessment revealed that Ag–Au nano mixtures retained the highest detection activity after 90 days (96.8% of initial), followed by Au–Cu nano mixtures (92.0%), Ag–Cu nano mixtures (91.9%), Au NPs (91.8%), Ag NPs (90.2%), and Cu NPs (82.3%). All formulations except Cu NPs met the predefined criterion of ≥ 90% retained activity for acceptable diagnostic reagent stability (Supplementary 5).

Correlation between LFA signal intensity and E6/E7 oncoprotein expression

To establish a direct link between LFA results and the oncogenic potential of detected HPV infections, E6 and E7 oncoprotein levels were measured in a subset of clinical samples. Discussion

The present study reports the development and comprehensive evaluation of bimetallic nano mixture-based lateral flow assays (LFAs) for the rapid and ultrasensitive detection of HPV16 and HPV18 L1 proteins. The strategic combination of two distinct metals within a single nano mixture platform—specifically Au–Cu, Ag–Cu, and Ag–Au formulations—was systematically investigated to harness synergistic properties that could overcome the inherent limitations of conventional monometallic nanoparticle-based diagnostics. Our findings demonstrate that bimetallic nano mixtures, particularly the Ag–Au formulation, exhibit superior analytical and clinical performance compared to their monometallic counterparts, achieving limits of detection as low as 0.15 ng/mL, response times of 10 min, and clinical sensitivity of 95% with specificity exceeding 91%.

The exceptional clinical performance of our bimetallic nano mixture-based LFAs must be interpreted within the broader context of HPV biology and cervical carcinogenesis. As detailed in the introduction, persistent infection with high-risk HPV genotypes, particularly HPV16 and HPV18, constitutes the necessary initiating event in cervical cancer development through the well-characterized actions of viral E6 and E7 oncoproteins [1, 4]. The E6-mediated degradation of p53 and E7-mediated inactivation of pRb represent the fundamental molecular lesions that drive genomic instability, abrogate cell cycle control, and ultimately lead to malignant transformation [5–9]. Given this mechanistic understanding, the clinical utility of any HPV diagnostic platform ultimately derives from its ability to identify individuals harboring persistent high-risk infections before cellular transformation has occurred, thereby enabling timely intervention and cancer prevention [10].

A key finding of this study is the demonstration that our Ag-Au LFA detects HPV infection during the early pre-integrative phase, 4 days before detection of integrated viral DNA (Table 5). This is clinically crucial because viral genome integration into the host chromosome represents a critical milestone in cervical carcinogenesis—it is associated with increased stability of E6/E7 oncogene expression, loss of viral replication control, and progression to high-grade cervical lesions [9]. The ability to detect infection prior to integration provides a window of opportunity for intervention before the establishment of persistent E6/E7 expression and the associated disruption of p53 and pRb tumor suppressor pathways.

The strong correlation observed between LFA signal intensity (reflecting L1 capsid protein concentration) and E6/E7 oncoprotein levels (r = 0.82 for E6, r = 0.79 for E7; Tables 6 and 9) is particularly noteworthy. This correlation indicates that our LFA not only detects the presence of HPV but also provides semi-quantitative information about the expression level of the oncoproteins directly responsible for malignant transformation. Higher L1 detection correlates with higher E6/E7 expression, which in turn is associated with greater degradation of p53 and pRb and thus greater oncogenic risk [4, 9].

The absence of cross-reactivity with p53 and pRb tumor suppressor proteins (Table 7) confirms that our assay specifically detects viral antigen without interference from the very host proteins targeted by E6 and E7 during carcinogenesis. Similarly, the absence of cross-reactivity with recombinant E6 and E7 oncoproteins (Table 8) confirms that our assay specifically detects the L1 capsid protein of intact virions and not intracellular oncoproteins that might be released from damaged cells.

The analytical validation revealed a striking hierarchy of performance among the investigated nanoparticle formulations, with bimetallic nano mixtures dramatically outperforming their monometallic counterparts across all evaluated parameters. The Ag–Au nano mixture achieved the most impressive performance (LOD = 0.15–0.16 ng/mL, LOQ = 0.50 ng/mL), representing a 6.7-fold improvement over monometallic Au nanoparticles and an 80-fold improvement over monometallic Cu nanoparticles. This dramatic enhancement can be attributed to several synergistic mechanisms inherent to bimetallic systems. First, the combination of two distinct metals creates hybrid electronic structures that modify surface plasmon resonance properties, potentially amplifying optical signal generation upon nanoparticle aggregation [17]. Second, the presence of two different metal surfaces may provide heterogeneous binding sites that enhance antibody orientation and accessibility [22]. Third, bimetallic nanoparticles often exhibit enhanced catalytic activity and surface reactivity compared to their monometallic constituents [23].

A particularly intriguing finding of this study is the dissociation between analytical sensitivity and clinical performance observed for certain nanoparticle formulations. While Au nanoparticles demonstrated superior analytical sensitivity among monometallic systems (LOD = 1 ng/mL), they exhibited the poorest clinical sensitivity (80–81%) when applied to actual patient samples. Conversely, Cu nanoparticles, despite their inferior analytical performance (LOD = 12 ng/mL), achieved the highest clinical sensitivity among monometallic systems (92% for HPV-16, 84% for HPV-18). This apparent paradox highlights the critical distinction between controlled buffer-based analytical validation and real-world clinical performance, and underscores the importance of evaluating diagnostic platforms under conditions that recapitulate the complexity of biological specimens. The bimetallic nano mixtures successfully bridged this analytical-clinical performance gap, with all three formulations demonstrating excellent clinical sensitivity (90–95%) that correlated well with their superior analytical characteristics.

The analytical and clinical performance achieved by our bimetallic nano mixture-based LFAs compares favorably with existing HPV diagnostic technologies. Conventional PCR-based methods, while highly sensitive and specific, require sophisticated instrumentation, trained personnel, and processing times of several hours [11]. Commercial HPV tests achieve excellent clinical performance but remain laboratory-based and relatively expensive [21]. Among nanoparticle-based approaches, our Ag–Au nano mixture LFA (LOD = 0.15 ng/mL, 10 min response time) substantially outperforms previously reported colorimetric assays, such as those by Chica et al. [24], Gao et al. [25], and Barra et al. [26].

The 95% clinical sensitivity and 91–92% specificity achieved by our Ag–Au nano mixture LFA meet or exceed the performance targets established by the World Health Organization for point-of-care HPV screening tests, which recommend minimum sensitivity of 90% for cervical cancer screening applications [27].

Despite the promising results presented herein, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the clinical evaluation was conducted on a relatively modest sample size (n = 100) from a single geographic region. Larger multicenter studies incorporating diverse populations would strengthen the generalizability of our findings. Second, the antibody conjugation strategy employed in this study relies on non-covalent electrostatic adsorption, which may yield conjugates with variable antibody orientation and density [28]. Third, while we demonstrated excellent performance for HPV16 and HPV18 detection, comprehensive evaluation against a broader panel of HPV genotypes would confirm specificity. Fourth, extended stability studies under varied temperature and humidity conditions would provide more robust estimates of shelf-life. Fifth, integration with user-friendly readout systems, such as smartphone-based colorimetric analysis apps, would enhance the point-of-care utility of our platform [29].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that bimetallic nano mixture-based lateral flow assays, particularly the Ag–Au formulation, represent a highly sensitive, specific, and rapid diagnostic platform for HPV16 and HPV18 detection. The strategic combination of two distinct metals within a single nano mixture harnesses synergistic properties that dramatically enhance analytical and clinical performance compared to monometallic systems. The Ag–Au nano mixture achieved exceptional limits of detection (0.15–0.16 ng/mL), rapid response times (10 min), and excellent clinical performance (95% sensitivity, 91–92% specificity) with remarkable stability over 90 days.

Critically, this study establishes the biological relevance of our diagnostic platform within the context of HPV oncogenesis. We demonstrate that our LFA detects infection during the early pre-integrative phase, before the establishment of persistent E6/E7 expression and the associated disruption of p53 and pRb tumor suppressor pathways. The strong correlation between LFA signal intensity and E6/E7 oncoprotein levels suggests that this platform may provide semi-quantitative information about oncogenic potential. By enabling early identification of high-risk HPV infection—the critical initiating event in cervical carcinogenesis driven by E6/E7-mediated disruption of p53 and pRb tumor suppressor pathways—this platform addresses a crucial need in cervical cancer prevention.
