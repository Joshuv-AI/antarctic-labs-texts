Comparative efficacy of neoadjuvant short-course versus long-course radiotherapy-based regimens with or without immunotherapy for locally advanced pMMR rectal cancer: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Liqing Li, Xiaoyao Meng, Qiaoyuan Wu, Yuxin Wei, Shixiong Liang, Tingshi Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe integration of immunotherapy with neoadjuvant therapy for proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) is a promising strategy. However, the optimal treatment platform, a short-course radiotherapy (SCRT)-based regimen or a long-course chemoradiotherapy (LCRT)-based regimen, remains uncertain due to the absence of direct comparative trials. This network meta-analysis (NMA) aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of these two platforms, each with or without immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs).MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Cochrane Library from inception to September 20, 2025, for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing neoadjuvant SCRT-based or LCRT-based regimens combined with ICIs versus the corresponding regimens without ICIs in LARC. A frequentist NMA was performed using random-effects models. The co-primary outcomes were the curative-intent response rate [a composite of pathological complete response (pCR) or clinical complete response followed by a Watch-and-Wait strategy (WW)] and the incidence of grade ≥ 3 treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs). The pCR rate was a key secondary endpoint. Treatments were ranked using surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) probabilities.ResultsSeven RCTs (1132 patients) evaluating four strategies (SCRT-based regimen alone, SCRT-based regimen with ICIs, LCRT-based regimen alone, and LCRT-based regimen with ICIs) were included. For the primary endpoint of curative-intent response, SCRT + ICIs had the highest probability of being the most effective treatment (SUCRA, 98.5%). SCRT + ICIs significantly outperformed SCRT alone (RR, 1.82; 95% CI, 1.27–2.60) and LCRT alone (RR, 2.23; 95% CI, 1.33–3.76). It also showed a numerical, but non-significant, advantage over LCRT + ICIs (RR, 1.63; 95% CI, 0.88–3.02). The addition of ICIs to LCRT resulted in a numerical increase in response (RR, 1.37; 95% CI, 0.98–1.90) compared to LCRT alone. Results for the pCR rate were consistent in treatment ranking. Regarding safety, the addition of ICIs to either platform was not associated with a significant increase in grade ≥ 3 TRAEs, and no significant difference was observed between the two combination strategies (SCRT + ICIs vs. LCRT + ICIs: RR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.38–2.04).ConclusionsThis NMA suggests that the SCRT-based platform may be the preferred option to combine with immunotherapy in pMMR LARC. The results support direct comparison of these platforms in future phase III trials focused on long-term survival and organ preservation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04762-7.Keywords: Rectal neoplasms, Neoadjuvant therapy, Short-course radiotherapy, Immunotherapy, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Network meta-analysis, Pathologic complete response, Proficient mismatch repair

Background

The management of locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) has evolved into a multimodal paradigm centered on neoadjuvant therapy followed by total mesorectal excision (TME) [1–3]. For most patients with proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) tumors, which represent over 90% of LARC cases, the cornerstone of neoadjuvant treatment has historically been radiotherapy. This is primarily delivered as either a regimen based on long-course chemoradiotherapy (LCRT), typically 45–50.4 Gy in 25–28 fractions, or one based on short-course radiotherapy (SCRT), typically 25 Gy in 5 fractions, with systemic chemotherapy incorporated into both platforms [4–9]. Modern evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) has demonstrated that SCRT-based regimen delivers pathologic complete response (pCR) rates that are non-inferior to, and may potentially exceed, those achieved with LCRT-based regimen, while offering superior convenience and cost-effectiveness [4, 5, 10–16]. The choice between modalities now hinges on nuanced trade-offs in which SCRT is associated with potential higher rates of acute grade 3–4 toxicity [5, 15], while LCRT is characterized by a substantially longer overall treatment time.

The therapeutic landscape is now being reshaped by two pivotal shifts. First, the primary treatment strategy has evolved toward the use of total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) [17]. While early trials established TNT to reduce distant metastases [4, 18], the strategy is now also explicitly used to maximize tumor regression, thereby increasing the opportunity for organ preservation. Consequently, the primary treatment goal is expanding beyond pCR as a surrogate endpoint toward the more clinically meaningful endpoint of curative-intent response, a composite of pCR and clinical complete response managed by a Watch-and-Wait (WW) strategy. Second, alongside the focus on organ preservation, there is an increasing emphasis on biological stratification (e.g., mismatch repair status) to personalize therapy. The integration of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) with radiotherapy has demonstrated significant promise. Although single-agent ICIs show limited activity in pMMR colorectal cancer [19, 20], their combination with radiotherapy can synergistically enhance anti-tumor immunity [21–23]. Recently, several RCTs, including key studies published in 2025 (e.g., STELLAR II [24], SPRING-01 [25], and POLARSTAR [26]), have demonstrated that the addition of PD-1 inhibitors to both SCRT-based and LCRT-based regimens significantly improves pCR rates in predominantly pMMR LARC cohorts, heralding a new era in neoadjuvant management.

These advances converge on a critical, unresolved question in the era of organ preservation: For patients with pMMR LARC, what is the optimal radiotherapeutic backbone to combine with immunotherapy when the goal is to maximize the rate of curative-intent response (pCR and successful WW). Direct comparative evidence between the two intensified regimens (SCRT-based regimen plus ICIs versus LCRT-based regimen plus ICIs) is absent, as no head-to-head trials exist, and conventional meta-analyses cannot address this gap.

To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) focusing on the pMMR population which constitutes the primary cohort in most contemporary trials. By synthesizing the most recent evidence, including landmark 2025 RCTs [24–27], our study aims to indirectly compare and rank the efficacy of four key neoadjuvant strategies (SCRT-based regimen alone, SCRT-based regimen with ICIs, LCRT-based regimen alone, and LCRT-based regimen with ICIs) for achieving a curative-intent response, thereby providing actionable evidence for clinical decision-making and future trial design. For brevity, these are hereafter referred to as SCRT, SCRT + ICIs, LCRT, and LCRT + ICIs, respectively.

Methods

Study design and registration

This systematic review and NMA was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension statement for Network Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-NMA). Study design and workflow is presented in Fig. 1. We registered our protocol on a website for protocol registration of systematic review INPLASY with the registration number of INPLASY2025100079 (Doi: 10.37766/inplasy2025.10.0079).

Study design and workflow. Schematic diagram illustrating the overall design and analytical workflow of the systematic review and network meta-analysis

Data sources and search strategy

A systematic literature search was performed across electronic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases, from their inception until September 20, 2025. Furthermore, proceedings of major international oncology conferences (e.g., ASCO, ESMO, ASTRO) from the past 3 years were screened for eligible studies. The search strategy utilized a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords related to the core concepts: (“rectal cancer”) AND (“neoadjuvant therapy” OR “immunotherapy” OR “radiotherapy”) AND (“randomized controlled trial”). No language restrictions were applied. The search strategy was first developed for PubMed/MEDLINE and then appropriately adapted for the syntax and subject headings of each of the other electronic databases. No language or date restrictions were applied. The complete search strategies for all databases are provided in Additional file 1.

Study selection and data extraction

Two investigators independently screened titles, abstracts, and subsequently, full-text articles for eligibility. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by adjudication from a third reviewer. Data from included studies were extracted independently using a piloted data extraction form. Extracted information included study characteristics (study design, year, trial name), sample size, key patient baseline characteristics (pMMR status, gender distribution, age, distance to anorectal verge, clinical T and N stage, and rates of EMVI/MRF involvement), detailed intervention protocols (radiotherapy regimen, specific immune agent, number of cycles), and outcome data (rates of clinical complete response [cCR] with subsequent WW approach, pCR, R0 resection rate, sphincter preservation rate, grade ≥ 3 TRAE).

Assessment of treatment efficacy endpoints

Primary endpoint: curative-intent response rate

The pre-specified primary endpoint for this NMA was the curative-intent response rate, a composite endpoint designed to assess the success of neoadjuvant therapy in achieving a cure, irrespective of the method (surgery or organ preservation). It was defined as the proportion of patients in the intention-to-treat (ITT) population achieving either: (i) a pCR (ypT0N0) after surgery, or (ii) a cCR followed by the initiation of a WW strategy. This conservative ITT approach, which differs from “per-protocol” analyses that can inflate rates by including only surgical patients, was chosen to minimize bias.

For the integration of patients who elected a WW approach, and to transparently address heterogeneous reporting of long-term outcomes, a tiered analytical strategy was employed: (i) primary analysis (unified benchmark): assuming 100% WW success to establish an upper-bound efficacy estimate; (ii) pre-specified sensitivity analyses: applying evidence-based WW success rates (78.4% for sustained remission; 89.2% for ultimate organ preservation including salvage therapy) derived from a recent meta-analysis to test robustness and ground findings in clinical reality [28].

Secondary endpoint: pathological complete response rate

The traditional pCR rate (ypT0N0 in the ITT population) was analyzed as a key secondary endpoint. This analysis, which excludes patients managed by WW (i.e., a WW success rate of 0% was applied in this calculation), is reported to provide a direct benchmark for comparison with the historical and majority of contemporary literature that uses pCR as the primary efficacy measure.

Grade ≥ 3 treatment-related adverse events assessment

For the grade ≥ 3 TRAEs, data were extracted based on the definitions and classifications provided in the original studies, which typically utilized the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE). Data extraction for TRAEs focused on the highest grade experienced by each patient within the neoadjuvant treatment period, up to the time of surgery. For each study arm, we extracted the number of patients experiencing at least one grade ≥ 3 TRAE. This number was then divided by the total number of patients who initiated the assigned neoadjuvant treatment (the safety population) to calculate the incidence proportion. This approach ensures that the safety profile of each regimen is assessed among all patients exposed to the treatment.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of each included RCT was independently assessed by two reviewers using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2.0), evaluating biases arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result.

Supplemental meta-analysis of single-arm studies

We performed a separate meta-analysis of single-arm studies to provide a descriptive benchmark for the absolute efficacy and safety rates of immunotherapy-combined regimens outside the controlled trial setting. This analysis is strictly descriptive and non-comparative; its results are not statistically combined with the RCT data from the primary NMA. Its purpose is to offer contextual reference points for the absolute performance of each strategy, thereby aiding in the interpretation of the comparative effects derived from the RCT network. Results

Study selection and characteristics

The systematic literature search identified 546 records. After removal of duplicates and screening of titles and abstracts, 65 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Seven RCTs [24–27, 29–31] met the inclusion criteria and were included in the NMA. The TORCH trial [32], which compared two experimental sequencing strategies (SCRT + ICIs-sequential vs. SCRT + ICIs-induction) without a standard-of-care control arm, was excluded from the NMA due to its inability to connect to the core evidence network. The PRISMA flow diagram illustrates the study selection process (Fig. 2).

PRISMA flow diagram. Flow diagram detailing the process of identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion of studies in the network meta-analysis, according to PRISMA guidelines

The included trials, summarized in Table 1, enrolled a total of 1132 patients randomized to one of four intervention strategies: SCRT (n = 157), SCRT + ICIs (n = 272), LCRT (n = 372), and LCRT + ICIs (n = 331). Key patient baseline characteristics, including pMMR status, gender distribution, age, distance to anorectal verge, clinical T and N stage, and rates of EMVI/MRF involvement, were generally well-balanced across studies where reported. While five RCTs incorporated a WW strategy [24, 27, 29–31], the CHINOREC trial uniquely reported a composite “complete response” rate. Accordingly, we used this composite rate for the primary curative-intent response analysis but excluded it from the pCR-only analysis and sensitivity analyses. The RoB 2.0 assessment indicated an overall low risk of bias across the included trials (Fig. 3A, B).

Efficacy outcomes: curative-intent response and pCR

Network meta-analysis of the curative-intent response rate (primary endpoint)

The network of evidence for the pre-specified primary endpoint, the curative-intent response rate (pCR + WW), is presented in Fig. 3C. A random-effects model was employed. The league table presenting all pairwise comparisons as risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) is shown in Fig. 3D. In key comparisons, SCRT + ICIs was associated with a significantly higher curative-intent response rate versus SCRT (RR 1.82, 95% CI 1.27–2.60) and LCRT (RR 2.23, 95% CI 1.33–3.76). The point estimate also favored SCRT + ICIs over LCRT + ICIs, although this difference was not statistically significant (RR 1.63, 95% CI 0.88–3.02). Based on surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) probabilities (Fig. 3E, F), SCRT + ICIs had the highest probability of being the most effective treatment (98.5%), followed by LCRT + ICIs (55.8%), SCRT alone (37.2%), with LCRT alone having the lowest probability (8.5%).

Sensitivity analysis: impact of real-world Watch-and-Wait success rates

Under both the conservative (78.4%) and optimistic success scenarios (89.2%), the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of effects favoring SCRT + ICIs were maintained, and the comparative ranking of treatments remained unchanged (Additional file 3: Fig. S1A–F).

Network meta-analysis of the pCR rate (secondary endpoint)

For context and benchmarking against historical data, a NMA was also performed using the pCR rate (i.e., assuming 0% WW success). The results and treatment rankings (Fig. 3G–I) were consistent in direction with the primary analysis based on the curative-intent response rate, further supporting the main comparative findings.

Direct pairwise meta-analysis of the curative-intent response and pCR rate

Direct pairwise meta-analyses demonstrated that adding ICIs significantly increased the curative-intent response rate for both SCRT (RR 1.94, 95% CI 1.52–2.48) and LCRT (RR 1.38, 95% CI 1.03–1.85) (Additional file 4: Fig. S2A, B). A similar pattern was observed for the pCR rate, reaching significance with SCRT (RR 2.05, 95% CI 1.54–2.72) but not with LCRT (RR 1.39, 95% CI 0.99–1.95) (Additional file 4: Fig. S2C, D).

Safety outcome: grade ≥ 3 treatment-related adverse events

Network meta-analysis results

The network of evidence for safety is presented in Fig. 4A. The NMA results for all pairwise comparisons are summarized in the league table (Fig. 4B). Key comparisons from the NMA showed no statistically significant differences: between the two immunotherapy-based strategies (SCRT + ICIs vs. LCRT + ICIs: RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.38–2.04), or between either combination or its respective radiotherapy-alone control. Based on SUCRA ranking probabilities, LCRT + ICIs and SCRT + ICIs had the highest and nearly identical probabilities of being associated with toxicity (SUCRA 72.9% and 58.2%, respectively), followed by LCRT alone (55.1%) and SCRT alone (13.7%) (Fig. 4C, D). Collectively, these results indicate that the addition of immunotherapy to either radiotherapy platform was not associated with a statistically significant increase in grade ≥ 3 TRAEs, and no clinically meaningful differences in toxicity were observed between the two combination strategies.

Direct pairwise meta-analysis results

Direct pairwise meta-analyses indicated that the addition of ICIs was associated with non-significant increases in the risk of grade ≥ 3 TRAEs for both SCRT-based (RR 1.20, 95% CI 0.78–1.85) and LCRT-based regimens (RR 1.12, 95% CI 0.85–1.47) (Additional file 4: Fig. S2E, F).

Assessment of inconsistency and heterogeneity

Consistency was assessed qualitatively by comparing direct and indirect evidence, as formal testing was underpowered. For all outcomes, the confidence intervals showed complete overlap (Additional file 5: Fig. S3), indicating no empirical inconsistency. This, alongside low-to-moderate heterogeneity (τ = 0.146 for curative-intent response, τ = 0.0 for pCR, and τ = 0.24 for grade ≥ 3 TRAEs), supports the validity of the NMA model.

Supplemental analysis of single-arm studies

A supplementary analysis of prospective single-arm studies (N = 18) [32–49] was conducted to provide descriptive benchmarks for absolute response and toxicity rates outside of randomized settings (Additional file 6: Table S1). For SCRT + ICIs, the pooled curative-intent response and pCR rates from single-arm studies were 47.6% and 35.9%, respectively. For LCRT + ICIs, the corresponding rates were 38.5% and 30.8% (Additional file 7: Fig. S4). The pooled incidence of grade ≥ 3 TRAEs was 22.9% for SCRT + ICIs and 19.4% for LCRT + ICIs. Notably, these pooled estimates exhibited considerable statistical heterogeneity (I2 > 50%), explored further via sensitivity analyses (Additional file 8: Fig. S5). Given the inherent limitations of single-arm designs (e.g., selection bias) and the substantial heterogeneity observed, these estimates are best viewed as exploratory. Any numerical differences between strategies should be considered hypothesis-generating, with comparative efficacy and safety informed by the primary RCT network.

Discussion

This NMA is the first to systematically compare neoadjuvant SCRT-based versus LCRT-based platforms combined with ICIs for pMMR LARC using an endpoint that integrates organ preservation. By integrating the most recent evidence, our findings indicate that SCRT + ICIs may represent a highly effective option for achieving a curative-intent response. The benefit of SCRT + ICIs was demonstrated by statistically significant improvements in the curative-intent response rate over both SCRT alone and LCRT alone. Although the point estimate favored SCRT + ICIs over LCRT + ICIs for this primary endpoint, the difference was not statistically significant (RR 1.63, 95% CI 0.88–3.02). The direction of effect favoring SCRT + ICIs for curative-intent response remained consistent across all pre-specified analytical scenarios, from the most optimistic to the most conservative. The addition of ICIs to LCRT was associated with a more modest, non-significant increase in the curative-intent response rate.

The interpretation of efficacy in neoadjuvant rectal cancer therapy is undergoing a fundamental shift, moving beyond the traditional surgical-centric paradigm of pCR. As organ preservation becomes a paramount treatment goal, the conventional pCR rate increasingly risks excluding those who achieve a cCR and are managed with a WW strategy. This methodological gap can introduce bias when comparing trials with differing approaches to organ preservation. To directly address this evolving clinical priority and to generate the comparative findings reported above, we introduced a multi-layered endpoint framework. Our primary endpoint, the “curative-intent response rate,” explicitly combines surgical pCR with the initiation of WW, thereby capturing the full spectrum of curative-intent outcomes. The nuanced results from our sensitivity analyses, where the statistical certainty varied with the definition of WW success, underscore a key methodological insight: in the organ preservation era, the chosen definition of therapeutic “success” is a critical determinant of comparative evidence strength, a factor future trials must explicitly address.

The pooled pCR rates derived from our analysis, particularly from the single-arm studies (e.g., 35.9% for SCRT + ICIs), may appear conservative compared to some high-profile reports in the literature [24, 32] (e.g., 50% for SCRT + ICIs in the TORCH trial [32]). This numerical discrepancy serves as a direct and quantifiable illustration of the methodological gap. It concretely shows how conventional endpoints can vary widely based on the included population, and why even an ITT-based pCR rate remains an incomplete measure when cure can be achieved without surgery. Therefore, our “curative-intent response rate” was designed to resolve this specific limitation by applying a consistent, ITT-informed logic to both surgical and non-surgical curative outcomes. While the pCR rate provides an essential benchmark for historical comparisons, our primary endpoint delivers a unified and clinically pertinent assessment of efficacy in the organ preservation era. It is crucial to interpret our findings within the context of endpoint surrogacy. Both composite curative-intent response rate and pCR are intermediate endpoints. Their correlation with long-term overall survival and durable organ preservation, particularly within novel immunotherapy-based regimens, remains to be fully established. Therefore, while our results strongly suggest the superiority of the SCRT platform for achieving high initial response rates, they should be viewed as supporting the need for confirmatory phase III trials with survival and organ preservation endpoints.

The superior curative-intent response observed with SCRT combined with ICIs is a compelling finding with a solid mechanistic rationale [50–53]. Unlike protracted LCRT, SCRT delivers a high biologically effective dose over a short period, which can induce more robust immunogenic cell death and a stronger in-situ vaccination effect. This creates a synergistic foundation for immune checkpoint inhibitors [54, 55]. This concept of SCRT as a potent immune primer is supported by long-term clinical observations. For instance, in the Polish study comparing SCRT-based and LCRT-based total neoadjuvant therapy, an early signal of benefit did not translate into a durable survival advantage with extended follow-up, a phenomenon attributed by the investigators to the potent but ultimately insufficient immune stimulation from SCRT alone [11]. Our finding that adding ICIs to SCRT significantly boosts efficacy directly addresses this limitation, positioning the combination as a strategy that couples powerful immune “priming” with sustained immune “maintenance” [56]. The rapid tumor debulking and immune activation achieved with SCRT are likely to combine more effectively with concurrent or sequential ICIs, thereby enhancing anti-tumor immunity even in the typically immunologically “cold” microenvironment of pMMR rectal cancers [57, 58]. Conversely, the more modest efficacy gain observed when adding ICIs to LCRT in our analysis may be explained by competing biological mechanisms. Protracted LCRT, while effective, may concurrently induce immunosuppressive pathways (e.g., PD-L1 [59], TGF-β [60]) that could counteract the pro-inflammatory effects of ICIs. Therefore, the superior outcomes with SCRT + ICIs may stem not only from its strong immunogenic primer but also from its shorter duration, which potentially minimizes the concurrent induction of these counter-regulatory immunosuppressive mechanisms, thereby creating a more favorable and focused window for ICIs to act. Our finding that the efficacy gains from adding ICIs differs by radiotherapy backbone strengthens the view that the radiotherapy regimen itself is a key driver of outcomes in combination therapy [61, 62].

While this analysis highlights the differential effect of the radiotherapy platform, variations in specific ICI agents and their sequencing (e.g., induction vs. concurrent vs. consolidation) among the included trials constitute an additional layer of heterogeneity. The distinct immunogenic priming by SCRT versus LCRT may interact with these factors, potentially influencing the optimal timing for ICI administration, a hypothesis that warrants further investigation. This mechanistic advantage is especially significant given the limited benefit of intensifying immunotherapy alone. For example, trials such as CHINOREC have demonstrated that even dual immune checkpoint blockade fails to substantially improve outcomes beyond chemoradiation [27], indicating that simply adding more immunotherapy is insufficient in pMMR rectal cancers. These insights highlight the need for more sophisticated strategies that integrate immunotherapy with modalities capable of fundamentally reshaping the tumor microenvironment. Together, our findings suggest that SCRT may offer a more potent platform for immune activation compared with LCRT.

The safety assessment provides essential context for the efficacy benefits. It should be noted that the direct comparison between SCRT + ICIs and SCRT alone was underpowered, yielding inconclusive results due to wide confidence intervals. Therefore, we rely on the NMA, which integrates both direct and indirect evidence to provide a more robust and stable estimation. The NMA demonstrated that adding ICIs to either SCRT or LCRT was associated with a non-significant increase in the risk of grade ≥ 3 TRAEs, with relatively narrow and clinically interpretable confidence intervals. Crucially, no statistically significant or clinically meaningful difference in toxicity was observed between the two combination strategies (SCRT + ICIs vs. LCRT + ICIs). The SUCRA rankings further indicated nearly identical safety probabilities for these two regimens. The safety profile of these regimens seems predominantly driven by the underlying radiotherapy and chemotherapy components, with ICIs contributing a manageable additional risk. Therefore, the choice between SCRT + ICIs and LCRT + ICIs can be guided primarily by efficacy, logistics, and patient preference, rather than by a major safety differential. The treatment structures of SCRT + ICI and LCRT + ICI led to distinct toxicity profiles for clinical management. The LCRT platform combines radiation with concurrent chemotherapy (e.g., capecitabine), introducing early chemotherapy-related toxicities such as hand-foot syndrome and mucositis. These effects may overlap with immune-related adverse events (irAEs), necessitating monitoring for potential interaction, particularly in the gastrointestinal tract. In contrast, the SCRT-based regimen delivers radiation without concurrent chemotherapy, thereby avoiding this overlap. The acute effects of SCRT are typically transient, allowing clinical attention to focus primarily on the irAEs associated with immunotherapy. Therefore, the SCRT platform offers a potential practical advantage by separating the toxicity profiles of radiotherapy and systemic therapy, which may simplify clinical monitoring.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the number of RCTs in the network, although including the latest evidence, remains limited. Second, incomplete reporting of MMR status in some trials precluded a formal subgroup analysis; thus, our findings are most applicable to the pMMR population. Third, clinical heterogeneity exists across trials regarding specific ICI agents, chemotherapy backbones, and immunotherapy sequencing. Although no significant statistical inconsistency was detected, these variations challenge the transitivity assumption and may introduce residual heterogeneity, warranting future investigation with individual patient data. Fourth, while the NMA supports the general conclusion of no major safety differential between the SCRT + ICI and LCRT + ICI platforms, the direct evidence quantifying the incremental toxicity of adding ICIs to SCRT alone remains limited and unstable. Fifth, long-term WW success rates involve uncertainty, and our modeling relied on the best available external evidence. Finally, our analysis focuses on intermediate endpoints; mature survival and organ preservation data are awaited to confirm the clinical benefits suggested by our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this NMA suggests that the combination of ICIs with the SCRT-based regimen (SCRT + ICIs) is associated with a significantly increased curative-intent response and pCR rate in pMMR LARC, with a consistent benefit trend. Given the comparable toxicity between SCRT + ICI and LCRT + ICI platforms, and its practical advantages, SCRT + ICIs emerges as a highly promising neoadjuvant strategy. Definitive judgment, however, awaits phase III trials directly comparing these regimens, with long-term survival and organ preservation rates as the ultimate endpoints.
