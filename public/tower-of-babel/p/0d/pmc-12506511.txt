Arthroscopic suture-bridge repair of small and medium supraspinatus tears: a simplified Lassoloop technique with single medial and knotless lateral anchors
Authors: Songlin Liu, Liang Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe transition in arthroscopic rotator cuff repair from single-row to double-row techniques has highlighted the efficacy of the suture-bridge configuration in providing enhanced biomechanical stability and coverage of the footprint. However, traditional methods often require multiple anchors and complex knot-tying procedures, resulting in prolonged surgical duration and increased costs. This study evaluated a customized suture-bridge approach incorporating a Simplified LassoLoop Suture, utilizing a single medial row anchor and a knotless lateral row anchor, to treat small to medium-sized rotator cuff tears.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on 132 patients who received treatment at Jingzhou Hospital, Affiliated with Yangtze University, between June 2021 and June 2022. All patients underwent arthroscopic repair utilizing the specified technique. Clinical outcomes were evaluated preoperatively and at the final follow-up using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), University of California at Los Angeles (UCLA) shoulder rating scale, and Constant-Murley score.ResultsThe arthroscopic repair was conducted on 132 patients with small to medium Rotator cuff tears using simplified lassoloop suture technology. Background

Rotator cuff tears primarily cause shoulder pain and functional impairment, significantly affecting patients' quality of life and daily activities [1–3]. Small to medium-sized tears, typically measuring 1 to 3 cm in the anterior–posterior dimension, are commonly seen in clinical practice [4]. While conservative management may benefit some patients, surgical intervention is often required for those with persistent symptoms or functional limitations [5]. Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair has become the preferred approach for addressing these tears, offering the advantages of minimally invasive surgery, reduced soft tissue trauma, and quicker recovery compared to open techniques [6, 7].

In the past two decades, advancements in arthroscopic techniques have resulted in the development of various repair strategies, including single-row, double-row, and suture-bridge configurations [8]. The suture-bridge technique has garnered widespread acceptance for its capacity to deliver a biomechanically robust repair, distributing load over a larger footprint and promoting tendon-to-bone healing [9, 10]. Nevertheless, conventional double-row suture-bridge techniques often require multiple anchors and intricate suture management, leading to prolonged surgical duration, increased expenses, and heightened risk of complications [11, 12].

The Simplified LassoLoop Suture Technique, along with a single medial row suture anchor and knotless lateral row anchor for suture-bridge fixation, provides a novel solution to these challenges. This strategy streamlines suture management and is designed to preserve the biomechanical benefits of a double-row repair. Utilizing a single medial row anchor and a knotless lateral row anchor in a suture-bridge setup reduces the number of implants needed. It substantially reduces the need for knot tying, thereby potentially enhancing surgical efficiency and cost-effectiveness compared to traditional double-row techniques, although direct comparative data are not presented in this study.

This study sought to evaluate the outcomes of 132 patients who underwent arthroscopic repair of small to medium rotator cuff tears using the Simplified LassoLoop Suture Technique. This technique utilizes a single medial row suture anchor and knotless lateral row anchor for suture-bridge fixation, aiming to establish a tension-free, stable construct that enhances tendon healing while simplifying the surgical process.

Methods

Subjects and inclusion & exclusion criteria

This retrospective study initially screened 158 patients and finally comprised 132 patients treated for small to medium Rotator cuff tears at Jingzhou Hospital, Affiliated with Yangtze University, between June 2021 and June 2022. Twenty-six patients were excluded for the following reasons: 8 due to large or massive tears (> 3 cm), 5 due to prior shoulder surgery, 4 due to concurrent brachial plexus injury, 3 due to significant medical comorbidities contraindicating surgery (uncontrolled diabetes, cardiopulmonary insufficiency), 4 due to additional injuries to rotator cuff tendons other than the supraspinatus (e.g., subscapularis, infraspinatus), and 2 due to inability to complete follow-up. Ethical approval was obtained from Jingzhou Hospital, affiliated with the Yangtze University Ethics Committee, and all patients provided surgical consent. The demographic characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1, including 46 male and 86 female patients, with 72 right and 60 left rotator cuffs. The patients' ages ranged from 45 to 65 years, with a mean age of 55.77 ± 3.36 years.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: 1)Patients with Rotator cuff injuries experiencing pain, functional limitations, and unsuccessful non-surgical management for at least 3months.2)Diagnosis of small to medium-sized supraspinatus rotator cuff tears (1–3 cm in anteroposterior dimension as measured on preoperative MRI and confirmed during arthroscopy), demonstrating good tendon quality, defined as minimal retraction (Patte stage 1 or 2) and minimal fatty infiltration (Goutallier stage 0–2 on preoperative MRI)0.3) Hamada stage 1 or 2 only.4) Isolated supraspinatus tendon involvement.5) Patients capable of completing full follow-up and evaluation.Exclusion criteria were: 1) Prior shoulder surgery. 2) Concurrent brachial plexus or axillary nerve injury. 3) Presence of medical conditions contraindicating surgery (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, coagulation disorders, severe cardiopulmonary insufficiency, active local or systemic infection). 4) Additional injuries to Rotator cuff tendons other than the supraspinatus. 5) Large or massive tears (> 3 cm). 6) Advanced fatty infiltration of the rotator cuff muscles (Goutallier stage 3 or 4). 7) Concomitant significant intra-articular pathology requiring extensive repair (e.g., large SLAP lesions, significant cartilage defects > Outerbridge grade II, biceps tendon instability or tears requiring tenodesis/tenotomy).

Tear size was measured intraoperatively using a calibrated probe introduced through the lateral portal, with the arm in abduction and neutral rotation. The anteroposterior dimension was recorded as the maximum distance from the anterior to the posterior edge of the tear. Tendon mobility and elasticity were assessed subjectively by the operating surgeon using a tissue grasper to reduce the tendon to its anatomical footprint with minimal tension.

Surgery procedures

All surgical procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon under general anesthesia with a brachial plexus block. Patients were positioned in the lateral decubitus position with the affected arm abducted at 60° and subjected to 4–5 kg of traction. Following standard surgical preparation and draping, arthroscopic portals were established to comprehensively evaluate the glenohumeral joint, glenoid labrum, subscapularis tendon, and long head of the biceps tendon to identify any concurrent injuries. Patients exhibiting only mild subacromial impingement underwent concomitant subacromial decompression. Those with adhesive capsulitis symptoms underwent capsular release. Patients with the aforementioned significant concomitant pathologies were excluded.

After identifying Small to medium-sized supraspinatus rotator cuff tears (Fig. 1A, D), the torn edges were debrided, and the footprint area was exposed. The tendon stump and footprint were prepared to promote biological healing (Fig. 1B). A 4.5 mm bone awl was used to create a bone tunnel at approximately a 90° angle to the medial cartilage margin at the tear's midpoint. Subsequently, a 4.75 mm PEEK suture anchor (HEALIX ADVANCE BR, Depuy Mitek, USA) was inserted as the medial row anchor. The four suture strands were then passed through the supraspinatus tendon in a horizontal mattress configuration from posterior to anterior using a suture passer (Fig. 1C).

The two central sutures of corresponding colors were securely knotted (Fig. 1E). Subsequently, all four strands were pulled through the cannula onto the greater tuberosity to ensure complete footprint coverage (Fig. 1F). In cases where a "dog-ear" deformity was present,the LassoLoop technique was employed to manage the tissue redundancy [13]. This technique involves passing a suture in a looping manner around the base of the "dog-ear" tissue fold: A curved suture passer (e.g., a suture lasso or a sharpened bird beak) is used to penetrate the full-thickness of the rotator cuff tissue at the apex of the "dog-ear" fold, typically passing from the bursal to the articular side. One limb of the suture (often from the posterior set) is shuttled through this tissue passage, creating a loop that encircles the base of the tissue redundancy.The free end (tail) of the same suture limb is then passed through this loop, effectively forming a sliding, self-locking noose.The tail of the suture is then grasped and pulled taut, which tightens the loop around the gathered tissue, thereby reducing the "dog-ear" deformity and securing the tissue fold firmly against the footprint. This maneuver provides broad, secure tissue capture and distributes tension across a wider area of the tendon, minimizing the risk of suture cut-through compared to simple suture passage techniques.

A 4.5 mm bone awl was then used to create a bone tunnel below the greater tuberosity. The four suture strands were passed through this tunnel into a knotless lateral row anchor (4.75 mm PEEK suture anchor, Knotless, Rejoin Hangzhou, China). After proper tensioning and confirmation of the anchor's optimal depth, the knotless anchor was impacted into the bone tunnel, and any excess suture tails were trimmed (Fig. 1G, H).

Rehabilitation

Postoperatively, patients were instructed to wear an abduction brace for 6 weeks and participate in a supervised rehabilitation program overseen by the same physical therapist. During the initial phase (1–6 weeks postoperatively), the shoulder was immobilized with a brace, and passive range of motion (ROM) exercises, such as pendulum exercises, were initiated, with external Rotation limited to less than 30°. In the subsequent phase (6–12 weeks postoperatively), patients progressed to active-assisted and active ROM exercises, emphasizing scapular stabilization and isometric strengthening. The final phase (12–20 weeks postoperatively) included progressive resistance training, proprioceptive exercises, and dynamic stability training. At the 6-month postoperative mark, patients underwent evaluation for pain-free ROM, muscle strength recovery, and functional stability. Patients meeting the criteria were cleared to resume sports or high-intensity activities.

Evaluation of clinical outcomes

Perioperative variables, including operative duration, hospitalization length, intraoperative blood loss, complications, wound healing, and duration of hospital stay, were meticulously documented. Preoperative and postoperative evaluations (conducted at the final follow-up visit, mean 23.9 ± 1.75 months) encompassed shoulder ROM, pain severity measured by the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), functional outcomes evaluated using the University of California at Los Angeles (UCLA) shoulder rating scale, and the Constant-Murley score. All postoperative clinical evaluations were performed by an independent research fellow who was not involved in the surgical procedures and was blinded to the specific technical details of the repair, in order to minimize assessment bias. A shoulder MRI was performed during the final follow-up (ranging from 12 to 30 months postoperatively) to assess the healing progress of the rotator cuff tendons. Tendon healing was defined as the continuous low-signal intensity of the tendon on T2-weighted images without fluid-filled gaps at the footprint on MRI. MRI was performed in all 132 patients to confirm tendon integrity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), and continuous variables were described as mean values with standard deviations (mean ± SD) < insor medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots. Preoperative and postoperative continuous variables were compared using paired t-tests for normally distributed data; otherwise, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (two-sided) was used. A significance level of P < 0.05 was set for statistical significance. No adjustments for multiple comparisons were made as the primary analyses were planned comparisons of pre- and post-operative states.

Results

One hundred and thirty-two patients were followed up for 12 to 30 months postoperatively, with an average follow-up duration of 23.9 ± 1.75 months. All included patients achieved the minimum 12-month follow-up. All patients underwent successful surgery and were discharged between postoperative days 5 and 7, with an average hospital stay of 6.8 ± 0.39 days. The average operative time was 33.8 ± 3.15 min, ranging from 30 to 50 min. Intraoperative blood loss averaged 7.6 ± 1.37 mL, ranging from 5 to 15 mL. The mean anteroposterior diameter of Rotator cuff tears was 1.5 ± 0.60 cm, ranging from 1 to 3 cm (Table 2). During the follow-up, none of the patients experienced complications such as joint infection, anchor failure, or rotator cuff re-tear. Discussion

The study demonstrates that the simplified LassoLoop Suture Technique, utilizing a single medial Row suture anchor and knotless lateral Row anchor for suture-bridge fixation, is a practical and reproducible approach for arthroscopic repair of small to medium supraspinatus rotator cuff tears. This technique is designed to combine the biomechanical principles of a double-row repair with the simplicity of a reduced-implant configuration, potentially overcoming limitations associated with traditional suture-bridge techniques. By employing a single medial row anchor and a knotless lateral row anchor reduces implant burden and surgical complexity. The mean operative time of 33.8 ± 3.15 min in our study is shorter than the times frequently reported in the literature for conventional double-row repairs [11, 12, 14], suggesting improved operative efficiency. This reduction in time, coupled with the use of fewer implants, may contribute to lower overall procedure costs, although a formal cost–benefit analysis was beyond the scope of this study and should be investigated in future comparative trials [15].

The term "dog ear" refers to excess tissue or puckering at the edges of a repaired rotator cuff, typically resulting from uneven tension distribution or irregular tissue margins [16–18]. This phenomenon is commonly seen in small to medium-sized tears, where achieving precise alignment of tissue edges during suturing can be challenging. A "dog ear" may compromise the biomechanical integrity of the repair, leading to heightened stress concentration at the repair site, potential gap formation, and impaired healing [19–21]. The Simplified LassoLoop Suture Technique offers notable advantages in suture management. with enhanced tissue capture, reduced soft tissue trauma, and improved biomechanical stability compared to simple suture passage techniques.The lasso-loop configuration may enhance suture–tendon purchase and reduce slippage compared with simple passage; however, our study was not designed to test biomechanical differences, and clinical outcomes—not biomechanics—form the basis of our conclusions. This technique proves effective and efficient for arthroscopic repair of small to medium rotator cuff tears. It involves a single, strategic pass of the suture around the apex of the "dog-ear" deformity enabling more consistent tissue gathering and decreasing the likelihood of tissue redundancy, thereby ensuring a smoother and anatomically aligned repair. Additionally, the knotless lateral row anchor enhances the repair process by eliminating the need for knot tying, thereby lowering the risk of suture loosening and improving load distribution across the footprint [13, 22, 23].

Arthroscopic techniques for rotator cuff repair exhibit variability in re-tear rates across different surgical approaches [14, 24, 25]. Rotator cuff re-tears have a multifactorial etiology, potentially linked to patient age, tendon retraction, fatty infiltration, surgical technique, and postoperative rehabilitation protocols [26]. Optimal initial fixation strength and sustained mechanical stability are crucial for reducing re-tear rates. The tendon-suture interface is key to maintaining this stability. Tailoring suture techniques to different rotator cuff injuries is essential for enhancing the strength of this interface and improving healing rates [27–29]. This study notably observed no rotator cuff re-tear, which highlights the potential biomechanical stability and clinical efficacy of the Simplified LassoLoop Suture Technique in this specific patient cohort. However, the zero re-tear rate must be interpreted with caution. Several factors contributed to the favorable outcome. Firstly, the LassoLoop device securely captures tissue, anchoring the tendon to the medial row suture anchor to minimize suture pullout or tissue failure. Secondly, the knotless lateral row anchor evenly distributes the load across the footprint, reducing stress concentrations that could cause re-tear. Thirdly, the tension-free repair technique preserves tissue integrity and supports healing.Most importantly, the strict patient selection criteria, including the exclusion of large tears, tears with poor tissue quality (high-grade retraction or fatty infiltration), and involvement of multiple tendons, significantly contributed to this optimal outcome by selecting a patient population with a inherently lower risk of failure [17]. The absence of re-tears in this optimized cohort, while encouraging, may not be generalizable to all tear patterns. The sample size, while adequate for demonstrating clinical improvement, remains relatively small for definitively establishing a true complication rate, and the mean follow-up of 23.9 ± 1.75 months, though substantial, may not capture very late failures.

Biomechanically, the suture-bridge configuration achieved through this technique is designed to provide a broad contact area between the tendon and bone, which is crucial for enhancing biological healing. Research indicates that double-row repairs and suture-bridge techniques enhance contact pressure and footprint coverage compared to single-row repairs, resulting in decreased re-tear rates and improved clinical outcomes [30–32].However, it is important to note that the present study did not include direct biomechanical testing, and the claim of preserved biomechanical advantages is inferred from the favorable clinical and radiological outcomes observed, rather than from direct comparative biomechanical evidence.However, conventional double-row techniques' complex nature and cost have limited their widespread acceptance. Our method simplifies the procedure while aiming to preserve repair strength and clinical effectiveness.

When compared to other techniques addressing similar challenges, our modified simplified lasso-loop method shares the goal of minimizing "dog-ear" deformities with techniques like the modified suture-bridge [22] and the "Trident" technique [21]. However, it differs by utilizing only a single medial anchor and a knotless lateral fixation, potentially offering a simpler and more economical construct. The clinical outcomes appear comparable to those reported for the locking-loop suture bridge repair described by Wang et al. [27], though direct comparison is limited by the retrospective nature of our study.

The simplified LassoLoop Suture Technique has demonstrated significant clinical efficacy in arthroscopic repair of small to medium rotator cuff tears in this study. This is supported by notable improvements in patient-reported outcomes and functional measures, as evidenced by postoperative assessments using the VAS, the UCLA Shoulder Score, Constant-Murley Score, and shoulder ROM. The absence of re-tears in the study suggests satisfactory stability of the construct. Additionally, this approach's potentially reduced surgical time and implant costs could enhance its attractiveness for surgeons and healthcare systems. However, the prolonged average hospital stay observed in this study (6.8 ± 0.39 days) may be attributable to the implementation of structured postoperative rehabilitation protocols. Consequently, the economic advantages derived from reduced implant utilization must be critically evaluated against the potential cost implications associated with extended hospitalization, which may simplifiedly offset the initial savings.

Despite these promising results, this study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and the absence of a control group treated with an alternative technique limit the strength of our conclusions regarding comparative efficacy and cost-effectiveness. The claims of reduced operative time and cost, while supported by our absolute values, require validation through direct comparative studies. Second, the zero complication rate, while a positive outcome, must be considered in the context of the study's design. The relatively small sample size, the strict inclusion criteria selecting for optimal candidates (smaller tears, good tissue quality, isolated supraspinatus involvement, low Hamada grade), and the mid-term follow-up duration are likely contributing factors. Larger prospective studies with broader inclusion criteria are needed to more accurately determine the true incidence of complications and re-tears. Third, the biomechanical properties of this novel construct were not directly tested. The claims regarding biomechanical advantages are extrapolated from the technical principles and clinical outcomes and require confirmation in dedicated biomechanical studies [13, 33]. Fourth, the generalizability of the findings is limited to small and medium-sized supraspinatus tears with favorable tissue characteristics [34]. Fifth, intraoperative elasticity was judged subjectively with a tissue grasper, which lacks validated reliability and may introduce observer bias. Lastly, the rehabilitation protocol, while standardized, could be a confounder for the excellent outcomes, and its contribution is difficult to isolate [35].

Future prospective, randomized controlled trials with larger cohorts, longer follow-up periods, direct biomechanical comparison, and formal cost-effectiveness analyses are needed to validate these findings and establish the technique's role in the broader context of rotator cuff repair.

Conclusions

The Simplified LassoLoop Suture Technique, combining a single medial row suture anchor with a knotless lateral row anchor in a suture-bridge configuration, presents a promising simplified approach for arthroscopic repair of small to medium supraspinatus rotator cuff tears. This approach simplifies suture management, reduces operative complexity, and minimizes implant usage while demonstrating satisfactory clinical outcomes in this study, showing significant improvements in pain, shoulder function, and range of motion, with no complications such as re-tears or anchor failures observed during the follow-up period. However, further research with larger cohorts, extended follow-up periods, comparative designs, and biomechanical testing must confirm this method's long-term efficacy, biomechanical performance, and broader applicability in rotator cuff repair. Nevertheless, this streamlined strategy presents a potential alternative for enhancing surgical outcomes and optimizing healthcare resource utilization.
