Discourse of patriarchy through gendered language: A study of EFL textbooks in Bangladesh
Authors: Afroza Aziz Suchana
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study aims at exploring the equal representation of male and female characters in the textbooks for young learners through the examination of the linguistic usages in the textbooks to show how biased discourses may demonstrate patriarchal social norms. Using a mixed-methods approach, it analyzed the English for Today (EfT) textbooks for Classes I, II, III, IV, and V of Bangladesh. Analyzing the contents of the textbooks, the current study examined the linguistic usage of the textbook writers. The findings of the study revealed that female characters, in comparison to male characters, were underrepresented through the use of language. For example, male characters outnumbered females in most of the cases in terms of nouns, pronouns, etc. Biased diction was also used for females. The results of the study, thus, exposed the unfair manifestations of gender and patriarchal oppression through discourses. At the end, the study suggested that textbook writers should produce gender-equitable textbooks and teachers should convey gender-neutral messages in the classroom. Hence, this study will contribute towards eliminating disparity and enhancing gender equality through the textbooks of the primary level of education in Bangladesh.

Introduction

Gender equality and the dismantling of patriarchal norms are pivotal in promoting a just and equitable society. Language, as a potent tool for communication and cultural transmission, plays a significant role in shaping societal beliefs, norms, and attitudes. English as a Foreign Language (EFL) textbooks are considered tools for learners to make connections to the whole world and markers of social and educational achievements [19].

While learning English (the target language), learners also learn about culture and social practices from EFL textbooks. According to Khalid & Ghania [17], “Although the principal purpose of the textbook in the EFL classroom is to provide opportunities for language learning and skills practice, it can serve as an ideal vehicle for the embodiment and transmission of moral values” (p. 774). However, Khalid and Ghania [17] also discovered widespread discrimination in favor of males in the EFL textbooks in Algeria. Such discriminatory gender roles in EFL textbooks can be an invisible barrier to learning moral values and social practices [1,20]. In the context of Bangladesh, Kabir [16] also exposed gender discrimination in the textbooks. However, he worked on a secondary level textbook (for Classes IX and X). Kabir's study provided a comprehensive understanding of gender discrimination in textbooks, but it did not explore the specific representations of gender equality. Moreover, despite numerous studies on gender equalities in educational materials, few focused on primary textbooks in Bangladesh, particularly in relation to patriarchal representations. This lack of focused research created a gap, as existing studies largely overlooked whether these textbooks continue to reflect traditional patriarchal norms or acknowledge the evolving role of women in contemporary Bangladesh. This study aimed at filling that gap by providing a more nuanced analysis of the gender dynamics in primary textbooks, offering insights into whether they perpetuate outdated stereotypes or align with contemporary societal changes. Through this investigation, the study contributed to the growing body of literature by addressing an area that remains unexplored in previous studies.

The traditional society of Bangladesh is based on patriarchal values. Thus, domination of men and subordination of women exist in Bangladeshi society. Domestic violence, oppression, unequal pay, and low wages for women, for instance, are still found as common practices. Though women in Bangladesh are supposed to enjoy gender equality by the general law and constitution of Bangladesh, patriarchal explanations and interpretations of the law paves the way towards practicing patriarchy [33]. Women in Bangladesh, however, are now educating themselves and entering into the paid labor forces to resist patriarchy [6]. Such change in women was viewed as ‘new womanhood’ by Chowdhury [5] and Hussein [14]. While steps have been made to advance women's rights, subtle and pervasive manifestations of patriarchy might persist, influencing language and communication patterns. EFL textbooks, being crucial educational resources for millions of Bangladeshi students, hold the potential to either foster inclusive and equal attitudes or perpetuate gender biases and inequalities.

Therefore, this research aims to critically examine the representation of gender through discourse and the reinforcement of patriarchal norms in EFL textbooks used in the primary schools in Bangladesh. By identifying the underlying gendered language and analyzing how it constructs gender roles, stereotypes, and power relations, valuable insights can be gained into the role of language in shaping attitudes towards gender. The findings will encourage the materials developers to produce more gender equitable textbooks. Thus, the study will contribute towards eliminating disparity and enhancing gender equality through the primary textbooks.

Hence, the study delves into the discourse of patriarchy through gendered language in the English for Today (EfT) textbooks for Classes I to V in Bangladesh prescribed by the National Curriculum and Textbook Board (NCTB). In Bangladesh, primary education spans from Class I to Class V, forming the foundation of the country's educational system. The government of Bangladesh has prioritized primary education as a key focus area and has implemented several policies to ensure equitable access to quality education for all children, regarding their socio-economic background. One such policy mandates that students receive their textbooks free of charge, ensuring that the same textbooks are uniformly distributed and used in all schools across the country. Thus, there are no regional differences in the distribution or use of these textbooks.

The national primary school curriculum includes subjects such as Bangla, English, mathematics, science, social studies, religion and moral education, and physical education. While the medium of instruction is predominantly Bangla, English is taught as a foreign language, with English for Today (EfT) being the government approved textbook series for English instruction. These textbooks, developed by the National Curriculum and Textbook Board (NCTB), are used in both Bangla medium and English version schools for Classes I to V, making them a central part of English language education across the country.

Given their widespread use and the uniformity in their distribution, EfT textbooks offer a unique opportunity to examine how gender is represented at the primary education level. The fact that these textbooks are used nationwide underscores their potential to influence students’ perceptions of gender roles from a young age. This study specifically focuses on the EfT textbooks because of their critical role in shaping the linguistic and cultural understanding of gender among primary school children in Bangladesh.

Literature review and theoretical framework

Considering the universal dominance of English, textbook researchers expressed their concern about investigating gender bias in English Language Teaching (ELT) materials. ELT resources, such as textbooks, often convey both implicit and explicit cultural and moral values [38]. However, the prevalent marginalization of women was revealed globally in the English language textbooks by Mihira et al. [20] and Tyarakanita et al. [38]. In their study, Mihira et al. [20] chose thirty articles published in 2010–2021 to conduct secondary research on gender inequality in textbooks. They used the results of the previous studies to answer their research questions. Findings revealed the depiction of women as less dominant, and inferior than men, mostly domestic, usually weak, powerless, voiceless, and passive in the English language textbooks. In their study, Mihira et al. [20] analyzed the linguistic features, such as the use of nouns, pronouns, titles, etc. Tyarakanita et al. [38] also revealed restricted social roles of women compared to men in two English language Indonesian textbooks of junior high school level. Such restricted roles for women and men's linguistic supremacy may reinforce the patriarchal narrative.

Discourse of patriarchy

The term ‘discourse of patriarchy’ can be defined as the ways in which language and communication practices legitimate, reinforce, and regularize the dominance of men over women in various social, cultural, political, and economic contexts. According to Lazar [21], "patriarchal discourse is a system of language that functions to maintain the power relations and social structures that privilege men and subordinate women" (p. 6). This discourse operates by embedding and reproducing patriarchal values through everyday language and communicative practices.

Hence, language can serve as a medium of conveying societal structures, including gender, whic.h can impact perception and cognition [38]. ‘Linguistic representations’ refer to examples of representing gender through any level of language, such as lexical items, grammatical structures, turn-taking, etc. [26]. Numerous textbook researchers conducted their investigation on gender equality from different perspectives, which included linguistic features such as - the use of nouns, pronouns, firstness, and choice of words. The linguistic choices of the authors can considerably affect learners' attitudes and dispositions toward themselves and others [19]. In their study, Lee and Gahrouei [19] stated that gender inequality can manifest in the form of linguistic sexism. Linguistic sexism “refers to the use of words or expressions in a way that intentionally or unintentionally excludes a gender, usually females” [19, p. 1110]. Similarly, Khalid and Ghania [17] also suggested that fairness and equality between the two sexes can be maintained through language. When language is used in a way that perpetuates sexism, it can influence the cognitive and emotional aspects of its users [36]. Thus, gender discrimination can be minimized through the use of equal language. Hence, the study analyzed the use of nouns, pronouns, and adjectives; the sequence of male-female terms and titles, dictions, and dialogues.

Prevalent usage of masculine nouns and pronouns was identified in the previous research studies (from 2010 to 2021) by Mihira et al. [20]. They traced that discrimination in the use of nouns and pronouns can manifest the indiscernibility of the female characters. The use of masculine generics such as he/him/his, man/men is one of the common examples of linguistic sexism. Besides, compound words ending with ‘man or men’ for unknown genders also represent sexist language [19]. Inequality in the use of nouns and pronouns in textbooks was found by Bahman and Rahimi [3]. In their study on gender representation in the three volumes of Iranian English textbooks, Bahman and Rahimi [3] employed both qualitative and quantitative approaches to examine the number of female and male referring terms and the frequency of first-place occurrences of words attributed to males and females. Their study exposed unfair manifestations of women and men. The appearance of men was more emphasized than that of women in terms of names, nouns, pronouns, and adjectives attributed to them. They found that the percentages of nouns, pronouns, and adjectives of men were 79 %, 78.8 %, and 71.5 %, but those of women were 21 %, 21.2 %, and 28.5 % in the volumes, respectively.

Besides, enlisting male terms before female is one kind of linguistic discrimination [3]. The sequence of male terms before females is another trait of the unequal and unfair treatment of women and men, and this certainly prioritizes men [23]. Equality was not maintained with regard to firstness in numerous textbooks across the globe. Bahman and Rahimi [3] found that when both female and male referring terms came in a text, it was almost always the male term that exceeded the female term. Such discrimination in ordering represents the social construction of gender in patriarchal societies, which prioritize men.

Inequality was also found in the titles of the English language and the dictions used by the textbook writers. Islam and Asadullah [15] found the use of “Mrs.” or “Miss/Ms.” a significant category in linguistic sexism. The term “Mrs.” identifies a married female in terms of her male counterpart. Such discrimination in titles in the textbooks manifested male dominance [20]. In their study, Mihira et al. [20] mentioned that the titles Mr. and Ms. showed signs of gender inequality and unfairness in the textbooks. Tyarakanita et al. [38] referred to the use of “Mrs.” as a form of marginalized representation of gender that distinguishes women based on their marital position. By using such discriminatory words, textbook writers present the discriminatory personality traits of male and female characters. Therefore, Sunderland [36] suggested using “Ms.” to indicate both married and unmarried women to elude linguistic sexism.

Further, gender inequality can also be manifested through dialogues; if textbooks represent gender-imbalanced dialogues, female students may be affected [19]. Islam and Asadullah [15] pointed out that women were often found weak, victimized, passive, and subordinate in their interactions in textbooks. On the other hand, the representation of male characters reflected quite the opposite personality; they were portrayed as bold, courageous, and dynamic agents in society.

To sum up, it is beyond doubt that unequal treatment of gender in textbooks can be an obstacle to gaining gender-balanced knowledge. Blumberg [4] studied gender equality in school textbooks. His case studies from Syria, India, Romania, China, and the United States found unfair treatment between men and women in the textbooks, and he mentioned this discrimination as virtually an obscure obstacle on the way to gender parity in education.

Hence, the current study analyzed the linguistic features to investigate discourse of patriarchy in the English language textbooks of primary level education in Bangladesh. The study looked to see whether unequal power relationships and patriarchal oppression were presented through the use of language using a feminist linguistic approach as its theoretical underpinning.

Feminist linguistic approach as a theoretical framework

Since the idea of patriarchal discourse is firmly anchored in feminist theory and critical discourse analysis, the study employed a feminist linguistic approach as a theoretical lens to examine the discourse in the textbooks. The feminist linguistic approach focused on the use of language in discourse. Though Lakoff's [18] deficit theory viewed men's language as standard and women's language as deficient, the feminist linguistic approach viewed such discriminatory languages as patriarchal discourses. Thus, the diction used by the writers in the textbooks should manifest equality with regard to the frequency of male referring nouns, pronouns, male-female appearances, etc., as unequal languages demonstrate the power of one gender.

The feminist linguistic approach also viewed language as a tool to convey beliefs and social practices, and language can raise awareness among people. Domination of one gender, power, and social stratification are treated as important variables in feminist linguistic approaches. Mills and Mullany [24] worked on feminist linguistic approaches and stated that ‘gendered language’ conveys the discourse of patriarchy, where women become disempowered.

Discourse has a variety of meanings, and the actual meanings can be comprehended through the use of language and social context [35]. Mills and Mullany [24] also referred to Sunderland's concepts of ‘gendered discourses’ in their work. Sunderland [35] preferred the term ‘gendered’ to ‘gender-related’. She pointed out that ‘gendered’ is stronger as it describes that ‘gender is already a part of the thing’. Sunderland [35] referred to the ‘discourse of gender difference’ as a significant lens to view social reality.

Therefore, the feminist linguistic approach allowed the researcher a finer understanding of how the social constructions of gender, unequal power relationships, and patriarchal oppression have been represented in the textbooks of the primary level of education in Bangladesh through the use of language.

Methodology

To carry out the current study, a mixed-methods approach was applied, which combines both quantitative and qualitative data collection processes. One of the strengths of the mixed-methods approach is that “by using both QUAL and QUAN approaches researchers can bring out the best of both paradigms” [9]p. 45. The main justification for adopting a mixed-methods approach is its comprehensive ways of interpreting data. Hence, by using a mixed-methods approach, the study included statistical information and at the same time observed whether the textbooks portrayed gendered discourse in stories and exercises.

To investigate gender equality, the entire contents of the EfT textbooks (from Class I to V) were examined, focusing on some criteria. At first, the study measured the contents of the textbooks in terms of frequency. For example, it quantified how frequently male/female referring nouns and pronouns appeared and analyzed the frequency of firstness, which is how many times females/males came first. Then the study applied a qualitative content analysis approach. It traced the way female and male characters were depicted in the texts and the images. Their personality traits were revealed. The choices of words by the textbook writers were identified and then interpreted carefully.

This study chose the content analysis method for collecting both numeric data and for observing and interpreting the stories, images, messages, and exercises of the textbooks. According to Weber [40], content analysis is a systematic methodology used to derive valid inferences from textual data through a set of objective and consistent measures. In this research, the contents of the EfT textbooks for Classes I to V were analyzed using both quantitative and qualitative criteria. The quantitative analysis focused on linguistic features such as the frequencies of male and female nouns, pronouns, and the representation of gender in titles and expressions. This included examining equality in ‘firstness’ (which gender is placed first) and the dictions used in gendered terms. The data were manually counted and percentages were calculated, with tables and figures illustrating the findings. The manual approach was found sufficient given the relatively small sample size (five textbooks).

For the qualitative analysis, the study explored the active versus passive roles assigned to male and female characters, the diction used in dialogues, and whether the contribution of women were minimized through gendered language. These features were analyzed whether patriarchal discourse persists in the textbooks. This qualitative content analysis follows the principles outlined by scholars such as Schreier [37] who emphasize the systematic approach to interpreting textual data.

Thus, both obvious and implied messages were examined to explore the discourse of patriarchy by using quantitative and qualitative data collection tools. It also interpreted the implicit hidden meanings of the content of the textbooks.

Results

To identify how gender roles are portrayed in the textbooks through language, this section first investigated the frequency of male and female referring nouns and pronouns, as well as their occurrences in first or second place, using tables and figures to illustrate these frequencies. Additionally, it explored gender roles through a qualitative analysis of the stories, exercises, and image captions. This analysis highlighted the stereotypical presentations and assessed the discourse of patriarchy within the textbooks.

Firstness

The linguistic aspect of representing male and female characters can involve the concept of firstness, which refers to the priority or prominence given to a particular gender. In this study, firstness was recorded by counting how many times females or males were placed first in sentences or dialogues in the textbooks for Classes V, IV, and III. However, as there were very few dialogues in the textbooks for Class II, and Class I, firstness in the images was counted instead of contents. Thus, firstness was recorded by counting manually how many times females or males were placed first. Then some examples were included, and interpretations were added.

The priority of the male characters was noticed in the findings of Fig. 1. Findings revealed that in the fifth-grade (Class V) textbook, in 41 % of cases, female-referring terms came first, whereas, in 59 % of cases, male-referring terms came first. In 17 sentences, male-female pairs were used, where female names came first in 7 sentences and male terms came first in 10 sentences. Diction in titles and expressions

The textbooks revealed instances of linguistic sexism in the authors' choice of language. Discriminatory practices were evident in the titles, vocabulary, and expressions used in the English language textbooks for Classes V, IV, III, and II. These biased representations highlighted the underlying discourse of patriarchy.

Class V

Discrimination was explored in the title used for men and women in the fifth grade (Class V) textbook. In Lessons 3 and 4 of Unit 2, two women were introduced as Mrs. Haider and Ms. Smith. Here, one male character was introduced at the same time, named Mr. Saha. All of them were working people. In the case of Mr. Saha, the question did not come whether he was married or unmarried, but when Mrs. Haider was introduced, it showed that she must disclose that she was married. In the case of Ms. Smith, her marital status was not clear. Using the title Mrs. for one lady and Ms. for another one, students might be curious about the marital status of the second one, but for their male counterparts, no such question would arise. The lesson explained the use of the titles in the following ways:

For men, use Mr. With the full name, or just with the last name. For married women, use Mrs. and add the husband’s last name. For all women, you can also use Ms. (Pronounced Miz). Ms. does not tell you if a woman is married or not. [27], p.8

From this lesson, students may perceive that a woman must take up her husband's last name after marriage since she has to take up the title “Mrs.” after marriage. Also, the book gave examples of Ms. Smith. Here, students might be confused about whether the last name Smith was her husband's or father's name. In Unit 16, Lessons 1 and 2, another school teacher was found named Mrs. Alam. The use of the title “Mrs.” to address a woman suggests her identity in relation to her husband or male partner, while the titles “Miss.” or “Ms.” refer solely to her identity. However, in Bangladesh, usually teachers are not addressed by “Mrs.”; instead, their original names are used.

Besides these, male characters often appeared as stronger than females, which demonstrated the discourse of patriarchy. In Unit 1, Lessons 4 and 5, at the Town Hall Language Club, Tamal and Sima, two young children listened to English language CDs and watched DVDs, as well as conversing in English with their friends. Equality was found there, as one male and one female were shown practicing English together. But they met a new person named Andy Smith. The male child Tamal knew him and Sima did not. And Tamal took the responsibility to introduce the girl to him. Again in unit 2, lessons 1 and 2, Tamal was shown very busy as he was taking medicine to his grandmother. In unit 12, lesson 1, again, the dialogue between Andy and Tamal was found to involve Andy wanting to know about some tourist spots in Bangladesh. Andy has already visited Cox's Bazar. Then Tamal recommended Andy visit Sreemangal and Saint Martin's Island. Here, Tamal shared his knowledge about various tourist places and offered suggestions. This is how the knowledge and leadership qualities of the boy Tamal were demonstrated through dialogues and expressions in the textbook for Class V, whereas Sima appeared as a very passive girl. Thus, discrimination was exposed as the dictions used for Sima and Tamal did not show equal attributions.

Class IV

The discourse of patriarchy was also revealed in the textbook for Class IV. A large number of boys were found in a race competition in Unit 10, Lessons 1–2, of the textbook for Class IV. In the race competition, three girls and five boys participated. Along with the large number, it has also been noticed that a boy named “Fazle’ won the match, whereas a girl named ‘Tamanna’ got the second position. Such representation showed the secondary position of women. The boy “Fazle’ was also referred to as ‘very fast’. Besides, another boy named ‘Samir’ got the third position.

Similar discrimination in the diction was noticed in Unit 17, Lessons 1–2, through an image where some boys and girls were shown. Among them, one boy was running, and a girl named Mita mentioned, “The boy is running very fast” [28] p. 34. Further, a boy named Salam informed that the girls were playing, but no adjective was used with girls.

In addition, positive attribution was used to refer to the male characters only. For example, in Unit 15, Lessons 1–3, Sagar's grandfather was referred to as a ‘very smart’ man. However, in this unit, the female characters – Sagar's grandmother and mother - were shown busy with mundane household chores, and they are not referred to as ‘smart’ persons.

Thus, clear gender discrimination was noticed in the use of adjectives, since ‘fast’ and ‘smart’ – these were reserved only for men. These selective uses of adjectives reinforced stereotypical gender roles, portraying men as capable and dynamic, while women were relegated to traditional, passive roles without acknowledgement of their qualities.

Class III

Discrimination in the title was found in the English language textbook for Class III, like the textbook for Class V. For instance, in Unit 1, Lessons 1–3 of the EfT textbook for Class III [29], a female teacher was addressed as Mrs. Fareha. The name Mrs. Fareha appeared again in Unit 30, Lessons 1–3, along with a male teacher named Mr. Mahbub. Another female teacher was addressed as Mrs. Moury in Unit 31, Lessons 4–5. Discrimination was revealed here as the title ‘Mrs.’ indicates the marital identities of those teachers, but this issue (marital status) did not arise while addressing the male teacher. Moreover, social reality was not presented here, as in Bangladesh, teachers are not addressed as Mr. or Mrs.

Class II

Linguistic sexism was noticed through the discriminatory title in the textbook for Class II, like the other textbooks. In Unit 26, Lessons 1–3, a female teacher was introduced as Miss Mariam. The title ‘Miss.’ referred to the marital identity that she was unmarried. However, male characters did not face such discrimination.

Hence, the EfT textbooks were found embedded with a biased demonstration of gender in terms of the use of language. The use of language, including nouns and pronouns, the preference for placing men first, and the word choices made by the writers revealed the extent of male dominance. In contrast, women were underrepresented in a range of aspects, such as the use of nouns and pronouns, firstness, leadership qualities, etc. Such dominance of men and diminished role of women demonstrated discourse of patriarchy in the textbooks.

Discussions

The study showed a notable imbalance in the use of male-referring nouns and pronouns compared to female ones in the textbooks. Though some classes (e.g., Class II) showed a more balanced representation, while others (e.g., Class V) are more biased. Moreover, male characters were often mentioned first in sentences and dialogues, reflecting a prioritization of male presence. This was evident across multiple classes, nevertheless some equality was noted in specific instances in Classes II and I. Besides, the use of titles and descriptions often highlighted the marital status of female characters while leaving male characters' statuses unaddressed. Male characters were also depicted with more active and leadership-oriented attributes.

These results are in line with a substantial body of research that showed enduring gender biases in instructional materials. Global trends are consistent with the male-dominated language use patterns, narrative prioritization, and biased title usage. However, depending on local cultural factors and the particular content of the textbooks studied, there may be differences in the level of bias and the particular examples used. This study identified certain areas where changes might be made and recommended developing more gender-balanced educational resources.

Domination of male-referring nouns and pronouns

Discrimination in the male-female ratio may create the impression among the learners that textbooks demonstrate a gender hierarchy that favors males [8]. However, the findings of the current study revealed that textbooks at the primary level of education are dominated by male-related nouns. Among the five, male referring nouns outnumbered the three textbooks. Discrimination was highly visible in the textbook for Class I, where the male-female ratio was 78 % and 22 %. More male-referring terms indicated male domination. Mihira et al. [20] revealed that the domination of men in English textbooks may reproduce the marginalization of women among young school children. Thus, the supremacy of men may disregard women. However, in the use of the pronoun, gender balance was found in the four textbooks among the five. Though this was an encouraging example, more could have been done, as the more predominant use of male referring pronouns than female ones manifests dominance [20]. Besides, the frequent use of male-referring nouns and pronouns in the textbooks implies that the textbooks have been constructed from an androcentric point of view [1]. If more examples highlighting female characters are represented by textbook writers, girls will be conscious regarding their rights and power, as consciousness acts as a liberating force [32]. If equality is found in portraying female and male characters, both groups will be aware of their power and potential. Eventually, they will equally contribute to society.

Second-place position of women

The referencing of male characters before female characters manifests the second-place position of women. The findings of the study revealed that the primary textbooks lacked equality in terms of firstness. Male and female characters were not paired equally. The use of ‘he’ before ‘she’ in most of the sentences in the textbooks demonstrated the first-place position of men. Such first-place positioning of men in the use of pronouns might indicate the sexist language [39]. Equality was not maintained with regard to firstness in images along with languages (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Though a female image appeared first in Fig. 3, embedded discrimination was noticed as a boy started the conversation, which displayed ‘masculinity traits’. Mihira et al. [20] listed speakers as being bold, formal, brave, and strong - masculinity traits- and, in contrast, listeners as being weak, passive, and dependent – femininity traits. Thus, though Fig. 3 showed two girls and two boys, the critical image analysis exposed implicit discrimination. Such subtle distinctions can be considered as signs of males' superiority over females [17].

Hence, equality was not maintained in the ‘order of appearance’ in the textbooks for Classes V, IV, III, II, and I. Such ordering reinforces the second-place status of women and the superiority and dominance of men [1,13]. Moreover, stating males before females in textual content gives more worth to males, which reflects or adheres to some deeply rooted traditional attitudes. Thus, the precedence of one gender exposes the social reality in the textbook. However, despite this social reality and contextual constraints, equality can be established in textbooks through the conscious efforts of the textbook writers.

Patriarchy through diction

Variation in the choice of words for women and men depends on some social and cultural norms. Nevertheless, such linguistic variations may marginalize women [7,36]. Stereotypical femininity and masculinity were revealed in this study through the dictions and dialogues used by the textbook writers in the primary textbooks in Bangladesh. Discrimination in the use of titles, leadership qualities of men, support and guidance of men, and dependence of women became visible. Moreover, adjectives denoting positive qualities, such as – ‘very smart’ and ‘very fast’ were only associated with male characters, which reinforced the superiority of men. Representing only men (not women) with positive attributes has been viewed as linguistic patriarchy by Azad [2].

Different titles for males and females appeared in the EfT textbooks for Class V, Class III, and Class II. Women were addressed as Mrs./Miss. and Ms.; these titles exposed their marital identities, whereas for males, only one title ‘Mr.’ is used. The English language offers only one title for men and multiple titles for women, which may mislead young learners. Moreover, using one title (Mr.) for all men and three titles (Miss, Mrs., or Ms.) for women exposes linguistic sexism. A language that exhibits sexism can have an impact on both the cognitive and emotional aspects of the individuals [36]. Though linguistic sexism is a feature of the language and the English language often offers gender-biased terms, Musty [25] suggested that textbook writers can decide to use some unconventional terms for betterment. “According to feminists such differences in the ascription of titles for both genders are mentioned as a major cause of gender discrimination” [1, p. 11]. Besides, feminist linguistic approaches considered linguistic discrimination as a way to reinforce gender inequality. Mills and Mullany [24] worked on feminist linguistic approaches and stated that gendered language conveys the discourse of patriarchy, where women become marginalized. Hence, to promote gender fairness, it is crucial to replace discriminatory language with more inclusive alternatives [38].

Besides, presenting Tamal as a knowledgeable person in the textbook for Class V (Lesson 1, Unit 12) demonstrated the superiority of men. Tamal's leadership quality was also noticed through the dialogues with Andy and Sima in the story, which reflected the supremacy of a man. Such dominance of men portrays women as weaker linguistic partners [26]. Montgomery [25] also revealed that traditionally, men are viewed as competitive in conversation and dialogues, whereas women are treated as being supportive and cooperative. Montgomery's findings also resemble Lakoff's [18] ‘Deficit Model’, which created a dichotomy between women's language and men's language. Lakoff [18] explored that women's language demonstrates their lower status and weaker position and men's dominance in society. However, such dominance of men through language and in adopting an active role asserts men's authority in society [17]. Thus, it can be said that Tamal's active role was presented in a ‘polarized’ way through the dialogues in unit 12, lesson 1. Such ‘polarization’ and gender differences through language convey the discourse of patriarchy [24]. Besides, in the story ‘How far is Saint Martin's?’ (Lessons 1–8, Unit 12), Sima was presented as a passive girl who lacks leadership qualities. Patriarchy shows the passive and powerless position of women, and such traditional repressive power imposes restrictions [12]. Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) theorists argued that gendered language reflects patriarchal power relations by positioning men and women differently. According to Fairclough [10], gendered language is one of the ways in which power relations are reflected and reinforced in discourse.

Besides, the success of men in sports was also depicted in the textbooks. Showing the boy Fazle's first position and the girl Tamanna's second position in sports in the EfT textbook for Class IV (Lessons 1–2, Unit 10) represented the inferiority of one gender. Moreover, women's success in sports was ignored there. From this example, students in primary schools may perceive that in sports, boys' participation and capacities are higher than those of girls. In reality, the Bangladeshi women's cricket team won the T20 series against Ireland in 2018. Earlier, they (the women's cricket team) won the women's T20 Asia Cup in Malaysia. Thus, the discourse of patriarchy was demonstrated in the textbook by underrepresenting women in sports. Such underrepresentation and inferiority of one gender may create boundaries and hierarchies in social life [14]. Thus, limiting the participation and success of women may reproduce patriarchal ideology.

Moreover, ‘gendered discourse’ was also visible through the use of adjectives. In the English language textbook for Class IV, the boy ‘Fazle’ (Unit 10, Lessons 1–2) and another boy (Unit 17, Lessons 1–2) were referred to as ‘very fast’. Likewise, in unit 15, lessons 1–3, Sagar's grandfather was attributed as ‘very smart’. However, no female character was referred to as ‘very fast’ or ‘smart’ in the textbooks from Class I to Class V. A similar type of adjectival discrimination was noticed by Musty [26]. In his study, Musty [26] mentioned that such discriminatory words reinforce stereotypes. Such dictions – ‘fast’ and ‘smart’ in the textbook for Class V indexed with masculinity, and these masculine traits showed greater respect for men. Such stereotypical masculinity marginalizes women [22]. Thus, women were linguistically underrepresented.

Hence, linguistic differences between genders were found in the above examples. From such discriminatory languages, learners may expect that boys will always be bold, active, saviors, and responsible, and girls will act under their guidance. However, in reality, in contemporary Bangladesh, women are participating and acquiring success in all spheres of society. Here, women are actively involved in politics and achieving success in sports. Even history revealed the leadership of women in Bangladesh through their (women's) participation in various movements [11]. In her study, Falgunee [11] explored how women in Bangladesh struggled and occupied themselves in various movements from the ancient period and became successful. However, such success stories of women were not projected through language in the stories or exercises in the textbooks. Nevertheless, the connection between the use of language and gender was focused on by Mihira et al. [20]. In their study, Mihira et al. [20] mentioned that many dictions and sentence constructions in textbooks create a demeaning effect on women. They (women) may perceive their inferior identity, as poststructuralist theory views language and identity as inseparable aspects [34]. Mihira et al. [20] also revealed that linguistic explanations often foster the biased perception that women cannot hold equal positions with their counterparts. They are treated unequally socially, culturally, politically, and professionally.

Thus, this study uniquely explored the discourse of patriarchy through gendered language in Bangladeshi EFL textbooks, an area that received limited attention in existing research. By employing a comprehensive mixed-methods approach, the study quantitatively analyzed the use of male and female referring nouns and pronouns and qualitatively examined narratives, exercises, and images. This dual analysis revealed how linguistic choices contribute to the reinforcement of patriarchal norms. The concept of 'firstness' was also introduced to highlight the subtle prioritization of males in dialogues and narratives. Spanning multiple grade levels, this research provided a longitudinal perspective on how gender roles were portrayed and evolved in early education. The detailed examination of diction and illustrative examples further exposed the nuances of linguistic sexism, offering fresh insights and practical recommendations for creating gender-inclusive educational materials. Hence, this study contributes significantly to the discourse on gender equality in education by exposing the underlying patriarchal biases in language and promoting awareness among educators and textbook authors.

Finally, to promote gender equality in education, the study recommends to develop gender-inclusive EFL materials. Textbook writers should ensure balanced representation by using equal numbers of male and female referring nouns and pronouns, prioritizing both genders equally in narratives, and avoiding biased titles and expressions. Incorporating stories and examples that showcase both boys and girls in active, leadership roles can help break down stereotypes. Additionally, it is crucial to raise awareness among teachers and textbook writers about the importance of gender-sensitive language and imagery. Training programs and workshops can equip educators with the tools to critically evaluate and select materials that foster an inclusive learning environment. Implementing these strategies can lead to the creation of educational resources that reflect and promote gender equality, thereby empowering all students.

Conclusion

To sum up, the study identified the assimilation of the patriarchal association of power and gender biased language in the textbooks of the primary level of education in Bangladesh through a minute examination of the contents of the textbooks. It examined linguistic discriminations in the textbook writers' word choices, firstness, and usage of nouns and pronouns. The findings of the study revealed that biased representations of gender are present in the EfT textbooks for Classes I, II, III, IV, and V. Thus, equality was not maintained in the ‘order of appearance’ in the textbooks. Such biased attributions were evidence of linguistic patriarchy. Therefore, women may be marginalized as a result of this discriminatory depiction in language. Hence, the study suggested raising awareness among textbook writers to produce gender-equitable textbooks to develop a sense of equality among young learners. Further, it recommended raising gender awareness among the teachers so that they promote gender equality in the classroom.
