Dynamic and precise electromagnetic levitation of single cells
Authors: Malavika Ramarao, Victor Garcia-Gradilla, Mehmet Burcin Unlu, Ronald W. Davis, Naside Gozde Durmus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe biophysical properties of single cells are crucial for understanding cellular function and behavior. Magnetic and density characteristics have been used to separate cells in microfluidic channels; however, precise control over individual cell positions is challenging, especially after cells reach equilibrium. Here, we introduce the “Electro-LEV” platform, which uniquely combines electromagnetic and magnetic levitation principles for precise 3-D control of cell position. Unlike traditional systems, Electro-LEV offers spatiotemporal control over cellular levitation without altering paramagnetic conditions, magnetic configuration, or device geometry. Furthermore, Electro-LEV enables dynamic control and sorting capabilities, allowing micrometer precision of single-cell levitation height and enhancing cell sorting efficiency by 10-fold. Electro-LEV’s refined sorting and precision have the potential to significantly advance cellular biology and therapeutic strategies.

Cells, the fundamental units of life, possess micro- and nanoscale components that contribute to their unique magnetic and density signatures. These biophysical properties, arising from genetic and proteomic variations, as well as differences in organelle composition, reflect diverse cellular states and are crucial for understanding various physiological and pathological conditions (1, 2). Characterizing these properties at the individual cell level can reveal subpopulations within seemingly homogeneous groups, offering deeper insights into cellular heterogeneity.

Previously, we demonstrated the magnetic levitation of single cells as a label-free approach with broad applications in biology and medicine. Using the magnetic levitation approach, cell populations can be characterized and separated based on their density and biophysical properties. In the levitation platform, cells levitate in a paramagnetic medium between permanent magnets, reaching an equilibrium height determined by the balance of magnetic, buoyancy, and gravitational forces. Magnetic levitation has successfully sorted various cell types and states, including cancer cells from blood cells, infected red blood cells from healthy counterparts, sickle red blood cells from normal red blood cells, and cardiomyocytes with different lipid cargo (3–7). Moreover, magnetic levitation has been applied to self-assemble single cells into complex 3D structures (8–10). Although magnetic levitation technologies have been applied to various cell types, precise control and manipulation of levitation profiles were limited to altering levitation medium composition, paramagnetic concentration, or fluid density (1).

Here, we present the Electro-LEV platform that uniquely enables precise control over magnetic manipulation and sorting of cells, via precise electrical currents running over wire coils with an iron core (electromagnets) along the z-axis. In Electro-LEV, the magnetic field components are directly proportional to the electromagnet current, allowing for the superposition of fields generated by multiple electromagnets (11, 12). We present that this linear relationship enables optimization of the magnetic field distribution by controllably adjusting the applied currents in different coils, providing a straightforward method to modulate dynamically and precisely the magnetic force acting on the cells. Cells within the device migrate to different equilibrium levitation heights in response to changes in the magnetic field, enabling enhanced dynamic control and flexibility over cell positioning along the channel. Crucially, Electro-LEV allows for real-time adjustments to cellular levitation profiles without necessitating the preparation of new samples and solutions with altered paramagnetic concentrations. We demonstrated that Electro-LEV significantly improves the purity and efficiency of the levitational sorting process, without the need to adjust microfluidic flow rates. This capability introduces an approach to analyze a key dimension in cellular biology: cell variability within seemingly homogeneous or heterogeneous populations (13, 14). The ability to make on-demand adjustments to the applied electromagnetic field is essential for resolving cell populations during real-time levitational sorting. Hence, Electro-LEV introduces an additional dimension in cell sorting technology, offering high-resolution separation without the need for fluorescent labels or antibodies.

Results

Platform Design and Development.

The Electro-LEV platform consists of i) two permanent neodymium bar magnets (50 mm length, 2 mm width, and 5 mm height), ii) two wire-coil electromagnets, and iii) a glass capillary (1 mm × 1 mm cross-section, 50 mm length, and 0.2 mm wall thickness) between the magnets (Fig. 1A). The permanent magnets are positioned with the same poles facing each other, and the electromagnets are vertically aligned and concentric with the permanent magnets on two axes. The fundamental equations governing electromagnetism in physics state that for any closed loop path, the dot product of the length elements and magnetic field is equivalent to permeability multiplied by the electric current enclosed within the loop. By controlling the current through the electromagnet coils, the magnetic field applied to the cells can be precisely manipulated. Additionally, the levitation position of cells can be dynamically adjusted along the z-axis by controlling the polarity and current of the electromagnets.

During real-time sorting operations within Electro-LEV, the capillary is integrated with a microfluidic chip consisting of an inlet and two outlets (top and bottom). This setup allows heterogeneous cell populations to be sorted into two separate outlets based on their magnetic levitation heights. The current applied to the electromagnets can be adjusted during sorting to manipulate the levitation height of different cell states (i.e., live and dead cells) (Fig. 1B). For instance, as the current increases, the equilibrium height of the cells within the capillary also increases (Fig. 1B). Thus, Electro-LEV achieves z-axis control, integrating electromagnetic fields, microfluidics, and magnetic levitation to achieve targeted and customizable levitation positions to sort heterogeneous populations with high purity and efficiency.

Cells in the capillary reach their equilibrium height when the gravitational force (Fg) equals the buoyancy force (Fb) added to the magnetic induction force (Fm) acting on the cells. Cells equilibrate to the center of the capillary (y0), where the magnetic induction strength is the lowest, and Fg = Fb + Fm. The exact equilibrium height of the cells can be calculated using the magnetic induction force (B) and the expanded form of Ampere’s Law (1, 15–17).Δχμ0Br∂Br∂r+Bz∂Bz∂z-Δρg=0.

In this equation, the cylindrical coordinate system is used, where Br is the radial and Bz is the axial component of the magnetic field. Both components are equally important in understanding the magnetic field inside the capillary. The azimuthal component is neglected due to the symmetry of the setup. The axial field component Bz primarily provides the lift force that balances gravity and levitates the cells. Δχ¯ is the difference in magnetic susceptibility between the cells and the surrounding paramagnetic solution χc-χs, where the magnetic susceptibility of the cells is assumed to be negligible compared to solution susceptibility. μ0¯ is the permeability of free space, and Δρ¯ is the difference in density between the cells and the paramagnetic solution (ρc-ρs¯). When no current is applied to the electromagnets, the magnetic field is symmetric about all axes from the center of the capillary, and the equilibrium height of the cells depends on their density, as we have previously demonstrated (Fig. 1C) (1). When current is applied to the electromagnets, the magnetic field in the capillary changes, and, in particular, when the current of the electromagnets has the same polarity, the location of the minimum magnetic field in the capillary changes in height according to the current magnitude (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Therefore, applying current to the electromagnets allows rapid adjustment of the cells’ equilibrium position.

Electromagnetic Levitation of Cells.

We first validated the capabilities of Electro-LEV by manipulating the levitation profiles of polyethylene beads as well as a variety of cell types including, MDA-MB-231 (breast adenocarcinoma), NIH 3T3 (fibroblasts), A549 (lung carcinoma), SK-MEL-28 (melanoma), circulating hybrid cells (CHC), and white blood cells (WBC) (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S3). By controlling the applied current direction, we demonstrated the on-demand manipulation of cells “up” toward the top magnet when the electromagnet polarity is set in the “up” direction and 2A current is applied. For example, the average levitation height of MDA-MB-231 cells at 50 mM Gd was 657.0 ± 42.0 µm at 0A, 787.7 ± 34.8 µm at 1A, 919.5 ± 52.7 µm at 2A, and 1035.6 ± 47.8 µm at 3A. Similarly, the average levitation height of murine white blood cells at 50 mM Gd was 691.8 ± 46.4 µm at 0A, 770.2 ± 50.97 µm at 1A, 969.9 ± 55.8 µm at 2A, and 1063.0 ± 42.1 µm at 3A (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Across different cell types, a positive 1A change in current consistently resulted in an approximate 100 µm increase in levitation height. For each cell line, the current applied to the electromagnets significantly correlated with the levitation height of the cells (one-way ANOVA, P < 0.0001). Each 1A change in current led to a significant change in levitation height (Tukey’s multiple comparison test, P < 0.0001). Interestingly, even at low paramagnetic concentrations of 10 or 20 mM, where cells typically levitate too low in the capillary for effective sorting, the effect of applied current on the electromagnets remained significant (two-way ANOVA, P < 0.0001) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). In addition, we demonstrated that applying current to the electromagnets levitated the cells to a more suitable height for sorting (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Thus, by precisely controlling the electrical current intensity in the electromagnets, the levitation height of cells can be further manipulated using Electro-LEV.

To further demonstrate the dynamic control capabilities of Electro-LEV, we manipulated the levitation heights of MDA-MB-231 cells in the capillary by alternating the current between positive and negative values at approximately 10-s intervals (Fig. 3A and Movie S1). Cells responded rapidly to these changes in the magnetic field, rapidly altering their levitation heights. Thus, this experiment illustrates the precise temporal control over levitation heights achievable with Electro-LEV through modulation of the electromagnet current. Such programmable dynamic and spatial control over cellular positions was previously unattainable in magnetic levitation platforms. Traditional magnetic levitation systems can only fine-tune levitation heights by altering paramagnetic medium concentration, magnet strength, or intermagnet distance (1, 3–5, 18). In contrast, Electro-LEV offers spatiotemporal control over cellular levitation positions without requiring adjustments to paramagnetic conditions, magnet design, or device geometry.

In addition, we analyzed the levitation heights and velocities of individual cells and cell clusters over time (Fig. 3 B and C). Interestingly, single cells and cell clusters exhibited distinct responses to changes in the magnetic field. Upon current application to the electromagnets, cell clusters reacted more rapidly and demonstrated higher velocities compared to the single cells as their equilibrium positions shifted (Fig. 3C). This observation aligns with our previous findings that larger levitating objects reach their equilibrium positions more quickly (1). The differential response between single cells and cell clusters in the Electro-LEV system introduces a unique dimension for analyzing seemingly homogeneous cell line populations, potentially revealing subtle heterogeneities within cultured cell lines.

Electro-LEV Sorting of Live and Dead Cells.

We then evaluated Electro-LEV’s capability to sort cells in different physiological states. First, we generated levitation height profiles of live and dead cells from two different cell types: NIH3T3 and MDA-MB-231. As anticipated, when current was applied to the electromagnets, the levitation height of both live and dead cells increased (Fig. 4 A and B). Both the paramagnetic media concentration and the applied current led to highly significant changes in the levitation heights of live and dead cells (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4C). To optimize the paramagnetic concentration and applied current to separate live and dead cells, we generated comprehensive levitation height profiles and calculated separation distances (Fig. 4 C and D). Our goal was to maximize the separation between live and dead cells while ensuring that cells were positioned at an appropriate levitation height for accurate sorting into top and bottom outlets. Based on these criteria, we determined that 50 mM Gd and 2A current provided ideal conditions for real-time sorting of live and dead cells. Under these optimized conditions (50 mM Gd, 2A current), MDA-MB-231 live cells exhibited an average levitation height of 919.5 ± 52.7 µm, while dead cells levitated at 695.8 ± 97.3 µm, yielding a separation of approximately 200 µm. Although lower paramagnetic media concentrations resulted in greater separation distances, dead cells under these conditions levitated too close to the bottom of the capillary, potentially complicating collection and increasing the risk of clogging of the device. In addition, having established that the Electro-LEV system could achieve substantial separation between live and dead cells, we investigated whether exposure to the paramagnetic solution and increased magnetic field affected cell viability. Our results indicated no significant difference in cell viability after exposure to the magnetic field generated by high current (3A) and/or 50 mM [Gd] (P = 0.0797, SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

To further demonstrate the application of Electro-LEV for the enrichment of live cells, we analyzed the efficacy of real-time sorting under two different conditions: 50 and 10% starting viability. In both conditions, applying a 2A current to the electromagnets allowed the live and dead cells to levitate at appropriate heights for sorting into the top and bottom outlets, respectively (Fig. 5 A and B and Movies S2 and S3). In contrast, the traditional magnetic levitation platform without current resulted in both live and dead cells levitating in the bottom half of the capillary, and thus being collected in the bottom outlet (Fig. 5 A and B). To quantify the sorting efficacy, we performed viability assays on samples from the original heterogeneous solution and the sorted fractions from both outlets. Fluorescence microscopy of stained cells demonstrated selective enrichment of live cells with high purity in the top outlet, while dead cells were collected in the bottom outlet (Fig. 5 C and D). The enrichment of live cells in the top outlet was maintained even as the ratio of live to dead cells decreased significantly, demonstrating the utility and versatility of Electro-LEV for processing samples with low viability (Fig. 5E). For instance, with a 50% starting viability, Electro-LEV achieved a 10-fold enrichment of live cells in the top outlet, resulting in an output viability of 92.52% ± 0.44 (Fig. 5E and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). In contrast, without Electro-LEV (0A current), we observed a 0.18-fold de-enrichment of live cells in the top outlet, with a final viability of 19.76% ± 17.77 (Fig. 5E and SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). In addition, with a 10% starting viability, Electro-LEV achieved an 18.8-fold enrichment of live cells in the top outlet, resulting in an output viability of 69.53% ± 9.93 (Fig. 5E and SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Without Electro-LEV, there was a 0.05-fold de-enrichment of live cells in the top outlet, resulting in a final viability of 0.71% ± 1.23. These results were consistently replicated in both MDA-MB-231 and NIH3T3 cell lines, resulting in similar purity and fold enrichments (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). We also showed that Electro-LEV sorting did not affect the long-term cell viability and proliferation over 7 d (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). In summary, Electro-LEV sorting is a gentle method that can purify samples with a wide range of starting viability into a consistently high viability output, while retaining a high viable cell recovery rate and throughput. Additionally, cells sorted using Electro-LEV show a proliferation rate ranging from 88.86% immediately postsorting to 97.75% at 7 d postsorting.

Discussion

Magnetic levitation is a key technique used to analyze the biophysical properties of cells and isolate different cell populations. However, to our knowledge, no method exists to manipulate the levitation position of magnetically levitated particles with high dynamic precision. This dynamic control without changing the sample composition or device design is especially key for accurate sorting, where slight differences in cell levitation height can drastically alter sorting purity and efficiency, potentially wasting important clinical information the sample contains.

Here, we have developed a dynamic platform, Electro-LEV, that enables real-time manipulation of levitation profiles for cells or particles suspended in a paramagnetic solution. The Electro-LEV design incorporates wire coils along two axes to introduce precise levitation control unattainable in previous magnetic levitation approaches. Using Electro-LEV, we demonstrated the ability to rapidly and consistently manipulate the magnetic field using electromagnets, thereby altering the levitation profiles of cells or particles. We validated the consistency of our results across various cell lines. By tracking the levitation height and velocity of cells, we identified differential responses between single cells and cell clusters to changes in the magnetic field, suggesting Electro-LEV’s potential to explore subtle variations within seemingly homogeneous cell populations.

As a technical innovation, Electro-LEV introduces on-demand z-axis movement and improved control for real-time adjustments during levitational sorting. For instance, the biophysical properties of cells change during apoptosis, making dead cells denser than their live counterparts (1). This alteration makes magnetic levitation an ideal technique for sorting of live and dead cells. Traditional magnetic levitation platforms (e.g., MagDense) lack the capabilities for high-purity sorting, as cells do not levitate at the correct heights to be directed into top and bottom outlets, respectively. Electro-LEV addresses these limitations by allowing the optimization of cell levitation heights in static experiments and real-time adjustments during sorting. Using Electro-LEV, we achieved extremely high purity (>90%) of live cells from a 50% starting viability heterogeneous mixture. Even with an initial sample viability of 10%, we attained over 18-fold enrichment of live cells in the top outlet. We demonstrated the efficacy of this sorting procedure on two different cell types: MDA-MB-231 (breast cancer) and NIH3T3 (fibroblast). The biophysical differences between live and dead cells are likely present in other cell lines, suggesting the broad applicability of this platform across diverse biological samples (1, 19, 20).

Cell sorting has become essential in many laboratories, particularly as an initial step for downstream sample processing in molecular biology and tissue engineering. A persistent challenge in sample preparation is the removal of dead cells and debris, which can cause nonspecific binding and background noise (21–23). For instance, single-cell RNA sequencing requires samples free of extraneous RNA, debris, and dead cells to obtain high-quality data (24, 25). Most sequencing kits require >85% viability in the starting sample, as dead cells release ambient DNA that can lead to inaccurate read counts. In drug toxicity testing and selection, rapid label-free live/dead sorting enhances the sensitivity of downstream cell screening assays (26–28). Even in routine cell culture, when low-viability stocks are thawed, dead cells can release toxic factors, underscoring the need for live/dead sorting to improve the initial viability of plated cells (29–31). In clinical settings, during stem cell transplantation procedures, implanted apoptotic bodies can trigger inflammatory responses, potentially leading to infection, organ failure, and patient mortality (32–34). While fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) and magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) are commonly used for viable cell enrichment, they have limitations. FACS subjects cells to high shear pressures, reducing postsorting viability, while MACS requires cell attachment to beads that can damage cell membranes (35–38). Alternative label-free methods such as acoustophoresis, passive cell sorting based on membrane stiffness, and dielectrophoresis have been developed; however, these approaches often expose cells to harsh forces or chemicals, lack versatility across cell types, or show poor enrichment at low (<30%) starting viabilities (39–41). Our results indicate that Electro-LEV sorting is gentle on cells, as they are not exposed to shearing forces, harsh chemicals, or binding agents present in other live cell enrichment protocols. Postsorting, cells maintained high viability and continued to proliferate in culture. The automated Electro-LEV system can process large sample volumes rapidly, with a single unit capable of processing approximately 72 mL/h through a 1 mm2 cross-sectional area. This high throughput is particularly crucial in clinical settings where rapid turnaround time is essential to preserve cell viability and morphology.

In conclusion, Electro-LEV represents a significant advancement in cell sorting technology, offering a label-free, efficient, and gentle method for live cell enrichment. Its ability to process samples with low initial viability makes it particularly valuable for analyzing and sorting frozen clinical samples, removing dead cells and debris to preserve the integrity of downstream analyses. The versatility, efficiency, and cell-preserving nature of Electro-LEV position it as a promising tool for various applications in basic research, drug discovery, and clinical sample processing.

Materials and Methods

Electro-LEV Design and Operation.

The Electro-LEV levitation device consists of two permanent N52 neodymium bar (50.80 mm by 5 mm by 2 mm) magnets (K&J Magnetics) with poles facing each other and 18 gauge laminated iron core inductors (each 76.05 mm by 15.32 mm by 15.27 mm with approximately 300 wrapped wire coils) held in place by a stereotaxic system (1). The electromagnets have an inductance of 10 mH with a 3.49 mm distance between the two electromagnets. Two DC power supply devices control the current supplied to the inductors. Directly between both the bar magnets and inductors is a 1 mm channel where the capillary, for static measurements, or the flow-based system fits. The flow-based system was constructed of a 1 mm glass capillary, laser-cut polymethyl methacrylate pieces, Tygon microbore tubing, and 30-gauge blunt-end needles that serve as the top and bottom outlets. The flow rates at the top and bottom outlets were kept at 5 ul/min for all experiments and were controlled using a syringe pump. The live cells would be collected in the syringe connected to the top outlet, and the dead cells or debris would be collected in the syringe connected to the bottom outlet.

Modeling and Simulation.

To model the magnetic field generated by the two permanent magnets and two electromagnets, we used the “Magpylib” library in Python. The permanent magnets were modeled as cuboids with opposite magnetization directions, separated by a 1 mm gap. The electromagnets were represented as current loops positioned above and below the permanent magnets. We varied the currents applied to the electromagnets for different scenarios.

The simulation was performed on a 2D grid spanning the length of the electromagnets and the gap between them. For each top electromagnet current value, the magnetic field components were calculated using the “getB” method of the “Collection” class, which takes into account the contributions from all magnetic elements. The resulting magnetic field was then visualized using a streamplot, where the color of the streamlines represents the logarithm of the magnetic field strength.

The simulations were repeated for four different top and bottom electromagnet currents (i.e., 0A, 1A, 2A, and 3A). The results were presented in a systematic subplot layout, with each subplot corresponding to a specific current configuration. The subplots were clearly labeled with the respective top and bottom electromagnet currents, and a colorbar was provided to map the streamline colors to the logarithm of the magnetic field strength, ensuring a comprehensive and clear presentation of the findings. This simulation provides insights into the interaction between the permanent magnets and electromagnets, and how the magnetic field can be manipulated by varying the current supplied to the electromagnets and observing the changes in the magnetic field distribution.

Cell Culture.

NIH-3T3, MDA-MB-231, SK-MEL-28, and A549 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (Cytiva), high glucose with L-glutamine, supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco). Circulating hybrid cells (CHCs) are an MC38-derived hybrid cell line (42), graciously supplied by Dr. Melissa Wong’s lab at Oregon Health & Science University, cultured in DMEM (Cytiva), high glucose with L-glutamine, supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum, 1 mM Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco), 1 mM MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids (Gibco), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Sigma), 1 mM Hepes (Sigma), and 0.05 mM Gentamicin Sulfate (Corning). Cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere.

Static Magnetic Levitation.

For imaging of static magnetic levitation, cells were diluted to a concentration of 100,000 cells per mL in a 100 μL sample with the indicated Gd concentration ([Gd]). The [Gd] referred to in static and sorting experiments is gadobutrol, a nonionic, FDA-approved paramagnetic solution containing Gd3+, that is used for contrast imaging. For the live/dead separation static images, cells were pulled from the same cell culture flask, divided into two containers, one of which was subjected to 60 C for 45 min, which resulted in extensive cellular damage and death of these cells (43). The live and dead cells were each diluted to a concentration of 50,000 cells per mL in a combined 100 μL sample with the indicated Gd concentration ([Gd]). Then, 30 μL of the sample was loaded into a 1 mm square glass capillary (Vitrocom) and placed in the levitation device. Cells were given 10 min to reach their levitation equilibrium height in the device before static images were taken. All static images for levitation height analysis were taken using Amscope software and saved using a “.jpg” format. The images were analyzed using a MATLAB automated image processing code developed by the lab (1, 3–5, 18).

Real-Time Electromagnetic Levitation Sorting of Live and Dead Cells.

For real-time sorting experiments, live and dead cells were diluted to a total cell count of 100,000 cells per mL, with a starting viability of 10 or 50%, in a 1 mL sample with 50 mM Gd. The indicated current, either 0A (as control) or 2A, runs on the electromagnets throughout the sorting experiment. Approximately 250 μL of the sample is sorted during each experiment with flow rates of 5 μL per minute at both the top and bottom outlets. Then, 75 μL of the initial sample was saved along with the collected samples from the top and bottom outlets for analysis in a 48-well plate. All wells were stained with calcein AM (Invitrogen) for live cells and NucSpot 555/570 (Biotium) for the dead cells. All images of the wells were taken at 10x using the EVOS M5000 microscope and saved as “.tiff” files for analysis using a MATLAB viability image processing code previously developed by the lab (1, 3–5, 18).

Long-Term Viability Analysis After Electro-LEV Sorting.

After exposure to different [Gd], current, or sorting conditions, MDA-MB-231 cells were collected and plated on a 48-well plate. Cells were stained with calcein AM (Invitrogen) for live cells and NucSpot 555/570 (Biotium) for the dead cells at different time points. All images of the wells were taken at 10x using the EVOS M5000 microscope and saved as .tiff files for analysis using a MATLAB viability image processing code previously developed by the lab.

Levitation Height and Velocity Analysis.

All static images taken for levitation height analysis were analyzed using an in-house MATLAB code, as previously reported (1, 3–5, 18). Briefly, all RGB images are converted to grayscale, and Gaussian filtering is used to blur the image and decrease high-frequency noise. The image is then converted to binary, and an edge filter is used to detect the outlined shadows of each cell. The centroids of each qualifying area are found, the circularity of each is calculated, and the pixel height is counted to obtain the average levitation height of the cells. To perform the velocity tracking of the cells, the levitation height of the cell was recorded (using the technique above) for every other frame in the 26 fps video, and a first derivative of the curve with a 0th-order smoothing polynomial was performed using GraphPad.

Viability Immunohistochemistry Analysis.

All fluorescent images of cells stained with calcein AM and NucSpot 555/570 were analyzed using a MATLAB viability image processing code. The colorThresholder app was used to create red and green RGB masks, which were then applied to the RGB images before they were converted to greyscale. Gaussian smoothing filters are applied to reduce image noise. The image is then converted into binary. Each grouping of positive pixels is evaluated based on size and circularity to determine whether it represents a cell. All identified red or green cells are counted. Purity or cell viability is calculated as the number of calcein AM stained cells divided by the total number of counted cells (calcein AM or NucSpot 555/570 stained cells) in the well.

Statistical Analysis.

All statistical analyses, including two-way ANOVA, one-way ANOVA, and Tukey Multiple Comparisons, were performed using the inbuilt analysis features in GraphPad Prism Version 10.2.2.
