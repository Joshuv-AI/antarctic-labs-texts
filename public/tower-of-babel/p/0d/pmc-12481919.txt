Complex genetic landscape in CLL: Clinical impact on patients’ survival and insights into the altered molecular pathways
Authors: Carlo Caputo, Massimo Donadelli, Mariateresa Scupoli, Giovanni D’Arena, Giuseppe Pietrantuono, Aldo Giudice, Angela Giliberti, Silvestro Volpe, Rossella Sperlongano, Domenico Cautela, Bernardina Scafuri, Angelo Facchiano, Alessia Maria Cossu, Marco Bocchetti, Gabriella Misso, Angela Lombardi, Michele Caraglia, Anna Grimaldi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is characterized by a remarkably complex landscape. We performed a molecular characterization of liquid biopsies from 137 leukemia samples and 10 healthy subjects. Almost all patients express single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) mutations of the TP53 gene and the most common (n = 132 patients) was the Pro72Arg missense mutation. It was associated with significantly higher overall survival (p < 0.001) in untreated patients (n = 73) compared to treated patients (n = 58). However, this TP53 mutation is identified in both patients and healthy volunteers being likely a germinal mutation classified as SNP. In addition, a rare missense SNP mutation (Arg337Cys) of TP53 gene, correlated positively with clinical outcome (204 months; p = 0.00001) was also found. Its effects on p53 stability and function were subsequently evaluated. A missense mutation (Met541Leu) in the KIT gene was found in 18 patients and it was also significantly associated (p < 0.05) with a favorable outcome in untreated patients (n = 10) compared to treated ones (n = 8). We performed a comparative analysis among CLL patients classified into three prognostic groups (Binet A, B, and C). Survival analysis confirmed that Binet C patients had a significantly reduced survival compared to Binet A patients (p < 0.05).

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is defined as an acquired, clonal, chronic lymphoproliferative disorder that can be characterized by the accumulation of lymphocytes in both lymphoid and extra-lymphatic organs. The diagnosis of CLL requires the presence of ≥5 × 109/L lymphocytes in the peripheral blood, sustained for at least three months. The clonality of these B lymphocytes must be confirmed by demonstrating immunoglobulin light chain restriction using flow cytometry. The leukemia cells observed in the blood smear are characteristically small, mature lymphocytes with a narrow cytoplasmic border and a dense nucleus that lacks discernible nucleoli and features partially aggregated chromatin. Gumprecht nuclear shadows, or smudge cells, found as cellular debris, are additional morphological features commonly associated with CLL.1,2,3,4,5 CLL or small cell lymphocytic lymphoma (SLL) may be suspected in otherwise healthy adults who have an absolute increase in clonal B lymphocytes, but who exhibit <5 × 109/L B lymphocytes in the blood. However, in the absence of lymphadenopathy or organomegaly (as detected by physical examination or imaging studies), or of disease-related cytopenia or symptoms, the presence of <5 × 109/L B-lymphocytes is defined as monoclonal B lymphocytosis.6 A small percentage of larger or atypical cells, or prolymphocytes, can be found mixed with morphologically typical CLL cells. Finding ≥55% prolymphocytes would support a diagnosis of prolymphocytic leukemia; however, this diagnosis remains challenging and is based solely on morphological criteria, as no reliable immunological or genetic marker has been identified. A significant proportion of circulating prolymphocytes (≥10%) appears to indicate a more aggressive form of CLL (with NOTCH1 or genetic TP53 aberrations).7,8 The average age at diagnosis is around 65–70 years and less than 15% of cases are diagnosed before the age of 60. In 1990, Juliusson et al. first recognized that some chromosomal abnormalities are significant in CLL in terms of prevalence and prognostic impact.9 They are identifiable in approximately 40% of cases; the most common are trisomy 12 and 13q14 deletion.10 In a study of 325 untreated CLL patients, Döhner et al. identified the frequent localization of cytogenetic abnormalities at chromosomes 6, 11, 12, 13, 14, and 17 in CLL. More precisely, there are five recurrent alterations: 13q deletion; trisomy 12; and 11q, 17p, and 6q deletion. The loss of 13q14.3 represents the most frequent cytogenetic anomaly in CLL, with a prevalence of 40%–60%.11,12,13 It differs from other chromosomal aberrations because it can occur in heterozygous form (monoallelic in 76% of cases) or in homozygous form (biallelic in 24%). Trisomy 12 is among the most frequent aberrations in CLL, occurring in 10%–20% of cases.11 In addition, as additional evidence of the favorable prognostic significance of trisomy 12, the latter is rarely associated with TP53 mutations and, in 25% of cases, with NOTCH1 gene mutations, especially in patients with unmutated IGVH14 and presents a more aggressive outcome.15 Approximately 20% of CLL patients with advanced stage disease have a deletion of the 11q region.10 Patients with del11q- typically have numerous lymphadenopathy, type B systemic symptoms, young age, very rapid progression, a shorter treatment-free survival (TFS) and reduced overall survival (OS).16 The chromosomal region typically deleted is represented by the band 11q22.3-q23.1 which contains the ATM gene which codes for a protein kinase activated in case of double-stranded DNA damage, thus participating in the regulation of the activity of cell cycle “checkpoints” crucial for the control of apoptosis and DNA repair.10 17p deletion occurs rarely in untreated CLL patients at onset (3%–8%), while it is frequent in patients with advanced and treatment-refractory disease (30%).11,17,18 The prognostic significance of this anomaly is unfavorable. From a pathogenetic point of view, in case of del17p-, the constant loss of TP53 gene is observed. This deletion is associated with resistance to conventional chemotherapy, such as fludarabine and alkylated agents, as their mechanism of action is TP53-dependent.10,15 In 20% of CLL cases, a cytogenetic profile defined as a complex karyotype is detected, characterized by the simultaneous presence of at least 3 chromosomal abnormalities. In these instances, the disease exhibits increased aggressiveness.19,20,21 Therefore, in cases with normal fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) results, it is advisable to perform also metaphases cytogenetic analysis using the new stimulating agents.10 In CLL, the inactivation of TP53 can be secondary to deletions and/or mutations. In the majority of cases (approximately 70%), the 17p13 deletion is also associated with TP53 mutations in the second allele, while it is monoallelic in 20% of CLLs; in 10%, only the TP53 mutation is present in the absence of del17p13.15 The molecular alteration found in TP53 is mostly a “missense” substitution (75% of cases) especially at the level of exons 5–8, encoding the DNA binding domain; in the remaining 25%, these are “truncating” events, “non-sense” substitutions and mutations in “splice” sites. Inactivation of TP53 is the most frequent alteration in cases of high-risk CLL. In fact, the frequency of this mutation is rare at diagnosis (5%–10% of cases), increases at the first disease progression (10% of all cases), and further to 40% in relapsed/refractory CLL. Several authors have studied the prognostic impact of this anomaly, demonstrating how the inactivation of TP53 gene leads not only to a reduction in survival but is also associated with a poor response to treatment with purine analogues (fludarabine, pentostatin, and cladribine) and alkylating agents (chlorambucil, bendamustine, and cyclophosphamide) in combination or not with rituximab.22,23 Therefore, the study of TP53 activity may be important not only for the definition of the prognosis but also for the choice of first-line treatment.15,24,25 Clinical management of CLL patients is based mostly in a “watch and wait” strategy unless a clear disease progression is demonstrated. Currently, the therapeutic scenario is drastically changed for reasons that include age and performance status of the patients, introduction of new drugs and/or therapeutic regimes and, finally, the identification of different prognostic factors, both related to progression and clinical course of the disease.26,27 For many years, monotherapy based on alkylating agents, such as chlorambucil and cyclophosphamide, was the gold standard treatment.28 In the present study, we have analyzed the mutational pattern of CLL patients using a panel of 52 different genes with next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology. The CLL patients were collected from the real-world practice in the frame of the evidence-based medicine to define new molecular markers able to predict the response to standard treatments in CLL and to elucidate the signaling pathways responsible for resistance to therapy.

Results

Chromosomal alterations’ analysis

We observed that more than half of CLL patients showed at least one cytogenetic abnormality. Therefore, we divided them into different groups based to determine the impact that each of them on OS. The OS of CLL patients with chromosomal aberrations was, as previously reported,9 more unfavorable than patients with normal karyotype. We have compared the survival of patients with different chromosomal aberrations. We found that del(17p) or del(11q) patients had a poorer prognosis with a median overall survival (mOS) of 26 months (95% confidence interval [CI]: −32.6–127.6 months, p value: 0.155) and 42 months (95% CI: 7.4–114.6 months, p value: 0.032), compared to patients with trisomy 12, normal karyotype or del(13q) who instead showed a mOS of 146 months (95% CI: 80.4–171.8 months, p value: <0.001), 151 months (95% CI: 126.4–153.4, p value: <0.001) and 143 months (95% CI: 119.1–152.8, p value: <0.001), respectively (Figure 1). This could be explained by the involvement of the tumor suppressor genes TP53 and ATM on chromosome 17p and 11q, respectively. Among patients with abnormal karyotypes, those with trisomy 12 showed an intermediate prognosis with a mOS of 146 months (95% CI: 80.4–171.8 months, p value: <0.001). On the other hand, the clinical outcome of patients with del(13q) with mOS of 143 months (95% CI: 119.1–152.8, p value: <0.001) was similar or even better than patients without cytogenetic abnormalities who showed a mOS of 151 months (95% CI: 126.4–153.4, p value: <0.001) (Figure 1).Figure 1The impact of cytogenetics abnormalities on the probability of survival from the date of diagnosis among patients with CLLChromosomal alterations: del11q group del13q14 group del17 group normal karyotype group tris12 group.

Survival analysis by stratification of CLL patients based on the type of treatment

CLL patients, categorized into Binet A and Binet B stages, were stratified into distinct groups according to the treatment. This classification aimed to assess whether significant survival differences existed between patients undergoing different treatments in the different Binet stages. Stage B patients were divided into 3 groups: FCR (fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, and rituximab), RC (rituximab and chlorambucil), anduntreated. A significant difference emerged between FCR and RC groups (Figure 4). In fact, FCR group (n = 6) showed a longer mOS of 145 months (95% CI: 68.2–185.8 months, p value: 0.003) than RC group (n = 4) with a mOS of 119 months (95% CI: 25.9–181.5 months, p value: 0.024) (Figure 4). Moreover, FCR group (n = 6) also showed a longer mOS of 145 months (95% CI: 68.2–185.8 months, p value: 0.003) compared to untreated group (n = 13) with a mOS of 115 months (95% CI: 99.9–149.9 months, p value: <0.001) (Figure 4). However, given the small sample size of the groups treated with FCR and RC, additional studies with larger cohort are required to enable a statistically significant analysis. Effects of Arg337Cys substitution on p53 stability and function

We have analyzed the rare p.Arg337Cys mutation observing that it creates a conformational alteration of the protein that does not allow binding with the transcriptional factor p63. Arginine 337 is included into the region from 325 to 356, involved in oligomerization mechanism. Many studies in literature describe the 3D structure of p53 domains, and we investigated in detail the role of arginine 337 in the structure of the oligomerization domain on the structure described by the nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)-based study of Clore et al. The positively charge side chain of arginine337 is involved into inter-chain salt bridge with Asp352 as negative counterpart, and that salt bridge has a stabilizing role in the dimerization of p53. Substitution of Arg337 by cysteine removes the positive charge of arginine side chain and introduces a neutral side chain, thus making not possible the salt bridge formation, with a destabilizing effect on the dimer structure. The prediction of stability effects by means of two independent predictors, i.e., DUET and INPS-MD (see materials and methods) confirms the destabilizing effect of Arg337 substitution by cysteine. Both predictors return negative energy values (indicating destabilizing effect) for substitution occurring in any of the four chains (i.e., −0.76, −0.79, −0.61, and −0.79 kcal/mol in A, B, C, and D chain, respectively, by DUET server, and −0.50, −0.48, and −0.35 by INPS-MD server) (Figure S3). The function of p53 is mediated by interaction with other proteins. In particular, we evidenced by the Interactome 3D server the interaction between p53 and p63. Arg337 appears directly involved into the interaction in two out of the three different models of the interaction reported. This suggests that substitution of Arg337 by cysteine may determine a loss of ability of interacting with p63 (Figure S3).

Discussion

CLL is a chronic lymphoproliferative disease characterized by the abnormal proliferation of mature lymphocytes (B lymphocytes in 95% of cases and T lymphocytes in the remaining 5% of cases). Although these cells appear phenotypically normal, they exhibit impaired differentiation and reduced immunologic activity, contributing to their neoplastic nature.1,2,3,4 The diagnosis of CLL requires the presence of >5 × 109/L lymphocytes in the peripheral blood, sustained for at least three months. However, autoimmune complications are relatively common in patients with CLL, occurring in approximately 7%–25% of cases. The most frequent of these are autoimmune cytopenias, including autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) and immune thrombocytopenia (ITP). These disorders arise when non-malignant B cell clones produce autoantibodies that target membrane antigens on red blood cells (in AIHA) or on platelets (in ITP). These complications are also associated to a more severe prognosis.29 Interestingly, we have found that in our series, only 6 patients had both thrombocytopenia and anemia and displayed a worse survival if compared with the overall population (Figure S5). Moreover, in our series, no patients reported Richter syndrome.30 CLL has a variable progression, remaining stable for more than ten years in some cases, while rapidly progressing in others. CLL is the most common leukemia in the Western world and it is typical of elderly males. The average age at diagnosis is around 65–70 years and less than 15% of cases are diagnosed before the age of 60. It affects approximately 5 people in 100,000 each year and the incidence increases with age.31 The causes of CLL are unclear. At present, there is no evidence to show a relationship between the disease and exposure to radiation, chemicals, infections, or diet. However, several studies have shown that genetic or familial factors may predispose to the development of the disease. The diagnosis of CLL is established through the analysis of peripheral blood, allowing for the quantification and characterization of distinct lymphocyte populations. The diagnostic process proceeds with morphological examination and immunophenotyping that characterizes the surface molecules of CLL cells (co-expression of CD19 and CD20 together with CD5, CD23, CD43, and CD200) to distinguish them from normal lymphocytes and other forms of hematological malignancies.7,32,33 Currently in clinical practice, there are two different staging systems, Binet and Rai, which assign a stage to the disease based on some clinical characteristics such as the presence of anemia, thrombocytopenia, splenomegaly, and/or lymphadenopathy.34,35 In addition, there are other biological factors that allow for more accurate stratification of patients based on risk, thus improving prognosis and response to treatment such as the analysis of chromosomal aberrations (13q deletion, 11p deletion, 17p deletion, and trisomy 12),9,11,17 the analysis of mutations affecting the TP53 gene, and the analysis of the rearrangement of the immunoglobulin heavy chain genes. CLL is a slow-growing disease, often not requiring immediate treatment, but periodic monitoring to assess disease progression and to decide eventual therapeutic intervention. A broad range of therapeutic strategies is currently available for CLL, from chemotherapy or chemoimmunotherapy to targeted therapy.5,25,36 Anti-CD20 and anti-CD52 antibodies in combination with fludarabine represent the gold standard in the treatment of progressive CLLs, anti-CD52 being more promising for the relapsing patients. BTK inhibitors such as ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, or zanubrutinib are, moreover, a breakthrough treatment option for patients with CLL. Given their excellent toxicity profile, these agents may represent a suitable therapeutic option for those patients who failed first line treatments or have contraindications (i.e., advanced age, multiple comorbidities, and poor performance status). In fact, it is consolidated that BTK inhibitors are highly active in the treatment of CLL in the relapsed/refractory setting, inducing durable remissions in predominantly elderly, heavily pre-treated patients with high-risk disease characteristics. Use of BTK inhibitors is expected to dramatically change the treatment paradigm of CLL in the next years. TP53 aberrations are indeed strongly associated with both clonal evolution and aggressiveness of CLL. Techniques frequently used for assessing TP53 status in CLL include FISH, Sanger sequencing, and NGS for TP53 gene mutations. TP53 sequencing should cover exons 4–10 (corresponding to the DNA binding domain at codons 100–300 and the oligomerization domain at codons 323–365) at a minimum. Sequencing of the whole coding region (exons 2–11) and adjacent splice sites is highly recommended using NGS, as emerging evidence indicates that pathogenic variants, though less frequent, can also occur in exons outside the DNA-binding domain. The pathogenicity of frequent TP53 mutations is well known, with functional analyses demonstrating that all TP53 hotspot mutations result in a clear loss of p53 activity. The pathogenicity of some less frequently occurring TP53 mutations may be less clear, particularly in the case of missense mutations that can have functional consequences. Pathogenicity is assessed based on multiple criteria, including whether the mutation results in an amino acid substitution occurs within a conserved region or a mutational hotspot region, and is predicted to impact protein function through effects on splicing, structural integrity, or post-translational modification. In this scenario, the aim of this work was to analyze the impact of the complex genetic landscape of CLL on the OS probability to improve the definition of CLL risk stratification. The analysis of chromosomal alterations provides another important prognostic information on CLL patients’ survival.9,11,37 Consistent with previous findings, our cohort also demonstrated that patients without chromosomal aberrations exhibited longer OS compared to patients harboring del(17p) and del(11q), both of which showed an unfavorable prognosis with a drastic survival reduction. On the other hand, patients with trisomy 12q had an intermediate prognosis. However, molecular studies have allowed us to identify other important prognostic factors involved in CLL such as the mutational status of specific genes (e.g., TP53, KIT, KDR, and PKI3CA) that may or may not be associated with chromosomal alterations. In this perspective, we performed a molecular characterization of liquid biopsies from newly diagnosed CLLs by NGS. In our study, we found a relatively high incidence of TP53 mutations, mainly Pro72Arg (in 96.3% of the cohort), and other different missense SNP mutations expressed in very few patients. In addition, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed a significant correlation between Pro72Arg and Met541Leu mutations on TP53 and KIT genes, respectively, and patients’ survival. In particular, they were associated to a significantly higher overall survival in untreated patients compared to the treated ones. Previous literature indicates that CLL patients are frequently muted in the TP53 gene. Consistent with these findings, even in the analyzed cohort, most patients displayed mutations in this gene. However, these mutations have also been identified in healthy subjects, suggesting that TP53 mutational status, although serving as a reliable prognostic factor, is not likely involved in the pathogenesis of the disease. Interestingly, we found a rare TP53 mutation (Arg337Cys), not present in healthy volunteers, that induces a conformational change in the protein, impairing its ability to interact with p63. Specific residues in the α-helix and β-strand of the tetramerization domain were sensitive to inactivation by amino acid substitutions. These 15 residues are Phe-328, Leu-330, Ile-332, and Arg-333 in the β-strand; Gly-334 in a turn; and Arg-337, Phe-338, Phe-341, Arg-342, Leu-344, Asn-345, Ala-347, Leu-348, Leu-350, and Lys-351 in the α-helix. Leu-330, Ile-332, Gly-334, Phe-341, Leu-344, and Ala-347, especially, were highly susceptible to functional inactivation, being inactivated by at least three distinct substitutions. Substitution of the hydrophobic residues (Phe, Leu, Ile, or Ala) that form a hydrophobic core of the domain by non-hydrophobic residues or by structure-destroying proline tends to inactivate p53 function.38 To improve the understanding of the complex genetic landscape in the prognostic assessment of CLL patients, we performed a comparative analysis between stages A, B, and C (generally used by the Binet staging system) to improve risk stratification based on molecular differences. It was found that stage A patients had a more complex mutational pattern and showed longer survival than patients with stages B and C. Furthermore, the chemoimmunotherapy approach based on the combination of chemotherapy and MAbs, produced more effective therapeutic effects than other treatment strategies. These findings reinforce a broader use of BTK inhibitors in the management of CLL, particularly for patients with an unfavorable genetic profile. We have also identified novel and canonical mutations of genes that are able to predict the survival of CLL patients including KDR, KIT, PIK3CA, and MET. However, for some subgroup analyses (in detail, the subgroup del(17p), del(11q), the untreated group with p.Ile391Met mutation of PIK3CA gene, the treated group with p.Met541Leu mutation of KIT gene, the untreated and treated groups with p.Phe384Leu, p.Gly13Asp, p.Val722Ile, p.Asn375Ser, and p.Phe858Leu muation of FGFR3, KRAS, JAK3, MET, and ATM mutated groups, the stage C group, the FCR group stage A and B, the RC group stage B, the chemoimmunotherapy group stage A, the chlorambucil group stage A and the target-therapy group stage A), the number of patients analyzed is low thus deserving future investigation in larger multicenter cohorts. In conclusion, these data collectively suggest the importance of analyzing chromosomal aberrations before starting any treatment in CLL, as they have an important prognostic value for disease progression and survival. In fact, our study confirms their indication as a tool for the identification of prognostic subgroups at the time of diagnosis. In addition to the Binet staging system and cytogenetic analysis, it is also necessary to evaluate the mutational status at the gene level since specific alterations or mutational patterns can help in the prognostic and predictive stratification of CLL patients. Moreover, different gene mutations identified in our study could be target of innovative therapeutic strategies already included in ongoing clinical trials for patients who failed previous standard lines of treatment (Table S2). In fact, the identification of key pathways playing critical roles in the therapeutic response to CLL therapy, provides a foundation for additional research and development of targeted therapies. Overall, the obtained results provide a molecular framework for further investigations aimed at elucidating the genetic mechanisms underlying the onset and progression of CLL.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

A total of 137 liquid biopsies were collected from CLL patients enrolled at the U.O.C. of “Fondazione Casa Sollievo della Sofferenza” Opera San Giovanni Rotondo and at the Department of Neuroscience, Biomedicine and Movement of University of Verona. The study also included 10 liquid biopsies from healthy subjects as controls. This study was performed according to the informed consent procedure approved by local internal Review Board (protocol no. 20140040750–November 18, 2014), and it conforms to the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki. All CLL patients and healthy volunteers provided written informed consent prior to study enrollment. Furthermore, for the analysis of survival curves, CLL patients were assigned to two groups: treated and untreated. A total of 61 patients were assigned to the treated group and 76 to the untreated group.

Patient selection

The study started on July 2017 and ended on December 2023. The blood collection was performed at the moment of the first diagnosis and prior the beginning of any treatment. Inclusion criteria included a confirmed diagnosis of CLL or SLL, and meeting specific criteria for requiring treatment based on the International Workshop on CLL guidelines. The diagnosis of CLL was demonstrated by the presence of ≥5 × 109/L B lymphocytes in the peripheral blood, sustained for at least 3 months. The clonality of these B lymphocytes was confirmed by demonstrating immunoglobulin light chain restriction using flow cytometry. Exclusion criteria were active, uncontrolled infections, significant cardiac or liver dysfunction.

Mononuclear cell isolation and extraction of genomic DNA

Genomic DNA was extracted from Ficoll-separated peripheral blood mononuclear cell pellets. For each patient and healthy control in the study, 7.5 mL of peripheral blood was recovered and diluted 1:2 with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) without Ca2+/Mg2+. Mononucleated cells were recovered from peripheral blood samples by centrifugation using a Ficoll Histopaque (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 320 × g. The mononuclear cell ring was isolated and washed twice with PBS at 320 × g for 3 min. DNA extraction was carried out using PureLinkTM Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer. The DNA isolated from spin column was eluted in 25 μL of nuclease-free water and then DNA quantity was measured by a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies) using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The extracted DNA was stored at −20°C until use.

Molecular cytogenetics in CLL

For the analysis of genomic aberrations in CLL, we performed interphase FISH, which currently represents the standard approach since it has a higher resolution than conventional cytogenetics, allowing to detect chromosomal alterations in interphase cell nuclei and, therefore, independently of the cell division capacity. The FISH panel used contained fluorescent-labeled DNA probes for the most commonly altered regions in approximately 80% of LCC cases, including trisomy 12 as well as deletions of 13q, 11q, 17p, and 6q. Approximately 7.5 mL of blood was collected from each patient in EDTA. Each peripheral blood sample was treated with hypotonic solution (0–075 mol/L potassium chloride) and fixed with three changes of 2:1 methanol and glacial acetic acid.39 Blood specimens were first processed by density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll Histopaque (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 320 × g prior to cell preparation and FISH study.

Molecular profiling of chronic lymphocytic leukemia

The DNA was diluted to a final concentration of 0.67 ng/uL and 10 ng total of each sample was used for library preparation with Ion AmpliSeq Kit for Chef DL8 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), which, leveraging the automation capabilities of the Ion Chef System (ThermoFisher Scientific), allows for library preparation of eight samples in a single run, providing a single tube of pooled libraries diluted to 100 pM, purified, and equalized. Final library concentration was quantified by Qubit dsDNA HS Assay kit (Life Technologies) using the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies). DNA libraries were stored at −20°C. Subsequently, using Ion 510 & Ion 520 & Ion 530 Chef Reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific), the resulting purified libraries were automatically enriched and charge onto Ion 520 Chip for downstream sequencing, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the massive parallel sequencing of DNA libraries, we used Ion GeneStudio S5 Plus System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) that exploits the pH variations occurring during the incorporation of the single deoxyribonucleotide into the reaction catalyzed by the DNA polymerase.

Sequencing data analysis

We used the Torrent Suite Software v.5.18.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to assess run performance and data analysis. Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV v 2.2, Broad Institute) was used for visual inspection of the aligned reads. Specifically, only variants with allelic coverage ≥5× and a quality score ≥20 within an amplicon coverage of at least 500× alleles were called. Additionally, only mutations with variant allelic frequency ≥5% were recorded. The BED files from Torrent Suite were analyzed using Ion Reporter Software (IR v.5.20.8.0, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and filtered according to the quality control. We selected the SNVs resulting in a non-synonymous amino acid change, or a premature stop codon, and in short indels resulting in either a frameshift or insertion/deletion of amino acids. All SNVs were analyzed for previously reported hotspot mutations (somatic mutations described in COSMIC database) and novel variations, i.e., new mutations detected by NGS but not reported in either COSMIC or db SNP databases.

Statistical analysis

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were performed using IBM SPSS software (version 27.0) and the differences between the comparison groups were tested by the log rank test. Statistical analysis was set at two-sided and statistical significance was accepted at p value <0.05.

Molecular structure investigations

The molecular structure of the oligomerization domain of the tumor-suppressor p53 from the work of Clore et al.40 was obtained by consulting the RCS PDB database (www.rcsb.org),41 where it is archived under the accession number 1OLG. This archive provides data on the spatial arrangement of atoms within a protein in solution using NMR, which exploits the magnetic properties of atomic nuclei to measure their absorption of electromagnetic radiation when immersed in a strong magnetic field. Analysis of the 3D structure has been performed with DiscoveryStudio software, used also to generate image of the model. Predictions of the effects of amino acid substitution on p53 protein stability have been obtained by in-silico methods based on various chemical-physical and evolutionary hypotheses. In particular, two integrated computational approaches, DUET42 and INPS-MD,43 have been adopted, combining the analysis of complementary methods based on different paradigms and exploring different protein structural properties, improving the accuracy of predictions compared to single methods. DUET generates a prediction by combining the results obtained separately from two complementary methods: site detector mutator (SDM) and cutoff scanning matrix (mCSM). The SDM method explores the local structural environment of wild-type and mutant residues (secondary structure, solvent accessibility, and side-chain hydrogen bond satisfaction). Specifically, it compares the amino acid propensities for wild-type and mutant proteins in the folded and unfolded states to estimate the free energy differences between wild-type and mutant, thereby generating a stability score. In contrast, mCSM is a method for predicting the influence of missense mutations based on graph-based structural signatures. These encode patterns of distances between atoms and are used to represent the environment of protein residues and to train predictive models. mCSM uses a graphical representation of the wild-type residue environment to obtain geometric and physicochemical models that are then used to represent the 3D chemical environment. mCSM signatures demonstrate that the impact of a mutation can be related to the patterns of atomic distances surrounding an amino acid residue. The other integrated approach is INPS-MD (impact of non-synymous mutations on protein stability-multi dimension) that combines sequence and structural information to predict the perturbation of protein stability following a point mutation. In detail, INPS relies on features extracted from the protein sequence to encode a point mutation: single substitution score, the Kyte-Doolittle hydrophobicity scores of the native and modified residues (two descriptors), the mutability index of the native residue, the molecular weights of the native and modified residues (two descriptors), and the difference in alignment score between the native and variant sequences and an HMM, encoding the evolutionary information of the wild-type sequence. Instead, INPS3D also includes descriptors based on the 3D protein structure: a descriptor corresponding to the relative solvent accessibility (RSA) of the native residue, and another descriptor encoding the local energy difference (ED) between native and mutated protein structures. To study the interaction networks between p53 and other proteins, Interactome3D was queried, a web service based on a fully automated computational approach, able to collect, manage and integrate all the structural information, available in different databases, necessary for both single proteins and binary interactions, automatically generating the most reliable protein-protein interaction models.44

Analysis of the main pathways affected by the mutational events

We performed over-representation and pathway-topology analysis employing Reactome’s “Analyze Gene List” tool, which identifies whether certain pathways are over-represented in our dataset and examines the connectivity between molecules within these pathways (Figure S4). After submitting the gene list, the analysis tool performed the following processes: (1) the provided gene identifiers were mapped to Reactome’s database entries, ensuring accurate pathway associations; (2) the statistical test determined whether specific pathways were enriched in our dataset beyond what would be expected by chance, providing a probability score corrected for false discovery rate; and (3) the analysis examined the distribution of our gene set within the context of pathway structures, offering insights into how our data relate to the overall pathway architecture.
