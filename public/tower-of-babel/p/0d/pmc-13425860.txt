Selective reduction of thalamic glutathione in migraine in the absence of detectable N-acetylaspartate alterations
Authors: Rui Wang, Seungho Kim, Hongying Huang, Dafa Shi, Zhongruowen Ren, Changho Choi, Gen Yan, Yongmin Chang, Renhua Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOxidative stress has been implicated in migraine pathophysiology, but direct evidence of brain antioxidant alterations and their relationship to neuronal integrity remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS) study measuring seven metabolites in a single thalamic voxel of 131 participants (left or right, selected for spectral quality): 36 healthy controls, 46 migraines without aura, 17 migraines with aura, and 32 tension-type headache patients.ResultsGlutathione (GSH) was the only metabolite showing significant group differences (Welch’s F = 31.74, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.432). Thalamic GSH was reduced in migraine without aura (1.76 ± 0.16) and migraine with aura (1.70 ± 0.13) compared to healthy controls (2.07 ± 0.20), with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 1.70 and 2.14, respectively). N-acetylaspartate (NAA), a marker of neuronal integrity, showed no group differences (p = 0.74). Equivalence testing showed that the GSH-NAA correlation was statistically equivalent to zero within predefined bounds (TOST p = 0.015), indicating that GSH reduction occurred in the absence of a detectable linear association with NAA in this sample. GSH demonstrated good diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.895, 95% CI 0.821–0.952; sensitivity 93.7%; specificity 69.4%), validated by permutation testing (p = 0.001).ConclusionsThese findings indicate selective thalamic GSH reduction in migraine in the absence of detectable NAA alterations. These observations are consistent with reduced thalamic antioxidant capacity that is not accompanied by measurable neuronal loss, although NAA cannot exclude subtle neuronal or synaptic dysfunction. Thalamic GSH may represent a candidate biomarker that requires independent validation, and the potential of antioxidant strategies remains hypothesis-generating.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02456-2.

Background

Migraine is a highly prevalent neurological disorder affecting approximately 15% of the global population, ranking among the leading causes of disability worldwide [1, 2]. Despite significant therapeutic advances, objective biomarkers for diagnosis remain elusive, and pathophysiological mechanisms are incompletely understood [3, 4].

The thalamus has emerged as a critical hub in migraine pathophysiology, serving as a relay center for nociceptive processing and demonstrating altered connectivity during and between migraine attacks [5, 6]. Oxidative stress—an imbalance between reactive oxygen species and antioxidant defenses—has been implicated through peripheral marker studies and evidence of mitochondrial dysfunction [7–9]. However, direct measurement of brain antioxidants in migraine has been limited.

Glutathione (GSH), the brain’s most abundant endogenous antioxidant, can be quantified non-invasively using proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS) and provides an index of brain antioxidant capacity [10, 11]. Reduced brain GSH has been reported in neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders [12, 13], but data in migraine remain sparse. It remains unclear whether changes in thalamic antioxidant capacity in migraine are accompanied by detectable changes in markers of neuronal integrity.

N-acetylaspartate (NAA), synthesized exclusively in neuronal mitochondria, serves as an established marker of neuronal integrity [14]. Jointly measuring GSH and NAA therefore allows the pattern of antioxidant and neuronal-integrity markers to be characterized within the same voxel. Where a relationship between two markers is of interest, equivalence testing can quantify whether any association lies within predefined bounds, providing more informative inference than a non-significant correlation alone [15].

We therefore measured thalamic GSH, NAA, and other metabolites in patients with migraine, patients with tension-type headache (TTH), and healthy controls (HC), in order to characterize antioxidant and neuronal-integrity markers and the relationship between them.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study enrolled 131 participants from the Headache Clinic between January 2025 and December 2025: 36 HC, 46 migraines without aura (MwoA), 17 migraines with aura (MwA), and 32 TTH. The dataset analyzed here originates from the same protocol as our earlier report on thalamic GSH differences between migraine and TTH (Huang et al., J Headache Pain 2026 May 4;27:141) [16]. The two papers consequently share their ethical approval, the distinguishing feature of the current work is its additional focus on a broader edit-off metabolite panel and, in particular, on how thalamic GSH relates to NAA as assessed by equivalence testing.

Patients were eligible if they (i) met International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) criteria [17] for episodic migraine, with or without aura, or for episodic TTH; (ii) were 18–50 years old; and (iii) could be imaged in the interictal state, operationalized as a minimum of 72 h since the preceding attack together with confirmation that no further attack occurred within the subsequent 48 h. We did not enroll individuals who had any other neurological or psychiatric condition, a history of head injury, a secondary or medication-overuse headache, or a second primary headache type; who carried both a TTH and a migraine diagnosis; who were taking, on a long-term basis, agents liable to alter cerebral metabolism (preventive antimigraine drugs within the preceding 4 weeks included); or for whom MRI was contraindicated. Each participant also completed a structured clinical work-up encompassing demographics, a detailed headache history, and a neurological examination; for migraineurs we additionally logged disease duration (years), monthly attack frequency, usual attack length (hours), peak attack pain, and HIT-6 and MIDAS scores. MRS acquisition

Imaging was carried out on a 3.0-T clinical system (Discovery MR750w, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) fitted with a 24-channel head coil. A typical session ran a three-plane localizer; a 3D Brain Volume (BRAVO) T1-weighted volume analogous to MP-RAGE (TR 6.2 ms, TE 2.3 ms, flip angle 12°, TI 450 ms, FOV 240 × 240 mm2, matrix 256 × 256, 1.5-mm slices, 0.9 × 0.9 × 1.5 mm3 voxels, ≈1 min 49 s) used both to place the spectroscopic voxel and to drive tissue segmentation; coronal T2-FLAIR series (TR 8000 ms, TE 120 ms, FOV 240 × 240 mm2, 5-mm slices, ≈1 min 44 s) and sagittal T2-FLAIR series (TR 9000 ms, TE 145 ms, FOV 240 × 240 mm2, 5-mm slices, ≈1 min 57 s); and MEGA-PRESS spectroscopy of single-voxel MEGA-PRESS spectroscopy of one thalamus per participant. Although bilateral thalamic acquisition was initially attempted, a single thalamic voxel per participant was analyzed: to keep the single-session examination within a tolerable duration and, above all, to ensure that every included spectrum met the pre-specified quality thresholds (water and tCr linewidth and CRLB), the thalamic voxel that achieved acceptable spectral quality in each participant was retained, without constraining the analysis to a fixed hemisphere. Because left and right thalamic GSH are essentially symmetric with no significant interhemispheric difference, using the better-quality side per participant does not bias the group comparison.

GSH was detected selectively with single-voxel MEGA-PRESS. Using anatomical landmarks on the T1-weighted images, a 20 × 20 × 20 mm3 region of interest was centered on the thalamus while deliberately keeping skull, sinus, and cerebrospinal-fluid contributions to a minimum. We acquired 128 averages each for the edit-on and edit-off conditions (256 in total) at TR 2000 ms and TE 79 ms [11], which translated to about 9 min 20 s of acquisition per voxel. Editing relied on a pair of 10-ms frequency-selective Gaussian pulses (7% truncation, 120-Hz bandwidth): for edit-on acquisitions these were centered at 4.56 ppm so as to refocus the GSH cysteine resonance and reveal its coupled partner at 2.95 ppm in the difference spectrum, and for edit-off acquisitions they were disabled. The vendor’s VAPOR routine, run with default settings, provided water suppression, and an unsuppressed water reference was collected at the start of every block—ahead of the water-suppressed data—to support channel combination and eddy-current correction. First-order B₀ shimming was applied automatically before each spectral acquisition.

We enforced strict acceptance thresholds, namely a water FWHM below 10 Hz, a total-creatine (tCr) singlet FWHM below 10 Hz, and a GSH Cramér-Rao lower bound (CRLB) under 10%; any spectrum breaching even one of these was discarded. For each metabolite, fitting reliability was indexed primarily by the LCModel-reported CRLB [18].

MRS processing and quantification

Raw MEGA-PRESS data were processed offline before fitting. The individual free-induction decays from the edit-on and edit-off conditions were first frequency-aligned and phase-corrected and then averaged within each condition, and the edit-off average was subtracted from the edit-on average to yield the GSH difference spectrum (Fig. 1A). Both the difference and the edit-off spectra were zero-filled to 32K points and apodised with a 1.5-Hz exponential plus a 1.5-Hz Gaussian line-broadening function prior to Fourier transformation. GSH was quantified from the difference spectrum with LCModel over the 0.6–3.93 ppm range, using a basis set restricted to the metabolites that survive J-difference editing at this echo time—GSH together with the NAA aspartate moiety, NAAG, total NAA (tNAA), lactate, and threonine—in addition to the LCModel macromolecular and lipid components (Fig. 1A, C). The non-edited (edit-off) spectrum was fitted separately over the 0.5–4.0 ppm range with a fuller basis set comprising GSH, glutamate (Glu), glutamine (Gln), γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), myo-inositol (mI), tCr, total choline (tCho), NAA, NAAG, tNAA, lactate, and threonine, again with macromolecular and lipid components included (Fig. 1B, D). For both spectra the basic functions were generated by density-matrix simulation that reproduced the actual RF-pulse shapes and timing of our MEGA-PRESS implementation. In all subsequent analyses, GSH was taken from the difference-spectrum fit (Fig. 1C); the edit-off GSH estimate was retained only for internal consistency checking.

Metabolite levels were expressed in millimolar units by internal referencing to tCr. In practice, LCModel first returned the fitted tCr signal (for the representative spectrum, 3.8 mM; Fig. 1D), and this signal was then rescaled to an assumed tCr concentration of 8 mM, which served as the internal concentration reference; every other metabolite, including GSH, was scaled to the same reference so that all concentrations are reported relative to a fixed tCr level of 8 mM [19]. Because identical sequence timing, voxel size, and referencing were applied to every participant, the resulting values are directly comparable across individuals and groups. The 79-ms echo time was optimized for GSH editing; metabolites with long T₂ relaxation (tNAA, tCr, tCho) are therefore quantified reliably, whereas shorter-T₂ species (Glu, Gln, mI) are subject to T₂-related signal attenuation and are reported as apparent concentrations.

To account for partial-volume effects, the 3D T1-weighted BRAVO image was segmented in SPM12, the MEGA-PRESS voxel was co-registered to this image, and the gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) fractions within the voxel were derived. GSH concentrations were corrected for CSF dilution as GSH_corrected = GSH_raw/(1 − f_CSF), where f_CSF is the voxel CSF fraction; this correction compensates for the negligible metabolite content of CSF. The three tissue fractions were compared across groups with the Kruskal–Wallis test to verify that any group-level difference in GSH could not be attributed to differences in voxel tissue composition.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in Python 3.12, with two-sided p < 0.05 considered significant. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD or, for skewed clinical variables, as median (interquartile range); normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests. The analyses were organized into a predefined hierarchy of primary, secondary, and exploratory levels, as detailed below, so that the confirmatory and the hypothesis-generating components of the study are clearly distinguished.

Primary analysis

The primary analysis was the between-group comparison of thalamic GSH across the four groups (HC, TTH, MwoA, MwA). Because Levene’s test indicated unequal variances, the group effect was evaluated using Welch’s ANOVA [20], with η2 reported as the effect size. All pairwise post-hoc comparisons were then performed and corrected for multiple testing; we report the corrected p-value and Cohen’s d for every pair, including the clinically important migraine-versus-TTH and MwoA-versus-MwA contrasts.

Secondary analysis

Secondary analyses comprised: (i) between-group comparisons of the remaining edit-off metabolites (Glu, Gln, mI, tCho, NAA, NAAG, tNAA), each tested across groups with the same omnibus approach and adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate (FDR) procedure; (ii) an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) on thalamic GSH adjusting for age and sex, to confirm that the group effect was independent of these demographic factors; and (iii) the relationship between thalamic GSH and NAA, assessed both with Pearson correlation and with equivalence testing using the two one-sided tests (TOST) procedure [15].

The equivalence bounds were justified on biological as well as statistical grounds. Biologically, GSH and NAA index largely distinct processes—GSH reflects antioxidant/redox capacity, whereas NAA is a marker of neuronal–mitochondrial integrity—and a genuine mechanistic coupling between an antioxidant pool and a neuronal-integrity marker measured in the same voxel would be expected to manifest as at least a moderate association. In the context of in vivo MRS, where metabolite estimates carry intrinsic measurement variance from spectral fitting (reflected in the CRLBs), partial-volume effects, and physiological fluctuation, weak correlations of |r| <0.3 are routinely produced by shared technical and tissue-composition variance rather than by any true biological dependence; such a correlation explains less than 9% of the variance and would not support a meaningful neuronal–antioxidant relationship. We therefore regarded |r| <0.3 as practically equivalent to the absence of a biologically relevant GSH–NAA coupling, consistent with a small effect under Cohen’s conventions. The robustness of the equivalence conclusion was further confirmed under alternative bounds of ±0.2 and ±0.4.

Exploratory analysis

The following were treated as exploratory: a prespecified sex-stratified analysis of the GSH group effect, performed because of the sex imbalance across groups; receiver-operating-characteristic (ROC) analysis of the diagnostic performance of thalamic GSH, reporting the area under the curve, sensitivity, specificity, and the Youden-optimal cut-off, with internal cross-validation and permutation testing (1,000 iterations). Because no independent external validation cohort was available, the ROC results are reported as exploratory and as estimates derived within the same sample used to identify the group difference. Categorical variables were compared with the chi-square test, and voxel tissue fractions (GM, WM, CSF) were compared across groups with the Kruskal–Wallis test.

Post-hoc comparisons revealed significantly lower GSH in migraine versus HC: MwoA (difference = −0.31, p < 0.001, d = 1.70, 95% CI 1.26–2.27) and MwA (difference = −0.36, p < 0.001, d = 2.14, 95% CI 1.58–2.95). TTH also showed a statistically significant but small reduction in thalamic GSH relative to HC (d = 0.50, 95% CI 0.01–0.98, p = 0.042). However, this effect was approximately one-quarter the magnitude of that observed in migraine (d = 1.7–2.1) and was of borderline significance; it should therefore be interpreted with caution and does not alter the principal finding that GSH reduction is markedly more pronounced in migraine than in TTH. Both migraine groups differed significantly from TTH (p < 0.001). Thalamic GSH did not differ significantly between the two migraine subtypes (MwoA vs MwA: Δ = 0.06, d = 0.37, p = 0.168).

Association between thalamic GSH and migraine burden

To determine whether the thalamic GSH reduction reflected disease burden, we examined, within the combined migraine group, the relationship between thalamic GSH and indices of headache burden and disability—disease duration, attack frequency, pain intensity, MIDAS, and HIT-6—using Spearman’s rank correlation, with p-values adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate (FDR) procedure. None of these associations was statistically significant: disease duration (ρ = −0.09, FDR-corrected p = 0.79, n = 29), attack frequency (ρ = 0.26, FDR-corrected p = 0.77, n = 32), pain intensity (ρ = −0.09, FDR-corrected p = 0.79, n = 23), MIDAS (ρ = −0.14, FDR-corrected p = 0.79, n = 22), and HIT-6 (ρ = −0.06, FDR-corrected p = 0.79, n = 22). The correlation coefficients were small in magnitude (all |ρ| ≤0.26), and no measure remained even marginally associated with thalamic GSH after correction for multiple comparisons.

GSH-NAA association

Pearson correlation analysis showed a non-significant GSH-NAA correlation (r = −0.116, p = 0.186). Critically, TOST demonstrated this correlation was statistically equivalent to zero within the predefined bounds (±0.3): TOST p = 0.015 (Fig. 3), with the 90% CI (−0.256 to 0.029) lying entirely within these bounds. Diagnostic performance

ROC analysis was performed as an exploratory, within-cohort assessment of the discrimination of thalamic GSH migraine versus HC: AUC = 0.895 (95% CI 0.821–0.952), sensitivity = 93.7%, specificity = 69.4% (Fig. 4). Cross-validation accuracy, estimated within the same cohort, was 81.8 ± 6.9% (5-fold), 83.0 ± 9.0% (10-fold). Permutation testing indicated that performance exceeded chance (p = 0.001). Additional analyses

ANCOVA controlling for age and sex confirmed the group effect (F = 31.76, p < 0.001). GSH showed no age correlation (r = 0.09, p = 0.30). Sensitivity analyses using Winsorized data (F = 33.53, p < 0.001) confirmed robustness. A prespecified female-only sensitivity analysis reproduced the group effect on thalamic GSH (Welch’s F = 15.18, p < 0.001; migraine vs HC Cohen’s d = −2.44), and GSH did not differ between women and men within any group, indicating that the reduction was not attributable to the sex imbalance.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional MEGA-PRESS study, thalamic GSH was selectively reduced in migraine relative to healthy controls and to patients with TTH, whereas NAA, an established marker of neuronal integrity, did not differ among groups. Equivalence testing further indicated that the GSH–NAA correlation was statistically equivalent to zero within predefined bounds. Together, these findings are consistent with reduced thalamic antioxidant capacity occurring in the absence of detectable NAA alterations, rather than with overt neuronal loss. We interpret this pattern cautiously: preserved NAA argues against substantial neuronal or mitochondrial damage, but cannot exclude subtle neuronal, synaptic, or glial dysfunction, and the cross-sectional design does not permit inferences about causality or temporal sequence.

We focused on the thalamus because it occupies a pivotal position in the trigeminovascular pain pathway and in migraine pathophysiology more broadly. The thalamus houses the third-order relay neurons that receive nociceptive input from the spinal trigeminal nucleus and convey it to the somatosensory and other cortical regions, placing it at an anatomical bottleneck for the transmission and modulation of migraine head pain [21]. Beyond simple relay, thalamic trigeminovascular neurons are densely innervated by glutamatergic, GABAergic, and monoaminergic afferents, making the thalamus a key site at which ascending pain signals are amplified or suppressed and at which excitatory–inhibitory balance can be disturbed [22]. Consistent with a central pathophysiological role, structural imaging has demonstrated volumetric and shape abnormalities of specific thalamic subnuclei in migraine patients [23], and functional imaging has revealed altered thalamocortical connectivity both during and between attacks [21]. Because migraine-related metabolic and neurochemical disturbances—including altered glutamatergic tone and energy metabolism—have been reported in this region, and because the thalamus is repeatedly and cumulatively exposed to trigeminovascular input over years of recurrent attacks, it represents a biologically motivated target in which to probe brain antioxidant capacity. These considerations, together with the constraints of single-voxel MEGA-PRESS acquisition time, led us to prospectively select the thalamus as the region of interest for GSH quantification rather than performing a whole-brain survey.

The reduction in thalamic GSH was both selective and substantial. Among the metabolites quantified, GSH alone differed significantly among groups, with large effect sizes for migraine versus controls, whereas glutamate (Glu), myo-inositol (mI), total choline (tCho), and NAA were comparable across groups. Several considerations may explain why these other metabolites were unchanged. First, they index biological processes that are not primarily redox-dependent—NAA reflects neuronal–mitochondrial integrity, Glu glutamatergic neurotransmission, mI glial/osmotic regulation, and tCho membrane turnover—whereas GSH directly reflects antioxidant/redox capacity; a selective fall in GSH with preserved NAA, Glu, mI and tCho is therefore consistent with an early or compartment-specific shift in redox balance that has not yet produced measurable neuronal loss, glial proliferation, or membrane breakdown. Second, interictal sampling may be relevant: neurotransmitter-related metabolites such as Glu have most often been reported to change during or close to migraine attacks, so their normalization between migraine attacks would be expected, whereas a depleted antioxidant reserve may persist interictally [24, 25]. Third, the long echo time (TE = 79 ms) used here was optimized for GSH editing and attenuates the signals of short-T₂, J-coupled metabolites (Glu, Gln, mI), which reduces sensitivity to small group differences in these specific metabolites. This metabolite selectivity also argues against a generalized spectral-quality or partial-volume artifact and points to a relatively specific alteration in the thalamic antioxidant pool. The finding extends prior peripheral-blood evidence of altered oxidative-stress markers in migraine [26, 27] by providing a direct, in vivo measure of reduced brain antioxidant capacity in a deep grey-matter structure central to nociceptive processing.

The TTH-migraine distinction supports different underlying mechanisms [17]. The selective reduction of thalamic GSH in migraine, but not in TTH, is consistent with the possibility that antioxidant-related mechanisms differ between these two primary headache disorders. This interpretation should be made cautiously: the TTH sample was modest in size, no other chronic-pain, neurological, or psychiatric control group was included, and the difference between migraine and TTH does not by itself establish disease specificity. Replication in larger and more diverse comparison groups will be required before reduced thalamic GSH can be regarded as a migraine-specific feature.

From a translational standpoint, thalamic GSH discriminated migraine from controls with good accuracy in an exploratory, within-cohort ROC analysis [3]. Because these estimates were obtained in the same sample used to identify the group difference and were not tested in an independent cohort, they are necessarily optimistic and should be considered preliminary; external validation is essential before any diagnostic application can be claimed. Similarly, although the reduced antioxidant pool provides a biological rationale for evaluating antioxidant or GSH-supporting strategies (for example, N-acetylcysteine or coenzyme Q10) [28, 29], no treatment was administered in this study, and any therapeutic implication is at present hypothesis-generating rather than supported by the present data.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional, interictal design cannot establish causality or determine whether reduced thalamic GSH is an early, stable, or consequential feature of migraine; longitudinal and multi-phase studies are needed. Second, we measured total GSH but not oxidized glutathione (GSSG) or the GSH/GSSG ratio, so reduced GSH should be interpreted as diminished antioxidant capacity rather than as a direct readout of oxidative stress. Third, the clinical-burden measures were available for only a subset of patients, so the absence of significant GSH–burden correlations is therefore underpowered and should be regarded as exploratory. Fourth, the MwA and TTH groups were small, and no additional disease control group was included, so the specificity of reduced thalamic GSH to migraine remains to be confirmed. Fifth, the diagnostic performance was derived within a single cohort without external validation and is consequently subject to optimism. Sixth, the equivalence-bound choice (±0.3) is, despite its biological and statistical justification, an investigator-defined threshold; we mitigated this by confirming the conclusion under alternative bounds (±0.2 and ±0.4), but some subjectivity is unavoidable. Seventh, clinical characterization was incomplete: while core variables are reported (Table 1), several others were not collected—including days since the last and until the next migraine attack, acute and preventive medication use, supplement use, menstrual-cycle phase, hormonal contraceptive use, caffeine intake, sleep, and comorbidities—and their absence may introduce unmeasured confounding.

Conclusions

Using MEGA-PRESS spectroscopy, we found that thalamic GSH is selectively reduced in migraine, relative to both healthy controls and patients with TTH, in the absence of detectable alterations in NAA. Equivalence testing indicated that the GSH–NAA correlation was statistically equivalent to zero within predefined bounds, consistent with reduced thalamic antioxidant capacity that is not accompanied by measurable neuronal-integrity changes—though without establishing mechanistic independence. The reduction was comparable across migraine subtypes and was not associated with the available measures of disease burden. Thalamic GSH may therefore represent a candidate biomarker of migraine that warrants independent, external validation, and the antioxidant mechanisms it reflects provide a hypothesis-generating rationale for future therapeutic studies. Confirmation in larger, longitudinal, and multicenter cohorts will be needed before any diagnostic or therapeutic role can be established.
