Serum long non-coding RNA SNHG9 as a diagnostic biomarker for acute coronary syndrome and a predictor of prognosis following percutaneous coronary intervention: a clinical evaluation
Authors: Jinhe Chen, Yujuan Gao, Fan Liu, Yuan Li, Li Liu, Xiaona Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the change of lifestyle and the aging of the population, the incidence of Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is increasing and showing a trend of younger patients. Therefore, the study of risk factors for ACS has important clinical implications for the diagnosis and prognosis of patients. The purpose of this study was to explore the role of long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) SNHG9 in the diagnosis and prognosis of ACS, with a view to developing new biomarkers for the diagnosis and prognosis of ACS.MethodsA total of 130 ACS patients and 99 healthy subjects were included in this study, and their serum SNHG9 levels were measured by RT-qPCR. The diagnostic value of SNHG9 in ACS was evaluated by ROC curve and binary Logistic analysis. Risk factors for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients with ACS were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves and multivariate Cox regression. Correlation of SNHG9 levels with clinical indicators was analyzed using Pearson and Spearman methods.ResultsSNHG9 was highly expressed in ACS patients compared to healthy subjects. Logistic analysis and ROC results showed high diagnostic accuracy of SNHG9 in ACS patients (OR = 7.106, P < 0.001; AUC = 0.929). Furthermore, SNHG9 expression was upregulated in the MACE events group compared to the group without MACE events. Kaplan-Meier curves indicated lower survival in ACS patients with high SNHG9 expression (P = 0.002). Cox results showed that SNHG9 is a risk factor for MACE events in ACS patients after treatment. Besides, SNHG9 was positively and significantly correlated with cTnI, NT-proBNP, hs-CRP, Gensini score, and the number of diseased vessels.ConclusionsSNHG9 has high predictive value for the diagnosis and prognosis of ACS patients, which may become a biomarker for clinical diagnosis and prediction of survival outcome in ACS patients.Keywords: lncRNA SNHG9, Acute coronary syndrome, Diagnosis, Prognosis

Background

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) refers to an acute ischemic syndrome of the heart caused by rupture or erosion of unstable atheromatous plaques in the coronary arteries, causing thrombus formation [1]. ACS is a serious type of coronary heart disease [2]. ACS has a sudden onset, rapid development, and high mortality [3]. At present, the treatment of ACS mainly relies on drug therapy, surgical therapy, and interventional therapy [4]. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has become one of the main clinical treatments for ACS, with approximately 5 million patients undergoing PCI each year globally [5]. However, current treatment measures cannot eliminate vascular lesions, only control the condition and alleviate symptoms [6]. Therefore, it is of great significance to screen new detection and prognostic indicators for accurate prevention, diagnosis, and subsequent survival judgment of ACS patients.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a class of non-coding RNAs with a length greater than 200 nucleotides [7]. It is generally believed that lncRNAs participate in life activities such as epigenetics, cell cycle, and cellular biology [8]. Research has shown that lncRNAs are involved in the occurrence of tumors, hypertension, diabetes, and other diseases, and have positive or negative regulation on the development process and mechanism of diseases, which is the focus of research at this stage [9–12]. Previous studies have suggested that some lncRNAs are biomarkers for the diagnosis and prognosis of ACS, regulating the occurrence of ACS and associated with patient survival, such as lncRNA TPRG1-AS1, lncRNA PELATON, and lncRNA SNHG7 [13–15]. lncRNA SNHG9 is a long non-coding RNA associated with disease. Many explorations have confirmed that the lncRNA SNHG9 expression is significantly upregulated in breast cancer, prostate cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma [16–18]. SNHG9 expression is reduced in non-small cell lung cancer [19], and decreased SNHG9 expression can induce cell proliferation and migration in ovarian cancer [20]. Previous studies have suggested that SNHG9 may play a role in cardiac pathology. For example, it has been reported that its expression is upregulated in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy [21]. However, its expression pattern, diagnostic and prognostic value in ACS have not been elucidated.

In this study, we investigated the diagnostic value of SNHG9 for ACS by analyzing its differential expression in healthy individuals and ACS patients. In addition, we further analyzed whether MACE events occurred in ACS patients after the implementation of PCI therapy, hoping to assess the predictive value of SNHG9 on the prognosis of ACS patients.

Methods

Enrollment of subjects

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 130 patients diagnosed with ACS at the Affiliated Hospital of Panzhihua University from 2020 to 2023 were selected. These included 38 patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), 41 patients with non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and 51 patients with unstable angina (UA). In addition, 99 age-sex matched healthy individuals who had a medical check-up in our hospital during the corresponding term were included. Inclusion criteria for ACS patients: all patients met the diagnostic criteria for ACS [22]; the levels of myocardial injury markers were significantly increased; coronary angiography showed at least one stenosis and required PCI; patients voluntarily participated in the study and were able to complete the relevant examinations and treatments as prescribed. Exclusion criteria: patients with previous malignant tumors, immune system diseases, or cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases; Patients with congenital heart disease, cardiomyopathy, and cardiogenic shock; Patients who have previously undergone cardiac surgery.

All the enrolled subjects signed the informed consent form voluntarily, and this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Panzhihua University. The procedures used in this study adhere to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Collection of clinical data and detection of biochemical indicators

General data such as age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) of patients and healthy individuals were collected and recorded through medical records retrieval.

Venous blood of the subjects was collected and placed into anticoagulant vacuum collection vessels and sent to the examination center of our hospital for the detection of biochemical indicators such as total cholesterol (TC) and triglyceride (TG).

Processing of blood samples

Venous blood samples were collected from all ACS patients after admission, before PCI treatment, and the next morning in a fasting state. Venous blood were centrifuged at low temperature and high speed. The centrifugation temperature was 4℃, the rotational speed was 3000 rpm, and the centrifugation time was 5 min. The upper serum was collected and placed in a centrifuge tube and conserved in a fridge at -80℃ for future experiments.

Real-time qPCR assays

Trizol reagent (Thermo, USA), chloroform solution, and isopropanol (Beijing Chemical Plant, China) were successively added to serum samples, and total RNA was obtained. The concentration and purity of RNA were detected using Nanodrop2000 (Thermo, USA). RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary cDNA in accordance with the instructions of the PrimeScript RT Kit (Takara, Japan). The RT-qPCR reaction system was configured using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix Kit (Invitrogen, USA) with cDNA as template and GAPDH as internal reference. lncRNA SNHG9 expression was computed by the 2−ΔΔCt.

Patient follow-up

Patients with ACS were followed up after the implementation of PCI. Patients were followed up at 6 months postoperatively by telephone, outpatient or readmission records, and major adverse cardiac events (MACE) occurring in patients during the follow-up period were recorded. MACE includes events cardiac death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, unplanned revascularization (repeat PCI or coronary-artery bypass grafting in either coronary vessel for recurrent myocardial ischemia), and severe arrhythmia (MACE analyses included only sustained ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation that resulted in hemodynamic disturbance, required urgent electrical cardioversion, or defibrillation; asymptomatic or non-sustained ventricular tachycardia was excluded). In addition, for patients with multivessel disease on coronary angiography, only the culprit lesion causing the acute symptom was treated during the initial PCI. Patients with ACS were divided into two groups, those who had a MACE event and those who did not have a MACE event, according to the results of follow-up.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 21.0 and GraphPad 9.0 software were used for statistical analysis and chart drawing. Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed by Student t-test. The Chi-square test was used to analyze differences between groups. The diagnostic accuracy of SNHG9 for ACS was evaluated by ROC curve and binary logistic analysis. The predictive potential of SNHG9 for the prognosis of ACS patients was analyzed by multivariate Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier curve. Correlation of SNHG9 expression levels with clinical indicators was analyzed using Pearson and Spearman methods. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Analysis of the basic data of the subjects

This study recruited 130 patients with ACS and 99 cases of healthy subjects, and general information is shown in Table 1. In the two groups, age, sex, and BMI had no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05). Furthermore, there was no significant difference in hypertension, diabetes, TC, TG, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) between ACS patients and healthy individuals (P > 0.05). However, compared with the healthy control group, the cardiac troponin I (cTnI), N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), and hypersensitive C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels in the ACS group were significantly upregulated, and the difference was significant (P < 0.001).

Expression and diagnostic potential of SNHG9

The level of SNHG9 in serum samples was detected by RT-qPCR. High levels of SNHG9 are found in patients with ACS (P < 0.001; Fig. 1A). Additionally, SNHG9 levels showed significant differences among different subtypes of ACS. Specifically, STEMI group exhibited the highest SNHG9 levels, followed by NSTEMI group, and the lowest in UA group, but all were significantly higher than the control group (P < 0.001; Fig. 1B).

Relative expression levels of SNHG9. (A) Relative expression levels of SNHG9 in the serum of healthy individuals and ACS patients. (B) Relative expression levels of SNHG9 in different subgroups of ACS patients. ***P < 0.001

Binary Logistic analysis suggested SNHG9 is an independent risk factor predicting the development of ACS (OR = 7.106, P < 0.001; Fig. 2A). ROC results showed that at a cut-off value of 1.47, SNHG9 had high sensitivity (81.5%) and specificity (88.9%), and was effective in distinguishing between ACS patients and healthy subjects (AUC = 0.929; Fig. 2B). This reflects the value of SNHG9 in the early diagnosis of ACS.

Diagnostic value of SNHG9 in ACS patients. (A) Binary Logistic analysis of risk factors associated with ACS. (B) ROC curve analysis of the accuracy of SNHG9 to distinguish ACS patients from healthy individuals

Relationship between prognosis and clinical indicators in ACS patients

To further explore the predictive value of serum SNHG9 on the prognosis of ACS patients, we followed up the patients for 6 months. Patients were categorized into two groups according to the occurrence of MACE events, as shown in Table 2. There were 31 cases (23.8%) in the group with MACE events and 99 cases (76.2%) without MACE events. Among them, 2 cases were cardiac death, 5 cases were non-fatal myocardial infarction, 18 cases were recurrent severe angina requiring urgent revascularization, and 6 cases were severe arrhythmia. There were no significant differences between the two groups in age, gender, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, TC, TG, LDL, and HDL indicators (P > 0.05). However, compared with the group without MACE events, the MACE events group demonstrated significant differences in Gensini score, cTnI, NT-proBNP, and hs-CRP levels, and the number of diseased vessels (P < 0.05).

The predictive ability of SNHG9 for the occurrence of MACE events in ACS patients

The expression level of SNHG9 was detected by RT-qPCR in the group with and without MACE events, and the results showed that SNHG9 levels were significantly higher in the MACE events group (P < 0.001; Fig. 3A), suggesting that overexpression of SNHG9 may have a bearing on poor prognosis in ACS patients. ACS patients were divided into high and low expression groups according to the average SNHG9 level, and Kaplan-Meier curves showed that the high expression group was more prone to MACE events and had a lower patient survival rate (P = 0.002, Fig. 3B). Besides, Cox regression analyses were performed by selecting the indicators with significant differences in the previous study and SNHG9 to assess the risk factors for the occurrence of MACE events. The results revealed that in addition to SNHG9, Gensini score, and hs-CRP were also risk factors for MACE events in patients after treatment (P < 0.05, Fig. 4). In addition, we further analyzed the correlation between SNHG9 levels and various indicators of ACS patients. The findings indicated that SNHG9 was positively and significantly correlated with cTnI, NT-proBNP, hs-CRP, Gensini score, and the number of diseased vessels (P < 0.001, Table 3).

Discussion

Acute coronary syndrome is a complex cardiovascular disease composed of acute myocardial infarction and unstable angina pectoris, and is the most perilous and deadly type of coronary heart disease [23]. In the past decades, the incidence rate and mortality of ACS patients have been on the rise, and it is difficult to cure at present [24]. Hence, there is an urgent need to explore a new biomarker to more effectively diagnose ACS and predict the condition.

LncRNA SNHG9 belongs to the small nucleolus host gene family, and the coding gene is located on chromosome 16p1310. Previous studies have shown SNHG9 may be elevated in dilated cardiomyopathy [21]. We detected the serum of ACS patients and found that the expression of SNHG9 in ACS patients was significantly higher than that in healthy individuals, which is consistent with previous studies. It is suggested that SNHG9 may serve as a key miRNA for the diagnosis of ACS. With the gradual increase of ACS patients, research on ACS is also constantly deepening. Therefore, we conducted an analysis comparing ACS patients with healthy individuals. To further analyze the value of SNHG9 in the diagnosis of ACS, we tested the serum of patients with other relevant indicators. Gensini score is primarily used to assess the severity of coronary artery disease [25]. cTnI is a sensitive indicator to check the heart for myocardial damage [26]. NT-proBNP is used to evaluate cardiac function and detect the presence of heart failure in patients [27]. hs-CRP is an inflammatory biomarker that can be used for risk assessment of cardiovascular disease [28]. It has been shown that cTnI, NT-proBNP levels are elevated in patients with right ventricular dysfunction in acute pulmonary embolism [29]. Our test results showed that compared with healthy individuals, serum levels of cTnI, NT-proBNP, and hs-CRP in ACS patients were significantly increased. In addition, logistic analysis showed that SNHG9 was an independent risk factor for the occurrence of ACS, and the results of the ROC curve indicated that SNHG9 had high accuracy in diagnosing ACS. This indicates that in clinical practice, the diagnosis of ACS patients may be confirmed by detecting the expression level of SNHG9.

In addition, this study found that SNHG9 expression showed a gradient pattern across different ACS subtypes, specifically STEMI > NSTEMI > UA. This gradient is consistent with the severity of different ACS subtypes [30]. It suggests that serum SNHG9 levels may not only distinguish ACS patients from healthy individuals but also serve as an indicator reflecting the severity of acute myocardial ischemic injury. We hypothesize that the underlying mechanism may involve more severe plaque rupture and thrombosis causing more extensive myocardial ischemia and inflammatory responses, thereby significantly stimulating SNHG9 release. This requires further validation in the future.

To further investigate the clinical value of SNHG9 in ACS, we followed up patients for 6 months after PCI treatment to evaluate the short-term prognostic value of SNHG9. Li et al. showed that SNHG9 expression is elevated in prostate cancer and may serve as a prognostic biomarker, but studies of SNHG9 as a prognostic biomarker in cardiovascular disease have not yet been reported [17]. In addition, cTnI, NT-proBNP, and hs-CRP may serve as prognostic factors for adverse cardiovascular events [31]. In our study, the results showed that the SNHG9 expression level was significantly higher in the group with MACE events than in the group without MACE events. And Gensini score, cTnI, NT-proBNP, hs-CRP, and the number of diseased vessels were significantly higher than in the control group. More importantly, SNHG9 was significantly correlated with the occurrence of MACE events. In studies of coronary heart disease and chronic heart failure, changes in the above indicators are consistent with our results [32, 33]. These experimental results illustrate that high expression of SNHG9 may be one of the important prognostic indicators for ACS.

This study validated the potential of SNHG9 as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for ACS. Combining previous studies with our findings, we speculate that it may play a pathogenic role. Studies have shown that SNHG family members often act as competitive endogenous RNAs, regulating downstream pathways by binding specific miRNAs and influencing disease progression [34, 35]. For example, SNHG4 has been reported to regulate cardiomyocyte apoptosis and inflammation via miR-148b-3p [36]. We hypothesize that in ACS, the upregulation of SNHG9 may exert a similar mechanism by releasing the suppression of pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic pathways, thereby exacerbating myocardial injury. If this hypothesis is validated in future basic research, targeted inhibition of SNHG9 may emerge as a novel strategy for intervening in disease progression. Therefore, SNHG9 is not only a potential biomarker but also a therapeutic target worthy of further exploration.

However, the study of SNHG9 in ACS is still in its infancy, and the above-mentioned mechanistic hypotheses and therapeutic prospects still require rigorous experimental validation. This study has several limitations. Firstly, this study was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size. Future studies should validate the universal applicability of SNHG9 in diagnosis and prognosis through multicenter and large-sample prospective cohort studies. Secondly, this study did not systematically collect cardiac imaging functional data such as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) from patients, thus lacking evidence of the association between SNHG9 and cardiac function. Future studies should incorporate more comprehensive indicators for cardiac function assessment. Finally, this study mainly focused on clinical relevance. The specific molecular mechanisms by which SNHG9 plays a role in ACS still require in-depth exploration through basic experiments.

In this study, lncRNA SNHG9 was significantly upregulated in ACS patients, which displayed predictive value for diagnosis and prognosis, and may become a biomarker for clinical diagnosis of ACS and prediction of patient survival outcome.
