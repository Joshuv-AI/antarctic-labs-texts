Molecular surveillance of influenza A and B in namibia: seasonal patterns and clinical correlates (2021–2023)
Authors: Nathalia !Garus-oas, Cara Mia Dunaiski, Paulina N. Naupu, Ndahafa Frans, Harris Onywera, Andreas Shiningavamwe, Kapena Tjombonde, Iyaloo N. Konstantinus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfluenza viruses cause widespread respiratory illness. Due to limited surveillance, influenza dynamics in Namibia remain poorly understood. This study aimed to conduct molecular surveillance of influenza A and B viruses from 2021 − 2023, characterize circulating strains, and explore symptom correlations to inform public health responses and improve case management.MethodsFrom October 2021 to December 2023, nasopharyngeal or oropharyngeal swabs were collected from patients with influenza-like illness (ILI) in a clinic-based setting. Viral detection, subtyping, and genotyping were conducted using real-time reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). Seasonal trends of influenza and SARS-CoV-2, as well as symptom co-occurrence patterns were examined using statistical and visualization tools in R, Epi Info, and SPSS.ResultsA total of 1186 nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal samples form ILI patients were collected. Influenza was detected in 25.7% (280/1088) of samples, with influenza A accounting for 20.2% (235/1162) and influenza B for 4.3% (45/1049). Among influenza A cases, A(H1N1)pdm09 was detected in 26.4% (62/235) and A(H3N2) in 67% (157/235). Influenza B (Victoria) accounted for nearly all influenza B cases (96%), while no influenza B(Yamagata) cases were detected. SARS-CoV-2 was detected in 19.2% (227/1182) of samples. Significant associations were observed between influenza infection and cough (OR 2.2, 95% CI 1.2 − 3.9, p = 0.0076) and myalgia (OR 2.3, 95% CI 1.5 − 3.6, p = 0.00044). The strongest correlation was between loss of smell and loss of taste (r = 0.71, p < 0.001).ConclusionsInfluenza activity followed distinct seasonal patterns, with influenza A more common than B. Cough and myalgia were stronger predictors of infection, while sore throat and loss of smell were less so. These findings highlight the importance of ongoing influenza surveillance and preparedness to manage seasonal outbreaks, while symptom correlations can enhance case management and monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11554-6.

Background

Influenza viruses, members of the Orthomyxoviridae family, are enveloped, segmented, negative-stranded RNA viruses that pose a significant public health concern globally [1, 2]. Influenza A and B viruses are primary causes of respiratory infections in humans, contributing substantially to morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. Influenza A viruses have a broad host range, affecting humans, swine, birds, and horses, whereas influenza B viruses are largely confined to humans and seals [3, 4]. Of the 18 known subtypes of influenza A, H1, H2, and H3 infect humans, while others such as H5, H6, H7, and H9 are of concern due to their pandemic potential [4]. Conversely, influenza B viruses do not have distinct subtypes but are classified into two antigenically and genetically distinct lineages − B/Victoria/2/87-like and B/Yamagata/16/88-like – that have co-circulated globally since the 1980 s [3, 5, 6].

Current seasonal influenza vaccines are typically trivalent, including one influenza B strain, although research suggests that incorporating both B lineages may improve vaccine efficacy [7]. Traditionally, influenza surveillance and immunization strategies have focused on temperate regions, where transmission follows a clear seasonal pattern during winter months. However, in tropical and sub-tropical regions, including sub-Saharan Africa, influenza circulates year-round with peaks during the rainy season [8]. Despite this, surveillance and vaccine coverage in these regions remain limited, especially among high-risk populations like the elderly and those with chronic medical conditions, leading to gaps in understanding local disease dynamics and suboptimal public health interventions.

Namibia, located in southwestern Africa, has an arid to semi-arid climate with rainy season from November to April and dry season from May to October. The capital city, Windhoek, is home to approximately 500,000 residents and serves as a key site for influenza surveillance activities. Limited influenza surveillance and the absence of routine influenza vaccination program within the Ministry of Health and Social Services (MoHSS) in Namibia have impeded efforts to monitor and mitigate the burden of disease [9]. Without reliable, locally generated data, public health authorities face challenges in making evidence-based decisions regarding testing strategies, vaccine rollout, and the protection of vulnerable populations. This deficiency highlights the urgent need for epidemiological studies to inform and guide national influenza preparedness and response strategies.

To address this gap, the MoHSS initiated influenza surveillance in October 2021, aimed at strengthening local diagnostic capacity and generating actionable data on circulating influenza strains. This study builds on that initiative by analysing data collected between 2021 and 2023 to describe the epidemiology of influenza A and B in Namibia. It also examines symptom correlations to better understand co-occurrence patterns, with the goal of informing targeted public health interventions and improving clinical management for respiratory infections.

Methods

Study design, setting and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted during the period of October 2021 until December 2023, as part of an active surveillance program, an initiative of the Namibia MoHSS, at the Robert Mugabe clinic in Windhoek.

In the present study, participant recruitment was conducted at the outpatient clinic sentinel site. The sentinel site serves an estimated 8 − 10% of Windhoek’s population, based on outpatient attendance records from the Robert Mugabe clinic over the study period [10]. Given that Windhoek accounts for approximately 20 − 25% of Namibia’s population [11], the surveillance data represents roughly 3 − 5% of the national population.

Individuals from all ages and genders who presented with symptoms of respiratory infection were evaluated by trained healthcare workers. After obtaining informed consent, the healthcare workers completed standardized case investigation forms to record demographic and clinical information. This included age, sex, and date of collection, as well as presenting symptoms such as fever, cough, chills, sore throat, shortness of breath, vomiting, diarrhoea, myalgia, loss of smell or taste, headache, loss of appetite, and general flu-like symptoms. The form also recorded medical history and risk factors, including asthma, chronic respiratory disease, chronic cardiac disease, chronic liver disease, diabetes, chronic haematological disorders, hormonal therapy, and cholesterol levels. Additional variables included exposure to influenza antiviral drugs within the previous days and influenza vaccination status for the current season.

A case was defined as a patient of any age with an acute respiratory infection or influenza-like illness (ILI), presenting with fever or a measured temperature of ≥ 38 °C and/or cough within the past seven days, according to the WHO case definition of ILI. Nasopharyngeal and/or oropharyngeal swabs were collected from outpatients who visited the clinic during that time and met the criteria. In addition to influenza testing, SARS-CoV-2 testing was also conducted on the collected specimens as part of routine ILI surveillance. Those who did not meet the case definition were excluded.

Collected specimens were placed in viral transport medium, labelled with the participant’s unique requisition number and disinfected before being securely stored in specimen transport bags within cold boxes containing ice packs. To maintain sample integrity, all specimens were transported to the laboratory within 24 h of collection.

Laboratory results were recorded for influenza A and B viruses, including subtyping for A(H1N1)pdm09, A(H3N2), B(Victoria) and B(Yamagata) lineages. All data were extracted from the laboratory information system, anonymised, and securely stored for analysis.

Influenza detection and subtyping

Viral RNA extraction was performed using one of two platforms, depending on the sample volume received. The BIOER RNA/RNA purification system with the Total Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit III (Hangzhou BioER Technology Co., Ltd, China) or the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN GmbH, Germany), were utilized, following manufacturers’ protocols.

Respiratory specimens were screened and subtyped using the CDC Human Influenza Virus Real-Time RT-PCR Diagnostic Panel (CDC Influenza Division, Atlanta), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Influenza subtyping of positive samples was conducted using the Influenza A/B Typing Kit on a LightCycler 480 Instrument using real-time RT-PCR following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol.

Statistical analyses

Data were captured into a study-specific Excel document and exported first to Epi Info database v7.2.4.0 (Centres for Disease Control and Prevention, Atlanta, GA, USA) for crude analyses and then to into Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Statistics v28.0.1.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics Chicago, IL, USA) for adjusted statistical analysis, where appropriate.

Data was presented as absolute numbers with proportions, median with ranges, and mean with standard deviation, where appropriate. Data points with unknown values — due to missing records, incomplete forms, or untested samples—were excluded to ensure data consistency and allow for accurate comparison across groups. The Fisher exact test, where appropriate, was used to compare categorical variables between groups; odds ratios (ORs) were provided with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables between the groups. Forward logistic regression analysis was performed to calculate adjusted ORs from variables with p < 0.1 in the crude analysis. A p value of equal to or less than 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

To analyse the seasonal trends of influenza and SARS-CoV-2, as well as associated symptoms, we processed and visualized the data using R v4.4.2 in RStudio v2024.12.0.467). The following R packages were utilized: openxlsx v4.2.8 (for reading Excel files), tidyr v1.3.1 (for tidying data, e.g., handling missing data), dplyr v1.1.4 (for data manipulation, e.g., filtering, summarizing), lubridate v1.9.4 (for standardizing/handling date and time operations), and ggplot2 v3.5.1 (for data visualization, e.g., trends over time). The seasonal patterns analysed by aggregating cases and symptoms by month and year. The analysis aimed to capture cyclical patterns and potential shifts in seasonal peaks for both viruses.

To investigate the co-occurrence patterns of influenza symptoms, we constructed a Circos plot using correlation analysis. The circlize package v0.4.16 was used to generate a chord diagram, to highlight the relationships between symptoms, with line thickness representing the strength of the correlation and colour indicating the direction (positive or negative). A Pearson correlation matrix was computed using cor() to quantify the associations between the following symptoms: general/systemic symptoms (fever, chills, myalgia/body pain, headache, loss of appetite), respiratory symptoms (cough, sore throat, shortness of breath), gastrointestinal symptoms (vomiting, diarrhoea), neurological/sensory symptoms (loss of smell, loss of taste), and flu. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r), ranging from − 1 to 1, was used to assess the strength and direction of a linear relationship between the variables (i.e., symptoms). A positive correlation (r > 0) signifies that as one variable increases, the other also increases, whereas a negative correlation (r < 0) signifies that as one variable increases, the other decreases. If r = 0, it suggests no linear relationship between the variables.

Results

Study population

A total of 1186 nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal samples from individuals with ILI symptoms between October 2021 and December 2023 were included. The median age of the study population was 30 years (interquartile range (IQR) 25 − 40), and 58.2% (n = 686/1179) of the known gender were female. Influenza was identified in 25.7% of the nasopharyngeal, or oropharyngeal samples collected from study participants (n = 280/1088). Of the 1162 participants tested for influenza A, 20.2% (n = 235/1162) were positive. Among the influenza A cases, 26.4% (n = 62/235) were H1N1 and 66.8% (n = 157/235) were H3N2 positive. Influenza B was detected in 45 participants (4.3%). Among the influenza B cases, the Victoria lineage was detected in 43 participants (95.6%). The influenza B/Yamagata lineage was not detected in any of the participants. SARS-CoV-2 was also tested and detected in 19.2% of the samples collected (n = 227/1182).

Epidemiology of influenza

Over the study period, an estimated 8200 outpatient consultations were recorded at the sentinel site, based on routine clinic attendance reports submitted to the Health Information System [10]. On average, the clinic reports approximately 250 − 300 outpatient visits per month; multiplied by the 27 months of surveillance, this yields the estimate. The 1186 individuals who met the WHO case definition for ILI therefore represent an ILI consultation rate of approximately 14,463 per 100,000 outpatient visits. Monthly variations were observed, with higher ILI rates typically recorded during the colder months. To monitor and compare the seasonal trends of influenza and SARS-CoV-2 activity, an analysis was conducted to assess patterns of co-circulation over time. The first notifiable increase in cases occurred in October and November 2021, marking an early peak in influenza circulation. This was followed by a steady decline in activity from December 2021 through April 2022. A second, smaller wave emerged in May 2022 and June 2022, after which detection rates fell again. Influenza activity remained relatively low from July and August 2022, but another brief surge was observed in September and October 2022, before declining again in November 2022. This period was followed by several months of minimal to no influenza detection, particularly between December 2022 to May 2023, suggesting a seasonal lull.

A more pronounced resurgence occurred during the winter months of June, July and August 2023, consistent with southern hemisphere seasonal patterns. After this peak, influenza activity subsided once more, with minimal to no- cases detected through the end of the year.

Throughout the study period, influenza A was detected more frequently than influenza B, with A(H3N2) peaking in October − November 2021, May − June 2022 and August 2023. Influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 was the highest subtype in September 2022 and July 2023. Influenza B(Victoria) showed activity in October 2021 and September − December 2022, with consistent circulation from June − September 2023. Overall, these findings demonstrate a clear seasonal trend, with peaks observed in late 2021 and mid-2022, followed by a prolonged period of low or undetectable influenza activity until a resurgence in mid-2023, followed by a decline through late-2023.

Relationship between influenza and demographic and clinical factors

The analysis of clinical symptoms associated with influenza (Table 2) revealed significant associations across both influenzas A and B subtypes. Overall, cough (92% vs. 83%; OR 2.2; 95% CI 1.2–3.9, p = 0.008) and myalgia (23% vs. 11%; OR 2.3; 95% CI 1.5–0.36, p = 0.0004) were strongly associated with influenza, while sore throat (29% vs. 40%; OR 0.61; 95% CI 0.42–0.89, p = 0.010), loss of smell (3.0% vs. 37%; OR 0.19; 95% CI 0.057–0.62, p = 0.002), and headache (16% vs. 29%; OR 0.45; 95% CI 0.29–0.71, p = 0.0005) were associated with a lower likelihood of infection. Vomiting also showed a notable association (3.0% vs. 0.7%; OR 4.2; 95% CI 1.0–17, p = 0.05).

Symptom correlations

Understanding symptom correlations in ILI is important in improving disease diagnosis, management, and surveillance, especially during overlapping respiratory virus outbreaks. To identify patterns of symptom co-occurrence and investigate potential symptom clusters, we conducted a correlation analysis that revealed several significant associations among ILI symptoms, with varying degrees of strength (Fig. 3; Additional file 3). The strongest observed correlation was between loss of smell and loss of taste (r = 0.71, p < 0.001).

Moderate to strong correlations were identified among key ILI-like symptoms. Sore throat exhibited a notable association with headache (r = 0.32, p < 0.001), as well as with cough (r = 0.31, p < 0.001). Similarly, cough was moderately correlated with headache (r = 0.25, p < 0.001), indicating that these symptoms commonly co-occur in individuals presenting with ILI.

Mild but statistically significant correlations (0.1 < r < 0.2) were observed among systemic symptoms such as fever, cough, chills, and myalgia, with additional associations with shortness of breath, loss of appetite, and vomiting. Specifically, shortness of breath was correlated with headache (r = 0.15, p < 0.001) and fever (r = 0.14, p < 0.001), while chills showed a weaker yet significant association with shortness of breath (r = 0.10, p < 0.0004).

While weaker correlations were identified for symptoms such as ILI, diarrhoea, vomiting and loss of appetite, these associations remained statistically significant. Collectively, these findings highlight distinct symptom clusters within ILI presentations including neurological symptoms, characterised by loss of smell, loss of taste, and headache; respiratory symptoms, including fever, cough, shortness of breath, and sore throat; and systemic symptoms, encompassing myalgia, chills, loss of appetite, and vomiting.

Discussion

The study, conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, aimed to identify and characterize influenza viruses circulating in Namibia from October 2021 to December 2023. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study of its kind conducted in Namibia. While several African countries have worked to enhance their influenza surveillance systems to better understand the molecular epidemiology and disease burden of circulating influenza viruses [12], Namibia has had limited data un this regard.

In line with previous studies, our findings also highlight the fluctuating dynamics of respiratory viruses, with SARS-CoV-2 initially dominating during the early surveillance period, followed by a resurgence of influenza in later years. Shifts in respiratory virus dominance post-pandemic restrictions have been documented in South Africa, Senegal and the United States [13–15]. The observed temporal overlap between influenza and SARS-CoV-2 surges underscores the potential impact of co-circulating respiratory pathogens and importance of continued surveillance to monitor viral circulation, anticipate seasonal trends, and inform public health responses.

To contextualize our findings regionally, influenza surveillance data from the Southern African Development Community (SADC) region and the WHO African Regional Office (WHO AFRO) indicate similar influenza circulation patterns over recent years. Historical data from SADC countries reveal a predominance of influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 and A (H3N2) strains with marked seasonal peaks during the Southern Hemisphere winter months, typically between May and August [16, 17]. WHO AFRO reports further emphasize the increasing capacity for influenza surveillance across Africa, documenting fluctuating respiratory virus activity influenced by both climatic factors and public health interventions [18]. Compared to these regional trends, our data from Namibia align closely, underscoring the broader epidemiological patterns observed across southern Africa despite Namibia’s unique climatic and demographic context. This regional congruence reinforces the need for continued integrated surveillance and data sharing to improve influenza preparedness and vaccine strategy optimization within the region.

Our findings indicate that influenza virus activity in Namibia aligns with continental and global trends [1]. The predominant circulating strains during the study period included influenza A(H3N2), A(H1N1)pdm09, and influenza B (Victoria lineage), while no influenza B (Yamagata lineage) viruses were detected in our study population. Notably, A(H3N2) and A(H1N1)pdm09—typically associated with temperate regions—were the most prevalent strains in Windhoek, whereas influenza B (Victoria lineage), which is more common with trends observed in other temperate regions of southern Africa, where either A(H1N1)pdm09 or A(H3N2) has dominated in recent years [1, 12].

The absence of the B/Yamagata lineage in recent influenza cases is consistent with observations from other studies, suggesting a potential extinction of this lineage during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic [19]. This phenomenon is thought to result from reduced transmission opportunities due to public health measures such as social distancing and mask-wearing [1, 19]. Consequently, public health authorities like the WHO have recommended omitting the B/Yamagata lineage from influenza vaccines for the 2024 − 2025 season, reflecting its diminished prevalence [20].

Similar to our findings, previous studies have shown that influenza epidemics in southern Africa predominantly occur between May and August, corresponding with the winter season, with some exceptions [1]. Several studies suggest that circulation patterns are influenced by climatic factors, particularly temperature fluctuations in temperate regions [1]. More broadly, influenza transmission in Africa extends from March to October, encompassing the southern African transmission zone, with activity strongly shaped by climatic variations [1]. Climatic phenomena such as cold spells, El Niño, and La Niña events have been linked to fluctuations in influenza virus activity, often coinciding with late-winter peaks [1].

In the present study, we noticed an increase in influenza symptoms over time, which could be due to several factors such as seasonal patterns and increased ILI testing and awareness. The associations between influenza and its symptoms observed in this study align with existing literature. For example, cough and myalgia have been identified as key indicators of influenza, while symptoms such as vomiting have been more prominently linked to influenza B. Ebell and colleagues found that cough and a sore throat were predictive of influenza infection [21]. Additionally, research conducted in Australia highlighted that vomiting was strongly associated with influenza B infections [22]. Understanding these associations is crucial for improving early detection and management, particularly in clinical settings where symptom-based screening plays a critical role in timely intervention and treatment. Strong correlations among core flu-like symptoms can refine clinical case definitions and inform public health interventions. Notably, the strong link between loss of smell and taste has been observed in respiratory infections like Influenza and SARS-CoV-2 [7, 23–25]. This reinforces the significance of these symptoms in diagnosing respiratory diseases.

The WHO guidelines recommend starting influenza immunization in April. However, based on our findings, which need to be confirmed through large-scale studies, it would be more appropriate to initiate vaccination in March, as the first influenza cases typically begin appearing in April. For the 2025 southern hemisphere influenza season, the WHO recommends the use of trivalent vaccines with slightly differing components depending on the production method [20]. Our analysis indicate that these routine influenza vaccines may not provide optimal protection against the circulating strains identified in this study, highlighting the need to align vaccine composition more closely to local epidemiological trends.

The study benefited from a relatively large sample size and systematically collected clinical data, including detailed symptom profiles, which strengthen the reliability and relevance of the findings. Regardless, this study had several limitations. First, the study population, although relatively large, was restricted to Windhoek, limiting our ability to assess regional variations in influenza activity, particularly in relation to Namibia’s diverse climatic conditions. Future studies should aim to incorporate surveillance data from multiple regions to improve representativeness.

Second, the study did not include influenza A(H5N1) in the analysis. However, given that A(H5N1) has not circulated at a global scale since the 2009 pandemic, its exclusion is unlikely to have significantly impacted the findings.

Third, we observed high detection rates at the beginning of the study period (October − November 2021), which may have introduced a detection bias, potentially overestimating influenza activity at that time.

Fourth, the study was unable to perform time series decomposition methods to examine seasonal and trend components, as the available dataset did not meet the minimum duration for reliable analysis. These techniques typically require at least 24 − 36 months of continuous data, and the limited number of observations likely would have produced unstable or uninterpretable results.

Future studies should consider longer-term surveillance to account for such variations and for more advanced analytical approaches.

Conclusion

Strengthening influenza surveillance in Namibia is critical for improving public health preparedness and response strategies. Public health authorities must implement coordinated preparedness and control activities before epidemic periods. Despite gaps in surveillance systems, the synchronization of preparedness and control efforts across countries within the same transmission zones is essential to minimize the impact of influenza outbreaks.

Year-round molecular surveillance is needed to refine vaccine strategies for the prevention and control of influenza illness. Continuous monitoring will also be crucial in detecting genetic variations and identifying early signs of antigenic drift, ultimately contributing to improved influenza management in Namibia and the broader southern African region.

Three years of surveillance data indicate that influenza viruses circulating in Windhoek, Namibia, are consistent with those detected elsewhere in southern Africa. During this period, influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 and A(H3N2) exhibited strong activity, while influenza B virus of the Victoria lineage was also detected. Further research is needed to determine population-specific disease burden and the effectiveness of current intervention strategies.
