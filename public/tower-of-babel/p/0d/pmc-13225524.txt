Thoracodorsal Composite Vascular Interposition Graft for Contralateral Stacked Lumbar Artery Perforator Flap Breast Reconstruction
Authors: Richard F. Guidry, Devin M. Melancon, Stephen J. Delatte, Hugo St. Hilaire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Alternative breast reconstruction techniques are needed when abdominally based breast reconstruction is unavailable or has insufficient volume. Secondary donor sites, such as the lumbar artery perforator flap, have gained popularity in this select patient population. Traditionally, this technique requires vascular interposition grafting to the internal mammary vessels due to the difficult dissection near the transverse processes, with an increased complication profile leading to a short pedicle length. The deep inferior epigastric vessels are most commonly used as an interposition graft in these cases; however, this graft may be unavailable and requires an additional donor site, resulting in an additional scar with increased donor site morbidity. Here, we present a successful case and indications for using the thoracodorsal composite vascular interposition graft for contralateral stacked lumbar artery perforator flap breast reconstruction.

The lumbar artery perforator (LAP) flap is an increasingly popular alternative breast reconstruction option when the abdominal donor site is insufficient or unavailable.1,2 Advantages of the LAP flap include its consistency, shape, and volume for matching the premorbid breast. Additionally, donor site harvest has the added benefit of a more defined waistline with increased buttock projection.3 Disadvantages include its short pedicle, making an interposition graft necessary in the majority of cases. Deep inferior epigastric artery (DIEA) and vein (DIEV) interposition grafts are the primary graft of choice, as they can be harvested during recipient vessel dissection with a 2-surgeon approach.4 However, this adds an additional scar and may be unavailable if the DIEA/DIEVs have been harvested or damaged in prior procedures. Alternatively, the thoracodorsal artery and vein can be used as a composite vascular graft to the internal mammary artery and vein for additional pedicle length and to mitigate size mismatch. Here, we present the use of the thoracodorsal composite vessel interposition graft as an alternative option for contralateral stacked LAP flap breast reconstruction.

CASE

A 51-year-old woman with left breast cancer underwent a left mastectomy with tissue expander placement, followed by left breast radiation therapy. Subsequently, left breast stacked DIEA perforator flap reconstruction was performed, along with right breast prophylactic mastectomy and prepectoral tissue expander placement. She later presented for right breast reconstruction with a stacked LAP flap using a thoracodorsal interposition graft harvested from the left lateral chest.

Preoperatively, the patient was marked, and an 8-MHz handheld Doppler was used to confirm perforator locations based on preoperative computed tomography imaging. The patient was positioned supine, and right internal mammary artery recipient vessel dissection was performed in standard fashion after third rib cartilage removal. After recipient vessel preparation, the left thoracodorsal system was dissected and harvested to be used as an interposition graft from the prior lateral inframammary fold scar. Importantly, the graft was harvested to include a serratus branch extension, allowing for stacked flap reconstruction (Fig. 1).

Back-table anastomosis of a thoracodorsal composite vascular graft for stacked LAP flap reconstruction.

The patient was positioned prone and re-prepared and draped. Suprafascial LAP flap dissection was performed from medial to lateral, and perforator dissection was carried out until approximately 4 cm of pedicle length. During donor site closure, the flaps were sutured together, and the thoracodorsal artery and vein interposition graft, with serratus artery and vein extension, were anastomosed to the LAP flaps, creating a stacked flap reconstruction on the sterile back-table. A Cook-Swartz implantable Doppler probe was placed distal to the internal mammary artery anastomosis for flap monitoring. Incisions were closed in standard fashion (Fig. 2).

Preoperative and postoperative images. A, Preoperative anterior image of the patient after left breast stacked DIEA perforator reconstruction and right prepectoral tissue expander placement. Postoperative anterior (B) and posterior (C) images of the patient after right stacked LAP flap breast reconstruction with a left thoracodorsal vascular interposition graft.

The Foley catheter was removed intraoperatively, and compressive garments were applied. Flaps were monitored with q4-hour flap checks on the floor for temperature, color, and turgor.5 A clear liquid diet was started immediately postoperatively, and a regular diet was provided the following morning. The patient began ambulating immediately postoperatively and was discharged from the hospital after an uncomplicated postoperative course. Surgical drains were removed once output was less than 30 mL/d.

DISCUSSION

Abdominally based donor sites, such as the DIEA perforator flap, have been the established gold standard for breast reconstruction. However, in cases where there is inadequate infraumbilical adiposity or prior surgical procedures render this donor site unusable, second-line options, such as the LAP flap, have gained popularity.6 In LAP flap reconstruction, interposition grafts have become more mainstream, as complications such as neuropraxia or damage to nerve roots may occur with attempts to harvest additional pedicle length.7 Thus, when greater than 4 cm of pedicle length is required, the flap is harvested, and an interposition graft is routinely used to obtain adequate pedicle length and mitigate size mismatch for microsurgical anastomosis.2

With shorter pedicle length, emphasis on obtaining interposition grafts for flap inset and shaping is of utmost importance.8 Interposition grafts can be taken as arterial grafts such as the deep inferior epigastric, superficial inferior epigastric, superficial circumflex iliac, lateral circumflex femoral, serratus, and thoracodorsal arteries, or as venous grafts such as the saphenous vein, superficial veins of the arm, or vein grafts from the flap donor site.8 Furthermore, harvesting composite arterial and venous grafts rather than isolated artery or vein grafts is advantageous, as it provides similar vessel compliance, elastic modules, medial layer thickness, and less local turbulence at the anastomotic site.9 When the typical DIEA/DIEV vessels are previously harvested or damaged, the thoracodorsal artery and vein can be used as a composite interposition graft. This graft yields the added benefit of harvest within the same donor site of the chest or through prior contralateral breast scars without a separate surgical site and decreased scar burden. Incorporation of the serratus branch during graft harvest allows for unilateral stacked flap reconstruction with decreased ischemia time in cases requiring patient positioning, as graft anastomosis to the free flaps can be completed on the back-table during position changes.10 Additionally, due to the “Y” configuration of this interposition graft when the serratus branch is incorporated, stacked flap reconstruction can be performed with only 1 anastomosis in the chest.

A limitation to this technique is that it can prevent future use of ipsilateral local flap options, such as the thoracodorsal artery perforator flap or the latissimus dorsi flap, in future procedures. This technique is more beneficial in secondary breast reconstructive procedures when contralateral breast reconstruction has been successfully performed; thus, ipsilateral local flap options for breast reconstruction or revision would not be required. Additionally, extra staff are beneficial to facilitate position changes and counting while back-table microsurgery is performed, to decrease ischemia time.

Overall, the thoracodorsal composite vascular interposition graft for contralateral stacked LAP flap breast reconstruction is a viable alternative to the more standard deep inferior epigastric vessels. Use of this graft has the benefit of its access within the chest recipient site or previously reconstructed contralateral breast, obviating the need for additional scars.

CONCLUSIONS

Thoracodorsal composite vascular interposition graft for stacked lumbar perforator flap reconstruction is a viable alternative to the more traditional deep inferior epigastric vascular interposition graft for breast reconstruction, with preferential harvest from the contralateral chest.
