Factors associated with the mental health of parents during and after their child’s hospitalisation: an international longitudinal prospective cohort study
Authors: Renee Mehra, Thomas J Hoffmann, Dave Graham-Squire, Amy J Lisanti, Caryl Gay, Christine Hodgson, Jennifer Rienks, Michelle Pavlik, Sufiya Manju, Nitya Turaga, Michael Clay, Linda S Franck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA child’s hospitalisation may have long-term influences on parental mental health, which can affect child health.ObjectiveTo describe the prevalence of and examine factors associated with parents’ depression and anxiety symptoms over time.MethodsThis prospective cohort study in 14 countries measured parent mental health during their child’s hospitalisation, at discharge and 3 months postdischarge (2023–2024). We included parents ≥18 years staying at a Ronald McDonald House® during their child’s hospitalisation. Exposures were parent, family and child psychosocial and health factors and hospital experiences. Outcomes were parental depression and anxiety symptoms. We used mixed-effects logistic regression.Findings3350 parents, including 2594 (78.6%) mothers, 616 (18.1%) fathers and 131 (3.3%) other caregivers, participated during hospitalisation; 1751 (52%) at discharge, and 2395 (71%) postdischarge. Prevalence of depression and anxiety symptoms was high during hospitalisation (49.7% and 69.0%, respectively) and decreased modestly at discharge (44.6% and 59.5%, respectively), and postdischarge (42.8% and 59.2%, respectively). Over time, probabilities of depression symptoms decreased among those with high, moderate and low levels of social support (50%, 27%, 5%, respectively), and self-care (29%, 27% and 22%, respectively). Probabilities of depression symptoms decreased among those with high or moderate perceived levels of family-centred care (high: 20% decrease; moderate: 11% decrease, compared with low). In contrast, probabilities of depression symptoms increased among those with high or moderate levels of unmet basic needs (high: 18% increase; moderate: 8% increase, compared with low) and poorer ratings of their child’s health (high: 68% increase; moderate: 32% increase, compared with low (healthier)). These trends were similar for anxiety symptoms.ConclusionsPrevalence of depression and anxiety symptoms is high among parents and only decreases modestly postdischarge.Clinical implicationsRoutine screening and ongoing services may lower the risk of prolonged parental mental health symptoms, but more research is needed.

Background

A child’s hospitalisation may contribute to poorer parental mental health, both during and after hospitalisation.1 2 In turn, parental mental health is foundational to child health and development, with a strong evidence base and long-established theoretical models, including ecological systems theory and the biopsychosocial model, used to conceptualise how parental health influences child health.3 Depression and anxiety are among the most prevalent mental health conditions among parents. They often co-occur and may increase the risk for other adverse psychological outcomes, including post-traumatic stress.4 Systematic reviews and meta-analyses show that parental depression and anxiety are associated with poorer infant and child outcomes, including the parent–infant relationship and child developmental, cognitive, behavioural and mental health outcomes.3 A meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials of psychological interventions for parents of children with chronic illnesses, such as cancer and diabetes, showed some longitudinal improvements in child developmental, behavioural and mental health outcomes; however, the level of improvement differed by type of intervention and there are insufficient data on a range of child illnesses.5

Little is known about the mental health trajectories of parents of the wider population of hospitalised children, as previous studies have focused on parents of children admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU),6 7 or paediatric intensive care unit (PICU),8 or with heart conditions,9 or cancer.10 Yet only 2%–13% of child hospitalisations are for critical illnesses, heart conditions or cancer.1113 Additionally, many previous studies were conducted at a single site with a modest sample size or were cross-sectional in design.8 14

Objective

To address these gaps in the literature, we aimed to: (1) describe the prevalence of parents’ depression and anxiety symptoms over time; and (2) examine which parent, family and child factors and hospital experiences were associated with these symptoms over time. A better understanding of parental mental health trajectories will inform clinical practices in screening and service provision, which may improve hospitalised children’s long-term health and development.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a longitudinal prospective cohort study, collecting survey data at three time points: study enrolment while the child was hospitalised, at discharge from the hospital and 3 months postdischarge. We developed the survey using the National Institute on Minority Health and Health Disparities Research Framework15 and included questions on multiple domains and levels of influence. Further details about the study, including enrolment survey results, are reported elsewhere.16 We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines. Participants were recruited from 13 March 2023 to 31 March 2024 and followed through 31 August 2024.

Participants

Participants in 14 countries were recruited. Parents included biological or foster parents and family members who were the child’s primary caregiver. Only one parent aged 18 years or older of a child receiving any inpatient or outpatient hospital care, who stayed at a Ronald McDonald House® for at least three consecutive days, and who spoke one of the available survey languages was eligible to participate. Ronald McDonald House® provides temporary accommodation, meals, peer support and other services to meet the basic needs of families of children receiving hospital care (https://ronaldmcdonaldhouse.org). All parents were asked to complete the enrolment and 3-month postdischarge surveys. Parents who completed the enrolment survey within 7 days of discharge were not asked to complete the discharge survey because the main measures were unlikely to be sensitive to change in such a short time frame, and to minimise the survey burden of completing an additional 15–30 min survey.

Outcomes

We used the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) to measure parental depression and anxiety symptoms experienced over the past 2 weeks. HADS is a well-validated measure of depression and anxiety extensively used in research with many populations.17 The 14-item scale includes a 7-item subscale each for depression and anxiety. Subscale scores range from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating more severe/frequent symptoms. We dichotomised scores using standard clinically significant or concerning symptoms cut-off (≥8).17

Variables of interest

We chose factors a priori, informed by previous findings16 (online supplemental figure S1 and table S1). Parent factors included social support (using the validated 8-Item Duke/University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (UNC) Functional Social Support Questionnaire with the total score ranging from 8 (least) to 40 (most); online supplemental table S1) and basic self-care (total score in past month ranging from 0 (less frequent) to 15 (more frequent)). Family factors included number of unmet basic needs (eg, transportation, healthcare services, food, clothing, child’s needs and utilities) and worry about not having stable or safe housing in the next 2 months. Child factors included parental rating of the child’s current health (ranging from 1 (very healthy) to 5 (very sick, fragile or injured)) and number of health concerns or disabilities (eg, chronic illness, mental or physical health concern and developmental or learning disability). Healthcare experiences included perception of family-centredness of hospital care (mean score ranging from 1 (least) to 7 (most)). Variables were measured at all three time points, except for perceived hospital family-centred care, which was measured in the enrolment and discharge surveys.

Control variables

Control variables included the following parent characteristics: age, parental role (mother, father, other caregiver), partner status (living with a partner or not), education (high school or less, some college/trade school, completed college/trade school, graduate degree), work status (working or not), health insurance (public, private, none/uninsured, other), serving as a main caregiver for others besides their children (yes or no), prior experience of discrimination in daily life (includes discrimination attributed to race, ethnicity and other characteristics; ranging from 0 (never) to 5 (very frequently)), ability to speak the main language of the site of care (very well compared with well, not well and not at all), needing help understanding health information (needing help sometimes or more compared with not needing help) and length of stay at the Ronald McDonald House before completing the enrolment survey. We also included the following family characteristics: household size, annual household income (low (approximately at or below the poverty line) or not low) and geographical region; and child characteristics: child older than a neonate (>28 days). All control variables were measured in the enrolment survey only, except for parent partner status, work status, health insurance, health literacy and household size, which were measured at all three time points.

Statistical analyses

Additional detail is presented in the supplementary methods, online supplemental table S2 and in earlier work,16 including power analysis and multiple imputation methods. We calculated all statistics using survey sample weights to give population estimates, as our sampling was stratified by sites within countries. If an individual was missing items on any instrument, the participant-specific mean multiplied by the number of questions was used. The internal consistency of the HADS depression and anxiety subscales over time was measured using Cronbach’s alpha for complete cases at each time point. Dichotomised depression and anxiety outcomes at three time points were modelled with mixed-effects logistic regression models with an individual-level random effect. To determine independent associations with outcomes, we conducted backwards stepwise regression analyses, a common data-driven approach for variable selection. For each variable, we first conducted univariable regressions, retained those with Wald test p≤0.2 and then tested an interaction with time for variables measured at all three time points (a more stringent p≤0.05), using pairwise listwise deletion due to low missingness for individual variables. These variables were then subjected to a two-stage backwards stepwise regression analysis using multiple imputations (500 imputations) to address missing data, first testing interaction terms (p≤0.05) and then main effects (p≤0.05). For interpretability, we also report main results as estimated marginal means, with levels of continuous variables defined as low (1 SD below the mean), moderate (mean) and high (1 SD above the mean).

Findings

Of 8807 eligible parents, 4119 (47%) signed a permission form to share contact details to be invited into the study, and 3350 (28%) completed an enrolment survey and were included in analyses (online supplemental figure S2). Of those included in analyses, 2098 were sent a discharge survey and 1751 (83%) completed it (52% of those included in analyses), and 3063 were sent a postdischarge survey and 2395 (78%) completed it (71% of those included in analyses). Variable missingness for the enrolment survey was 3.3%, and listwise deletion was 42.5%. The enrolment survey was completed a median of five (IQR=2–10) days after check-in to the Ronald McDonald House, the discharge survey completed a median of two (IQR=0–7) days after hospital discharge and the postdischarge survey completed a median of 95 (IQR=90–108) days after discharge. The internal consistency of the HADS measures was similar over time. Cronbach’s alpha for the depression subscale was 0.84 during hospitalisation, 0.85 at discharge and 0.86 postdischarge. Cronbach’s alpha for the anxiety subscale was 0.79 during hospitalisation, 0.80 at discharge and 0.81 postdischarge.

Parent median age was 35 years (IQR=29–42), 2594 (78.6%) were mothers and 616 (18.1%) were fathers (table 1). Most parents (2511, 76.3%) were living with a partner and 1354 (44.1%) were working. Most hospitalised children (2405, 76.2%) were older than a neonate. Cardiology (846, 24.3%), neonatal (679, 23.1%) and oncology/haematology (750, 20.9%) were the most common reasons for child’s hospitalisation. Most families (1538, 69.0%) were staying at a Ronald McDonald House in the USA, 995 (11.7%) in Latin America, 486 (9.7%) in Canada, 183 (6.2%) in the Asia-Pacific region and 148 (3.4%) in Europe. Additional characteristics are presented in online supplemental table S3. Factors including worry about housing, parent rating of child’s health and sociodemographics were associated with the completion of the discharge and postdischarge surveys (online supplemental table S4).

Mean social support was similar during hospitalisation (31.9 (SD 7.8)) and at discharge (31.6 (SD 8.0)), then decreased postdischarge (30.3 (SD 8.4); table 2). Mean self-care increased from hospitalisation to discharge (5.9 (SD 2.9) to 7.2 (SD 2.9)) and decreased postdischarge (6.0 (SD 2.8)). Mean number of unmet needs decreased slightly from hospitalisation to discharge (0.88 (SD 1.62) to 0.86 (SD 1.57)), but increased postdischarge (1.14 (SD 1.89)). Over time, parents’ rating of their child’s health improved while the number of child’s health concerns increased.

In regression analyses, depression symptoms generally decreased over time. Overall, parents with higher levels of social support and self-care had lower predicted probabilities of depression symptoms. The probability of depression symptoms during hospitalisation was lower among parents with high social support (0.26; 95% CI 0.23 to 0.30) compared with those with moderate (0.45; 95% CI 0.42 to 0.49) or low social support (0.66; 95% CI 0.61 to 0.71; table 3). Similarly, the probability of depression symptoms during hospitalisation was lower among parents with high self-care (0.24; 95% CI 0.20 to 0.28) compared with those with moderate (0.45; 95% CI 0.42 to 0.49) or low self-care (0.68; 95% CI 0.64 to 0.72). However, social support and self-care modified depression symptoms over time (figure 1; table 3; online supplemental table S5). The higher the level of social support, the greater the reduction in the probability of depression symptoms from hospitalisation to postdischarge (figure 1, panel a; interaction term p<0.001, online supplemental table S5). The probability of depression symptoms among those with high social support decreased 50% from 0.26 (95% CI 0.23 to 0.30) during hospitalisation to 0.13 (95% CI 0.10 to 0.16) at postdischarge (table 3). Among those with moderate social support, the probability of depression symptoms decreased 27% from 0.45 (95% CI 0.42 to 0.49) during hospitalisation to 0.33 (95% CI 0.29 to 0.37) at postdischarge. Among those with a low level of social support, the probability of depression symptoms only decreased 5% from 0.66 (95% CI 0.61 to 0.71) during hospitalisation to 0.63 (95% CI 0.57 to 0.68) at postdischarge. The probability of depression symptoms reduced from hospitalisation to postdischarge among those with high (29% reduction), moderate (27% reduction) and low (22% reduction) levels of self-care (table 3; figure 1, panel b; interaction term p=0.32, online supplemental table S5). However, those with a high level of self-care had a 25% increased probability of depression symptoms between hospitalisation and discharge (0.24 (95% CI 0.20 to 0.28) during hospitalisation to 0.30 (95% CI 0.26 to 0.35) at discharge), while those with a low level of self-care had a 9% reduction (0.68 (95% CI 0.64 to 0.72) during hospitalisation to 0.62 (95% CI 0.56 to 0.68) at discharge; interaction term p<0.0067, online supplemental table S5).

The higher the level of unmet basic needs, the higher the probability of depression symptoms over time. Compared with parents with a low level of unmet basic needs, the probability of depression symptoms was 18% higher among those with a high level and 8% higher among those with a moderate level (table 3). The poorer the rating of their child’s health, the higher the probability of depression symptoms over time. Compared with parents who rated their children as healthier, the probability of depression symptoms was 68% higher among those whose children were rated more sick, fragile or injured, and 32% higher among those whose children were rated as moderate. The higher the perceived level of family-centred care, the lower the probability of depression symptoms over time. Compared with those with a low perceived level of family-centred care, the probability of depression symptoms was 20% lower among those with a high perceived level of family-centred care and 11% lower among those with a moderate perceived level of family-centred care.

Anxiety symptoms generally decreased over time. Overall, parents with higher levels of social support and self-care had lower predicted probabilities of anxiety symptoms over time. The probability of anxiety symptoms during hospitalisation was lower among parents with high social support (0.72; 95% CI 0.68 to 0.76) compared with those with moderate (0.80; 95% CI 0.77 to 0.82) or low social support (0.86; 95% CI 0.82 to 0.89; table 3). Similarly, the probability of anxiety symptoms during hospitalisation was lower among parents with high self-care (0.63; 95% CI 0.58 to 0.68) compared with those with moderate (0.80; 95% CI 0.77 to 0.82) or low self-care (0.90; 95% CI 0.88 to 0.92). However, social support and self-care modified anxiety symptoms over time (figure 1; table 3; online supplemental table S6). The higher the level of social support, the greater the reduction in the probability of anxiety symptoms from hospitalisation to postdischarge (figure 1, panel c; interaction term p<0.001, online supplemental table S6). The probability of anxiety symptoms among those with high social support decreased 39% from 0.72 (95% CI 0.68 to 0.76) during hospitalisation to 0.44 (95% CI 0.39 to 0.50) at postdischarge (table 3). Among those with moderate social support, the probability of anxiety symptoms decreased 18% from 0.80 (95% CI 0.77 to 0.82) during hospitalisation to 0.66 (95% CI 0.62 to 0.70) at postdischarge. Among those with a low level of social support, the probability of anxiety symptoms only decreased 3% from 0.86 (95% CI 0.82 to 0.89) during hospitalisation to 0.83 (95% CI 0.79 to 0.86) at postdischarge. Parents with higher levels of self-care had greater reductions in the probability of anxiety symptoms from hospitalisation to postdischarge (17% reduction for high and 18% reduction for moderate, compared with 13% reduction for low level; figure 1, panel d; table 3; interaction term p=0.018, online supplemental table S6). There were differences in the reduction in the probability of anxiety symptoms from hospitalisation to discharge (3% reduction for high, 9% reduction for moderate and 8% reduction for low level; interaction term p=0.012, online supplemental table S6).

The higher the level of unmet basic needs, the higher the probability of anxiety symptoms over time. Compared with parents with a low level of unmet basic needs, the probability of anxiety symptoms was 12% higher among those with a high level and 7% higher among those with a moderate level (table 3). The poorer the rating of their child’s health, the higher the probability of anxiety symptoms over time. Compared with parents who rated their children as healthier, the probability of anxiety symptoms was 36% higher among those whose children were rated more sick, fragile or injured, and 21% higher among those whose children were rated as moderate. The higher the number of child’s health concerns, the higher the probability of anxiety symptoms over time. Compared with parents of children with a low number of health concerns, the probability of anxiety symptoms was 12% higher among those with a high number and 7% higher among those with a moderate number.

Discussion

In our large, international sample of parents of children hospitalised for a range of health conditions, we found the prevalence of depression and anxiety symptoms was high and persisted throughout the study period, decreasing only modestly over time (14% reduction from hospitalisation to postdischarge). Depression and anxiety symptoms were associated with factors in multiple domains (eg, biological, behavioural, sociocultural environment and healthcare system) and levels of influence (eg, individual and interpersonal).15 Parents reporting higher levels of social support and self-care had decreased probabilities, whereas those with more unmet needs and poorer rating of their child’s health had increased probabilities of depression and anxiety symptoms over time, and those reporting higher levels of perceived hospital family-centred care had a decreased probability of depression symptoms over time, suggesting these factors are important potential targets for future interventions.

14% of the global population experiences mental disorders, although the prevalence of depressive and anxiety disorders varies by country and increased after the COVID-19 pandemic.18 In our study, depression and anxiety symptoms prevalence was higher and magnitude of change over time was lower compared with most prior studies. In prior NICU and PICU studies, the prevalence of parental depression and anxiety symptoms ranged from 8% to 51%, and 23% to 62%, respectively.168 14 19 Among parents of children with cancer, 28% had depression symptoms (range: 7%–91%) and 21% had anxiety symptoms (range: 5%–65%) at diagnosis,20 whereas among parents of children with heart conditions, up to 46% had depression symptoms and up to 25% had anxiety symptoms during hospitalisation.2 9 Many longitudinal studies showed a declining prevalence in depression and anxiety symptoms over four to 6 months following hospital discharge (up to a 51% reduction in prevalence).6 7 However, some studies showed up to a 20% increase in prevalence up to 6 months following hospital discharge or no change over time.2 6 10 Our rigorously conducted, population-weighted, multicountry study provides contemporary data on the prevalence of parental depression and anxiety symptoms across hospital settings and children’s diagnoses and clearly shows the scale of the problem over time. While the optimal tools and timing for mental health screening have not been established for this population,4 and should be the focus of future research, our findings add to prior research suggesting the need for routine screening of depression and anxiety in parents at the hospital and after discharge.21

Parental mental health may be improved through enhanced access to psychological and psychosocial services. Among parents of children with chronic illnesses, a meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials showed that problem-solving therapy, but not cognitive–behavioural therapy (CBT), improved parental mental health post-treatment and follow-up.5 Although a review found that CBT and interpersonal therapy may prevent perinatal depression.22 Additionally, in a randomised-controlled trial, a 15-month peer support programme was associated with reduced parental depression and anxiety postdischarge.23 However, there is an ongoing need for further research on psychological, psychosocial and other interventions that may improve parental mental health over time.

Our study is one of the few studies to test for interactions. We found there were modest decreases in probabilities of depression and anxiety symptoms postdischarge among those with higher levels of social support, but little change among those with a low level of social support. Based on these interactions with time, there may be some indication to screen for social support among parents of hospitalised children. Our findings for social support among parents of children hospitalised for a range of health conditions are in contrast to a longitudinal study among parents of children with congenital heart disease that found that decreased social support over time was associated with an increased risk of depression,9 perhaps due to differences in study populations, measures of mental health and social support, and analytic methods. Our findings extend prior work by showing that in a large, international study of parents of children hospitalised for a range of health conditions, social support is associated with both depression and anxiety symptoms over time. More research is needed to understand the context, type and timing of social support associated with lower probabilities of parental depression and anxiety, especially among parents with a low level of social support, to guide future supportive interventions.

In our study, parents with more frequent self-care had lower probabilities of depression and anxiety symptoms over time. There is little research on the trajectories of self-care and mental health among parents of hospitalised children. In longitudinal qualitative studies, parents of children with critical illnesses reported prioritising the care needs of their children and families before their own self-care or mental health, and 20% had not fully recovered their emotional well-being after 2 years.21 Future research should explore whether interventions to support parents in maintaining basic self-care during and after their child’s hospitalisation are protective of parent mental health.

A prior longitudinal study found that higher perceived family-centred care was associated with decreased risk of depression symptoms among parents of infants discharged from the NICU.7 Our findings add to the literature that higher perceived family-centred care is associated with a decreased probability of depression symptoms in a broader population of parents of hospitalised children. Family-centred care is an approach to healthcare based on mutually beneficial partnerships between healthcare providers, patients and families.24 A systematic review found that family-centred interventions were associated with improved outcomes in children and parents.25 Future research can explore which aspects of family-centred care are protective of parental mental health, such as parent engagement in care or shared-decision making, that could be targeted for intervention.

In our study, social needs, such as higher number of unmet basic needs and worry about housing, were associated with higher probabilities of depression and anxiety symptoms over time. This is consistent with studies among parents of children with cancer26 and heart conditions.9 However, previous studies have rarely examined or accounted for the association between housing insecurity and mental health symptoms, even though this association is well-documented in the literature.27 Parents report needing social, financial and transitional support both in the hospital and in the following months and years.21 Our findings add to the literature that housing worry is also associated with parental mental health and should be included in the screening process. However, screening should be family-centred and integrated with referrals and linkages to community-based resources, otherwise inequities may be exacerbated.28

As expected, we found that the more severe parents perceived their child’s health condition and the more chronic health concerns or disabilities their child had, the higher the probabilities of depression and anxiety symptoms over time, which is consistent with other studies.29 While this may be a non-modifiable factor, awareness is important so that mental health services can be offered to parents who perceive their child to be seriously ill.

Strengths and limitations

Despite our large, diverse, international sample of parents of hospitalised children and longitudinal study design with statistical weighting and multiple imputation, this study has some limitations. We used a convenience sample, so our results may not be generalisable beyond the included countries. The results may not be representative of all parents who did not receive Ronald McDonald House accommodation and services during their child’s hospital stay, although approximately one-third of participants stayed elsewhere for part of their child’s hospital stay, and their experiences are reflected in the results. We did not collect hospital data on the child’s diagnosis or clinician-assessed data on severity of illness, including whether a child spent time in an ICU, so we were unable to assess differences. However, all children had conditions that required a family member to stay at a Ronald McDonald House for at least 3 days and clinician assessed severity of a child’s illness may have less impact on parents’ mental health than parents’ perception of their child’s illness.9 14 Children of parents who completed the discharge survey had longer hospital stays, which may have impacted parental mental health. We did not collect data on rehospitalisation status and were unable to control for this in the analyses. Countries vary in their conceptualisation of race/ethnicity, making it challenging to measure this construct in international studies. Moreover, it is racial/ethnic discrimination, not race or ethnicity per se, that can negatively impact health and healthcare.30 In our study, we collected data on discrimination attributed to multiple characteristics, including race/ethnicity. Future research studies are needed to assess discrimination related to specific social characteristics. However, the high postdischarge survey completion rate among those included in the analyses and the low variable missingness increase the robustness of the findings of this study.

Clinical implications

We found a high prevalence of depression and anxiety symptoms among parents during and after their child’s hospitalisation, with a modest decrease in prevalence over time. Acute and primary care health systems should consider routine screening at the hospital and after discharge to identify parents with mental health symptoms needing intervention. Given the risk to children, parents, families and society of persistent poor parental mental health, these findings should motivate hospitals and non-government organisations to provide and explore evidence-based strategies to better meet the ongoing social and relational needs of parents of hospitalised children.
