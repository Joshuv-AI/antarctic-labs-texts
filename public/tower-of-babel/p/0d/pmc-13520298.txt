Clinical and molecular characterization of TCF12 variants in an Asian pediatric cohort with craniosynostosis
Authors: Rongle Zhong, Lei Zheng, Qing Yan, Zhe Gao, Chunyu Zhong, Xianli Zhang, Bixia Zheng, Ling Zhang, Wei Zhou, Gang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCraniosynostosis is a genetically heterogeneous craniofacial disorder caused by the premature fusion of one or more cranial sutures. Pathogenic variants in TCF12, encoding a basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) transcription factor, represent a major cause of autosomal dominant coronal craniosynostosis and are characterized by incomplete penetrance and marked phenotypic variability. However, clinical and molecular data from Asian pediatric populations remain limited.MethodsTrio-based whole-exome sequencing was performed on ten pediatric patients with cranial deformities and their parents. The identified TCF12 variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines and validated by Sanger sequencing. Detailed clinical and radiological data were collected. In addition, a comprehensive literature review was conducted to summarize previously reported TCF12 variants and associated phenotypes.ResultsTen distinct heterozygous TCF12 variants were identified in ten unrelated pediatric patients, all of which were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic according to ACMG criteria. Six variants were inherited, and four occurred de novo. Seven patients had imaging-confirmed craniosynostosis, predominantly involving the coronal sutures (five bilateral and one unilateral), while one patient presented with multisuture craniosynostosis (left coronal and sagittal sutures). Three patients showed cranial deformities without radiographic evidence of suture fusion. Phenotypic heterogeneity and incomplete penetrance were observed, including a mildly affected parent. Most pathogenic variants were truncating variants distributed mainly across exons 14–19 and predicted to induce loss of function, either through nonsense-mediated mRNA decay or the production of truncated proteins lacking the entire C-terminal bHLH domain. Structural modeling analysis further indicated that the bHLH-domain-located missense variant p.Arg603Trp alters the local DNA-binding conformation of TCF12 and impairs its binding affinity to the E-box DNA motif.ConclusionsThis study provides additional clinical and molecular data on TCF12-related craniosynostosis in a pediatric cohort from an Asian population. Our findings support haploinsufficiency as the central pathogenic mechanism, primarily driven by truncating variants affecting the C-terminal bHLH domain. The marked clinical heterogeneity, the presence of mild or evolving phenotypes, and incomplete penetrance observed in our cohort underscore the importance of early diagnosis and longitudinal clinical surveillance in affected families.

Introduction

Craniosynostosis is a common craniofacial developmental disorder characterized by the premature fusion of one or more calvarial sutures, with an estimated incidence of approximately 6 per 10,000 live births [1]. Clinically, craniosynostosis is broadly classified into syndromic and non-syndromic forms. Non-syndromic craniosynostosis accounts for nearly 70–80% of cases and typically involves the premature fusion of a single cranial suture, most commonly the sagittal, coronal, or metopic suture. In contrast, syndromic craniosynostosis is more frequently associated with multisystem anomalies, identifiable pathogenic variants, and familial inheritance patterns [2].

Premature fusion of cranial sutures restricts skull growth perpendicular to the affected suture, while compensatory growth occurs at unaffected sutures to accommodate brain development, resulting in characteristic cranial deformities. In some cases, complications such as visual impairment, dental abnormalities, and other craniofacial abnormalities, as well as neurodevelopmental delay, may occur. The severity of clinical manifestations varies widely depending on the number of sutures involved, the timing of fusion, and the underlying genetic etiology [3].

Advances in molecular genetics and high-throughput sequencing technologies have substantially expanded the understanding of the genetic architecture of craniosynostosis. To date, multiple genes—including FGFR2, FGFR3, TWIST1, EFNB1, MSX2, and SMAD6—have been implicated in cranial suture biology [4, 5]. These genes are involved in key developmental signaling pathways such as FGF, BMP, and WNT, which regulate osteoblast proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis during cranial vault formation. Disruption of these pathways alters the balance between bone formation and suture patency, ultimately leading to premature ossification of the cranial sutures [2, 6, 7].

Among the genes that contribute to coronal craniosynostosis, TCF12 has emerged as a major etiologic factor. TCF12 encodes a basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factor belonging to the E-protein family, which regulates gene transcription through dimerization and binding to E-box motifs. Pathogenic variants in TCF12 were first identified in patients with craniosynostosis by Sharma et al. establishing TCF12 as a frequent genetic cause of autosomal dominant coronal craniosynostosis [8]. As the number of reported cases has increased, it has been observed that individuals carrying TCF12 variants can exhibit a broad spectrum of cranial suture-related phenotypes, ranging from bilateral coronal synostosis to mild cranial asymmetry or subtle cranial flattening, sometimes without definitive radiographic evidence of suture fusion, particularly in early infancy [8–10]. Collectively, these variable presentations highlight the clinical heterogeneity and incomplete penetrance of TCF12-related craniosynostosis.

Large-scale sequencing studies have shown that the majority of pathogenic TCF12 variants are truncating in nature, including nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site mutations, whereas missense variants are less common [8, 10–13]. This distribution strongly supports haploinsufficiency due to loss-of-function as the principal pathogenic mechanism. Additional evidence from chromosomal microdeletions and intragenic exon rearrangements further confirms that reduced TCF12 gene dosage alone is sufficient to cause craniosynostosis [11, 14, 15]. For the smaller subset of reported missense variants—many of which affect the bHLH domain—current evidence suggests that they may disrupt protein dimerization and/or DNA binding, thereby weakening TCF12 transcriptional function. However, functional validation is available for only a limited number of variants, and the pathogenic effects of many missense changes remain to be clarified [8]. Notably, data from Asian pediatric populations remain scarce, limiting comprehensive genotype-phenotype correlations across diverse ethnic backgrounds.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed ten pediatric patients with cranial deformities who were found to carry pathogenic or likely pathogenic TCF12 variants through trio-based whole-exome sequencing at a tertiary pediatric medical center in China. By integrating detailed clinical phenotyping with molecular genetic analysis and a comprehensive review of previously reported TCF12 variants, we aimed to provide additional clinical and molecular data on TCF12-related craniosynostosis and to further clarify its clinical variability and underlying pathogenic mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Study participants and clinical evaluation

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. 202312004-1). Pediatric patients presenting with cranial deformities were recruited from the Department of Neurosurgery, including both inpatient and outpatient settings. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants prior to enrollment.

In total, 230 probands underwent trio-based whole-exome sequencing during routine clinical evaluation for suspected craniosynostosis. After variant filtering and interpretation using our standard pipeline, 10 patients harboring confirmed heterozygous TCF12 variants were identified and included in the present study. Thus, this study was designed as a case series of TCF12-positive patients derived from a larger sequenced cohort.

All patients underwent comprehensive clinical evaluation performed by experienced pediatric neurosurgeons. Cranial morphology was assessed through physical examination and radiological imaging, including computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), when indicated. The involvement of cranial sutures and the severity of deformities were evaluated by senior radiologists. Neurodevelopmental status was assessed using the Denver Developmental Screening Test (DST), with particular attention to gross motor, fine motor, language, and social development domains.

Whole-exome sequencing and variant validation

Trio-based whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed for each proband and their biological parents. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using standard protocols. Exome capture and sequencing were conducted on an Illumina platform with paired-end sequencing.

Bioinformatic analysis included read alignment to the human reference genome GRCh38 (hg38), variant calling, and annotation. Variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) greater than 1% in public population databases were excluded. Population frequency annotation was performed using dbSNP, the 1000 Genomes Project, ExAC, and the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD). Candidate variants were prioritized based on predicted functional impact and inheritance pattern, and variant filtering was conducted using a phenotype-driven approach applied to the whole-exome dataset, rather than a predefined craniosynostosis-related gene panel. Variants were further prioritized according to their relevance to genes known to be associated with craniosynostosis.

The pathogenicity of identified variants was interpreted in accordance with the standards and guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) [16]. All candidate TCF12 variants were validated by bidirectional Sanger sequencing in probands and available family members. Variant nomenclature followed Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) recommendations, using the MANE Select transcript NM_207037.2 (GRCh38) as the reference sequence.

In silico variant analysis

Population allele frequencies of TCF12 variants were further assessed using gnomAD. In silico prediction tools, including PolyPhen-2, MutationTaster, CADD (phred-scaled scores), and NMDEscPredictor, were applied to evaluate the potential functional impact of missense and truncating variants.

Evolutionary conservation analysis was performed using Clustal Omega for multiple sequence alignment. The protein domain architecture of TCF12 and the localization of identified variants were illustrated based on publicly available protein annotation resources.

Exploratory structural modeling of the TCF12 p.Arg603Trp variant within a bHLH-DNA complex

Exploratory structural modeling was performed to visualize the potential local effect of the p.Arg603Trp substitution within a bHLH-DNA complex. After predicting the human TCF12 bHLH monomer via AlphaFold3 and adopting the murine orthologous E47/NeuroD1-DNA complex (PDB ID: 2QL2) as docking template [17], conformational sampling was conducted using the HDOCK program [18, 19], which applies a hybrid docking strategy [18] to generate 100 putative structural conformations. The conformation with the lowest energy score was selected as the structural reference, and the resulting model was visualized using PyMOL software (Version 3.0.3) for result analysis and graphical presentation. This analysis was intended only to provide a qualitative structural interpretation of the variant and was not used as direct functional evidence.

Literature review

A comprehensive literature review was conducted to identify previously reported TCF12 variants and associated clinical phenotypes. Searches were performed in PubMed and the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) using the keywords “TCF12”, “craniosynostosis”, and “mutation”. Articles published up to October 2025 were included.

Reported variants were curated and annotated according to HGVS guidelines using the NM_207037.2 reference transcript. Clinical information, including suture involvement, inheritance pattern, and phenotypic variability, was extracted to facilitate comparison with the present cohort.

Results

Clinical characteristics

Among the 230 probands with suspected craniosynostosis who underwent trio-based whole-exome sequencing, 10 patients harboring heterozygous TCF12 variants were identified and included in this study. The clinical manifestations ranged from mild cranial asymmetry without definitive radiographic evidence of suture fusion to complex multisuture craniosynostosis. Age at diagnosis ranged from the neonatal period to 20 months (median, 5 months); 6 of 10 patients were diagnosed before 5 months of age. Eight patients were male and two were female.

Among the ten patients, seven exhibited imaging-confirmed craniosynostosis. Coronal suture involvement was the most frequent presentation. One patient (Patient 3) had unilateral right coronal synostosis, while five patients (Patients 2, 4, 5, 7, and 9) demonstrated bilateral coronal synostosis. One patient (Patient 1) presented with premature fusion of the left coronal and sagittal sutures, resulting in a particularly severe cranial deformity. Two patients (Patients 6 and 8) showed cranial flattening and hypertelorism without clear radiological evidence of premature suture fusion at the time of evaluation. One patient (Patient 10) exhibited right frontal flattening accompanied by incomplete closure of the right eyelid. This may be attributed to abnormal cranial growth, leading to changes in orbital morphology and dimensions.

A familial occurrence was noted in one case, in which the mother of Patient 2 displayed cranial features consistent with bilateral coronal synostosis, primarily presenting with brachycephaly. Strabismus was observed in one patient (Patient 6). Developmental delay was identified in three patients: mild developmental delay was observed in Patient 5 based on developmental screening, and two patients (Patients 6 and 8) were diagnosed with global developmental delay. Identification of variants in TCF12

Trio-based whole-exome sequencing identified ten distinct heterozygous variants in the TCF12 gene across the ten probands. All variants were validated by Sanger sequencing and annotated using the MANE Select transcript NM_207037.2. Among the ten families reported here, variants in six patients (Patients 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, and 8) were inherited from one of their parents, whereas variants in the remaining four patients (Patients 4, 7, 9, and 10) occurred de novo. All variants were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic according to the ACMG criteria.

The identified variants comprised two nonsense variants, c.1128G > A and c.1127G > A, both predicted to result in p.Trp376Ter; four frameshift variants, including c.1836dup (p.Arg613ThrfsTer6), c.1642_1645del (p.Glu548ArgfsTer14), c.1841_1842dup (p.Arg615CysfsTer19), and c.973del (p.Thr325ProfsTer72); two canonical splice-site variants (c.1467 + 2T > G and c.1115–2 A > T); one missense variant, c.1807 C > T (p.Arg603Trp), and one splice-disrupting variant c.825G > C (p.Leu275Phe). Although c.825G > C is nominally annotated as p.(Leu275Phe), this variant has been functionally validated to disrupt normal splicing and cause intron 10 retention, rather than exerting a conventional missense effect [8]. In gnomAD v4.1.1 (GRCh38), c.1836dup (p.Arg613ThrfsTer6), c.1642_1645del (p.Glu548ArgfsTer14), c.1128G > A (p.Trp376Ter) and c.1807 C > T (p.Arg603Trp) have been detected in the general population, but all at extremely low allele frequencies. To our knowledge, c.1841_1842dup (p.Arg615CysfsTer19), c.973del (p.Thr325ProfsTer72), c.1128G > A (p.Trp376Ter), c.1467 + 2T > G and c.1115–2 A > T are newly identified variants in patients with craniosynostosis. Notably, the protein alteration p.Trp376Ter resulting from c.1128G > A has been reported for the adjacent variant c.1127G > A. Genetic and in silico analysis of identified TCF12 variants

When mapped to the TCF12 gene and protein structure, most variants were enriched in exons 14–19. Notably, the C-terminal bHLH functional domain is encoded exclusively by exons 19 and 20 in the NM_207037.2 transcript (Fig. 2A). These truncating variants (all frameshift variants and the nonsense variant p.Trp376Ter) are predicted either to undergo nonsense-mediated mRNA decay or to generate truncated proteins lacking the C-terminal bHLH domain. In addition, the frameshift variant p.Thr325ProfsTer72 was also expected to result in the loss of large portions of the N-terminal transcriptional regulatory regions (Fig. 2A). As shown in Fig. 3A, variants within the NMD+ segment are predicted to undergo nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, while those in the 3′ region are classified as NMD-.

The splice-site variants, including the canonical splice-site variants c.1115–2 A > T in intron 13 and c.1467 + 2T > G in intron 16, as well as the exon-border splice-disrupting variant c.825G > C in exon 10, were located at or near conserved exon–intron boundaries. These variants were predicted or previously demonstrated to disrupt normal pre-mRNA splicing, thereby leading to aberrant transcripts and impaired protein function.

The missense variant p.Arg603Trp, located in exon 19, affects a highly conserved arginine residue within the basic region of the C-terminal bHLH domain (Fig. 3B). This basic region mediates sequence-specific DNA binding to E-box motifs, whereas the helix-loop-helix region primarily contributes to dimer formation. Based on established structural studies of bHLH-DNA complexes, conserved residues within the basic region, including arginine residues, are likely to contribute to DNA phosphate-backbone interactions, groove positioning, and base recognition [21, 22].

Exploratory homology-based structural modeling of the p.Arg603Trp variant was performed using AlphaFold3 and the orthologous E47/NeuroD1/DNA complex structure (PDB: 2QL2). Visualization of the modeled complex showed that Arg603 is located within the DNA-binding basic region of TCF12, close to the interface with the E-box DNA motif. The substitution of the positively charged arginine by a bulky hydrophobic tryptophan appeared to alter the local spatial arrangement among the TCF12 basic region, its dimeric partner, and DNA, suggesting a potential disturbance of the DNA-binding configuration (Fig. 3C). Therefore, replacement of the positively charged Arg603 by a bulky hydrophobic tryptophan is predicted to impair local DNA-binding interactions, including phosphate-backbone anchoring, groove positioning, and/or E-box recognition, and may consequently affect TCF12-dependent transcriptional regulation.

Discussion

In this study, we performed an integrated clinical and genetic investigation of ten pediatric patients with cranial deformities harboring heterozygous TCF12 variants. By combining detailed phenotypic characterization with trio-based whole-exome sequencing, we provide additional clinical and molecular data on TCF12-related craniosynostosis, particularly in an Asian pediatric cohort.

We identified TCF12 variants with a predominance of truncating variants, including nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site variants. Integration of our data with published cases indicates that pathogenic TCF12 variants predominantly cluster within the C-terminal region of the gene, particularly in exons 14–19, with a subset directly affecting the bHLH domain encoded by exons 19 and 20 [8, 11, 15]. Collectively, published data indicate that pathogenic TCF12 variants are primarily loss-of-function, supporting haploinsufficiency as the central pathogenic mechanism underlying TCF12-associated craniosynostosis [4, 8].

Functionally, TCF12 is critical for maintaining cranial suture patency by regulating osteoblast differentiation during skull development [23]. TCF12 interacts with the class II bHLH transcription factor TWIST1 to regulate coronal suture development, and pathogenic variants in either gene disrupt this interaction, contributing to craniosynostosis [8, 15]. Additionally, co-occurring loss-of-function variants in SMAD6 and TCF12 have been reported, suggesting epistatic effects between TCF12 and bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling pathways that may further influence suture growth and premature fusion [24]. Loss of normal TCF12 function disrupts this regulatory balance, promoting excessive osteogenic differentiation and premature formation of bony bridges at the sutural margins [4].

The c.825G > C variant (nominally p.Leu275Phe) has been experimentally confirmed to disrupt pre-mRNA splicing at the exon 10-intron 10 boundary, leading to intron 10 retention, and therefore represents a pathogenic splice-disrupting variant rather than a missense variant [8]. No structural interpretation is applicable to this variant, as its pathogenic mechanism occurs at the transcript level.

Similar pathogenic missense variants affecting conserved residues within the bHLH domain have been reported previously, supporting the functional importance of the DNA-binding domain in TCF12-related craniosynostosis [10]. Notably, the p.Arg613His (c.1838G > A) variant, separated by 10 amino acids from p.Arg603Trp within the same highly conserved basic region, has been reported in individuals with coronal craniosynostosis [9]. Given that the basic region of bHLH transcription factors mediates E-box DNA binding and contributes to DNA phosphate-backbone interactions, groove positioning and base recognition, substitution of Arg603 by a bulky hydrophobic tryptophan is predicted to impair DNA binding and subsequent transcriptional regulation. Therefore, the recurrent occurrence of variants affecting conserved residues within this region supports the functional importance of the bHLH DNA-binding domain and is consistent with our hypothesis that the nearby p.Arg603Trp substitution may impair TCF12-DNA binding and thereby contribute to its pathogenic effect. Nevertheless, as our structural interpretation is based solely on predictive modeling, the exact functional impact of p.Arg603Trp requires further experimental validation.

Clinically, TCF12-related craniosynostosis (OMIM 615314) is an autosomal dominant condition characterized by marked incomplete penetrance and considerable phenotypic variability. To place our findings in context, we reviewed previously reported individuals with TCF12 variants in the published literature, focusing on studies that reported both genetic and phenotypic data. Previous reports have shown that probands with TCF12-associated craniosynostosis demonstrate a relatively consistent phenotypic pattern with respect to suture involvement. Coronal synostosis represents the predominant presentation, occurring in unilateral or bilateral forms, whereas multisuture or more complex craniosynostosis phenotypes are observed only in a minority of individuals [10]. Moreover, no consistent differences in suture involvement patterns are evident across variant types, suggesting that strong genotype–phenotype correlations may be limited [8, 25].

Consistent with previous reports, coronal suture involvement represented the predominant clinical manifestation in our cohort, with bilateral coronal synostosis being more frequent than unilateral involvement. Additionally, three individuals in our cohort presented with cranial flattening, but without definitive radiographic evidence of premature cranial suture fusion. Given the phenotypic associations reported for TCF12 variants, long-term clinical follow-up is warranted for these individuals who currently lack definitive craniosynostosis, with continued surveillance to monitor for potential delayed or progressive cranial suture involvement.

Beyond cranial manifestations, extracranial features have been reported in a proportion of individuals harboring pathogenic TCF12 variants, most prominently neurodevelopmental involvement such as learning difficulties and behavioral abnormalities [4, 8, 26]. In addition, isolated cases of hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism have been described in patients with TCF12 loss-of-function variants, highlighting the pleiotropic effects of TCF12 in human development [27]. In our cohort, three patients exhibited developmental delay. Mild developmental delay was observed in one patient, while two patients were diagnosed with global developmental delay. Although these features are variably documented across studies, their recurrent observation underscores the importance of clinical awareness and supports the integration of systematic neurodevelopmental assessment and longitudinal follow-up into the clinical management of individuals with TCF12-related craniosynostosis.

Our study further illustrates incomplete penetrance in TCF12-related craniosynostosis through the identification of a mildly affected or unaffected parent in families with TCF12 variants. Similar observations have been repeatedly reported in the literature and underscore the dose-sensitive nature of TCF12 function, as well as the likely contribution of modifier genes and developmental context to phenotypic expression. Consequently, the maintenance of cranial suture patency likely depends on a delicate equilibrium between transcriptional dosage, cross-talk with osteogenic signaling pathways, and the specific temporal requirements of skull morphogenesis [28].

Despite the relatively uniform molecular mechanism, the clinical presentation of TCF12-related craniosynostosis remains highly variable. In our cohort, phenotypes ranged from bilateral coronal synostosis to subtle cranial flattening and hypertelorism without definitive radiographic evidence of suture fusion at initial evaluation. This variability highlights an important clinical feature of TCF12 mutations: the presence of mild or evolving phenotypes, particularly in early infancy. Such cases may not meet conventional radiological criteria for craniosynostosis at presentation but may progress over time, emphasizing the need for careful longitudinal follow-up.

From a clinical standpoint, our findings have several important implications. First, coronal craniosynostosis—particularly bilateral involvement—should prompt consideration of TCF12 genetic testing, even in the absence of syndromic features. Second, subtle cranial deformities or isolated cranial asymmetry in infancy should not preclude genetic evaluation, especially when there is a family history suggestive of autosomal dominant inheritance. Third, identification of TCF12 variants in apparently unaffected or mildly affected parents highlights the importance of thorough family assessment and genetic counseling, as recurrence risks may be underestimated based solely on clinical appearance.

Collectively, our study contributes valuable data from an Asian pediatric population, addressing a notable gap in the existing literature. The overall molecular and clinical patterns observed in our cohort are consistent with reports from Western cohorts. Continued accumulation of data across diverse populations will be essential for refining genotype–phenotype correlations and improving risk stratification. Larger multicenter studies and functional analyses of identified missense variants may further elucidate the modifiers influencing penetrance and expressivity in TCF12-related craniosynostosis.
