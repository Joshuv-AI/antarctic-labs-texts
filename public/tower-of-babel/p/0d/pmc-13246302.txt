Noninvasive estimation of right atrial pressure using cardiac magnetic resonance in severe tricuspid regurgitation
Authors: Jihoon Kim, Jihee Son, Onyou Kim, Ki Hong Choi, Jeong Hoon Yang, Eun Kyoung Kim, Sung-A Chang, Sang-Chol Lee, Seung Woo Park, Sung Mok Kim, Sung-Ji Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile right atrial pressure (RAP) reflects disease severity and carries prognostic value in patients with severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR), its noninvasive estimation remains limited in accuracy. The aim of this study was to evaluate the ability of cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) to estimate RAP by identifying relevant imaging parameters in patients with severe TR.MethodsBetween September 2021 and January 2026, 47 consecutive patients who underwent cardiac catheterization and CMR for severe TR were enrolled from a tertiary center. In addition to conventional parameters, right ventricular (RV) and right atrial (RA) strain were measured from apical four-chamber images of CMR. Pressure profiles including the mean RAP were obtained via cardiac catheterization.ResultsThe median age was 69 years, and secondary TR was the predominant etiology (83.0%). The mean RAP was 10.0 mmHg, and the median interval between catheterization and CMR was 1 day. Among volumetric parameters, indexed RV end-diastolic volume (RVEDVi; r = 0.58, p<0.001), indexed RV end-systolic volume (r = 0.55, p<0.001), and indexed RA end-systolic area (r = 0.33, p = 0.024) were significantly associated with invasively measured RAP. Among functional parameters, RV ejection fraction and RV longitudinal strain were not significantly associated with RAP. In contrast, RA longitudinal strain (r = −0.59, p<0.001) and RA radial strain (r = −0.48, p<0.001) showed significant associations. In a multivariable linear regression model, RVEDVi and RA longitudinal strain remained significantly associated with RAP. The area under the curve for predicting mean RAP >10 mmHg was 0.78 for RVEDVi and 0.82 for RA longitudinal strain.ConclusionRVEDVi and RA longitudinal strain were significantly associated with invasively measured RAP in patients with severe TR. These findings suggest that CMR has potential as a noninvasive tool for estimating RAP in this population.

Introduction

Tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is a common valvular heart disease with significant morbidity and mortality, particularly in its severe form [1]. TR leads to chronic volume overload of the right atrium (RA) and right ventricle (RV), resulting in progressive right heart failure. Right atrial pressure (RAP) is a crucial hemodynamic parameter that reflects RV function and preload status while also indicating irreversible right heart remodeling [2]. Furthermore, elevated RAP has been associated with poor prognosis in patients with TR and serves as a key determinant in clinical decision-making [3], [4]. Therefore, accurately estimating RAP is essential for patient management in severe TR.

Traditionally, cardiac catheterization has been considered the gold standard for RAP measurement [5], but its use is limited due to its invasiveness and risk of vascular complications. Consequently, noninvasive methods using Doppler echocardiography have been developed, with RAP estimation based on the inferior vena cava (IVC) diameter and collapse index, which is now widely adopted [6], [7]. However, concerns remain regarding the accuracy of the echocardiographic approach in patients with severe TR [8], [9]. There is a paucity of validation for echocardiographic estimation of RAP, despite the unreliable application of the simplified Bernoulli equation due to rapid pressure equalization between RV and RA. Additionally, the IVC-based approach is limited in patients with severe TR, as the large regurgitant volume affects measurements. Therefore, alternative noninvasive imaging techniques are needed to improve RAP estimation.

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging provides high-resolution assessment of right heart structure and function, offering valuable insights into valvular anatomy and functional severity of TR. Modern imaging methods, particularly CMR, are increasingly recognized as the gold standard for quantifying RV volumes and function in TR, serving as a comprehensive, noninvasive tool for clinical evaluation [10], [11], [12], [13]. However, the correlation between CMR-derived parameters and hemodynamic measurements—especially RAP—remains unclear. Therefore, we hypothesized that CMR could reliably estimate RAP, providing clinicians with an integrated imaging modality capable of simultaneously assessing both anatomical remodeling and functional impairment in patients with severe TR.

Methods

Study population

Patients who underwent both cardiac catheterization and CMR for evaluation of severe TR were enrolled between September 2021 and January 2026 at Samsung Medical Center, Republic of Korea. TR was diagnosed through a comprehensive review of echocardiography, utilizing structural, qualitative Doppler, and semiquantitative assessment based on the recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography [14]. Among 60 patients enrolled, we excluded those without adequate CMR image quality for reliable volumetric analysis (n = 1), those with other cardiovascular conditions that could substantially influence CMR-derived volumetric parameters, such as intra- or extracardiac shunts (n = 1), and those with a time interval >7 days between catheterization and CMR (n = 11), to minimize temporal variability in hemodynamic status. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center, and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study (IRB No. 2020–03-013). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Transthoracic echocardiography

Standard two-dimensional echocardiographic measurements were conducted in accordance with the recommended guidelines [15]. The RA diameter was measured at end-systole in the apical four-chamber view, and the RV diameter was measured at end-diastole in the RV-focused apical four-chamber view. Multiple factors were considered in assessing TR parameters, including the morphology of the tricuspid valve apparatus, the size and systolic function of the right heart, the Doppler profile of TR, Vena contracta width, effective regurgitant orifice area, and hepatic vein systolic flow reversal [16]. TR maximal velocity was assessed by reviewing the continuous-wave Doppler signal throughout systole across multiple consecutive beats and imaging windows, and the highest peak velocity was selected for analysis. RV systolic pressure was calculated using the simplified Bernoulli formula, and IVC size was measured in the subcostal view at a point 1 to 2 cm from the IVC-RA junction. During imaging, patients were asked to rapidly inhale or perform a “sniff”, and a decrease in IVC diameter of less than 50% was considered indicative of plethora. IVC diameters were measured carefully to avoid transient dilatation caused by TR flow. Estimated RAP was calculated based on an IVC size of 21 mm or larger and the presence of plethora, yielding an estimated mean RAP of 3, 8, or 15 mmHg [15]. In patients with atrial fibrillation, echocardiographic parameters were averaged over three consecutive beats when the heart rate ranged between 60 and 100 per minute.

CMR imaging protocol

All MR images were obtained using a 1.5-T MR system (Magnetom Avanto; Syngo MR B17 version; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a maximum strength gradient of 45 mT/m, a slew rate of 200 mT/m/msec, and a 32-channel body array coil. After acquiring localizing images (axial, coronal, and sagittal), cine images of the left ventricle were obtained using a steady-state free-precession sequence in the short-axis, four-chamber, three-chamber, and two-chamber views. Each cine image set comprised 30 phases per cardiac cycle. CMR cine images were acquired using prospective electrocardiogram gating with temporal parallel acquisition. In patients with heart rate variability with atrial fibrillation, the acquisition window was aligned to the shorter R–R interval to reduce mixing of markedly different cycle lengths. Cine acquisition was initiated from the R-peak to ensure consistent inclusion of end-diastole, and adequate temporal sampling across the cardiac cycle was maintained. For volumetric analysis, end-diastole and end-systole were identified visually as the frames with the largest and smallest ventricular volumes.

End-diastolic volume, end-systolic volume, and ejection fraction of both ventricles were calculated from short-axis cine images using ARGUS software (Siemens Healthineers). Manual editing of automatically segmented endocardial and epicardial borders was performed for all datasets. Papillary muscles and endocardial trabeculation were included in the calculation of the blood pool. RA area was measured at end-systolic (RAESA) and end-diastolic (RAEDA) in the apical four-chamber view, and fractional area change was calculated as (RAESA - RAEDA) / RAESA. The RA area was measured using a picture archiving and communication system. All volumetric and area values were indexed to body surface area.

Strain measurements were obtained using Circle Cardiovascular Imaging software (CVI42 version 5.3.2, Calgary, Alberta, Canada) by a single observer (J.S.). Endocardial borders were automatically tracked and then refined with minor manual adjustments in all patients to optimize contour alignment and ensure consistent smoothing. Epicardial borders were manually traced. After baseline contour delineation, wall motion was automatically tracked across the cardiac cycle, with additional manual adjustments applied as needed. Acceptable tracking was defined as stable contour propagation without major endocardial dropout or contour slippage. The feature-tracking technique for atrial strain assessment has been previously described [17], [18], [19], [20]. Supplementary Figure 1 presents a representative example of strain measurement. No patients were excluded due to inadequate image quality or tracking failure for strain measurement. The intra-observer intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.94 for RA longitudinal strain and 0.94 for RV longitudinal strain. The inter-observer ICC was 0.92 for RA longitudinal strain and 0.95 for RV longitudinal strain.

Right heart catheterization

Cardiac catheterization was performed using a Swan-Ganz catheter at rest, without sedation, by experienced cardiologists [J.H.Y. and K.H.C.]. To ensure accurate pressure measurements, the transducer was positioned at the mid-chest level, corresponding to the left atrium’s position in a supine patient, aligned with the anterior part of the sternum and the mid-axillary line. Proper zeroing was performed before the procedure’s preparation and initiation. Heart rates were recorded at rest before pressure measurements. Pressure profiles, including RV systolic pressure, RV end-diastolic pressure, systolic, diastolic, and mean pulmonary artery pressure, pulmonary artery wedge pressure, and RAP were measured and recorded at end-expiration. RAP was recorded as both mean pressure and the V-wave, which indicates late systolic elevation due to the TR jet (Supplementary Figure 1). Cardiac output was determined using the indirect Fick method and indexed to body surface area. Pulmonary vascular resistance was calculated as the difference between mean PAP and pulmonary artery wedge pressure, divided by cardiac output.

Statistical analysis

There were no missing values for the key CMR and catheterization measurements, except for one patient with missing pulmonary capillary wedge pressure and pulmonary vascular resistance, which was excluded from the corresponding analysis. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are presented as medians (interquartile range). Between-group differences were compared with a chi-square test for categorical variables and either Student’s t-test or Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, depending on the normality of their distribution. A univariable linear regression model was used to assess correlations between RAP and CMR parameters. A multivariable linear regression model was used to identify independent predictors of the mean or V-wave of RAP, including parameters with a p value less than 0.1 in the univariable linear regression model, along with age and sex. Indexed left ventricular end-diastolic volume was excluded from the adjusted covariates, given its limited mechanistic relevance to RAP. Strain-rate parameters were not included. RV end-systolic volume index (RVESVi) and RA radial strain were not included in the multivariable analysis due to collinearity with RV end-diastolic volume index (RVEDVi) and RA longitudinal strain, respectively. We assessed potential non-linearity using natural cubic spline models; these did not improve model fit for RVEDVi (p = 0.475) or RA longitudinal strain (p = 0.150).

RVEDVi and RA longitudinal strain were stratified by their median values, and mean RAPs were compared across strata using Student’s t-test. Elevated RAP was defined as a mean RAP greater than 10 mmHg, consistent with the lower bound of the high RAP range (10–20 mmHg) described in the American Society of Echocardiography recommendations [15]. Variables were compared between patients with and without elevated RAP. The receiver operating characteristic curve and the area under the curve were used to evaluate the accuracy of parameters in estimating elevated RAP. Sensitivity analyses were performed using an alternative threshold of mean RAP ≥15 mmHg. Additionally, the net reclassification improvement and the integrated discrimination improvement were analyzed to compare the accuracy of CMR and echocardiographic estimation of elevated RAP (>10 mmHg) as invasively measured. For RVEDVi and RA longitudinal strain, the optimal cutoff values were determined based on the Youden index. All tests were two-sided, and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using R 3.6.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Correlation of CMR parameters with RAP

Among CMR-derived right heart volumetric parameters, RVEDVi, RVESVi, and RAESAi were significantly associated with mean RAP (Fig. 1 and Table 2). They were also significantly associated with the V-wave of RAP. Among functional parameters, RA longitudinal strain and radial strain showed a significant association with both mean RAP and V-wave of RAP (Fig. 2). However, RV functional parameters—including ejection fraction and RV longitudinal strain—did not demonstrate significant correlations with mean RAP or the V-wave of RAP. When patients were stratified based on the median values of RVEDVi (<112.4 or ≥112.4 mL/m2) and RA longitudinal strain (<6.6 or ≥6.6%), a significant difference in mean RAP was observed. Patients with larger RVEDVi and lower RA longitudinal strain had a mean RAP of 14.8 ± 3.8, whereas those with smaller RVEDVi and higher RA strain had a mean RAP of 6.7 ± 4.3 (p<0.001 for comparison among the four groups; Supplementary Figure 3).

Prediction of elevated RAP

When patients were divided into those with elevated RAP (mean RAP >10 mmHg) and those without elevated RAP (mean RAP ≤10 mmHg), there was a significant difference in RVEDVi and RA longitudinal strain among CMR parameters between the two groups (Table 3). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.78 (0.65–0.92) for RVEDVi and 0.82 (0.69–0.95) for RA longitudinal strain in predicting patients with elevated RAP (Fig. 3). A cutoff value of 112.4 mL/m2 for RVEDVi demonstrated 78.3% sensitivity and 66.7% specificity, while a cutoff value of 6.6% for RA longitudinal strain showed 78.3% sensitivity and 83.3% specificity in identifying elevated RAP. Discussion

In this exploratory study, we found that RVEDVi and RA longitudinal strain measured by CMR were significantly associated with invasively measured RAP in patients with severe TR. Utilizing these parameters, patients could be stratified effectively into low, intermediate, and high RAP categories. Furthermore, these CMR-derived indices correlated strongly with upstream hemodynamic profiles beyond the RA, including pulmonary pressures and IVC hemodynamics, suggesting their potential as reliable, noninvasive surrogate markers of cardiac remodeling in severe TR.

Importance of RAP and its estimation in TR

Severe TR leads to significant volume overload of the RA, resulting in systemic venous congestion and progressive organ dysfunction. Consequently, RA remodeling is increasingly recognized as an important indicator of TR severity and has prognostic significance for risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making. Previous studies have underscored the prognostic importance of RAP in severe TR, highlighting its close association with mortality and cardiovascular events [21], [22]. Elevated RAP reflects the hemodynamic burden on the RA, and progressive structural remodeling and stiffness of the RA can further elevate right-sided pressures. In addition, right-sided congestion, represented by RAP, has been associated with both procedural success and increased mortality following transcatheter repair of severe TR [3]. Therefore, accurate measurement of RAP is critical for the optimal management of patients with severe TR. Doppler echocardiography is the most widely used method for noninvasive RAP estimation. However, its accuracy has been questioned in patients with severe TR, as the criteria based on size and collapsibility of IVC–key determinants for RAP estimation–are affected by the large regurgitant volume from TR [8], [15]. As a result, there is a growing clinical need for additional noninvasive tools to improve RAP estimation in this population.

CMR utility in patients with severe TR

The complex geometry and variable morphology of the RV pose significant challenges to accurate and reproducible assessment by two-dimensional echocardiography. Therefore, CMR is widely acknowledged as the gold standard for evaluating RV size, volumes, systolic function, and structural remodeling in patients with severe TR [14], [23]. Indeed, previous investigations using CMR have extensively focused on RV size and function as prognostic markers associated with clinical outcomes and responses to surgical intervention in severe TR [13]. Additionally, CMR has emerged as a valuable noninvasive tool for estimating left ventricular filling pressures, suggesting its broader utility in a hemodynamic assessment tool beyond anatomical approaches alone [24]. Nonetheless, data on CMR-derived hemodynamic indices specifically for estimating RAP in severe TR remain limited.

In the current study, we expanded upon existing knowledge by evaluating the correlation between specific CMR-derived parameters and invasively measured RAP. Our findings indicate that RVEDVi and RA longitudinal strain are particularly valuable in this context, offering clinicians robust, noninvasive correlates of RAP. These findings carry important clinical implications, as they not only complement traditional echocardiographic approaches but also offer enhanced diagnostic precision for comprehensive hemodynamic evaluation and more accurate clinical risk stratification in severe TR.

Estimating RAP based on RV and RA remodeling

In this study, both RVEDVi and RVESVi, showed significant associations with invasively measured RAP. Prior research has identified RV dilation as a key marker of the hemodynamic burden on the right heart, which may be reversible following TR surgery [12]. A larger regurgitant volume and subsequent tricuspid valve inflow contribute to progressive RV enlargement; thus, increased RV volume can serve as a surrogate marker for TR severity [14]. Our findings further support this relationship, indicating that RV enlargement reflects the complex interplay between structural remodeling and hemodynamic stress, underscoring its close association with elevated RAP.

RV systolic function is a well-established prognostic factor in patients with severe TR [13], [25]. However, in our study, neither RV ejection fraction nor RV strain was significantly associated with RAP. This finding does not diminish the clinical importance of RV systolic function but suggests that RV systolic indices may reflect overall disease severity and long-term prognosis rather than RA filling pressure per se. Previous studies have mainly evaluated RV systolic function in the context of TR surgery, where postoperative changes—including abrupt increases in preload and afterload—make RV systolic dysfunction clinically relevant. In contrast, in patients who are not undergoing surgery, reduced RV afterload due to regurgitant flow may partially preserve RV systolic performance despite increased preload and elevated filling pressures, thereby attenuating its association with RAP.

RA compliance is one of the primary determinants of RAP. Atrial strain has emerged as a useful tool for evaluating chamber remodeling and functional deterioration, and we previously demonstrated that atrial strain correlates with the extent of surgically confirmed atrial fibrosis [26]. Accordingly, reduced RA strain—indicative of advanced fibrosis and decreased compliance—was associated with elevated RAP in response to TR jet. Notably, although a larger RA area correlated with higher RAP, this association did not reach statistical significance in multivariable analysis. These findings suggest that RA compliance plays a more pivotal role in RAP elevation than enlargement alone. An additional key finding was that RA strain was significantly associated with pulmonary artery capillary wedge pressure, pulmonary artery pressure, and IVC hemodynamics. Previous studies have reported a close relationship between right and left heart filling pressures in heart failure patients [27], as well as in those with severe TR [3]. Therefore, our findings highlight the potential of RA strain as a surrogate marker not only for estimating RAP but also for reflecting broader hemodynamic conditions such as pulmonary hypertension and pulmonary congestion.

Clinical implications and study strengths

CMR has been well validated for anatomic and functional assessment, and it provides prognostic information in patients with severe TR. The present study offers novel hemodynamic insights that extend the classical role of CMR by demonstrating its additional diagnostic value alongside echocardiography. Given longstanding concerns regarding the limitations of noninvasive hemodynamic assessment [8], [9], particularly in evaluating volume status and pressure profiles in this complex patient population, CMR-derived parameters, especially RVEDVi and novel RA strain, may enhance clinical evaluation. These parameters can help differentiate patients with elevated RAP from those without, complementing established prognostic markers such as RV size and TR severity. Moreover, our findings illustrate how right heart remodeling is closely linked to the resultant pressure overload in severe TR, as evidenced by RV enlargement and RA stiffness. This mechanistic understanding further supports the clinical utility of CMR in guiding risk stratification and individualized management strategies in this population.

RA strain analysis can be derived from routine four-chamber cine CMR images without the need for additional scan time. Although dedicated post-processing software is required, the additional analysis typically takes less than 30 min. CMR is not intended to replace invasive pressure measurements but is primarily performed to assess cardiac structure and RV function in patients with severe TR. Within this clinical context, RA strain may provide complementary, noninvasive information for estimating RAP and may help reduce the need for repeated cardiac catheterization and its associated risks and costs.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it was retrospective, and thus potential selection bias cannot be excluded. Additionally, the sample size (n = 47) was relatively small, which limits the generalizability of the findings. However, it is important to emphasize that patients with severe TR are often older and have significant comorbidities, which frequently preclude the use of cardiac catheterization. As such, our cohort size reflects real-world clinical constraints associated with multimodality evaluation in this population. Furthermore, to our knowledge, comparable external datasets integrating invasively measured RAP with CMR-derived parameters in this population are not currently available. Future large-scale, prospective studies are needed to validate these preliminary findings and to establish robust, clinically applicable cutoff values. Nonetheless, our patient population, primarily older individuals with predominantly secondary TR, accurately represents contemporary clinical practice [8], [21]. Second, there might have been hemodynamic changes due to the time interval between CMR and catheterization. However, simultaneous assessment is not feasible, and in our cohort, the median time between the two procedures was 0 days, minimizing this concern. Third, we did not include clinical outcome data, which could have further strengthened our study findings. Nevertheless, RV volume is a well-established prognostic factor in severe TR [28], and RAP is known to be prognostically relevant, serving as a surrogate marker for clinical outcomes. Fourth, in patients with atrial fibrillation, beat-to-beat R–R interval variability during CMR acquisition may have affected the temporal consistency of diastolic frames and introduced additional variability in RV volumetric measurements; however, prospective electrocardiogram gating and volume-based identification of end-diastole were used to mitigate this limitation. Fifth, because our cohort predominantly consisted of secondary TR, in whom RA and annulus remodeling are further influenced by underlying conditions, the generalizability of the observed RAP-CMR relationships to primary TR may be limited. Despite these limitations, our study provides important preliminary evidence supporting the use of CMR-derived parameters in the noninvasive assessment of RAP, which may guide future diagnostic strategies and personalized management in patients with severe TR.

Conclusion

In patients with severe TR, CMR-derived RVEDVi and RA longitudinal strain were significantly associated with invasively measured RAP. These parameters also correlated with pulmonary and IVC hemodynamics, reflecting broader pressure profiles beyond the RA. CMR offers diagnostic benefit for estimating RAP compared to the conventional echocardiographic approach, and may serve as a useful noninvasive tool in the assessment of right heart remodeling in severe TR.
