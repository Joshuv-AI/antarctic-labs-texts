The relationship between emotional eating and depression, anxiety, and stress in Turkish adults with obesity
Authors: Nuriye Efe Ertürk, Fatma Ayhan, Yelda Koçak, Ebru Zeyrek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe number of individuals with obesity is increasing rapidly. Türkiye is the country with the highest obesity rates in Europe. The aim of the present study was to assess the relations between emotional eating, and depression, anxiety and stress in adults with obesity.MethodThe study was carried out as a descriptive and correlational design. The sample of the study consisted of a total of 200 adults with obesity who applied to the dietitian outpatient clinic. The participants filled out the Information Form, the Emotional Eater Questionnaire (EEQ-TR), and the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21). Relations between variables have been inspected.ResultsThe total score of DASS-21 was positively significantly and weakly correlated with the total score of the EEQ-TR (p < 0.01; r = 0.338). The higher the mean score of DASS-21, the higher the level of obesity (F = 4.059 p = 0.019). It was determined that the DASS-21 score explained 11.8% of the variance in the mean EEQ-TR score (ß:0.344; p < 0.001). Income status was found to be related to depression, anxiety, and stress, while no relationship was observed with emotional eating status. Income status was found to be related to depression, anxiety, and stress, while no relationship was observed with emotional eating status. In our study, there was no statistically significant difference between emotional eating and body mass index, although the average EEQ-TR score increased as the BMI class increased. The emotional eating scores were higher in younger participants, and the difference between groups was significant (F = 13.354 p < 0.001).ConclusionsWe determined that emotional eating is positively and weakly correlated to depression, anxiety, and stress, and the mean DASS-21 score in adults with obesity in Türkiye. As the level of obesity increases in individuals, the average DASS-21 score also rises.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04214-0.Keywords: Depression, Emotional Eating, Obesity, Stress

Background

Obesity, which is defined as abnormal and excessive fat accumulation, is a complex and multifactorial disease that adversely affects health. Today, obesity ranks second among the preventable causes of death after smoking. Obesity is associate with many diseases such as Type II diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, hyperlipidemia, strokes, cancers, and psychological disorders [1].

According to the 2016 data from the World Health Organization (WHO), 39% of adults worldwide are overweight and 13% are obese [1] In Türkiye, the prevalence of obesity in society, especially in women, is gradually increasing. As of 2025, Turkey, with an adult obesity prevalence of 36%, ranks among the countries with the highest obesity rates in the European region [2, 3].

Emotional eating is a disordered eating pattern that has been shown among the causes of obesity in recent years [4–6]. It is generally accepted that negative emotions suppress the desire to eat in individuals [7–9]. However, in individuals with emotional eating problems, this situation may work differently. It is reported that negative emotions such as anger, fear, sadness, and distress, which form the basis of emotional eating, increase the consumption of sugary and high-energy foods, leading to obesity in individuals [10–12]. Furthermore, Alongside emotional eating, psychological factors such as depression, anxiety, and stress have been associated with an increased risk of obesity [13–15]. When these problems, which are significant risk factors for obesity on their own, come together, they form a complex network, further deteriorating the situation.

Studies have shown that the disordered eating patterns are increasing in people with obesity [5] and therefore obesity should be addressed not only physically but also psychologically. As a result of this phenomenon, in recent years, the psychological aspect of obesity has been discussed and attention has been drawn to this aspect of obesity in the guidelines [2, 16]. Despite the presence of studies worldwide aimed at identifying psychological factors in the development of obesity, there are a few studies conducted in Türkiye on this subject [17–20]. In these a few studies, the effect and psychological relationship of quarantines during the COVID-19 pandemic on BMI and emotional eating were investigated [17, 19]. Additionally, Türkiye is a gastronomy country. Türkiye has 3 cities with the title of gastronomy city, which is a title given to cities by UNESCO within the scope of the “Creative Cities Network” project. Gaziantep, Hatay and Afyonkarahisar [21]. Kahramanmaraş, the city where the study was conducted is a province that borders Hatay and Gaziantep provinces. It is thought that a rich culinary culture, the meaning attributed to eating, and the factors influencing dietary habits may have an impact on the way nutrition is shaped. For all these reasons, this study aimed to determine emotional eating, depression, anxiety, and stress in Türkiye, whose number of individuals with obesity is increasing day by day and the relationships between these conditions. In this way, we aim to draw attention to the management of emotional health in obesity management. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first attempt to investigate between emotional eating and depression, stress, anxiety in Türkiye. We anticipate that data obtained from this study will contribute to the planning of interventions that can be applied to patients during obesity treatment in Türkiye.

Methods

Participants and procedure

The aim of the study is to assess the relations between emotional eating, depression, anxiety and stress in a sample of adults with obesity in Türkiye. This descriptive and correlational study was conducted in a dietitian clinic located in a Faculty of Medicine hospital in a city, in the south of Türkiye. The study data were collected between July 2022 and February 2023. The sample of the study consisted of a total of 200 adults with obesity who applied to the dietitian outpatient clinic. When the participants applied to the dietitian outpatient clinic, they filled out the Sociodemographic Information Form, the Turkish Version of the Emotional Eater Questionnaire (EEQ-TR), and the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21). The participants were informed in writing and verbally by the researchers about the purpose of the study. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee (protocol number: 2022/05–07). The researchers obtained institutional permission from the Medical Faculty Hospital where the study was conducted (protocol number: 20.06.2022–133152). The data were collected by the researchers using a face-to-face interview method.

To determine the sample size, G*Power (version 3.1.9.2., Düsseldorf, Germany) software was used. According to the study by Mansoury [22], the effect size of the EBPQ/BMI variable is 0.2335811. In addition to the effect size, when power 95%, alpha 0.05 was selected in the G*Power program, the minimum sample size was determined as 200. The adequacy of the sample was calculated using a post hoc power analysis in the G*Power program. In this calculation, with a 95% confidence interval, a correlation of 0.58, an alpha level of 0.05, and a sample size of 200, the power of the study was determined to be 100%. The study was completed with 200 participants.

Measures

Sociodemographic information form

The Sociodemographic Information Form consists of a total of 11 questions that were prepared by the researchers within the scope of the literature review and questioned the socio-demographic characteristics of individuals such as age, gender, marital status, economic status, educational status, presence of chronic disease, weight, and height.

Emotional Eater Questionnaire (EEQ)

The Emotional Eater Questionnaire was a self-reported scale that measures emotional eating and was developed by Garaulet et al. [23]. The four-point Likert scale consists of 10 questions and three subscales (disinhibition, type of food, and guilt). The scale items are marked as never (0 points), sometimes (1 point), often (2 points), and always (3 points). The lowest possible score on the scale is “0,” and the highest score is “30.” High scores indicate that the person has a high tendency to emotional eating. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Arslantaş et al. [24] and Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient was stated as 0.84 for the overall scale. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient of the scale was calculated as 0.80.

Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21)

The DASS-21 was created by Lovibond and Lovibond [25] by shortening the DASS-42. Psychometric properties of the Turkish version of the DASS-21 in the normal and clinical samples were made by Yıldırım et al. [26]. In the normal sample, test-retest correlation coefficients were found to be r = 0.68 for the depression subscale, r = 0.66 for the anxiety subscale, and r = 0.61 for the stress subscale. The scale is a 4-point Likert scale consisting of a total of 21 questions (7 questions measuring the depression dimension, 7 questions measuring the stress dimension, and 7 questions measuring the anxiety dimension). If an individual scores 5 or more in the depression dimension, 4 or more in the anxiety dimension, and 8 or more in the stress dimension, it indicates the presence of the respective problem. All the subscales and the total score of DASS-21 showed good to excellent psychometric properties: Depression: a = 0.88; Anxiety: a = 0.72; Stress: a = 0.84; Total score: a = 0.90.

Statistical analysis

The analysis of the study data was performed using SPSS 23 (Statistic Package For Social Science) software. In the study, descriptive statistical analyses such as number, percentage distribution, mean, and standard deviation were used. The normality of the distribution of the scale scores was assessed using skewness and kurtosis values, with values between − 1.5 and + 1.5 being regarded as normal [27]. Univariate analyses were applied (Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for parametric tests. The relationship between the variables was examined by correlation analysis. Linear regression analyses were conducted to explore associations between DASS-21 scores, EEQ-TR scores, and BMI within the context of a cross-sectional study; therefore, these analyses were exploratory and did not imply causality. The statistical significance value was set to p < 0.05.

Mean scores and standard deviations of the EEQ-TR and DASS-21 scales across participant characteristics are presented in Table 1. No significant differences were observed in the overall EEQ-TR or its subscale scores by gender. In contrast, females had higher mean scores on the overall DASS-21 and its anxiety, depression, and stress subscales compared to males. Age-related differences were observed in emotional eating measures. Participants aged 18–30 and 31–45 years had higher mean disinhibition subscale scores than those aged 46 years and older. The type of food subscale mean score was higher among participants aged 18–30 years compared to older age groups. Overall EEQ-TR mean scores were also higher in participants younger than 46 years compared to those aged 46 years and above. Educational status was associated with emotional eating scores. Participants with a university education or higher had higher overall EEQ-TR, disinhibition, and type of food subscale mean scores compared to those with a primary school education or lower. Differences were also observed across BMI categories. Participants classified as BMI class I had lower overall DASS-21 and anxiety subscale mean scores compared to those in BMI class III. The mean depression score was lower in BMI class I compared to BMI class II. Additionally, the EEQ-TR guilt subscale mean score was lower in BMI class I than in BMI class III. Income level was not associated with differences in overall EEQ-TR or subscale mean scores. However, participants whose income was lower than their expenditures had higher overall DASS-21 and anxiety, depression, and stress subscale mean scores compared to those whose income was equal to expenditures. Participants with chronic diseases had higher anxiety subscale mean scores compared to those without chronic diseases, whereas the EEQ-TR disinhibition subscale mean score was lower among participants with chronic diseases (Table 1).

The mean overall EEQ-TR score was 8.97 ± 5.96. Subscale mean scores were 5.00 ± 4.16 for disinhibition, 1.99 ± 1.61 for type of food, and 1.98 ± 1.67 for guilt. The mean overall DASS-21 score was 16.96 ± 10.99, with subscale means of 5.25 ± 3.51 for anxiety, 5.05 ± 4.87 for depression, and 6.74 ± 4.75 for stress (Table 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to explain the relationship between emotional eating and depression, anxiety, and stress in adults with obesity in Türkiye. As a result of this study, we identified significant relationships between many variables. It is important to note that the cross-sectional design of this study limits interpretation of the findings to correlational relationships. Temporal ordering between emotional eating, psychological distress, and obesity cannot be established, and causal inferences cannot be drawn. The observed associations should therefore be interpreted cautiously and viewed as indicative of potential relationships that warrant further investigation using longitudinal or experimental designs. It was determined that there was a significant, weak, and positive correlation between the overall EEQ-TR mean score and the overall DASS-21 mean score. In addition, it was found that as BMI increased, there was an increase in the overall EEQ-TR mean score and the overall DASS-21 mean score, and this increase was found to be statistically significant in the overall DASS-21 mean score. It was determined that the DAS-21 overall and all subscale mean scores were significantly higher in females compared to males. The income status was found to be effective on depression, anxiety, and stress but did not affect emotional eating status. In addition, emotional eating tendency was found to be higher in young adults, those with university degrees, and those without chronic diseases. These findings were in line with previous studies examining the relationship between emotional eating, depression, anxiety, and stress [6, 10, 14, 15, 28].

Emotional eating, one of the factors attributed to the dramatic increase in obesity in recent years, is a pattern of eating behavior that is generally associated with overeating and unhealthy eating patterns that can be influenced by age [4, 29, 30]. In the study conducted by Dakanalis et al. [4] to examine the effect of emotional eating on BMI, it was reported that there was a significant difference between emotional eating and age and that young people tended to emotional eating more than the elderly. In our study, it was determined that there was a statistically significant difference between the age variable and the mean EEQ-TR scores of the participants, and the highest emotional eating was in the 18–30 age range, where the mean scores of emotional eating decreased as the age progressed. Similar studies on emotional eating in young adults also show that emotional eating is common in young people [18, 28, 30–33]. A study conducted on Polish adolescents found that, especially in females, overweight and obese participants had a higher contribution of emotional eating to weight gain compared to normal-weight individuals [33]. In similar studies conducted on university students, it is reported that emotional eating is common [18, 32]. It may be effect that the hormonal increase that starts with adolescence and the accompanying mood changes on emotional eating. These studies conducted in young populations suggest that the increase in the incidence of obesity will continue and that obesity-related chronic diseases will occur at an earlier age in the future. From a future research perspective, further studies may examine whether integrating psychiatric consultations into obesity management-particularly for adolescents and young adults-is associated with improved weight-related outcomes. Another variable that is investigated for its impact on emotional eating is gender. Some studies show that females have more emotional eating tendencies than males [4, 34, 35]. However, in our study, no difference was determined in terms of gender in emotional eating. It is considered that the low proportion of men in our sample (16%) was effective in the lack of difference in emotional eating in terms of gender in our study.

Depression, anxiety, stress, and adverse conditions have been frequently reported to be associated with emotional eating [34, 36, 37]. It is reported that depressive symptoms are associated with emotional eating, and emotional eating has been linked to higher BMI levels [12, 14, 32]. In our study, it was determined that there was a weak positive relationship between the overall EEQ mean score and the overall DASS-21 mean score. In addition, in the regression analysis between BMI, EEQ, and DASS-21, it was found that DASS-21 and EEQ-TR scores had a statistically significant relationship with BMI, but the overall EEQ-TR score did not make a significant contribution to BMI. Additionally, the analysis revealed a strong association between BMI and DASS-21 scores, with DASS-21 demonstrating a stronger association with BMI compared to emotional eating measures. Lazarevich et al. [32] found significant relationships between depressive symptoms and emotional eating and between emotional eating and BMI. It has also been reported that emotional eating mediates between depression and BMI, and depression affects BMI through emotional eating. Some studies have suggested potential interrelationships among depression, emotional eating, and BMI, indicating that these variables may be interrelated rather than independent [14, 28]. The findings obtained from our study are consistent with the literature in determining the relationship between depressive symptoms and BMI. However, in our study, no relationship was found between EEQ-TR and BMI. Türkiye is the country with the highest number of people with obesity in Europe. As Turkish researchers, we expected this relationship to be at a higher level considering the obesity rates in Türkiye. These results indicate that our country is richness in terms of gastronomy but poverty in terms of obesity management. The city where the study was conducted is located in the gastronomy region of Türkiye. This region is accepted as one of the gastronomy region around the world and receives awards about eating culture from international authorities. These findings suggest that culturally specific eating practices, such as cooking traditions and enjoyment of food independent of emotional states, may also be relevant factors to consider when examining obesity patterns in Türkiye. Therefore, different studies such as hedonic eating cultural eating, and other eating behaviors may be planned to reveal the reasons for the dramatic increase in the number of individuals with obesity in Türkiye.

Psychological disorders such as depression, stress, and anxiety are more common in females in Türkiye as well as in the world [38–40]. In general, the frequency of depression in females is estimated to be twice as high as in males. Our study found that the DASS-21 overall and subscale mean scores were higher in females compared to males and the difference was statistically significant. In our study, another variable that was found to have a significant relationship with emotional eating is educational status. It was found that as the education level of the participants increased, the mean EEQ-TR scores increased and the difference between the groups was statistically significant. It was determined that there was no significant difference in EEQ-TR mean scores of the participants according to their income status and low income status did not prevent emotional eating. On the other hand, the income status of participants significantly affected their DASS-21 overall and subscale mean scores, and it was observed that those with lower income had higher DASS-21 mean scores. This result supports the idea that economic difficulties should be considered as a significant risk factor for psychological disorders.

In conclusion, our study determined that emotional eating is positively and weakly correlated to depression, anxiety, and stress, and the mean DASS-21 score increased as the obesity level of the individuals increased. These results indicate that conditions such as emotional eating, depression, stress, and anxiety should be questioned, especially in individuals who cannot lose weight despite treatment. Such inquiries may provide valuable insights for improving patient outcomes. Since emotional eating and its triggering factors are of psychological origin, individuals need to be evaluated not just physiologically by health professionals, but also holistically. Studies showing the effect of behavioral therapies on weight loss are also an indication of this situation [37, 41]. In addition, special attention should be drawn to the importance of adequate and balanced nutrition in the fight against obesity in societies with a passion for cooking and eating such as Türkiye.

To promote healthy eating behaviors, the authors recommend developing educational content-such as mindful eating-tailored to regional dietary characteristics, investigating the effects of hedonic nutrition, and conducting longitudinal or interventional studies with larger samples in Türkiye, a country increasingly progressing toward an obese society. Obesity, though highly prevalent, remains a preventable condition with effective lifestyle and community-based strategies.

The limitations of the study

The cross-sectional nature of the study limits causal inferences. This study was conducted in a region-specific clinical population that was predominantly female. Given the characteristics of the study sample, the generalizability of the findings to broader populations, including community samples, male participants, or other cultural contexts, is limited. In addition, the clinical setting from which the sample was drawn may have influenced the observed patterns and variability of the study variables, as individuals seeking clinical care may differ systematically from community-based populations in terms of psychological distress and eating-related behaviors. The marked gender imbalance, with a predominance of female participants, may have further affected the variability of emotional eating and psychological distress measures and may have influenced the magnitude of observed associations. Finally, the regional specificity of the sample, drawn from a culturally distinct area with strong culinary traditions, may have shaped eating behaviors and psychosocial characteristics, potentially impacting both the variability and effect sizes of the observed relationships. These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings and underscore the need for future studies with more diverse, balanced, and representative samples.
