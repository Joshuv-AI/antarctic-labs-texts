Effect of fibrinogen like protein 1 on cisplatin-resistance in laryngeal carcinoma through targeting integrinβ1 in vivo and in vitro
Authors: Zhen Li, Jianlin Fang, Hongzhou Ge, Xiaoyong Wang, Hui Dong, Hang Yu, Yansha Liu, Tao Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the relationship between fibrinogen like protein 1 (FGL1) and cisplatin (CDDP) resistance in laryngeal carcinoma, as well as dissect the underlying molecular regulatory pathways.MethodsThe functional roles of FGL1 in cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis were confirmed using the CCK-8 proliferation assay, wound healing assay, transwell invasion assay, and flow cytometry-based apoptosis quantification. In order to further elucidate the impact of FGL1 downregulation on tumor growth, a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model was established. TUNEL staining for apoptotic cells, immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis and western blot analysis were performed to detect the number of apoptotic cells and expression profiles of key regulatory proteins.ResultsFGL1 knockdown significantly suppressed the proliferative capacity, migratory potential, and invasive ability of CDDP-resistant laryngeal carcinoma cells while inducing marked apoptosis. Mechanistic investigations revealed that inhibition of FGL1 could suppress cellular resistance to CDDP and downregulate drug-resistant proteins by blocking its direct interaction with Integrinβ1, specifically leading to downregulation of CDDP-resistant effector proteins, namely multidrug resistance-associated protein 1 (MRP1) and B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2). In the xenograft model, FGL1 depletion attenuated tumorigenic potential of TU212-R cells, enhanced CDDP chemosensitivity, and counteracted Integrinβ1-mediated oncogenic signaling hyperactivation.ConclusionFGL1 plays a pivotal role in CDDP resistance development by activating the Integrinβ1-dependent pro-survival pathway in laryngeal carcinoma. Therapeutic targeting of FGL1 may represent a promising strategy to circumvent chemoresistance and improve clinical outcomes for CDDP-resistant laryngeal cancer patients.Keywords: Laryngeal carcinoma, Cisplatin-resistance, Fibrinogen like protein-1, Integrinβ1

Introduction

Laryngeal cancer is one of the prominent subtypes of head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCCs), and has emerged as the second most lethal respiratory malignancy globally, trailing only lung cancer in terms of incidence and mortality rates [1]. Although therapeutic methods and strategies have been continuously improved in recent years, the high rate of recurrence and poor prognosis remain major obstacles to its treatment [2]. With advances in tumor research, the poor clinical response in laryngeal cancer is partly attributed to its tendency to develop uncontrollable local infiltration, cervical lymph node metastasis, and chemotherapy resistance. Thus, it is crucial to investigate the primary factors driving drug resistance in laryngeal cancer and identify novel therapeutic targets to enhance chemotherapy effectiveness.

Fibrinogen-like protein 1 (FGL1) is a hepatocyte-derived secretory protein that forms homodimers via disulfide bonds, sharing high homology with fibrinogen, and is primarily involved in proliferation and metabolism [6]. In addition to its role in patients with radiation-induced hepatic injury [3], FGL1 has been implicated in tumorigenesis across multiple solid malignancies, including lung cancer, melanoma, colorectal cancer [4] and gastric cancer [5]. Additionally, FGL1 downregulation has been shown to impede cell proliferation and invasion of HNSCC cells [7], suggesting its involvement in modulating tumor cell behavior and drug sensitivity. Notably, FGL1 serves as a potential marker for predicting the treatment response of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients with acquired resistance to gefitinib. Elevated FGL1 levels have been detected in the plasma and tumor tissues of laryngeal cancer patients, where it promotes TU-686 cell proliferation and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-mediated metastasis [19]. Recent studies have reported that FGL1 is closely related to laryngeal cancer invasion and metastasis. However, the precise role of FGL1 in regulating cisplatin (CDDP) resistance and apoptosis in laryngeal cancer cells remains largely unexplored.

Integrinβ1 belongs to the transmembrane heterodimeric integrin family and orchestrates diverse cellular processes by engaging with extracellular matrix ligands [8]. Integrinβ1 activates various downstream signaling pathways by binding to different ligands, mediating cell adhesion [9], facilitating cell-extracellular matrix interactions [10], as well as promoting tumor cell migration [11], invasion and proliferation [12–14]. It also contributes to tumor angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis, thereby enhancing metastatic potential [15]. Studies have reported that integrinβ1 is a novel potential therapeutic target for overcoming chemotherapy resistance [16]. Integrinβ1 is significantly associated with acquired gemcitabine resistance and worse prognosis in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), down-regulation of Integrinβ1 suppresses chemoresistance in PDAC cells by inhibiting cell division control protein 42 (Cdc42) and protein kinase B activity. The attenuation of cancer stem cell-like features and CDDP resistance in laryngeal cancer occurs via modulating Integrinβ1 expression [17]. Downregulation of Integrinβ1 in epithelial cells leads to epithelial atrophy. Specifically, in advanced oral submucous fibrosis (OSF) characterized by severely atrophic epithelium, the expression of Integrinβ1 is significantly reduced, accompanied by the lowest level of stem cell activity [18]. Hence, the present study suggests a possible drug-resistant signalling pathway of integrinβ1 in laryngeal cancer.

Despite the recognized regulatory functions of fibrinogen family members and integrins, the interplay between FGL1 and Integrinβ1 in laryngeal cancer remains elusive. This study hypothesizes that FGL1 may influence CDDP resistance and apoptosis in laryngeal cancer cells by regulating Integrinβ1 signaling. To test this, we aim to characterize the basal expression of FGL1 in the TU212 laryngeal cancer cell line, delineate its relationship with CDDP chemosensitivity, and elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which FGL1 modulates CDDP resistance through Integrinβ1 signaling both in vitro and in vivo.

Methods

Cell culture

The human laryngeal epidermoid carcinoma cell line TU212 and the immortalized human nasopharyngeal epithelial cell line NP69 were purchased from Shanghai Jinyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. TU212 cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and NP69 cells were cultured in defined keratinocyte serum-free medium (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). All the cells were cultured in a humidified thermostatic incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2, and the medium was replaced every two days.

Establishment of the TU212 CDDP-resistant subline

In this study, CDDP resistance was induced in TU212 cells via a concentration gradient increment method. Cells in logarithmic growth phase were first inoculated in complete medium containing 1 μM CDDP, and the survival status of the cells was observed using an inverted microscope daily until the cells grew stably, the concentration of CDDP was gradually increased to 2 μM, 4 μM, 8 μM. After four months, TU212 cells grew stably in the presence of 8 μM CDDP and a drug-resistant cell line was successfully constructed (named TU212-R). The survival rates of TU212 and TU212-R cells under various concentrations of CDDP (1~40 μM) were determined separately.

Cell grouping and treatment

To explore the effects of FGL1 and Integrinβ1 on CDDP resistance in laryngeal cancer, shRNAs targeting FGL1 and Integrinβ1 agonist Pyrintegrin (CAS NO.1228445–38-2, Adooq Bioscience Co., USA) were used in this study. The TU212-R cells were divided into six groups: control, CDDP, sh-FGL1-NC + CDDP, sh-FGL1 + CDDP, Pyrintegrin + CDDP, sh-FGL1 + Pyrintegrin + CDDP.

The shRNAs that targeted FGL1 (sh-FGL1) (Gemma Biotechnology Co., China) and a control shRNA (sh-FGL1-NC) (Gemma Biotechnology Co., China) were cloned and inserted into into the PGMLV-SC5 vector (BioVector NTCC Inc., China), then transfected into TU212-R by Lipofectamine 3000, 48 h after transfection, western blot was used to verify the knockdown efficiency levels of FGL1. Each group of cells was treated with 8 μM CDDP for 24 h, 48 h or 72 h. Pyrintegrin was used at 1 μM, as previously described, and cells were treated for 48 h [20].

Cell viability assay and calculation of drug resistance index

Cell-counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assays were used to detect cell viability according to the manufacturer’s instructions. TU212 and TU212-R cells were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured overnight at 37℃ and 5% CO2. CCK-8 reagent (C0038, Beyotime, China) was added to each microplate. After incubation for 2 h, the optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader (Bio-Tek, USA). CDDP concentration was plotted on the x-axis. The growth inhibition curves of TU212 and TU212-R cells were plotted using GraphPad Prism 9.4 software, and the IC50 values of CDDP on different groups of cells were calculated.

Colony formation assaay

The colony formation assay was performed to evaluate the clonogenic capacity of cells. Briefly, TU212 and TU212-R cells were trypsinized and counted. A total of 500 cells per well were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated at 37℃ with 5% CO2. After 24 h of attachment, cells were treated with 8 μM CDDP as described in section 2.3, with the control group receiving equal volume of medium.The culture medium was replaced every 3 days. Until visible colonies formed, the medium was discarded, and colonies were gently washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, followed by staining with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 20 min. Excess stain was rinsed off with tap water, and the plates were air-dried.Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted using a colony counter. Western blot assay

Total protein was extracted from cells or tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein concentrations were determined via the BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) assay. Equal amounts of proteins (30 μg) were separated with 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes, which were then blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature. Then, the following primary antibodies were added and incubated overnight at 4℃: anti-FGL1 (1:500, 16000-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-Integrinβ1 (1:5000, 12594-1-AP, Proteintech, CHN), anti-MRP1 (1:2000, ab260038, Abcam, UK), anti-P-gp (1:1000, 25081-1-AP, Proteintech, China) and anti-GAPDH (1:5000, 10494-1-AP, Proteintech, China). The following day, the membrane was incubated with the horseradish peroxidase (HRP)- conjugated secondary antibody (1:2000, ab288151, Abcam, UK) at 37 ℃ for 90 min. The membranes were exposed to enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (A38554, Thermo Scientific, USA), and protein bands were imaged using a protein gel image analysis system (GS-900, Bio-Rad, USA). GAPDH was used as the internal control. The intensities of all the protein bands were analyzed by Image J software.

Wound healing assay

Cell migration was assessed by change in the area of scratch. The TU212-R cells were cultured in 6-well plates for 48 h, and scratches were made using a 10 μL pipette tip. Cells were washed with PBS buffer and cultured in serum-free medium. Areas of cell migration were captured at 0 h (A0) and 48 h (A48) by using an optical microscope (10 ×). The gap area can be quantitatively evaluated via Image J software. The cell migration capacity was expressed as the cell migration rate (%):

Transwell assay

TU212-R cells (2 × 104 cells/well) were resuspended in serum-free medium and added to the upper chambers of a Transwell insert (CLS3470, Corning Co., USA) coated with Matrigel (356,234, Corning USA), and complete medium containing with 10% fetal bovine serum was added to the lower chambers. After incubation for 24 h at 37℃, the invasive cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with a 0.1% crystal violet solution. The invaded cells were counted under an inverted microscope.

Flow Cytometry

Cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were cultured in a 6—well plate for 72 h. Next, the cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin without EDTA and resuspended in 500 µL of annexin binding buffer. The cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI (C1062S, Beyotime, CHN) for 15 min in the dark. The apoptotic cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA).

Animals grouping and treatment

In this study, BALB/c nude mice (SPF, 4 weeks old, male) were purchased from the Shandong Experimental Animal Center. The different groups of cells were injected subcutaneously into the left axilla of the mice at a density of 1 × 107 cells. The length (L) and width (W) of the tumor were measured and the weights were measured every 6 days.

The mice were randomly assigned to five groups (6 mice per group): control, CDDP, sh-FGL1 + CDDP, Pyrintegrin + CDDP, and sh-FGL1 + Pyrintegrin + CDDP. They were treated with an equal dose of CDDP (10 mg/kg, i.v.) [21] every three days. The Pyrintegrin + CDDP and sh-FGL1 + Pyrintegrin + CDDP groups were also treated with an equal dose of Pyrintegrin diluted with DMSO (10 mg/kg, i.v) [20] every two days, and the control group received an equal dose of saline. At the end of the experimental period, the mice were subsequently euthanized and the tumors were collected for TUNELstaining, immunohistochemistry assay and western blot analysis. The maximal tumor size/burden permitted by the Ethics Committee is 20 mm in diameter or 2000 mm3 in volume. The maximal tumor size/burden was not exceeded during the experiment.

TUNEL staining

Tumor tissues were extracted and preserved in 4% neutral formaldehyde before being embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections, 3 μm in thickness, were cut, mounted on glass slides after deparaffinization and rehydration. For antigen retrieval, sections were incubated in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95℃ for 20 min and cooled to room temperature. After rinsing with PBS, sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. TUNEL assay was performed utilizing the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Roche, Indianapolis, IN, USA) following the manufacturer's guidelines. The sections were incubated with 50 µL streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase conjugate for 30 min at 37℃, then stained with 200 µL DAB solution. A light microscopy was utilized to observe apoptotic cells (with brown nuclei), and the images were captured at a magnification of 200 × and subsequently analyzed via Image J software.

Immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin sections (3 μm thick) were deparaffinized and rehydrated. For antigen retrieval: Sections stained with anti-Ki67, anti-FGL1, and anti-P-gp were incubated in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min; Sections stained with anti-Integrinβ1 were incubated in Tris/EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) at 95 °C for 20 min (per antibody manufacturer’s instructions). All sections were rinsed with PBS and treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Non-specific binding was blocked by incubating sections with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at room temperature. The primary antibodies used were anti-Ki67 (1:200, 12202, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-FGL1 (1:50, 16000-1-AP, Proteintech, CHN), anti-Integrinβ1 (1:1000, ab179471, Abcam, UK), and anti-P-gp (1:20, 25081-1-AP, Proteintech, CHN), which were applied to the sections overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, the sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L secondary antibody (1:2000) for 1 h at 37 °C. Hematoxylin staining was then performed, succeeded by DAB chromogen development. Images were acquired using a light microscopy (Revolve FL, USA) at a magnification of 100×. The positive expression levels of Ki67, FGL1, Integrinβ1, and P-gp were assessed using Image J software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.4 software. All experimental data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and differences between multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s test. Differences between two groups were analyzed by unpaired student t-test. p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

FGL1 knockdown attenuates CDDP resistance in TU212-R cells

In accordance with established protocols, TU212-R cells were generated via stepwise CDDP induction. CCK-8 assay revealed that TU212-R cells exhibited significantly reduced sensitivity to CDDP compared with parental TU212 cells, with a half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 4.06 µM for the TU212 cells and 28.73 µM for the TU212-R cells (Fig. 1A). Additionally, colony formation assay was performed to assess the long-term proliferation ability. It was observed that TU212-R cells formed more colonies than parental cells when exposed to CDDP (Fig. 1B and C).

The IC50 values for CDDP in TU212 and TU212-R cells are shown. B Colony formation assay was performed to evaluate the colony-forming ability of TU212 and TU212-R cells with or without CDDP treatment. Quantitative analysis of colony numbers is shown in C. D FGL1 expression in TU212 and TU212-R cells was evaluated via western blot assay after transfection with sh-FGL1. The grey value of FGL1 was analyzed by Image J software, and expression levels were normalized to GAPDH levels. E Cell viability was assessed to evaluate the proliferation capacity at 24, 48 and 72 h after CDDP administration, n=3. ns indicates no significant difference, * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01,*** P < 0.001.

To further explore whether FGL1 expression is related to CDDP resistance in TU212-R cells, the cells were transfected with sh-FGL1 and sh-FGL1-NC, and western blot confirmed efficient knockdown of FGL1 (Fig. 1D and E). Following transfection, TU212-R cells treated with 8 μM CDDP for 24–72 h showed enhanced CDDP sensitivity upon FGL1 depletion, as evidenced by suppressed proliferative capacity (Fig. 1F). These data indicate that FGL1 modulates CDDP resistance by regulating laryngeal cancer cell proliferation.

Integrinβ1 is a functional mediator of CDDP resistance regulated by FGL1 in TU212-R cells

Western blot analysis revealed overexpression of FGL1 and Integrinβ1 in TU212-R cells compared with normal NP69 and parental TU212 cells (Fig. 2A). To assess the FGL1/Integrinβ1 axis in CDDP resistance, the Integrinβ1 activator Pyrintegrin was used. CCK-8 assay showed that sh-FGL1-induced cell viability inhibition was significantly abrogated by Pyrintegrin, which also attenuated CDDP induced cytotoxicity (Fig. 2B). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that FGL1 knockdown suppressed Integrinβ1 expression, an effect reversed by Pyrintegrin treatment (Fig. 2C and D).

FGL1 depletion inhibits migratory and invasive phenotypes via integrinβ1 in TU212-R cells

Wound healing and Transwell assays were performed to investigate the impact of FGL1 on metastatic properties. Wound healing assay results indicated that compared with the CDDP group, Pyrintegrin enhanced migratory capacity, while sh-FGL1 transfection potently inhibited cell migration, an effect partially abrogated by Pyrintegrin (Fig. 3A and B). In addition, transwell invasion assays confirmed that FGL1 knockdown suppressed invasive potential, which was reversed by integrinβ1 activation (Fig. 3C and D). Taken together, these results indicate that FGL1 modulates migratory and invasive phenotypes by targeting Integrinβ1.

Downregulation of FGL1 inhibited the migration and invasion abilities of TU212-R cells through regulating Integrinβ1. The migration and invasion abilities of TU212-R cells were determined by wound healing assay (scale bar: 50 μm) (A) and transwell assay (scale bar: 50 μm) (B) after FGL1 knockdown and treatment with Pyrintegrin. n=3, ns means no significant difference, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001.

FGL1 knockdown enhances CDDP-induced apoptosis and reduces chemoresistance-related protein expression

We investigated whether the enhancement of CDDP sensitivity in TU212-R cells by FGL1 inhibition is related to the expression of drug-resistant proteins (MRP1 and P-gp). Western blot showed substantial downregulation of MRP1 and P-gp in sh-FGL1-transfected cells, while pyrintegrin rescued their expression (Fig. 4A). The apoptosis rate of TU212-R cells transfected with sh-FGL1 and treated with Pyrintegrin was measured by flow cytometry. The results showed that FGL1 inhibition significantly promoted apoptosis, and this promotional effect was attenuated by Pyrintegrin, which inhibited apoptosis (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that FGL1 knockdown sensitizes cells to CDDP by promoting apoptosis and suppressing resistance-related proteins.

FGL1 depletion suppresses in vivo tumorigenicity of TU212-R cells

To validate these findings in vivo, a CDDP-resistant laryngeal cancer xenograft model was generated in nude mice. FGL1 knockdown combined with CDDP treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth, as reflected by reduced tumor volume and weight. However, Pyrintegrin promoted tumor growth and antagonized the efficacy of CDDP, resulting in larger tumor volumes and greater weights compared to the CDDP group, while also weakened the tumor-inhibitory effect of FGL1 (Fig. 5A-C). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed reduced immunoreactivity for Ki-67, FGL1, Integrinβ1 and P-gp in the sh-FGL1 group compared with the CDDP control (Fig. 5D-H).

FGL1 modulates in vivo apoptosis via the integrinβ1/resistance protein axis

TUNEL staining of tumor tissues showed that FGL1 knockdown enhanced apoptosis, while Pyrintegrin suppressed apoptotic levels (Fig. 6A and B). Then, we verified the relationship between FGL1 and resistance-related proteins in vivo, western blot results confirmed that FGL1 depletion inhibited Integrinβ1 activation and resistance protein expression, with these effects abrogated by Pyrintegrin (Fig. 6C). These in vivo findings confirm the pivotal role of FGL1 and Integrinβ1 in the progression of CDDP resistance in laryngeal cancer.

Discussion

Laryngeal cancer is characterized by high lethality and a dismal prognosis, and remains a therapeutic challenge primarily due to its intrinsic propensity for chemotherapy resistance [22]. Therefore, further research on new treatment strategies and drug resistance mechanisms for laryngeal cancer is needed. In this study, we identified aberrant upregulation of FGL1 and Integrinβ1 in cisplatin-resistant laryngeal cancer cells, and demonstrated that FGL1 depletion ameliorates CDDP resistance via direct modulation of the Integrinβ1 signaling axis in both in vitro and in vivo models.

FGL1 has emerged as a critical regulator of tumor progression across multiple malignancies, with its expression frequently correlated with invasive phenotypes [23]. Notably, the expression of FGL1 is positively correlated with the expression of the proliferation marker ki67 [24], and knockdown of FGL1 can inhibit the proliferation Lewis lung cancer cells, while recent studies highlight its role in promoting laryngeal cancer metastasis [6]. We found that FGL1 was highly expressed in laryngeal tumor tissues, and its expression was further upregulated in TU212-R cells compared with parental TU212 cells, after down-regulating the expression of FGL1, the proliferation, migration and invasion abilities of CDDP-resistant laryngeal cancer cells were significantly weakened, and the apoptosis of TU212-R cells was promoted. In vivo, FGL1 knockdown combined with CDDP treatment significantly inhibited xenograft tumor growth, as evidenced by reduced Ki-67 expression and tumor burden. Mechanistically, this effect was associated with downregulation of chemoresistance markers (P-gp, MRP1), consistent with previous reports linking FGL1 to apoptosis evasion in non-small cell lung cancer [25]. The immunohistochemical results showed a similar trend. These data collectively suggest FGL1 as a key driver of CDDP resistance in laryngeal cancer.

Previous studies have found that the fibrinogen α chain inhibits gastric cancer progression by suppressing the expression of Integrin α5 [26]. The mouse extracellular vesicular fibrinogen induces inflammation by binding to Integrinβ, which further prove that integrinβ is the binding receptor for fibrinogen [27].

The study of Maniaci et al. [28] highlights that chemotherapy resistance is a key therapeutic challenge in laryngeal cancer, and identifies cancer stem cell (CSC) characteristics and aberrant signaling pathways as critical drivers of this resistance. Specifically, Integrinβ1 exerts its effects through downstream pathways such as Integrin-linked kinase (ILK)/AKT and Focal adhesion kinase (FAK)/Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), which inhibit apoptosis and promote cell survival. A recent study by Wang et al. [29] demonstrated that circ_0085495 promotes adriamycin resistance in breast cancer by sponging miR-873-5p to upregulate Integrinβ1 expression, which highlights the critical role of integrinβ1 in chemoresistance and underscores the importance of exploring the regulatory mechanisms and functional interactions of integrinβ1 in the context of cancer therapy.

Since the expression level of Integrinβ1 in TU212-R cells was higher than in normal TU212 cells, we verified the role of Integrinβ1 in CDDP-resistant laryngeal cancer. Integrinβ1 is tightly linked to drug resistance in various malignant tumors, for example, chemotherapy resistance in pancreatic cancer can be reduced by down-regulating Integrinβ1 [30]. The interaction between Integrinβ1 and Galectin-1 can promote drug resistance of breast cancer cells [31]. Similarly, Reelin promotes adherence and resistance to multiple myeloma cells by activating Integrinβ1 and STAT3 signaling pathways [32]. In the present study, we demonstrated that the overactivation of Integrin β1 completely counteracts the therapeutic effect of CDDP, while FGL1 knockdown inhibits its activation and down-regulates the proliferation, migration, and invasion of TU212-R cells, conversely, the Integrin β1 activator Pyrintegrin antagonizes the effects of FGL1 depletion.

Inhibition or downregulating the expression of drug-resistant proteins (including P-gp and MRP1) has been recognized as an effective strategy to overcome chemotherapy resistance [33]. The increased expression of P-gp and MRP1 in CDDP-resistant TU212-R cells, as well as the decreased efficacy of CDDP, could be reversed by FGL1 knockdown. In vivo experiment showed a consistent trend after administration. These results indicate that the expression of Integrinβ1 is regulated by FGL1, while the regulatory mechanism of the drug-resistant proteins requires further experimental verification.

We explored the relationship between FGL1 and Integrinβ1 in vivo to verify the regulatory role of FGL1 on Integrinβ1. The results confirmed tha down-regulating FGL1 expression significantly enhances the tumor-inhibitory effect of CDDP, reduces the tumor weight and volume, and inhibits the expression of related drug-resistant proteins. We acknowledge the lack of transcriptomic/proteomic data and propose this as a futher direction to identify additional FGL1-regulated resistance pathways. Further studies are needed to elucidate the role of FGL1 on a wider range of cell lines, as well as more clinical studies to evaluate the safety and efficacy of FGL1 regulation.

Conclusion

Collectively, our study demonstrates that FGL1 promotes cisplatin resistance in laryngeal cancer by activating an Integrinβ1-dependent pro-survival and pro-migratory signaling network. Targeted depletion of FGL1 enhances CDDP efficacy by suppressing Integrinβ1-mediated drug efflux and restoring apoptotic sensitivity. These findings establish FGL1 as a promising therapeutic target for overcoming chemoresistance in laryngeal cancer, paving the way for novel combination strategies to improve patient outcomes.
