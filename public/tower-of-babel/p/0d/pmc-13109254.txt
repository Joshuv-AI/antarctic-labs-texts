Canagliflozin attenuates CMR-quantified myocardial fibrosis in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus at high cardiovascular risk: a randomised open-label controlled trial
Authors: Hongmei Yan, Jiaojiao Liu, Zhitian Zhang, Shuangshuang Chen, Xinxia Chang, Hang Jin, Xiaoying Li, Yinyin Chen, Ying Chen, Mengsu Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims/hypothesisWe aimed to investigate canagliflozin’s effects on myocardial fibrosis, cardiac structure and function, and microcirculation in high-cardiovascular-risk type 2 diabetes mellitus through cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) quantification, while investigating the cardiovascular protective mechanisms of sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors.MethodsThis open-label parallel RCT recruited 45 high-risk participants (18–75 years) with type 2 diabetes (HbA1c 53.0–91.3 mmol/mol [7.0–10.5%]) and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) >50% from the Endocrinology Outpatient Clinic of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (August 2022 to November 2024). Participants were 1:1 randomised via a computer-generated sequence through the centralised iClinicalStation system (allocation concealed, performed by independent personnel not involved in outcome assessment). They received canagliflozin 100 mg/day (n=23) or sitagliptin 100 mg/day (n=22) for 26 weeks. Consistent with the open-label design, participants and care providers were aware of treatment assignments; however, outcome assessors (radiologists, sonographers) and data analysts remained blinded to group allocation to minimise bias. The primary endpoint was CMR-quantified extracellular volume (ECV) change. Secondary outcomes included ventricular structure and function parameters, for example, left ventricular end-diastolic volume, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter, LVEF, etc.ResultsAmong 67 individuals screened, 45 completed the intention-to-treat analysis (age 60.4 ± 9.7 years, BMI 26.4 ± 2.6 kg/m2, HbA1c 63.0 ± 8.7 mmol/mol [7.9 ± 0.8%]). At 26 weeks, compared with sitagliptin, canagliflozin significantly reduced the primary outcome of ECV (adjusted mean difference [AMD]: −3.67%; 95% CI −5.33, −2.01; p<0.001). Significant improvements were also observed in cardiac structure, including left ventricular end-diastolic volume (AMD: −20.72 ml; 95% CI −36.30, −5.14; p=0.010) and echocardiography-derived end-diastolic diameter (AMD: −2.82 mm; 95% CI −4.95, −0.70; p=0.010). Introduction

The global diabetes epidemic poses a critical public health challenge, with CVD accounting for 50-70% of all-cause mortality in individuals with type 2 diabetes and conferring a two- to fourfold greater risk of CVD-related disability than in nondiabetic individuals [1–3]. While microvascular complications primarily compromise quality of life, macrovascular events such as atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) are the primary drivers of mortality risk, thus establishing them as key targets in diabetes management.

Cardiovascular outcome trials revealed distinct profiles of sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors (SGLT2is): empagliflozin reduced major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) by 14% and cardiovascular mortality by 38% in individuals with type 2 diabetes and established CVD (EMPA-REG OUTCOME) [4]. Canagliflozin similarly lowered the MACE risk in individuals with type 2 diabetes and ASCVD or high-risk populations (HR 0.86, CANVAS) [5], whereas dapagliflozin was noninferior but not superior (DECLARE-TIMI 58) [6]. Notably, all agents reduced hospitalisations due to heart failure: empagliflozin by 35% [4], canagliflozin by 33% [5] and dapagliflozin by 27% [6], suggesting class-specific benefits against heart failure progression.

Despite proven clinical benefits, the mechanisms underlying the cardioprotection of SGLT2is, particularly their heart failure benefits, remain elusive. Contemporary guidelines (European Society of Cardiology [ESC] 2021/American Heart Association [AHA]/American College of Cardiology [ACC] 2022) [7, 8] recognise myocardial fibrosis as a central pathological mechanism in heart failure, especially in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF; defined as heart failure characterised by a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≥50% and signs/symptoms of heart failure, alongside structural heart disease or diastolic dysfunction). The extracellular volume (ECV) fraction, quantified through cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging, has been extensively validated as a noninvasive imaging biomarker for interstitial fibrosis assessment [9–11]. Myocardial fibrosis exacerbation in individuals with type 2 diabetes independently predicts heart failure hospitalisation and mortality, outperforming traditional cardiac structural parameters such as left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD)/left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) in early risk stratification [11–13]. These findings position fibrosis reversal as a plausible therapeutic target for SGLT2is.

Although echocardiography has provided valuable evidence regarding the effects of SGLT2 inhibitors on cardiac structural remodelling [14–17], its inherent limitations in fibrosis quantification restrict comprehensive mechanistic investigations.

In recent years, CMR technology has demonstrated distinctive clinical value because of its noninvasive nature and capability for multiplanar, multiparametric imaging. By using a single scan, CMR enables integrated assessment of cardiac structure and function, including key parameters such as left ventricular (LV) mass and volume, ejection fraction and myocardial perfusion characteristics. Furthermore, advanced techniques, including late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and T1 (longitudinal relaxation time) mapping, provide precise quantification of both focal and diffuse myocardial fibrotic alterations [18, 19]. These methodological advancements establish a foundation for rigorous investigations into the pathophysiological effects of SGLT2 inhibitors on myocardial fibrosis progression.

Several clinical trials have examined alterations in structural and functional parameters as assessed by CMR following SGLT2i therapy. The SUGAR-DM-HF trial revealed empagliflozin-mediated reductions in LV volume indices among individuals with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and type 2 diabetes or prediabetes following 36 weeks of therapy [20]. The EMPA-HEART study reported significant 6 month attenuation of LV mass indexed to body surface area in individuals with type 2 diabetes and coronary artery disease [21]. The DAPA-LVH trial documented dapagliflozin-induced LV mass regression over 12 months in comorbid individuals with type 2 diabetes and left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) [22]. Conversely, the REFORM trial failed to establish statistically significant LV remodelling alterations after 52 weeks of dapagliflozin treatment in individuals with type 2 diabetes and heart failure [23]. A limited number of studies have examined the impact of SGLT2 inhibitors on the myocardial fibrosis parameter (ECV). In individuals with type 2 diabetes and coronary artery disease, empagliflozin led to a 6 month ECV reduction (−1.40% vs placebo, p=0.03) [24]. Dapagliflozin showed divergent outcomes: no significant ECV changes in participants with type 2 diabetes without heart failure over 12 months [25], whereas participants with nonischaemic cardiomyopathy and HFrEF exhibited ECV reduction (Δ −1.54%, p=0.001) at the 6 month follow-up [26]. Notably, the effects of canagliflozin on myocardial ECV remain unexamined through CMR quantification.

This study used CMR to evaluate the effects of canagliflozin on myocardial fibrosis (ECV), structural remodelling (LV mass/volume) and microcirculatory perfusion in participants with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk, thereby elucidating the imaging-based mechanisms underlying the heart failure benefits associated with SGLT2is.

Methods

Study design

This 26 week single-centre, randomised, open-label trial enrolled individuals with type 2 diabetes and high cardiovascular risk from the Endocrinology Outpatient Clinic at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (August 2022 to November 2024). The protocol was approved by the Zhongshan Hospital Ethics Committee [B2021-764(2)] (Helsinki Declaration 1996) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (registration no. NCT05367063). All participants provided written informed consent.

Participants

Individuals with high-cardiovascular-risk type 2 diabetes were recruited from the Endocrinology Outpatient Clinic of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Shanghai, China), a tertiary general hospital, predominantly from Shanghai and surrounding economically developed regions. All were Han Chinese; gender distribution and age range (18–75 years, consistent with inclusion criteria) align with the broader population of high-cardiovascular-risk type 2 diabetes in urban China. Regional generalisability is limited (not rural/other regions). Socioeconomic data (e.g. education, income) were not systematically collected, given homogeneous access to standard diabetes care in tertiary hospitals. Race/ethnicity data were not collected, reflecting local ethnic homogeneity and that ethnicity was not a pre-specified covariate.

Inclusion criteria

18–75 years; type 2 diabetes with HbA1c 53.0–91.3 mmol/mol (7.0–10.5%); stable glucose-lowering medication for ≥4 weeks before inclusion; high ASCVD risk (≥3 risk factors [male >55 years/female >65 years, BMI ≥25 kg/m2, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, smoking]) or target organ damage (renal dysfunction [albumin/creatinine ratio (ACR) ≥300 mg/g, eGFR 45–89 ml/min per 1.73 m2], echocardiographically confirmed LVH, nonproliferative/treated diabetic retinopathy); LVEF >50%.

Exclusion criteria

History of CVDs, such as myocardial infarction, hospitalisation for unstable angina, cerebral infarction (ischaemic stroke), coronary artery bypass grafting, percutaneous coronary intervention (with or without stenting), peripheral vascular reconstruction (angioplasty or surgery), heart failure, arrhythmia and valvular heart disease; aspartate aminotransferase (AST) or alanine aminotransferase (ALT) ≥3 times the upper limit of normal; eGFR <45 ml/min per 1.73 m2; drug allergies; use of restricted glucose-lowering agents; severe organ dysfunction; active malignancy; recent participation in other trials (full criteria at ClinicalTrials.gov registration no. NCT05367063).

Randomisation and interventions

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either canagliflozin or sitagliptin using a computer-generated randomisation sequence via the centralised iClinicalStation system (https://edc-cloud.medsci.cn/#/login). Allocation concealment was ensured by the web-based system, which kept the sequence hidden from investigators until the moment of assignment. Randomisation was performed by independent personnel not involved in outcome assessment. Although this was an open-label trial where participants and care providers were aware of the treatment assignment, outcome assessors (including radiologists and sonographers) and data analysts remained blinded to the group allocation to minimise bias (examiners were blinded to all clinical data except anonymised examination numbers). Participants received canagliflozin (Invokana) 100 mg/day (intervention) or sitagliptin (Januvia) 100 mg/day (control) for 26 weeks; baseline glucose-lowering medications remained unchanged.

Anthropometric measures

Body weight, BMI, heart rate and blood pressure were collected per standardised protocols.

CMR

All pre-/post-intervention CMR scans were performed by a blinded experienced radiologist using a 3.0 T scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma; Siemens Healthcare) [27] with electrocardiogram gating and a 16-channel coil.

T1 mapping and ECV calculation

Diffuse fibrosis was assessed via a modified look-locker inversion recovery (MOLLI) sequence [3(3)3(3)5 scheme] at basal, mid-ventricular and apical short-axis slices. Segmentation followed the 17-segment American Heart Association (AHA) model; CMR analyses were performed using cvi42 software (version 6.1.2; Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Calgary, Canada) excluding papillary muscles, epicardial fat, and blood pool. Global native T1 and ECV were averaged from basal/mid-ventricular segments (apical slices excluded per Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance [SCMR] guidelines to reduce variability).

LV structure/function

Short-axis cine sequences were used to measure LVEDV and left ventricular end-diastolic volume index (LVEDVi), left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV) and left ventricular end-systolic volume index (LVESVi), and LVEF (Cvi42 automatic contouring). The LVEDVi and LVESVi were normalised to the body surface area via the LVEDV and LVESV.

Due to safety concerns and contrast agent intolerance, paired pre- and post-treatment CMR perfusion data were available for only a portion of participants (canagliflozin: n=11; sitagliptin: n=7) and are presented as exploratory analyses in electronic supplementary material (ESM) Table 1.

Reproducibility analysis demonstrated high intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) for ECV (intra-observer ICC=0.959, inter-observer ICC=0.947). Reproducibility for other CMR measurements is reported in ESM Table 2 due to space constraints.

Echocardiography

Blinded certified sonographers performed echocardiograms per 2015 American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) guidelines (GE Vivid E95 system, Horten, Norway, M5Sc probe). The key metrics included the LVEDD/left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD), LVEF, interventricular septum (IVS) thickness, left ventricular posterior wall (LVPW) thickness, left atrial anteroposterior diameter (LAAP), aortic root diameter (AORD) at the sinus of Valsalva and pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP). All parameters were averaged over three cardiac cycles.

Reproducibility for LVEDD is reported in ESM Table 2 (intra-observer ICC=0.939, inter-observer ICC=0.960).

Laboratory evaluations

Overnight fasting blood/urine samples were collected and analysed at Zhongshan Hospital’s central laboratory: plasma glucose (hexokinase assay), HbA1c (HPLC) and fasting lipids (automated biochemical analysis). Oral glucose-lowering drugs were withheld on sampling days.

Assessment of fibrosis biomarkers

Plasma levels of galectin-3 (also known as galactoside-binding lectin 3), procollagen type I carboxy-terminal propeptide (PICP), suppression of tumorigenicity 2 (ST2), tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-1 (TIMP-1) and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-2 (TIMP-2) were measured via ELISA using commercial kits (Shanghai Weiaobio Biotechnology); detailed methods are provided in the ESM Methods (Detailed methods for the measurement of serum biomarkers).

Safety evaluations

Adverse events were monitored via medical history and laboratory tests, with investigators recording treatment-related abnormalities and assessing their associations with the study drugs.

Endpoints

Primary endpoint

The primary endpoint was change in myocardial ECV from baseline to 26 weeks.

Secondary endpoints

The secondary endpoints were changes in CMR-derived parameters (native T1, post-contrast T1, LVEDV, LVEDVi, LVESV, LVESVi), LVEF, echocardiographic parameters (LVEDD, LVESD, LVEF, IVS, LVPW, LAAP, AORD, PASP), metabolic markers (HbA1c, uric acid, etc.) and fibrosis biomarkers.

Strategies to mitigate bias in open-label design

Measures included: blinded assessment of imaging/laboratory endpoints while the primary endpoint (ECV) was derived from an automated quantitative algorithm to minimise subjective human error; standardised non-study medication prescription; uniform international guideline-aligned lifestyle counselling; and >95% adherence in both groups.

Sample size

Based on an assumed between-group ECV difference of −1.4% (SD 2%) [28], α=0.05 and 90% power, 22 participants per group were required. Accounting for 20% attrition, 56 participants were planned; the final cohort included 45 (23 canagliflozin, 22 sitagliptin), with 43 completing follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Analyses followed the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD (normal distribution) or median (IQR) (non-normal); categorical variables are reported as n (%). Comparisons used two-tailed t tests/Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (paired) or Mann–Whitney U tests (unpaired); χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical data. Missing data were imputed via multiple imputation (20 datasets).

Primary/secondary endpoints

Statistical analysis

Continuous efficacy endpoints (including primary and other endpoints) were analysed using a mixed-effects model for repeated measures (MMRM) based on the ITT population. The primary efficacy analysis included all participants as randomly assigned (canagliflozin: n=23; sitagliptin: n=22). The model included the observed value at each visit as the dependent variable, with treatment group, visit and treatment-by-visit interaction as fixed effects, and the baseline value of the specific endpoint as a covariate. An unstructured covariance matrix was used to model the within-participant correlation. No multiplicity adjustment was performed for secondary/exploratory analyses; nominal p values are reported.

Sensitivity and robustness analyses

To verify the robustness of the primary findings, the following pre-specified sensitivity analyses were performed:

Per-protocol (PP) analysis

The MMRM was applied to the PP population, defined as participants who completed both baseline and follow-up CMR assessments with evaluable data (canagliflozin: n=20; sitagliptin: n=19).

Multiple imputation analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed using an MMRM on datasets generated via multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) to address missing data. Additionally, a post hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted, adjusting for potential confounders including age, gender, BMI, hypertension status and antihypertensive medication use (ACE inhibitor [ACEI]/angiotensin II receptor blocker [ARB]/angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor [ARNI]/calcium channel blocker [CCB]); these results are presented in ESM Tables 3 and 4.

All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2. (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-tailed p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant flow

Of 67 screened individuals, 22 were excluded per the inclusion/exclusion criteria, and 45 were randomised 1:1 to canagliflozin (n=23) or sitagliptin (n=22); 43 completed the 26 week treatment (ESM Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics were generally balanced between the canagliflozin and sitagliptin groups. The overall study population had a mean age of 60.4 ± 9.7 years, BMI of 26.4 ± 2.6 kg/m2, HbA1c of 63.0 ± 8.7 mmol/mol (7.9 ± 0.8%) and LVEF of 65.4 ± 3.9%.

Specifically, ALT levels were 37.5 ± 31.2 U/l in the canagliflozin group and 21.7 ± 11.7 U/l in the sitagliptin group. The prevalence of hypertension was 61% in the canagliflozin group and 82% in the sitagliptin group; the use of ACEI/ARB/ARNI was 35% vs 55%, and CCB use was 26% vs 55% between the two groups.

No notable differences were observed between groups in blood pressure, lipid profiles, AST, kidney function or other medical history variables (Table 1).

Sensitivity analyses corroborated the primary finding

Per-protocol analysis

The MMRM restricted to the PP population (n=20 canagliflozin vs n=19 sitagliptin) yielded a consistent result (AMD: −3.53%; 95% CI −5.23, −1.83; p<0.001) (Fig. 1).

Adjusted imputation analysis

A post hoc sensitivity analysis (MMRM adjusted for baseline ECV, age, gender, BMI, hypertension status and ACEI/ARB/CCB use) on 20 imputed datasets yielded consistent results (AMD: −3.25%; 95% CI −4.75, −1.76; p<0.001), verifying the robustness of the primary finding (ESM Table 3, ESM Fig. 2).

Secondary endpoints: CMR parameters

Native T1

Baseline native T1 was 1198.96 ± 62.56 ms (canagliflozin) and 1239.04 ± 40.26 ms (sitagliptin). The MMRM (adjusted for baseline, treatment allocation, time and group-by-time interaction) showed no significant between-group difference at 26 weeks (AMD: 9.04 ms; 95% CI −43.19, 61.26; p=0.728) (Table 2).

Post-contrast myocardial T1

Baseline post-contrast myocardial T1 was 401.03 ± 143.36 ms (canagliflozin) and 452.89 ± 97.19 ms (sitagliptin). The MMRM (adjusted for baseline, treatment allocation, time and group-by-time interaction) revealed no significant between-group difference at 26 weeks (AMD: 31.76 ms; 95% CI −34.78, 98.31; p=0.340) (Table 2).

LV structure/function

Baseline LVEDV was 109.60 ± 26.85 ml (canagliflozin) and 127.79 ± 29.29 ml (sitagliptin). The MMRM (adjusted for baseline, treatment allocation, time and group-by-time interaction) showed a significant between-group difference favouring canagliflozin at 26 weeks (AMD: −20.72 ml; 95% CI −36.30, −5.14; p=0.010) (Table 2). LVEDVi also showed a significant reduction with canagliflozin (MMRM-adjusted AMD: −8.94 ml/m2; 95% CI −16.59, −1.30; p=0.023) (Table 2). No significant between-group differences were observed for LVESV, LVESVi or LVEF (Table 2).

Secondary endpoints: echocardiography

Baseline LVEDD was 46.39 ± 3.37 mm (canagliflozin) and 45.50 ± 4.35 mm (sitagliptin). The MMRM (adjusted for baseline, treatment allocation, time and group-by-time interaction) showed a significant between-group difference in LVEDD favouring canagliflozin at 26 weeks (AMD: −2.82 mm; 95% CI −4.95, −0.70; p=0.010) (Table 2).

A post hoc sensitivity analysis (MMRM further adjusted for hypertension status and ACEI/ARB/ARNI/CCB use) confirmed consistent significance (AMD: −2.30 mm; 95% CI −4.23, −0.36; p=0.020) (ESM Table 3).

No significant between-group differences were observed for LVESD, LVEF, IVS, LVPW, LAAP, AORD or PASP (Table 2).

Baseline haematocrit was 42.39 ± 3.00% (canagliflozin) and 41.72 ± 2.81% (sitagliptin). The MMRM (adjusted for baseline, treatment allocation, time and group-by-time interaction) showed a significant between-group difference favouring canagliflozin at 26 weeks (AMD: 2.29%; 95% CI 0.11, 4.47; p=0.040) (Table 3).

Urinary tract infections occurred in 17.4% (4/23) of the canagliflozin group and 0% (0/22) of the sitagliptin group. One case of acute coronary syndrome (4.3%, 1/23) was reported in the canagliflozin group, which was assessed as unrelated to the study drug; no such events were observed in the sitagliptin group (p=0.109) (ESM Table 5).

No treatment-related discontinuations occurred.

Discussion

In this randomised, open-label sitagliptin-controlled trial, we present initial evidence suggesting that 26 weeks of canagliflozin treatment may induce favourable cardiac remodelling in individuals with type 2 diabetes and high cardiovascular risk: specifically, a significant reduction in myocardial ECV fraction (ECV is a gold-standard marker of interstitial fibrosis), alongside potential improvements in CMR-derived LVEDV and echocardiographic LVEDD. Notably, these effects appear independent of glycaemic control, systemic inflammation or conventional blood pressure lowering, which may point to a targeted cardioprotective action in subclinical diabetic cardiomyopathy.

Core findings and clinical significance

Myocardial ECV is a pivotal prognostic marker in type 2 diabetes, and prior evidence links every 3% ECV increase to a 52% higher risk of heart failure hospitalisation or all-cause death [11], and each 1% elevation to a 16% increased risk of composite heart failure events [28]. Our data align with this prognostic relevance: canagliflozin treatment resulted in a reduction in ECV (mean change: −2.61 ± 3.10%) compared with a minimal change in the sitagliptin group (+0.44 ± 2.36%). The primary MMRM analysis confirmed a significant between-group difference (AMD: −3.67%; 95% CI −5.33, −2.01; p<0.001). This magnitude suggests a potential 40–45% lower future heart failure risk, consistent with the 33% heart failure hospitalisation reduction observed with canagliflozin in the CANVAS trial [4].

This anti-fibrotic signal aligns with other SGLT2is, supporting a class effect. The EMPA-HEART trial [24] (empagliflozin, type 2 diabetes with coronary artery disease) reported a 1.4% ECV reduction over 6 months, while the recent dapagliflozin trial of Albulushi et al (type 2 diabetes with overt HFpEF) [29, 30] observed a 3.5% reduction over 12 months. Magnitude differences likely reflect study populations (secondary prevention in coronary artery disease vs subclinical diabetic cardiomyopathy vs overt HFpEF) and follow-up duration, rather than inherent drug potency.

Our study provides unique contributions: first, we focus on a subclinical diabetic cardiomyopathy cohort (stage B heart failure), extending anti-fibrotic potential to primary prevention; second, the active sitagliptin control (identical HbA1c reductions, −0.7%, p=0.838) reduces the potential interference of glycaemic confounders, a challenge in placebo-controlled trials.

Secondary cardiac structural parameters: LVEDV and LVEDD

Among secondary cardiac endpoints, CMR-derived LVEDV, a key marker of diastolic structural remodelling, showed a significant between-group difference favouring canagliflozin at 26 weeks, which was consistent with the significant reduction in LVEDVi in the canagliflozin group. These findings were further supported by echocardiographic LVEDD results (Table 2).

We observed a significant reduction in LVEDV with canagliflozin treatment (AMD: −20.72 ml, p=0.010). It is worth noting that the sitagliptin group had numerically higher baseline volumes and a higher prevalence of hypertension (82% vs 61%) compared with the canagliflozin group. Despite these baseline imbalances, our primary MMRM analysis, which adjusts for baseline LVEDV, identified a clear treatment benefit. Importantly, this finding remained consistent in our sensitivity analysis (ESM Table 3) which further adjusted for hypertension status and antihypertensive medication use. This robustness suggests that the observed reverse remodelling is attributable to canagliflozin treatment rather than baseline confounding factors.

Consistent with the volumetric findings from CMR, echocardiographic assessment also demonstrated a significant reduction in LVEDD (AMD: −2.82 mm, p=0.010). The concordance between these two independent imaging modalities reinforces the validity of our structural findings. Together with the reduction in myocardial ECV (fibrosis), these changes indicate that canagliflozin induces favourable diastolic remodelling, characterised by a reduction in both chamber size and interstitial fibrosis.

Contextualising remodelling

The potential LVEDV/LVEDD improvement, without changes in LVESV or LVEF, aligns with subclinical diabetic cardiomyopathy pathophysiology, where myocardial fibrosis and diastolic remodelling emerge as early lesions before systolic impairment. While the LVEDV signal is preliminary (model-dependent), its alignment with LVEDD results, resolved baseline confounders and the primary ECV finding points to a coordinated, targeted effect of canagliflozin on early diabetic cardiac structural drivers.

Mechanisms underpinning canagliflozin’s cardiac benefits: SGLT2-dependent, glucose-independent pathways

Canagliflozin’s potential to improve myocardial fibrosis and diastolic remodelling in high-risk individuals with type 2 diabetes is centrally driven by SGLT2-dependent, glucose-independent pathways. Its anti-fibrotic and remodelling actions do not rely on low-affinity sodium–glucose cotransporter 1 (SGLT1) inhibition, supported by other SGLT2is (e.g. empagliflozin, dapagliflozin) with minimal SGLT1 binding that similarly reduce myocardial fibrosis in distinct type 2 diabetes populations, and confirming SGLT2 inhibition as the core mediator. Critically, despite comparable glycaemic improvements with sitagliptin, only canagliflozin conferred favourable cardiac structural changes, reinforcing that the cardiovascular benefits of SGLT2is are not glucose-mediated.

Key metabolic and haemodynamic effects synergise to amplify these benefits. A central haemodynamic mechanism is plasma volume contraction, evidenced by canagliflozin-induced haematocrit elevation, an established SGLT2i action that lowers LV preload and alleviates myocardial wall stress and fibroblast activation (key fibrosis drivers), and is distinct from conventional blood pressure lowering given balanced blood pressure between groups.

The specificity of canagliflozin’s anti-fibrotic action is supported by stable native T1 values, which mitigate the likelihood of non-fibrotic confounders (e.g. myocardial oedema) and validate that ECV reduction reflects true interstitial fibrosis. Notably, low-grade inflammation is a known contributor to diabetic cardiac pathology, but neither group showed significant changes in CRP over 26 weeks and there were no between-group differences, indicating CRP-mediated inflammatory pathways do not mediate canagliflozin’s cardiac benefits. Canagliflozin treatment showed trends towards reducing BMI (p=0.078) and serum uric acid (p=0.054), consistent with the drug’s known metabolic benefits, and which may indirectly contribute to its beneficial effect on myocardial fibrosis.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that canagliflozin targets early structural abnormalities in subclinical diabetic cardiomyopathy through SGLT2-dependent haemodynamic improvements, independent of glycaemic control or CRP-driven inflammation.

Study limitations

Several inherent limitations should be considered when interpreting our results. First, the single-centre design may introduce selection bias, limiting generalisability to diverse populations with type 2 diabetes and subclinical diabetic cardiomyopathy. Second, the study used an open-label design; however, rigorous bias mitigation strategies were implemented: blinded CMR imaging and laboratory biomarker analyses, standardised clinical data collection and pre-specified statistical approaches (including MMRM) to address confounding and missing data, collectively minimising open-label-related bias. Third, the 26 week follow-up is insufficient to assess the long-term durability of structural improvements or whether these changes translate to reduced clinical heart failure risk (e.g. heart failure hospitalisation). Fourth, we lacked measurements of myocardial metabolism (e.g. ketone levels) and microcirculatory function, limiting exploration of additional potential mechanisms of SGLT2i action. Finally, the absence of a placebo control group prevents full exclusion of non-specific effects, although the active sitagliptin control helps address glycaemic confounders.

Conclusions

In high-risk individuals with type 2 diabetes, 26 weeks of canagliflozin treatment significantly reduces myocardial fibrosis, as indicated by ECV, and improves LV diastolic structure, evidenced by reductions in LVEDV and LVEDD. These effects, independent of glycaemic control, are mediated through haemodynamic pathways and are consistent with the established cardioprotective mechanisms of SGLT2is. Our findings extend SGLT2i anti-fibrotic evidence to the critical primary prevention setting, highlighting canagliflozin’s potential to halt progression from subclinical fibrosis to overt HFpEF and establishing a link to its established cardiovascular benefits.
