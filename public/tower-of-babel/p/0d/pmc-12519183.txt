Reconstructing the evolutionary history of stoneflies: Phylogenetic insights and temporal dynamics
Authors: Yehao Wang, Xiao Yang, Michael S Engel, Qing-Bo Huo, Yu-Zhou Du, Chenyang Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryStoneflies (order Plecoptera), one of the earliest winged insects, are ecologically vital as freshwater bioindicators. Despite their ecological and evolutionary significance, a robust phylogeny for stoneflies has remained elusive. Here, we analyzed mitogenomes from 97 species representing all 17 families, employing site-heterogeneous models to reconstruct a comprehensive phylogeny of global Plecoptera. Our results provide several key insights: 1) Scopuridae is the earliest diverging lineage within Euholognatha; 2) Taeniopterygidae and Leuctridae form sister groups, representing the second diverging clade within Euholognatha; 3) Capniidae is resolved as the sister group to Nemouridae + Notonemouridae; and 4) the phylogenetic relationships within Systellognatha are resolved. Furthermore, by integrating palaeontological and chronostratigraphic data, we selected well-vetted fossil calibrations to reconstruct a temporal framework for Plecoptera evolution. Our study identifies key periods in Plecoptera’s early divergence and the origins of extant stonefly families, establishing a foundation for future research into their biogeography, morphology, and behavioral evolution.Subject areas: Zoology, Entomology, Evolutionary biology, Evolutionary ecology, Evolutionary history

Introduction

Stoneflies (Plecoptera) represent one of the earliest-diverging lineages of polyneopteran insects and have a long evolutionary history (Figure 1).1 Plecoptera comprises approximately 3,900 extant species across 17 families distributed globally, except for Antarctica.2,3,4 Stoneflies undergo hemimetabolous development, featuring egg, nymph, and adult stages. Nymphs require high levels of dissolved oxygen and thus prefer cool to cold, fast-flowing riffle areas of streams where coarse mineral substrates predominate.5 Although adults are terrestrial, they generally remain close to their natal habitats, resting on or beneath vegetation or rock substrates during their reproductive stage.6 Throughout both nymph and adult stages, stoneflies occupy multiple trophic levels, functioning as shredders or predators, while also serving as prey for higher-level consumers in both aquatic and terrestrial food webs.7 They contribute substantially to nutrient cycling and the transfer of energy between freshwater and terrestrial ecosystems.7 Furthermore, their sensitivity to habitat alteration makes stoneflies effective bioindicators for aquatic ecosystems, alongside mayflies and caddisflies.8,9

The systematic placement of stoneflies within the polyneopteran Tree of Life (ToL) has long been contentious. Zwick explored various hypotheses regarding the sister group of Plecoptera, but did not reach a definitive conclusion.10 Morphological evidence has suggested that Plecoptera are sister to all remaining neopteran or polyneopteran lineages.11,12,13,14 However, most phylogenomic studies have suggested that Plecoptera are closely related to Dermaptera, together forming a well-supported monophyletic clade named Dermoplectopterida,15,16,17,18,19 particularly when site-compositional heterogeneity is adequately modeled.19

The phylogenetic relationships within Plecoptera are even more enigmatic. The phylogeny of Plecoptera has been widely studied based on morphological data.20,21,22,23,24,25,26,27,28 Within the currently widely accepted phylogenetic framework,26 stoneflies are divided into sixteen families within two suborders, Antarctoperlaria and Arctoperlaria. The suborder Arctoperlaria is further divided into two infraorders, Euholognatha and Systellognatha, with the early diverging family Scopuridae often being problematically placed in the Plecoptera and is usually not assigned to an infraorder within Arctoperlaria. Recently, Kathroperla was separated from Chloroperlidae and established as Kathroperlidae within the infraorder Systellognatha.29 While detailed morphological apomorphies have provided arguments for each high-level lineage (suborders, infraorders, superfamilies, and families),26 phylogenomic studies have challenged our understanding of plecopteran evolution.6,30,31,32

Earlier studies were limited by gene sampling, with only six or fewer genes sampled.33,34,35,36 Due to insufficient phylogenetic signal and potential alignment issues with the 18S rRNA dataset, earlier phylogenies of Plecoptera yielded results contradictory with morphology-based studies.26,33 As the locus number increased, phylogenetic resolution improved significantly, showing consistency with Zwick’s morphology-based framework and strongly supporting the monophyly of most high-level taxa.34

Mitochondrial genomes (mitogenomes), as easily accessible markers, have been widely used in insect phylogeny.37 Preliminary phylogenetic analyses of global stoneflies based on mitogenomes have been conducted.30,31,38,39 However, due to insufficient model comparison and selection, results were difficult to reconcile between different matrices and methods, particularly for early diverging families in each infraorder.30,31

With advancements in sequencing and supercomputing technologies, phylogenomic studies have yielded significant insights into the evolution of stoneflies. Letsch et al.32 combined transcriptomes of 17 species and Sanger sequences (seven genes) of 498 species to construct an evolutionary timescale for stoneflies. In this study, the phylogenetic relationships among families within the infraorder Systellognatha were consistently resolved across all analyses, with the Styloperlidae emerging as the earliest-diverging lineage, forming a sister group to the clade (Peltoperlidae, (Pteronarcyidae, (Kathroperlidae, (Perlidae, (Chloroperlidae, Perlodidae))))). This phylogenetic relationship shows high consistency with Sanger-sequence-based phylogenies.34,40 By contrast, the phylogeny within the other infraorder, Euholognatha, remains perplexing.32,40

The most comprehensive phylogenomic analysis to date, incorporating 1,400 orthologous genes across 94 taxa, further corroborated the phylogenetic framework of Systellognatha and provided significant information on the Euholognatha problem.6 Capniidae were identified as the sister group to Nemouridae instead of the traditionally recognized Leuctridae, though taxon sampling was insufficient, lacking Scopuridae and Notonemouridae.6 Notably, the phylogenetic positions of Taeniopterygidae and Leuctridae within Euholognatha remain inconclusive.6

The lack of consensus on higher-level relationships within Plecoptera is also attributed to deficiencies in compositional heterogeneity (site-specific compositional biases arising from biochemical constraints) modeling. While the Gamma model and third-codon-removal strategy have been widely applied to mitigate rate heterogeneity among sites, across-site compositional heterogeneity remains inadequately addressed.6,40 Partitioned methods can account for compositional heterogeneity among genes to some extent, but they do not adequately address among-site compositional heterogeneity.32,40,41,42 Site-heterogeneous models, such as LG4X, C10–C60 models, and the infinite mixture CAT-based models, effectively mitigate among-site compositional heterogeneity and have been tested in many insect groups.43,44,45,46,47,48,49 However, these models were rarely applied to the phylogeny of Plecoptera.30,32,50 Ding et al. demonstrated that the CAT-GTR model did not perform well due to long-branch attraction artifacts that could not be mitigated, which was likely caused by inappropriate outgroup selection.30

In this study, we sequenced 29 plecopteran mitogenomes and collected previously published data from 68 taxa, achieving full coverage of all 17 known families of Plecoptera at the genomic level. Using both maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI) methods, we comprehensively compared multiple models and proposed an updated phylogenetic framework for global Plecoptera. Furthermore, we constructed an evolutionary timescale for Plecoptera and revealed important periods of divergence for the clade. This study represents a paradigmatic case of comprehensive phylogenetic analysis in Plecoptera, significantly advancing our understanding of the evolutionary history of this ancient insect order and establishing a methodological framework for future large-scale global phylogenetic studies on stoneflies.

Results

Phylogeny of Plecoptera

The monophyly of the suborders Antarctoperlaria and Arctoperlaria, as well as the infraorders Euholognatha and Systellognatha, was well supported by both ML and BI analyses across Matrices 1–4 (Figure 3). However, under LG4X + FO + R, a spurious result was observed, in which Megaleuctra emerged as the first branch, sister to the remaining stoneflies, and Scopuridae as sister to Antarctoperlaria + Systellognatha (Figures S1‒S12).

Within Antarctoperlaria, Eustheniidae, together with Diamphipnoidae, formed the clade Eusthenioidea (Figure 3). This clade was sister to Gripopterygoidea, which includes Gripopterygidae and Austroperlidae (Figure 3). This phylogenetic relationship received consistent support across all analyses (Figures S1‒S12), decisively rejecting previous claims regarding the paraphyly of Antarctoperlaria.

Within Euholognatha, Scopuridae was recovered as the first-diverging lineage, forming a sister group with other euholognathans in all BI analyses (Figures 3 and S1–S4). In ML analyses, this relationship was also consistently revealed by site-heterogeneous models (excluding LG4X model) (Figures S6 and S7). Note that this does not support a broader clade Holognatha,51 as such a grouping also united euholognathans with Antarctoperlaria, a demonstrably paraphyletic assemblage. However, the position of Scopuridae was unstable under the site-homogeneous model (Figures S5 and S9–S12). Scopuridae were recovered as the first branch of Euholognatha in Matrices 1 and 4, but grouped with Taeniopterygidae or Leuctridae in Matrix 2 and Matrix 3, respectively (Figures S10 and S11). This inconsistency suggests some instability in the phylogenetic placement of Scopuridae. Leuctridae consistently emerged as the second branch of Euholognatha, followed by either Taeniopterygidae alone or Taeniopterygidae + Capniidae as the third branch in most ML analyses and in BI analysis under the GTR model (Figures S5‒S9 and S12). When Taeniopterygidae alone served as the third diverging lineage, Capniidae was sister to Notonemouridae + Nemouridae. In contrast, under the CAT-GTR+G4 model, Leuctridae grouped with Taeniopterygidae to form the second branch of Euholognatha, with Capniidae sister to the clade (Notonemouridae + Nemouridae) (Figures 3 and S1‒S4). Notonemouridae + Nemouridae consistently received robust support in all analyses, representing the most recently diverged lineage within Euholognatha (Figures S1‒S12). Notably, the genera within the subfamilies Nemourinae and Amphinemurinae did not form distinct clades in all analyses (Figures 3 and S1‒S12). For instance, Nemoura was a sister to Amphinemura, indicating that both subfamilies are non-monophyletic.

Within Systellognatha, the phylogenetic positions of the early-diverging families Styloperlidae, Peltoperlidae, and Pteronarcyidae varied across different models and matrices. In ML analyses, Styloperlidae were recovered as the first branch of Systellognatha, followed by Peltoperlidae and Pteronarcyidae as the second and third branches, respectively, under the mtART+C60 + F+G model (Figure S6). This relationship was also recovered under the partitioned model based on Matrices 1, 3, and 4, albeit with relatively lower support (Figures S9, S11, and S12). However, Styloperlidae grouped with Kathroperlidae based on Matrix 2, becoming sister to the clade (Pteronarcyidae, (Chloroperlidae + Perlodidae)), albeit with lower support (Figure S10). Additionally, Styloperlidae grouped with Peltoperlidae as the first branch under the LG4X + FO + R model, followed by Pteronarcyidae as the second branch (Figure S8). Conversely, Styloperlidae emerged as the second-diverging lineage of Systellognatha against Peltoperlidae as the first branch under the mtART+C20 + F+G model, followed by Pteronarcyidae as the third-diverging lineage (Figure S7). In BI analyses, the results from the mtART+C60 + F+G model were further reproduced by analyses of Matrices 1, 2 and 4 under the CAT-GTR+G4 model (Figures 3, S1, S2, and S4). Conversely, Pteronarcyidae were recovered as the second branch against Peltoperlidae as the third branch in the analysis of Matrix 3 under CAT-GTR+G4 model (Figure S3). Additionally, the phylogenetic results of these three families under LG4X + FO + R model were reproduced by the GTR model (Figure S5). For the phylogenetic position of Kathroperlidae, it consistently occupied the fourth-diverging lineage within Systellognatha, sister to the clade (Perlidae + (Chloroperlidae + Perlodidae)), in almost all analyses (Figures S1‒S9 and S12). In Chloroperlidae, Chloroperlinae were monophyletic under the analyses on Matrices 1 and 4 (Figures S1, S4, and S5–S9), whereas the results based on Matrices 2 and 3 were different (Figures S2, S3, S10, and S11). In the BI analyses of Matrices 1, 2 and 4 under CAT-GTR+G4, and in the partitioned ML analysis of Matrix 2, the perlodid genus Pseudomegarcys, belonging to subfamily Perlodinae, forms a sister group with the subfamily Isoperlinae (Figures 3, S1‒S3, and S10). However, in other ML and BI analyses, Pseudomegarcys is sister to Isoperla bilineata (Figures S3, S5–S9, S11, and S12). Consequently, Perlodinae are suggested to be non-monophyletic. Additionally, the perlid genera within Acroneuriinae do not form a monophyletic clade in any analyses, and Perlinae are also non-monophyletic in the ML analyses under partitioned model (matrices 1 and 4), mtART+C60 + F+G and mtART+C20 + F+G models, as well as in the BI analysis under the GTR model (Figures S5–S7, S9, and S12). The two tribes Acroneuriini and Kiotinini within the Acroneuriinae were consistently polyphyletic in all analyses (Figures S1–S12).

Model comparison

The model comparison metrics based on leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV), widely applicable information criterion (wAIC), and Posterior Predictive Checking (PPC) provided strong evidence on the performance of different models, offering a reliable basis for selecting the best topology. As illustrated in Figures 2 and S13A, CAT-GTR+G4 exhibited a superior fit to the dataset compared to GTR+G, mtART+C20 + G, and mtART+C60 + G, as indicated by the LOO-CV scores (ΔCV (CAT-GTR+G4 vs. GTR+G) = 1.7616; ΔCV (CAT-GTR+G4 vs. mtART+C20 + G) = 1.8291; ΔCV (CAT-GTR+G4 vs. mtART+C60 + G) = 1.5745) and wAIC (ΔwAIC (CAT-GTR+G4 vs. GTR+G) = 1.7579; ΔwAIC (CAT-GTR+G4 vs. mtART+C20 + G) = 1.8252; ΔwAIC (CAT-GTR+G4 vs. mtART+C60 + G) = 1.5706). In contrast, the mtART+C20 + G model was the worst-fitting.

The p-value for all tested models was 0, with the Z score serving as the critical standard. For the CAT-GTR model, the Z score was 3.56364, suggesting that the observed diversity is slightly higher than the mean predicted diversity. In contrast, the GTR and mtART+C60 + G models yielded Z-scores of 26.0024 and 7.52455, respectively, signifying a substantial discrepancy between the observed diversity and the mean predicted diversity (Figures 2 and S13B).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that CAT-GTR+G4 provides a significantly better fit to the dataset, followed by mtART+C60 + G, GTR+G, and mtART+C20 + G (Figure 2). Topologies reconstructed with CAT-GTR+G4 were adopted as the preferred trees for elucidating the evolutionary history of Plecoptera.

Divergence-time estimates

Three MCMC chains converged perfectly (R2 = 1) with each other, allowing the results from all three runs to robustly represent the divergence-time estimates (Figures S14A‒S14C). Consequently, the results from run 1 were adopted (Figure 3). We identified a Pennsylvanian origin for crown Plecoptera (319‒302 Ma). Crown Antarctoperlaria were estimated to have originated over a broad interval from the early Guadalupian to the Middle Triassic (271‒237 Ma), with families diversifying during the Late Triassic to Middle Jurassic. Crown Arctoperlaria diverged in the Cisuralian (297‒277 Ma). Crown Euholognatha originated between the latest Cisuralian and the middle Lopingian (276‒254 Ma), with the earliest-diverging family, Scopuridae, diverging during the middle Early Jurassic to earliest Cretaceous (187‒137 Ma). Crown Nemouroidea emerged during the late Guadalupian to Middle Jurassic (264‒242 Ma). The clade (Leuctridae + Taeniopterygidae) originated during the Triassic (248‒224 Ma), nearly contemporaneously alongside the clade (Capniidae + (Notonemouridae + Nemouridae)) (243‒222 Ma). Crown Leuctridae diverged slightly earlier (216‒188 Ma) than Taeniopterygidae, which diverged during the latest Triassic to early Middle Jurassic (203‒170 Ma). Crown Capniidae diverged between the early Middle Jurassic the middle Late Jurassic (171‒153 Ma). The enigmatic family Notonemouridae diverged from ancestral Nemouridae between the middle Late Triassic to late Early Jurassic (209‒184 Ma), with Nemouridae further diversifying in the early Late Jurassic to middle Early Cretaceous (154‒125 Ma).

Crown Systellognatha were estimated to have originated between the early Guadalupian and earliest Middle Jurassic (268‒245 Ma). Due to sample limitations, the divergence-time estimate for the earliest-diverging family, Styloperlidae, was not obtained. Crown Peltoperlidae and Pteronarcyidae originated during the middle Late Triassic to middle Early Jurassic (217‒188 Ma) and the late Early Jurassic to earliest Late Jurassic (180‒160 Ma), respectively. Crown Perloidea diverged during the Late Triassic (229‒204 Ma). Additionally, Perlidae diverged from the early Late Jurassic to early Cretaceous (156‒132 Ma). The clade (Perlodidae + Chloroperlidae) originated between the late Early Jurassic and earliest Late Jurassic (185‒159 Ma), with the crown groups of these two families diverging during the Early Cretaceous (143‒115 Ma) and the latest Early Cretaceous to late Cretaceous (112‒82 Ma), respectively.

Discussion

Efficacy of mitogenome in plecopteran phylogeny

Mitogenomes have been extensively used for phylogenetic studies across various insect groups.37,46,52,53 However, their effectiveness in clarifying insect evolution, especially within orders, is often hindered by rate and compositional heterogeneity across sites in the dataset.37 Ding et al. found varying results across different datasets and methods, particularly some misleading topologies,30 casting doubt on the reliability of mitogenomes for phylogeny of Plecoptera.6 Using site-heterogeneous models (e.g., CAT-GTR) has been shown to significantly reduce these issues.46,47,50 However, these models are seldom used in plecopteran phylogenetics. Letsch et al. exclusively considered mixture models LG4X and LG4M in their gene tree reconstruction,32 while LG4X performed the worst in our experiments. The CAT-GTR model was applied to polyneopteran phylogeny using mitochondrial data, but it struggled with persistent long-branch attraction artifacts, affecting the phylogenetic placement of Dermaptera, Zoraptera, and Embiodea, and causing the non-monophyly of Arctoperlaria.30 This was likely due to the selection of unsuitable outgroups for Plecoptera.

Our experiments with outgroup selection revealed that when Dermaptera was used as the outgroup, both Arctoperlaria and Systellognatha were found to be non-monophyletic (Figures S18‒S20). Furthermore, when a combination of basal Polyneoptera and other closely related basal insect groups was substituted, Euholognatha clustered with Antarctoperlaria (Figure S24), a pattern also observed by Ding et al.30 and Letsch et al.,32 which has been identified as an artifact.30 In contrast, excluding these outgroups resulted in phylogenetic relationships that closely aligned with the framework obtained using mayflies as the outgroup (Figures S21–S23 and S25). Nevertheless, some branches in our analysis remained relatively short (Figures S1–S12 and S18–S25), even with mayflies as the outgroup. These short branches exhibited lower support values, primarily due to the limited number of informative sites in the mitogenomes. Wang et al. validated the efficacy of the CAT-GTR model for phylogenetic reconstruction in Plecoptera, demonstrating that this model significantly enhanced the resolution of phylogenetic relationships.50 We also obtained a highly consistent topology from analyses using mayflies as outgroups based on this model (Figure 3), where the phylogeny within Systellognatha is corroborated by both transcriptomic and Sanger sequencing data.6,32 Overall, while mitogenomes prove valuable for plecopteran phylogeny, careful consideration must be given to both outgroup selection and model choice. Future studies should integrate large-scale nuclear genomic data with more comprehensive taxon sampling (particularly filling sampling gaps for key basal lineages), coupled with phylogenetic analyses employing multiple models (e.g., comparing site-heterogeneous and site-homogeneous models) for cross-validation with mitogenomic reconstructions. This integrated approach will facilitate the evaluation of the reliability of mitochondrial phylogenetic signals while providing more robust resolution of Plecoptera's evolutionary history.

Origin and divergence of Plecoptera

The inferred divergence time in our study was older than the results of previous studies.30,32,36 We found that crown-Plecoptera originated in the Pennsylvanian period. The earliest member of the stem group of Plecoptera, †Gulou carpenteri (†Archiplecoptera, later in discussion), was reported from the Qilianshan entomofauna in China, dating to the latest Bashkirian to middle Moscovian.54,55 One author has argued that the differences between †Gulou and †Emphyloptera do not justify the establishment of †Guloidae, considering G. carpenteri as a member of †Emphylopteridae among the broader Orthopterida.56 This assertion is poorly founded and has been roundly refuted,57 and †Gulouidae are assuredly part of the broader clade encompassing all Plecoptera. Regardless of such debate, this suggests that the Pennsylvanian period was significant for the origin of Plecoptera, and efforts should continue to find corresponding fossil records. The Cisuralian period was estimated as a crucial period for the divergence of Arctoperlaria. Plecoptera have been suggested as diversifying between approximately 310 Ma and 275 Ma, corresponding to the period from the middle Pennsylvanian to the Cisuralian58 Therefore, the emergence of Arctoperlaria was synchronous with the early diversification of Plecoptera.

In contrast, Antarctoperlaria originated later than Arctoperlaria, with broad credibility intervals for antarctoperlarian lineages, consistent with previous studies.30,32,36 Many antarctoperlarian fossils, reported from the Guadalupian to the Aptian-Albian periods, lack precise systematic placement. Some, such as those in the family Eustheniidae, require critical reevaluation.26,59 Due to these uncertainties, these fossils cannot be used for time calibration, contributing to the uncertainty around Antarctoperlaria. Euholognatha and Systellognatha diverged from the latest Permian to the Early Triassic, coinciding with the mass extinction event in Plecoptera as inferred from fossil records.60 Although Plecoptera are an ancient group, the extant families may have evolved relatively recently.26 Most families originated between the Jurassic and Early Cretaceous, probably reaching peak diversification following the breakup of the continents.32,58

Phylogenetic relationships among Plecoptera

In our model comparison, CAT-GTR+G4 emerged as the best fit for the dataset, and the phylogenetic relationships among the families were consistent across Matrices 1, 2, and 4. Thus, the discussion is primarily based on this stable topology derived from this model. Within a monophyletic Antarctoperlaria, the monophyly of Eusthenioidea and Gripopterygoidea was well supported, aligning with both morphological and molecular evidence.26,30,32,36,40 Austroperlidae appeared non-monophyletic due to the anomalous positioning of the genera Acruroperla and Crypturoperla.32 However, we cannot conclusively test the monophyly of Austroperlidae due to insufficient sampling.

In a monophyletic Arctoperlaria, Zwick positioned Scopuridae as a basal group, sister to Nemouroidea, within Euholognatha based on morphology.26 However, this relationship has seldom been supported by molecular evidence.30,31,32,61 Contrarily, our study robustly validates this hypothesis. Traditionally, Taeniopterygidae were recognized as the second-diverging branch of Euholognatha based on morphology, with Leuctridae as the sister group to Capniidae, forming the third-diverging clade Leuctroidea.26 Various molecular studies have yielded conflicting results regarding the relationships among these three families.30,31,32,34,40,61 A phylogenomic study involving 1,400 orthologous genes suggested Capniidae as the sister group to Nemouridae rather than the traditionally allied Leuctridae, though the positions of Taeniopterygidae and Leuctridae remain inconclusive.6 Our study not only provided further support for the hypothesis regarding Capniidae proposed by South et al.,6 but also suggests an alternative topology in which Leuctridae clusters with Taeniopterygidae, forming the second-diverging branch within Euholognatha. Notably, the clade Taeniopterygidae + Leuctridae was exclusively recovered under the CAT-GTR+G4 model, while results from other models were similar to previous studies.6,30 Furthermore, Leuctridae were suggested to be polyphyletic due to the artifactual positioning of the subfamily Megaleuctrinae.32,33,34 However, our analysis supports Megaleuctrinae as the sister group to Leuctrinae, consistent with phylogenomic and morphological evidence.6,26,62 The cryptic family Notonemouridae, endemic to the Southern Hemisphere, was identified as the sister group to Nemouridae based on morphology.26 This relationship has been challenging to recover using Sanger sequence datasets due to insufficient informative sites,32,40 yet it has been robustly validated in almost all phylogenetic studies based on mitogenomes.30,31,39,61 As expected, the clade Notonemouridae + Nemouridae was robustly supported in our analyses.

Compared to Euholognatha, the phylogenetic relationships within Systellognatha have gradually become clearer. The basal three families were traditionally grouped into Pteronarcyoidea based on morphological evidence.26 However, the monophyly of Pteronarcyoidea has not been corroborated by molecular data.6,32,50 The phylogenetic relationships within another superfamily, Perloidea, have long been disputed.22,25,26,34 Letsch et al. combined transcriptome and Sanger sequence data to reconstruct the phylogenetic relationships within Systellognatha as (Styloperlidae, (Peltoperlidae, (Pteronarcyidae, (Kathroperlidae, (Perlidae, (Chloroperlidae, Perlodidae)))))).32 This phylogenetic framework for Systellognatha was reproduced by García-Girón et al.40 and further supported by analyzing a dataset with the highest number of orthologous loci used in a phylogeny of Plecoptera to date.6 Our analysis also reproduced this phylogenetic relationship within Systellognatha, providing further support for it.

Status of the recently established family Kathroperlidae

The monophyly of Chloroperlidae and its subfamily Paraperlinae has been challenged by the peculiar position of Kathroperla. This genus has been recovered either as a sister group to Chloroperlidae + Perlodidae or as a sister to the remaining Perloidea, but these relationships lacked consistency in coalescent or concatenated analyses based on 1400 orthologous genes.6 The latter relationship was also produced by Sanger sequence datasets.32,34

A reinvestigation with increased taxon sampling of Kathroperla and Paraperlinae indicated that the genus is indeed sister to the other three perloid families, leading to the establishment of Kathroperlidae.29 Kathroperlidae are distinguished from other perloid families by the postocular head length from the posterior margin of the compound eye to the posterior margin of the head capsule, which is approximately two eye lengths.29 Nevertheless, previous mitogenome-based analyses failed to provide consistent support for the phylogenetic placement of Kathroperlidae. Its position was unstable across different datasets and evolutionary models.50 In our study, the phylogenetic placement of Kathroperlidae as a sister group to the traditional Perloidea was robustly supported, providing compelling evidence from mitochondria.

Mating behavior in Scopuridae

Plecoptera have developed one of the most complex intersexual communication systems among insects, utilizing vibrational signals transmitted through low-frequency substrates such as dried tree bark, leaves, and rocks.63,64 These vibrational signals, produced through drumming by percussion, stridulation, and tremulation, play a crucial role in the mating system of stoneflies for individual search and recognition.64 Although Antarctoperlaria have never been observed using vibrational communication, they may have evolved highly specific encounter-site aggregation behaviors that bring the sexes close enough to facilitate mate-finding.64 Comparisons with outgroups and Antarctoperlaria suggest that the ancestors of stoneflies were non-drummers, and that Arctoperlaria independently developed and refined vibrational communication as part of their mating system.64 Various arctoperlarian groups share a suite of behavioral and morphological traits related to drumming, except for Scopuridae (Table S1), which provides robust support for the monophyly of Arctoperlaria.26,65 The presence of terminal filaments and/or anal gills has led some authors to relate Scopuridae to Austroperlidae, Gripopterygidae, and Peltoperlidae.22,66 In our study, Scopuridae were recovered as the first branch of Euholognatha, suggesting that the absence of drumming-related behaviors and structures in Scopuridae is likely a secondary loss.

It is noteworthy to mention that the absence of morphological adaptations to drumming behavior is not confined to Scopuridae. It can also be found in other arctoperlarian families, including Chloroperlidae, Perlidae (Perlesta), and Perlodidae (Perlodinae, excluding Diploperlini).67,68 This pattern indicates that the loss of drumming behavior has evolved independently multiple times throughout the evolutionary history of Plecoptera. In these non-vibrational species, successful mating depends more heavily on suitable encounter sites where both sexes gather, as well as on visual and tactile cues for mate recognition.69,70 The possible involvement of sexual pheromones in these species warrants further investigation.70

Conclusion

We show a robust, family-level phylogeny of global Plecoptera, encompassing all extant stonefly families. Our analyses resolved the phylogenetic relationships within Systellognatha as follows: (Styloperlidae, (Peltoperlidae, (Pteronarcyidae, (Kathroperlidae, (Perlidae, (Chloroperlidae, Perlodidae)))))). Within Euholognatha, Scopuridae emerged as the earliest diverging lineage, aligning with morphological evidence and suggesting that the absence of mating behavior in this family represents a secondary loss. Notably, we propose a revised relationship for the contentious Leuctridae and Taeniopterygidae, identifying them as sister groups that collectively form the second divergence within Euholognatha. Divergence time estimates indicate that crown-Plecoptera originated during the Pennsylvanian period, with key early divergences occurring from the Cisuralian to the Early Triassic. Our study highlights the utility of mitogenomes in resolving plecopteran phylogeny, particularly when employing the best-fitting CAT-GTR model. Future studies should improve the plecopteran Tree of Life by expanding taxon sampling, incorporating genomic data, and applying evolutionary models capable of addressing compositional heterogeneity, thereby providing deeper insights into the complex evolutionary dynamics of stoneflies.

Limitations of the study

This study provides insights into the phylogeny and temporal framework of Plecoptera using mitogenomes under models accounting for across-site compositional heterogeneity. However, intrinsic limitations of mitochondrial genomes, such as a scarcity of informative sites and elevated evolutionary rates, result in reduced support for some contentious basal nodes within Euholognatha and Systellognatha, and may lead to overestimated divergence times. Consequently, the evolutionary timeline of Plecoptera remains subject to considerable uncertainty. Moreover, the phylogenetic placement of numerous fossil taxa within the order has yet to be formally resolved, rendering many of these fossils unusable, particularly those potentially related to Antarctoperlaria, which further contributes to ambiguity in divergence time estimations. To address these challenges, future research should integrate nuclear genomic data from a global sampling of Plecoptera, clarify and incorporate a broader range of fossil evidence, and employ comprehensive phylogenetic frameworks incorporating multiple models and strategies for molecular dating, thereby enabling a more robust reconstruction of Plecoptera’s evolutionary history.

Method details

Taxon sampling and specimens

Previous mitogenome-based studies have been limited by taxon sampling.30,31,39 Here we achieved full coverage of all stonefly families, including 29 sequenced and 68 published species, representing 65 genera and covering all 17 families (Table S2). It has been observed that almost all polyneopteran groups evolve much faster than Plecoptera at the mitochondrial level.18,30 Therefore, to mitigate long-branch attraction artifacts, three mayflies (Ephemeroptera) were used as the outgroup (Table S2). Additionally, to examine the impact of outgroup selection on the internal relationships of stoneflies, we conducted experiments by varying the outgroups (without outgroups, Dermaptera-only as outgroup, or basal Polyneoptera, Paleoptera, Zygentoma, and Archaeognatha as outgroups), while keeping ingroups consistent. Publicly available mitogenomes were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (available at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). For more details, refer to Table S2. The workflow of this study, from species sampling to divergence time estimation, is shown in Figure S26.

DNA extraction, sequencing and assembly

DNA was extracted from the body of each specimen using the QIAamp DNA Micro Kit (QIAGEN, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The DNA quality was assessed by electrophoresis and NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific). All qualified samples were sequenced on the BGI platform at Berry Genomics (Beijing, China) following a paired-end 150 strategy. Approximately 6G of data were produced for each sample to ensure the minimum sequencing coverage of 10–15X. The sequenced mitogenomes were assembled and annotated by MitoZ v2.471 with default parameters, and then manually checked as previously described.81,82 Unfortunately, the subfamily Megaleuctrinae and family Kathroperlidae currently lacks publicly available mitogenomes. Therefore, we downloaded the transcriptomic raw data of two species, Megaleuctra williamsae (SRR8924835) and M. stigmata (SRR8924885), from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) at NCBI. By referencing the mitochondrial 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs) of closely related species (Leuctra fusca), we annotated the 13 PCGs of these two species utilized Geneious v9.0.272 with default parameters. The whole mitogenome of Kathroperla siskiyou was assembled with the genomic SRA data (SRR13743953) using MitoZ with default parameters. The DNA sequences used in this study were deposited at GenBank (Table S2).

Sequence alignment and trimming

The nucleotide (NT) sequences of 13 PCGs were aligned based on their amino acid (AA) translations using MACSE v2.06, with the Invertebrate Mitochondrial Code (-gc_def 5) applied.73 During the alignment process, no refinement iterations were performed (-max_refine_iter 0). This method yielded the corresponding aligned AA sequences for the 13 PCGs. For the alignment of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes, considering their secondary structure, the Q-INS-i algorithm was applied using the MAFFT v7 online service.74 The AA alignments of the 13 PCGs and the NT sequences of the rRNA were trimmed using trimAl v1.4 with the ‘-automated1’ strategy.75 The trimmed NT sequences of the 13 PCGs were generated based on the backtranslation of the corresponding trimmed AA alignments.

Matrix generation and phylogeny

The third codon positions of PCGs exhibit a higher mutation rate compared to the first and second codon positions, resulting in increased heterogeneity among sites within the sequences.83 Therefore, we excluded the third codon positions from all 13 PCGs in our analysis. Four distinct matrices were constructed for phylogenetic analyses using FASconCAT v1.0476: (ⅰ) Matrix 1 consists of AA sequences of the 13 PCGs, (ⅱ) Matrix 2 includes two rRNAs and the first and second codon positions of the 13 PCGs, (ⅲ) Matrix 3 contains only the first and second codon positions of the 13 PCGs, and (iv) Matrix 4 is generated by removing the outgroups (Ephemeroptera) from Matrix 1. Phylogenetic inferences were performed using IQ-TREE v2.2.2.777 and PhyloBayes MPI v1.9.78 For ML analyses, a partitioning scheme was applied to all four matrices. The optimal partitioning scheme and substitution model were determined using ModelFinder, implemented in IQ-TREE, with the parameter ‘-m MFP+MERGE --msub mitochondrial’.60,84 Additionally, the site-heterogeneous models LG4X+FO+R, mtART+C20+F+G, and mtART+C60+F+G were applied to the ML analysis of Matrix 1. Node support values for all ML trees were calculated with 1,000 bootstrap replicates using the SH-like approximate likelihood ratio test85 and ultrafast bootstrap approximation.86 BI analysis was conducted using the site-heterogeneous infinite mixture model CAT-GTR+G4 for all matrices and the site-homogeneous GTR+G model for Matrix 1. Two independent Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains were run until convergence criteria were met (maxdiff < 0.3 and effsize > 200).78,87 All phylogenetic trees were visualized and edited using FigTree v1.4.4 (https://github.com/rambaut/figtree) and iTOL v6 (available at https://itol.embl.de/).79

Model examination

To estimate the relative fit of alternative models, the LOO-CV score and the wAIC were computed based on post-analysis.88 The alignment of LOO-CV and wAIC scores suggests that wAIC is a reliable approximation of LOO-CV. We calculated ΔCV and ΔwAIC as the difference in estimated predictive performance between the two tested models. Additionally, PPC was performed using PhyloBayes to evaluate the model adequacy employed in our phylogenetic analyses. The p-value indicates the extent to which the model’s predictions align with the observed data. Furthermore, the Z-score quantifies the number of standard deviations by which the observed diversity (i.e., mean number of distinct amino acid residues per site) deviates from the mean predicted diversity.

Fossil calibrations

Eight fossil calibrations were selected based on a combination of phylogenetic, stratigraphic, and geochronological evidence (Methods S1). Each fossil used in the time calibration represents the earliest record of the corresponding clade and is distributed as uniformly as possible throughout the stonefly phylogenetic tree. The evaluation of the fossil calibrations adhered to the criteria established by Parham et al.89 Fossils were chosen based on unambiguous synapomorphies and detailed phylogenetic analyses, which determined the specific nodes calibrated by the respective fossils. The minimum node age was derived from the latest chronostratigraphic evidence, while the soft maximum age was generally constrained subjectively. To avoid the use of arbitrary statistical distributions with uniform maxima, the soft maximum age was defined based on the absence of related clades in well-studied Lagerstätten. This means that corresponding clade members (and the presence of taphonomic, ecological, and/or biogeographic counterparts) are absent from deposits intermediate in age between the minimum and soft maximum constraints. This absence suggests a very low (but nonzero—hence the designation as a ‘soft’ maximum) prior probability that the clade originated before this time. The earliest known convincing pterygote species, Delitzschala bitterfeldensis, appeared in the Upper Mississippian from the upper Serpukhovian of the Bitterfeld/Delitzsch area, Germany.90 The age of the boundary between the Serpukhovian and Bashkirian is 323.23 ± 0.4 Ma,91 thus constraining the maximum age of the root to 322.83 Ma.

Divergence-time estimation

Divergence-time estimation was performed using the approximate likelihood calculation in MCMCTree, implemented in PAML v4.10.6,80 incorporating soft bounds to account for uncertainties in fossil dates for node calibration on the phylogenetic tree.92 Matrix2, which contained more informative sites than the other two matrices, was selected for divergence time estimation. We conducted three independent MCMC chains, and obtained 10,000 samples with a sampling frequency of 50 from each run, discarding the first 10,000 iterations as burn-in (Figures S15–S17; Table S3). Other parameters were set as follows: ‘model = 7’, ‘alpha = 0’, ‘ncatG = 5’, ‘cleandata = 0’, ‘BDparas = 1 1 0’, ‘kappa_gamma = 6 2’, ‘alpha_gamma = 1 1’, ‘rgene_gamma = 2 10 1’, and ‘sigma2_gamma = 1 10 1’. Convergence was tested in Tracer by comparing estimates from three independent chains (Figures S14A‒S14C). Analyses were conducted using the correlated-rates model, which was found fit better than the independent-rates model.93 In all analyses, the soft minimum and maximum bounds were augmented by a 2.5% tail probability (Table S3). To ensure our priors were appropriate, we ran the MCMCTree analysis without sequence data to establish whether the effective priors were compatible with the specified priors (Figures S14D‒S14F).
