Incidental finding of a cardiac paraganglioma following ischemic stroke: A case report
Authors: Mohammed Usman Syed, Tausif Ahmed Siddiqui, Zahin Alam, Pavan K Kottamasu, Peter A Rosella, Brij Reddy, Akm A Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiac paragangliomas are rare neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) that may cause morbidity through mass effect, embolic events, and catecholamine excess. This case report describes a 52-year-old woman with a history of atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response presenting with ischemic stroke. Incidental imaging revealed an 8-cm prevascular mass abutting the left ventricle with regions of central necrosis. Urinary metanephrine testing was elevated, indicating a functional mass. Cu-64 DOTATATE PET/CT confirmed absence of distant metastases and increased radiotracer uptake along the rim of the mass, which supported the diagnosis of a NET. CT-guided percutaneous coaxial core needle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of a paraganglioma. Upon resection, postoperative urinary metanephrine levels normalized. This report highlights the multidisciplinary approach to the management of a cardiac paraganglioma presenting after ischemic stroke.Keywords: Stroke presentation, Cardiac paraganglioma, Urinary metanephrine testing, Coaxial core needle biopsy, Surgical resection

Introduction

Paragangliomas are nonepithelial, neural crest-derived NETs arising from the extra-adrenal paraganglia and are composed of para-neuronal, peptide hormone-secreting neuroendocrine cells [1]. They are classified into 2 major categories: parasympathetic paragangliomas, which predominantly occur in the head and neck, and sympathetic paragangliomas, which are most often located in the retroperitoneum, thorax, and pelvis [2]. These tumors all possess metastatic potential and may arise almost anywhere in the body except within the brain and bones. Therefore, they are described as metastatic or nonmetastatic rather than benign or malignant. Metastases are diagnosed only when tumor deposits occur in locations lacking normal chromaffin tissue, such as the bone, brain, or lymph nodes. Paragangliomas have the highest degree of heritability among all human tumors, with germline mutations present in approximately 40% of cases. They occur in association with several genetic syndromes, including multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) types 1 and 2, von Hippel-Lindau disease, and neurofibromatosis type 1. Mutations in succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) genes can cause hereditary paraganglioma/pheochromocytoma syndrome, which often presents with multifocal tumors [1,3].

Clinically, paragangliomas may be functional or nonfunctional. Functional tumors present with symptoms of catecholamine excess, such as palpitations, hypertension, sweating, headaches, and episodic catecholamine crises. Nonfunctional tumors may manifest with chest pain, fatigue, and shortness of breath, or through mass effect on cardiac structures, leading to compression of chambers, valves, or coronary arteries [4,5]. Diagnosis relies on a combination of imaging and biochemical testing. Echocardiography serves as an initial tool for evaluating suspected cardiac masses, while CT and MRI provide detailed anatomical localization. Functional imaging, such as 123I-MIBG scintigraphy or 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT, can assess tumor activity, and biochemical confirmation is achieved via plasma or urinary catecholamine and metanephrine testing [2,6,7]. Although rare, cardiac paragangliomas account for 1%-3% of primary cardiac tumors, most often arising in the left atrium, followed by the aortic body, interatrial septum, and atrioventricular groove. They are typically highly vascularized, ovoid or round masses that may recruit blood flow from the coronary arteries [[8], [9], [10]]. Ischemic stroke may rarely occur in association with cardiac paraganglioma as a result of catecholamine-induced vasospasm, cardioembolic events, or paradoxical embolism. To our knowledge, only 1 prior case report describes findings of ischemic stroke in association with cardiac paraganglioma [11].

Case report

A 52-year-old woman with a history of hypertension, supraventricular tachycardia, and new-onset atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response (RVR) presented with a BP of 188/124 mmHg, right-sided seizure-like activity without loss of consciousness, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 16, right gaze preference, left homonymous hemianopsia, lower facial weakness, hemiparesis, sensory deficit, and extinction to double simultaneous stimulation. She received IV thrombolytics, labetalol as needed for hypertension, and levetiracetam for seizure concern. CT angiography revealed high-grade narrowing of the A3 segment of the right anterior cerebral artery (ACA) with evidence of acute to subacute ischemic infarction. MRI of the head confirmed parenchymal diffusion restriction with corresponding T2 FLAIR hyperintensity in the right frontal premotor and para-falcine/cingulate regions (Fig. 1). This is consistent with evolving acute-to-subacute multi-territorial ischemic infarction in the right ACA and MCA distributions. The mechanism of stroke was thought to be cardioembolic due to atrial fibrillation. Atorvastatin was initiated for elevated LDL of 129 mg/dL. Metoprolol was initiated for atrial fibrillation rate control. For anticoagulation, she was initially placed on an unfractionated heparin drip, which was transitioned to apixaban after 20 days. Three weeks after initial presentation, her Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score was 4, indicating moderately severe disability. On 6-week follow-up, the patient presented with continued left-sided weakness without additional seizure or stroke symptoms and was in a skilled nursing facility where she participated in physical, occupational, and speech therapy.

Incidental to the neuroimaging workup, a chest radiograph identified a well-circumscribed opacity (6.7 cm) abutting the left heart border. The presence of the hilum overlay sign indicated an anterior or posterior mediastinal mass (Fig. 2A). Follow-up contrast-enhanced chest CT identified an 8-cm prevascular mass abutting the superior aspect of the left ventricle, exhibiting peripheral enhancement with a hypodense center suggestive of necrosis (Fig. 2B). Cardiac MRI further delineated the mass, demonstrating displacement of the left atrial appendage and left superior pulmonary vein, as well as bi-atrial enlargement, with a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (70%) (Fig. 2C). On transthoracic echocardiogram, the extracardiac anterior mediastinal mass was observed alongside bi-atrial enlargement with abnormal diastolic filling and absence of a right-to-left shunt. Urinary testing revealed elevated metanephrines of ∼2 nmol/L (normal <0.5 nmol/L) and elevated normetanephrines of ∼20 nmol/L (normal <0.9 nmol/L). This supported the diagnosis of a functional, catecholamine-secreting mediastinal mass, which contributed to this patient’s hypertension and risk for stroke.

Following treatment with doxazosin, an alpha-1 blocker, coaxial CT-guided core biopsy of the mediastinal mass was carried out (Fig. 2D). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of biopsy specimens was positive for chromogranin, synaptophysin, INSM1, and GATA3. This supported a preoperative diagnosis of cardiac paraganglioma. Cu-64 DOTATATE PET/CT revealed increased radiotracer uptake along the rim of the mass, characteristic of NET somatostatin receptor expression, and the absence of distant metastases (Fig. 2E).

Four months after initial stroke presentation, the patient underwent trans-sternal resection of the mediastinal mass following preoperative alpha-blockade. Intra-operatively, the patient required intravenous nicardipine to control hypertension followed by fluids and pressors for hypotension. Presurgical metanephrine levels were abnormally elevated at 1.2 nmol/L, while postsurgical levels were normal at below 0.5 nmol/L. Blood pressure following resection was 119/78 mmHg. Macroscopic examination of the resection specimen revealed an 8 cm tan-white, solid-and-cystic mass with focal hemorrhage and vascular invasion. Serial sectioning demonstrated a solid component comprising 30%-40% of the mass, with the remainder being cystic. The IHC profile was positive for chromogranin and S100, highlighting focal sustentacular cells that are characteristic of paragangliomas. The staining was negative for pan-cytokeratin, ruling out epithelial cell origin. Eighteen months postresection, structural cardiac MRI revealed normal cardiac function with minimal tricuspid regurgitation and mildly enlarged atria as well as absence of the mediastinal mass, scar, inflammation, or fibrosis. These findings indicate durable control of the cardiac mass with resolution of the left atrial and pulmonary vein displacement.

Discussion

This report highlights the incidental imaging, diagnosis, and treatment of a cardiac paraganglioma following presentation for ischemic stroke. The peripheral enhancement with central necrosis of the mediastinal mass on CT represents a characteristic imaging appearance of cardiac paraganglioma [12]. Given their high vascularity, percutaneous biopsy of paragangliomas is generally discouraged due to the risk of hemorrhage and catecholamine surge [13]. However, biopsy of this mass was safely performed to aid surgical planning following doxazosin treatment to mitigate the effects of catecholamine release. At 18-month follow-up, MRI revealed durable control of the cardiac mass with largely normal cardiac function.

To our knowledge, only 1 prior case report describes cardiac paraganglioma associated with ischemic stroke. In this report, a 58-year-old woman with stage II hypertension presented with exercise-exacerbated acute chest pain and expressive aphasia/dysarthria which resolved after aspirin and unfractionated heparin administration. Brain imaging demonstrated acute ischemic stroke in the left parietal lobe. Elevated cardiac enzymes were consistent with myocardial infarction and echocardiography demonstrated a right atrial mass and patent foramen ovale (PFO). Following trans-sternal resection and PFO closure, histological examination confirmed diagnosis of a cardiac paraganglioma, and the patient underwent uneventful postoperative recovery at 2 months follow-up [11].

In this prior report, the right atrial paraganglioma and PFO were considered the likely source of a paradoxical embolus that resulted in ischemic stroke [11]. This contrasts with the current report which describes a patient with atrial fibrillation, left-sided paraganglioma, bi-atrial enlargement, and the absence of a right-to-left shunt. These findings indicate a left-sided embolus, secondary to atrial fibrillation as the likely embolic source. In addition to causing catecholamine-induced hypertension, the functional cardiac mass may have contributed to further displacing or enlarging the left atrium, exacerbating this patient’s new-onset atrial fibrillation and increasing her risk for embolic stroke. In both cases, postoperative resolution of hypertension and metanephrine levels indicated the cardiac tumors were both functional.

Paragangliomas often display a classic zellballen architecture with chromogranin-positive tumor cells and S100-positive sustentacular cells, as observed in this case [2]. Approximately 25%-40% of paragangliomas harbor germline SDH mutations, which were absent in this case [14]. Importantly, malignant potential cannot be determined by histologic features alone and must be defined by the presence of metastatic disease, which was markedly absent in this patient on Cu-64 DOTATATE PET/CT [15].

Complete surgical resection is the basis of treatment and offers the best opportunity for long-term disease control. Resection is often technically complex due to tumor hypervascularity, proximity to vital cardiac structures, and frequent coronary artery involvement. This often necessitates cardiopulmonary bypass and multidisciplinary surgical planning aided by preoperative biopsy and diagnosis [15,16]. For functional tumors, a 10- to 14-day preoperative treatment with alpha-blockade is used to increase blood volume and mitigate the hypertensive effects of released catecholamines. Subsequently, beta-blockade is used if blood pressure remains uncontrolled or reflex tachycardia develops [16].

Adjunct therapies, including radiotherapy and chemotherapy, have limited and poorly defined roles and are generally reserved for unresectable, recurrent, or metastatic disease [7]. Emerging treatment strategies such as peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) and targeted therapies for SDH-mutated tumors have shown promise in advanced paragangliomas [7,14]. Further advances in genetic screening and molecular profiling are improving risk stratification and guiding surveillance strategies [14]. While prognosis after complete surgical resection is generally favorable and recurrence is rare, long-term monitoring is necessary [9].

Conclusion

This case illustrates the treatment of a functional cardiac paraganglioma incidentally diagnosed on chest imaging following ischemic stroke. Cardiac imaging revealed limited extent of cardiac invasion. Functional imaging confirmed the absence of distant metastases. CT-guided coaxial core needle biopsy allowed preoperative diagnosis of paraganglioma. Following complete surgical resection, the patient’s urinary metanephrine levels normalized. Finally, IHC analysis of the resection specimen confirmed this diagnosis. Continued follow-up to monitor cardiac function and urinary metanephrine levels remains necessary.
