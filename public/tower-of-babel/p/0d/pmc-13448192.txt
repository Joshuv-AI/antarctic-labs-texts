The Spectrum of Kidney Biopsy Findings in Dakota People in the United States
Authors: Mohammed S. Abdalla, Lisa E. Vaughan, Kambiz Kalantari, Lihong Bu, Tasneem I. Musa, Samih H. Nasr
Published: 2026

Introduction

American Indians (AI) experience disproportionately high rates of end-stage kidney disease, with a prevalence 3.5 times greater than white Americans and significant regional and tribal variations.1 Although diabetes accounts for 69% of new end-stage kidney disease cases in AI,2 emerging evidence suggests substantial nondiabetic renal disease (NDRD) burden. The Zuni Kidney Project demonstrated high rates of albuminuria among nondiabetic and hematuria in both diabetic and nondiabetic individuals, indicating significant NDRD prevalence.3 A cross-sectional study across 5 states also found that a higher degree of Indian blood was strongly associated with albuminuria independent of diabetes status, suggesting genetic susceptibility varying by tribal affiliation and geographic region.4 These variations underscore the importance of understanding the complete pathological spectrum of kidney disease in AI populations.

Identifying NDRD in AI, potentially masked by high diabetes prevalence, is essential for developing targeted management strategies. However, studies evaluating the pathological spectrum of kidney disease among AI have primarily focused on southwestern populations and only included small numbers of patients.5 The aims of the study are: (i) describe the pathological spectrum of kidney diseases among AI; and (ii) examine associations among baseline clinical characteristics, laboratory findings, biopsy indications, and likelihood of NDRD diagnosis among AI with diabetes, addressing a critical knowledge gap in this high-risk, understudied population.

Methods, clinical and pathologic definitions, and statistical methods are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Results

Of the 202 native biopsies from AI patients initially identified, 140 (69%) were from patients with diabetes, 136 of which had adequate biopsy. The remaining 62 (29%) biopsies were from nondiabetic patients, 56 of which had adequate biopsy. Of the 136 diabetic biopsies, 76 (56%) showed diabetic nephropathy (DN) alone, 53 (39%) had NDRD + DN, and 7 (5%) had NDRD alone. DN comprised 95% of diagnoses in AI diabetics, higher than that in our control group (58.6%). NDRD was identified in 44% of biopsies from AI diabetics, with coexistent DN in most cases. Discussion

The higher percentage of diabetes among our AI patients who underwent kidney biopsy (69%) than the control Caucasian group (30%) and the general population (24%6) reflects the high diabetes prevalence in the AI population.7 The high frequency of acute tubular injury among AI diabetics may reflect poorer kidney reserve from advanced DN, rendering kidneys more susceptible to hemodynamic insults. This finding is particularly concerning given the high end-stage kidney disease rates in AI, as acute kidney injury episodes are associated with cumulative risk for end-stage kidney disease progression, especially in diabetics.8 Acute kidney injury was more common in our diabetic cohort compared with non-AI diabetic cohorts,6 and the presence of acute kidney injury whether alone or in a background of chronic kidney disease was strongly associated with NDRD on biopsy in our study.

IgA nephropathy was the most common non-DN glomerulopathy in AI, both diabetics and nondiabetics, which aligns with previous reports of high IgA nephropathy prevalence in AI.9 Notably, bacterial infection-related glomerulonephritis was the second most common non-DN glomerulopathy in AI diabetics, accounting for 10% of NDRD (compared with 5% in our control group and 1.6% in general populations6), and the second most common diagnosis in nondiabetic AI (17.9%), suggesting factors beyond diabetes susceptibility contributing to this form of glomerulonephritis. Thus, although the incidence of bacterial infection-related glomerulonephritis in the general population is declining, our data suggest that it remains 1 of the most common forms of glomerulonephritis in AI.

NDRD was diagnosed in nearly half of AI diabetics, with 88.3% having NDRD superimposed on DN versus 11.7% NDRD alone. Although there was a higher rate of NDRD in diabetics in our control group (65.5%), only one-third of NDRD was superimposed on DN. The higher prevalence of DN in AI patients makes clinical suspicion for NDRD particularly challenging because of the potential masking effect of DN. Positive serology and low complement levels predicted NDRD in AIs in our study (largely driven by high bacterial infection-related glomerulonephritis rates with hypocomplementemia), emphasizing the importance of full serologic workup in these patients for diagnosing NDRD. The most common NDRD diagnoses— acute tubular injury, IgA nephropathy, bacterial infection-related glomerulonephritis, membranous nephropathy, and pauci-immune necrotizing and crescentic glomerulonephritis —would yield treatment changes in the vast majority of these patients. Therefore, a low threshold for kidney biopsy in AI patients with diabetes is justifiable after weighing risks and benefits, particularly given the potential for targeted therapies that differ from standard DN management and may improve clinical outcomes in this high-risk population.

A limitation of this biopsy-based study is the selection bias, and thus the findings can only be interpreted as applicable to those patients whom the treating nephrologist would consider candidates for biopsy.
