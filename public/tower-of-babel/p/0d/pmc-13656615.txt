Sequential Talaromyces marneffei and Legionella pneumophila infections leading to the diagnosis of anti–interferon-γ autoantibody–associated immunodeficiency: a case report
Authors: MingJing Yu, YuLing Xiao, Ya Liu, ZhongHao Wang, Haixia Zhou, YongJiang Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOpportunistic infections caused by uncommon intracellular pathogens can serve as important clues to underlying immune dysfunction.Case presentationWe report a 57-year-old male who presented with chronic cough, dyspnea, fever, and weight loss. Chest imaging revealed diffuse pulmonary infiltrates and mediastinal lymphadenopathy, mimicking tuberculosis or lymphoma. Despite empirical anti-tuberculosis and broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, his condition deteriorated. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) and culture confirmed Talaromyces marneffei infection. During antifungal therapy, he developed Legionella pneumophila pneumonia—an unusual sequential infection that raised suspicion of an immune defect. Comprehensive immunological evaluation revealed anti–interferon-γ (anti–IFN-γ) autoantibodies, supporting the diagnosis of AIGA-associated immunodeficiency. The patient had also shown repeatedly indeterminate interferon-γ release assay (IGRA) results, retrospectively suggesting impairment of the IFN-γ pathway.ConclusionsThis case illustrates that recurrent or sequential infections with intracellular pathogens, particularly Talaromyces marneffei and Legionella pneumophila, should prompt evaluation for cell-mediated immunodeficiency such as AIGA syndrome. Early recognition may facilitate tailored antimicrobial therapy, immunological follow-up, and consideration of immunomodulatory treatment in selected patients.Keywords: Anti–IFN-γ autoantibody, Immunodeficiency, Talaromyces marneffei, Legionella pneumophila

Introduction

AIGAs represent an acquired adult-onset immunodeficiency characterized by heightened susceptibility to intracellular pathogens such as non-tuberculous mycobacteria, Salmonella, and Talaromyces marneffei [1]. The condition predominantly affects adults in East and Southeast Asia, with case clusters reported in Thailand, Taiwan, and southern China [2]. Although awareness of this entity has increased, AIGA-associated immunodeficiency remains underrecognized in clinical practice because of its heterogeneous manifestations and the lack of routine immune screening. Affected individuals often present with recurrent or disseminated infections that mimic tuberculosis, malignancy, or autoimmune disease, leading to diagnostic delays or misdiagnosis [3–5].

Pathophysiologically, circulating anti–IFN-γ autoantibodies neutralize interferon-γ, a cytokine essential for macrophage activation, antigen presentation, and intracellular pathogen clearance. [6–7] Neutralization of IFN-γ results in defective Th1-mediated immunity and impaired killing of intracellular microorganisms. Importantly, both Talaromyces marneffei and Legionella pneumophila are facultative intracellular pathogens. Their elimination relies primarily on cell-mediated immunity rather than humoral responses, as antibodies alone cannot effectively eradicate organisms residing within host cells. Activated Th1 lymphocytes secrete IFN-γ to stimulate macrophages, enabling phagolysosomal killing of these pathogens. Consequently, the presence of neutralizing anti–IFN-γ antibodies creates a unique immunologic milieu that permits uncontrolled replication of such intracellular organisms.

Among these opportunistic pathogens, T. marneffei is a dimorphic fungus endemic to Southeast Asia and southern China, whereas L. pneumophila is an environmental Gram-negative bacterium that rarely causes disease in immunocompetent hosts. The sequential occurrence of these two facultative intracellular pathogens in the same patient is exceedingly rare and should raise suspicion of impaired IFN-γ–mediated cellular immunity [8, 9]. Recognizing such infection patterns can therefore provide a critical clue to underlying immune dysregulation even before immunological testing confirms the diagnosis.

Here, we describe a previously healthy adult who sequentially developed disseminated T. marneffei infection followed by L. pneumophila pneumonia. This unusual sequence of intracellular infections prompted a targeted immunologic evaluation, which revealed anti–IFN-γ autoantibodies and supported the diagnosis of AIGA-associated immunodeficiency. This case underscores the importance of considering AIGAs in patients with recurrent or sequential intracellular infections and follows the CARE reporting guidelines.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old male, a former construction worker with a history of heavy smoking and chronic dust exposure, presented with a 10-month history of intermittent cough and dyspnea that had progressively worsened over the preceding two months, accompanied by fever and a 15-kg weight loss. He had no known history of recurrent, severe, or unusual infections before this illness. Initial chest imaging revealed diffuse pulmonary infiltrates, mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy, multiple osteolytic bone lesions, and bilateral pleural effusions, raising suspicion for tuberculosis or malignancy (Fig. 1).

Serial imaging and bronchoscopic findings of the patient. a Initial chest CT and PET/CT at first admission showing multiple pulmonary nodules, pleural effusion, mediastinal lymphadenopathy, and bone destruction (white arrow), raising suspicion for tuberculosis or malignancy. b Follow-up chest CT after the diagnosis of Talaromyces marneffei infection and antifungal treatment. c Chest CT and bronchoscopic findings at second admission, suggestive of a new infectious process. d Chest CT after antibiotic therapy for Legionella pneumophila pneumonia

A comprehensive diagnostic workup was performed, including bronchoscopy, lymph node biopsy, bone marrow aspiration, and multiple microbiological assessments. HIV testing was negative, and there was no history of immunosuppressive medication or autoimmune disease. Despite these evaluations, repeated tuberculosis tests remained inconclusive; therefore, an empirical anti–tuberculosis regimen (HRZE) was initiated. However, the patient’s condition continued to deteriorate, with persistent high-grade fever (40.6 °C) and progressive respiratory distress. A repeat lymph node biopsy revealed reactive hyperplasia with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) positivity but no evidence of malignancy or granulomatous inflammation.

During hospitalization, a soft subcutaneous nodule with surface ecchymosis appeared on the anterior chest wall. Superficial ultrasonography revealed a hypoechoic lesion measuring approximately 52 × 22 × 50 mm beneath the right nipple, suggestive of a subcutaneous abscess. Culture of aspirate obtained from this chest wall lesion confirmed Talaromyces marneffei (Fig. 2). Given that T. marneffei is a facultative intracellular fungus typically affecting immunocompromised hosts, empirical anti-tuberculosis therapy was discontinued after the fungal infection was confirmed. The patient then received amphotericin B at 30 mg once daily as induction antifungal therapy, together with piperacillin/tazobactam 4.5 g every 8 h for broad-spectrum antibacterial coverage. After clinical and radiological improvement, the patient received sequential oral antifungal therapy with voriconazole 200 mg every 12 h for 10 weeks, followed by itraconazole 200 mg once daily until completion of a 12-month total antifungal course.

Approximately one month after discharge, the patient was readmitted with severe respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation. Chest CT demonstrated dense right-lung consolidation and bronchial obstruction. Bronchoscopy revealed extensive airway granulation. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) detected Legionella in sputum with 2,258 sequence reads, and Legionella pneumophila was also detected in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid with 3 sequence reads. Together with the compatible clinical and radiological findings and subsequent improvement after anti–Legionella therapy with moxifloxacin 0.4 g and azithromycin 0.5 g once daily for 3 weeks, these results supported the diagnosis of Legionella pneumophila pneumonia. The patient gradually recovered and was successfully extubated. Hospital water systems, nearby construction sites, and cooling towers were not formally sampled or investigated. However, the case was reviewed in the context of infection-control surveillance, and no concurrent clustered cases or known Legionella outbreak in the ward was identified during the same period. Serum anti–IFN-γ autoantibody was measured using a commercially available human anti–IFN-γ antibody ELISA kit (BY-EH179824; Nanjing Boyan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China), performed in the clinical laboratory of West China Hospital. The assay is a double-antibody sandwich ELISA designed to quantify human anti–IFN-γ antibody in serum, plasma, cell culture supernatant, and tissue samples, with a detection range of 0.3125–10 ng/mL and a reported sensitivity of 0.1 ng/mL. The manufacturer reported intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation of < 10% and < 15%, respectively, and a recovery range of 85%–115%. A manufacturer-defined positivity cutoff or clinical reference interval was not provided; therefore, the result was interpreted in conjunction with the clinical phenotype, repeatedly indeterminate IGRA results, and exclusion of alternative immunodeficiency states. In this patient, the serum anti–IFN-γ autoantibody concentration was 7.96 ng/mL, which was within the upper part of the assay’s quantitative range and supported the presence of circulating anti–IFN-γ autoantibodies. However, this ELISA result should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive evidence of functional neutralization, because the assay does not directly assess IFN-γ–neutralizing activity. Functional validation assays were not available in this case. This pattern suggested functional impairment of the IFN-γ pathway. Together with the compatible opportunistic infections and exclusion of other major immunodeficiency states, these findings supported the diagnosis of AIGA-associated immunodeficiency. Additional evaluation was performed to exclude other major causes of immunodeficiency and autoimmune disease. HIV antibody testing was negative. Peripheral blood analysis showed anemia and thrombocytosis but no marked lymphopenia, with an absolute lymphocyte count of 2.12 × 10⁹/L. Peripheral lymphocyte subsets were measured by flow cytometry, including CD3⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, CD19⁺ B cells, and CD16⁺/CD56⁺ NK cells. TBNK lymphocyte subset analysis showed preserved T-cell and NK-cell compartments, including CD3⁺ T cells of 1503 cells/µL, CD4⁺ T cells of 673 cells/µL, CD8⁺ T cells of 840 cells/µL, and NK cells of 655 cells/µL. The CD19⁺ B-cell percentage was decreased, whereas the absolute CD19⁺ B-cell count remained within the reference range. This finding was not interpreted as clinically meaningful B-cell deficiency, because the absolute B-cell count was preserved; rather, conventional lymphocyte subset analysis mainly served to exclude profound T-cell, NK-cell, or B-cell lymphopenia in this case.

Autoimmune serological tests, including ENA profile, ANCA, anti–dsDNA antibodies, anti–CCP antibodies, anticardiolipin antibodies, anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies, and lupus anticoagulant, were negative. Cervical lymph node pathology showed reactive lymphoid hyperplasia with EBV infection but no evidence of lymphoma, and bone marrow examination did not support hematological malignancy.

After recovery from L. pneumophila pneumonia, the patient was followed clinically. During follow-up, no documented relapse of T. marneffei infection or L. pneumophila pneumonia occurred. Repeat IGRA testing and serial anti–IFN-γ autoantibody measurements were not available, which limited assessment of dynamic IFN-γ pathway recovery or persistent autoantibody burden. However, 10 months later, he developed a herpes-like penile lesion. Biopsy of the lesion showed focal atypical hyperplasia of squamous epithelial cells and dermal infiltration by plasma cells and lymphocytes. Based on the morphological and immunohistochemical findings, the lesion was interpreted as benign lymphoid hyperplasia with Epstein–Barr virus infection, accompanied by increased plasma cells and a partial increase in IgG4-positive plasma cells. The pathological differential diagnosis included IgG4-related disease, plasma cell proliferative disease, and immunodeficiency- or immune dysregulation-related disease. Subsequent testing showed a normal serum IgG4 level, while herpes simplex virus type 2 DNA was positive. No histological evidence of lymphoma or malignant epithelial tumor was identified. Although a direct causal relationship with AIGA-associated immunodeficiency cannot be established, this follow-up finding suggests ongoing immune dysregulation and highlights the need for long-term surveillance for viral reactivation, mucocutaneous lesions, and EBV-associated lymphoproliferative disease in such patients (Tables 1 and 2).

Discussion

This case highlights sequential infections with unusual intracellular pathogens as a key diagnostic clue to anti–IFN-γ autoantibody-associated immunodeficiency. In an apparently immunocompetent adult, disseminated Talaromyces marneffei infection followed by severe Legionella pneumophila pneumonia, together with repeatedly indeterminate IGRA results, prompted targeted immunological evaluation and led to the detection of anti–IFN-γ autoantibodies. These findings suggest that AIGA should be considered when recurrent, disseminated, or sequential intracellular infections occur after HIV infection and iatrogenic immunosuppression have been excluded.

AIGA-associated immunodeficiency is a rare but increasingly recognized form of adult-onset acquired immunodeficiency, reported predominantly in East and Southeast Asia [1, 2]. A recent Thai registry-based cohort of 65 patients showed a mean age of 56.4 years and a female predominance, with opportunistic infections present in most patients at diagnosis and nontuberculous mycobacteria remaining the leading pathogens [10]. This regional clustering suggests that host susceptibility and environmental exposure may both contribute to disease development. Regional clustering may partly reflect host genetic susceptibility, as previous studies have linked neutralizing anti–IFN-γ autoantibodies with HLA-DRB1*16:02/HLA-DQB1*05:02 and related HLA-DR/DQ haplotypes in Asian populations [11, 12]. HLA typing was not performed in our patient, which limits assessment of genetic predisposition. Although the exact trigger remains unclear, genetic predisposition, molecular mimicry, chronic microbial exposure, and dysregulated immune tolerance have been proposed as potential mechanisms for the generation of anti–IFN-γ autoantibodies [1, 13]. The clinical spectrum typically includes recurrent or disseminated infections caused by intracellular organisms, such as nontuberculous mycobacteria, Salmonella, T. marneffei, Burkholderia, Nocardia, and varicella-zoster virus [10, 13, 14]. Although L. pneumophila is also a facultative intracellular pathogen and is therefore biologically plausible in the setting of impaired IFN-γ–mediated immunity, it has rarely been emphasized as a dominant or typical pathogen in AIGA cohorts or major case series. Thus, this case should not be interpreted as evidence that Legionella is a common manifestation of AIGA-associated immunodeficiency. Instead, it highlights that severe or sequential Legionella pneumonia after another opportunistic intracellular infection may serve as an additional clue to IFN-γ axis dysfunction in endemic settings. In southern China, prospective data have further shown that T. marneffei infection in HIV-negative patients is closely associated with anti–IFN-γ autoantibodies, and higher autoantibody titers are linked to more severe and complex disease courses [15].

Diagnostically, the presence of anti–IFN-γ autoantibodies should be interpreted in the context of clinical phenotype and exclusion of alternative immunodeficiency states. AIGA-associated immunodeficiency is usually suspected in adults with recurrent, disseminated, or sequential infections caused by intracellular pathogens, particularly after HIV infection, iatrogenic immunosuppression, hematological malignancy, and major primary immunodeficiency have been reasonably excluded. Detection of anti–IFN-γ autoantibodies by ELISA supports the diagnosis, but ELISA alone does not directly determine whether the antibodies have neutralizing activity. Functional assays, such as assessment of IFN-γ–induced STAT1 phosphorylation, downstream cytokine responses, or other IFN-γ-dependent cellular responses, can provide stronger evidence of neutralization when available. However, such assays are not routinely accessible in many clinical settings. In this context, repeatedly indeterminate IGRA results may provide an important indirect functional clue, especially when the positive control tube also shows absent or markedly reduced IFN-γ response, because this pattern suggests impaired IFN-γ production or signaling rather than a TB-specific immune response [16, 17]. In the present case, the sequential occurrence of disseminated T. marneffei infection and severe L. pneumophila pneumonia, positive anti–IFN-γ autoantibody testing by ELISA, repeatedly indeterminate IGRA results with absent positive-control response, negative HIV testing, preserved conventional T-cell and NK-cell compartments, negative autoimmune serology, and no evidence of hematological malignancy together supported the diagnosis of AIGA-associated immunodeficiency. Building on these observations, this case suggests a practical diagnostic approach for adults with sequential intracellular infections in endemic settings. When an apparently immunocompetent adult presents with recurrent, disseminated, or sequential infections caused by intracellular pathogens, clinicians should first exclude common secondary immunodeficiency states, including HIV infection, iatrogenic immunosuppression, hematological malignancy, organ transplantation, and profound lymphocyte subset abnormalities. IGRA results should then be reviewed carefully. An indeterminate IGRA with absent or markedly reduced mitogen/positive-control response should not be dismissed as a nonspecific technical failure; in the appropriate clinical context, it may serve as a bedside clue to IFN-γ axis dysfunction. Anti–IFN-γ autoantibody testing should therefore be considered, with functional validation assays performed when available. Confirmed or strongly suspected cases require prolonged pathogen-directed therapy, individualized secondary prophylaxis, and long-term surveillance for relapse, new opportunistic infections, viral reactivation, and possible need for immunomodulatory treatment. Recent case reports have also emphasized that AIGA may initially present as occult immune dysfunction with sequential intracellular infections, reinforcing the need to consider this diagnosis even when conventional lymphocyte subset analysis is not markedly abnormal [18].

Management of AIGA-associated immunodeficiency requires both pathogen-directed antimicrobial therapy and consideration of the underlying immune dysregulation. During acute infection, antifungal or antibacterial therapy remains the cornerstone of treatment; however, antimicrobial therapy alone may be insufficient in patients with persistent, relapsing, disseminated, or life-threatening infections. In such situations, immunomodulatory therapy may be considered, particularly when infections recur despite appropriate antimicrobial treatment, when prolonged antimicrobial therapy is required, or when anti–IFN-γ autoantibodies persist. Reported immunomodulatory strategies include rituximab, cyclophosphamide, and, in refractory cases, other B-cell- or plasma-cell-directed therapies, with the aim of reducing pathogenic autoantibody production and restoring IFN-γ-mediated host defense [10]. Nevertheless, standardized treatment criteria remain lacking, and immunomodulatory therapy should be individualized according to infection severity, disease activity, autoantibody burden, treatment response, and the risk of treatment-related immunosuppression. Long-term management should also include prevention of relapse and surveillance for new opportunistic infections. Because standardized prophylactic strategies for AIGA-associated immunodeficiency have not been established, prophylaxis should be individualized according to the causative pathogens, extent of disease, treatment response, and immune status. For patients with disseminated T. marneffei infection, prolonged antifungal therapy and secondary prophylaxis may be considered, particularly when immunodeficiency persists or immunomodulatory therapy is planned. Follow-up should include monitoring for recurrent fever, respiratory symptoms, lymphadenopathy, cutaneous or subcutaneous lesions, radiological relapse, drug toxicity, and emergence of additional opportunistic infections. In selected patients, repeated assessment of anti–IFN-γ autoantibody levels and multidisciplinary follow-up may help guide the duration of antimicrobial therapy and the need for immunomodulatory treatment. In addition, recent reports suggest that long-term follow-up should include surveillance not only for recurrent opportunistic infections but also for EBV-associated lymphoproliferative disease or lymphoma in selected patients [19, 20].

This case has several clinical implications. First, AIGA-associated immunodeficiency should be considered when an apparently immunocompetent adult develops recurrent, disseminated, or sequential infections caused by intracellular pathogens, particularly after HIV infection and iatrogenic immunosuppression have been excluded. Second, indeterminate IGRA results should not be dismissed as merely technical or nonspecific, especially when the mitogen response is profoundly impaired; in the appropriate clinical context, this finding may provide an early clue to IFN-γ pathway dysfunction. Third, uncommon infection patterns should prompt multidisciplinary evaluation involving infectious disease specialists, immunologists, pulmonologists, and clinical microbiologists. From an infection-control perspective, L. pneumophila pneumonia should prompt review of possible environmental exposure, particularly when hospital-acquired infection or clustered cases are suspected. In this case, no concurrent clustered cases or known Legionella outbreak was identified in the ward during the same period. Therefore, the infection was interpreted primarily as reflecting individual susceptibility in the setting of impaired cell-mediated immunity rather than an increased person-to-person transmission risk. This report is limited by the absence of a formal functional neutralization assay for anti–IFN-γ autoantibodies, such as IFN-γ–induced STAT1 phosphorylation or downstream cytokine-response testing. Therefore, the diagnosis was based on the compatible clinical phenotype, positive anti–IFN-γ autoantibody testing by ELISA, repeatedly indeterminate IGRA results suggesting functional impairment of the IFN-γ pathway, and exclusion of alternative immunodeficiency states. The single-case design also precludes causal inference regarding the association between AIGA and L. pneumophila infection.

The follow-up course was also informative. Although no recurrent pneumonia was documented after discharge, the patient developed an HSV-2–positive genital lesion with EBV-positive benign lymphoid hyperplasia 10 months later. This finding cannot be attributed directly to AIGA, but it is consistent with the broader concept that impaired IFN-γ–mediated immunity may be accompanied by persistent immune dysregulation and susceptibility to viral reactivation or lymphoid proliferative lesions [13]. Therefore, long-term follow-up of AIGA-associated immunodeficiency should include surveillance not only for recurrent fungal, mycobacterial, or bacterial infections, but also for mucocutaneous viral disease, EBV-associated lymphoproliferative disorders, and malignancy [19].

Conclusion

This case underscores the critical importance of evaluating underlying immunodeficiency in patients who present with recurrent or disseminated opportunistic infections. Indeterminate results on TB-IGRAs should alert clinicians to the possibility of AIGA, particularly when infections involve intracellular pathogens. The sequential occurrence of T. marneffei infection and L. pneumophila pneumonia in this patient highlights a potential but rarely reported infectious pattern associated with AIGA. Prompt recognition of this condition is clinically important because affected patients may require prolonged pathogen-directed therapy, individualized secondary prophylaxis, close follow-up for relapse or new opportunistic infections, and, in selected cases, immunomodulatory treatment.
