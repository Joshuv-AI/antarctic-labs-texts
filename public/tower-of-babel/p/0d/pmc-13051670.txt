Long-term control of grade 3 intracranial solitary fibrous tumor achieved with pazopanib and stereotactic radiotherapy: illustrative case
Authors: Mahiro Totani, Takenori Kato, Kyoko Kuwabara, Masayoshi Hasegawa, Toshinori Hasegawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDIntracranial solitary fibrous tumors (SFTs) are rare mesenchymal neoplasms that often recur and metastasize. No standard therapy exists for patients with multiple extracranial metastases.OBSERVATIONSA 56-year-old man with a left cerebellopontine angle tumor initially treated with Gamma Knife radiosurgery (GKRS) later underwent subtotal resection, confirming WHO grade 2 SFT. Despite repeated GKRS, he developed multiple intracranial and extracranial metastases; subsequent histopathological analysis confirmed progression to grade 3. Pazopanib was initiated for rapidly progressive bone and visceral disease and maintained at 200–400 mg daily. Marked responses were observed in lung, liver, and pancreatic metastases, with reduced development of new bone metastasis, requiring fewer stereotactic body radiotherapy courses. Intracranial disease progressed, necessitating two additional GKRS sessions. The patient survived 26 months after pazopanib initiation and approximately 8 years from diagnosis.LESSONSPazopanib demonstrates site-dependent efficacy in metastatic CNS SFT, with marked response in visceral metastases and reduced progression of bone metastases; however, a limited effect on intracranial disease is observed, requiring continued stereotactic irradiation. Solitary fibrous tumor (SFT) is a rare mesenchymal neoplasm accounting for 0.5%–1% of CNS tumors.1 Historically, SFT and hemangiopericytoma (HPC) were regarded as separate entities. However, the discovery of the NAB2-STAT6 gene fusion as a shared genetic hallmark led to their unification in the 2016 WHO classification of CNS tumors under the designation “solitary fibrous tumor/hemangiopericytoma.”2 In the 2021 WHO CNS classification, the term “hemangiopericytoma” was retired, and the entity is now simply referred to as “solitary fibrous tumor,” graded 1–3.3

WHO grade 3 SFT exhibits aggressive behavior, with high recurrence rates (20%–70%) and a propensity for extracranial metastasis (approximately 28% of cases), most commonly involving the lungs, liver, and bones. While resection and adjuvant radiotherapy remain the standard treatments for localized disease, no established therapy exists for patients with multiple extracranial metastases.4 Conventional chemotherapy, including doxorubicin-based regimens, has shown limited efficacy.5

Pazopanib, a multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor targeting VEGFR, PDGFR, and FGFR, has demonstrated promising activity in systemic SFT.6 However, its efficacy in WHO grade 3 CNS SFT with extensive metastases remains largely unknown. We report a case of long-term disease control achieved using pazopanib combined with stereotactic radiotherapy.

Illustrative Case

In October 2013, a 56-year-old Japanese man presented with progressive left-sided hearing impairment. MRI revealed a homogeneously enhancing, well-circumscribed, dura-based mass in the left cerebellopontine angle, measuring approximately 3 cm in diameter (Fig. 1A). The imaging characteristics—homogeneous enhancement and broad dural attachment—were most consistent with meningioma, while the features were atypical for vestibular schwannoma. Based on the presumptive diagnosis of meningioma, progressive symptoms attributable to the tumor, the patient’s preference to avoid surgery, and a tumor size amenable to radiosurgery with potential for hearing preservation, Gamma Knife radiosurgery (GKRS) was performed in February 2014 with a marginal dose of 13 Gy. Follow-up imaging confirmed early and marked tumor reduction, with the maximal shrinkage observed at 11 months (Fig. 1B). Although this rapid and pronounced shrinkage was atypical for meningioma, the near-complete response left no viable target for biopsy, and the patient was followed with serial imaging. However, tumor regrowth was noted in July 2016 and was accompanied by progressive dysgeusia and aural fullness (Fig. 1C).

Given the unusual clinical course and the need for a definitive diagnosis, resection was performed in September 2016. The tumor extended from the extradural to the intradural space. A planned subtotal resection strategy was undertaken; complete resection of the intradural component was achieved, while the extradural tumor within the petrous bone was intentionally left unresected to minimize surgical morbidity (Fig. 1D). Histopathological examination revealed an SFT with increased mitotic activity (≥ 5 mitoses per 10 high-power fields) without necrosis, and an MIB-1 labeling index of approximately 13%. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated strong nuclear STAT6 positivity, confirming the diagnosis. These findings were consistent with WHO grade 2 SFT (Fig. 2A–D). As planned, postoperative fractionated GKRS (second GKRS) was administered to the residual tumor in the petrous bone beginning in October 2016 (27 Gy in 3 fractions at 2-week intervals). Local control was excellent; however, multiple distant intracranial metastases developed in March 2019, approximately 5 years after the initial treatment. A third GKRS was performed to target the intracranial metastases (marginal dose of 16 Gy). Concurrent systemic imaging revealed extracranial metastases involving the cervical spine, thoracic vertebrae, ribs, and lungs.

Following the discovery of systemic diseases, local therapies were sequentially administered to symptomatic or progressive lesions. Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) was delivered to rib and sternal metastases in April 2019 (30 Gy in 10 fractions) and to cervical spine metastases (C5–7) in July 2019 (30 Gy in 10 fractions) (Fig. 3A and B). The subcutaneous scalp tumor was resected in August 2019. Histopathological examination revealed necrosis in addition to increased mitotic activity (≥ 5 mitoses per 10 high-power fields), meeting the criteria for WHO grade 3 SFT. The MIB-1 labeling index increased by approximately 20% (Fig. 2E and F). The fourth GKRS was performed in September 2019 for the new intracranial lesions (a marginal dose of 16 Gy). By November 2019, the disease progression had accelerated markedly. Bone metastases appeared at an increasing rate, requiring four separate SBRT courses in the preceding 9 months. Visceral metastases involving the lungs, liver, and pancreas emerged and progressively enlarged (Fig. 4A, B, D, E, G, and H). As local therapy alone became impractical, pazopanib was initiated at 800 mg daily. The dose was reduced to 400 mg after 2 weeks because of fatigue and dizziness and further reduced to 200 mg after 4 weeks due to persistent diarrhea. The dose was subsequently maintained at 200–400 mg depending on tolerability, without treatment interruption. Pazopanib demonstrated marked efficacy against extracranial disease. Follow-up imaging showed a significant reduction in pulmonary, hepatic, and pancreatic metastases (Fig. 4C, F, and I), and the rate of new bone metastasis development substantially decreased, with only three SBRT courses required during 26 months of pazopanib therapy. However, intracranial disease continued to progress despite pazopanib treatment, necessitating a fifth GKRS at 6 months (May 2020; Fig. 1E) and a sixth GKRS at 14 months (January 2021; Fig. 1F) after pazopanib initiation, treating 26 lesions (18 Gy marginal dose each). SBRT remained necessary for a symptomatic left ischial metastasis in December 2020 (30 Gy in 10 fractions) (Fig. 3E and F), as well as for right iliac bone and right scapular lesions in April 2021.

Despite ongoing pazopanib therapy, progressive cervical and upper thoracic spinal metastases developed at new sites in June 2021. An intradural extramedullary cervical lesion resulted in quadriparesis and bladder bowel dysfunction (Fig. 3G). Palliative low-dose radiation (3 Gy in 5 fractions) was administered to the cervical spine. The patient elected home hospice care with continuation of pazopanib therapy. He died peacefully at home in January 2022 due to disease progression, approximately 8 years after the initial diagnosis and 26 months after the initiation of pazopanib therapy.

Discussion

Observations

This case demonstrates the site-dependent efficacy of pazopanib in WHO grade 3 intracranial SFT with extensive metastases. The visceral metastases showed a marked and durable response to pazopanib alone. Bone metastases exhibited reduced progression, with the frequency of SBRT decreasing from four courses in the 9 months before pazopanib initiation to three courses over 26 months during therapy. Notably, this reduction occurred despite histological confirmation of grade progression, suggesting that pazopanib provided benefits even against more aggressive diseases. In contrast, intracranial metastases demonstrated minimal response, requiring two additional GKRS sessions despite ongoing pazopanib therapy. This differential response pattern informed a multimodal treatment strategy combining systemic therapy with repeated local interventions.

When widespread metastases develop, particularly when lesions are numerous or anatomically unsuitable for local therapies, no established systemic treatment exists.7 Standard soft tissue sarcoma chemotherapy regimens, including doxorubicin, gemcitabine-docetaxel, and paclitaxel, have demonstrated limited efficacy in SFT.8 Combination therapy with temozolomide and bevacizumab has shown activity in HPC/SFT;9 however, evidence supporting its use in CNS SFT with extensive extracranial metastases remains sparse.

Pazopanib is a multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor targeting VEGFR-1/-2/-3, PDGFR-α/-β, and FGFR-1/-3. The PALETTE phase 3 trial established pazopanib as an effective agent for advanced nonadipocytic soft tissue sarcoma, demonstrating significantly prolonged progression-free survival compared to placebo (median 4.6 vs 1.6 months).10 In 2019, a dedicated phase 2 trial further demonstrated pazopanib activity specifically in malignant and de-differentiated SFT, with an overall response rate of 51% by Choi criteria and a median progression-free survival of 5.6 months.11 Additionally, limited but encouraging data suggested pazopanib activity in CNS SFT/HPC, including a case report of successful pazopanib use in refractory metastatic HPC12 and a report of two intracranial SFT/HPC cases showing partial responses.13 At the time of pazopanib initiation in our patient (November 2019), this collective evidence—established efficacy in soft tissue sarcoma, demonstrated activity in malignant SFT, and early reports of responses in CNS SFT/HPC—provided the rationale for selecting pazopanib as systemic therapy for rapidly progressive multiorgan metastatic disease that was no longer amenable to local therapy alone.

Subsequent evidence has further supported the use of pazopanib in SFT. A second cohort analysis from the same phase 2 trial demonstrated a 58% overall response rate and a median progression-free survival of 9.8 months in SFT,14 and additional case series have reported pazopanib use in CNS SFT.15,16 To contextualize our findings within the emerging literature, we reviewed all reported cases of CNS SFT treated with pazopanib (Table 1).12,13,15–17 Including our case, 9 patients have been reported. The median age was 52 years (range 31–60 years), with 5 female and 4 male patients. Among the cases with documented WHO grades, 4 were grade 3 and 3 were grade 2. Most patients (7/9, 78%) had metastatic disease at the time of pazopanib initiation. Pazopanib dosing ranged from 200 to 800 mg daily, with treatment duration ranging from 2 to 64 months (median 11.5 months). Treatment response was assessed using RECIST (Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors) or Choi criteria.18 The Choi criteria, originally developed to evaluate imatinib response in gastrointestinal stromal tumors, incorporate both tumor size and CT density changes; a partial response is defined as a ≥ 10% decrease in tumor size or ≥ 15% decrease in tumor density.19 These criteria may be more sensitive for detecting responses to anti-angiogenic therapies, which can induce tumor necrosis and density reduction without substantial size change.18,19 By RECIST criteria, the best overall response included partial response in 2 patients (29%), stable disease in 2 (29%), and progressive disease in 1 (14%). By the Choi criteria, a partial response was observed in 2 patients and stable disease in 2.

Notably, a consistent pattern of site-dependent differential efficacy was observed among patients with documented disease sites. Intracranial progression occurred in 3 of 5 evaluable patients despite ongoing pazopanib therapy, while extracranial progression occurred in none of the 5 evaluable patients. Although limited by small numbers and incomplete reporting, this pattern supports the site-dependent response observed in our case and suggests that intracranial disease requires dedicated local treatment strategies, even during effective systemic therapy.

Our patient achieved 26 months of overall survival after pazopanib initiation, representing one of the longest reported durations among CNS SFT patients treated with pazopanib. However, progression-free survival varied markedly by disease site: visceral metastases showed durable control until death, skeletal metastases developed at a reduced rate and were managed with repeated SBRT, and intracranial disease continued to progress, requiring two additional GKRS sessions during pazopanib therapy (six sessions in total over the clinical course). This prolonged survival was achieved through individualized dose adjustment (200–400 mg daily) combined with aggressive local therapies targeting new or progressive lesions at isolated sites, while systemic disease remained otherwise controlled. These findings are consistent with the treatment rationale established prior to pazopanib initiation and with subsequent reports demonstrating differential site-dependent efficacy in CNS SFT.

Common adverse events in our patients and in reported cases included gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhea and nausea), which were generally manageable with dose reduction.14 More serious adverse events were uncommon, supporting the tolerability of long-term pazopanib therapy.

Collectively, these findings suggest that pazopanib provides meaningful disease control for extracranial metastases in CNS SFT but has limited efficacy against intracranial disease, necessitating continued local treatment strategies even during systemic therapy.

This differential efficacy, also reported in other CNS SFT cases treated with pazopanib, suggests that organ-specific microenvironments or tumor biological heterogeneity influence treatment sensitivity.16,17 One hypothesis is that limited blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration contributes to reduced intracranial efficacy; preclinical studies demonstrate that pazopanib brain distribution is constrained by efflux transporters (P-glycoprotein and breast cancer resistance protein).20 However, because some CNS SFT cases have shown significant intracranial responses to pazopanib, BBB permeability alone cannot fully explain this variable efficacy.15 Other factors, such as tumor biological characteristics, including potential clonal divergence between intracranial and extracranial lesions, may also contribute to differential responses.

Long-term control was achieved through an integrated multimodal strategy, not pazopanib monotherapy alone, but combined with repeated local interventions. Intracranial progression was managed with sequential GKRS, and symptomatic skeletal metastases were controlled using SBRT. This approach of treating progressive sites with focal therapies while continuing effective systemic treatment may apply to other patients with metastatic CNS SFT.

Dose reduction from the standard 800 mg daily to 200–400 mg maintained therapeutic efficacy for visceral and skeletal metastases, suggesting that individualized adjustment based on tolerability may be appropriate without compromising benefits. This experience may be particularly relevant for Asian patients in whom lower doses of tyrosine kinase inhibitors are sometimes better tolerated.21 This approach enabled the patient to maintain a quality of life sufficient for home-based hospice care during the final months.

This case highlights important diagnostic considerations for CNS SFT. The initial decision to perform GKRS without tissue diagnosis was based on imaging findings strongly suggestive of meningioma—a clinical scenario in which primary radiosurgery without prior biopsy is a widely accepted practice.22–27 The tumor’s homogeneous enhancement, dura-based attachment, and cerebellopontine angle location were all consistent with meningioma. However, the rapid and near-complete tumor shrinkage within 11 months was atypical for meningioma, which generally shows gradual reduction over years following GKRS. When subsequent tumor regrowth occurred, the combination of this atypical initial response and unexpected recurrence raised concern for an alternative diagnosis, prompting resection that ultimately revealed WHO grade 2 SFT. The WHO grading system for CNS SFT is based on mitotic activity and necrosis: grade 1 (< 5 mitoses per 10 high-power fields), grade 2 (≥ 5 mitoses without necrosis), and grade 3 (≥ 5 mitoses with necrosis).3 Our initial resection specimen demonstrated increased mitotic activity (≥ 5 mitoses per 10 high-power fields) without necrosis and an MIB-1 labeling index of approximately 13%, consistent with grade 2. Preoperative differentiation between intracranial SFT and meningiomas is challenging due to overlapping imaging features, including dural attachment, homogeneous enhancement, and similar signal characteristics on MRI.28 Although our initial management was reasonable given the presumed diagnosis, this case underscores that unexpected tumor regrowth following radiosurgery for a presumed meningioma should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis and tissue sampling. Earlier histological confirmation might have facilitated more timely surveillance for extracranial metastases, given the known propensity of SFT for delayed distant dissemination.

Importantly, metastatic lesions resected in August 2019 showed progression to grade 3, defined by the appearance of tumor necrosis in combination with high mitotic activity (≥ 5 mitoses per 10 high-power fields). The increased MIB-1 labeling index (approximately 20% vs 13% in the initial specimen) further supports more aggressive tumor biology. This grade progression suggests possible clonal evolution during disease progression and may partly explain the accelerated pace of metastasis development thereafter.29 This observation emphasizes obtaining tissue confirmation in metastatic sites when feasible, as tumor biology may evolve and influence treatment decisions.

Despite excellent local control of the primary tumor, metastases appeared 61 months after initial therapy. Even in apparently well-controlled cases, CNS SFT can exhibit delayed systemic dissemination, necessitating long-term surveillance including regular chest, abdomen, and pelvis imaging, particularly in grade 2 or 3 disease.30 In this case, brain MRI was performed every 3–4 months throughout the clinical course, and systemic CT imaging was initiated every 2–3 months following the detection of extracranial metastases in 2019.

Pazopanib initiation was prompted by accelerated bone metastases requiring increasingly frequent radiotherapy and progressive visceral disease, which is a practical threshold when local therapies become insufficient. For CNS SFT, intracranial disease should be managed primarily with local therapies even during systemic treatment.31 The approach is systemic therapy to control extracranial disease while continuing local therapy for intracranial lesions; systemic therapy should not replace local treatment.

This study has several limitations. First, it represents a single case, which limits generalizability. Second, the optimal pazopanib dose and duration for CNS SFT remain undefined, although phase 2 data support its activity in advanced SFT.14 Third, we did not directly assess BBB penetration or intratumoral drug concentrations; thus, the proposed explanation for limited intracranial efficacy remains speculative, although consistent with preclinical pharmacokinetic data.20 Fourth, the mechanisms underlying tumor grade progression remain unexplored, although clonal evolution and treatment-induced selection are plausible explanations.20,29 Finally, we did not compare pazopanib with other systemic options reported for SFT, such as temozolomide plus bevacizumab, so relative efficacy cannot be inferred.9

Lessons

This case demonstrates that metastatic WHO grade 3 CNS SFT requires site-specific multimodal treatment strategies. Intracranial metastases show limited response to pazopanib and require repeated stereotactic radiosurgery. The skeletal metastases were managed with SBRT combined with pazopanib. Visceral metastases showed a marked response to pazopanib alone. Long-term surveillance is essential, as distant metastases may develop many years after the initial treatment.
