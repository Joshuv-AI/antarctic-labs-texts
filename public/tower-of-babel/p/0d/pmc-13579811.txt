The effect of TIPS on the development of hepatocellular carcinoma in patients with cirrhosis: a meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaotong Xu, Minjie Jiang, Yunlai Fu, Qinghua Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver cirrhosis is a leading cause of global liver-related morbidity and mortality, with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) being the most common and fatal complication. Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) is a minimally invasive treatment for ascites and gastrointestinal bleeding in patients with cirrhosis. Whether transjugular intrahepatic portalsystemic shunt (TIPS) increases the incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in patients with cirrhosis remains controversial. This study aimed to explore the association between TIPS and the development of HCC.MethodsWe systematically researched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases from their inception until March 7, 2023, to identify eligible studies. The incidence of HCC was extracted and analyzed using a random-effects in RevMan. The primary outcome was the cumulative incidence of HCC, and secondary outcomes included subgroup HCC risk by etiology, stent type, follow-up duration, and overall survival. This study was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023442262).ResultsTen studies comprising 2753 patients were included. Among the 1070 patients who received TIPS, 11.31% (121/1070 ) developed HCC, compared with 8.85% (149/1683) developed HCC (8.85%) in 1683 non-TIPS patients. TIPS did not increase the incidence of HCC [OR = 1.16, 95%CI (0.76,1.77), P = 0.51, I²=50%]. We then performed the analysis according to etiology and stent type. In studies primarily focused on alcoholic liver disease, TIPS did not increase the incidence of HCC [OR = 1.07 95% CI (0.61,1.88), P = 0.82, I²=67%]. Comparable results were observed in studies predominantly using bare stents [OR = 1.52, 95% CI (0.98,2.37), P = 0.06, I²=12%]. Furthermore, TIPS did not increase the cumulative incidence of cancer in 1 year [OR = 0.73 95%CI (0.20,2.69), P = 0.63, I²=74%] and 5 years [OR = 0.99 95% CI (0.52,1.87), P = 0.96], nor did it affect the survival rate [OR = 1.22,95% CI (0.65,2.32), P = 0.53, I²=44%].ConclusionsTIPS is not associated with an increased risk of HCC development in cirrhosis patients. Clinicians can safely select TIPS as a treatment option for cirrhotic patients with portal hypertension, without excessive concern about an elevated risk of HCC associated with the procedure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05027-6.Keywords: Liver cirrhosis, Transjugular intrahepatic portalsystemic shunt, Hepatocellular carcinoma

Introduction

Liver cirrhosis is a leading cause of global liver-related morbidity and mortality, with portal hypertension being its most common and debilitating complication. Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) has emerged as a minimally invasive treatment for portal hypertension-related complications, including acute variceal gastrointestinal bleeding and refractory ascites, significantly improving patients’ quality of life and short-term survival outcomes [1, 2]. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most frequent and fatal malignant complication of cirrhosis, accounting for the majority of deaths in patients with end-stage liver disease. As such, the oncological safety of TIPS—specifically whether the procedure alters hepatic hemodynamics and increases the risk of postoperative HCC development—has become a core concern for both clinicians and patients, with direct implications for clinical decision-making and postoperative patient monitoring.

While TIPS has been widely adopted in clinical practice, its long-term safety profile remains incompletely characterized. Known postoperative adverse events of TIPS include abdominal hemorrhage, biliary tract injury, hepatic encephalopathy, and stent occlusion, all of which have been well documented in previous studies [3]. However, the association between TIPS placement and HCC development has remained highly controversial for decades, with conflicting findings reported in both preclinical and clinical research. A retrospective study on liver transplantation in patients with the congenital extrahepatic portosystemic shunt (CEPS) showed that abnormal portal vein blood flow in CEPS may induce the formation of some hepatic cell nodules with malignant potential [4, 5]. In a murine portosystemic shunt model, large hepatic nodules were observed to form along the trajectory of portal vein shunting, providing a potential mechanistic basis for the hypothesis that TIPS may promote hepatocarcinogenesis [6].

Concurrently, an early study [7] reported that the incidence of HCC after TIPS was three times higher than in non-TIPS patients (RR = 3.28, 95% CI 1.52–7.45). However, another study [8] reported that TIPS did not increase the risk of HCC in cirrhosis patients with Child-Pugh A or B. On the contrary, a recent study [9] demonstrated a significant correlation between TIPS and a reduced HCC incidence in end-stage liver disease patients (OR = 0.35, 95% CI 0.163–0.746). HCC is a complication of cirrhosis and one of the leading causes of mortality in end-stage liver disease patients. Owing to the limited number of studies and inconsistent findings on the association between TIPS and HCC, a published meta-analysis [10] has shown that TIPS is not associated with a an increased risk of HCC in cirrhotic patients.

Despite the growing body of research on this topic, critical gaps and unresolved inconsistencies remain in the existing literature, justifying the need for the present study. In this study, we included more research analyzing the impact of TIPS on the incidence of HCC in patients with different stent types and liver disease etiologies, as well as the impact of TIPS on postoperative survival and cumulative HCC incidence, in order to provide reference for clinical monitoring of patients after TIPS.

Methods

This systematic review was designed and reported in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‑Analyses) guidelines to ensure transparent, complete, and reproducible reporting of the review process. A PRISMA flow diagram and checklist are provided to document the study selection process and reporting completeness. This meta-analysis was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42023442262).

Retrieval strategy

Comprehensive searches were performed in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane databases using a combining of MESH theme terms (“portal system shunt, intrahepatic jugular vein” and “cancer, liver cells”) and free-text words. The subject headings and their corresponding free-text terms were linked with the Boolean operator “OR”, while the remaining search terms were combined using “AND” (as shown in Supplementary materials). Data were retrieved from the earliest accessible date in the database until March 7, 2023 and references were also manually searched.

Inclusion criteria

The experimental group consisted of patients with portal hypertension who underwent TIPS treatment. The control group was portal hypertension patients who did not receive TIPS treatment. Eligible studies were required to report information regarding HCC incidence during patient follow-up.

Exclusion criteria

Repeated publications, case reports, reviews, meta-analysis, unrelated literature, and animal experiments were excluded. Studies involving patients with malignancies prior to TIPS, or those that failed to provide relevant data, were also excluded.

Literature screening and quality assessment

All study screening was independently performed by at least two trained reviewers, with any discrepancies resolved by consensus or a third senior reviewer to minimize selection bias. The search process was shown in Fig. 1. The methodological quality of all 10 included cohort studies was independently assessed by at least two trained reviewers using the validated Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for non-randomized observational studies [11]. The NOS evaluates study quality across three core domains: participant selection, comparability of the TIPS and control groups, and ascertainment of the incident HCC outcome., as shown in Table 1. There was no quality assessment for studies with only abstracts.

Data extraction and analysis

Core variables extracted included study characteristics (first author, publication year, country, study design), participant baseline clinical features, TIPS intervention details, and incident HCC outcome data, as shown in Table 2. Statistical analysis was performed in RevMan using a random-effects model Stata 17.0. Publication bias was assessed for all primary outcome measures in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, using both visual and quantitative statistical methods. Visual assessment was performed using funnel plots. Quantitative statistical assessment was conducted using two validated tests: Egger’s linear regression test and Begg’s rank correlation test. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for the presence of publication bias.

Results

A total of 10 studies met the inclusion and exclusion criteria, comprising 2 randomized controlled trials [15, 17], 5 observational studies [7–9, 14, 18], and 3 conference abstracts [12, 13, 16]. The majority of the studies were of good quality, whereas the three abstracts did not provide adequate data for full assessment. Begg’s rank correlation test (P = 0.31) and Egger’s linear regression test (intercept P = 0.81) suggested no significant publication bias in this meta-analysis.

The incidence of HCC

A total of 10 studies, involving 2753 patients, were included to compare HCC incidence between TIPS and alternative treatments. There was no significant difference between the two groups according to the research results [OR = 1.16, 95%CI (0.76,1.77), P = 0.51]. But there was heterogeneity (I²=50%), as shown in Fig. 2. A study [9] was considered to be a source of heterogeneity due to significant differences in follow-up duration between the two groups, and heterogeneity disappeared after exclusion. There was still no significant difference in the incidence of HCC between the two groups [OR = 1.33 95% CI (1.00, 1.78), P = 0.05, I²=0%], as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Incidence rate of HCC in specific population

Given the wide range of etiologies of liver disease in the literature, a subgroup analysis of studies on alcoholic liver disease (six studies) [7–9, 13, 17, 18] as the principal cause (≥ 50%) was conducted. There was no difference between the two groups [OR = 1.07 95% CI (0.61,1.88), P = 0.82, I²=67%], as shown in Fig. 3. The heterogeneity originated from the same study [9], so it was excluded, and comparable results were found [OR = 1.32 95% CI (0.91,1.91), P = 0.15, I²=19%], as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. Later, we analyzed patients with non-alcoholic liver disease as the main cause of liver disease, and the research results were consistent with those of patients with alcoholic liver disease as the main cause [OR = 1.43 95% CI (0.77,2.65), P = 0.25, I²=0%], as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3.

Incidence of HCC in most patients with alcoholic liver disease

According to the different types of stents used, studies that primarily used bare stents were analyzed. The results showed that TIPS did not increase the incidence of HCC [OR = 1.52, 95% CI (0.98,2.37), P = 0.06, I²=12%], as shown in Fig. 4. No further analysis was carried out, as some studies did not mention the use of stents and the literature on covered stents was limited.

Incidence of HCC in bare stents

Finally, we further analyzed the literature based on its publication timeline. The results showed that the research findings at different time periods were consistent, that is, TIPS surgery did not increase the incidence of postoperative HCC, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5.

Cumulative incidence and survival rate of HCC

There were a total of 4 studies involving the cumulative incidence of HCC at 1 and 5 years after TIPS. There was no significant difference in the incidence of HCC between the two groups at 1 year and 5 years [OR = 0.73 95% CI (0.20, 2.69), P = 0.63, I²=74%; OR = 0.99 95% CI (0.52, 1.87) P = 0.96, I²=79%], as shown in Fig. 5. The significant heterogeneity was still considered originate from the 2018 study, as its data were derived from all study subjects and there was some difference in baseline data. However, the other 3 articles had already matched the baseline data. The result after deleting it were roughly the same [OR = 1.18 95% CI (0.63, 2.23), P = 0.60, I²=0%; OR = 1.35 95% CI (0.93, 1.95) P = 0.11, I²=0%], as shown in Supplementary Fig. 6–7. Four studies included data on survival after TIPS, and after analysis, there was no significant difference in survival between the two groups [OR = 1.22,95% CI (0.65,2.32), P = 0.53, I²= 44%], as shown in Fig. 6.

Discussion

Through the shunt, TIPS reduces the pressure in the portal vein system, thereby altering the blood supply to the liver, and modifying the intestinal microenvironment [19]. However, there is currently no consensus on whether TIPS increase the risk of HCC. Therefore, this study aimed to perform a meta-analysis of previously published data to evaluate the impact of TIPS on HCC incidence. A total of 10 studies were included. Among them, 2 showed that TIPS significantly increased the risk of HCC, 1 demonstrated a decreased risk, and the remaining studies showed that although the incidence of HCC after TIPS was relatively high, there was no significant difference between the two groups. Based on the data analysis, the final results showed that there was no significant difference in the incidence of HCC between the TIPS treatment group and the standard treatment group.

Further analysis based on etiology and stent use types found that bare stents did not increase the incidence of HCC. Additionally, TIPS was not associated with an increased HCC incidence in patients with alcoholic liver disease. Compared to the published meta-analysis [10], our study included more studies and analyzed stents type, liver disease etiologies, postoperative survival, and cumulative HCC occurrence. The results were consistent, indicating that TIPS did not increase the risk of HCC. Meanwhile, a survival benefit and a decrease in the Child-Pugh score were observed in patients with HCC undergoing TIPS [20]. Nevertheless, these observations could be attributed to the selection of early-stage HCC patients, whose prognosis is generally better and thus leads to a longer survival period. In our study, due to limited data, further analysis was not conducted based on different liver function states. Previous studies on consecutive liver cirrhosis transplant patients with or without TIPS and HCC have found that TIPS did not worsen the prognosis of transplanted HCC patients. Therefore, for HCC patients waiting for liver transplantation, TIPS should no longer be contraindicated due to oncological reasons [21].

A previous study [8] found that in patients with TIPS, the time to HCC development was significantly shortened, and the right lobe had a higher tendency to develop nodules. This findings indicated that TIPS might accelerate the development of HCC, particularly in the right liver lobe. Bjorneboe M [7] found that the increased probability of HCC after TIPS may be attributed to the proliferation of liver sinusoidal cells within the scaffold following bare stent implantation [22]. These cells secrete transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), which promotes smooth muscle cell migration, proliferation, and pseudo intima formation. TGF- β has potential cancer-promoting effects and may influence HCC development [23]. However, this study found that the use of bare stents did not increase HCC occurrence, a finding consistent with the study by De Santis A [8]. There was no significant difference in postoperative HCC occurrence between bare and covered stents. Some other authors suggested that [24], TIPS implantation shunts part of the portal vein blood flow to the systemic circulation, thereby reducing liver parenchyma oxygenation. This process activates hepatic stellate cells, induces angiogenesis, and promotes the secretion of various growth factors, such as hepatocyte growth factor and vascular endothelial growth factor, which could contribute to the occurrence of HCC. Research in the TIPS population suggests that patients with Child-Pugh grade IV liver cirrhosis and CT-detected upper gastrointestinal bleeding may have an increased risk of HCC after TIPS [25].

Another study showed [18] a relatively high incidence of HCC after TIPS, attributing this to TIPS - prolonged survival. Due to the prolongation of survival time, the incidence of HCC may be overestimated. Although our meta-analysis found no significant survival benefit of TIPS, this could be attributed to limited survival data included in the research. In the 4 included studies, the primary indication for TIPS was gastrointestinal bleeding. Previous randomized controlled trails comparing TIPS and medication for prevention advanced liver cirrhosis-related gastrointestinal bleeding found similar 2-year survival rates between the two groups [17]. However, recent research indicated that for patients with ascites and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, TIPS reduced the incidence of further decompensated events and improved patient survival compared to standard treatment (HR = 0.44; 95% CI 0.37–0.54). Therefore, this conclusion cannot be generalized to all TIPS patients. For patients with refractory ascites, TIPS effectively prolonged the survival period of patients [26].

Cancer incidence was increased in patients with cirrhosis due to altered gut microbiota [27], damaged mucosal barrier and translocation of heterotrophic bacteria [28–30]. Lipopolysaccharide, a pathogen-associated molecular model, can promote inflammatory reactions and activate the pro-fibrosis signaling pathway upon binding to TLR4. This process was also considered to be associated with hepatocarcinogenesis [31]. Therefore, the perspective that TIPS reduces HCC occurrence [9] may be attributed to portal pressure reduction. Portal hypertension has been identified as an independent predictor of HCC development [32]. When the portal pressure gradient exceeds 10 mmHg, the risk of HCC development increases six-fold. Meanwhile, TIPS may alleviate liver inflammation and counteract hypoxia-related adverse by improving the intestinal mucosal barrier, regulating intestinal microbiota, and reducing toxic substances [33]. Current research findings mostly showed that the incidence of HCC after TIPS was relatively high, though not statistically significant compared to controls. Studies in liver transplant patients found that [14] TIPS was not a risk factor for HCC but could lead to liver tissue pathological changes such as fibrosis. Due to the relatively short post-TIPS follow-up in this study, there may be an underestimation of the risk of HCC occurrence. Therefore, there is currently a lack of prospective follow-up on a large sample of TIPS recipients in clinical practice to provide more convincing evidence. Due to the potential of HCC progression, recurrence and tumor spread, there is also debated about the safety of TIPS in patients with HCC [20, 34, 35].

Limitations of the study

Per PRISMA 2020 guidelines, this meta-analysis has limitations affecting result interpretation and generalizability. The 10 included cohorts carry inherent selection, information, and confounding bias, with significant clinical heterogeneity from variable sample sizes, study designs, TIPS protocols, patient characteristics, follow-up, and HCC diagnostic criteria. Our search was restricted to four English-language databases, excluding non-English and grey literature, with potential for language/publication bias and missed studies despite pre-specified searches and dual-independent screening/extraction/quality assessment. Limitations included partially unexplained moderate-to-high heterogeneity despite random-effects modeling, subgroup/sensitivity analyses, reduced statistical power from the limited study number, minor potential error from effect measure conversion, and unfully ruled-out publication bias. Results were predominantly from European, North American, and Asian single-center studies, lacking large multi-center prospective data, limiting generalizability to all cirrhosis patients, especially those with rare etiologies, advanced liver dysfunction, or low-resource settings. Therefore, high-quality multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate this association and identify high-risk subgroups.

Conclusion

TIPS is not associated with increased incidence of postoperative HCC in patients with cirrhosis.
