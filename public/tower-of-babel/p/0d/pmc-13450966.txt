Phenotyping of Heart Failure in CKD Using Electrocardiography Features
Authors: Qandeel H. Soomro, Niveda Shekar, Shahidul Islam, Chinyere Okpara, Soo Young Kim, Jasmin Divers, David M. Charytan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Introduction

Cardiovascular (CV) disease is the leading cause of death among individuals with CKD.1,2 Specifically, the bidirectional relationship between heart failure and CKD has become increasingly apparent with an incidence of de novo heart failure in those with preexisting CKD that is three-fold higher in those with compared with those without CKD.3,4 Although the high coprevalence of the two conditions has been widely recognized, causal links remain incompletely understood and an area of exploration.5 Despite the high rates of mortality, recurrent hospitalization, and reduced quality of life when heart failure and CKD occur concurrently,6 tools for early identification and prediction of de novo heart failure in patients with CKD remain limited.7,8

Cardiac biomarkers and echocardiographic findings have been studied as markers of heart failure risk in the setting of CKD.9 However, these tools need further validation, have high cost, and in the case of echocardiogram, require specially trained technicians and subspecialty input for appropriate interpretation. By contrast, electrocardiography (ECG) is a ubiquitous resource that is quick to perform and cost-effective. Several patterns noted on routine ECGs, such as QRS duration ≥100 ms, wider QRS-T angle, and frequency of premature ventricular complexes, have been associated with heart failure in the general population.10–13 Although ECG measures have been used to stratify cardiac risk and predict CV deaths in the general population, there is limited research on whether ECG features in patients with CKD can predict risk of new onset heart failure in the setting of CKD, where the incidence may be much higher than in the general population.14,15

Early prediction of heart failure in the CKD population could facilitate more timely intervention with prophylactic therapies and improve management of patients with CKD, thereby reducing health care burden and improving patient quality of life. In this study, we used a database of ECGs from the New York University (NYU) Langone Health system to analyze the association of ECG features with the risk of incident heart failure hospitalization in individuals with CKD but without heart failure at baseline. We hypothesized that ECG features would be independently associated with the risk of de novo heart failure hospitalizations among individuals with CKD.

Methods

Study Population

We used the NYU MUSE (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL) ECG database together with linked electronic health records (EHR) for NYU Langone Health for this analysis. MUSE is a comprehensive cardiology information system that acquires, analyzes, and stores cardiac data such as ECGs. Records from three linked hospitals and their affiliated practices, including two tertiary care hospitals (NYU Manhattan and NYU Long Island) and a safety net hospital (NYU Brooklyn), were included. We created a dataset identifying a cohort of adult patients ≥18 years and diagnosed with CKD between the 2012–2021 period. Inclusion required at least two values of eGFR <90 ml/min per 1.73 m2 occurring at least 90 days apart and within 1-year of an index ECG. The date of the first qualifying eGFR was used as the index date, and eGFR was computed from serum creatinine using the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration 2021 equation.16

To identify active patients in the EHR with adequate follow-up information and to eliminate patients with truncated history, the eGFR criteria had to be satisfied within 1 year of an index ECG. The index ECG was considered the first ECG performed after the first qualifying eGFR date (baseline) and was required to be performed before initiation of dialysis, ESKD, or transplant. Individuals with a history of heart failure at baseline were excluded on the basis of presence of any inpatient or outpatient heart failure International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) billing codes as defined below (heart failure definition) or the presence of heart failure in the problem list before the date of the index ECG. The heart failure outcome (primary) was defined by ICD-10 codes in the primary discharge position. We additionally excluded individuals with an initial heart failure event occurring within 30 days of the index ECG, as these ECGs may have been performed because of conditions and symptoms causally related to the onset of heart failure.

Baseline demographic variables were collected from the EHR. Baseline laboratory values were defined as the most recent results obtained within 6 months before the index ECG date. When multiple measurements were available, the value closest in time to the ECG date was selected.

ECG features were extracted from the institutional MUSE GE Healthcare system, which integrates ECG data into EHR. Heart rate variability data consisting of time domain features were extracted, preprocessed, and denoised from waveform data (lead I, II, V1-V6, and rest of the lead waveforms were derived) available in MUSE. Python heart rate analysis packages were used. BioSSPy was used for graphing and peak detection (url: https://biosppy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/). SciPy Signal Processing was used for manual calculations of time domain metrics using BioSSPy R-peak output (url: https://www.kaggle.com/code/stetelepta/exploring-heart-rate-variability-using-python). HeartPy-Python Heart Rate Analysis was used for time domain measurements from detected peaks defined per package (url: https://python-heart-rate-analysis-toolkit.readthedocs.io/en/latest/algorithmfunctioning.html). ECGs with atrial arrhythmia were excluded because neither the PR interval nor the heart rate variability can be calculated in the presence of atrial arrhythmia. ECGs were classified as screening or for clinical indication on the basis of the diagnosis entered during order placement in EHR.

Heart rate variability (HRV) measures the time between consecutive heart beats in milliseconds, the HRV parameters included were time domain features that are statistical measures of the heart beat such as SD of the interval between R-R peaks on ECG in milliseconds (ms; SDNN), which reflects overall autonomic activity, both sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems contribute to SDNN, with lower values associated with higher risk of mortality.17 Root mean square of successive differences between normal heartbeats (RMSSD) and the percentage of adjacent normal-to-normal (NN) intervals that differ from each other by more than 50 milliseconds (ms; PNN50%), which reflect primarily parasympathetic tone.17

Heart Failure Definition

The primary outcome was time to the first heart failure hospitalization occurring at least 30 days after the index ECG. For simplicity this is referred to as a heart failure event or time to heart failure, as appropriate, throughout the results section. We identified the index heart failure hospitalization on the basis of heart failure-related ICD-10 codes (i.e., 150.X, I11.0, I13.0, I13.2, I09.81, I12.5, I42.0, I42.9, I43.X, and P29.0) listed as the primary discharge diagnosis.18 We excluded individuals with a prior history of heart failure on the basis of the presence of any ICD codes or if listed as comorbidity in the EHR problem list, and those with heart failure event within 30 days of the qualifying eGFR date.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics are presented as median (interquartile range, [IQR]) for continuous or n (%) for categorical variables. Univariate associations between key ECG and HRV variables, and heart failure outcome were assessed by log-rank test. HRV measures were dichotomized at the median of their log-transformed values using log(x+1) to address right skewness and to ensure balanced groupings. QT interval corrected for heart rate (QTc) was categorized using the median of the standardized QTc measure.

To predict heart failure outcomes, we used a training and validation approach by which Cox proportional hazard model–variable selection was fit using 9128 observations (heart failure=700, [8%]) in the training set. Follow-up was censored on December 31, 2021. Models were fitted using standardized continuous variables (mean=0, SD=1) and unscaled categorical or binary covariates. Categorical variables were treated as factors with defined reference levels, and binary comorbidities were coded as binary 0/1. Time-to-event and event indicators (tte, y) were not scaled.

We assessed proportional hazards assumptions by exploring martingale residuals. We applied a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator–penalized Cox proportional hazards model for variable selection. Predictor coefficients were estimated across a sequence of penalization strengths, and the optimal tuning parameter was chosen using ten-fold cross-validation on the basis of the λ.1se criterion to favor a more parsimonious and stable model. All predictors with nonzero coefficients at this penalty value were retained for inclusion in the final multivariable Cox model. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor. We used an 80/20 random split for reproducibility. Although using the full dataset could slightly increase power for association estimates, the held-out validation subset was necessary to evaluate model performance on unseen data. To assess the incremental predictive value of ECG features, we compared prediction performance across nested Cox models with and without ECG variables by reporting Harrell C-statistics in the training and test sets, the change in discrimination using compareC, and category-free net reclassification index (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) with bootstrap 95% confidence interval (CI) using survIDINRI. Category-free NRI was used to avoid arbitrary risk thresholds. Model 1 included Clinical only with baseline demographics and comorbidities, model 2 included Clinical+ECG features, and model 3 included Clinical+ECG+HRV.19

We performed sensitivity analysis in the population with CKD stage 3a–5 as well by restricting the outcomes analysis to 2 years after the index date. In addition, we assessed for consistency in outcomes when analyzing death, dialysis, or transplant as competing risk for heart failure hospitalization using the Fine and Gray approach.20 P < 0.05 was considered significant. All analyses were performed using R statistical software (Version 4.3.3, Vienna, Austria).

Internal validation of model discrimination was assessed using ten-fold cross-validation, bootstrap resampling (500 replications), and 100 repeated random 80/20 train-test splits. For ten-fold cross-validation, models were fit on nine folds and evaluated on the held-out fold using out-of-fold linear predictors. For bootstrap validation, models were refitted within each bootstrap sample and evaluated both within the bootstrap sample and when applied to the original dataset; optimism was defined as the difference between these estimates, and optimism-corrected C-statistics were obtained by subtracting the mean optimism from the apparent C-statistic. For repeated train-test splits, models were fit on the training data and evaluated on the held-out test data, with variability summarized using the SD.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The final study population included 11,409 individuals (Figure 1, Patient Flow Diagram), including 10,529 (92%) without heart failure events during follow-up and 880 with a heart failure event (8%). The overall median (interquartile range [IQR]) follow-up time was 976 (492–1606) days for participants without an event compared with 674 (342–1161) days in those with heart failure events with a maximum time to event of 4302 days (11.8 years). Approximately half of the cohort was male in each group, and most were White (Table 1). Those with heart failure were significantly older compared with those without heart failure during follow-up (76 versus 72, P < 0.001). Most, both with and without heart failure, were covered by Medicare.

Among 11,409 patients, the mean qualifying eGFR was 42 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (SD=20.3), and the mean eGFR on the day of the index ECG was 48 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (SD=23.8). The average within-patient difference was +4.2 ml/min per 1.73 m2, and 3% patients (318 of 11,409) had an ECG-day eGFR >90 (Supplemental Table 1). CKD severity was similar among those with and without heart failure events, with stages 3a and 5 CKD being the most frequent. During the median follow-up period, 615 patients (5.4%) progressed to dialysis or kidney transplantation, and 428 patients (4%) died without prior heart failure or ESKD. Cumulative incidence of heart failure at 5 years was 11% (95% CI, 9.8% to 11.4%) for heart failure, 6% (95% CI, 5.8% to 7.1%) for ESKD, and 4% (95% CI, 3.7% to 4.8%) for death (Figure 2).

Cumulative incidence: heart failure, dialysis/transplant, and death. The figure shows cumulative incidence curves for heart failure, dialysis or kidney transplant, and death estimated using a competing risks framework on the basis of the Fine–Gray method. CI, confidence interval.

ECG features at baseline were significantly different in those with and without heart failure during follow-up. For instance, the PR interval, QRS duration, QT interval, and corrected QT were all significantly prolonged in the heart failure group compared with the heart failure-free group. ECG-derived heart rate variability features were severely reduced in the CKD population overall; however, there was no significant difference in SDNN values between groups, whereas RMSSD and pNN50% were comparatively lower in individuals without a heart failure event during follow-up compared with those with heart failure events. The most recent laboratory tests within ≤6 months before ECG also differed between groups. Hematocrit and serum albumin concentrations were lower, BUN, and bicarbonate concentrations were higher in those with compared with those without heart failure event. Finally, a history of cardiac arrhythmia, valvular disorders, diabetes, hypertension, pulmonary circulation disorders, and peripheral vascular disorders were more common in the heart failure group (P < 0.001). Among those who experienced heart failure events, 65% of ECGs were performed for screening, and 35% for a clinical indication.

Univariate Associations of ECG and HRV Parameters with Risk of Heart Failure Events

Heart failure-free survival for those above and below the median values of log-transformed SDNN was not significantly different (Figure 3A and P > 0.90). Results were analogous for RMSSD, which reflects parasympathetic tone (Figure 3B, P = 0.08). For PNN50%, heart failure-free survival was higher in those at or below the median compared with those with values above the median (Figure 3C, P < 0.001). Adjusted Associations and Competing Risk

Associations were initially investigated in the training set using a Cox proportional model with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator–variable selection fit (Table 2). Older age was independently associated with an increased risk of heart failure (hazard ratio [HR]) 1.16 per 1-year increase (95% CI, 1.06 to 1.27; P = 0.002). Several comorbidities, such as arrhythmia, valvular disease, and diabetes were independently associated with risk of heart failure events. Among ECG features, the strongest ECG predictors were corrected QT interval (HR, 1.33; 95% CI, 1.22 to 1.45; P < 0.001), PR interval (HR, 1.20; 95% CI, 1.12 to 1.27; P < 0.001), and T axis (HR, 1.23, 95% CI, 1.15 to 1.32; P < 0.001), and were also independently associated with increased risks of heart failure. Among the HRV parameters, only PNN50% was selected for the model, but it was not significantly associated with the risk of heart failure (HR, 1.07; 95% CI, 0.99 to 1.14, P = 0.07). Performance of an ECG for screening purposes rather than for clinical indications was associated with 25% lower risk of heart failure (HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.64 to 0.88). The model performed well with test set C-indices of 0.73 (95% CI, 0.68 to 0.77). Results for both ECG features and clinical parameters were qualitatively similar in models accounting for competing risks of death or ESKD (Supplemental Table 2).

Model Performance and Incremental Value of ECG Features

To assess the incremental predictive value of ECG features, we compared three nested models: clinical variables alone, clinical variables plus standard ECG features, and clinical plus standard ECG features and HRV parameter (Table 3). The clinical model without ECG features achieved C-statistics of 0.70 (95% CI, 0.66 to 0.74) in the test set. Addition of four ECG features in model 2 (PR interval, QRS duration, QTc, and T axis) resulted in improved reclassification and discrimination, change in C-statistic=0.03, P = 0.01, NRI%=0.30, IDI%=2.16. There was no further improvement with addition of HRV parameter (pNN50%) to model 2.

Sensitivity Analyses

To understand associations in more advanced CKD, we restricted the analysis to individuals with CKD stages 4–5 (Table 4). All key findings were replicated in those with advanced CKD. Regarding comorbidities, cardiac arrhythmia remained significantly associated with risk of heart failure as in the overall cohort (HR, 1.75; CI, 1.37 to 2.23, P < 0.001). Similar to the overall model, ECG variables such as PR interval, QTc, and T axis were significantly associated with risk of heart failure hospitalization. Discrimination remained excellent (test sets C- index 0.74).

Since a single ECG might be expected to more accurately predict near-term rather than long-term outcomes, we also analyzed results in a cohort restricting follow-up time to the first 2 years after the index eGFR. Results in this analysis (Table 5) were qualitatively similar to those for CKD stages 4–5. Next, we analyzed the results in those with preexisting cardiac comorbidities (cardiac arrhythmia or valvular disease). The results were consistent for standard ECG features (Table 6); however, in this cohort higher values of pNN50% were significantly associated with an increased risk of heart failure (HR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.02 to 1.19). Internal Validation

Internal validation results are presented in Supplemental Tables 5–8. In the overall cohort, the optimism-corrected bootstrap C-statistic was 0.756, with comparable out-of-sample C-statistics from ten-fold cross-validation (0.754) and repeated 80/20 train-test splits (0.753). Similar results were observed in analyses restricted to patients with CKD stages 4–5, those with at least 2 years of follow-up, and those with a history of cardiac arrhythmias or valvular disease.

Discussion

ECG is an easy and commonly used tool for stratifying CV risk; however, its use in risk prediction for heart failure, a common and morbid complication in patients with CKD, is underexplored. In this study, we assembled a dataset linking a large number of ECGs with associated EHR data for the purpose of identifying specific ECG features in patients with CKD associated with the risk of developing de novo heart failure. We found several key findings such as: ECG features, including ECG-derived HRV features, that were different at baseline in those who experienced a heart failure event compared with those without heart failure during follow-up; PR interval; QRS duration; QT interval; and QT corrected were all higher among patients who experienced a heart failure event. We identified several ECG features (PR interval, QTc, and T axis) that independently predict heart failure hospitalization after accounting for competing risk of death and ESKD progression, and these results remained consistent in advanced CKD and in those at high risk of CV disease. Consistent with prior reports of autonomic dysfunction in populations with CKD,21,22 HRV features such as SDNN, RMSSD, and pNN50% were severely depressed in those with CKD. Contrary to our expectations; however, the values showed paradoxical associations in crude analyses, with higher values associated with worse composite outcomes for pNN50% likely because of floor effects limiting discriminative ability. Notably, these associations disappeared in adjusted models. In addition, the improvement in the C statistic from 0.71 to 0.73 with addition of ECG features to models using clinical features alone is consistent with a modest increment in discrimination and predictive utility for heart failure when ECG features are incorporated into clinical tools.

Individuals with CKD, even in early stages, have a tremendous burden of CV morbidity and mortality compared with the general population, and exponential risk increases as CKD advances.23 An easy tool to risk-stratify individuals with CKD at high risk for a heart failure event is appealing. Several studies have suggested the potential for widespread use of ECGs to improve prediction of cardiac risk. QTc has been associated with stroke risk;7 PR prolongation with increased risk of atrial fibrillation, pacemaker implantation, and death;8 and increased QRS duration with increased risk of heart failure.10 A recent study of 320,974 patients, using ECG variables collected via long-term ambulatory ECG monitoring found that addition of ECG covariates such as average heart rate and ventricular ectopy to the CHA2DS2-VASc risk score improved prediction of heart failure hospitalization (change in C-statistic from 0.73 [95% CI, 0.72 to 0.74] to 0.85 [95% CI, 0.84 to 0.86]) and prediction of new onset heart failure (change in C-statistic from 0.61 [95% CI, 0.60 to 0.62] to 0.70 [95% CI, 0.69 to 0.71]).24

On the contrary, HRV features derived from Holter ECG recordings have been studied as possible markers of risk for heart failure, with lower SDNN having strong associations with a lower risk of heart failure and higher RMSSD and pNN50% associated with lower risk. In addition, HRV features have been found to be independent predictors of survival in heart failure.25 However, these data are not readily generalizable to the CKD population because of unique risk factors that create a complex milieu for adverse CV pathology.

Two prior studies in the CKD population have demonstrated associations between ECG features, such as prolonged QRS or the QT interval, and CV death. However, these studies were limited to patients >65 years, used the same database (The Cardiovascular Health Study), analyzed a limited range of features, did not specifically evaluate heart failure as an outcome, and included primarily early stages of CKD, limiting analysis of associations in advanced CKD.26,27 Our study adds new information bridging the gap to a broader segment of the CKD population because we specifically analyzed associations within a substantial cohort of individuals with advanced CKD. In addition, we examined a wide variety of ECG-related features along with rich clinical data and assessed associations over both long-term and medium-term time horizons.

Notably, the addition of time-domain HRV features derived from ECG did not improve model performance. This could reflect a true lack of causal links between autonomic function and the development and progression of heart failure in the setting of CKD. However, measured HRV parameters were low overall (consistent with dysautonomia), in our cohort, both for those with and without heart failure. If a threshold effect exists, we might have missed associations with heart failure outcomes if most of our studied population was below that threshold. Finally, HRV data were also highly skewed, suggesting a higher degree of variability in the derived values which may indicate that a longer duration ECG strip is required to reduce heterogeneity and derive maximally predictive information.

Our findings provide insight into the early characteristic pathophysiologic changes driving CV disease in CKD. Heart failure is associated with several structural and functional changes which can be reflected in ECG abnormalities. Consistent with the associations we observed, depolarization and repolarization defects on ECG have been associated with myocardial fibrosis, scar tissues, and ischemic injury that may precede clinical symptoms and predispose to atrial arrhythmia and diastolic dysfunction.28,29 For instance, PR interval reflects the state of atrioventricular conduction. In one study increases in PR interval were associated with a 13% greater 10-year risk of heart failure (HR, 1.13; 95% CI, 1.0 to 1.23) and prolonged PR interval >200 ms was associated with a 46% increased risk of incident heart failure (95% CI, 1.11 to 1.93) in other populations.30 Atrioventricular conduction is hypothesized to be altered in the setting of electrical and structural remodeling associated with heart failure in addition to being associated with several common risk factors for heart failure such as hypertension and obesity.30,31 Similarly, prolonged QTc a marker of fibrosis, ischemia, and electrolyte abnormalities reflects delayed ventricular repolarization and elevated sympathetic tone, and has been found to be independently associated with heart failure and sudden cardiac death in patients with acute myocardial infarction.32 Appropriate next steps would include correlating our ECG findings with functional or structural imaging, validating our findings in an independent cohort.

There are some limitations to this study. Similar with all EHR-based datasets, the relevant comorbid conditions and outcomes were derived from data generated as part of clinical care and therefore can be prone to misclassification or incompleteness. Similarly, we used ECG that had been performed in the clinical setting. This may introduce selection bias and affect the strength or magnitude of associations. The association between screening ECG indication and reduced heart failure risk, despite adjustment for comorbidities, suggests residual confounding by unmeasured factors. To minimize the misclassification of heart failure, we used ICD codes only in primary discharge diagnoses.

As noted above, HRV measures were derived from standard 12-lead ECG, 10-second tracings. In addition to requiring extra steps to extract the data from the ECG, tracing of this duration may be more susceptible to noise and artifacts than dedicated studies with longer HRV recording. Albuminuria data and echocardiographic data were available only in a minority of participants and were therefore not included in analyses. These values may be associated with the risk of heart failure outcomes; further studies will be needed to better assess the value of including a baseline ECG in risk prediction models on top of albuminuria or echocardiographic data. This was a single-center study with a population restricted to the NYU Langone patient population. Although we used a split sample to avoid overfitting our data and validate findings, the findings of ECG phenotypes and heart failure need to be externally validated in other populations of individuals with CKD. Despite that, our sample of 11,409 individuals with CKD is one of the largest kidney disease datasets to undergo systematic analysis for ECG abnormalities and predictive value for de novo heart failure.

Given these limitations, external validation in independent CKD cohorts is the key to confirm generalizability before clinical implementation. Second, comparison and integration with existing risk scores is needed. Finally, randomized trials are needed to determine whether ECG-based risk prediction improves outcomes through targeted therapies.

In summary, several ECG features seem to have a strong association in CKD population and development of heart failure. Given the frequent use of ECG in practice, our analysis suggests that ECG measures have potential to improve prediction models for heart failure in the CKD population. Further research is needed to evaluate the utility of ECG phenotypes and early identification to mitigate CV risk in the CKD population.
