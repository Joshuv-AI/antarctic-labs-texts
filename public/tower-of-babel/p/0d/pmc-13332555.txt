Large Hydatid Cyst in the Cardiac Interventricular Septum: A Rare Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Lin Gao, Yixiu Zhang, Xiao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHydatid disease is a parasitic disease with a high incidence in pastoral areas in Tibet. The heart is an uncommon location of echinococcosis, and involvement of the interventricular septum is even rarer. This disease can remain asymptomatic for a long time, but may result in cardiac tamponade and sudden death. We report a 58‐year‐old woman who was diagnosed with a giant cardiac hydatid cyst involving the interventricular septum associated with complete right bundle branch block. However, owing to insufficient treatment, spontaneous rupture of the cardiac hydatid cyst occurred 6 months after diagnosis, which caused sudden death of the patient.Keywords: echinococcosis, echocardiography, hydatid disease, ventricular septum

Introduction

Hydatid disease, also known as echinococcosis, is a parasitic disease that is caused by the larval stage of Echinococcosis granulose or E. multilocularis (McManus et al. 2003; Fennira, Sarray, et al. 2019). Tibet is an endemic region of hydatidosis (Han et al. 2019; Su et al. 2019). Cardiac hydatid cysts are uncommon and found in 0.5%–2% of cases of hydatidosis. Patients with a cardiac hydatid cyst may be asymptomatic, but this is a serious condition. We describe here a case of a 58‐year‐old woman who was diagnosed with a giant cardiac hydatid cyst involving the interventricular septum associated with complete right bundle branch block. Because of poor living, economic, and cultural conditions, the patient missed an early diagnosis and treatment, and then sudden death occurred. We also discuss the pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, complications, and treatment of cardiac hydatid cyst, and provide a review of the recent literature.

Case Report

A 58‐year‐old female patient visited the cardiology outpatient clinic for a history of occasional slight chest pain and palpitation, which lasted 3 years. In the most recent year, these worsened with dyspnea. The Tibetan patient had no previous known medical conditions, was a farmer by occupation, had lived in pastoral areas her entire life, and had a habit of eating raw beef and mutton. A physical examination showed that the boundary of the heart was enlarged, and systolic murmur was heard on the left edge of the sternum. A frontal chest x‐ray showed enlargement of the heart shadow. Transthoracic echocardiography detected a 74 × 72 × 72‐mm globular, multilocular, thin‐walled, anechoic mass in the ventricular septum, with a clear boundary and a regular shape (Figure 1, Video S1). Color Doppler flow imaging showed no visible blood flow signal around and inside the mass (Figure 2). The left and right ventricles were compressed and relatively reduced. The blood flow velocity of each valve was within the normal range and moderate regurgitation was detected in the mitral valve. The left ventricular ejection fraction was 69% and the left ventricular short axis shortening rate was 39%. The above‐mentioned signs suggested the diagnosis of echinococcosis. Chest computed tomography showed a well‐bordered, huge multicystic space occupying the interventricular septum, with a smooth wall and without obvious calcification (Figure 3). This finding suggested that echinococcosis should be considered. Electrocardiography showed complete right bundle branch block. A laboratory examination showed a leukocyte count of 8.9 × 109/L, eosinophil count of 0.49 × 109/L, and eosinophil ratio of 5.5%. A serological hydatid immunoglobulin G test was positive. All of these examinations supported the diagnosis of hydatid cyst. No involvement was detected in the lungs, liver, kidneys, or other organs.

These findings suggested that the patient should be operated on as soon as possible to remove the hydatid cyst. However, the operation could not be carried out by the local hospital for technological reasons. Because of economic reasons, the patient asked not to be transferred to a more appropriate hospital for treatment. The patient was provided albendazole 800 mg/day for conservative treatment. Unfortunately, there were serious side effects of this medication, including ascites and abnormal liver function. Therefore, albendazole was stopped and the patient was followed up monthly. Six months later, the patient suffered from acute severe retrosternal chest pain and died on the way to the hospital. This was considered to be due to rupture of the cardiac hydatid cyst.

Discussion

This case was a middle‐aged woman who lived in a pastoral area and had the habit of eating raw meat. Although the onset of disease was slow and almost asymptomatic, the echocardiographic manifestations were highly sensitive and specific. A multilocular cystic mass with an intact capsule and multiple independent daughter cysts is typical ultrasonic manifestations of a hydatid cyst, which is similar to type II hepatic echinococcal cyst. Echocardiography and computed tomography are the main diagnostic methods used for diagnosing this condition. A laboratory examination and hydatid antibody test are helpful for confirming the diagnosis of this disease. Our patient progressed to rupture of echinococcosis, which caused death owing to the lack of timely and effective treatment.

Human hydatidosis is a systemic parasitic disease, which mostly occurs in areas with livestock, and it remains a serious health problem (Han et al. 2019). Humans usually become infected by ingesting contaminated food, meat, milk, or water containing the eggs of the parasite. The eggs of the genus Echinococcus hatch in the duodenum into larvae, pass through the intestinal wall, and enter the blood through the portal circulation. The liver is the most affected organ (70%) by this parasite, followed by the lungs (20%). Cardiac echinococcosis is rare because most larvae are filtered out by the liver or the lungs, which are also the most common sites. Only a small number of the larvae can escape into the systemic circulation. Contraction of the heart provides a natural resistance to the presence of viable hydatid cysts. Studies have shown that the oncosphere enters the systemic circulation after entering the coronary artery, or through the lymphatic system and the superior or inferior vena cava, and then invades the myocardium to form a hydatid cyst. The distribution of cysts in the heart is mainly related to the blood supply of the myocardium. The most common site of cysts is the ventricular wall with the most abundant blood supply, followed by the atrial wall, with little involvement of the cardiac cavity. The rates of locations of these cysts are as follows: left ventricular wall, 50%–60%; right ventricle, 10%; pericardium, 7%; interventricular septum, 5%–10%; left atrium, 8%; right atrium, 4%; and interatrial septum, 2%.

The symptom of a cardiac hydatid cyst depends on the size and site of infection. Because of the density of myocardial fibers, the hydatid cyst grows slowly between cardiac fibers, and 90% of the cases are insidious before occurrence of complications (Fennira, Kamoun, et al. 2019; Modani et al. 2018). Most patients with cardiac hydatid cyst have chest pain, dyspnea, cough, and other nonspecific symptoms. If valve function is affected, there is a cardiac murmur and angina pectoris may appear when the coronary artery is compressed. Additionally, palpitations can occur owing to irritation of the conduction fibers in the ventricular septum, and arrhythmia may appear when the conduction system is invaded (Fennira, Kamoun, et al. 2019). The main complication of cardiac echinococcosis is rupture of hydatid cysts (Oraha et al. 2018). Spontaneous rupture occurs in 25%–40% of cardiac hydatid sacs. If the fragments or subcapsules of the capsule enter the heart cavity and blood circulation, they may lead to multiple arterial embolisms, extensive pulmonary embolism, and acute myocardial infarction. Additionally, if cystic fluid enters the blood circulation, it can cause a hypersensitivity reaction and anaphylactic shock (Fennira, Kamoun, et al. 2019). If a pericardial cyst ruptures into the pericardium, pericardial tamponade occurs. A surgical operation is the first choice for treatment of cardiac echinococcosis, and postoperative oral administration of albendazole is an auxiliary approach, which can prevent recurrence of echinococcosis (Fennira, Sarray, et al. 2019; İyigün et al. 2020).

Conclusion

In conclusion, a hydatid cyst located in the interventricular septum is rare, but it can lead to many serious complications. Diagnosis of this condition should be suspected in patients who live in endemic regions and present with an unexplained cardiac symptom. Echocardiography and other imaging technologies are effective modalities for diagnosis and location of cardiac hydatidosis. All patients with a cardiac hydatid cyst should undergo surgery with concurrent albendazole therapy to prevent life‐threatening catastrophic complications.
