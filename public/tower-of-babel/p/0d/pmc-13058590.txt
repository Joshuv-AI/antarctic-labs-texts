A new super-pangenome pipeline reveals domestication signatures of conserved noncoding sequences in the orange subfamily
Authors: Lei Tan, Shenchao Zhu, Junli Ye, Wen-Biao Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvances in genome sequencing have accelerated the assembly of hundreds of plant genomes. Because of limited tools, current pangenome approaches overlook conserved noncoding sequences, though these sequences potentially harbor diverse regulatory elements. Here, we present panCG, an integrative pipeline for family level super-pangenome analysis across coding and noncoding sequences. PanCG enables the reference-free construction of pangene and pan-conserved noncoding sequences (panCNS) indices and integrative analyses between them. Applying this pipeline to the orange subfamily (Aurantioideae) with 22 representative genomes, we identified 167,989 panCNSs, 44.63% of which exhibited potential functionality, as evidenced by multiomic data (eg ATAC-seq, ChIP-seq). Functionally, approximately half of duplicated genes showed expression divergence, likely mediated by sequence variants in these panCNSs. Although transposable element (TE) mobilization resulted in substantial CNS loss, it also contributed to the origin of a small portion of functional CNSs. By integrating panCNS and pangene data, we identified 1,391 conserved colocalized blocks of CNSs and genes across all selected genomes, providing clear evidence for coevolution between CNSs and their target genes. Notably, leveraging these pan-genomic resources, we pinpointed numerous CNSs and gene–CNS regulatory modules as candidates underlying the domestication of citrus fruit traits from wild relatives. Our study presents a powerful tool for interspecific super-pangenome analysis, whereas shedding new light on the functional roles and evolutionary dynamics of CNSs in citrus, and provides a valuable resource of candidate regulatory elements for citrus fruit biology and breeding research.

Introduction

Understanding the regulation of gene expression patterns across diverse temporal, spatial, and environmental contexts hinges on the identification of transcriptional regulatory elements. These cis-regulatory elements (CREs), encompassing promoters, enhancers, and silencers, are predominantly derived from functional noncoding regions. Notably, CREs exhibit a higher degree of sequence conservation compared with nonfunctional regions (Visel et al. 2009; Reynoso et al. 2019; Andrews et al. 2023). Consequently, comparative genomic approaches offer rapid and cost-effective means to identify CREs by identifying conserved noncoding sequences (CNSs). The remarkable progress in high-throughput sequencing technologies has revolutionized genomics, enabling the assembly of thousands of plant and animal genomes. This technological leap has paved the way for large-scale comparative genomic studies, facilitating the discovery of numerous functional CNSs in a wide spectrum of plant species. Notable examples include Arabidopsis (Haudry et al. 2013), rice (Zhou et al. 2022), tomato (Hendelman et al. 2021), maize (Song et al. 2021), and cucumber (Xin et al. 2024).

However, strategies that rely on alignment to a single-reference genome introduce significant reference bias. This method systematically omits CNSs elements that are absent from the reference genome, potentially leading to a substantial underestimation of the total CNSs complement (Feng et al. 2020; Song et al. 2021). As demonstrated in recent comparative genomic studies on mammals and apes, this limitation can be mitigated by reference-free alignment methods such as Cactus (Armstrong et al. 2020; Christmas et al. 2023; Yoo et al. 2025). Moreover, existing research on CNSs typically operates in isolation, primarily focusing on their conservation and regulatory functions whereas neglecting systematic analyses of the genes associated with these functions. This fragmented approach hinders a comprehensive understanding of the functional relationships between CNSs and their regulatory targets. Similarly, many comparative genomic or interspecies super-pangenome studies have concentrated solely on protein-coding genes, disregarding noncoding sequences altogether (Chen et al. 2020; Shi et al. 2024). Meanwhile, amid the rapidly expanding repository of species genomes, current methodologies lack robust cross-species pangenome analysis tools capable of integrating both protein-coding genes and noncoding sequences. This limitation obscures the coevolution of genes and noncoding sequences across species, leaving them buried within the deluge of genomic data.

The orange subfamily (Aurantioideae), which includes many citrus fruit crops, provides an ideal system to address these gaps. Citrus fruits enjoy global popularity, cherished for their delectable taste, enticing aroma, and rich nutritional profile, which is abundant in vitamin C, carotenoids, and flavonoids, all offering substantial health benefits (Liu et al. 2012; Damián-Reyna et al. 2017; Karn et al. 2020). The citrus genus encompasses 3 primary original species—mandarin, pummelo, and citron—and their numerous hybrids, such as sweet orange, lemon, and grapefruit (Wang et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2018). Recent breakthroughs in citrus genomics have led to the release of numerous genome assemblies from the orange subfamily (Huang et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2025). Although comparative genomic studies have successfully characterized the interspecies pangenome at the gene or sequence level (Huang et al. 2023), a significant gap exists in the cross-species characterization of functional noncoding sequences and their roles in gene expression regulation. A systematic identification and characterization of these functional CNSs could unlock valuable insights into the regulatory mechanisms underlying important traits, thereby facilitating fruit breeding efforts.

Inspired by gene-level pangenome studies, we expanded the scope of this framework to integrate both genes and conserved noncoding sequences (CNSs) across phylogenetically close species within the same genus, subfamily or family, adopting the established “super-pangenome” concept (Khan et al. 2020; Raza et al. 2026). To operationalize this concept, we developed the panCG pipeline, an advanced tool engineered to systematically investigate the conservation and variation patterns of genes and CNSs across species. Applying this pipeline to the orange subfamily with 22 representative genomes, we identified ∼168 thousand nonredundant CNSs, whose functions were characterized using multiomic datasets. Furthermore, we explored how variants within CNSs regulate the expression of duplicated genes and the role of transposable element (TE) mobilization in shaping CNS evolution. Through cross-species analyses of CNS presence/absence patterns and CNS-gene syntenic networks, we elucidated the evolutionary dynamics of CNSs and revealed coevolutionary patterns and domestication-associated trends between CNSs and their linked functional genes during citrus domestication.

Results

The panCG pipeline

To address the limitations of existing cross-species pangenome tools, including single-reference bias in noncoding sequence analysis and lack of integration between coding and regulatory elements, we developed the panCG pipeline (Fig. 1), which integrates 3 functionally complementary modules (panCNS, pangene, CG) to unify coding and noncoding super-pangenome analysis. The panCNS module is engineered to annotate CNSs across multiple genomes of closely related species, enabling a full characterization of the interspecies super-pangenome at the CNS level (defined as panCNS). By indexing panCNSs among all genomes based on genomic synteny, it facilitates the identification of presence/absence variations (PAVs) and copy number variations (CNVs) of CNSs. Similarly, the pangene module is developed to construct the super-pangenome at the protein-coding gene level (defined as pangene). The pangene module refines orthologous gene clustering (a limitation of sequence-only tools like OrthoFinder) via synteny and phylogeny. It also supports indexing all genes among different genomes to facilitate identification of gene-PAVs, CNVs, and local synteny. Its gene indices provide the “functional anchor” for linking CNSs to target genes in the CG module. The CG module bridges panCNS and pangene indices to support integrative analyses of the genes and their potential regulatory elements as a functional module or block across species.

Overview of the panCG pipeline including panCNS, pangene, and CG modules. a) Workflow of the panCNS module. (1) Multiple genome alignment: Input multiple genomes are used for reference-free multiple genome alignment via Progressive Cactus. (2) CNS identification: Each genome is individually designated as the reference genome to generate whole-genome alignments. PhastCons is then employed to identify conserved sequences; conserved sequences overlapping with CDSs are filtered out, yielding initial CNSs set for each genome. (3) Homologous group identification: Homologous CNS groups are identified based on the aforementioned multiple genome alignments and pairwise CNS comparisons. (4) Synteny cluster construction: Undirected CNS networks are constructed based on syntenic relationships of CNSs between species; these are connected networks. Rectangular nodes represent CNSs, with different colors indicating different species. Edges represent syntenic relationships. (5) Index assignment: Members of each connected component within the synteny network are assigned a unique index. (6) CNS retrieval: Some CNSs are detected in certain genomes but missed in others because of small variations within or adjacent to these elements. Their homologous regions in other genomes are retrieved as missing CNSs and assigned the same panCNS index if they meet preset criteria. (7) Index reassignment: This step corrects potential mis-assigned indices. For example, the panCNS module reassigns indices for singleton CNSs (no syntenic counterparts, caused by structural variations) to match those of their best homologous sequences in other genomes (red links indicate such relationships). b) Workflow of the pangene module. (1) Ortholog group identification: OrthoFinder is used to identify homolog groups. Circles represent genes, with colors distinguishing homologous genes from different species. Genes in gray ovals belong to the same gene group. (2) Syntenic ortholog clustering: Synteny networks are constructed for genes in each group, and genes are further clustered using the clique percolation method (CPM). Nodes represent genes, and gray edges represent syntenic relationships between genes; the set of genes enclosed by the dashed line denotes a gene cluster identified via CPM. (3) Inclusion of nonsyntenic orthologous genes: For genes within ortholog groups but lacking synteny, best-hit information is used to include them to extend the gene synteny network. Red edges indicate homologous best-hit gene pairs. (4) Tree-based clustering for paralog distinction: For gene indices containing paralogous genes, the phylogenetic relationships between genes are considered to further refine index assignments. Finally, each gene has a unique index, and a reference-free pangene is obtained. c) Workflow of the CG module. (1) CNS-gene colocalization analysis: CNSs located in the upstream and downstream regions of each gene are extracted to form a CNS set. (2) Synteny network construction: CNS and gene sets corresponding to each gene index are independently derived from the panCNS and pangene modules, and used to construct CNS synteny networks and gene synteny networks, respectively. (3) Integration of gene–CNS network: A unified network for panCNSs and pangenes is generated by merging CNS and gene synteny networks.

By adopting a reference-free whole-genome alignment strategy, the panCNS tool mitigates the missingness of CNSs caused by single-reference bias, a common limitation of reference-dependent annotation pipelines. It includes the following steps (Fig. 1a): First, reference-free whole-genome multiple alignments are generated using Progressive Cactus; these alignments are then used to identify initial sets of CNSs in each genome via the PhastCons pipeline (Siepel et al. 2005). Next, the MCscan algorithm and blastn are employed to detect syntenic and homologous CNS pairs in all pairwise genome comparisons. Subsequently, homologous CNS groups are identified based on the aforementioned multiple genome alignments, synteny relationships, and similarity scores. CNS synteny networks are then constructed, and they are connected networks, where nodes represent CNSs from different genomes and edges indicate that the 2 connected CNSs originate from a pairwise syntenic region. All connected components in each of these networks are assigned a unique reference-free CNS index. Additionally, based on the multiple alignments and this CNS index, panCNS incorporates a refinement step to retrieve CNSs that are missed in some genomes because of small variations in the vicinity of these regions and stringent cutoffs applied in previous steps (Figure S1). Given that structural variations may disrupt synteny in certain species—leading to fragmentation of clusters that originally belonged to a single index—best-hit information is used to reassign these fragmented indices. Finally, after retrieval and reassignment, a reference-free panCNS is constructed (Fig. 1a; see Materials and Methods for details).

For the pangene tool, OrthoFinder (Emms and Kelly 2019) is first used for gene family clustering. A similar approach to that of panCNS is then applied to identify syntenic homologous gene pairs. Subnetworks of members within the same gene family are extracted from the gene synteny network; thereafter, the clique percolation method (CPM) (Palla et al. 2005) is employed to further cluster genes within each gene family, leveraging synteny information between gene pairs. Like the construction of panCNS, genes lacking synteny alignments are incorporated into the gene index based on the best hit in gene sequence alignments. To further distinguish paralogous genes, a phylogenetic gene tree-based method is integrated during indexing. Through the above steps, a reference-free pangene is obtained (Fig. 1b; see Materials and Methods for details).

The CG module assigns CNSs to potential regulatory target genes based on their proximity (default: within 2 kb upstream or downstream of the gene) and coindices these CNS-gene pairs. This integrative indexing enables the identification of colocalized CNSs and potential target genes across different species, facilitating studies on the coevolution and divergence of genes and noncoding sequences. If this colocalization is consistent in at least 2 species, we define these pairs as conserved gene and noncoding sequence modules (CGNMs), which may reflect the long-term evolutionary maintenance of the gene–CNS regulatory relationship. CGNMs from multiple genes (default: ≥2 genes) that are in close proximity (default: <5 kb apart) are defined as conserved gene and noncoding blocks (CGNBs), in which these genes and CNSs exhibit considerable evolutionary conservation and may collectively participate in regulatory networks.

Additionally, panCG provides a function to associate the gene-PAV or CNV across species with phenotypic divergence, facilitating the identification of trait-related candidate genes. An example is the association between gene-PAV and leaf shape complexity. Leaf shape complexity is a well-characterized trait, with REDUCED COMPLEXITY (RCO) as a key regulatory factor (Vlad et al. 2014) (Figure S2a). We selected 15 Brassicaceae species with defined leaf shape complexity to reconstruct the pangene, which was then used for trait association analysis (Kiefer et al. 2019) (Table S1). A total of 132 gene indices were identified as significantly associated with leaf shape complexity (P-value < 0.05, two-sided Mann–Whitney U test), among which RCO ranked 61st (P-value = 0.02476) (Figure S2b). Given the limited number of species included, we expect that expanding the genome sample size will reduce the false positive rate.

The panCNS and pangene landscape of orange subfamily

We applied the panCG pipeline to the orange subfamily to identify CNSs and construct pangenes. Specifically, we selected 22 representative genomes from the orange subfamily, including economically important fruit species from the Citrus genus (eg sweet orange, pummelo, and mandarin) and additional species from 6 other genera. These genomes exhibited high assembly quality, with an average contig N50 of 11.62 Mb and an average BUSCO assembly completeness score of 98.11% (Table S2).

Across these genomes, we identified 116,671 (6.21 Mb) to 152,220 (13.05 Mb) CNSs, yielding a combined set of 3,035,675 CNSs (Fig. 2a). The average length of CNSs ranged from 53 to 91 bp across genomes (Fig. 2a and Figure S3). Most CNSs were localized in introns (38.11% to 48.61%), promoters (23.94% to 29.49%; defined as the 2 kb upstream region of the transcriptional start site), and gene-distal regions (12.45% to 27.83%; outside genes and their 2 kb upstream/downstream regions) (Fig. 2a). This distribution pattern is analogous to that reported in the cucumber genome (Xin et al. 2024).

Using the panCG pipeline, we defined a total of 167,989 panCNS indices, where each index corresponded to syntenic homologous CNSs from multiple genomes. These CNSs were categorized into 4 types (core, softcore, shell, and cloud) based on their presence frequency across the 22 genomes (Fig. 2b). Notably, 36.8% of panCNSs were core elements (shared by all 22 selected genomes), which was much higher than the proportion of core pangenes (15.9%) (Fig. 2c). In parallel, we grouped 627,439 genes from the 22 genomes into 54,026 gene indices (ie the pangene set) (Fig. 2c). As the number of included genomes increased, the total counts of panCNSs indices and pangene indices both increased gradually. However, panCNS indices reached a plateau faster than pangene indices, indicating a more conserved regulatory landscape in the orange subfamily (Fig. 2d and e).

As expected, in a Citrus population with 213 individuals, the SNP density, nucleotide diversity (π), and Watterson's theta (θW) of CNSs showed a decreasing trend with increasing levels of conservation (Figure S4a to c and Table S3). Notably, π and θW of the 4 CNS categories were consistently lower than those of CDSs, indicating that CNSs have experienced stronger evolutionary constraints than CDSs. Additionally, CNSs contained a higher proportion of rare variants (Figure S4d). These results suggest that CNSs in Citrus are under purifying selection, consistent with previous findings in Arabidopsis, maize, and rice (Haudry et al. 2013; Song et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2022).

Using the CG module of panCG, we indexed 92,578 to 120,506 CNS-gene pairs, where CNSs were located within the genes or their 2 kb upstream/downstream regions. Specifically, 62.79% to 80.56% (16,926 to 21,148) of genes were linked to at least 1 CNS. Notably, the 4 panCNS categories showed preferential colocalization with pangenes of matching conservation types (eg core panCNSs colocalized more frequently with core pangenes) (Fig. 2f), suggesting that panCNSs and pangenes share similar conservation pattern. Furthermore, we observed that CNSs with higher conservation levels were associated with greater synteny and sequence conservation in their target genes across species, demonstrating significant colocalization between CNSs and their regulatory target genes (Fig. 2g and h).

Functional implications of candidate cis-regulatory CNSs of the orange subfamily

Next, we annotated candidate regulatory elements within CNSs using 3 complementary omics datasets, including ATAC-seq, histone modification-based ChIP-seq, and transcriptional factor-based ChIP-seq/DAP-seq (Table S4). Approximately 44.63% of (74,971) panCNSs were annotated with at least 1 omics feature (Fig. 3a). Notably, the largest subset (26.33%, 44,231) of these panCNSs overlapped with accessible chromatin regions (ACRs; annotated based on ATAC-seq), whereas others mapped to other functional profiles include ChIP-seq peaks for histone modifications (H3K4me3 and H3K27me3), transcriptional factor binding sites (TFBS), and TF footprints. These panCNSs showed 0.64 to 3.11-fold enrichment compared with random regions, suggesting their potential role as transcriptional regulatory elements (Figure 3a and b and Figure S5a). Additionally, panCNSs exhibited a positive correlation with local chromatin accessibility (Figure S6a), reflecting their involvement in active transcriptional regulation. Among these functionally annotated panCNSs, highly conserved elements were significantly enriched in promoter regions yet depleted in distal intergenic regions. Conversely, less conserved panCNSs displayed the opposite distribution pattern (Figure S6b). These observations might point to functional divergence: highly conserved panCNSs likely serve as core promoter elements, whereas less conserved ones may act as distal enhancers. Besides, CNSs were enriched in hypomethylated regions where chromatin is typically open and enriched for transcriptional regulatory elements (Fig. 3b and Figure S5b and c). Moreover, CNS density was significantly higher in expressed genes than in nonexpressed genes (Figure S7), further supporting their involvement in transcriptional regulation. Collectively, these results indicate that CNSs are effective candidates for identifying regulatory elements, which are crucial for deciphering the regulatory mechanisms of gene expression.

Given that core CNSs are conserved across all orange subfamily species, they are likely to play evolutionarily conserved regulatory roles. We further focused on these core CNSs to identify subfamily specific regulatory elements by comparing their presence/absence with outgroup species including Aesculus chinensis (Sun et al. 2023), Arabidopsis thaliana (Lamesch et al. 2011), Malus domestica (Daccord et al. 2017), Melia azedarach (Martin et al. 2023), Pistacia vera (Kafkas et al. 2023), Sapindus mukorossi (Xue et al. 2022), Solanum lycopersicum (Hosmani et al. 2019), Toona ciliata (Wang et al. 2022), Xylocarpus granatum (He et al. 2022), Zanthoxylum armatum (Hu et al. 2022), and Zanthoxylum bungeanum (Hu et al. 2022).

We identified 30,206 orange subfamily specific CNSs (absent in outgroup species) (Fig. 3c), whose associated target genes are enriched in the positive regulation of multiple metabolic pathways (FDR = 3.63e-4) (Table S5). This finding suggests that these CNSs may function as regulatory elements that regulate the expression of target genes involved in metabolism-related processes. Among the 63,895 core CNSs shared by all orange subfamily species, 23,161 (36.25%) overlapped with ACRs and were thus defined as accessible core CNSs. Notably, 2,598 (11.22%) of these accessible core CNSs were orange subfamily specific (Fig. 3c), of which 38.53% (1,001) were localized to promoter regions, where they might function as cis-regulatory elements (Fig. 3d). For example, a Ruby1-binding motif (AACCACC) identified in the CsMYB3 promoter was located at 218 bp upstream of the transcriptional start site within an ATAC-seq-defined ACR (Fig. 3e and Figure S8). The R2R3-MYB TF Ruby1 binds this motif to regulate anthocyanin biosynthesis in citrus (Huang et al. 2018, 2020). Notably, this Ruby1-binding CNS is absent in outgroup species, suggesting that it may have evolved specifically in the orange subfamily to fine-tune anthocyanin biosynthesis, a trait critical for citrus fruit quality. Similarly, a CNS in the PH5 promoter contains a PH4 binding site (Huang et al. 2023) (Figure S9a). Additionally, 618 (23.79%) of the subfamily specific regulatory CNSs localized to the gene-distal regions (Fig. 3d). For example, a distal enhancer-like CNS upstream of CsLMI1 harbors a binding site for the ERF transcription factor CsDRNL (Wang et al. 2024a), which is also absent in outgroup species (Figure S9b).

Consistent with previous studies (Maurano et al. 2012), several trait-associated genome-wide association study (GWAS) loci were found within CNSs (Table S6) (Minamikawa et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2024; Liang et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2024b). A striking example is a SNP significantly associated with the β-cryptoxanthin content (a key carotenoid in citrus) that maps to a CNS 17.7 kb downstream of CitCYP97B (Zhang et al. 2024a) (Fig. 3f and Figure S8). As distal CNSs often function as enhancers, we hypothesize that this SNP may alter TF-binding affinity, thereby regulating CitCYP97B expression and β-cryptoxanthin accumulation. These findings highlight CNSs as promising candidates for unraveling transcriptional regulatory networks in the orange subfamily, though functional experimental validation is essential to confirm their biological roles.

CNS variants associate with expression differentiation of duplicated genes

To further explore the functional role of CNSs, we examined how sequence variants and PAVs of CNSs impact the expression of duplicated genes. Gene duplication is a prevalent phenomenon in the plant kingdom, with whole-genome duplication (WGD) events being a major driver (Jiao et al. 2011; Qiao et al. 2019). In orange subfamily species, only WGD events conserved across angiosperms have been identified. We first identified 13,279 to 28,609 duplicated gene pairs across orange subfamily genomes and classified them into 5 duplication modes: WGD (10.03% to 32.02%), tandem (TD) (6.30% to 11.62%), proximal (PD) (5.17% to 10.54%), transposed (TRD) (13.12% to 24.63%), and dispersed (DSD) (27.19% to 59.11%) duplication modes (Table S7). Among them, WGD-derived duplicated gene pairs showed the highest conservation in the pangene set, whereas PD-derived duplicates tended to exhibit species- or lineage-specific retention patterns (Figure S10). For the panCNS set, 71,270 to 92,603 CNSs colocalized (located within genes or in their 2 kb flanking regions) with 13,279 to 28,609 duplicated gene pairs. Given that gene duplication is often accompanied by coduplication of regulatory elements, we categorized the CNSs associated with duplicated genes into 2 types: codup-CNSs (duplicated CNSs, where each copy colocalizes with one member of a duplicated gene pair) and solo-CNSs (singleton CNSs that colocalize only with one member of a duplicated gene pair) (Fig. 3g).

We focused on the sweet orange genome for detailed analysis of CNS-duplicate gene associations, as it is a well-assembled reference genome for the orange subfamily with characterized transcriptomic data. Our analysis revealed a significantly higher prevalence of solo-CNSs (78,468 CNSs, 96.91%) compared with codup-CNSs (2,499 CNSs, 3.09%) in the sweet orange genome. Transcriptomic profiling demonstrated that gene pairs associated with solo-CNSs exhibited more pronounced expression divergence than those linked to codup-CNSs (two-tailed Wilcoxon test, P-value = 3.1e-7), which is likely attributed to sequence divergence in their regulatory elements (Fig. 3h). In particular, duplicated genes originating from WGD and TRD exhibited more significant expression differences (Figure S11). These observations collectively indicate that sequence diversification and degradation of duplicated CNSs may drive the evolution of divergent expression profiles among duplicated gene pairs. For solo-CNSs-associated gene pairs where CNS conservation was lost because of sequence variation, 49.75% of these pairs exhibited expression differences that could be linked to variants within CNSs. Further motif analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity in TFBS composition among solo-CNSs, whereas codup-CNSs maintained relatively uniform motif architecture (Fig. 3i). These results imply that variants in CNSs may lead to the loss of TFBSs, thereby triggering expression divergence of duplicated genes.

The impact of TE mobilization on CNS evolution in the orange subfamily

Given that TE are major drivers of genomic variation (Huang et al. 2023), we investigated the impact of transposable element insertions on panCNSs across genomes of the orange subfamily. On average, 30.66% of panCNS loss per species was associated with TE insertion in the CNSs or TE-associated deletions of partial or whole CNS regions (Fig. 4a). Notably, approximately half of the CNS sequence losses were caused by long terminal repeat (LTR)-type TEs (Fig. 4b). Beyond inducing CNS loss, TE mobilization also shapes CNS distribution. More conserved CNSs (core > softcore > shell > cloud) were located farther from TEs (Fig. 4c) and showed significant depletion within TEs, particularly LTRs (Fig. 4d). Across all 22 orange subfamily genomes, a low TE-CNS overlap (4.32% to 9.11%) pattern was observed (Fig. 3a). For example, only 7,498 (5.69%) CNSs in the sweet orange genome overlapped with TEs (Fig. 3a), with shell CNSs accounting for the majority (71.30%) (Fig. 4e). Furthermore, CNSs tended to localize within older LTRs, which exhibit higher sequence divergence (Figure S12a). We further found that ancient TE insertions (divergence rate > 0.2) contributed to 54.77% of TE-derived CNSs, whereas recent TE insertions (divergence rate < 0.05) contributed 21.98% (Figure S12b). These results together suggest that older TEs may accumulate mutations that “domesticate” them into stable CNSs, whereas young, active TEs may disrupt CNS conservation.

Despite their low proportion, TE-overlapping CNSs may harbor CREs as 51.75% of them overlapped with ATAC-seq peaks or other functional genomic profiles in the sweet orange genome. These functional TE-overlapping CNSs are also supported by previous studies, which demonstrated that TEs can harbor transcriptional regulatory elements and modulate downstream gene expression (Wang et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2021). For instance, a core CNS within a miniature inverted-repeat transposable element (MITE) insertion in the RWP gene promoter cosegregates with the polyembryony trait (Wang et al. 2017) (Fig. 4f and Figure S13a). Additionally, functional enrichment analysis revealed that the target genes of core and softcore TE-overlapping CNSs were enriched for GO terms including “catalytic activity on DNA” and “calcium-dependent phospholipid binding” (Table S8), further supporting their potential role in transcriptional regulation and stress responses. Collectively, these demonstrate that whereas TEs strongly impact the evolutionary maintenance and genomic distribution of CNSs, they can also drive the origin of functional CNSs.

The coevolution of CNSs and function-associated genes in the orange subfamily

As CREs and their target genes function cooperatively, they are expected to exhibit coordinated evolutionary patterns. We further investigated the coevolutionary relationships between CNSs (as candidate CREs) and their potentially functional target genes across the entire orange subfamily. Using the panCG pipeline with the sweet orange reference genome, a total of 22,115 CGNMs (including 22,115 CNSs and 6,488 genes) were identified and found to be conserved across all 22 orange subfamily genomes, indicating a highly conserved coevolutionary relationship between these CNSs and their target genes. Specifically, within these CGNMs, we identified 1,391 core CGNBs, of which each showed conserved colocalization of CNSs and their associated genes. As defined by panCG, each core CGNB contained multiple (≥2, default) closely positioned CGNMs, with an average of 2.5 genes and 8.6 CNSs per CGNB (Fig. 5a to c).

CNSs (11,963) within these CGNBs were significantly enriched for TF motifs from the bHLH and bZIP families (Fisher's exact test, P-value < 1e-15) (Fig. 5d), both of which have been well-documented to play essential roles in developmental regulation and stress responses (Dröge-Laser et al. 2018; Tan et al. 2021). To further validate this, we performed phylogenetic analyses based on the CNSs and their associated genes within core CGNBs. The resulting phylogenetic trees exhibited nearly identical topologies across species (Fig. 5f), providing further evidence for the coevolution of CNSs and their target genes.

Domesticated genes and CNSs in the orange subfamily

The orange subfamily includes economically important domesticated species such as mandarin, pummelo, and their hybrid species sweet orange. Citrus domestication has been linked to improved fruit taste and yield, alongside reduced fruit acidity, altered levels of certain secondary metabolites, and diminished stress tolerance (Rao et al. 2021). Plant domestication is often accompanied by the gain and loss of key genes (Long et al. 2024a), a process that can be accurately inferred from interspecies syntenic network reconstruction. To identify candidate domestication-related genes, panCG provides a synteny network-based approach, which has been successfully applied to capture gene-PAVs across multiple species (Zhao et al. 2017). We used panCG extracted subnetworks from the gene syntenic network of the orange subfamily, focusing on 4 cultivated species (C. sinensis, C. maxima, C. reticulata “Ponkan,” and C. limon) and 6 closely related wild species (C. articulata, L. scandens, M. paniculata, A. marmelos, A. buxifolia, and C. lansium). In cultivated species, 128 syntenic gene subnetworks were significantly expanded and enriched for Gene Ontology (GO) terms such as monooxygenase and oxidoreductase activity (Fig. 6a and Table S10). Genes annotated with these terms are typically involved in flavonoid, sugar, and organic acid metabolism. This suggests that expanded subnetworks may contribute to the domestication of citrus fruit color and flavor.

Notably, a MADS-box transcription factor family syntenic network was significantly enriched in the cultivated species. To resolve its evolutionary dynamics, we constructed a synteny network for 573 MADS-box genes across the cultivated-wild species and identified 75 subnetworks (Fig. 6c). Cluster 67, one of these subnetworks, showed significant expansion in cultivated species and corresponded to a tandem duplication cluster within the MADS-box gene family (Fig. 6b and Table S11). This gene cluster was absent in most wild species, with the exception of M. paniculata (Fig. 6c). Previous studies have demonstrated that the MADS-box transcription factor family plays a critical role in regulating citrus fruit traits such as coloration (Lu et al. 2018, 2021; Zhu et al. 2023). Thus, we hypothesize that this tandem gene cluster in the MADS family contributed to citrus domestication.

Apart from gene-PAVs, the gain or loss of CREs can also drive domestication (Rubin et al. 2010; Zheng et al. 2019b) by reorganizing syntenic networks of noncoding sequences. Using the panCNS dataset, we identified 2,575 CNS syntenic networks specific to cultivated citrus compared with wild relatives. A notable example is a 76 bp CNS located within an ACR (Figure S14a), 650 bp downstream of CCD4b—a gene encoding a key enzyme in carotenoid metabolism that synthesizes β-citraurin and the C30 apocarotenoid β-citraurinene (Zheng et al. 2019a). This CNS was specific to cultivated species (Figure S14b), suggesting it may be linked to the domestication of citrus fruit coloration.

To complement interspecies analyses, we used intraspecies population genetics to explore CNS roles in domestication. These analyses leveraged a published resequencing dataset of 12 wild and 57 domesticated mandarin individuals (Wang et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2024a). Using Fst and XP-CLR scores, we identified 11,891 CNSs within or adjacent to regions under selective sweeps between wild and domesticated mandarin (Fig. 6d). These CNSs were within or near (2 kb distance) genes and corresponded to 2,147 CGNMs (with 2,147 CNSs and 883 genes). GO enrichment analysis revealed these genes were significantly enriched in terms related to hydrolase activity (FDR = 2.23e-2), which are associated with the formation of fruit sweetness in citrus (Table S12). For example, 1 CGNM associated with SWEET16, which has been shown to regulate sugar accumulation during citrus ripening (Yin et al. 2025), contained 2 CNSs in its promoter region (Fig. 6e). Both CNSs reside within open chromatin regions, indicating their role in regulating SWEET16 expression (Fig. 6e).

The panCNS dataset directly captures cross-species CNS-PAVs and facilitates the identification of species-specific sequence variants within CNSs (Figure S15a). Using this dataset, we identified 21 deletions, 1,087 insertions, and 3,828 SNPs within CNSs of the sweet orange reference genome (Table S13). Among these variants, 1,976 (40.03%) were predicted to disrupt TF-binding motifs, including those from the DOF and NAC TF families that are associated with fruit development (Figure S15b), though experimental validation is required. In addition, across all 16 Citrus species, we detected 153 deletions, 1,659 insertions, and 10,802 SNPs within CNSs (Table S13). Of these variants, 38.05% were predicted to affect the binding of specific TF families, similar to the pattern observed for sweet orange-specific CNS sequence variants (Figure S15c). Additionally, target genes of variant-containing CNSs were significantly enriched in pathways related to sequence-specific DNA-binding and developmental processes, suggesting that CNS sequence variation may have shaped developmental regulation during citrus evolution (Table S14).

Based on these findings, we propose a hypothetical molecular evolution model for the domestication of citrus fruit traits, highlighting the potential synergistic roles of genes and CNSs (Fig. 7). First, gene family expansion or contraction, such as the tandem duplication of the MADS-box TF cluster, may lay the foundation for fruit trait variation. Furthermore, critical CNS-PAV (for instance the cultivated-specific CNS downstream of CCD4b) or sequence variants in CNSs linked to key genes (such as SWEET16) might introduce novel or disrupt existing transcriptional regulatory elements. These genic and noncoding variations are hypothesized to jointly restructure transcriptional networks, with CNS gains/losses and polymorphisms likely fine-tuning gene expression. Collectively, these multidimensional molecular changes may converge to drive core citrus domestication traits, including enhanced fruit color, optimized sugar content and improved flavor.

Discussion

Intraspecies pangenome studies have revealed the conserved and variable components of genomes, elucidating the diversity among different varieties (Li et al. 2023; Long et al. 2024b; Zhang et al. 2024b). Cross-species pangenome studies further expand this framework by incorporating genomic information from multiple species, enabling insights into genome characteristics from broader evolutionary and functional perspectives (Shi et al. 2024; Benoit et al. 2025). However, applying intraspecies pangenome methods, such as graph-based approaches, to cross-species studies faces significant challenges (Du et al. 2025), primarily because of substantial sequence divergence between species. Current cross-species pangenome research predominantly focuses on gene-level analytical paradigms and lacks systematic characterization of noncoding regions, even though these regions can function as promoters, enhancers, and various other key transcriptional regulatory elements. Furthermore, gene-based pangenome studies typically rely on orthologous gene clustering methods, which may yield relatively coarse gene family classifications and hinder precise gene-PAV association analyses. These technical limitations are particularly evident when analyzing tandemly repeated genes like RCO, which regulates leaf shape complexity in Brassicaceae (Vlad et al. 2014; Kiefer et al. 2019).

In this study, we developed the panCG pipeline to construct cross-species pan-genomes at both the gene and noncoding sequence levels. Unlike traditional pan-genome studies that rely on orthologous genes (eg GeneTribe and GENESAPCE), panCG not only constructs gene-level pan-genomes but also introduces the concept of panCNS, representing the repertoire of potential CREs across genomes. For initial panCNS construction, panCG employs a reference-free strategy, similar to recent comparative genomic research on mammals and apes (Christmas et al. 2023; Yoo et al. 2025), to mitigate the single-reference bias inherent to other CNS annotation methods (Siepel et al. 2005; Liang et al. 2018; Tan et al. 2019; Song et al. 2021).

Notably, panCG incorporates a refinement step that is not utilized in studies on mammals and apes (Christmas et al. 2023; Yoo et al. 2025). PanCG can further utilize this initial panCNS as a template to retrieve the missing CNSs for each genome because of imperfect initial multiway alignment in some local regions (Figure S1). This refinement step enables capturing a more complete panCNS repertoire and reducing the number of false CNS-PAVs. For example, this step retrieved 338,344 (11.15% of the full panCNS set) CNSs in total across 22 orange subfamily genomes. Further characterization of 10,587 (8.06%) retrieved CNSs in sweet orange revealed that they presented typical patterns of evolutionary conservation and epigenetic signatures with other CNSs (Figure S16).

Moreover, by integrating synteny network analyses and phylogenetic analyses, panCG precisely defines homologous genes and CNSs, which is critical for associating gene-PAVs or CNS-PAVs with interspecies phenotypic divergence. In contrast, traditional methods such as OrthoFinder (Emms and Kelly 2019) do not consider genome collinearity when identifying homologous genes. Compared with another synteny-aware pangene analysis tool, GENESPACE (Lovell et al. 2022), panCG achieved comparable performance in refining homologous gene assignment (Figure S17 and Table S15), and better performance in resolving complex orthologous and paralogous relationships for proximal and tandem duplicated genes (Figure S18 and Table S16). This is largely attributed to the refinement step via CPM clustering and the incorporation of phylogenetic relationships for each initial syntenic gene group. Notably, in interspecific PAV-trait association analysis for the RCO locus, the panCG-constructed pangene set detected a significant association (P-value = 0.02476; two-sided Mann–Whitney U test), whereas GENESPACE failed to identify this signal (P-value = 0.12477, Figure S17a).

However, constructing robust syntenic relationships depends on the phylogenetic distance among the focused species. For pangene construction with panCG, we estimated that the maximum phylogenetic distance between target species should ideally not surpass 80 Mya (corresponding to an average of 50% collinear regions), with an optimal performance range within 60 Mya (60% collinear regions on average) (Figure S19). In addition, for gene-PAV association with panCG, our sensitivity assessment indicated that at least 13 species were required to achieve sufficient statistical power for the case of RCO associated with leaf phenotype (Figure S20). With the growing availability of sequenced species, such interspecies association studies hold promise for disentangling trait divergence across lineages.

We systematically applied the panCG pipeline to characterize the pangenome of the orange subfamily, identifying a large set of noncoding sequences with potential regulatory functions. Consistent with findings in Brassicaceae and maize (Haudry et al. 2013; Song et al. 2021), the CNSs in the orange subfamily are under strong purifying selection. Additionally, these CNSs are significantly enriched in open chromatin regions and other functional genomic profiles, which aligns with observations in Solanaceae and Cucurbitaceae (Hendelman et al. 2021; Xin et al. 2024), supporting their roles in modulating gene expression. Unlike these prior studies, however, our work presents a genome-wide, species-comprehensive panCNS catalog for each genome in the orange subfamily. This exhaustive panCNS repertoire serves as an important resource for dissecting the genetic basis of fruit biology in the orange subfamily, a lineage characterized by many economically important domesticated fruit crops and substantial phenotypic diversity.

Our results show that CNS variation is associated with expression divergence of duplicated genes in citrus, which is similar to observations in maize subgenomes (Song et al. 2021). Consistent with reports of regulatory region duplication in soybean (Fang et al. 2023), sequence variations in duplicated CNSs often lead to loss of TFBSs, which may rewire the regulatory networks of duplicated genes. These findings highlight the critical role of CNSs in the functional evolution of protein-coding genes. Notably, TE mobilization is a major driver of CNS loss, although a small subset of CNSs may originate from TE insertions. In particular, TE-derived ready-made regulatory motifs can be coopted by the host into novel CNSs, which are fixed during domestication (Batista et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2022). Additionally, TEs facilitate piggybacking, where mobilization events capture and relocate existing CNSs to new loci (Butelli et al. 2012; Bubb et al. 2025), such as the presence of a MITE in the promoter region of the RWP in our study. Finally, TEs induce local environmental remodeling, which can either generate new CNSs by unmasking latent regulatory sequences or destroy existing ones through the spread of epigenetic silencing (Noshay et al. 2020; Colonna Romano and Fanti 2022; Liu et al. 2022).

Integrative analyses of CNSs and their colocalized genes can theoretically help uncover their coevolution during speciation and crop domestication. Remarkably, using the panCG pipeline, we found that transcription factors were significantly enriched in highly conserved CGNMs, suggesting strong coevolution between transcription factors and their regulatory sequences. This co-conservation likely maintains the stability of TF–TF regulatory networks and shapes the coevolutionary dynamics in the orange subfamily. Additionally, lineage-specific gene synteny networks partially reflect cultivated-wild divergence during the domestication of fruit species (Kuwada et al. 2024). Importantly, interspecies synteny network analyses combined with intraspecies population genetics revealed numerous candidate genes and CNSs linked to domestication, supported by some known examples (Zheng et al. 2019a, 2019b; Yin et al. 2025). Thus, our study provides functional CNS candidates in the orange subfamily for future functional validation and genomic-assisted breeding. Remarkably, these candidate CNSs and their associated genes synergize via multifaceted alterations to rewire transcriptional regulatory networks and modulate gene expression, thereby driving the evolution of key citrus domestication-related fruit traits (Fig. 7). Subsequent investigations that incorporate additional epigenomic or regulomic data (eg histone modifications and 3D chromatin interactions) together with functional experiments will help to elucidate the precise regulatory mechanisms by which these key CGNMs contribute to citrus domestication.

Future research could establish a cross-species functional annotation platform for CNSs, facilitating the transfer of regulatory element knowledge across lineages. This platform would enable systematic exploration of panCNSs and pangenes across diverse species, along with their transcriptional and epigenetic features. Additionally, it could leverage increasingly available multiomics data and emerging deep learning frameworks to dissect CNS functions (Sokolova et al. 2024; Barbadilla-Martínez et al. 2025).

Materials and methods

The development of panCG pipeline

The panCG pipeline consists of 3 modules including panCNS, pangene, and CG.

The workflow of panCNS

The panCNS module is designed to annotate CNSs across multiple genomes of phylogenetically closely related species, thus enabling comprehensive characterization of the interspecies super-pangenome at the CNS level (defined as panCNS). Through indexing panCNSs across all genomes via genomic synteny, it facilitates identifying PAV and CNVs of CNSs. The panCNS module requires FASTA-format assembly sequence files and GFF-format gene annotation files from multiple genomes as inputs. Its workflow includes 7 steps.

(1) Multiple genome alignment: The panCNS module starts by performing reference-free multiple genome alignment using Progressive Cactus (v2.5.1) (Armstrong et al. 2020). (2) CNS identification: The phyloFit and phastCons algorithms from the PHAST package (v1.6) are used to identify CNSs (Hubisz et al. 2010). First, based on whole-genome alignments and CDS annotations, the msa_view program extracts 4-fold degenerate synonymous sites. Next, PhyloFit trains a nonconserved model whereas PhastCons (with the “–estimate-rho” parameter), trains a conserved model. PhastCons (with the “–score” parameter), then assigns a conservation score (PhastCons score) to each base. Following the approach described by Haudry et al. (2013), CNSs are defined as regions meeting 3 criteria: (i) spanning at least 5 bp (default) with continuous phastCons scores higher than 0.82 (default, noted as hPC); (ii) flanked by bases with phastCons scores higher than 0.54 (default, noted as lPC); (iii) not overlapping with CDS regions or assembly gaps. This entire process is iterated with each input species as the reference genome, identifying all potential CNSs across all genomes, which are designated as the initial CNS set.

(3) Identification of homologous CNS groups: Conceptually, this step is similar to orthologous gene clustering in OrthoFinder. First, for each CNS in the initial set, its homologous CNSs in other genomes are identified using the halLiftover tool from the hal toolkit (Hickey et al. 2013) based on multiple sequence alignments generated in Step (1). To alleviate the loss of some homologous CNSs caused by imperfect multiple genome alignment in specific regions, and to provide sequence similarity scores for Step (7), we additionally use Blastn (v2.13.0) (with parameters: “-task blastn-short -word_size 7 -gapopen 5 -gapextend 2 -reward 1 -penalty -3”) to identify homologous CNS pairs. Besides, we use MCScan (v1.4.21) (Wang et al. 2012) to identify syntenic CNS pairs within syntenic blocks that contain at least 5 homologous CNSs and fewer than ten gaps. Finally, CNSs from all genomes are incorporated into homologous CNS networks, where each node corresponds to a CNS and each edge indicates that the 2 connected CNSs form a syntenic pair (derived from MCScan outputs) or a homologous pair (derived from halLiftover and Blastn outputs). Each connected component in these networks (ie any 2 nodes are mutually reachable) is defined as a homologous CNS group. (4) Identification of synteny CNS subgroups: Subsequently, each homologous CNS group is divided into 1 or multiple synteny CNS subgroups (ie subnetworks) based on the syntenic relationships within the network.

(5) Index assignment: All CNSs corresponding to nodes in each syntenic CNS subgroups (derived from different species) are assigned a common unique index, referred to as the panCNS index. This step standardizes the annotation of orthologous CNSs across the super-pangenome. (6) CNS retrieval: Some CNSs are initially identified in certain genomes but missed in others because of small variations localized within or adjacent to these elements. For the homologous regions of these missed CNSs in other genomes, if their PhastCons scores are greater than hPC+lPC2 and they do not overlap with CDS regions, they are regarded as missing CNSs and assigned the same panCNS index as their homologous counterparts.

(7) Index correction: This step corrects potential misannotations caused by sequence variations or structural differences, ensuring the accuracy and consistency of panCNS indices. (i) Two adjacent smaller CNSs in 1 genome may share the same homologous CNS in all other genomes, attributed to small-scale sequence variations between them. In such cases, both of the two adjacent CNSs are assigned to the same panCNS index. (ii) For panCNS indices with only 1 CNS and no syntenic counterparts in other genomes (caused by structural variations), the panCNS module reassigns the same index as that of the CNS's best homologous sequence (with the highest Blastn score) in other genomes. (iii) Additionally, the panCNS module excludes panCNS indices corresponding to CNSs shared by fewer than 3 species (default threshold), as these sequences are likely species-specific.

The workflow of pangene

The pangene module is designed to construct the super-pangenome at the protein-coding gene level (defined as pangene), enabling indexing all genes among different genomes and identifying gene-PAVs, CNVs, and local synteny. It includes 4 steps and requires the same input files as panCNS.

(1) Ortholog group identification. Orthologous gene groups are identified using OrthoFinder (v2.5.4) (Emms and Kelly 2019). (2) Syntenic ortholog clustering: First, Diamond (v2.1.9) (Buchfink et al. 2021) is used to perform blastp searches, identifying homologous protein pairs. Subsequently, MCScan (v1.4.21) (Wang et al. 2012) is used to identify syntenic gene pairs from the outputs of Diamond, following the same criteria as panCNS. Then, for each ortholog group resulting from Step (1), syntenic gene networks are constructed based on gene synteny across species, with nodes representing genes and edges denoting synteny relationships between them. Next, the Clique Percolation Method (Palla et al. 2005) is applied to further partition genes into subnetworks within each syntenic gene network.

(3) Inclusion of nonsyntenic orthologous genes. For genes within ortholog groups but lacking synteny with other members, best-hit relationships inferred from Diamond outputs are incorporated to ensure their inclusion into the syntenic gene network. A best-hit gene is defined as the gene with the highest alignment score in the gene pool of the analyzed species.

(4) Tree-based clustering for paralog distinction: To distinguish paralogous genes because of gene duplication, genes within each subnetwork obtained in the previous step are further clustered based on phylogenetic gene tree topology. Specifically, for each subnetwork, protein sequences of all constituent genes are aligned using MAFFT (v7.490) (Katoh and Standley 2013). Phylogenetic gene trees are then constructed from these alignments using FastTree (v2.1.11) (Price et al. 2010). The resulting gene trees are parsed using ETE3 (v3.1.3) (Huerta-Cepas et al. 2016), with paralogous gene clades distinguished according to tree topological structures.

The workflow of CG

(1) CNS-gene colocalization analysis: ChIPseeker (v1.40.0) (Yu et al. 2015) is used for CNSs annotation of genomic distribution and associated them with their potential target genes independently for each species. Sequences within 2 kb upstream of the gene's transcription start site (TSS) and 2 kb downstream of the transcription termination site (TTS) are defined as promoter and downstream regions, respectively. If a CNS is located within a gene or its promoter or downstream region, the gene is designated as the target gene of that CNS.

(2) Synteny network construction: CNS and gene synteny networks are obtained by panCNS and pangene, respectively, as described above. (3) Integration of gene–CNS networks: To generate a unified regulatory network for evolutionary analysis, the CNS and gene synteny networks are merged into gene–CNS networks, where nodes represent genes or CNSs and edges indicate syntenic relationships (for interspecies CNS–CNS or gene–gene synteny) or gene–CNS colocalization. To characterize the evolutionary conservation of gene–CNS colocalization, 2 terms, CGNMs and CGNBs, are defined. A CGNM is defined as a gene–CNS pair wherein the annotated target relationship is conserved across a minimum number of species (default: ≥2). Multiple (default: ≥2) adjacent (default: <5 kb) CGNMs are further grouped into CGNBs.

Construction of panCNS and pangene in the orange subfamily

Twenty-two high-quality genomes from species within the orange subfamily were selected (Table S2). For those genomes assembled only at the contig level, RagTag (v2.1.0) (Alonge et al. 2022) was employed to scaffold them to the chromosome level based on the genomes of closely related species.

We first performed gene family analysis using OrthoFinder (v2.5.4), resulting in the identification of 3,382 single-copy orthologous genes. The corresponding amino acid sequences from the 22 species were then aligned using MUSCLE (v5.1) (Edgar 2004). Based on this concatenated alignment, a maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using IQ-TREE2 (v2.4.0) (Minh et al. 2020) with the parameters “-B 1000 –bnni.” Finally, divergence times were estimated using the MCMCTree program within the PAML (v4.9) (Yang 2007). The resulting phylogenetic tree served as the guide tree for the Progressive Cactus pipeline to facilitate multiple sequence alignment. Utilizing the aforementioned panCG pipeline, we constructed the initial CNS repertoire, the panCNS, and the pangene.

Gene-PAV association analysis

Because of constraints in assembly quality, we selected 15 Brassicaceae species with leaf shape complexity traits (Kiefer et al. 2019) and constructed a pangene set. For each gene index in the pangene, gene-PAV was associated with the trait. The two-sided Mann–Whitney U test was conducted to calculate the P-value for each gene index-trait association, with P-values < 0.05 considered indicative of candidate gene indices associated with the trait.

Differential syntenic gene and CNS networks analysis across lineages

For the analysis of lineage-specific syntenic gene networks, the following steps were performed: First, each cluster in the syntenic gene network was extracted, and the number of genes in each species within the cluster was counted. The two-sided Mann–Whitney U test was used to calculate the P-value. The average gene number across the 2 lineages was used to calculate the fold change. Clusters with “P-value < 0.05 and |log2FoldChange| > 1” were considered as gene clusters with differential copy number variation.

For the analysis of lineage-specific CNS networks, the following criteria were applied: (i) The syntenic CNS cluster contained a majority of species from lineage I. (ii) No species from lineage II were present in the cluster. Such CNS clusters were defined as lineage I-specific CNSs clusters.

Evolutionary analysis of CNSs

Sequence variation in CNSs across species

For analyzing sequence variation in CNSs, sweet orange was selected as the reference genome. The cactus-hal2maf utility from the Cactus toolkit was employed to generate pairwise MAF alignments between the reference and target genomes. Subsequently, wgatools (v0.1.0) (Wei et al. 2025) was used to identify SNPs and indels based on these alignments, and the resulting variant calls were integrated using bcftools (v1.9) (Danecek et al. 2021). Within the same CNS index, variations that occur exclusively in the CNS of the target species, whereas remaining conserved in the corresponding CNSs of all other species, were defined as species-specific sequence variations.

CNSs and duplicated genes

The DupGen_finder pipeline (Qiao et al. 2019) was employed for the identification of duplicate genes. To identify duplicated CNS pairs, a self-genome alignment was performed to obtain CNSs and their corresponding mapping regions (CMRs). In cases where both a CNS and its CMR overlapped the regulatory regions of duplicated gene pairs (defined as the region from 2 kb upstream of the TSS to 2 kb downstream of the TTS), 2 scenarios were distinguished: If the CMR corresponds to a CNS, the pair was designated as a codup-CNS pair; otherwise, it was classified as a solo-CNS pair. Motif occurrences within CNSs were identified using FIMO (v5.5.5) (Grant et al. 2011), applying a P-value threshold of <0.0001. Motif counts were normalized by length, with motif data sourced from JASPAR2024 (Rauluseviciute et al. 2023). For gene pairs associated with solo-CNSs that have lost conservation because of sequence variations, we examined the types of motifs within the CNSs of duplicate genes. If |log2FoldChange| > 1 and the motif types differ, we considered this as potentially resulting from functional variations within the CNSs.

Phylogenetic tree construction

For phylogenetic tree construction, protein sequences of genes and CNSs within core CGNBs were extracted for phylogenetic analyses. Following the methodologies described above, multiple sequence alignments for both protein (genes) and nucleotide sequences (CNSs) were performed using MUSCLE. Subsequently, ML phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using IQ-TREE2.

Intraspecies genetic diversity in CNSs

For variant calling, raw reads were processed using fastp (v0.23.2) (Chen et al. 2018) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. Filtered reads were aligned to the sweet orange reference genome using BWA MEM (v0.7.17-r1198), and SAMtools (v1.15) was used for sorting. Duplicate reads were removed using GATK (v4.6.0.0) (McKenna et al. 2010). Variants were called using GATK HaplotypeCaller with the “-ERC GVCF” parameter. The GenomicsDBImport and GenotypeGVCFs in GATK were used for merging. Finally, variant filtration was performed using gatk VariantFiltration with the following parameters: “QD < 2.0, FS > 60.0, MQ < 40.0, MQRankSum < −12.5, ReadPosRankSum < −8.0.” BCFTools was used to filter out variant sites with MAF < 0.05 and a missing rate >0.5.

Regions under selective sweeps were identified by combining population fixation index (Fst) and the cross-population composite likelihood ratio (XP-CLR). VCFtools (v0.1.17) (Danecek et al. 2011) was used to calculate pairwise Fst values between wild and domesticated citrus populations, using 50-kb windows with 10-kb sliding steps. The same window parameters were applied for XP-CLR (v1.1.2) (https://github.com/hardingnj/xpclr) analysis. Regions falling within the top 10% of both Fst values and XP-CLR scores were selected as the final candidate regions for selective sweeps.

Analysis of TE mobilization on CNS evolution

To investigate the impact of TE mobilization on CNS evolution, we first performed a systematic and comprehensive annotation of TEs across the orange subfamily. A high-quality and nonredundant TE library was constructed using the EDTA pipeline (v2.2.2) (Ou et al. 2019). TEsorter (v1.5.1) (Zhang et al. 2022) was employed to further classify the TE sequences initially annotated as “unknown” by the EDTA pipeline. Subsequently, RepeatMasker (v4.1.2) (Tarailo-Graovac and Chen 2009) was employed, leveraging this de novo repeat library, to comprehensively identify and annotate repeat sequences across the entire genome. If a CNS is absent from a given genome yet present in other related genomes and TEs directly overlap with the homologous CNS (at least 1 bp) in the phylogenetically closest genome, the loss of this CNS is inferred to be driven by TE mobilization.

Functional annotation of CNSs

BEDTools (v2.30.0) (Quinlan and Hall 2010) was used to detect overlaps between CNS and genomic feature regions, such as ACRs and TEs. Overlaps covering more than 50% of the CNS length were classified as derived CNS. To identify orange subfamily specific CNS, we selected an additional 11 outgroup species alongside the 22 orange subfamily species. These outgroups included 2 additional Rutaceae species and 9 species from other families (Table S2). A 33-way multiple sequence alignment was generated to obtain PhastCons scores using the same method. Orange subfamily specific CNS were defined as CNS sequences unique to the orange subfamily, with no shared sequences found in any outgroup.

Since the previously reported GWAS loci are based on a different genome version than the one used in this study, we employed Progressive Cactus to perform a whole-genome alignment between the previous genome version and the sweet orange genome used here, generating a HAL file. It was converted to pairwise chain format using the cactus-hal2chains utility. Cross-species coordinate translation was performed using LiftOver with stringent alignment parameters (-minMatch = 0.9).

Analysis of ATAC-seq, ChIP-seq, DAP-seq, BS-seq, and RNA-seq data

We generated a chromatin accessibility dataset for sweet orange and additionally downloaded publicly available transcriptome (RNA-seq) data, histone modification ChIP-seq datasets, DNA methylation (BS-seq) and TF-binding profiles (ChIP-seq and DAP-seq) for orange subfamily (Table S4).

For ATAC-seq data, the raw data were processed using fastp to remove adapter sequences and filter out low-quality reads. The filtered reads were aligned to the reference genome using BWA MEM (v0.7.17-r1198) (Li and Durbin 2009), and the aligned results were converted to BAM format and sorted with SAMtools (v1.15) (Li et al. 2009). Reads aligned to different chromosomes and low-quality mappings were removed using the parameters “-f 2 -q 30.” PCR duplicates were removed with Picard (v2.27.5). The parameters of peak calling using MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) (Zhang et al. 2008) were “-f BAMPE –nomodel –shift -100 –extsize 200 -B -q 0.05 –keep-dup all.” The aligned BAM files were converted to BigWig format using the bamCoverage command from the DeepTools (v3.5.1) (Ramírez et al. 2016) package with the parameters “–binSize 10 –normalizeUsing RPKM –smoothLength 50.”

ChIP-seq and DAP-seq data were analyzed similarly to ATAC-seq. For TF DAP-seq data, MEME-ChIP (v5.5.5) (Bailey et al. 2015) was used to predict TF-binding motifs. FIMO was then employed to scan TF motifs within the peaks, and the motif occurrence closest to the peak summit was designated as the TFBS. For footprinting analysis, TOBIAS (v0.13.3) (Bentsen et al. 2020) was applied with default parameters. BS-seq data were aligned using Bowtie2, and duplicates were removed with deduplicate_bismark from the Bismark toolset. Genome-wide methylation sites were extracted using bismark_methylation_extractor.

Raw RNA-seq reads were preprocessed to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads using fastp. The resulting high-quality reads were then mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v8.5.0) (Kim et al. 2019). Subsequently, featureCounts (v1.6.2) (Liao et al. 2013) was employed to quantify the raw read counts per gene. Finally, a custom script was utilized to transform these raw gene counts into transcripts per million (TPM) for quantifying the expression level.

To assess the enrichment or depletion of CNSs in specific genomic regions, such as ACRs and TEs, “bedtools shuffle” was used to generate random genomic regions matching the length and number of CNSs. These randomized regions were compared with specific genomic regions similar to CNSs. The null distribution of CNS overlaps was determined through 10,000 permutations. Empirical P-values were calculated by comparing the observed proportion of CNS overlaps with the null distribution.

Comparisons between panCG and other related tools

We first evaluated its efficacy in identifying single-copy ortholog groups through pairwise genomic comparisons across all 22 orange subfamily genomes as mentioned above. For comparative analysis, pangene sets were constructed using OrthoFinder and GENESPACE under default parameters to ensure a standardized evaluation, with assessments performed by randomly sampling species pairs from the 22 constituent genomes across 25 independent iterations. Performance was quantified using 2 primary metrics: the number of identified single-copy ortholog groups and the syntenic conservation ratio, defined as the proportion of single-copy genes anchored within syntenic blocks relative to the total number of identified single-copy genes. Within this framework, a syntenic block was defined as a genomic region containing at least 5 single-copy genes with a maximum gap of 10 nonsingle-copy genes, where higher values for both metrics were considered indicative of superior performance in refining homologous gene assignments.

Furthermore, we assessed its clustering performance for tandem and proximal duplicated genes using a curated dataset of 1,971 to 4,800 tandem/proximal paralogs from the aforementioned 22 orange subfamily genomes. Pangene indices were then extracted for all identified genes within these duplication categories. The primary evaluation metric, designated as proportion of successful ortholog clustering, is defined as the ratio of the number of unique pangene indices assigned to the duplicate genes to the total number of identified duplicates. A higher granularity score indicates a superior capability to effectively separate and resolve complex duplication events into distinct orthologous positions.
