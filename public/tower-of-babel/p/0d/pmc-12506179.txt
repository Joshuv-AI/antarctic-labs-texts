Adjunctive Chinese herbal medicine versus acupuncture post-PCI: comparative effectiveness and safety – protocol for systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Yongxing Dai, Yang Jiao, Yibin Xu, Shanshan Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary artery disease (CAD) represents a significant global health challenge. Despite advancements like percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), patients remain at risk for recurrent cardiovascular events and persistent symptoms such as angina. Chinese herbal medicine (CHM) and acupuncture are commonly used adjunctive therapies, particularly in East Asia, but robust evidence synthesising their effects when combined with standard post-PCI care is needed. Therefore, this systematic review aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of adding CHM or acupuncture to standard care compared with standard care alone (with or without placebo/sham) in adult patients following PCI.Methods and analysisWe will include parallel-group randomised controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating CHM or acupuncture plus standard care vs standard care (± placebo/sham) in adults post-PCI for CAD. Major international and Chinese electronic databases and clinical trial registries will be searched from their inception without language restrictions. Two reviewers will independently perform study selection based on predefined eligibility criteria and extract data using a standardised form. Risk of bias for included RCTs will be assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. Where appropriate based on clinical and methodological homogeneity (assessed using the I² statistic), pairwise meta-analyses will be conducted using random-effects models. We will calculate risk ratios (RR) or OR for dichotomous outcomes like major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and mortality, and mean differences (MD) or standardised mean differences (SMD) for continuous outcomes such as angina scores and health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Furthermore, if the evidence network permits, a network meta-analysis (NMA) will be performed to indirectly compare the relative effectiveness and safety of CHM vs acupuncture. If quantitative synthesis (pairwise or network) is not feasible, a narrative synthesis will be provided. The certainty of the evidence for key outcomes will be assessed using the GRADE approach. This evaluation will encompass the certainty derived from direct pairwise comparisons as well as the indirect comparisons and overall network estimates derived from the NMA.Ethics and disseminationThere are no ethical issues as this study did not conduct any experiments, surveys or human trials. We will ensure that the findings are shared through pertinent channels.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251040037.

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CAD) remains a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, imposing a significant health and economic burden.1 Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has revolutionised CAD management, substantially improving outcomes for many patients.2 However, despite optimal PCI procedures and subsequent guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT),3 a considerable number of patients continue to face residual risks of major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACE), including myocardial infarction, stroke and cardiovascular death.4 Furthermore, persistent symptoms like angina pectoris frequently impair functional capacity and diminish health-related quality of life (HRQoL) following PCI, highlighting limitations within current standard care paradigms alone.5

Standard post-PCI care typically involves GDMT—encompassing dual antiplatelet therapy, statins, beta-blockers and other indicated cardiovascular medications—alongside lifestyle modifications.6 While this constitutes the cornerstone of secondary prevention,7 the persistence of adverse events and symptoms underscores an unmet need for complementary or adjuvant therapeutic strategies that can further mitigate risks and enhance patient well-being beyond the benefits afforded solely by standard pharmacological treatments.8 This has spurred growing interest in exploring the potential contributions of traditional medicine therapies integrated with conventional care.

Rooted in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), Chinese herbal medicine (CHM), often using complex multicomponent formulas,9 and acupuncture, involving the stimulation of specific acupoints, are widely used therapeutic modalities.10 Particularly prevalent in East Asia, they are increasingly applied adjunctively in cardiovascular disease management. Potential mechanisms underlying their cardiovascular effects are under investigation, hypothesised to include modulation of inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial function, platelet activity and lipid metabolism, possibly exerting direct cardioprotective or anti-anginal effects through neurohormonal pathways.11 Various specific CHM formulations and acupuncture techniques, such as manual or electroacupuncture, have been studied within the context of CAD recovery.12

Despite their increasing integration into clinical practice in some regions, the evidence for CHM and acupuncture as adjuncts to post-PCI care requires rigorous synthesis. While numerous randomised controlled trials (RCTs) exist, their results are varied.13 14 Previous systematic reviews often have limitations, such as including studies with a high risk of bias, failing to manage significant heterogeneity or using outdated search strategies.15 This systematic review aims to address these gaps by conducting a comprehensive and up-to-date search, applying strict methodological assessment using the Cochrane risk of bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, and using network meta-analysis (NMA) to synthesise the evidence. A robust and current synthesis of high-quality RCTs is essential to clarify the true efficacy and safety of these therapies in the post-PCI setting.

Therefore, we present a protocol for a systematic review of RCTs to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of CHM or acupuncture when added to standard care post-PCI. Our primary goal is to assess their impact on clinical outcomes, including MACE and angina relief. Critically, we will employ NMA to indirectly compare the relative effects of CHM vs acupuncture, providing evidence where direct head-to-head trials are lacking.

Methods

Study registration

This protocol has been developed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines to ensure methodological transparency and rigour.16 The protocol is registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with the number CRD420251040037.

Timeline

The planned start date for this systematic review is 30 June 2024, and the anticipated completion date is 31 January 2026.

Eligibility criteria for study inclusion

Types of studies

This review will include only RCTs evaluating CHM or acupuncture added to standard post-PCI care in adult patients. Studies must use formal random allocation.

Parallel-group RCT designs are the primary focus.16 Cluster-randomised trials analysed appropriately for clustering effects and rigorously designed crossover trials with adequate washout periods may also be included if suitable for the outcomes assessed.17

Excluded study designs are: quasi-randomised trials allocated by methods like date of birth or alternation, non-randomised controlled trials (NRCTs), uncontrolled trials, observational studies including cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional designs, case reports or case series, and preclinical studies.

Systematic reviews will not be included for data synthesis, but their reference lists will be screened for potentially eligible primary RCTs. Full peer-reviewed publications are preferred; however, conference abstracts reporting sufficient quantitative data from eligible RCTs may be included if full publications are unavailable. This focus on RCTs aims to minimise selection bias and confounding, providing the highest level of evidence for assessing causal effects.

Types of participants

Eligible studies must enrol adult participants (≥18 years) diagnosed with CAD who have completed a PCI before or on trial entry, focusing on the post-PCI stage. Inclusion is comprehensive regarding the reason for PCI, accepting studies with patients presenting with stable angina, unstable angina or acute myocardial infarction. Likewise, common baseline health conditions like diabetes mellitus and hypertension, or indicators of disease severity such as prior myocardial infarction, number of affected vessels, or left ventricular ejection fraction, will not be exclusion factors, as long as the study population represents adult CAD patients post-PCI as examined in the original RCTs.

Types of interventions

Eligible interventions encompass either CHM or acupuncture administered explicitly as adjunctive therapy to standard post-PCI care. This review will include RCTs evaluating any form of CHM (such as specific traditional formulas, individual herbs, or extracts, irrespective of preparation, dosage, or duration) or any acupuncture technique (including manual or electroacupuncture, regardless of the specific acupoints or stimulation parameters used), provided these therapies are added to standard guideline-directed medical therapy and lifestyle advice. Interventions where CHM or acupuncture replaces standard care, or comparisons made against other non-standard active therapies without an eligible standard care (± placebo/sham) control group, will be excluded.

Types of comparators/controls

Eligible studies must compare the CHM or acupuncture intervention added to standard care against a control group receiving standard care without the specific traditional therapy.

Acceptable control groups include:

Standard post-PCI care alone: Participants receive guideline-directed medical therapy including antiplatelets and statins, plus lifestyle advice, but no active CHM or acupuncture.

Standard post-PCI care plus placebo: Used in CHM trials, where the placebo mimics the active CHM but is pharmacologically inert.

Standard post-PCI care plus sham acupuncture: Used in acupuncture trials, employing methods like non-penetrating needles or needling at non-relevant points to maintain blinding.

Studies will be excluded if they lack a concurrent control group receiving standard care, placebo or an appropriate sham procedure. Direct comparisons between different active CHM or acupuncture interventions without such a control, or comparisons against standard care plus another non-standard active therapy, are ineligible. A clear description of the control management is required for inclusion.

Types of outcomes

Primary outcomes: The primary outcomes will assess major cardiovascular events and angina status. These include:

MACE, evaluated as a composite endpoint typically comprising cardiovascular death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, stroke and target vessel revascularisation.

Angina relief/frequency, measured using validated tools such as the Canadian Cardiovascular Society classification or Seattle Angina Questionnaire scores, or through patient reports of attack frequency or nitroglycerin use.

Secondary outcomes: The secondary outcomes will encompass broader clinical and patient-reported measures, including:

All-cause mortality.

Rehospitalisation, with a focus on cardiac-related readmissions.

HRQoL assessed with validated generic or disease-specific instruments.

Adverse events attributed to the Chinese herbal medicine or acupuncture interventions.

Exclusion criteria

Ineligible study designs: NRCTs, quasi-randomised trials, observational studies (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional), uncontrolled trials, case reports/series, preclinical studies (animal/in vitro).

Ineligible population: Studies not involving adults (≥18 years) diagnosed with CAD who have undergone PCI.

Ineligible interventions/comparators: Studies where CHM or acupuncture replaces standard post-PCI care; studies lacking an eligible control group (standard care alone, standard care+placebo, or standard care+sham acupuncture); studies evaluating CHM/acupuncture against another non-standard active therapy.

Ineligible outcomes reporting: Studies not reporting quantifiable data for at least one predefined primary or additional outcome measure relevant to this review.

Duplicate publications: Redundant publications reporting on the same patient cohort (only the most comprehensive report will be included).

Non-empirical reports/insufficient data: Abstracts, letters, editorials, narrative reviews, commentaries, or study protocols lacking sufficient data for eligibility assessment or analysis.

Critically flawed studies: Studies assessed during quality appraisal as having critical methodological flaws that invalidate their results or containing unresolvable data inconsistencies.

Search methods for identification of studies

Electronic data sources

A comprehensive literature search will be conducted across multiple major international and Chinese electronic databases to identify all potentially relevant RCTs. We will systematically search the following international databases from their inception up to April 2025: PubMed (via MEDLINE), Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) in the Cochrane Library, Web of Science (Science Citation Index Expanded) and Scopus. Furthermore, to ensure broad coverage of studies published in Chinese, we will search the primary Chinese electronic databases: China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data Information Site, VIP Information/Chinese Scientific Journals Database (CQVIP) and the SinoMed database (formerly known as the Chinese Biomedical Literature Database, CBM). No restrictions on language or publication date will be applied during the initial search process to maximise sensitivity.

Data collection and analysis

Selection of studies

The study selection process will be conducted in two stages by two independent reviewers (Yongxing Dai, Yang Jiao). First, all records retrieved from the electronic database searches and other sources will be imported into reference management software (EndNote) to remove duplicates. Following deduplication, the two reviewers (YD, YJ) will independently screen the titles and abstracts of the remaining records against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies clearly not meeting the criteria will be excluded at this stage.

In the second stage, the full texts of potentially relevant articles identified during the title and abstract screening will be obtained and assessed independently by the same two reviewers (YD, YJ) for final eligibility. Any discrepancies or disagreements between the two reviewers at either the title/abstract screening stage or the full-text assessment stage will be resolved through discussion and consensus. If consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer (Yibin Xu or Shanshan Yang) will be consulted to make the final decision. The entire selection process, including reasons for excluding studies at the full-text stage, will be documented and presented in a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram (figure 1).

Data extraction and management

Two reviewers, Yongxing Dai and Yang Jiao, will independently extract data from all included RCTs. They will use a predesigned data extraction form, developed in Microsoft Excel or directly within systematic review software such as Covidence, which will be piloted on a small sample of 3–5 studies and refined as necessary before full application.

The extraction form will capture comprehensive study characteristics, including the first author, publication year, country or region, specific study design elements like parallel or cluster randomisation, total and per-group sample sizes, duration of follow-up, funding sources and any declared conflicts of interest. Participant details will also be meticulously recorded, covering age and sex distribution, key inclusion and exclusion criteria, baseline characteristics relevant to coronary heart disease post-PCI like disease type, history of myocardial infarction, number of diseased vessels and left ventricular ejection fraction, significant comorbidities such as diabetes or hypertension and baseline values for the outcome measures under investigation.

Detailed information regarding the interventions will be extracted. For CHM studies, this includes the specific formula or herbs used, dosage, preparation method, administration route, frequency and total duration. For acupuncture studies, details will encompass the technique employed, specific acupoints used, stimulation parameters including duration, frequency, and intensity, needle retention time, treatment frequency, and total duration. Information on standard post-PCI care and any other concurrent therapies received by the intervention group will also be noted. Similarly, details of the comparator intervention will be captured, including descriptions of standard care alone, placebo characteristics covering composition and blinding methods, or specifics of sham acupuncture techniques and blinding methods, along with concurrent therapies received by the control group. To address potential variability in standard care, we will specifically extract details on the components of GDMT (eg, dual antiplatelet therapy, statins, beta-blockers, Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors (ACEIs)/ Angiotensin Receptor Blockers(ARBs)) and, where reported, their dosages in the control group. This information will be tabulated to assess consistency across studies.

Outcome data relevant to this review, encompassing all primary and additional outcomes defined in Section 2.2.5 like MACE components, angina measures, mortality, HRQoL and rehospitalisation, will be extracted. This includes the measurement tools or definitions used and the time points of assessment, such as baseline, end-of-treatment and specific follow-up points. Quantitative results for each outcome at each time point will be recorded, reported as the number of events and total participants for dichotomous outcomes like MACE, or as means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous outcomes like SAQ scores. We will attempt to calculate missing SDs from reported standard errors, confidence intervals or p-values where possible. Effect estimates reported by the studies themselves, for instance, Risk Ratios or Mean Differences with their 95% confidence intervals, will also be extracted. Furthermore, data on adverse events, including type, frequency and severity reported in each group and specifically attributed to the intervention or comparator, will be collected.

Specific data points relevant to assessing each domain of the Cochrane RoB 2 tool will also be extracted to facilitate the quality assessment process.

Any discrepancies encountered in the extracted data between the two reviewers (YD, YJ) will be resolved through discussion and consensus. If agreement cannot be reached, a third reviewer, either Yibin Xu or Shanshan Yang, will be consulted for arbitration. Should essential data be missing or reported unclearly in the publications, we will attempt to contact the corresponding authors via email for necessary clarification. All extracted data will be entered into the chosen software, meticulously cross-checked for accuracy and managed securely throughout the review process.

Assessment of risk of bias in included studies

Two reviewers, Yongxing Dai and Yang Jiao, will independently assess the methodological quality and risk of bias for each included RCT. This assessment will use the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials, which evaluates potential bias across five key domains: the randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome and selection of the reported result. Guided by signalling questions specified in the RoB 2 guidance, each domain will be judged as having ‘Low risk of bias’, ‘Some concerns’ or ‘High risk of bias’.

Following the domain-level assessments, an overall risk of bias judgement will be derived for each study outcome. This overall judgement will be categorised as ‘Low risk of bias’ if all domains are rated as low risk, ‘Some concerns’ if at least one domain raises some concerns but none are high risk or ‘High risk of bias’ if there is a high risk in at least one domain or some concerns across multiple domains that substantially lower confidence in the result. Any disagreements arising between the two reviewers during this assessment process will be resolved through thorough discussion aimed at reaching consensus. Should consensus not be achievable, a third reviewer, either Yibin Xu or Shanshan Yang, will be consulted to provide a final judgement.

The findings of the risk of bias assessment will be presented graphically, potentially using tools like robvis, and also summarised in tabular format to clearly depict the quality of the included studies. These assessment results are crucial and will be carefully considered when interpreting the overall findings of the review. Furthermore, the risk of bias judgements will directly inform subsequent sensitivity analyses, such as analyses excluding studies judged at high risk of bias, and will be a key component in assessing the certainty of the evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.

Data synthesis

We plan to synthesise quantitative data using Review Manager v5.4, potentially using packages such as ‘meta’ and ‘netmeta’. The synthesis approach will involve both pairwise MA and network MA (NMA).

Pairwise MA

For direct comparisons, specifically CHM+standard care vs standard care±placebo/sham, and acupuncture+standard care vs standard care±placebo/sham, standard pairwise meta-analyses will be conducted if studies are sufficiently homogeneous regarding participants, interventions, comparators and outcomes. Heterogeneity will be assessed both qualitatively by examining study characteristics and quantitatively using the χ² test (p<0.10 indicates significance) and the I² statistic, interpreted as low (<25%), moderate (25%–75%) or high (>75%) heterogeneity. A random-effects model will be the default approach. Risk ratios (RR) or OR with 95% CIs will be calculated for dichotomous outcomes like MACE and mortality. Mean differences (MD) or standardised mean differences (SMD) with 95% CIs will be calculated for continuous outcomes, such as angina scores and HRQoL, depending on scale consistency across studies.

NMA

Subject to feasibility assessment, an NMA will be performed to synthesise direct and indirect evidence and enable comparisons between all interventions: CHM+standard care, acupuncture+standard care and standard care±placebo/sham.

Feasibility and network geometry: We will construct a network plot visualising the available direct comparisons. NMA is feasible only if the network is connected, meaning every treatment can be linked to every other treatment via a path of direct comparisons.

Assessment of Assumptions:

Transitivity (similarity): We will qualitatively assess the transitivity assumption by comparing the distribution of potential effect modifiers—including baseline patient characteristics, specific standard care components and study quality—across different pairwise comparisons that form loops in the network. Significant differences may invalidate the NMA.

Consistency (coherence): If a closed loop involving direct and indirect evidence exists (for instance, involving comparisons of CHM vs standard care, acupuncture vs standard care, and potentially CHM vs acupuncture trials), we will assess consistency between direct and indirect estimates. This may involve methods like node-splitting analysis or design-by-treatment interaction models. Significant inconsistency, indicated by a p value <0.10 in the node-splitting analysis, will be investigated for potential clinical or methodological sources. If the inconsistency cannot be explained, the NMA for the affected comparisons will be deemed inappropriate, and we will rely on a narrative synthesis or present only the direct pairwise meta-analyses.

Statistical model: A random-effects NMA model within a frequentist framework will likely be used, potentially employing established packages like ‘netmeta’ in R. This approach accounts for heterogeneity both within and between comparisons.

Reporting: Results will be presented as summary effect estimates (RR/OR or MD/SMD) with 95% CIs for each intervention relative to a common comparator, which will likely be standard care±placebo/sham. We will also present relative effects for all pairwise comparisons, including the key comparison of CHM vs acupuncture, potentially presented in a league table format. Treatment rankings based on probabilities, such as P-scores or Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking curve (SUCRA) values, may also be calculated and reported cautiously.

Narrative Synthesis

If quantitative synthesis (either pairwise for a specific comparison or NMA for the network) is deemed inappropriate due to significant heterogeneity, inconsistency, insufficient data—such as having fewer than two studies available for a particular comparison—or violation of fundamental assumptions, a narrative synthesis will be performed. This will involve summarising study characteristics and findings textually and potentially using tables, focusing on identifying patterns, differences and limitations across the included studies.

Dealing with missing data

Missing data can occur at various levels in a systematic review, including missing studies, missing outcomes within studies, or missing participant data for specific outcomes, often resulting from participant attrition. Our primary strategy for handling unclear or missing information crucial for eligibility assessment, risk of bias assessment or data analysis will be to attempt to contact the corresponding authors of the included studies via email up to two times over a period of 4 weeks to request clarification or the required data.

For missing outcome data within included studies, particularly for the primary analyses, we will follow the principles of intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis whenever possible, using the data as reported in the original publication if ITT was appropriately handled. If ITT data are not available and only per-protocol or completer analysis data are reported, we will use these data but acknowledge this limitation and its potential impact on the results. If significant amounts of outcome data are missing, such as situations involving high attrition rates reported without adequate handling, we will assess the potential impact through sensitivity analyses. These might involve making different plausible assumptions about the outcomes of participants with missing data, including exploring best-case and worst-case scenarios, or excluding studies with very high levels of unexplained missing data from the primary MA.

Regarding missing summary statistics required for MA, specifically missing SDs for continuous outcomes, we will first attempt to calculate them from reported standard errors, CIs, t-values or p values using established formulas. If calculation is not possible, we will explore imputation methods based on SDs from other studies in the MA if deemed appropriate, or based on standard formulas if IQRs are provided. If these methods are unsuitable or yield uncertain results, we will report the available data narratively or exclude the study from the specific MA for that outcome, clearly stating the reason. The potential impact of handling missing data on the overall findings will be discussed in the review.

Assessment of heterogeneity

Heterogeneity among the included studies will be carefully assessed to understand the variability in results beyond that expected by chance. We will investigate heterogeneity using a combination of methods. First, clinical and methodological heterogeneity will be explored qualitatively by examining the variability across studies in participant characteristics (P), interventions (I), comparators (C) and outcomes (O), as documented in the ‘Characteristics of included studies’ table. This applies to understanding variability within direct pairwise comparisons and across studies contributing to the overall evidence network. Visual inspection of forest plots will also be performed, focusing on the degree of overlap between CIs and the consistency of point estimates for direct comparisons.

Second, statistical heterogeneity will be quantified for each planned pairwise MA using the χ² (χ² or Cochran’s Q) test and the I² statistic. A p value less than 0.10 for the χ² test will be considered indicative of statistically significant heterogeneity, acknowledging the test’s low power when the number of studies is small. The I² statistic will quantify the percentage of total variation across studies attributable to heterogeneity rather than chance, interpreted using predefined thresholds: less than 25% indicating low heterogeneity, 25% to 75% indicating moderate heterogeneity, and greater than 75% indicating high heterogeneity. Within the NMA framework, heterogeneity will be assessed by considering the heterogeneity estimates from the contributing direct pairwise comparisons, including metrics like the I² statistic, and the overall estimate of heterogeneity across the network, often represented by the common between-study variance derived from the random-effects NMA model.

The assessment of both statistical and clinical/methodological heterogeneity will guide subsequent decisions regarding data synthesis (both pairwise MA and NMA). For instance, these findings will help confirm the appropriateness of using a random-effects model for both pairwise MA and NMA, determine the necessity of conducting subgroup analyses to explore sources of variation, or indicate if performing a narrative synthesis is more suitable than pooling quantitative data via pairwise MA or NMA. Even if statistical tests are non-significant, substantial clinical or methodological diversity may still warrant caution in interpreting pooled pairwise estimates or NMA results, potentially necessitating a narrative synthesis approach. The degree and potential reasons for any identified heterogeneity will be thoroughly discussed in the review.

Assessment of publication biases

Potential publication bias for primary outcomes (MACE, angina relief/frequency) will be assessed if 10 or more studies inform an MA, whether it is a specific pairwise comparison or a key comparison within the NMA. Our primary approach involves visually inspecting funnel plots, which plot study effect estimates against a measure of precision, commonly the SE or inverse variance. Asymmetry in the plot, particularly suggesting an absence of smaller studies showing no significant effects, may indicate publication bias.

Formal statistical tests for funnel plot asymmetry, such as Egger’s linear regression test or Begg’s rank correlation test, may supplement visual inspection, with a p value less than 0.10 considered indicative of significant asymmetry. However, interpretation of these assessments requires caution. Funnel plot asymmetry does not solely arise from publication bias; it can also result from genuine heterogeneity between studies (particularly relevant across different comparisons within an NMA), methodological variations correlated with study size leading to ‘small-study effects’, or simply chance.

Consequently, these assessments are considered exploratory. Their findings will be interpreted within the broader context of the overall evidence base and the risk of bias assessments for included studies. The potential impact of any identified publication bias on the review’s conclusions will be thoroughly discussed.

Subgroup analysis

To explore potential heterogeneity sources and investigate effect variations, we plan prespecified subgroup analyses for primary outcomes (MACE, angina relief/frequency), contingent on sufficient data (typically ≥2 studies per subgroup). These analyses, applied within pairwise or network meta-analyses where feasible, will examine:

Overall intervention type: Contrasting CHM vs acupuncture studies.

Specific intervention details: Comparing types of CHM (if classifiable, perhaps by traditional function like ‘activating blood circulation’) or types of acupuncture (such as manual vs electroacupuncture), data permitting.

Control type: Distinguishing placebo/sham-controlled trials from those using standard care alone.

Intervention duration: Categorising based on treatment length (potentially using thresholds like ≤3 months vs >3 months, refined based on observed data).

Risk of bias: Comparing studies judged at low/some concerns vs high risk.

Differences between subgroups will be assessed visually via forest plots (consistency of effects, overlap of CIs) and formally using interaction tests within the appropriate meta-analytic models. However, subgroup analysis results will be interpreted cautiously. Given their observational nature and potential for confounding, especially with few studies per subgroup, they will primarily serve to explore heterogeneity rather than establish definitive differential effects, unless evidence is notably strong and consistent.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of the synthesised findings for our primary outcomes (MACE, angina relief/frequency), derived from both pairwise and network meta-analyses, we will conduct several prespecified sensitivity analyses. These involve repeating the primary MA under modified assumptions or datasets to assess the influence of specific studies or methodological choices.

Planned sensitivity analyses include:

Excluding studies with high risk of bias: Re-running analyses using only studies judged at ‘Low risk of bias’ or ‘Some concerns’ via RoB 2.

Excluding small studies: Removing studies with potentially unstable estimates due to small sample sizes; a possible threshold could be fewer than 50 participants total, although this may be adjusted based on the observed data distribution.

Excluding influential studies: If any single study appears to disproportionately affect the overall result, conducting an analysis without that study.

Altering the MA model: Comparing results using a fixed-effect model vs the primary random-effects model, particularly when heterogeneity is observed to be low, indicated perhaps by an I² value below 25%.

Exploring publication characteristics (if applicable): Restricting analyses by publication language or status, such as including only peer-reviewed articles, if non-English or grey literature is incorporated in the main analysis.

Varying missing data handling: Assessing the impact of alternative imputation methods if significant data imputation was performed.

Addressing NMA assumptions (if NMA is performed): Potentially excluding studies identified as possibly violating key NMA assumptions like transitivity or consistency, should such concerns arise.

We will compare the results of these sensitivity analyses, specifically the effect estimates and CIs, with the primary analysis. Consistency across scenarios will strengthen confidence in the findings’ robustness. Substantial changes observed during these analyses will be highlighted and discussed, indicating that the findings are sensitive to the specific factor investigated.

Grading the quality of evidence

Two reviewers (Yongxing Dai, Yang Jiao) will independently assess the overall certainty of evidence for the primary outcomes (MACE, angina relief/frequency) and key secondary outcomes (including all-cause mortality, rehospitalisation, HRQoL and pooled major adverse events) using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach.

This certainty assessment will apply to evidence derived from direct pairwise comparisons. If an NMA is conducted, the GRADE assessment will also extend to the resulting indirect comparisons and the overall network estimates, considering factors pertinent to NMA such as potential intransitivity and incoherence.

Any disagreements between the reviewers during the GRADE assessment will be resolved through discussion and consensus, or by involving a third reviewer (Yibin Xu or Shanshan Yang) if needed. The final certainty ratings for each outcome, along with explicit reasons for any downgrading, will be presented clearly in a ‘Summary of Findings’ (SoF) table, which will be generated using software such as GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool (GDT).

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient or public involvement in the development of this systematic review protocol.

Dissemination and ethics

Findings will be disseminated via peer-reviewed publication (targeting cardiology, complementary/alternative medicine, or internal medicine journals) and relevant scientific conferences. Summaries may also be shared with guideline groups and patient organisations for knowledge translation. As this review synthesises published data, formal ethical approval is not required. However, the study will strictly adhere to ethical research principles, including transparency, objectivity and accurate reporting.

Discussion

Rationale and Evidence Gap for Post-PCI Adjuncts

This protocol outlines a systematic review evaluating two widely used traditional therapies, CHM and acupuncture, as adjuncts to standard care after PCI for CAD. The review is driven by a significant clinical reality: despite successful PCI and standard medical therapy, many patients still experience residual angina, recurrent MACE and reduced quality of life. This unmet need fuels interest, especially in East Asia, in integrating complementary approaches like CHM and acupuncture to potentially improve post-PCI outcomes.

However, current evidence for these adjunctive therapies post-PCI is fragmented, often methodologically limited including potential bias, and lacks comprehensive synthesis. Existing reviews may be outdated, lack rigour or struggle with the inherent heterogeneity of these interventions. Crucially, direct evidence comparing the effectiveness of CHM vs acupuncture as adjuncts is scarce.

To address this critical gap, this protocol details a systematic review using established, transparent methods, including a comprehensive search strategy and adherence to PRISMA guidelines. It incorporates plans for both pairwise MA and NMA. The NMA approach is key, aiming to synthesise direct evidence—comparing CHM or acupuncture against standard care, potentially including placebo or sham controls—and, importantly, enable indirect comparisons between CHM and acupuncture. This offers a more holistic evidence landscape. Standardised tools like the Cochrane RoB 2 for risk of bias assessment and the GRADE framework for evaluating evidence certainty will ensure a robust appraisal of the available data. Ultimately, this structured approach seeks to provide the most current, comprehensive synthesis of randomised controlled trial evidence to guide clinical practice and future research concerning optimal post-PCI care.18 The findings will be discussed in the context of their implications for clinical guidelines and patient counselling.

Methodological Strengths and Network MA Approach

This protocol incorporates several methodological strengths to ensure a rigorous and transparent synthesis. Our systematic approach features a comprehensive search strategy across major international and Chinese databases, without language restrictions, aiming to capture all relevant RCTs and minimise selection bias. Adherence to PRISMA guidelines ensures transparency and reproducibility.

We will employ standardised tools: the Cochrane RoB 2 for assessing trial quality and the GRADE framework for evaluating evidence certainty, providing a structured basis for interpretation. The entire process, including study selection, data extraction and assessments, will involve two independent reviewers to minimise errors, with clear procedures for disagreement resolution. Prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses are planned to explore heterogeneity and test the robustness of findings.

A key strength is the planned use of NMA, contingent on network feasibility. While individual trials exist, direct comparisons between CHM and acupuncture as adjuncts post-PCI are scarce.19 NMA addresses this gap by synthesising both direct evidence (such as comparisons of CHM or acupuncture against standard care) and indirect evidence within a single framework. This allows estimating relative treatment effects between interventions not directly compared in trials, notably the comparative effectiveness and safety of CHM vs acupuncture.20 This capacity promises a more comprehensive and clinically relevant evidence base than standard pairwise meta-analyses alone, aiming to better inform clinical decision-making for patients considering these adjunctive therapies post-PCI.

Anticipated Challenges and Mitigation Strategies

Despite the planned methodology, synthesising evidence on complex traditional interventions like CHM and acupuncture post-PCI presents anticipated challenges. Recognising these limitations allows for proactive planning and transparent interpretation.

First, substantial heterogeneity across studies is expected concerning participants, interventions (including varied CHM formulas, dosages, preparations, and diverse acupuncture points, techniques, durations), comparators (differing ‘standard care’ protocols), and outcomes (varying MACE definitions or scales for angina/HRQoL). This heterogeneity challenges quantitative synthesis and potentially threatens the NMA’s transitivity assumption.21 Our mitigation strategy uses random-effects models, rigorous quantitative and qualitative heterogeneity assessment (using statistics like I²), and planned subgroup and sensitivity analyses exploring factors like intervention type or duration to evaluate result robustness. If heterogeneity prevents meaningful pooling or violates NMA assumptions (checked via methods like node-splitting if appropriate), we will report this transparently and rely mainly on narrative synthesis, possibly adding pairwise meta-analyses for more homogeneous subgroups.

Second, included RCTs may have variable methodological quality. Concerns common in traditional medicine trials include inadequate reporting of randomisation, particularly allocation concealment, and difficulties blinding participants or assessors to distinct CHM interventions or physical acupuncture procedures. Such issues risk introducing bias affecting selection, performance or detection, especially for subjective outcomes like angina scores. To address this, we will meticulously assess risk of bias using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. These assessments will inform sensitivity analyses, such as excluding high-risk studies, and critically contribute to the GRADE evidence certainty ratings, likely leading to downgrading where bias risks are high.

Third, publication bias, potentially favouring positive results, is a concern. We plan to assess this using funnel plots and statistical tests like Egger’s test if sufficient studies (≥10) are available per comparison, but acknowledge limitations, especially with few studies or heterogeneity that can mimic bias (small-study effects). Therefore, our interpretation will be cautious and explicitly discuss potential publication bias impact.

Finally, other limitations include the challenge of standardising complex interventions like multicomponent CHM or individualised acupuncture for research, affecting generalisability. Additionally, many trials may have short follow-up periods, hindering long-term efficacy assessment (especially for MACE) and safety evaluation. While our review cannot fix primary study flaws, we will carefully document intervention details, assess generalisability, note data gaps and emphasise these limitations when interpreting conclusions and making recommendations.

Possible Mechanisms of Action

While the precise biological mechanisms underlying the potential benefits of CHM and acupuncture in the post-PCI setting remain under investigation and require further elucidation,22 several plausible pathways have been proposed based on preclinical studies and research in related cardiovascular conditions. It is important to recognise that these are largely hypothesised actions that warrant rigorous scientific validation.

For CHM, often involving complex multicomponent formulas designed to act synergistically, potential mechanisms may include:

Modulation of inflammatory responses23: Affecting proinflammatory cytokines and signalling pathways implicated in atherosclerosis progression and plaque instability.

Reduction of oxidative stress24: Enhancing endogenous antioxidant capacity or scavenging reactive oxygen species that contribute to endothelial dysfunction and myocardial injury.

Improvement in endothelial function25: Promoting nitric oxide bioavailability and vasodilation.

Antiplatelet aggregation effects26: Potentially influencing platelet activation and thrombus formation.

Favourable modulation of lipid metabolism27: Contributing to lipid profile improvement beyond standard therapies.

For acupuncture, the proposed mechanisms frequently centre on neuromodulation and systemic physiological responses:

Regulation of the autonomic nervous system28: Influencing sympathetic and parasympathetic balance, which can affect heart rate variability, blood pressure and myocardial oxygen demand.

Stimulation of endogenous opioids and other neurotransmitters29: Potentially contributing to pain relief (relevant for angina) and stress reduction.

Influence on vasomotor function and microcirculation30: Potentially improving blood flow in ischaemic tissues.

Crucially, these potential mechanisms are often inferred and require confirmation through well-designed basic and translational research, specifically focusing on the post-PCI context. This systematic review, by evaluating the overall clinical effectiveness and safety data from RCTs, will provide valuable clinical evidence that can, in turn, help assess the plausibility of these proposed underlying actions and guide future mechanistic investigations.
