Short‐term risks and benefits of hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide)‐induced transfusion independence in patients with beta‐thalassaemia syndromes
Authors: B. R. Sevanthini, Santhosh Hegde, Tejashree Sridhar, Deepa Trivedi, Mohan Reddy, Vaibhav Shah, Vidhi Mayur Panchamia, T. Geetha, Dharmik Vora, H. Pushpa, Rajat Kumar Agarwal, Lawrence Faulkner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Clinical outcomes of hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide)‐induced transfusion independence in patients with β‐thalassaemia syndromes.

To the Editor,

Transfusion‐dependent thalassaemia (TDT) causes severe anaemia due to defective haemoglobin (Hb) production. Regular blood transfusions are essential but result in iron overload, especially in the heart, liver and endocrine system. Despite chelation, iron overload significantly contributes to morbidity and mortality. 1 The risks of transfusions highlight the need for alternative therapies to reduce dependence. Additionally, liver enlargement significantly impacts haematopoietic stem cell transplantation outcomes, serving as a key prognostic factor, 2 and spleen size may also play a role. 3

Hydroxycarbamide or hydroxyurea (HU) is a cell proliferation inhibitor 4 extensively used in sickle cell anaemia 5 and also for its potential benefits in reducing transfusion requirements in patients with thalassaemia. 6 HU, by inducing fetal Hb, may alleviate anaemia in TDT patients. 5 However, the effect on relevant parameters such as Hb levels achieved, serum ferritin levels changes, liver and spleen size modifications or pulmonary hypertension (PAH) has not been well documented. The meta‐analysis of 27 clinical trials involving 1748 β‐thalassaemia patients found that HU significantly reduced transfusion needs and increased Hb levels, with neutropenia and leucopenia as the most common side effects. 7 However, more robust randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm its safety and efficacy.

This study evaluated HU's effectiveness and safety in inducing transfusion independence in thalassaemia patients, focusing on stable Hb ≥8 g/dL and impacts on ferritin, organ size and PAH. We assessed HU's ability to achieve Hb ≥10 g/dL in TDT. 8 , 9

Out of 2477 TDT patients, 663 diagnosed after age 1 were selected, as no infant‐diagnosed cases responded to HU. Among them, 482 received HU, starting at 10–15 mg/kg/day, adjusted to maintain tolerable cytopenia with absolute neutrophil count (ANC) 1.0–1.5 × 109/L or platelets 100–150 × 109/L.

HU therapy eligibility required TDT diagnosis with Hb <7 g/dL, ANC ≥2.0 × 109/L, platelets ≥150 × 109/L and normal creatinine, bilirubin and aspartate transferase (AST) levels. Patients receiving deferiprone and those with suboptimal compliance were excluded from the study. Informed consent was obtained, institutional ethical approval was granted and data were collected from our online IT platform. 10 Baseline data included transfusion history, chelation therapy, complete blood count, creatinine, bilirubin, AST and liver/spleen size. HU dose increased by 10%–20% biweekly due to myelotoxicity risk. The pre‐transfusion Hb target was lowered to 7 g/dL. HU was paused during fever or acute conditions.

Blood counts, growth parameters and organ size were monitored biweekly; ferritin, creatinine, AST and transfusion needs were evaluated quarterly. Hb electrophoresis changes were not assessed in this cohort.

Among 482 patients on HU, 195 showed a >25% reduction in transfusion frequency. Full response was achieved by 65 (13.4%), including 53 with beta‐thalassaemia, 5 with Hb‐E beta‐thalassaemia, 1 Hb Lepore and 6 without molecular analysis.

Among full responders, the median time to respond was 34 days, with most responding within 5 months. Full responders received a median maximum dose of 25 mg/kg and maintenance of 18 mg/kg, though nearly half still required occasional transfusions.

HU was discontinued in four full responders: three due to intolerance with worsening splenomegaly and one opting for a bone marrow transplant. No patients required splenectomy during HU treatment.

Of 195 patients (>25% reduction in transfusion frequency), CD 30 (G>C) (Hb Monroe) and IVS I‐5 (G>C) are the compound heterozygous mutations identified with the patient whose Hb was above 10 g/dL.

Of 65 patients, 15 developed cardiac complications (either PAH, mitral regurgitation or pulmonic regurgitation); 4 cases were detected before starting HU and 11 after, with a median onset of 651 days (interquartile range [IQR] 465.5–827). Only 2 of the 11 had pre‐HU echocardiograms; 65 were full responders and 130 were partial responders. Molecular analysis was available only for 172 responders.

Few TDT patients achieve Hb >8 g/dL on HU, mostly those diagnosed after 1 year of age. Only 13% achieved transfusion independence, and just 1 (1.5%) maintained Hb >10 g/dL. Even responders, on median doses of 25 mg/kg (max) and 18 mg/kg (maintenance), often needed occasional transfusions, with sustained median Hb of 8.7 g/dL, indicating persistent chronic anaemia. This increases the risk of PAH and cardiac complications, major causes of thalassaemia mortality. 11 , 12 Worsening splenomegaly in this cohort suggests an increased risk of extramedullary haematopoiesis and hypersplenism. In fact, most of our patients exhibited an increase in spleen size as opposed to what has been reported in the literature, that is, unchanged splenomegaly in 83% of non‐splenectomized patients. 13 Although HU demonstrates potential in the management of beta‐thalassaemia syndromes, its application should be approached with caution. Rigorous, long‐term studies are required to elucidate its risk–benefit profile, particularly in resource‐limited settings where curative modalities are inaccessible.
