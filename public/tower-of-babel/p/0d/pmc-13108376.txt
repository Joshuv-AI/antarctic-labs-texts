Pharmacokinetics, Pharmacodynamics, and Safety of Single‐Dose Luseogliflozin (TS‐071) in Japanese Children and Adolescents With Type 2 Diabetes: A Multicenter, Open‐Label, Parallel‐Group Phase 1 Study
Authors: Toru Kikuchi, Hidefumi Nakamura, Hideo Umeuchi, Iwao Kitajima, Tatsuhiko Urakami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and safety of a single dose of luseogliflozin (TS‐071), a sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor, in Japanese children and adolescents with type 2 diabetes, with the goal of informing dose selection for future research. In this multicenter, open‐label, parallel‐group Phase 1 trial, patients aged 9‐17 years received a single oral dose of luseogliflozin at 1.25, 2.5, or 5 mg. Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic parameters were assessed and compared with existing adult data. In total, 19 patients completed the study. The mean age was 14.3 ± 1.9 years, and the mean body weight was 77.28 ± 25.32 kg. Plasma luseogliflozin concentrations increased in a dose‐dependent manner. The maximum plasma concentration and the area under the curve were comparable to those observed in adults. Administration of luseogliflozin at all dose levels resulted in a substantial increase in urinary glucose excretion. No safety concerns emerged from the single‐dose administration. Luseogliflozin demonstrated favorable pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and safety profiles in pediatric patients, consistent with adult findings. These results support the selection of 2.5‐ and 5‐mg doses for a future Phase 3 pediatric study.Keywords: luseogliflozin, pediatric, pharmacodynamic, pharmacokinetics, type 2 diabetes

The prevalence of type 2 diabetes (T2D) among children and adolescents has been rising worldwide, mirroring the global increase in childhood obesity. 1 Similar to that observed in adult patients, the pathophysiology of T2D in children and adolescents involves a combination of pancreatic β‐cell dysfunction and insulin resistance. However, disease progression is considered to be more rapid in children and adolescents than in adults. 2 Evidence from recent studies indicates that pancreatic β‐cell function declines at a faster rate in this younger population, and that complications tend to develop earlier and with greater severity compared with individuals with type 1 diabetes or adults with T2D. 3 , 4 These observations underscore the urgent need for early therapeutic intervention in children and adolescents with T2D.

The management of pediatric T2D primarily focuses on lifestyle modifications, including dietary adjustments and increased physical activity, similar to treatment strategies used in adults. Pharmacotherapy is introduced when lifestyle measures alone fail to achieve glycemic targets, typically defined as HbA1c levels below 6.5% or 7.0%, depending on the patient's clinical context. 5 , 6 Recently, sodium‐glucose co‐transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, such as empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, and canagliflozin, have been approved for pediatric use in the United States and Europe at the same dosages and regimens as for adults with T2D. 7 However, Japan currently approves only metformin and glimepiride for use in children and adolescents with T2D. This gap underscores the pressing need to expand therapeutic options for pediatric T2D in Japan.

Luseogliflozin (TS‐071) is a selective SGLT2 inhibitor approved for adult use and marketed in Japan as well as in several countries across Asia and the Eurasian Economic Union. In adults with T2D, it lowers HbA1c levels via an insulin‐independent mechanism that enhances urinary glucose excretion. 8 Considering the similarities in the pathophysiology of T2D between the pediatric and adult populations, luseogliflozin has the potential to serve as a valuable treatment option for children and adolescents with T2D. Although studies on other SGLT2 inhibitors have shown comparable pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) profiles between adults and pediatric patients, 9 , 10 , 11 the corresponding data for luseogliflozin in children and adolescents with T2D remain unavailable. Therefore, this study presents the findings of a single‐dose PK and PD assessment of luseogliflozin in children and adolescents with T2D.

Methods

This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, the Japanese Pharmaceutical Affairs Law, and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board of each participating site. Before enrollment, written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants, along with assent from the participants themselves. The study was registered with the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (Trial ID: jRCT2080223964).

Study Design

This was a Phase I, single‐dose, open‐label, parallel‐group study comprising three treatment arms with different doses of luseogliflozin (1.25, 2.5, and 5 mg). The study was conducted at 20 clinical sites across Japan between August 2018 and September 2019.

The key inclusion criteria were as follows: patients aged 9‐17 years with a confirmed diagnosis of T2D, an HbA1c (NGSP) level between 6.5% and 10.5%, and not currently receiving antidiabetic medications. Prior metformin use was allowed if administered at a stable dose and under consistent conditions. Major exclusion criteria included a history of diabetic ketoacidosis, angiopathy‐related complications, hepatic or renal disorders, other types of diabetes, positivity for islet‐related autoantibodies, or endocrine/genetic syndromes affecting glucose metabolism.

During hospitalization, participants were provided standardized meals totaling 1800 kilocalories per day to allow accurate evaluation of urinary glucose excretion. To balance age distribution across dosage groups, participants were stratified into two cohorts: 9‐15 years and 16‐17 years. Each participant received a single oral dose of luseogliflozin (1.25, 2.5, or 5 mg) with 150 mL of water immediately before breakfast. Blood and urine samples were collected for 24 h postadministration, and all participants underwent a follow‐up examination within 4‐10 days after dosing.

Endpoints and Assessment

The PK endpoint was the plasma concentration of unchanged luseogliflozin and its metabolites. For each treatment group, descriptive statistics were calculated for plasma concentrations of the parent compound and its active metabolite (M2). PK parameters were determined using non‐compartmental analysis, with descriptive statistics summarized by treatment group. Dose proportionality for key PK parameters (Cmax and AUC) was also evaluated. The PD endpoint was the quantitative assessment of urinary glucose excretion. Descriptive statistics were calculated for urinary glucose values in each treatment group. Changes in urinary glucose excretion were determined by comparing 24‐h urine collections obtained prior to dosing (Day −1) and after dosing (Day 1). Dose–response relationships were analyzed using an analysis of covariance model with fasting plasma glucose (FPG) as a covariate, and dose–response was assessed using pre‐specified linear contrasts (−1, 0, 1), (−2, 1, 1), and (−1, −1, 2) across the 1.25‐, 2.5‐, and 5‐mg dose groups.

For PK and PD evaluations, blood samples were obtained at pre‐dose and at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h post‐dose. Urine was collected over two intervals: from −24 to 0 h (pretreatment, serving as the baseline for urinary glucose excretion) and from 0 to 24 h after dosing. Plasma concentrations of luseogliflozin and its metabolite (M2) were measured using a validated high‐performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) method. All bioanalytical work was performed at the Kobe Laboratory of CMIC PharmaScience Co., Ltd., in Nishiwaki, Hyogo, Japan. The lower limits of quantification for luseogliflozin and M2 in plasma were 0.05 and 0.1 ng/mL, respectively.

PK parameters were calculated from plasma concentration data using noncompartmental analysis with Phoenix WinNonlin (version 8.0). For luseogliflozin, assessed parameters included maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time 0 to 24 h (AUC0‐24 h), AUC extrapolated to infinity (AUC0‐∞), time to reach maximum concentration (tmax), terminal elimination half‐life (t1/2), apparent clearance (CL/F), and apparent volume of distribution during the terminal phase (Vz/F). For M2, only Cmax, AUC0‐∞, and t1/2 were determined. Dose proportionality of Cmax and AUC0‐∞ was evaluated using a power model to determine the regression coefficient and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Safety Analysis

Adverse events and side effects were summarized by treatment group in terms of frequency and incidence. Furthermore, frequency distributions were analyzed by treatment group and severity level. For clinical assessments—including body weight, vital signs, and electrocardiogram (ECG) measurements—descriptive statistics were calculated for quantitative variables by treatment group, and categorical variables were analyzed using cross‐tabulation.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size was determined to ensure adequate evaluation of the study's primary objectives—pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. In total, 18 participants were planned, with 6 participants assigned to each dosage group. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Japan). As this study was not designed to provide confirmatory or hypothesis‐testing evidence, no multiplicity adjustment was applied. The significance level was set at 5% (two‐sided). Participants were included in the PK, PD, or safety analysis sets if they met the respective criteria: (1) fulfilled minimum protocol requirements with no major deviations and had available PK data; (2) received at least one dose of the study drug and had at least one post‐dose PD assessment; or (3) received at least one dose and had at least one post‐dose safety or other relevant assessment. Participants excluded based on the Case and Data Handling Criteria were not included in any analysis set.

Statement of Ethics

This clinical trial has been performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board for each site approved the study protocol. Written informed consent for study participation was obtained from the parents/guardians of all participants, and the pediatric patients themselves provided assent.

Results

Study Sample, Demographics, and Baseline Characteristics

All participants (n = 19) were included in the PK, PD, and safety analysis sets. Of the 27 individuals screened, 19 were enrolled and assigned to treatment groups: 6 participants received the 1.25‐mg dose, 6 received the 2.5‐mg dose, and 7 received the 5‐mg dose of luseogliflozin. All participants completed the trial as planned. As shown in Table 1, demographic and baseline clinical characteristics were generally similar across the three groups. Although the protocol allowed enrollment of participants as young as 9 years, the youngest enrolled participant was 11 years old.

Evaluation of Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics were analyzed in the PK analysis set, with mean parameters for luseogliflozin summarized in Table 2. After a single oral dose, luseogliflozin was rapidly absorbed, reaching peak plasma concentrations (Cmax) at 0.500 h in all three dose groups. As illustrated in Figure 1 and Figure S1, plasma concentrations increased proportionally with dose. The terminal elimination half‐life (t1/2) ranged from 7.89 to 8.78 h across all doses.

The mean Cmax values for the 1.25‐, 2.5‐, and 5‐mg dose groups were 56.2, 114, and 226 ng/mL, respectively, whereas the corresponding mean AUC0‐∞ values were 399, 787, and 1500 h*ng/mL, respectively. Because both CIs included 1, these parameters were consistent with proportionality. Comparable dose‐normalized Cmax and AUC0‐∞ were observed across the 1.25, 2.5, and 5 mg (data not shown).

Evaluation of Pharmacodynamics

PD assessments, conducted in the PD analysis set, used urinary glucose excretion as the primary endpoint (Table 3). At baseline (Day −1), glucose excretion was minimal and comparable among the three dosage groups. Following a single dose of luseogliflozin (1.25‐5 mg), mean changes from baseline ranged from 48.68 to 67.19 g, indicating that all doses promoted urinary glucose excretion. However, no statistically significant dose–response relationship was detected for the changes in urinary glucose excretion (Table 4).

Table 5 compares the PK and PD profiles of luseogliflozin between pediatric and adult populations. 12 In both the adult and pediatric studies, a dietary restriction of 1800 kilocalories per day was implemented. Pediatric participants showed lower FPG levels, higher BMI, and higher estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) than adults. Evaluation of Safety

Safety was assessed in the safety analysis set (SAF), which included all 19 participants. Six participants experienced at least one adverse event: two of six in the 2.5‐mg group and four of seven in the 5‐mg group. Reported events in the 2.5‐mg group included nausea (n = 1) and nasopharyngitis (n = 1). In the 5‐mg group, adverse events comprised headache (n = 2), acetonemia (n = 1), and miliaria (n = 1), with the acetonemia deemed related to the study drug. All adverse events were mild in severity and resolved either spontaneously or with appropriate treatment. There were no deaths, serious adverse events, or other clinically significant issues reported.

Discussion

There is a significant unmet need for new pharmacological treatments for children and adolescents with T2D, especially in Japan, where available treatment options are more limited compared to global standards. This study is the first to present data on the pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and safety of a single dose of luseogliflozin, an SGLT2 inhibitor, in Japanese children and adolescents with T2D. Luseogliflozin is already approved for use in adults with T2D and has demonstrated established efficacy and safety. To support potential pediatric approval, it was essential to determine whether a dosage specifically tailored for children and adolescents is necessary, beyond the adult‐approved dose, through evaluation of PK, PD, and safety profiles in this younger population.

The key finding of this study is that the PK profile of luseogliflozin—currently approved only for adult use—was comparable between pediatric and adult populations. AUC, Cmax, and other PK values showed that exposure to luseogliflozin and its metabolite at 2.5‐ and 5‐mg doses was similar in both groups. This similarity may be attributed to the relatively advanced physiological development and adult‐like body weight of children and adolescents with T2D. Normal eGFR values in the pediatric patients further support the interpretation that PK profiles were comparable between pediatric and adult populations. Moreover, exposure to M2, the active metabolite of luseogliflozin primarily formed via CYP3A4/5, 13 was also comparable between the two populations, aligning with the fact that CYP3A4/5 expression generally reaches adult levels by around 5 years of age. 14 In addition, M2 exhibits lower in vitro potency than luseogliflozin and its molar AUC ratio on Day 7 after multiple dosing is approximately 14% in adults, 12 suggesting that M2 contributes only a small fraction to total SGLT2 inhibition in pediatric patients as well.

The PD effect of luseogliflozin on urinary glucose excretion was evident in children and adolescents with T2D. At doses of 2.5‐5 mg, mean urinary glucose excretion ranged from 57.4 to 67.2 g/day, whereas adult patients receiving the same doses showed higher values, ranging from 94.8 to 99.8 g/day, indicating reduced excretion in the pediatric group. A PKPD study of the SGLT2 inhibitor canagliflozin (100 and 300 mg) in children and adolescents with T2D similarly reported lower urinary glucose excretion (74.1 and 68.6 g/day), attributed to low baseline plasma glucose levels (134.0 and 106.3 mg/dL). 11

It is well established that urinary glucose excretion with SGLT2 inhibitors is reduced by factors such as low FPG, female sex, and low eGFR. 10 , 15 Notably, the PD response to SGLT2 inhibitors is strongly dependent on plasma glucose concentration, as the mechanism involves renal clearance of excess glucose through relatively simple saturation kinetics. 16 , 17 In this study, the overall reduction in urinary glucose excretion among children and adolescents was likely driven by participants with lower baseline glucose levels, since excretion levels in the subgroup with elevated FPG (≥126 mg/dL) were comparable to those of adults. The observed discrepancy between low plasma glucose and high HbA1c levels remains unclear but may be due to transient improvements in glycemic control following hospitalization and dietary calorie restriction during the trial period. Because the majority of participants had not been following a calorie‑restricted diet prior to enrollment in the pediatric study, whereas eligibility for the adult study required at least 4 weeks of dietary therapy, it is likely that the dietary restrictions imposed during hospitalization were more stringent than those in the outpatient setting. Given the unique pharmacological mechanism of SGLT2 inhibitors—where urinary glucose excretion positively correlates with FPG—this property may help reduce the risk of hypoglycemia in children and adolescents with T2D.

This study has certain limitations, including its single‐dose design and the relatively small sample sizes across the three treatment groups. Although plasma concentration–time profile of luseogliflozin in the terminal elimination phase demonstrated linearity in a semi‐log scale graph (Figure S1), the interpretation of the pharmacokinetic parameters may be limited because the sampling period was restricted to 24 h post‑dose. The pharmacokinetics of luseogliflozin remain unclear in pediatric patients with type 2 diabetes who have renal impairment. To confirm the long‐term efficacy and safety of luseogliflozin in children and adolescents with T2D, placebo‐controlled studies with longer treatment periods are necessary.

In summary, after a single oral dose of luseogliflozin, the PK and PD profiles, urinary glucose excretion, and short‐term safety outcomes in children and adolescents with T2D were comparable to those observed in adults. These findings suggest that the adult dosing regimen can reasonably be applied to children and adolescents with T2D, supporting the appropriateness of initiating treatment at 2.5 mg in pediatric patients. These results provide a basis for the dosing strategy planned for the phase III pediatric trial (Japan Registry of Clinical Trials ID: jRCT2031200244).
