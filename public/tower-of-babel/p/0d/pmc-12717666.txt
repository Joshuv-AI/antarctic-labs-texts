Harnessing stromal vascular fraction-based therapies for wound healing: Mechanisms, synergies, and clinical translation
Authors: Jing Wu, Yao Wang, Wenjie Chen, Min Lin, Jinwen Jiang, Hongde Jiang, Zehuan Li, Kailei Xu, Bin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Wound healing remains a major challenge in contemporary medicine, particularly with the increasing incidence of chronic wounds, such as those associated with diabetes and thermal injuries. Current treatments such as vacuum sealing drainage, topical therapies, and autologous skin grafting are limited by issues like poor therapeutic efficacy, frequent dressing changes, and immune responses. Cell-based therapies have shown promise but are hindered by single-cell type limitations and hostile wound microenvironments. The stromal vascular fraction (SVF) has emerged as a potential solution, where the enzymatic digestion (E-SVF) contains various cell types, including adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), endothelial cells, and fibroblasts, while mechanical digestion (M-SVF) introduces additional extracellular matrix (ECM), termed stromal vascular matrix (SVM). This review systematically evaluates the applications of SVF and SVM in wound healing. SVF promotes wound repair through proangiogenic, immunomodulatory, and ECM remodeling effects. When combined with platelet-rich plasma (PRP) or biomaterials, its efficacy is further enhanced through the synergistic regulation of inflammation and angiogenesis. SVM, with preserved ECM, and SVM-conditioned medium (SVM-CM), rich in growth factors, demonstrate superior wound healing capabilities compared to conventional SVF. Despite the challenges posed by impaired viability and function of aged SVF-derived ADSCs and the low mechanical properties of SVM, emerging technologies, such as 3D cell cultures to enhance stemness and the integration of other biomaterials to improve mechanical strength, offer promising solutions. This review highlights the potential of SVF as an autologous, multifunctional strategy to address unmet needs in wound healing and provides insights into future clinical applications and research directions.

Introduction

Skin functions as the principal barrier against environmental pathogens, mechanical stress, and radiation, while also maintaining homeostasis through regulated repair mechanisms that preserve structural integrity and barrier function [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. In cases of skin injury, wound healing remains a major challenge in contemporary medicine, particularly with the increasing incidence of chronic wounds, such as those associated with diabetes and thermal injuries [5], which are manily caused by microbial colonization, leading to dysregulation across the hemostatic, inflammatory, proliferative, and remodeling phases, resulting in chronic non-healing wounds [2,6]. Standard management of hard-to-heal wounds includes vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) [7], topical therapies (e.g., silver sulfadiazine, petrolatum-based formulations, Aloe vera derivatives), and autologous skin grafting for structural restoration [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. Despite these interventions, the inherent pathophysiological complexity of chronic wounds continues to present therapeutic challenges and imposes a considerable socioeconomic burden on healthcare systems worldwide [14].

The recent advancements in wound healing technology have spurred the development of cell-based therapies aimed at improving wound healing outcomes. Compared to conventional methods, cell therapy offers advantages such as simplicity, reduced time consumption, and decreased surgical burden for patients during the treatment. Various wound-healing-related cells, such as fibroblasts, keratinocytes, bone marrow stem cells, and platelets, have shown potential to accelerate wound repair. However, the therapeutic efficacy of cellular interventions is significantly hindered by the use of a single cell type and the hostile wound bed microenvironment, which compromises cellular viability and retention during the healing process [15,16]. To address these challenges, numerous studies have combined cell therapy with hydrogels to enhance cellular viability, improve cell-matrix interactions, and reduce cell leakage. Despite these improvements, critical limitations remain, including immune-mediated foreign body responses [17], suboptimal drug-loading capacities, non-physiological release kinetics, and inadequate hydration maintenance. These material deficiencies necessitate frequent dressing changes, which increases the risk of iatrogenic tissue damage during clinical management [15,18].

The stromal vascular fraction (SVF) is a heterogeneous cellular population isolated from adipose tissue, comprising adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), adipose tissue-derived progenitor cells (AEPCs), adipose stromal vascular fraction-derived endothelial cells (SVF-ECs), and preadipocytes. This diverse composition underpins its therapeutic potential in regenerative surgery (Fig. 1) [[19], [20], [21]]. As an autologous adipose-derived biomaterial, SVF demonstrates favorable safety profiles, with a reduced incidence of adverse events, and exerts multifaceted regenerative effects, including proangiogenic, immunomodulatory, antifibrotic, and antiapoptotic activities, primarily mediated through paracrine signaling mechanisms [19,22,23]. Current clinical protocols have utilized autologous SVF in plastic and reconstructive surgeries to enhance ischemic tissue revascularization, accelerate wound healing, and reduce immune rejection risks [24]. Wound healing

Wound healing process

Wound healing is a highly coordinated physiological process that occurs in four distinct phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling (Fig. 2A). This intricate mechanism involves the coordinated interaction of growth factor cascades, proteolytic enzymes, and cytokine signaling networks, which collectively regulate cellular dynamics [26,27]. The hemostatic phase begins immediately after tissue injury, where vasoconstriction reduces hemorrhage at the wound site, followed by platelet adhesion to exposed subendothelial matrix components [26,27]. This initial consolidation enables the transition to the inflammatory phase, characterized by the cessation of active bleeding [28]. During the inflammatory phase, neutrophils and macrophages infiltrate the wound bed. These cells phagocytose bacterial pathogens, foreign particles, and necrotic tissue, while secreting chemokines and growth factors that regulate cellular recruitment and differentiation. Simultaneously, increased vascular permeability promotes leukocyte diapedesis and facilitates nutrient transport and metabolic waste clearance through interstitial fluid dynamics [[26], [27], [28]]. The proliferative phase begins 2–3 days post-injury and lasts for several weeks. It is marked by the proliferation of keratinocytes and fibroblasts, which generate granulation tissue, a vascularized provisional matrix composed of fibroblasts, inflammatory cells, and nascent capillaries, that progressively fills the wound defect. Epithelial regeneration occurs through centrifugal migration and differentiation of basal keratinocytes, completing re-epithelialization once the wound is covered [28]. During this phase, collagen deposition increases, and neovascularization occurs through endothelial sprouting, forming capillary networks vital for tissue perfusion [27,28]. The remodeling phase begins with a regulated collagen metabolic balance, characterized by a shift from type III to type I collagen, accompanied by fibril realignment into linear configurations to enhance tissue biomechanical strength. Simultaneously, vascular networks regress through selective capillary pruning and arteriovenous differentiation. Wound types

Wound healing processes differ significantly between acute and chronic wounds. Acute healing follows a coordinated, temporally regulated sequence of events, while chronic wound pathogenesis is characterized by dysregulated phase transitions that disrupt this biological orchestration, resulting in impaired progression through the regenerative phases (Fig. 2B). The transition from acute to chronic wound pathology is often triggered by microbial colonization in suboptimal healing conditions, leading to prolonged inflammatory responses that hinder the activation of the proliferative phase and impede tissue regeneration. Diabetic and thermal wounds serve as prime examples of this pathological process [27]. In addition, wounds typically transit from physiological alkalinity immediately after injury to an acidic microenvironment during normal repair. However, chronic wounds remain in an alkaline state due to persistent inflammation-mediated biochemical alterations, which promote microbial growth and cellular dysfunction [31].

Diabetic wounds

Diabetes represents one of the most rapidly escalating global public health crises of the 21st century. Between 2000 and 2021, the global prevalence of diabetes increased from 4.6 % to 10.5 %, with the number of individuals affected surpassing 537 million [32,33]. In China, the age-standardized prevalence rate increased by nearly 50 % between 2005 and 2023, rising from 7.53 % to 13.7 %. If current trends persist, China's national obesity prevalence is projected to reach 29.1 % by 2050 [34]. Among the most severe complications of diabetes are diabetic wounds, which affect approximately 18.6 million people worldwide each year [35]. Diabetic wounds epitomize a pathophysiologically intricate condition, characterized by glucolipid metabolic dysregulation and persistent hyperglycemia (Fig. 2C). This chronic hyperglycemia leads to the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products, which stiffen collagen and extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins and perturb nitric oxide production, resulting in impaired angiogenesis and endothelial dysfunction [[36], [37], [38]]. The induced macrovascular disease and microvascular complications further decrease tissue perfusion and inhibit nutrient and oxygen delivery, delaying wound healing. In addition, diabetes also damages sensory, motor, and autonomic nerves via oxidative stress, polyol pathway activation, ATP insufficiency, and PKC activation [39]. This leads to loss of protective sensation, deformities, dry cracked skin, and increased risk of unnoticed trauma. This vicious self-reinforcing cycle impedes the transition from the inflammatory to the proliferative phase during wound healing, leading to persistent non-healing ulcers characterized by edema, pain, and recurrent infection.

Thermal injuries

Thermal injuries initiate distinct pathophysiological processes depending on the mode of energy transfer, resulting in categorized burn wounds by depth [40]. Superficial (first-degree) burns are confined to the epidermis and present with erythema and pain but heal spontaneously within approximately 5–10 days. Superficial partial-thickness (second-degree) burns extend into the papillary dermis, often exhibit blistering, blanch under pressure, and are intensely painful, typically resolving in 2–3 weeks with conservative management, generally resulting in minimal scarring. Deep partial-thickness (deep second-degree) burns involve the reticular dermis, manifest as pale or mottled wound beds with reduced blanching, and surprisingly diminished pain due to partial nerve destruction; they may heal over 3–8 weeks but often require surgical debridement to prevent complications such as scarring and contracture. Full-thickness (third-degree) burns encompass the epidermis and dermis down to subcutaneous tissue; these injuries are characterized by leathery, non-blanching eschar and insensitivity due to complete nerve loss, necessitating early surgical excision and grafting for optimal recovery. Finally, fourth-degree burns extend through skin and subcutaneous layers into muscle, tendon, or bone, requiring aggressive infection prophylaxis and reconstructive surgery to restore structural integrity. Severe burns precipitate a progressive systemic response that evolves from localized inflammation to a sustained hypermetabolic and hypercatabolic state marked by proteolytic cascades, extensive protein wasting, and multiorgan dysfunction [40]. This sustained hypercatabolic state creates a persistent inflammatory milieu marked by epidermal barrier disruption, which compromises microbial defense mechanisms and exponentially elevates risks of polymicrobial colonization, sepsis, and systemic infections [41].

Conversely, thermal damage can also be produced by low temperatures, such as frostbite, which involves a biphasic pathogenesis: initial cryocrystallization leads to direct cellular membrane rupture and tissue necrosis, followed by ischemia–reperfusion injury characterized by microvascular thrombosis, endothelial damage, inflammation, edema, and oxidative‐free radical formation. Together, these dual mechanisms create a complex wound microenvironment with impaired angiogenesis and delayed re-epithelialization relative to thermal burns [[41], [42], [43]].

SVF

The SVF is a heterogeneous cellular population isolated from adipose tissue, primarily sourced from subcutaneous depots including abdominal and inguinal regions, with additional harvest sites comprising dorsal skin and interscapular adipose tissue [[44], [45], [46]]. This complex mixture includes adipose-derived stem/stromal cells (ADSCs), endothelial progenitor and mature endothelial cells, pericytes, fibroblasts, along with various immune and hematopoietic elements such as macrophages and lymphocytes (Fig. 3A) [47]. SVF isolation can be achieved via enzymatic digestion (E-SVF) or non-enzymatic mechanical digestion (M-SVF) (Fig. 3B). E-SVF, the predominant methodology, utilizes collagenase to enzymatically dissociate lipoaspirate, typically through incubation followed by centrifugation. This process separates the sample into four layers, including an oily supernatant, residual adipose tissue, an aqueous phase, and a dispersed cellular pellet, yielding significantly higher nucleated cell counts compared to mechanical approaches [48,49]. However, E-SVF entails substantial limitations, where it requires specialized laboratory infrastructure and is subject to stringent regulatory controls, as collagenase digestion is considered “more than minimally manipulated,” particularly by the FDA, thereby necessitating rigorous quality control and sterility assurance. Furthermore, reliance on xenogeneic enzymes introduces potential safety risks, including immune reactions, pathogen contamination, and enzymatic destruction of the ECM, that may adversely affect cell function and therapeutic efficacy.

M-SVF was developed to address the constraints of enzymatic methods by replacing collagenase digestion with mechanical processing techniques, such as agitation, pipetting, and ultrasonication. This approach yields a subcompartment that contains abundant native ECM enriched with SVF-like cells [50], named the stromal vascular matrix (SVM). The retention of ECM in SVM not only ensures structural integrity but also enriches the cellular content, enhancing the regenerative potential of mechanically isolated adipose tissue [48].

Adipose-derived stem/stromal cells

ADSCs, a multipotent subset of mesenchymal stromal cells derived from the SVF, are characterized by positive expression of CD73, CD90, CD105, and CD29 [52,53]. Compared to bone marrow stem cells, ADSCs offer a ∼500-fold higher yield per unit volume of adipose tissue, exhibit enhanced proliferative capacity, and demonstrate intrinsic antimicrobial activity against multidrug-resistant pathogens [45,46]. Functionally, ADSCs support tissue repair through paracrine secretion of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), interleukin 10 (IL-10), which modulate inflammation, and proangiogenic factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) [[54], [55], [56]], thereby addressing the interconnected pathophysiological triad of inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, and fibrotic remodeling [54,57]. In preclinical wound healing models, ADSC-laden hydrogels could accelerate re-epithelialization and tissue regeneration [58]. Additionally, ADSCs could also enhance therapeutic efficacy via immunometabolic pathway modulation, synergistically modify the wound microenvironment while preserving autologous transplantation advantages [52,57,59,60]. These cells are also efficacious in treating cryogenic injuries like frostbite due to their promotion of angiogenesis and immunomodulation [54]. In diabetic wound models, ADSCs could accelerate healing by resolving the pathophysiological triad of persistent inflammation, angiogenic impairment, and proliferative arrest, achieved through promoting M2 macrophage polarization during the proliferative phase [57,60].

Endothelial cells

Endothelial progenitor cells (EPC) and mature endothelial cells (EC) within SVF serve as central mediators of angiogenesis via coordinated vasculogenic processes [[61], [62], [63]]. EPCs in SVF are typically characterized by positive expression of CD34 and CD31, and negative expression of CD45 phenotype, enabling them to differentiate into functional ECs in vitro and contribute to both de novo vessel formation (vasculogenesis) and expansion of existing vasculature (angiogenesis), underscoring their potential for treating ischemic conditions [62,64]. In contrast, mature ECs, commonly characterized by positive expression of CD31, and negative expression of CD45 and CD34 phenotype, exhibit pronounced vasculogenic capacity, autonomously self-assembling into hierarchically branched vascular networks that demonstrate functional maturity [65]. During ischemic wound healing, ECs integrate with host vasculature via hybrid vessel formation, where donor EC-lined tubes recruit host pericytes and smooth muscle cells, enhancing structural stabilization and preventing regression [66]. In a diabetic mouse model of full-thickness dorsal wounds, transplantation of SVF-derived EPCs resulted in accelerated wound closure and mature vessel development, evidenced by elevated local VEGF and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) expression [67]. Additionally, induction of autologous endothelial differentiation from induced pluripotent stem cells has yielded ECs capable of promoting angiogenesis through short-term paracrine effects while simultaneously enhancing collagen deposition and recruiting macrophages to modulate inflammatory responses and tissue remodeling [68].

Fibroblasts

Fibroblasts, the principal connective tissue cells, synthesize ECM components essential for wound healing and tissue remodeling [69]. Transcriptomic analyses have identified core fibroblast markers, including Col1a1, Col1a2, Fbln2, as well as cell-surface receptors CD34 and PDGFRA, confirming their conserved ECM-producing roles across tissues [70]. Upon tissue injury, fibroblasts become activated and partially differentiate into myofibroblasts, both of which synthesize key ECM and basement membrane constituents, including collagens type I, III, IV, and V, to orchestrate physiological tissue regeneration while regulating fibrotic remodeling through spatiotemporal deposition control [[71], [72], [73], [74], [75], [76]]. By incorporating halloysite nanotubes into the hydrogel scaffold, fibroblasts demonstrated enhanced proliferation and upregulated expression of adhesion molecules, which collectively accelerated wound closure and epidermal regeneration in full-thickness mouse models [77]. In burn wound, silk fibroin scaffolds functionalized with lipoic acid-modified gold nanoclusters demonstrated accelerated wound healing through promoting fibroblast proliferation and migration, thereby accelerating burn wound healing in mice [78].

Macrophages

Macrophages involved in wound healing are primarily categorized into two subtypes, including proinflammatory M1 and proregenerative M2. M1 macrophages predominantly express CD86 and iNOS, and during the initial inflammatory phase, secrete IL-12 and reactive oxygen species to eliminate pathogens. As healing progresses, M2 macrophages, characterized by CD206 and Arg-1 expression, become predominant, releasing anti-inflammatory mediators such as IL-10 to suppress excessive inflammation and mitigate tissue damage, thereby facilitating repair. Thus, maintaining M1-M2 phenotypic balance is essential for effective wound healing [[79], [80], [81], [82]]. Moreover, macrophages secrete growth factors including VEGF-A and TGF-β, which not only stimulate cell proliferation and angiogenesis but also underpin cytokine-mediated neovascularization and matrix remodeling [65,83]. Macrophages are indispensable for SVF-derived vascular network formation, as evidenced by impaired neovascularization following macrophage depletion in recipient models. Transplantation of macrophage-depleted SVF into mice ischemic hindlimbs failed to establish functional vascular networks, however, VEGF-A supplementation significantly improved neovascularization. This recovery mechanism implicates macrophage-derived VEGF-A and ancillary angiogenic factors as critical mediators of donor-recipient vascular anastomosis through paracrine regulation of endothelial remodeling [84].

SVM

The SVM, a mechanically derived fraction of lipoaspirate, is isolated through emulsification techniques involving centrifugation, extraction of the intermediate layer, reciprocal syringe transfer for homogenization, and a final centrifugation step to yield SVM. This protocol preserves key SVF cell components while markedly enriching ECM content [85], such as collagens, glycoproteins, proteoglycans, and polysaccharides, which collectively regulate cell adhesion, mechanotransduction, and paracrine communication, maintaining the organizational integrity and regenerative potential of adipose tissue [86].

Collagens represent the predominant ECM components within adipose tissue, comprising a distinctive profile that includes types I, IV, V, VI, VII, VIII, and IX [86]. Type I collagen functions as the primary architectural scaffold, conferring structural integrity and mechanical stability to adipose depots [80]. Notably, type VI collagen is exceptionally abundant and plays an essential role in ECM homeostasis via formation of a unique microfibrillar network that enhances matrix resilience and facilitates cellular adhesion [87,88]. Beyond its structural contributions, type VI collagen is implicated in regulating adipose tissue inflammation, fibrosis, and systemic insulin resistance [89].

Fibronectin is prominently expressed in both mature adipose tissue and preadipocytes, where it serves as the principal mechanostructural glycoprotein within the ECM [86]. In the adipose ECM architecture, fibronectin collaborates with collagen I and IV to form dense supramolecular networks, while laminin predominates within the basement membrane, assembling continuous pericellular sheaths that ensheath adipocyte membranes [[90], [91], [92]]. Fibronectin also modulates adipocyte differentiation by regulating lipogenic gene expression and impeding the cytoskeletal remodeling necessary for adipogenesis. Dysregulated fibronectin deposition in obesity contributes to ECM rigidity, inflammation, and impaired adipocyte function, underpinning metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes [93]. Additionally, fibronectin contains multiple binding domains that anchor collagens, proteoglycans, fibulin, and thrombospondin, facilitating the structural integration of matrix components [94].

Hyaluronic acid (HA), a glycosaminoglycan polymer within the ECM, fulfills both structural and signaling roles [95]. As a core architectural component, HA interacts dynamically with collagenous and noncollagenous proteins to stabilize ECM maturation while engaging adipocyte surface receptors, notably CD44, to regulate downstream pathways governing adipogenesis, lipid and glucose metabolism, and cellular motility [86]. Beyond its biomechanical support, HA orchestrates pleiotropic biological processes, where the interaction between HA and CD44 may modulate inflammatory responses in adipose tissue, thereby influencing insulin effects and contributing to insulin resistance [86,96]. Beyond binding to CD44, HA interacts with the hyaluronan-mediated motility receptor, regulating cell proliferation, aggregation, migration, and activation [97].

SVF for wound healing applications

Leveraging its intrinsic paracrine and immunomodulatory capabilities, SVF has been explored as a versatile therapeutic platform in wound healing. It has been applied as monotherapy, augmented with other bioactive agents, or integrated into biomaterial scaffolds to orchestrate spatial and temporal control over regenerative processes—enhancing angiogenesis, modulating inflammation, and promoting ECM remodeling.

Monotherapy with SVF alone

SVF has demonstrated therapeutic potential in the treatment of thermal injuries (Table 1). Alexandre et al. administered SVF directly into burn sites and observed enhanced secretion of key pro-regenerative cytokines, including bFGF, VEGF, and TGF, which collectively promoted fibroblast proliferation, migration, and vascular regeneration. In addition, SVF treatment also significantly enhanced fibrin deposition compared to conventional wound dressing with 2 % silver sulfadiazine, a widely used clinical therapy (Fig. 4A) [98]. In a subsequent study, they further developed a full thickness burn model to evaluate the combined effects of SVF and photobiomodulation (PBM) therapy. The findings indicated a synergistic effect, characterized by a prolonged early inflammatory response, enhanced angiogenesis, accelerated fibroblast migration, and increased collagen deposition, all contributing to improved wound healing outcomes [99]. Similarly, Çağatay et al. investigated the efficacy of SVF in treating frostbite injuries using a rat model of hypothermic frostbite. Beyond thermal injuries, the therapeutic efficacy of SVF has also been demonstrated in the treatment of chronic wounds. In a co-culture study involving murine fibroblasts under hyperglycemic conditions, SVF enhanced the expression of key wound-healing cytokines, including PDGF, IL-1, bFGF, and IL-10, which collectively promoted fibroblast proliferation and migration, thereby accelerating wound healing in a diabetic microenvironment [102]. In a parallel study exploring wound repair under hyperglycemia, a full-thickness skin incisions were established on the backs of diabetic mice and administered with SVF and ADSCs. This combination therapy led to improved wound closure and significantly increased collagen deposition. Mechanistically, SVF contributed to vascular regeneration and matrix remodeling, while ADSCs facilitated fibroblast migration and promoted capillary network formation by human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (Fig. 4B) [101]. These findings suggest that locally administered autologous SVF may offer a promising therapeutic strategy for chronic wound management.

In deep dermal wound models, Sacharaswaty et al. reported significantly elevated expression of angiogenic factors following both topical and intradermal application of SVF/PRP compared to Vaseline-treated controls, with intradermal injection producing more pronounced angiogenic responses (Fig. 5B) [105]. In dermal regeneration, angiogenesis constitutes a critical component. Elisa et al. plated SVF cells on Integra® Dermal Regeneration Template (DRT) and cultured them in PRP-supplemented medium. This induced significantly elevated VEGF secretion from ADSCs and generated lacuna-like structures. CD31 immunostaining demonstrated positive labeling in cells circumscribing these structures, exhibiting a distribution pattern similar to smooth muscle α-actin (SMA), indicating the pro-angiogenic potential of SVF/PRP [106]. In full-thickness burn injuries, SVF/PRP treatment upregulated EGF expression, which promoted re-epithelialization through enhanced keratinocyte migration [107]. Moreover, SVF/PRP combinations modulated inflammatory responses; in second-degree burns, the therapy attenuated inflammation by downregulating proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, while concurrently promoting vascular reconstruction and edema resolution (Fig. 5C) [108]. In models of deep burns, SVF/PRP exhibited temporally dynamic regulation of inflammatory mediators-initially amplifying acute-phase markers such as COX-2 and enhancing polymorphonuclear leukocyte and lymphocyte infiltration, followed by immune resolution through M2 macrophage polarization [109]. These phase-appropriate responses highlight the immunomodulatory precision of SVF/PRP therapy. Several other studies have consistently shown superior efficacy of SVF/PRP over conventional interventions such as Vaseline and saline in burn wound management [105,[107], [108], [109]]. Collectively, SVF/PRP combination therapy acts as a multimodal regulator of wound healing by promoting angiogenesis and modulating inflammation, thereby accelerating burn repair through coordinated cellular and molecular mechanisms.

Biomaterial-based

Direct administration of SVF, either alone or combined with bioactive factors, has been shown to result in poor retention due to rapid dispersion into the extracellular fluid [111]. In contrast, scaffold-mediated delivery systems significantly enhance SVF engraftment and retention by providing structural support and promoting cell attachment within the wound microenvironment (Table 2) [111,112].

Biological materials are generally classified into natural, synthetic, and composite categories, each providing distinct advantages for their extensive use in wound healing applications [113]. For example, Pluronic F127 (PF127) is a synthetic biomaterials used in wound therapy due to its thermosensitivity, injectability, and biodegradability [114]. For example, Khan et al. developed a PF127 hydrogel containing SVFs for a full-thickness skin wound model, which resulted in significantly enhanced wound closure [115]. Similarly, other studies have shown that SVF embedded in PF127 promotes fibroblast migration, upregulates key cytokines involved in wound healing, and enhances the expression of related genes, thereby accelerating repair in excisional wound models [116].

Although synthetic materials provide superior adaptability for complex engineering designs, their biocompatibility and immune evasion capabilities are generally inferior to those of natural materials [117]. Collagen, the principal component of the ECM, plays a critical role in wound healing. Due to its exceptional biocompatibility, collagen is frequently combined with other biomaterials and encapsulated with SVFs to enhance tissue regeneration (Fig. 6A) [118,119]. For example, CD248+ cells isolated from SVFs embedded in pullulan-collagen hydrogels showed robust secretion of VEGF and HGF, which significantly promoted angiogenesis [120]. Additionally, Hajime et al. embedded SVF in collagen-glycosaminoglycan (c-GAG) scaffolds, synergistically enhanced full-thickness wound repair through MSC-mediated paracrine secretion of growth factors and cytokines. Moreover, angiogenic progenitors differentiated into functional ECs to promote vascular regeneration [44]. For diabetic wound healing, SVFs encapsulated within fibronectin-collagen hydrogels exhibited differentiation toward ECs, as indicated by the expression of CD31 and CD34, which progressively formed a vascular network. Gelatin, a hydrolyzed derivative of collagen, offers notable biological benefits due to its retention of RGD motifs and susceptibility to degradation by matrix metalloproteinases. These properties facilitate cellular recognition, adhesion, and proliferation [122]. Tilapia-derived gelatin hydrogels with three-dimensional porous architectures, mimicking native ECM topology, optimize SVF paracrine activity by elevating the expression of EGF, bFGF, and VEGF, thus accelerating epithelialization, angiogenesis, and type I collagen deposition (Fig. 6B) [123]. Despite its widespread use in tissue engineering, gelatin exhibits inherent structural instability, which is addressed by methacrylation, a chemical modification that produces gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA). This modification introduces photocrosslinkable moieties, enhancing mechanical resilience while preserving bioactivity [122,124]. Mauro et al. engineered GelMA scaffolds encapsulating SVF, which preserved cellular viability and proliferative capacity while stimulating migratory behavior. Mechanistic analyses confirmed that upregulated bFGF expression and induced adipogenic differentiation were key contributors to enhanced epidermal regeneration (Fig. 6C) [125].

In addition to the materials mentioned, numerous other polysaccharide-based biomaterials are widely used in wound healing due to their abundant availability and ease of cross-linking modification [126]. For instance, Helena et al. developed Gellan gum/Gellan gum-divinyl sulfone (GG/GGDVS)-peptide spongy hydrogels, mixed them with SVF, and successfully modulated the inflammatory phase duration in murine full-thickness wounds [118].

The preserved ECM provides superior cytoprotection for cells compared to SVF cell-only, maintaining adipose-derived cells within their native niche microenvironment (Table 3) [128]. This ECM-cell interactions ensure optimal proliferative capacity and tissue regenerative potential through enhanced cellular retention and paracrine factor stabilization (Fig. 7A) [129]. For chronic wounds, SVF treatment significantly attenuated diabetic wound pathogenesis by reducing localized inflammation, mitigating fibrotic deposition, enhancing neovascularization, and promoting peripheral nerve axon regeneration. Mechanistically, this therapeutic approach suppresses TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB axis activation, thereby inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokine cascades while potentiating growth factor-mediated tissue restitution [134]. In the context of flap transplantation, Zhang et al. utilized SVF to treat ischemic skin flaps in nude mice and observed a reduction in the ischemic area alongside an increase in the number of CD31+ capillaries in the treated flaps. The SVF treatment also enhanced the presence of ADSCs and elevated the expression of VEGF and bFGF. Furthermore, SVF directly transplanted onto the flaps acted as vascular components, exhibiting positive α-smooth muscle actin expression. The combined effects of these factors contributed to the improved survival of ischemic flaps [135].

The collective impact of SVF on fibroblasts substantiates its therapeutic efficacy in scar remediation. In rabbit ear hypertrophic scar models, SVF demonstrated multifaceted therapeutic superiority by significantly improving scar pliability and histoarchitectural normalization. This was achieved by preserving ECM structural integrity and cellular viability, thereby suppressing pathological fibrosis through mechanisms including attenuation of macrophage infiltration and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, as well as inhibition of myofibroblast transdifferentiation and reduced disorganized collagen deposition (Fig. 7B) [131]. Studies by Qian et al. compared the therapeutic efficacy of SVF to botulinum toxin type A (BTA), with both interventions significantly reducing fibroblast activation and suppressing inflammatory signaling. Notably, SVF demonstrated superior capacity to modulate the inflammatory microenvironment by enhancing M2 macrophage polarization, highlighting its differential immunomodulatory potency compared to BTX-A136. Furthermore, SVF combined with CO2 laser therapy has been shown to enhance scar pliability. Cai and Xiao et al. utilized a rabbit ear hypertrophic scar model and demonstrated that the combination of SVF with fractional CO2 laser therapy established a pro-regenerative microenvironment by attenuating inflammatory cell infiltration while promoting subcutaneous adipose regeneration via upregulation of adipogenic transcription factors C/EBPα and PPARγ (Fig. 7C) [132,137]. These findings collectively position SVF as an advanced therapeutic modality for scar remediation, integrating ECM preservation with phase-specific immune reprogramming to optimize wound healing [131,136].

In addition to direct application of SVM, the conditioned medium derived from SVM cultures (SVM-CM) has also been shown to promote wound healing. During preparation of SVM-CM, the SVM is precultured in growth medium to allow prolonged cell–ECM contact. This extended contact stimulates the secretion of elevated levels of cytokines and growth factors, which accumulate in the supernatant to form the conditioned medium. Consequently, SVM-CM contains significantly higher concentrations of key growth factors such as bFGF, VEGF, PDGF, and KGF compared to SVF-CM. These cytokines are crucial in regulating cellular migration, neovascularization, epithelial differentiation, and adnexal regeneration, all of which are essential for effective wound healing [138]. Deng et al. utilized SVM-CM to treat full-thickness skin wounds and found that this treatment accelerated wound healing through enhancing the proliferation and migration of keratinocytes and fibroblasts, upregulating the expression of growth factors, and promoting ECM synthesis [139]. In the subsequent study, a comparative study between SVM-CM and SVF-CM in diabetic wound models were conducted, where SVM-CM demonstrated enhanced secretion of growth factors mediated increased cellular proliferation and migration compared with SVF-CM, which in turn elevated type I collagen production and accelerated wound healing [140].

In vivo, intralesional SVF administration in a rat model of radiation dermatitis significantly expedited wound closure. Clinically, five patients with refractory radiation dermatitis received SVF in combination with HA, resulting in sustained epithelial regeneration, pain relief, favorable aesthetic outcomes, and no recurrence of lesions [141]. Similarly, Iddins et al. reported a case involving SVF treatment of a 0.5 cm papular radiation-induced wound. Following surgical debridement, targeted SVF injections were administered to the wound bed, peri-wound areas, and intra-thumb compartments. The clinical applications of SVF and SVM.

SVF has also shown efficacy in treating chronic wounds, Michael et al. investigated the clinical efficacy of non-expanded autologous SVF injections for treating chronic non-healing wounds in ten patients. All patients experienced effective pain relief, and angiographic analysis revealed neovascularization in five of the six patients evaluated [143]. In treating DFUs, a severe microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus, SVF drives vascular regeneration to expedite wound healing. In a cohort of 63 patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic DFUs, SVF-derived vascular progenitor cells were administered intralesionally. Patients exhibited significant reconstruction of the microvascular network alongside a marked slowing of DFU progression [144]. Notably, combining SVF with conventional clinical treatments can yields superior therapeutic efficacy compared to monotherapy. Wu et al. demonstrated that combining SVF with vacuum-assisted closure achieved an 89 % reduction in wound area in 20 patients through several mechanisms, which included ECM scaffolding for fibroblast migration and collagen deposition, SVF-derived paracrine factors driving angiogenesis via PI3K/AKT pathway activation, and CD34+ endothelial progenitor cell-mediated neovascular maturation [145].

Similarly, the application of SVM holds particular clinical relevance. In a prospective clinical study involving 20 patients with chronic wounds, it was reported that SVM-CM therapy led to elevated expression of multiple growth factors, promoting the proliferation and migration of human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs). Notably, all patients achieved complete wound closure, and no recurrence was observed during the follow-up period (Fig. 7D) [133]. Additionally, The combination of SVM with fractional CO2 laser therapy was applied clinically by the Xiao et al., where six patients with hyperplastic scarring were treated with SVM/CO2 laser therapy, showing improvements in scar appearance, pain, and itching [132].

Discussion

SVF has been extensively validated via preclinical and clinical studies, confirming its considerable therapeutic potential. As an autologous material sourced directly from patients, SVF inherently avoids immune rejection and minimizes rehabilitation burden. However, several inherent limitations persist. First, SVF from older donors often exhibits diminished proliferative, differentiation, and paracrine capacities, due to age-related shifts in cellular composition. Second, the mechanical properties of SVM are often compromised during isolation and processing, yielding scaffolds with suboptimal integrity for effective wound repair.

Aged SVF-derived ADSCs have impaired viability and functions

It has been reported that aged SVF-derived ADSCs have a significantly compromised in their functions in tissue engineering. For the proliferation phases, it has been reported that the 1-month-old ADSCs entered the exponential growth phase within a shorter period of time compared with 20-month-old ADSCs, implying a fast growth rate [147]. Similar phenomenon was also observed in other study, where the viability of ADSCs was significantly reduced in the elderly group as compared to that in the young group, leading to an increase in population doubling time [148]. This slower proliferation rate could significantly compromise the skin regeneration during the wound healing treatment. In addition, effective wound healing necessitates stem cells retaining robust differentiation capacity to generate diverse cell types required for tissue repair. Age detrimentally impacts not only proliferation but also cytokine secretion, where the cytokine secretions play an important role in wound healing, since it could influence both ADSC self-differentiation and paracrine functions. Researcher has found that elder ADSCs had significantly lower cytokine secretion compared with young ADSCs. For example, ADSCs from the old group tended to produce lower levels of angiogenic factors, including HGF and VEGF, which are growth factors that could modulate the ADSC differentiate in to ECs and impaired neovascularization. In addition, the alterations in receptor expression, such as BMP receptor 1A, was found to impair the osteogenic differentiation potential of ADSCs from the elderly group.

To address the above problems, researchers also developed several validated strategies to enhance ADSC proliferation, cytokine secretion, and differentiation capacity. Culturing ADSCs under hypoxic conditions (∼1–5 % O2) or treating them with HIF-1α stabilizers such as deferoxamine significantly improves cell proliferation, survival, and the secretion of angiogenic factors, including VEGF and bFGF [149]. Three-dimensional cell culture is an advanced in vitro technique in which cells are cultivated within a 3D environment, such as scaffold matrices or scaffold-free aggregates, allowing them to interact with neighboring cells and ECM in all spatial directions, unlike traditional two-dimensional monolayer cultures. 3D cell culture can significantly enhance ADSC differentiation, as well as improve proliferation and cytokine secretion. Specifically, 3D spheroid culture promotes autophagy, delays cellular senescence, and increases the secretion of key cytokines such as VEGF, IGF-1, and TGF-β [[150], [151], [152]]. Moreover, ADSCs encapsulated in 3D PEG-based hydrogels and cultured with VEGF exhibit robust CD31 expression and form endothelial tubule-like structures, outcomes not observed in conventional 2D cultures [151].

Prioritizing extraction from younger relatives for heterologous transplant is also advisable. ADSCs naturally possess intrinsic immunomodulatory properties, where they could inhibit T-cell proliferation, reduce B-cell and dendritic cell function, shift macrophages toward anti-inflammatory phenotypes, and promote regulatory T cell expansion, along with releasing immunosuppressive mediators like IL-10, TGF-β, IDO, and PGE2 [153]. These mechanisms collectively contribute to an immunosuppressive microenvironment favorable for allogeneic engraftment. This innate immunosuppressive capacity can be further enhanced via genetic or molecular modification. For instance, ADSCs engineered to express human cytomegalovirus US2 and US3 proteins exhibit markedly reduced surface MHC I expression through accelerated degradation and ER retention, effectively minimizing CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxicity without compromising functional properties [154]. Moreover, encapsulation of ADSCs within biocompatible biomaterials serves as a semi-permeable immunoisolation barrier. Such constructs permit diffusion of nutrients and paracrine factors while reducing direct interactions with host immune cells [155]. This approach not only enhances ADSC viability and differentiation but also physically shields grafts from host lymphocytes, thereby reducing immunogenicity in heterologous transplantation contexts.

Low mechanical properties of SVM compromise effective wound repair

Wound-repair biomaterials must exhibit sufficient mechanical robustness to withstand deformation under external forces. For instance, dressings covering joint wounds often dislodge during articulation, leading to repeated friction at the injury site and delayed re-epithelialization. Although SVM contains both therapeutically active SVF cells and ECM components that modestly augment mechanical integrity, adipose ECM predominantly comprises collagen types IV and VI, primarily serving to enclose lipid droplets and lacking significant tensile strength. In contrast, skin ECM is rich in collagen types I and III, which impart substantial tensile strength and elasticity, further reinforced by a dense elastin network that enhances resilience. Consequently, the mechanical properties of SVM are considerably inferior to those of native skin, rendering it prone to structural failure during patient movement. Such fractures create micro-niches conducive to bacterial colonization and compromise the continuity of the substrate, thereby disrupting fibroblast infiltration, alignment, and orchestrated wound healing. As a result, dressings require frequent replacement, imposing mechanical stress on nascent epithelium and perpetuating non-healing wounds. Beyond providing structural support, biomaterial mechanics significantly influence cellular behavior during wound repair. For example, hydrogels with higher stiffness (∼600–850 kPa) have been shown to concurrently promote pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory macrophage activation, whereas softer matrices reduce macrophage activation [156]. These functional variations correlate with cellular spreading capability on material surfaces. Specifically, relative to softer substrates, stiffer substrates enhance both cell spreading and functionality [157]. Accordingly, the inherent mechanical limitations of SVM may interfere with essential cellular processes, such as spreading and immunomodulation, thereby undermining coordinated tissue regeneration.

To enhance the mechanical strength of SVM, structural support can be achieved by incorporating biomaterials with superior mechanical properties. Alternatively, mechanical reinforcement can be accomplished through biomanufacturing technologies such as 3D bioprinting, which allows for precise placement of SVM in specific positions, where they can be further protected by other biomaterials. GelMA is a promising candidate for mixing with stiffer biomaterials to improve the mechanical properties of SVM. GelMA has been widely used as a bio-scaffold in wound healing due to its ECM-mimetic composition, which offers adequate mechanical integrity. This integrity can be modulated by controlling the degree of substitution and UV exposure time, enabling significant tunability [158,159]. The encapsulation of ADSCs and HUVECs within GelMA hydrogels enhances cellular extension and migration, facilitated by UV-induced crosslinking that creates an optimal mechanical microenvironment for cell migration and proliferation. Upon stimulation, the encapsulated cells undergo adipogenic differentiation and form vascular structures [160]. Chitosan also employed in the fabrication of hydrogel scaffolds. It offers not only tunable mechanical robustness but also intrinsic properties that enhance therapeutic efficacy in wound management. Chitosan, another material employed in hydrogel scaffolds, offers tunable mechanical strength and intrinsic properties that enhance its therapeutic efficacy in wound management. Under acidic conditions, chitosan retains a positive surface charge, which interacts electrostatically with the anionic bacterial surfaces, disrupting membrane permeability and exerting antibacterial effects. Concurrently, this positive charge promotes protein adsorption, enhancing cellular adhesion [161,162]. Incorporating these materials into hydrogels with SVM provides structural reinforcement, improving tensile strength and enabling better adaptation to the dynamic wound microenvironment, thereby optimizing the functional performance of SVM.

3D bioprinting offers an additional strategy to address the mechanical limitations of SVM in wound healing applications. This technology enables the fabrication of biomimetic structures that replicate both the architectural configurations and physiological functions of native tissues. For example, researchers have used 3D bioprinting to develop multi-layered human skin equivalents with different bioinks at each layer, mimicking the composition of epidermal and dermal skin layers [163]. Given that SVM inherently contains ECM components and incorporates multiple functional cell types, it is a suitable bioink material. A trilaminar architecture comprising stiffer biomaterials, SVM, and additional stiffer layers can be developed to create a protective niche for SVM during the wound healing process. To further enhance biomimetic fidelity, HDFs and human keratinocytes should be incorporated into the dermal and epidermal bioinks, respectively, to better replicate the native skin composition.

Conclusion

In this review, we summarized the therapeutic potential of SVF and SVM in wound healing. SVF promotes wound repair through its proangiogenic, immunomodulatory, and ECM remodeling effects. When combined with PRP or biomaterials, its efficacy is further enhanced by the synergistic regulation of inflammation and angiogenesis. SVM, with its preserved ECM, and SVM-CM, rich in growth factors, exhibit superior wound healing capabilities compared to conventional SVF. Overall, SVF presents a versatile, autologous, and multifunctional strategy to address unmet needs in wound healing, and the continued development of these technologies provides valuable insights for future clinical applications and research.
