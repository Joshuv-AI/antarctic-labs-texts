Neonatal glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase activity by gestational age and sex: evidence-based cut-offs for newborn screening in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Maryam Alkaabi, Abdul Rafiq Khan, Mohammed Al Balwi, Talal Al Harbi, Ibrahim Ali, Saif Alsaif, Kamal Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo characterise quantitative glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) activity in term and preterm neonates, compare activity by gestational age (GA) and sex and derive neonatal screening cut-offs using percentile and WHO methods.DesignRetrospective cross-sectional analysis of cord blood screening programme.SettingKing Abdulaziz Medical City, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, January 2016 to May 2022.ParticipantsAll live births with cord blood G6PD and GA. Of 59 465 births, 58 139 (97.8%) were screened. For reference range derivation only, results below the adult threshold (<5.7 U/g haemoglobin (Hb); n=1163) were excluded, yielding 56 976.Main outcome measuresDistribution by GA and sex; prevalence under three definitions: adult cut-off (≤5.7 U/g Hb), neonatal percentile cut-off (2.5th percentile) and WHO bands (<30% deficient, 30–70% intermediate, >70% normal). Secondary measures were stratum medians and the male share among detected cases.ResultsMean activity was 15.33±2.67 U/g Hb, higher in preterm than term. Sex differences were absent in preterm strata but present at term (males lower than females; p=0.002). The neonatal 2.5th percentile cut-off was 9.9 U/g Hb. Using the adult cut-off, 2.00% (1163/58 139) were deficient, 80.9% male. Using the neonatal cut-off, prevalence rose to 4.97% (2888/58 139), adding 1725 infants (+583 males, +1142 females) and reducing the male share to 52.8%. WHO bands identified a small <30% group and a female intermediate (30–70%) group across strata. Medians were 15.0 (≥37 weeks), 16.0 (33–36), 17.0 (29–32) and 17.6 (≤28) U/g Hb.ConclusionsG6PD activity in neonates shows clear GA and sex effects. Adult cut-offs underestimate deficiency, especially in females. Neonatal thresholds (2.5th percentile plus GA-specific WHO bands) improve detection. Adoption within newborn screening, with reporting of a female intermediate band, should strengthen follow-up; external validation and linkage to bilirubin outcomes are needed.Keywords: Neonatology, Biochemistry, Health Policy, Statistics, Genetics

Introduction

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) is the key enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway that generates reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH). In red cells, NADPH maintains glutathione in its reduced state and thereby limits oxidative injury.1,3 G6PD deficiency is X-linked and therefore more common in males. Females may be clinically affected if homozygous for a pathogenic variant or, when heterozygous, because of lyonisation. Heterozygous females show a wide spectrum of enzyme activity and clinical expression, ranging from severe (comparable to hemizygous males) to completely asymptomatic.4 5

Globally, about 500 million people are affected, with the highest frequencies in regions with current or historical malaria transmission, notably parts of Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, the Mediterranean and the Middle East.6 In Saudi Arabia, regional prevalence ranges from 2.0% in Riyadh to over 30% in Eastern Province cities like Qatif.7,9

In G6PD-deficient infants, haemolysis is typically precipitated by oxidative exposures such as fava beans, intercurrent infections and certain medications, notably the 8-aminoquinoline antimalarials primaquine and tafenoquine.10 11 G6PD deficiency is also an established risk factor for severe neonatal hyperbilirubinaemia and kernicterus, particularly in high-prevalence populations.12 A recent review of kernicterus cases in the USA suggested that approximately 21% of affected infants had G6PD deficiency, highlighting the critical importance of early identification.13

Quantitative studies consistently show that neonatal G6PD activity exceeds adult values. Mesner et al reported mean activities of 12.0 U/g haemoglobin (Hb) in term infants and 14.2 U/g Hb in preterm infants—both above typical adult levels.14 Likewise, in a study from Malaysia, term neonates (n=94) had a mean G6PD activity of 12.43±2.28 U/g Hb, compared with 9.21±2.60 U/g Hb in adults (n=295); the corresponding reference intervals were 10.15–14.71 U/g Hb for neonates and 6.61–11.81 U/g Hb for adults.15 These findings highlight that adult-based cut-offs are unsuitable for neonatal screening.

Adult-derived cut-offs are not calibrated to neonatal physiology and can under-detect partial deficiency, particularly in heterozygous females. Using a universal cord blood screening data set from a single tertiary centre (58 139 neonates with gestational age (GA) recorded), we characterised quantitative G6PD activity across GA strata and by sex; derived a neonatal screening cut-off from the cohort 2.5th percentile; and compared case classification and sex distribution under three definitions: the adult laboratory cut-off (≤5.7 U/g Hb), a neonatal percentile cut-off (2.5th percentile, 9.9 U/g Hb) and WHO percentage of normal bands based on the adjusted male median (AMM) (<30% deficient, 30–70% intermediate, >70% normal). We also report GA and sex-specific medians with corresponding thresholds to support local interpretation and implementation of neonatal G6PD screening.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a single-centre, retrospective cross-sectional newborn screening study conducted at King Abdulaziz Medical City (KAMC), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. KAMC is a tertiary referral centre under the Ministry of National Guard Health Affairs with 9000 deliveries/year. The study was undertaken jointly by the Neonatology Division, the institutional newborn screening programme, and Clinical Laboratory Services, and covered births from 1 January 2016 to 31 May 2022.

Study population

The study included all live-born neonates delivered at KAMC during the specified period. Cord blood samples were routinely collected at birth as part of the institution’s universal newborn screening protocol. Neonates were eligible for inclusion if their cord blood samples were successfully analysed for G6PD enzyme activity and if complete GA data were available

G6PD assay and sample handling

Cord blood was collected with standard technique into K₂EDTA (1.8 mg/mL) tubes (Vacutainer, BD, Plymouth, UK; Microtainer, Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA). Specimens were accessioned in the laboratory information system, stored at 2–8°C and batched for analysis within 4–6 hours of collection. Hb was measured the same day on Cell-Dyn Sapphire analysers (Abbott Diagnostics, Abbott Park, Illinois, USA) and used to report enzyme activity in U/g Hb. For the enzyme assay, 100 µL of well-mixed whole blood was added to 400 µL of the manufacturer’s lysing reagent, mixed and left to stand for 5 min; the haemolysate was then transferred to a sample cup and analysed immediately (within 1 hour) on the Udilipse Random Access Analyzer using a quantitative G6PD kit validated for neonatal and adult samples (United Diagnostics Industry, Dammam 31413, Saudi Arabia). The method uses ultraviolet spectrophotometry at 340 nm based on the reaction glucose-6-phosphate+NADP → 6-phosphogluconate+NADPH + H+, with activity proportional to the rate of NADPH formation.15 Analyser results were transmitted automatically to the laboratory information system, which applied the patient’s Hb value to express activity in U/g Hb.

Definitions and grouping

GA was determined using the best obstetric estimate based on early ultrasound and maternal history. Neonates were stratified into four GA groups: ≤28 weeks (extremely preterm), 29–32 weeks (very preterm), 33–36 weeks (moderate-to-late preterm) and ≥37 weeks (term). G6PD enzyme activity was analysed across these subgroups.

Exclusion criteria

For derivation of neonatal reference ranges and percentage of normal thresholds, we created a reference subset of screened neonates. Infants were excluded from this subset if (1) the cord blood G6PD assay was missing or failed quality control, (2) GA was not recorded or (3) G6PD activity was <5.7 U/g Hb (adult laboratory threshold), to avoid downward bias from biochemically deficient samples. These exclusions applied only to the reference range derivation. All summaries of activity distributions and all prevalence estimates under the adult cut-off, the neonatal percentile cut-off and WHO percentage of normal definitions used the full screened cohort, including infants below 5.7 U/g Hb.

Outcome measures and cut-off derivation

The primary outcome was the distribution of quantitative G6PD activity by GA and sex. Prevalence was reported under three pre-specified definitions: (1) the adult laboratory cut-off≤5.7 U/g Hb; (2) a neonatal lower reference limit (LRL) defined as the 2.5th percentile of the neonatal activity distribution; and (3) WHO bands derived from the AMM: <30% (deficient), 30–70% (intermediate) and >70% (normal).

The 2.5th percentile was selected in line with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) and International Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine (IFCC) reference-interval guidance for continuous biochemical traits to provide a sensitive neonatal screening threshold while avoiding over-classification that can arise with a 5th percentile. To derive the neonatal LRL, we calculated the percentile from the screened cohort after temporarily excluding values <5.7 U/g Hb to prevent downward bias from clearly abnormal left-tail results. Once the LRL was set, all prevalence estimates (adult cut-off, neonatal LRL and WHO bands) were computed on the full screened cohort with no exclusions.

For the WHO approach, the AMM was estimated from males after excluding clearly low male results per standard practice; percentage-of-AMM thresholds were then applied to the full cohort to classify activity. We also provide GA and sex-specific medians with corresponding WHO-based deficient and intermediate thresholds to aid interpretation.

Operationally, the neonatal 2.5th percentile is used as a screening reference limit to flag infants for closer jaundice follow-up; clinical classification should follow WHO AMM bands (<30%, 30–70%, >70%).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics, V.26 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). Quantitative variables (including G6PD activity) are presented as mean±SD and categorical variables as number (%). Within each GA stratum, independent samples t tests compared males versus females. Across GA categories within each sex, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) tested differences in mean activity; when significant, Bonferroni adjusted post hoc comparisons were performed. Categorical comparisons used χ² or Fisher exact tests, as appropriate.

Diagnostic classification was applied under three definitions: (1) the adult laboratory cut-off≤5.7 U/g Hb; (2) a neonatal percentile cut-off<9.9 U/g Hb (2.5th percentile derived from a non-deficient reference subset); and (3) WHO percentage of normal bands derived from the AMM after excluding clearly low male results, then applying %AMM thresholds to the full cohort (<30% deficient, 30–70% intermediate, >70% normal). For each definition, we reported overall prevalence with exact (binomial) 95% CIs and summarised the male–female distribution. All tests were two-sided with p<0.05 considered statistically significant. All prevalence and distribution analyses used the full screened cohort; the temporary exclusions were applied only for derivation of the neonatal 2.5th percentile.

Results

During January 2016 to May 2022, there were 59 465 live births; 58 139 neonates (97.8%) had a valid cord blood G6PD result with GA recorded. Records without a usable assay or with missing GA (n=1326) were excluded. Using the adult laboratory cut-off (<5.7 U/g Hb), 1163 screened infants (2.00%) were classified as G6PD deficient (80.9% male). For reference-range derivation only, these adult cut-off-deficient infants were excluded, yielding a subset of 56 976.

Discussion

We conducted a large hospital-based study in Riyadh to define neonatal G6PD screening thresholds and describe enzyme activity by GA and sex. We compared three approaches: the routine adult cut-off (<5.7 U/g Hb), a neonatal percentile cut-off (2.5th percentile, 9.9 U/g Hb) and WHO percentage-of-normal bands derived from GA-specific medians. Neonatal activity was higher in preterm than term infants and declined with advancing gestation; a sex difference was evident only at term, where males had lower mean activity than females. Threshold choice changed case detection: the adult cut-off classified 2.00% of screened neonates as deficient (80.9% male), the neonatal percentile cut-off increased this to 4.97% and yielded a near-balanced sex split (52.8% male), and the WHO<30% definition produced 1.86% with a strong male predominance (84.8%). These results support using a neonatal-specific threshold for screening, with explicit reporting of the WHO intermediate (30–70%) band in females to guide follow-up.

Our finding that neonatal G6PD activity is higher than adult values, with a gradual decline toward term, agrees with prior reports that attribute higher newborn activity to a larger proportion of young erythrocytes and reticulocytes with greater G6PD content.14 16 The sex difference appearing only at term is consistent with X-linked inheritance: hemizygous males more often show low activity, whereas heterozygous females display intermediate levels due to lyonisation. Notably, applying neonatal-specific cut-offs increased identification of partially deficient females, a group at documented risk for significant hyperbilirubinaemia even in the absence of overt haemolysis,17,20 supporting the use of infant-appropriate thresholds in screening programmes.

Use of adult cut-offs in newborn screening risks underdiagnosis. In our cohort, a neonatal 2.5th percentile cut-off of 9.9 U/g Hb identified 4.97% as deficient versus 2.00% with the adult cut-off, capturing many additional females; the WHO<30% definition yielded 1.86%. WHO percentage-of-normal criteria aid cross-study comparability and define an intermediate band that merits closer monitoring, especially for females, but they can underestimate deficiency in neonates compared with a neonatal percentile threshold.1 11 The neonatal distribution was right-skewed, with the adult 5.7 U/g Hb threshold below the main neonatal range. Activity was highest at earlier GAs and declined toward term, so a single adult threshold is prone to miss partial deficiency, particularly in females. Compared with prior neonatal screening studies that were often small and used coarse GA groupings, our analysis uses a large cohort with fine stratification (≤28, 29–32, 33–36, ≥37 weeks). It directly contrasts three threshold strategies and quantifies their effects on prevalence and sex balance. Sex differences were confined to term, and neonatal-specific criteria improved detection of partially deficient females. Earlier small studies typically applied a single adult cut-off. Together, a neonatal percentile cut-off and GA-specific WHO percentage-of-median thresholds provide a practical framework for screening and follow-up.

Across newborn programmes, the qualitative fluorescent spot test (FST) is still widely used but it under-detects partial deficiency, especially in heterozygous females. Head-to-head studies show that quantitative assays and handheld biosensors detect intermediate activity more reliably than FST.21,24 Large population programmes in China combined biochemical screening with genotyping and derived female-attuned cut-offs, showing how the threshold chosen shifts both prevalence and sex distribution.25 26 A recent US universal screening implementation recalibrated neonatal reference ranges to <4.9 U/g Hb (deficient) and <10.0 U/g Hb (intermediate), improving case finding and counselling.27 Consistent with these data, WHO guidance emphasises percentage-of-normal bands (eg, 30% and 70–80%) because they materially alter case ascertainment and comparability across settings. Preanalytical factors also matter: cord-blood versus peripheral-blood sampling has been evaluated locally in Saudi Arabia and supports cord blood for routine screening, while recent neonatal work suggests reticulocytosis has minimal impact on deficiency detection when modern quantitative methods are used.28 29

Within Saudi Arabia, our results are broadly consistent with the term cord-blood study by Al-Abdi and colleagues, but extend it in scope and application. Both studies used cord-blood samples. Al-Abdi’s analysis focused on healthy term newborns only (395 infants) and derived a single lower reference boundary using international laboratory-reference procedures recommended by the IFCC and the CLSI, without stratifying by GA and with limited ability to examine sex differences.7 Earlier Saudi reports—eg, Muzaffer from Yanbu, found ∼2% prevalence with marked male predominance using cord blood, but lacked prematurity stratification and evaluation of alternative neonatal thresholds.30 At our centre, Alsaif et al analysed 8139 neonates: in a paired subgroup, peripheral values were slightly higher than cord yet classification was similar; using peripheral as reference, cord screening sensitivity was 98.6% and negative predictive value 99.5%, supporting cord blood for routine screening.28

From a public health perspective, neonatal-specific cut-offs improve early detection, especially in females. In our cohort, moving from the adult cut-off to the neonatal 2.5th percentile threshold increased detected deficiency from 2.00% to 4.97% and reduced the male share from 80.9% to 52.8%, supporting closer follow-up of infants who would otherwise be missed. In settings with modest prevalence, these data support universal screening and routine reporting of a female ‘intermediate’ band (30–70% of the GA-specific median) to guide surveillance and parental counselling. The neonatal percentile should be used as a screening flag, not as a stand-alone basis for lifetime diagnosis or broad drug restrictions; WHO bands (and genotype where available) are more appropriate for clinical labelling and medication decisions. Our comparisons illustrate detection trade-offs between screening and classification frameworks. The GA stratified activity values are provided as reference data for laboratories and epidemiology and are not intended to change existing jaundice treatment protocols for preterm infants.

Our study has several strengths. These include the large, population-scale sample enabling precise, GA-specific and sex-specific reference values; consistent laboratory methodology with quality control across the study period; and a head-to-head comparison of three diagnostic criteria, allowing a direct assessment of how cut-off choice affects both prevalence and sex distribution. The provision of preterm-specific normative data at this scale is uncommon and adds practical value for neonatal programmes. Nevertheless, some limitations need to be acknowledged. The retrospective design and lack of routine measurements that may influence enzyme activity (eg, reticulocyte count or recent haemolysis) are potential sources of bias. We did not perform molecular genotyping and therefore could not examine variant-specific effects, nor did we link enzyme activity to clinical outcomes (eg, peak bilirubin or kernicterus), which would better define the clinical utility of the proposed thresholds. Additionally, we did not perform scheduled retesting after erythrocyte turnover; therefore, borderline or intermediate screening results could not be confirmed or reclassified. This is important because measured G6PD activity can change as red cells mature, particularly in heterozygous females, and some infants may move above or below the cut-off on repeat testing. Finally, this is a single-centre study; external validation in diverse populations with different variant spectra is warranted.

Conclusion

In this large hospital-based cohort, neonatal G6PD activity varied by GA and sex, and adult cut-offs underestimated deficiency, especially in females. A neonatal calibration that combines a cohort-derived 2.5th percentile cut-off (9.9 U/g Hb) with GA-specific WHO percentage-of-normal bands was feasible and compatible with routine screening. In our setting, it increased the estimated prevalence from 2.00% to 4.97% and reduced male predominance by capturing partially deficient females. These neonatal-specific thresholds can be implemented in newborn screening to support universal testing, explicit reporting of the female intermediate band, clearer follow-up guidance and earlier intervention. External validation across other populations and linkage of screening categories to bilirubin outcomes remain priorities.
