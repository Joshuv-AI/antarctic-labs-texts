Delayed Diagnosis to Definitive Treatment: Diagnostic Delay and Outcomes After Endoscopic-Assisted Fasciotomy for Chronic Exertional Compartment Syndrome
Authors: Cesar A Ramirez, Seif Al Masry, Gabrielle M Meli, Jose Zermeño, Faris Jafri, Edward Haupt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Chronic exertional compartment syndrome (CECS) is an often-overlooked cause of exertional lower leg pain that is frequently misdiagnosed. Overlapping symptoms and inconsistent diagnostic approaches may delay diagnosis and prolong morbidity. The purpose of this study was to characterize the diagnostic journey of patients ultimately treated surgically for CECS and to report postoperative outcomes.Methods:A retrospective chart review was performed following IRB approval. Patients diagnosed with CECS and treated operatively by the senior author between 2020 and 2025 were identified using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT), International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD-9 and ICD-10) codes. Demographics, symptom duration, prior diagnostic workup, operative details, and postoperative outcomes were collected. Median follow-up was 42 weeks (range, 15-184 weeks). Descriptive statistics were reported.Results:Thirteen patients met inclusion criteria (mean age, 27.3 years; mean BMI, 28.3). Mean symptom duration prior to definitive diagnosis was 51.5 months, and bilateral symptoms were present in 12 patients. Most patients underwent extensive diagnostic testing and multiple unsuccessful conservative treatment strategies before referral. All patients underwent endoscopic-assisted anterolateral compartment release, with open posterior compartment fasciotomy performed when clinically indicated. Nine patients underwent bilateral procedures. At final follow-up, all patients reported symptomatic improvement and return to baseline activity. Four patients required additional evaluation for persistent posterior compartment symptoms, and 2 ultimately underwent further posterior compartment release. There were no intraoperative complications.Conclusion:CECS remains a frequently delayed diagnosis because of nonspecific symptoms, substantial overlap with other causes of exertional lower-leg pain, and limitations of currently available diagnostic tools. Although the effect of diagnostic delay on postoperative outcomes could not be directly assessed, prolonged delays were associated with substantial diagnostic burden and management complexity. Following fasciotomy, all patients reported symptomatic improvement and return to baseline activity. Greater awareness of CECS and a structured diagnostic approach may help facilitate earlier recognition and treatment.Level of Evidence:Level IV, retrospective case series.Keywords: chronic exertional compartment syndrome, fasciotomy, endoscopic release, exertional leg pain, compartment syndrome, lower leg pain

Introduction

Lower leg pain is a frequent and frustrating occurrence among athletes at all levels, military personnel, and active individuals. The differential diagnosis is broad and includes medial tibial stress syndrome (MTSS), stress fractures, popliteal artery entrapment syndrome, and chronic exertional compartment syndrome (CECS) to name a few. Although MTSS and stress fractures are generally well-recognized causes of exertional lower-leg pain, CECS remains frequently underrecognized despite accounting for more than 30% of exercise-induced leg pain presentations. 1 Furthermore, De Bruijn et al 2 found that among 1411 patients who underwent intracompartmental pressure testing for exertional leg pain, nearly half were diagnosed with CECS, most often between the ages of 20 and 25 years.

CECS most commonly affects the anterior compartment (approximately 45%) and is bilateral in up to 95% of cases.3,4 Although its cause can be multifactorial because of compartmental constraints, fascial thickening, abnormal muscular hypertrophy, or diminished microvascular supply, its clinical presentation overlaps with other conditions like MTSS or vascular entrapment.5,6 These overlaps in symptoms and presentation often delay diagnosis, contributing to substantial patient morbidity and health care inefficiency. Many patients endure prolonged periods of pain and limited function, often referred from one provider to another without definitive relief or a clear management plan.

Studies highlight that CECS is more common among those who are active in sports and high intensity training however, it may also be related to activities of daily living. Edmundsson et al reported that only 33% of CECS patients were active athletes, highlighting that it is not limited to those who are active and athletes. 7 Furthermore, large military cohort analyses have shown a higher incidence in women, younger individuals, and junior-ranked service members, with risk varying by branch and activity type.2,8,9 The heterogeneity of the affected patient population may further contribute to delayed recognition and diagnostic uncertainty.

The clinical challenge of CECS lies in its subjective and activity dependent symptomatology. Diagnosis often relies on intracompartmental pressure testing, an invasive and frequently uncomfortable procedure with recognized limitations. Furthermore, normal pressure measurements do not completely exclude the diagnosis in patients with a characteristic clinical presentation. 10 Management remains challenging, as conservative therapies often provide limited durable benefit and surgical fasciotomy is associated with variable outcomes. 11 Consequently, both patients and clinicians may experience considerable uncertainty throughout the diagnostic and treatment process. The primary challenge in CECS is often not treatment, but recognition of the condition itself.

Given these challenges, the purpose of this study was to characterize the diagnostic journey of patients who ultimately underwent surgical treatment for CECS, including symptom duration, prior diagnostic workup, referral patterns, and clinical presentation. We additionally report surgical outcomes following endoscopic-assisted fasciotomy to illustrate the clinical impact of achieving a definitive diagnosis and appropriate treatment.

Methods

Study Design

Following IRB approval, a retrospective chart review from a single institution was performed. Using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) and International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD-9 and ICD-10) codes, patients diagnosed with CECS and treated operatively using scope assisted releases by the senior author between 2020 to 2025 were queried. A total of 117 patients were identified; 39 patients were screened, of which 13 met our inclusion criteria. The initial 117 patients were identified through a database search using broad search terms related to chronic compartment syndrome. Records were subsequently reviewed to identify patients with a confirmed diagnosis of lower-extremity CECS. Patients were excluded if they had upper-extremity compartment syndrome, traumatic or acute compartment syndrome, CECS listed only as a differential diagnosis without definitive diagnosis, nonoperative management, or lack of follow-up at the time of this study. Following application of these criteria, 13 patients met inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.

Patients were evaluated using a stepwise diagnostic approach consisting of clinical history, physical examination, and exclusion of alternative causes of exertional lower-leg pain through imaging and ancillary testing as clinically indicated. Clinical features raising suspicion for CECS included reproducible exertional pain, tightness, paresthesias, weakness, or transient foot drop that improved with rest. Compartment pressure testing was obtained when clinically indicated and available. Pressure measurements were interpreted according to the Pedowitz criteria and served as an adjunct to, rather than a replacement for, clinical judgment. 12 Final diagnosis was established using the overall clinical presentation, exclusion of alternative diagnoses, and compartment pressure measurements when available.

Data Collection

Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), laterality, compartments released, and duration of symptoms. Preoperative workup, including imaging results, symptom characteristics, and prior treatments or referrals, were recorded. Postoperative follow-up was recorded for all patients. Outcomes were assessed at the most recent clinical follow-up. Median follow-up duration was calculated and reported with the range. Operative details included compartments released (anterolateral vs 4-compartment fasciotomy) and revision status. The decision to perform posterior compartment release was individualized and based on the overall clinical presentation rather than compartment pressure measurements alone. Patients reporting symptoms localizing to the superficial or deep posterior compartments, including exertional calf pain, tightness, cramping, neurologic symptoms, or persistent symptoms despite prior compartment release, were considered for posterior compartment decompression. Given the known variability in symptom presentation and limitations of pressure testing, operative planning incorporated clinical history, physical examination, pressure measurements when available, and surgeon judgment. Intracompartmental pressure measurements were collected for all available cases, including pre-exercise, 1-minute post-exercise, and 5-minute post-exercise values for each compartment (anterior, lateral, superficial posterior, and deep posterior). Paired preoperative and postoperative PROMIS physical function (PF) and pain interference (PI) scores were recorded when available. Because of the descriptive nature of this retrospective case series, no primary or secondary outcome measures were prespecified. All analyses were exploratory and descriptive, with the goal of characterizing patient presentation, diagnostic evaluation, management, and postoperative outcomes. Descriptive statistics were reported as means ± SD for continuous variables and counts (n, %) for categorical variables. Missing data were handled using available-case analysis. Patients with incomplete compartment pressure measurements remained eligible for inclusion in the study if a diagnosis of CECS had been established clinically and they met all other inclusion criteria. Pressure data were summarized only for patients with available measurements.

Results

Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

Table 1 highlights our 13-patient cohort that met our inclusion criteria, with a mean age of 27.3 years and mean BMI of 28.3. There were 7 females and 6 males with varying activity levels and occupations. Symptoms had been present for an average of 51.5 months. Most patients were physically active, including high school and collegiate athletes, former competitive athletes, military service members, and individuals with physically demanding occupations. All patients but 1 had a history of associated orthopaedic or overuse comorbidities. A large proportion had prior lower extremity injuries, surgical history, or stress-related conditions. Two patients reported a prior diagnosis of CECS, with 1 having undergone unsuccessful compartment releases at an outside institution. All patients reported exertional lower leg symptoms, most described as tightness, pressure, cramping, or pain, frequently accompanied by neurologic complaints. Paresthesias, numbness, or tingling were present in most cases, and intermittent foot drop or subjective weakness was reported by several patients. Symptoms were bilateral in 12 of 13 patients, often asymmetric, with 1 patient reporting isolated unilateral symptoms. All patients reported worsen symptoms with activity that improved with rest, consistent with CECS.

Plain radiographs were obtained in most cases and were largely unremarkable. MRI findings varied, ranging from normal studies to nonspecific muscle edema or signal abnormalities. A small number of patients demonstrated benign soft tissue lesions or healing stress fractures. Those with previous EMG/NCS revealed no abnormalities except in 1 patient with chronic mild to moderate axonal polyneuropathy. Vascular studies including ABIs, doppler, MRA, and CTA consistently ruled out popliteal artery entrapment or arterial abnormalities. All patients had undergone compartment pressure testing, but only 10 were documented. Procedures and Patient-Reported Outcomes

Median postoperative follow-up was 42 weeks (range: 15-184 weeks). All patients had a minimum of 15 weeks of follow-up at the time outcome data were collected. Table 3 summarizes the surgical procedures performed and outcomes at the most recent follow-up for each patient. All patients underwent endoscopic-assisted anterolateral compartment release, with open posterior compartment fasciotomy performed as indicated, all performed by the senior author. Nine patients underwent bilateral fasciotomies, 4 involving anterolateral compartment releases and 5 involving all 4 compartment releases. Of these, 5 patients had both procedures completed during a single operating session. Among unilateral cases, 1 patient underwent right-sided anterolateral releases, 3 had left-sided procedures. All patients underwent superficial peroneal nerve neurolysis. One patient underwent concomitant common peroneal nerve neurolysis because preoperative MRI and EMG demonstrated abnormalities involving the common peroneal nerve and its distal branches, consistent with associated peroneal nerve pathology. Patients were evaluated at 2 weeks postoperatively, and most reported marked improvement by 6 weeks. Four patients reported persistent symptoms localized to their superficial and deep compartments, only 2 had undergone additional compartments released with the other 2 pending surgery or evaluation. At last follow-up, all patients reported improvement or resolution of their presenting symptoms and return to baseline activity level. Despite initial symptomatic improvement, 1 patient was subsequently diagnosed with MTSS and another with popliteal artery entrapment syndrome, highlighting the diagnostic complexity of exertional lower-leg pain. One patient who had undergone previous fasciotomy at an outside institution was diagnosed with SPN neuropraxia following revision.

Paired preoperative and postoperative PROMIS data were available for 7 of 13 patients (53.8%). PROMIS physical function improved from 43.9 ± 4.0 preoperatively to 47.0 ± 4.0 postoperatively (Δ = +3.1, 95% CI −0.4 to +6.7; P = .074), whereas PROMIS pain interference decreased from 59.9 ± 5.3 to 53.6 ± 6.9 (Δ = −6.3, 95% CI −14.1 to +1.5; P = .097).

Discussion

In this study, we sought to characterize the diagnostic journey of patients ultimately treated surgically for CECS. Our cohort experienced a mean symptom duration of 51.5 months prior to definitive diagnosis, underwent extensive diagnostic testing, and was frequently evaluated by multiple providers before referral for definitive management. These findings highlight that the principal challenge in CECS may be recognition of the condition rather than treatment itself. Although surgical fasciotomy was associated with symptomatic improvement and return to activity, the most striking finding of this study was the substantial delay in diagnosis despite characteristic clinical presentations.

To our knowledge, few studies have focused specifically on the diagnostic pathway preceding operative treatment for CECS. Although prior investigations have largely emphasized surgical techniques, pressure measurements, and postoperative outcomes, our findings highlight the substantial burden imposed by delayed recognition. Patients in our cohort experienced symptoms for more than 4 years before receiving a diagnosis. Many patients underwent evaluation by multiple specialists and extensive diagnostic testing before referral for definitive management, illustrating the fragmented and often cyclical nature of the diagnostic pathway experienced by patients with CECS. These prolonged diagnostic pathways may have contributed to persistent pain, activity limitations, increased health care utilization, and delayed access to definitive treatment. Symptoms such as exertional pain, tightness, cramping, paresthesias, and activity limitation overlap considerably with MTSS, stress fractures, popliteal artery entrapment syndrome, and other conditions that may coexist with CECS, contributing to diagnostic uncertainty.3,13-16 Table 4 illustrates this overlap and emphasizes the importance of a structured diagnostic approach. Four patients later required additional evaluation of their posterior compartments and 2 underwent further posterior compartment release, suggesting that delayed diagnosis may contribute to increased management complexity and the need for secondary procedures. Because of the small sample size and retrospective design, we were unable to directly determine whether longer diagnostic delays resulted in worse postoperative outcomes. However, prolonged delays likely contributed to extended symptom burden, activity limitations, repeated diagnostic evaluations, and delayed access to definitive treatment. This likely reflects the inherent difficulty of compartment localization in CECS, as symptoms frequently overlap between compartments and dominant anterior or lateral symptoms may initially obscure concurrent posterior compartment involvement.

Intracompartmental pressure testing according to the Pedowitz criteria remains the most commonly used diagnostic adjunct for CECS; however, its role in clinical decision making should be interpreted in the context of the overall clinical presentation. 12 Pressure measurements may be normal despite characteristic symptoms, and testing protocols remain heterogeneous.17-19 In our series, several patients were unable to complete the full testing protocol, and complete measurements were unavailable in some cases. Accordingly, pressure measurements were used to support the diagnosis when available, but operative decision making also incorporated symptom reproducibility, physical examination findings, exclusion of alternative diagnoses, and response to conservative management. These findings reinforce that compartment pressure testing should be viewed as an adjunct to, rather than a replacement for, clinical judgment.

The extensive diagnostic workup observed in our cohort underscores the need for improved diagnostic tools. Table 5 summarizes several emerging noninvasive modalities that may assist in the diagnosis of CECS, including MRI,20,21 near-infrared spectroscopy,13,20,22 electromyography,23-25 ultrasonography-based techniques, and predictive clinical models. 26 These approaches aim to reduce reliance on invasive compartment pressure testing and may be particularly useful in patients with equivocal pressure measurements or atypical clinical presentations. 17 However, diagnostic performance remains variable across studies, and no single modality has consistently demonstrated sufficient accuracy to replace compartment pressure testing.18,27 Accordingly, these tools are best viewed as complementary adjuncts that may aid clinical decision making when interpreted alongside the patient’s history, physical examination findings, and overall clinical presentation.

Determining the extent of compartment release remains one of the more challenging aspects of CECS management because symptoms frequently overlap between compartments and may coexist with other causes of exertional lower-leg pain. This challenge is amplified in patients with prolonged diagnostic histories, where symptom evolution and overlap between compartments may further obscure localization. The hybrid surgical strategy employed in this series reflects an attempt to balance the benefits of minimally invasive decompression with the reliability of direct visualization in anatomically complex compartments. Specifically, the anterior and lateral compartments are readily accessible through minimally invasive techniques, which may reduce soft tissue dissection, wound morbidity, and recovery time while achieving favorable clinical outcomes.28,29 In contrast, the posterior compartments contain more complex anatomy and critical neurovascular structures, making complete visualization and decompression more challenging through an endoscopic approach.30,31 Therefore, an open posterior release was used to facilitate safe and thorough decompression. Despite this strategy, 4 patients required additional evaluation for persistent posterior compartment symptoms, and 2 ultimately underwent further posterior compartment release, underscoring both the diagnostic complexity of CECS and the challenges of identifying posterior compartment involvement. Potential complications include superficial or common peroneal nerve injury, incomplete compartment release, persistent symptoms, and recurrence requiring revision surgery.32,33 These findings emphasize the importance of meticulous surgical technique, comprehensive compartment assessment, and appropriate patient selection.

Following surgery, all patients reported symptomatic improvement and return to baseline activity. Among the subset of patients with paired PROMIS data, improvements were observed in both physical function and pain interference following surgery. Although with the numbers available, no significant difference could be detected, the direction of change was consistent with the patient-reported symptomatic improvement documented throughout the cohort. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of patients with complete PROMIS data. Prior studies have reported postoperative pain relief and return-to-activity rates of approximately 70% to 80% following surgical decompression.7,34 In our series, 4 patients required additional evaluation for persistent posterior compartment symptoms, and 2 ultimately underwent further posterior compartment release. Previously reported causes of recurrence include incomplete release, untreated compartment involvement, fascial scarring, nerve injury, coexisting pathology, and psychological comorbidities.8,16,32,35,36

Strengths of this study include detailed characterization of the diagnostic pathway preceding operative treatment, inclusion of patients with prolonged symptom duration, and incorporation of both qualitative outcomes and validated PROMIS measures when available.

Taken together, these findings highlight the substantial burden imposed by delayed recognition of CECS. Patients in our cohort experienced symptoms for more than 4 years before receiving a definitive diagnosis and frequently underwent extensive diagnostic evaluations before appropriate treatment was initiated. Greater awareness of CECS, systematic evaluation of exertional lower-leg pain, and thoughtful integration of diagnostic adjuncts may help reduce diagnostic delays, facilitate earlier definitive treatment, and ultimately improve patient outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design is subject to selection bias and incomplete documentation. Second, the sample size was limited, reducing statistical power and limiting subgroup analyses. Third, compartment pressure testing and exercise-provocation protocols were not standardized, and complete pressure measurements were unavailable for all patients because of incomplete documentation, inability to tolerate bilateral testing, or revision presentations in which repeat testing was not uniformly performed. Fourth, PROMIS outcome data were only available for a subset of patients. Finally, postoperative follow-up durations varied considerably (range, 15-184 weeks), and longer-term outcomes may not be fully captured in patients with shorter follow-up durations. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insight into the real-world diagnostic and management challenges associated with CECS and highlights the substantial delays many patients experience before receiving definitive treatment.

Conclusion

CECS remains a frequently delayed diagnosis because of nonspecific symptoms, substantial overlap with other causes of exertional lower-leg pain, and limitations of currently available diagnostic tools. Although the effect of diagnostic delay on postoperative outcomes could not be directly assessed, prolonged delays were associated with substantial diagnostic burden and management complexity. Following fasciotomy, all patients reported symptomatic improvement and return to baseline activity. Greater awareness of CECS and a structured diagnostic approach may help facilitate earlier recognition and treatment.
