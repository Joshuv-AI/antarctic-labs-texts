The Effect of Analytic Variation on Empirical Biological Variation‐Derived Analytical Performance Specifications in the Veterinary Clinical Pathology Laboratory
Authors: A. Russell Moore, Addison Adams
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe empirical biologic variation (EBV) method to derive analytical performance specifications (APSs) has been provisionally evaluated for use in the veterinary clinical pathology laboratory. Analytical performance can affect EBV APSs. It has been argued that this effect is minimal, but the effect has not been evaluated for the range of performance found in veterinary laboratories.ObjectiveModel and compare EBV APSs derived across a range of analytical CV (CVA) observed in veterinary clinical pathology laboratories.MethodPublished data on biochemistry measurand CVA in veterinary clinical pathology laboratories and dog reference intervals (RIs) were used to model EBV APSs using Monte Carlo simulation. The modeled EBV APSs were compared with analogous APSs from total allowable error (TEA) and traditional BV models.ResultsGenerally, the EBV‐recommended permissible CVA (pCVA) was smaller than the observed CVA when analytical performance was poor. The EBV APSs did not have a consistent relationship with analogous APSs from TEA or traditional BV systems when analytical performance was judged acceptable using TEA or BV APSs. When analytical performance was unacceptable, the resulting EBV APSs increased dramatically (sometimes by > 200%) and lost their initial relationship with TEA or traditional BV‐derived APSs. Using log‐normal calculations for measurands with a Gaussian distribution produced wider EBV APSs.ConclusionThe EBV method produces growth‐oriented APSs under all modeled conditions and can produce notably more lenient APSs for poorly performing labs than better performing labs. The EBV method can produce acceptable APSs under some conditions and may not be suitable for use in veterinary medicine.

Introduction

Analytical performance specifications (APSs) are metrics used to ensure that laboratory results are of sufficient quality for the intended use and minimize wasted effort to remove meaningless deviations from perfect performance. Biologic variation (BV)‐based APSs are often very stringent and hard to achieve but target a performance level that does not mask meaningful clinical changes. They do this by setting acceptable analytical variation to be smaller than the variation found naturally within the patient [1, 2]. Traditional derivation of BV estimates involves intense and detailed data collection and statistical analysis to partition observed variation into that caused by the expected fluctuations within the healthy individual (CVI), differences in average values between individuals within the group (CVG), and variation associated with the analytical process (CVA).

Recently, the empirical biologic variation (EBV) method has been considered for use in the veterinary clinical laboratory [3]. This method was originally proposed in human medicine as a simpler way to approximate BV‐based APSs from readily available reference interval (RI) data and provide more easily attainable APSs [3, 4, 5, 6]. The veterinary literature provisionally evaluated the use of the EBV method when analyzers were performing acceptably based on previously established APSs and states that the method can be used when expert opinion or BV‐based APSs are not accessible, that is, when these are not available to confirm that the analyzer is performing acceptably or performance is deemed too stringent to be met.

The EBV method recognizes that RIs include the effects of CVG and CVI and employs a logarithmic manipulation of the width of the reference interval (WRI) to generate APSs using one of two sets of equations. One set of equations was derived for use when the underlying distribution is Gaussian. The other, using log‐normal calculations, was derived for use when the underlying distribution is non‐Gaussian or unknown. This practice would be correct for log‐normal measurands but may not be appropriate for measurands with unknown distribution. The effect of using equations derived for non‐Gaussian conditions on Gaussian datasets has not been evaluated previously.

The EBV method has an inherent circular flaw [5]. The WRI includes the effects of analytical variation, CVA, in addition to biologic variation, CVG, and CVI. Therefore, though it has been argued that the effect is minimal when CVA is smaller than the combined CVG and CVI, the CVA of the analyzer used to generate the RI impacts the APS used to define acceptable CVA. In short, the EBV method could produce more permissible APSs for labs with poorer analytical performance than for labs with high‐quality performance.

We sought to evaluate the effects of CVA on EBV APSs in the veterinary setting using real‐world data as a model for its use with analytical methods when other APSs are not available. We used quality performance data obtained in veterinary clinical pathology laboratories and data for canine biochemical measurands to derive and compare EBV APSs across the range of CVA seen in veterinary clinical pathology laboratory settings.

To model the best possible analytical performance, the smallest intrarun precision data for each measurand generated on a Cobas C501 using commercial quality control material (Bio Rad Multiqual 1 or Multiqual 3; Bio Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) at installation of the analyzer was collected and called the minimum observed CVA (oCVAmin) [2]. The RIs in use on the analyzer, used to generate oCVAmin, were used to model the in‐use RI for these measurands (Table 1). To model the maximum observed CVA (oCVAmax) of analyzers being used to generate clinical data, the largest CVA for each measurand was extracted from a published survey of analytical performance in veterinary clinical pathology laboratories, Rishniw et al. Table 4 [11]. Per Rishniw et al., the maximum observed CVA for sodium was 55.9 for QCM3 in an in‐clinic laboratory while the maximum observed CVA for reference laboratories was 3.0 for this QC material and the maximum observed CVA for the other QC materials did not exceed 9.9; the 55.9 value was considered to be an extreme outlier and 9.9 was used as the oCVAmax for sodium.

To assess the proposed EBV method, a simulation study was conducted as diagrammed in Figure 1. For all measurands, the data‐generating process (DGP) consisted of generating samples, each of sample size 120, independently drawn from either a log‐normal or normal distribution. The mean μ for each measurand was set equal to the average of the lower and upper limits of the RI in Table 1. The standard deviation σ was set equal to μ*CVA2+CVB2/100. For the standard deviation equation, it was assumed that the empirical coefficient of variation is related to CVA and CVB through the root of the sum of the squares. CVB was selected from Table 1.

For both the log‐normal and normal DGPs, CVA was ranged on an equally spaced grid of size 20 from oCVmin to oCVmax. For each of these 20 CVA, 10 000 independent samples were generated. From each sample, an RI was produced with the lower bound equal to the 3rd order statistic and the upper bound as the 117th order statistic. Thus, the DGP considered the random effects of analytical variation, CVA, at 20 increments of from oCVmin to oCVmax on healthy patient results to generate 20 sets of 10 000 n = 120 RIs.

For each value of CVA, the 10 000 resulting RIs were used to derive the APSs, namely permissible CVA (pCVA), permissible bias (pB), permissible measurement uncertainty (pU), and permissible measurement uncertainty for external quality assurance systems (pUEQAS), according to published equations [3, 4, 5, 6]. The average and standard deviation of the resulting APSs were calculated over their 10 000 realizations. These are the Monte Carlo approximations of the mean and standard error, given a sample size of 120, for each APS estimator at each CVA value. The sampling distribution of each APS estimator at each CVA value was summarized by an interval, similar to a confidence interval. That is, the upper and lower bounds of each interval were calculated as standard scores of 2 and −2, respectively.

To characterize the oCVA, the range between oCVAmin and oCVAmax was divided into acceptable and unacceptable performance, with 2*oCVA < TEA considered evidence of at least acceptable analytical performance and 2*oCVA > TEA considered evidence of unacceptable performance. This cut‐off for acceptable analytical performance has been previously used, though it should be recognized that 2*oCVA = TEA only produces a 2 sigma process if bias is negligible or ignored; therefore, it is unlikely to allow for statistical quality assurance [11]. A 3 sigma process is typically cited as the minimum for stable performance, and this is defined as the recommended CVA from TEA and is more likely to allow for statistical quality assurance [2].

The relationship between modeled EBV APSs across the range of oCVA and APSs derived by expert opinion and biologic variation, TEA, and BV, was graphically compared. Additionally, numerical values for the EBV APSs were calculated for 4 theoretical laboratories operating at the smallest observed analytical variation, oCVAmin, the biologic variation targeted analytical variation, BV CVA, the expert opinion targeted analytical variation, TEA CVA, or the maximum observed analytical variation, oCVAmax. The ratio pCVA:CVI was also calculated across the range of observed CVA. The change in pCVA across the range of oCVA was calculated in both CV units and percentage.

To evaluate the effects of the type of calculation on Gaussian‐distributed measurands, the change in pCVA across the range of oCVA was compared when equations derived for non‐Gaussian and Gaussian conditions were used.

Generally, when performance was poor, the EBV method yielded a permissible analytical variation, pCVA, that was smaller than the analytical variation observed in the lab at the time of RI generation, oCVA. This was seen as evidence that EBV would provide APSs that helped to improve analytical performance when improvement was needed.

The relationship of the EBV‐derived permissible analytical variation, pCVA, to recommendations based on expert opinion and biological variation, TEA CVA or BV CVA, was not constant across the range of observed analytical variation, oCVA. When performance was acceptable, there was minimal percentage or absolute change in pCVA, pCVA was frequently smaller than APS from other methods, TEA CVA or BV CVA, and the pCVA:CVI did not change dramatically but was not always smaller than the 0.5 ratio targeted in biological variation. When performance was unacceptable, pCVA increased; pCVA was often greater than APS from other methods, TEA CVA or BV CVA, and the pCVA:CVI increased and deviated further from 0.5.

The percent change in pCVA across the range of observed performance was variable, with a median change of 46.1% and an absolute difference of 0.9. The smallest observed change was 3.2%; however, some measurands had > 200% increase in pCVA across the range of observed performance.

As the permissible uncertainty, pU, and the permissible uncertainty for EQAS, pUEQAS, are multiples of permissible analytical variation, pCVA, changes in pU and pUEQAS represent a greater absolute difference. The permissible bias, pB, being a fraction of pCVA, represents a smaller absolute difference, but the general relationships for all are similar.

The median change for Gaussian distributed measurands increased from 10.8% and 0.6 units when normal calculations were used to 16.5% and 0.7 units when log‐normal calculations were used. The largest percentage change was seen in albumin, which increased from a 231% change to a 291% change. The pattern of relationships between the EBV APSs and expert opinion (TEA CVA) or biological variation (BV CVA) was not changed by the choice of calculation method.

Discussion

This work agrees with previous claims that the circular flaw in the EBV method has minimal effect when analytical performance is adequate. It also demonstrates that the EBV method will produce markedly less stringent APSs when analytical performance is less than ideal; the method allows poorer performing systems more leniency than better performing systems and does not provide a consistent relationship with other APSs as oCVA changes. The method might be usable within the veterinary laboratory as long as it is never used as the sole method of APS generation or an iterative process is used to confirm the accuracy of EBV APSs.

The modeled total protein data might give a good example of how this method would be implemented in the real world and highlight the disparity that the EBV method allows. For better performing labs (i.e., those operating within current performance recommendations as outlined by expert opinion or biological variation), the EBV method would suggest that target analytical variation (pCVA, Table 3) should be around 2.5%, acceptable error in a single diagnostic result (pU, Table 4) should be around 6%, and results of external quality assurance testing (pUEQAS, Figure 2) should be within 10%. In contrast, a poorer performing lab (i.e., one operating close to the maximum observed values reported by Rishniw) would be allowed double the amount of error in their budget. The EBV method would suggest that target analytical variation should be around 5.7% (pCVA, Table 3), acceptable total error in a single diagnostic result could be around 13.5% (pU, Table 4), and results of external quality assurance testing should not be in excess of 20% (pU, Table 4) off. Not only does this doubling of APS between labs create an inconsistent performance standard, the EBV method allows poorer performing labs to continue to perform poorly while suggesting that acceptable performance is being met.

The range of observed analytical variation, oCVA, evaluated in this work reflects actual performance in veterinary clinical pathology laboratories actively being used to produce results for diagnostic evaluations. Some of the oCVAmax were reported from in‐clinic laboratories. It could be argued that in‐clinic laboratories very rarely produce their own RIs, and therefore, they may not model the oCVAmax used to generate EBV APSs. However, for several measurands, the oCVAmax used in this study was extracted from reference laboratory performance. When it was not, the maximum CVA in reference laboratories was typically only slightly smaller than in‐clinic performance. Ultimately, as the intent of this work was to consider the effects of a range of performance, having a slightly larger oCVAmax was thought to be advantageous.

Since EBV APSs tend to suggest a target level of performance that is smaller than the observed performance of the analyzer, oCVA, when the APSs were developed, until the analytical performance is acceptable, the EBV method could be used to produce growth‐oriented APSs that drive improvement of laboratory performance when needed. However, the model argues strongly that those APSs might not always ensure that the method is fit for diagnostic use as judged by expert opinion or biological variation, TEA, or BV APSs. For some measurands, the relationship between EBV APSs and BV or TEA APSs changed as the CVA increased. This is significant. The APSs of other methods are benchmarked against clinically relevant specifications. For example, the biological variation benchmarks BV CVA to be < 0.5 of CVI because operating to this standard ensures that analytical variation will be smaller than the variation experienced within a stable individual and will not mask clinically relevant changes. TEA APSs are set based on the expert opinion of what amount of analytical inaccuracy will affect the clinical interpretation of the resulting data. The data suggest that the EBV method does not always produce APSs that meet these basic benchmarks and that the ability to meet these benchmarks is dependent on the analytical performance of the lab at the time that RIs are generated.

Previous treatment of the circular flaw in EBV argued that the circular flaw has minimal effect when analytical variation, CVA, is smaller than the combined biological variation, CVB, but failed to define what a small CVA would be [5]. It might be reasonable to define a smaller CVA as CVA ≤ 0.5*CVB, as has been targeted as a desirable relationship between CVA and CVI. We chose to use 2‐sigma based on TEA APSs as our threshold for an acceptable performance. In most biochemistry settings, a 2‐sigma process will have a larger CVA than a process operating at CVA = 0.5*CVB. As previously suggested, the EBV method is insensitive to changes in CVA when the analytical method is performing as at least a 2‐sigma process. Thus, the EBV method could comfortably be used if other APSs are available to show that analytical performance is acceptable. However, it seems redundant to generate APSs using a different method to confirm that analytical performance is adequate for generating APSs from the EBV method. This limitation would also be significant when using EBV to generate APSs for a novel analytical method where APSs are not available from other approaches, as has been suggested would be possible [3]. While some of the oCVAmin used in this study can be recognized to be very large based on our current knowledge of these measurands, consideration of the performance of the EBV method at the values models the scenario when oCVAmin cannot be recognized to be large because analytical performance specifications based on expert opinion and biological variation are not available. Our results show that when other APSs are not available to define acceptable performance, the EBV method can produce APS that are not biologically appropriate. It is unclear what advantages there are to using the EBV method if it can only be used confidently when other APSs are available.

Theoretically, it might be possible to use only the EBV method if an iterative process were employed; successive cycles of RI generation, EBV APS derivation, and analytical method improvement, ending when APSs show minimal change between iterations, might produce a consistent set of APSs independent of how poor quality was at the start of the process. For example, an albumin method operating unacceptably at oCVA = 38% and evaluated using the log‐normal equations would, with high probability, produce a permissible analytical variation, pCVA, of around 7.2%. If the method performance could be improved to oCVA = 7.2% prior to generating RIs and EBV APSs again, a new pCVA could very likely be 2.5%; the change in pCVA could be taken as evidence that analytical performance during the first iteration affected the EBV APSs generation and that 7.2% likely is not an acceptable target. Performing a third iteration of EBV APSs generation after method performance is improved to oCVA = 2.5% would very likely yield a new pCVA around 2.0%. The minimal change of pCVA between iterations 2 and 3 could be used to suggest that oCVA = 2.5% is not influencing the EBV APSs and is evidence of acceptable analytical performance. Comparing the pCVA generated at each iteration is informative. An albumin pCVA = 7.2% is larger than recommended by expert opinion or biologic variation, TEA CVA and BV CVA, and only produces a 2.1‐sigma process when TEA = 15% and Bias = 0% is used to calculate the sigma metric. This suggests that the first iteration of EBV APS would claim acceptable performance while clinically relevant errors occur in ~1/3 of the results. pCVA = 7.2% is an unacceptable APS based on other APS approaches. However, pCVA = 2.0 and 2.5% are smaller than the expert opinion recommendation, TEA CVA, and approximate the biological variation recommendation, BV CVA. It produces a ~6 sigma (i.e., world‐class) process when TEA = 15% and Bias = 0% is used to calculate the sigma metric. Through several iterations of the EBV process, method performance has been improved to where it will likely not impact diagnostic evaluations. While this iterative approach could theoretically be used to produce acceptable EBV APSs, it requires multiple RI generation steps. This is both expensive and labor‐intensive and unlikely to occur in all laboratories.

Calculation of EBV APS from RI generated using indirect methods has been suggested [3]. Indirectly derived reference intervals take advantage of complex statistical methods and large datasets of known healthy patients (i.e., samples collected during routine wellness exams that did not document evidence of disease) or mixed healthy and diseased patients. Indirect methods remove the need to collect samples specifically for RI generation, making this approach less costly and less labor‐intensive [12]. Current evaluations suggest that these large datasets should contain ≥ 5000 individuals with ≤ 20% pathologic samples [13]. An indirect approach for RI generation could be used to decrease the financial and labor costs of an iterative approach to EBV APS generation but would require assuming what the authors feel is a large degree of risk in some settings. Using the indirect RI generation method when expert opinion or BV data are not available requires the generation of > 5000 diagnostic patient results using a method that cannot be confirmed to be performing acceptably by other means. Each successive iteration would require the generation of another 5000 diagnostic patient results. While this may require only 2 iterations, as documented in the albumin example above, the iterative process may require distribution of (1) at least 5000 diagnostic results when performance is CV = 38% and sigma = 0.39 to produce a permissible analytical variation, pCVA = 7.2%, (2) at least 5000 results when performance is oCVA = 7.2 and sigma = 2.1 to generate a pCVA = 2.5%, and (3) a final set of at least 5000 results when pCVA = 2.5 and sigma > 6 to provide data sufficient to calculate a new pCVA = 2.2% and suggest acceptable performance was met 5000 cases ago. As a mixed population of healthy and diseased patients is commonly used, and the effects of analytical performance could impact the ability of the statistics used for indirect RI generation to exclude diseased patients from the healthy subpopulation, additional iterations or a larger sample size may be needed when combined with an iterative approach to EBV APS generation. Clearly, additional and extensive modeling of performance and risks of the combined use of indirect RI generation and an iterative EBV approach is needed before this approach can be used in practice.

Using log‐normal calculations for Gaussian distributed measurands yielded higher EBV APS. On average, the effect was minimal and would be predicted to have minimal impact on the APS. The data suggest the need for some caution when using log‐normal calculations for all measurands, but does not strongly refute the practice.

Ultimately, the model based on real‐world veterinary laboratory performance data confirms that the analytical performance at the time of RI generation can affect the EBV method and produce different APSs for different laboratories. The EBV APS produced this way can have a varying relationship with those generated by expert opinion, TEA, and/or biological variation, BV, generated performance standards. The EBV method could be consistently used to produce growth‐oriented APS goals, but did not ensure a consistent or acceptable analytical performance target in all conditions until the analytical method was operating near APSs derived by expert opinion or biological variation. When analytical performance at the time of RI generation was acceptable as defined by APSs from expert opinion or biological variation, the EBV method produced APSs similar to those of other methods. When analytical performance at the time of RI generation was not acceptable as defined by APSs from expert opinion or biological variation, the EBV method was capable of producing markedly larger APSs; this flaw compromises the utility of the EBV method in situations where other APSs have not been derived. An iterative process of generating EBV APSs might correct this flaw and improve the end result, but an iterative process would negate the labor and cost savings that are reported to be a large benefit of the EBV method or might require assumption of considerable risk when an iterative approach is combined with indirect reference interval generation. These weaknesses seriously compromise the utility of the method. The authors recommend that the EBV method not be used until it has been improved.
