Complex auditory hallucinations associated with hearing impairment in elderly adults
Authors: J V Ashwin, Mohit Kumar Shahi, Astha Singh, Bhupendra Singh, Sanjay Balasubramaniam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAuditory hallucinations are a common yet underexplored phenomenon in individuals with hearing impairment, particularly in the elderly. Oliver Sacks syndrome, often seen in patients with sensory deprivation, is typically characterized by musical hallucinations but can also present with nonmusical auditory experiences. This case series presents three elderly patients with hearing impairment who experienced a range of auditory hallucinations, including voices, instrumental music, and environmental sounds, all localized to the ear with greater hearing loss. Despite these auditory experiences, patients demonstrated good insight and no signs of psychosis or cognitive decline. Comprehensive clinical evaluations ruled out other conditions, and diagnoses of Oliver Sacks syndrome were confirmed. The neurobiology underlying these hallucinations involves sensory deprivation leading to maladaptive cortical reorganization, with overactivity in dopaminergic pathways and dysfunction in the auditory cortex contributing to the hallucinations. All patients were treated with risperidone, melatonin, and hearing aids, leading to significant improvements in the frequency and intensity of hallucinations. This case series highlights the diverse nature of auditory hallucinations in elderly individuals with hearing impairment, stressing the importance of recognizing sensory deprivation as a contributing factor and utilizing a comprehensive approach to treatment.

Hallucination, a perceptual abnormality occurring without external stimuli, is a well-recognized phenomenon in the context of different neurological and psychiatric illnesses.[1] Sensory deprivation, most common in the elderly, is a significant predisposing factor for these experiences as the brain compensates for reduced sensory input by generating spontaneous neural activity.[23] Charles Bonnet syndrome (CBS), characterized by vivid and complex visual hallucinations in individuals with impaired vision, provides a well-established model for understanding such phenomena.[4] Similarly, auditory hallucinations are frequently observed in individuals with hearing impairment, yet they remain less comprehensively studied.[5] Studies estimate that around 16% to 32% of individuals with significant auditory deficits report experiencing hallucinations.[67] This subset, often associated with Oliver Sacks syndrome, primarily involves auditory hallucinations typically attributed to sensory deprivation, most commonly in individuals with significant hearing impairment.[7] Musical hallucinations, such as hearing songs, instrumental melodies, or tunes, are frequently discussed in the literature. These hallucinations are often vivid, perceived as external, and tend to localize to the ear with greater hearing impairment.[78] Despite their realism, they are not usually accompanied by delusions or other psychotic features, helping to differentiate them from psychiatric conditions.[9] There are no formal diagnostic criteria specifically for Oliver Sacks syndrome. Diagnosis is based on clinical history, including the nature of hallucinations, sensory deprivation (e.g. hearing loss), and the exclusion of psychiatric or neurological conditions through neuroimaging, cognitive assessments, and metabolic evaluations.[910]

Elderly patients with hearing loss form a particularly vulnerable group as their sensory deprivation can lead to a range of hallucinations. Current literature has primarily emphasized the musical aspect of Oliver Sacks syndrome, overlooking these broader manifestations.[11] This limited perspective fails to capture the true heterogeneity of auditory experiences associated with sensory impairment, leaving clinicians without a comprehensive framework for diagnosis and management.[12] This case series addresses this gap by presenting detailed profiles of elderly individuals with hearing impairment who exhibit diverse auditory hallucinations.

CASE SERIES

Case 1

A 63-year-old right-handed, Hindi-speaking, illiterate female with no medical comorbidities and no prior psychiatric history presented with complaints of left hearing impairment. She reported a gradual onset of left-sided hearing loss over the past 3–4 years, which progressively worsened over time. Approximately 1 month before the presentation, she began experiencing auditory phenomena, initially in instrumental sounds such as tabla and violin music. Over time, these sounds evolved into hearing voices, including multiple people singing, humming, or speaking in a muffled tone. Despite these auditory experiences, the patient demonstrated good insight and attributed the sounds to her impaired ear, localized explicitly to her left ear in the external space. She denied any associated delusions, thought disturbances, or other symptoms suggestive of psychosis. Investigations, including blood tests, urine analysis, and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), were unremarkable. A provisional diagnosis of Oliver Sacks syndrome was made. An ear nose throat (ENT) evaluation revealed severe sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) in the left ear on pure-tone audiometry. Using a hearing aid resulted in minimal improvement in the hearing. As the voices and music continued to distress her significantly, low-dose risperidone was started, on which her distress, frequency, and intensity of hallucinations decreased subjectively, and the improvement was sustained during the 7-month follow-up.

Case 2

A 72-year-old male farmer with no medical comorbidities presented with a bilateral hearing impairment that had gradually progressed over the past 7–8 years. Over the past month, he reported auditory hallucinations characterized by a male voice repeatedly calling out his name throughout the day, which was persistent and was present only in his right ear. He experienced a persistent bell-ringing sound in the left ear but no voices. He expressed significant distress and fear related to the voice as his cultural beliefs led him to interpret it as the voice of an evil spirit. Initially, this raised suspicion of psychosis; however, the uneven distribution of hallucinations, the absence of other psychotic symptoms, preserved functioning, and the presence of hearing impairment argued against a psychotic disorder. There were no fluctuations in consciousness, and blood tests, urine analysis, and MRI brain results were unremarkable, allowing us to rule out delirium. These findings led to a provisional diagnosis of Oliver Sacks syndrome. An ENT evaluation revealed bilateral SNHL, with severe SNHL in the right ear and mild SNHL in the left ear, and a hearing aid was advised. He was started on risperidone and melatonin, which led to a significant improvement, and his improvement was sustained during the 6-month follow-up.

Case 3

A 68-year-old female presented with a 6-month history of persistent muffled auditory hallucinations resembling multiple people talking along with noises of water tricking in her left ear, occurring daily and more noticeable in quiet settings. She had good insight into her condition, and there was no other functional impairment associated with the hallucination. She had no psychiatric history, cognitive complaints, or seizures, but she reported progressive hearing loss in her left ear over the past 5 years. Relevant laboratory tests were normal. Neuropsychological testing revealed intact cognition. Pure tone audiometry confirmed severe SNHL in the left ear, and MRI showed no structural abnormalities. Metabolic workups, including thyroid and vitamin B12 levels, were normal. These findings supported a diagnosis of Oliver Sacks syndrome, an auditory variant related to sensory deprivation from hearing loss. She was started on low-dose risperidone (0.5 mg/day) to address distress from hallucinations, along with melatonin (5 mg nightly) for sleep disturbances. A hearing aid was prescribed to mitigate sensory deprivation. Over 3 months, her symptoms significantly improved.

DISCUSSION

This case series presents three elderly patients with auditory hallucinations in the context of severe SNHL. The hallucinations in our cases included hearing instrumental sounds, voices, and nonverbal phenomena such as bell ringing and water dripping, which reports variability in the content and localization of hallucinations, all related to hearing loss. A distinguishing feature of auditory hallucinations in Oliver Sacks syndrome is their strong association with sensory deprivation, particularly hearing loss, without accompanying psychiatric features such as delusions, paranoia, or significant functional impairment. These hallucinations are typically vivid, perceived as external, and localized to the affected ear, differing from pseudo hallucinations, which patients often recognize as originating internally.[67] Unlike hallucinations seen in psychotic disorders, those in Oliver Sacks syndrome lack a pervasive disruption of reality testing or thought processes.[8] Additionally, these hallucinations are distinct from tinnitus, which is typically perceived as nonstructured, repetitive sounds like ringing or buzzing rather than complex auditory experiences. This differentiation is critical for accurate diagnosis and management.[12]

The neurobiology of auditory hallucinations in SNHL is complex, with maladaptive neuroplasticity playing a key role. Prolonged hearing loss can lead to reorganization in critical auditory regions of the brain, such as the superior temporal gyrus, the geniculate nucleus of the thalamus, and weakened interhemispheric connections along the auditory pathways. These changes can result in the brain generating spontaneous auditory perceptions.[13141516] Disruptions in dopamine, serotonin, and glutamate pathways are closely linked to hallucinations.[1718] Overactivity in dopaminergic pathways leads to abnormal sensory processing. At the same time, N-Methyl-D-Aspartate (NMDA) receptor dysfunction in the auditory cortex disrupts glutamatergic signaling, leading to dysregulation of dopamine levels in the mesolimbic system (particularly the nucleus accumbens and ventral tegmental area), contributing to hallucinations.[18] Serotonin also modulates dopamine activity, particularly through 5HT2A receptors in the prefrontal cortex. These interactions create a complex network of neurotransmitter imbalances.[19] Antipsychotics help by addressing these disruptions. Second-generation antipsychotics like risperidone block dopamine D2 receptors and regulate serotonin activity, reducing hallucination severity.[17] While hearing aids were prescribed to address sensory deprivation, their minimal effect emphasizes the need for pharmacological interventions. Early intervention with hearing aids and regular auditory assessments may reduce the risk, but further studies are needed to establish effective prevention strategies.[20]

CONCLUSION

This case series emphasizes the variability in the presentation of auditory hallucinations in elderly patients with SNHL. While pharmacological treatment with risperidone and melatonin proved effective, a holistic approach that integrates pharmacological, psychological, and rehabilitative strategies is essential for addressing the complex needs of elderly patients with SNHL and auditory hallucinations.
