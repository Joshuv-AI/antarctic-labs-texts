Repair of Distal Biceps Femoris Avulsion and Lateral Collateral Ligament Reconstruction: Surgical Pearls
Authors: Jason Long, Samuel Huntley, Nate J. Dickinson, Jillian Neufeld, YuChia Wang, Thomas R. Hackett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIsolated avulsions of the distal biceps femoris are infrequent; however, they often occur in combination with other injuries to the posterolateral corner of the knee including the lateral collateral ligament (LCL). We describe a technique for LCL reconstruction with semitendinosus allograft through a bone tunnel in the fibula, followed by transosseous biceps femoris repair utilizing the cannulated biotenodesis screw from the LCL reconstruction. The LCL is reconstructed via bone tunnels and biotenodesis screws at both the femoral origin and fibular insertion utilizing the allograft. Two FiberTape sutures (Arthrex, Naples, FL) are passed in a Krackow fashion through the biceps femoris tendon, yielding two proximal and two distal limbs. The distal limbs are passed through the cannulated tenodesis screw in the fibula, bringing the biceps femoris tendon over the lateral fibula. These limbs are then brought back through the biceps femoris tendon and tied to the proximal limbs, completing the biceps femoris repair. The excess allograft can be sutured to the biceps femoris for reinforcement.

Begin by palpating and marking the head of the fibula. An incision beginning distal to the fibular head is extended proximally towards the lateral epicondyle approximately 10 cm, through the skin and subcutaneous tissue. The peroneal nerve is identified, released, and mobilized for protection. The stump of the biceps femoris is identified, and the tendon is mobilized to the fibular head to ensure adequate length and tendon quality. We begin by preparing the fibular tunnel which will serve both fibular collateral ligament (FCL) and biceps femoris procedures. The anterior fibular head is exposed, a retractor placed posterior to the fibula protects neurovascular structures, and a 2.4 mm guide pin is inserted. The fibular tunnel is drilled over the guide pin with a 7 mm cannulated drill then cleared of soft tissue. Next, we prepare the femoral attachment for the FCL reconstruction. The iliotibial band is split parallel with its fibers at the level of the FCL origin. A 2.4 mm guide pin with suture eye is inserted at the FCL origin, advanced through the medial femoral cortex and through the skin. Over the guide pin, a 7 mm cannulated reamer drills a 40 to 50 mm socket, which is cleared of soft tissue. A passing suture loaded in a Beath pin is pulled through the tunnel to facilitate later graft insertion. The allograft is prepared by whip stitching both ends. The prepared graft is pulled through the femoral tunnel using the passing suture and tensioned through the medial femur. Secure the femoral graft proximally with a biotenodesis screw inserted over a guidewire. The free end of the graft is passed distally under the iliotibial band from anterior‐to‐posterior towards the fibular head. The graft is passed through the fibular tunnel, again anterior‐to‐posterior using a transosseous suture passer. The knee is flexed 20° to 30° with a slight valgus force applied; then, the allograft is tensioned and secured in the fibular tunnel with a 7 × 20 mm cannulated biotenodesis screw over a guidewire. The cannulated portion of the screw will serve as the tunnel for the biceps femoris repair. Excess graft length is retained for augmentation of the biceps femoris at the end of the procedure. Proceeding with the biceps femoris repair, the previously identified tendon is readied with two suture tapes. The distance from the distal to proximal suture tape is determined by the anterior‐to‐posterior length of the fibular head or by reducing the biceps tendon to the posterior fibula and marking it. Beginning with the distal suture tape, four throws are passed in Krackow fashion working proximally, then four throws working back. This is repeated with the proximal suture. The tendon should now have 4 suture tails, 2 from the distal suture and 2 from the proximal. The 2 tails from the distal suture are brought through the cannulated biotenodesis screw in the head of the fibula from anterior‐to‐posterior using a suture passer. These sutures are loaded into a free needle and passed in horizontal mattress fashion, deep‐to‐superficial, back through the biceps femoris tendon, then tensioned and tied to the proximal suture tails. Tensioning should be done with the knee near full extension to avoid over tensioning. This completes the biceps femoris repair. Any remaining FCL tendon or allograft can then be sutured to the allograft and biceps femoris respectively to augment the reconstruction and repair. The leg is cycled from 0° to 90° to ensure no undue tension on the biceps femoris or peroneal nerve. The wound is irrigated, then closed in a standard fashion. The biceps femoris muscle runs along the posterolateral thigh and is comprised of long and short heads which merge as they extend distally. 1 The long head originates at the ischial tuberosity, the short head originates at the distal one‐third of the femoral diaphysis, on the linea aspera. 1 The biceps femoris tendon has several insertions including a tibial footprint and distal, medial, and proximal footprints on the fibular head. 2 The biceps femoris is both a dynamic and static knee stabilizer, 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 is a primary knee flexor, and synergistically extends the hip. 3 , 5

The lateral collateral ligament (LCL) originates an average of 1.4 mm proximal and 3.1 mm posterior to the lateral femoral epicondyle and inserts 8.2 mm posterior to the anterior margin of the fibular head and 28.4 mm distal to the tip of the fibular styloid process. 6 The LCL stabilizes the lateral knee throughout the complete range of motion and resists external rotation near full extension. 7 , 8

Injuries to the distal biceps femoris rarely occur in isolation and are frequently concomitant to multiligamentous knee injuries. 9 Retrospective case series have identified significant associations between distal biceps femoris injuries and LCL injuries. 9 , 10 Thus, surgical techniques to treat distal biceps femoris injuries should address both structures.

Previous biomechanical comparisons for distal biceps repair techniques have shown that suture tape passed through transosseous fibular tunnels has a greater mean failure load when compared with suture anchor repair. 11 However, limited bony area on the fibula may prevent multiple tunnels for multiple fixations.

This technical note describes combined LCL reconstruction with semitendinosus allograft and distal biceps femoris repair by exploiting a single fibular tunnel to create a biomechanically favorable repair (Video 1).

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Approach

An incision beginning distal to the fibular head is extended proximally towards the lateral epicondyle approximately 10 cm, through the skin and subcutaneous tissue. The peroneal nerve is identified, released, and mobilized for protection. The stump of the biceps femoris is identified, tendon quality assessed, and the tendon mobilized to the fibular head (Figure 1).

The head of the fibula is marked and the incision is begun at the distal aspect of the head of the fibula extending proximally 10 cm, and is carried through the skin and subcutaneous tissue. The PN is identified and a complete release of the nerve allows for mobilization to protect the nerve. The stump of the BF is identified, tendon quality assessed, and the tendon mobilized to the fibular head. This is a lateral view of the right knee. (BF, biceps femoris; LCL, lateral collateral ligament; PN, peroneal nerve.)

LCL Reconstruction

The anterior fibula is exposed, and a 2.4 mm guide pin is inserted. A retractor placed posterior to the fibula protects neurovascular structures. The fibular tunnel is drilled over the guide pin with a 7 mm cannulated drill then cleared of soft tissue. An aiming arm may facilitate pin placement and determine tunnel length (Figure 2).

(B) The fibular tunnel (black arrow) is drilled over the guide pin with a 7 mm cannulated drill then cleared of soft tissue. The biceps femoris tendon is retracted with a clamp. Green circle = fibular head.

The iliotibial band is split parallel with its fibers at the level of the LCL origin. A 2.4 mm guide pin with suture eye (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is inserted at the LCL origin, advanced through the medial femoral cortex and through the skin. Using the guide pin, a 7 mm cannulated reamer drills a 40 to 50 mm socket, which is cleared of soft tissue (Figure 3). A passing suture loaded in a Beath pin is pulled through the tunnel to facilitate later graft insertion.

(A) A guide pin is inserted placed at the LCL origin approximately 1.4 mm proximal and 3.1 mm posterior to the lateral epicondyle, and extends through the medial femoral cortex and skin of the medial knee. (B) A cannulated drill placed over the pin drills to a depth of about 45 mm to create a socket for graft fixation. Green arrow = Iliotibial band split at the height of the LCL origin; Black arrow = LCL origin. (LCL, lateral collateral ligament.)

The allograft is prepared by whip stitching both ends. Once prepared, the graft is pulled through the femoral tunnel using the passing suture and tensioned through the medial femur. A nitinol guidewire followed by a 7 × 30 mm biotenodesis screw (Arthrex, Naples, FL) secures the proximal graft. The free end of the graft is pulled under the iliotibial band from anterior‐to‐posterior towards the fibular head. A transosseous suture passer pulls the allograft from anterior‐to‐posterior through the fibular tunnel. The knee is flexed 20° to 30° with a slight valgus force applied; then, the allograft is tensioned and secured in the fibular tunnel with a 7 × 20 mm biotenodesis screw (Arthrex, Naples, FL) over a nitinol guidewire. Excess graft length posteriorly is retained for augmentation of the biceps femoris (Figure 4).

(B) The graft is pulled deep to the iliotibial band towards the fibular head (green arrow). Blue arrow = predrilled fibular tunnel. (C) The allograft is passed from anterior to posterior through the fibular tunnel (yellow arrow). The native LCL is held with a clamp. Blue sutures have been placed on the Biceps femoris. Biceps Femoris Preparation

Proceeding with the biceps femoris repair, the tendon is readied by placing two suture tapes (Arthrex, Naples, FL), one distally and another slightly proximally. The distance from distal to proximal sutures is either the anterior‐to‐posterior fibular length or determined by reducing the biceps tendon to the posterior fibula and marking it. Beginning with the distal suture tape, four throws are passed in Krackow fashion working proximally and then four throws working back. This is repeated with the proximal suture (Figure 5).

Two suture tapes are placed through the biceps femoris tendon, with four throws in Krackow fashion working proximally and four throws returning distally. This should leave two suture tapes exiting the distal biceps femoris tendon, one with two tails at the distal aspect and one with two tails about 2 cm, or the measured anterior to posterior length of the fibula proximal to the distal suture tails. Green arrow = biceps femoris tendon; blue arrows = suture tapes through distal end of biceps femoris tendon.

Biceps Femoris Repair

Next, the distal sutures from the biceps femoris tendon are brought through the cannulated biotenodesis screw from anterior‐to‐posterior using a suture passer. The sutures are loaded into a free needle and passed in horizontal mattress fashion, deep‐to‐superficial, back through the biceps femoris tendon. These sutures are tensioned and tied to the proximal suture tails with the knee near full extension to avoid over tensioning, completing the biceps femoris repair (Figure 6).

(B) The tails of the distal suture tape are loaded in the suture passer to facilitate passing through the biotenodesis screw. (C) Once through the cannulation of the biotenodesis screw (green arrow), the suture tails are loaded onto a free needle and passed, in horizontal mattress fashion, deep‐to‐superficial, back through the biceps femoris tendon. Blue arrow = distal aspect of torn biceps femoris tendon; purple arrow = free end of suture securing biceps femoris tendon. (D) These sutures are tensioned and (E) tied to the proximal suture tails with the knee near full extension. Green arrow = sutures passed deep‐to‐superficial through distal biceps femoris tendon.

Any remaining LCL tendon or allograft can then be sutured to the allograft and biceps femoris respectively to augment the reconstruction and repair (Figure 7). The leg is cycled from 0° to 90° to ensure no undue tension on the biceps femoris or peroneal nerve. The wound is irrigated, then closed in a standard fashion.

Rehabilitation

Postoperatively, patients are placed in a locked hinged knee brace, preventing terminal extension for the first 6 weeks. Patients are permitted 50% weight bearing for 2 weeks and then progress to full weight bearing as tolerated, continuing with brace use for 6 to 8 weeks. Crutches are used until gait normalizes. Passive range of motion begins immediately post operatively, avoiding hyperextension and active knee flexion. After 6 weeks, the patient progresses toward full active range of motion and gentle strengthening and may resume normal activities as tolerated at 3 months, guided by physical therapy.

DISCUSSION

Isolated avulsions of the distal biceps femoris are rare, more frequently occurring in combination with posterolateral corner injury, specifically to the LCL. 10 Treatment options include nonoperative and surgical management. Nonoperative treatment includes a period of immobilization in flexion, followed by physical therapy to regain range of motion and function. 12 Clinicians should counsel patients that many acute cases or cases that fail conservative management often require surgical intervention to optimize outcomes (Figure 8).

Existing literature regarding operative treatment of distal biceps femoris injuries is limited to small case series and case reports. 4 , 5 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 These describe techniques including suture anchors and transosseous repairs, which both show good results. The largest case series involving 22 elite athletes describing isolated distal biceps femoris repair utilizing suture anchors showed early return to preinjury performance (mean time 16.7 ± 8.7 weeks) with no recurrence within the 1 year follow up, low complication rates, and high patient satisfaction. 22

Two case reports discuss successful combined LCL repair and biceps femoris repair with suture anchors. Oshima et al. 15 performed suture anchor repair of both structures, as well as the popliteofibular ligament as the first operation in a staged procedure, followed by ACL reconstruction. Aldebeyan et al. 14 described suture anchor repair of both structures with LCL augmentation using Achilles allograft, and the individual successfully returned to American Football the following season. However, a recent biomechanical study showed superior strength with transosseous fixation compared with suture anchors. 11

Our technique provides several advantages. Firstly, minimal surface area on the fibular head limits numerous anchors and tunnels. In contrast, a singular cannulated biotenodesis screw facilitates both procedures without additional fixation sites. Secondly, suture anchor fixation on the fibular head can be tenuous depending on bone quality, whereas transosseous fixation provides robust anchoring. Finally, this technique may be incorporated into further posterolateral corner reconstruction when necessary.

There are risks and limitations associated with this technique. Firstly, surgery about the posterolateral knee carries risk of iatrogenic common peroneal nerve injury. Secondly, there may be cases where the biceps femoris tendon cannot be adequately reduced, in which cases tendon augmentation may be indicated. Finally, posterolateral corner injuries may involve structures beyond these LCL and biceps femoris injuries, and all injured structures should be treated as needed, which is not addressed in this technical note.

This technical note describes a technique for LCL reconstruction with semitendinosus allograft through a fibular tunnel with cannulated biotenodesis screw fixation, followed by transosseous biceps femoris repair utilizing the cannulation of the biotenodesis screw (Tables 1 and 2).
