Quantitative and large-scale investigation of human TCR-HLA cross-reactivity
Authors: Mingyao Pan, Yuhao Tan, Yizhou Tracy Wang, Jing Hu, Julia Fleming, Hailong Hu, Ziqi Yang, Xiaowei Zhan, Bo Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The interaction between human leukocyte antigens (HLAs) and T cell receptor (TCR) is essential for adaptive immune recognition. While it is known that one TCR can map to multiple HLA alleles, the extent of this cross-reactivity remains poorly understood. Here, we introduce THNet, a TCR-based HLA similarity network, and present a comprehensive analysis of HLA-TCR cross-reactivity, which is built upon more than 9 million significantly associated HLA-TCR pairs. We created similarity networks for both class I and class II HLA alleles, illustrating how peptide cross-presentation contributes to HLA-TCR cross-reactivity. Our analysis revealed disease susceptibilities missed by single-HLA enrichment analyses, especially in the Black population. Last, we demonstrated that THNet can prioritize optimal HLA mismatch candidates across different transplantation contexts, supporting its potential utility in donor selection strategies. In summary, our investigation of the HLA-TCR cross-reactive network provides useful insights into autoimmune risk prediction and improved transplantation outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

T lymphocytes are essential components of adaptive immunity, playing critical roles in targeting and eliminating infectious pathogens and tumor cells (1–3). The T cell receptors (TCRs) on the surface of T cells enable them to recognize and respond to specific epitopes presented by human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecules (4, 5). The specificity of TCR recognition, however, is not strictly one to one; rather, it follows a many-to-many pattern, where a single TCR can recognize multiple peptide-HLA (pHLA) complexes, and, conversely, a single pHLA complex can activate different T cell clones (6, 7). This ability of a single TCR to recognize diverse pHLA is termed TCR cross-reactivity, ensuring each individual’s TCR repertoire to recognize the broad array of potential pathogens (7, 8). Some previous studies suggest that a single TCR on average can bind to more than 106 different pHLA complexes (7, 9).

TCR cross-reactivity extends beyond individual HLA subtypes, with instances of single T cell clones being activated by allogeneic HLA molecules documented decades ago (10–13). Alloreactive T cells, which react to allogeneic HLA molecules, are key mediators of graft rejection (14–16), and studies have shown that ~45% of viral-specific memory CD4+ and CD8+ T cells can cross-react with at least one allogeneic HLA molecule (10, 17). This TCR-HLA cross-reactivity has been implicated as the cause of selected autoimmune disorders (10, 18). For instance, the cross-reactivity of human T cell clone Hy.2E11 between Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)–derived peptide presented by DRB5*01:01 and human myelin basic protein presented by DRB1*15:01 underlies the association between EBV infection and an increased risk of multiple sclerosis (18, 19). Additional examples of TCRs with identical α and β chains recognizing peptides presented by different HLA alleles can also be found in various TCR antigen-specificity databases, such as Immune Epitope Database and VDJdb (20, 21). Because of the experimental challenges of pairing TCRs with their cognate antigens, the full extent of cross-reactivity across different HLA alleles remains poorly understood. A more comprehensive and systematic investigation is therefore needed to better understand the degree and mechanisms of TCR-HLA cross-reactivity.

An individual’s HLA genotype plays a major role in shaping their TCR clonotype diversity and clonal expansion. During thymic selection, different HLA alleles select distinct subsets of thymocytes to develop into functional T cells (22, 23). Beyond thymus, different HLAs present distinct peptides derived from external pathogens or cancer cells and activate antigen-specific T cell populations (24). As a result, HLA alleles are among the most critical genes that regulate T cell responses and shape TCR repertoire (24, 25), with some alleles exhibiting strong disease associations (26, 27). Recent efforts have leveraged co-occurrence patterns between public TCRs and HLA alleles to identify statistically significant associations (28, 29). Among them, HLAGuessr incorporates fuzzy matching during regression modeling to enhance HLA typing prediction (28). However, the initial reliance on exact TCR-HLA matches for identifying HLA-associated TCRs may limit sensitivity to antigen-driven convergence and overlook rare or private TCR variants. In this study, we redefined TCR-HLA associations at the discovery stage by clustering similar TCRs, enabling the detection of broader antigen-specific patterns and substantially increasing the number of informative associations.

Our investigation also provided insights into the relationships among HLA alleles themselves. While most studies regard every HLA allele as a factor independent from each other, there is growing evidence that some alleles exert overlapping effects on the TCR repertoire (30, 31), motivating several efforts to group HLA alleles into similar clusters (32–36). Sequence- or structure-based methods typically rely on amino acid or structure similarity to infer HLA relatedness (32–35). However, these approaches may not fully capture true functional equivalence, given that the functional consequence of single amino acid alteration is often unpredictable (37–39). As an alternative, MHCcluster defines HLA similarity based on predicted peptide-binding profiles of each HLA and constructs a HLA similarity network (36). While this peptide-centric approach offers valuable insights into shared antigen presentation potential, it failed to consider how HLA variation shapes the TCR repertoire. On the other hand, examining HLA similarity from the perspective of their impact on the TCR repertoire may directly pick up the immunological consequences of HLA variation. Here, we developed a TCR-based HLA similarity network, THNet, to quantify HLA-TCR cross-reactivity and functional similarity between different HLA alleles. This is achieved through ultrafast clustering (40) of more than 47 million TCRs from individuals with known HLA genotypes. This method allowed us to put HLA alleles into functionally related networks and to identify unique HLA disease susceptibilities in the minority population. Moreover, THNet assisted in selecting optimal HLA mismatch candidates within limited donor pools to minimize the risk of antigraft T cell responses, serving as a prognostic modality across different types of transplantation. In summary, our systematic and quantitative investigation of TCR-based HLA similarity provided valuable insights into how different HLA alleles influence outcomes in various clinical settings.

RESULTS

The THNet framework

To quantitatively investigate TCR-HLA cross-reactivity, we compiled five unsorted TCR databases containing high-resolution (29, 41–44), sample-specific HLA information, totaling 1603 samples (1405 with complete class I and class II and 198 with only class I HLA data; Fig. 1A). We performed TCR cluster–based enrichment that incorporates both public and private TCRs to maximize the statistical power for finding HLA-associated TCRs (hereafter referred to as HA TCRs). Specifically, the top 30,000 most highly expanded TCRs from each sample were selected and pooled, resulting in a combined dataset of 46,963,799 TCRβ chains. These TCRs were clustered using the ultrafast TCR clustering algorithm Geometric Isometry–based TCR AligNment Algorithm (GIANA) (40), which groups TCRs based on similarity of the complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) and TCR variable gene. TCRs within the same cluster are likely to share antigen-specificity and HLA restriction (40, 45). We used GIANA because of the following: (i) It is highly scalable and computationally efficient, enabling clustering of more than 3 million TCR sequences within a reasonable time frame (40, 46); and (ii) in our benchmark using TCR-HLA pairs from the VDJdb database (21), it outperformed other well-known TCR clustering tools [TCRdist (47), GLIPH2 (48), and Hamming distance] in terms of HLA restriction purity, defined as the proportion of TCRs within a cluster that share the same HLA restriction (fig. S1). Specifically, when using high-quality TCR-HLA pairs (quality score ≥ 2), GIANA achieved an overall 99.5% HLA restriction purity, with 99.36% of clusters reaching purity above 90% (fig. S1, C and D), which is well aligned with the assumptions underlying our study. This finding is also confirmed in a recent benchmarking study by Hudson et al. (46). After TCR clustering, we then performed TCR cluster–based enrichment analysis on appropriately sized clusters and identified 9,125,142 significant TCR-HLA pairs (adj_p < 0.05; Fisher’s exact test), which constitutes by far the largest dataset on this topic. We developed THNet, a computational model designed to leverage this large-scale TCR-HLA dataset for both prediction and discovery. THNet can be applied to any high-throughput bulk TCRβ sequencing data for sample HLA typing prediction, offering broad HLA allele coverage without compromising accuracy (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, the HLA functional similarity network was used to perform population clustering and to assess the HLA mismatch tolerance for any given donor-recipient pair across different transplantation types (Fig. 1, C to E).

Identification of HA TCR clusters

The large number of HA TCR clusters allowed us to investigate TCR-HLA cross-reactivity. Notably, the number of HA TCRs varies greatly across different HLA alleles. More HA TCRs tended to be identified in alleles with a larger positive training sample size (Fig. 2A), yet the trend was insignificant (P = 0.053) for class I alleles. This suggested that other factors, such as varying levels of TCR-HLA cross-reactivity, may influence the number of HA TCRs associated with each HLA allele.

Most HA TCRs were associated with more than one allele (Fig. 2B). Specifically, only 44.3% of class I and 37.3% of class II HA TCRs were enriched in a single HLA allele, while 17.4% of class I and 17.0% of class II HA TCRs were enriched in more than four HLA alleles. This finding suggests a high level of TCR-HLA cross-reactivity, where a single TCR can be selected by multiple HLA alleles. However, TCR enrichment across class I and class II HLA alleles was much less common compared to cross-reactivity within the same HLA class (Fig. 2C), consistent with the distinct maturation paths typically associated with CD8+ (class I) and CD4+ (class II) T cells.

The absence of α-β pairing in bulk TCRβ datasets may compromise the precision of TCR-HLA mapping, potentially leading to inflated estimates of cross-reactivity. To assess the potential impact of missing α chain information, we performed additional analyses using a dataset containing both TCRα and TCRβ chains (49). Results show that the level of HLA cross-reactivity was highly similar between α and β chains, with comparable proportions of TCRs associated with more than one HLA allele (Fig. 2D). To further investigate whether pairing with different partner chains influences HLA restriction, we analyzed curated TCR-HLA pairs (quality score ≥ 2) from VDJdb. Pairing diversity (i.e., the number of distinct partner chains for a given α or β chain) did not substantially influence HLA restriction diversity (Fig. 2E). Specifically, we compared the pairing profiles of each chain to its HLA entropy, a metric quantifying the diversity of HLA alleles. A low entropy indicates strong bias toward one or a few HLA alleles, suggesting high specificity, while a high entropy reflects association with a broader range of alleles, indicating potential cross-reactivity. Regions II (low pairing diversity, high HLA entropy) and IV (high pairing diversity, low HLA entropy) both contain more samples than region III (high pairing diversity, high HLA entropy). This result indicates that high pairing diversity does not necessarily lead to high HLA entropy and is unlikely to be a major determinant of TCR-HLA cross-reactivity detected in our study. These findings suggest that β chain–only analyses capture most of HLA-specific signals. This is further supported by results from the HLAGuessr study, which showed that models using both TCRα and TCRβ chains performed only marginally better than those using β chains alone in predicting HLA typing (21).

Next, we investigated whether HLA restriction is determined by the CDR3 region, the V gene, or the combination of both. We paired the CDR3 regions of HA TCRs with various V genes and compared the enrichment levels of different CDR3-V gene combinations. Nearly all CDR3 regions exhibited significant enrichment only when paired with a specific V gene (Fig. 2, F and G). To determine whether the most enriched CDR3-V gene pairs resulted from their population prevalence, we compared the publicity of the most enriched pairs with remaining V gene combination. The results showed that, in most cases, the most enriched CDR3-V gene pair was not the most prevalent combination for that CDR3 (Fig. 2H), confirming that the observed enrichment is driven by the interaction between TCR and HLA, rather than TCR publicity. Similarly, V genes exhibited HLA enrichment only when paired with specific CDR3 regions (fig. S2A). These findings indicate that HLA restriction is determined by the specific combination of both the CDR3 region and the V gene, rather than by either component alone.

Reproducible HLA typing inference from TCR repertoire

To systematically characterize TCR-HLA interactions, we developed THNet, a framework leveraging 9 million significantly associated HLA-TCR pairs. Before exploring the broader implications of TCR-HLA cross-reactivity, we first asked whether the association patterns identified by THNet could be reproduced in unseen individuals. This served as an initial test of whether these HA TCRs capture generalizable, HLA-specific signatures. To do so, we built an L1-regularized logistic regression model for each HLA allele using the corresponding HA TCRs as input features, aiming to predict the presence or absence of that allele based on the individual’s TCR repertoire (see Materials and Methods).

To rigorously evaluate model performance and mitigate the variability introduced by random data splitting, we conducted fivefold cross-validation. All analysis steps, including TCR clustering, TCR-HLA association, and model training, were restricted to the 80% training split. Model performance was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC) of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve on the held-out 20% test set. Most HLA alleles showed strong predictive power on unseen samples, with a median AUC of 0.76 for class I alleles and 0.87 for class II alleles (fig. S3, A and B).

To maximize the performance of the HLA inference model, we used the HA TCRs found from the full dataset as input features to train the final HLA regression models. After training, we evaluated these models using two independent validation cohorts (Nolan and Ravishankar; n = 197) (50, 51). Because the Nolan cohort lacks four-digit HLA typing for class II alleles, model evaluation for class II HLA was performed only on the Ravishankar cohort (n = 134). Specifically, we calculated four metrics (accuracy, precision, sensitivity, and specificity) to assess model performance and compared THNet with the state-of-the-art model HLAGuessr (28). THNet achieved higher sensitivity and border HLA coverage while maintaining comparable precision to HLAGuessr (Fig. 3A and fig. S4A). Furthermore, the AUCs for most HLA class I models remained consistent between the independent cohorts and the cross-validation analysis (fig. S5A). The AUCs for class II models in the independent dataset were generally lower (fig. S5B), which may be attributed to the lower sequencing depth (average number of unique TCRs: 8607) in the Ravishankar cohort.

A key challenge in studying HLA-TCR association is the extreme allelic diversity and low frequency of most HLA alleles, which limits the number of positive training samples. Our TCR cluster–based strategy enabled the identification of substantially more HA TCRs per allele than HLAGuessr (fig. S2B), allowing THNet to detect HA TCR for 109 class I and 99 class II alleles. To further evaluate HLA coverage at the individual level, we compared how many HLA alleles were covered per sample between THNet and HLAGuessr across datasets from three distinct regions (Hong Kong, Mexico, and Russia) with population-specific HLA profiles. THNet covered significantly more HLA alleles across all tested datasets (Fig. 3B), enabling broader population coverage in downstream analyses.

The higher statistical power of THNet enables detection of extensive TCR-HLA cross-reactivity among HA TCRs. While this cross-reactivity likely reflecting genuine biological interactions, it introduces ambiguity during HLA typing predictions, as the presence of a single TCR can suggest multiple potential HLA alleles. To investigate how this cross-reactivity affects model performance, we conducted in silico simulations using cross-reactivity levels representative of both THNet (high cross-reactivity) and HLAGuessr (low cross-reactivity; see Materials and Methods). Model accuracy dropped substantially under high cross-reactivity, regardless of the number of HA TCRs used (fig. S4, B and C). As a result, THNet performed similarly to HLAGuessr in independent cohorts for alleles covered by both methods (fig. S4, D and E). Similarly, when applying the same fivefold cross-validation on the same dataset with the single TCR–based methods used in HLAGuessr, THNet did not outperform HLAGuessr in terms of model precision (fig. S3). This also explains why feature weighting by the regression model is particularly beneficial for THNet, as it helps preserve allele-discriminative signals in the presence of broader TCR-HLA cross-reactivity. When using the phenotypic burden metric, which assumes invariant weight for all the TCRs, THNet still retained substantial predictive power but showed slightly worse performance compared to HLAGuessr (fig. S6). In addition, we evaluated how prediction accuracy saturates with increasing HA TCR counts and observed that the performance plateaus with more than 10,000 HA TCRs (Fig. 3C). This finding is consistent with the minimal gain in performance observed with more HA TCRs in our model.

Given the extensive cross-reactivity observed among HA TCRs, we next examined their biological relevance. Specifically, we tested whether HA TCRs are enriched for HLA-restricted, antigen-specific TCRs that are likely engaged in prior or ongoing immune responses. We analyzed the Nolan cohort, which included annotated COVID-specific TCRs but lacks HLA genotyping for most samples (50). Using THNet, we selected high-confidence HLA predictions from 1414 individuals and applied Fisher’s exact test to evaluate enrichment of COVID-specific TCRs within HA TCRs (see Materials and Methods). Significant enrichment was observed in nearly all tested individuals—99.5% for HLA-A*01:01 and 100% for HLA-A*02:01 (Fig. 3, D and E). Moreover, individuals carrying the relevant HLA allele showed markedly higher odds ratios (OR) compared to those who did not (Fig. 3, D and E), thus confirming the biological relevance of HA TCRs. These results underscored the value of leveraging large-scale TCR-HLA associations to decode structured immune recognition in general population using only TCRβ repertoire data.

TCR-based HLA similarity network

We sought to investigate whether different HLA alleles can select a shared fraction of the TCR repertoire, an indication of potential cross-reactivity. To explore, we constructed an HLA functional similarity network using HA TCRs. Each TCR was transformed into a numerical embedding using our previously developed method (52), and the Hausdorff metric was used to calculate the mismatch level between every pair of HLA alleles as their distance (see Materials and Methods). TCR embeddings allow us to compare repertoires in a way that captures nuanced similarity patterns not apparent from raw sequence identity or direct overlap. Building on this, Hausdorff distance quantifies the maximal divergence between TCR repertoires and enables detection of subtle yet meaningful interallelic differences by emphasizing boundary-level mismatches in the embedding space. The resulting distances were normalized to a 0-to-1 scale (Fig. 4, A and B), where higher values indicate greater dissimilarity of the two TCR repertoires and, consequently, lower degree of cross-reactivity. Our analysis revealed that class I HLA alleles exhibit higher cross-reactivity with each other compared to class II HLA alleles. The average distance among class I HLA alleles was 0.72, while the average distances for class II HLA α and β chains were 0.79 and 0.83, respectively. Intragroup allele pairs (same two-digit group) and intergroup pairs (different two-digit groups) showed largely comparable distances across all class I and class II loci (fig. S7, A and B), with no consistent trend of intragroup pairs being more similar. This observation indicates that two-digit HLA allele grouping, while useful for summarizing alleles by sequence similarity, may not necessarily translate to functional similarity at the TCR repertoire level.

Next, we evaluated the stability of our HLA similarity network. Because the number of TCRs associated with an HLA allele can vary considerably with factors such as allele frequency and the threshold used to define HLA-TCR associations, we tested whether this variation could bias the network structure. To do so, we performed bootstrapping analyses by randomly selecting 1000 HLA allele pairs for each sampling ratio from 10 to 90%, with 10 repeated trials per ratio. We computed the Pearson’s correlation between subsampled and full-sample distances and evaluated the relative error of the subsampled estimates. When the sampling ratio reached 50%, the correlation exceeded 0.95, and the relative error dropped below 0.01 (fig. S7, C and D). These results demonstrate that the similarity network is highly robust to differences in HA TCR counts across HLA alleles.

We further investigated the relationship between HLA distances and their peptide-binding capacities. To begin, we explored the peptide-binding motifs of the two most dissimilar and two most similar HLA pairs (Fig. 4, C and D). Peptide-binding motifs were obtained from naturally presented ligands predicted using the NetMHCpan-4.1 online server (53). The most dissimilar class I HLA pair, HLA-B*27:05 and HLA-B*56:01, exhibited distinct peptide-binding features. Arginine was the dominant anchor amino acid at position 1 for HLA-B*27:05, whereas proline dominated the same position for HLA-B*56:01. These two amino acids differ substantially in structure, chemical properties, and biological functions, leading to distinct peptides presented by these HLA alleles and, consequently, distinct TCR repertoires. Similarly, the second most dissimilar pair, HLA-B*40:01 and HLA-B*56:01, displayed exclusive peptide-binding motifs at anchor position 1 (Fig. 4C). In contrast, the two most similar HLA pairs, HLA-B*15:02 and HLA-A*43:01 and HLA-C*02:10 and HLA-A*23:17, shared highly similar peptide-binding motifs without contradictory dominant anchor amino acids (Fig. 4D).

To validate these individual observations on a larger scale, we analyzed a dataset of 139,020 experimentally validated pHLA pairs from the Siranush cohort (54) that met the prediction criteria of NetMHCpan-4.1 (53) (see Materials and Methods). For each pHLA pair, we replaced the HLA allele with its closest and farthest counterparts defined by THNet and then predicted peptide-binding affinity using NetMHCpan-4.1. Peptides presented by a given HLA allele tend to show higher binding affinity to HLA alleles that are closer to that allele, compared to more distant ones (fig. S7E). Close HLA counterparts also shared a higher proportion of predicted peptide binders compared to more distant HLA pairs (fig. S7F). These results demonstrate that the HLA similarity network, defined from TCR outputs, partly reflects the similarity of peptide presentation across HLA alleles.

HLA-based population clustering and disease association

Next, we used our HLA similarity network to cluster populations into distinct groups based on their class I and class II HLA genotypes. Given that certain HLA alleles are well-established risk factors for autoimmune disorders, such as HLA-DRB1*03-DQB1*02:01 in type 1 diabetes and HLA-B27 in ankylosing spondylitis (55, 56), we reasoned that grouping functionally similar alleles into clusters could increase statistical power for detecting unique disease associations. In this analysis, we took advantage of approximately half of a million samples from the UK Biobank database (57). We first selected samples with complete HLA information and then grouped samples with identical HLA genotypes together, resulting in 65,323 and 36,597 unique class I and class II genotypes, respectively. To quantify the difference between an HLA genotype and others in the dataset, we computed the sum of its pairwise distances to all other genotypes using the HLA similarity network defined by THNet (see Materials and Methods). For class I HLA clustering, this process generated a 65,323 × 65,323 matrix (and a 36,597 × 36,597 matrix for class II HLA clustering), which was visualized in t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) plots (Fig. 5A and fig. S8A). The samples from different ethnic backgrounds are well separated, indicating distinct HLA allele distributions in different populations. We selected three major populations (White, Asian, and Black) for further analysis to avoid race-driven biases in the downstream analysis. Individuals with functionally similar HLA genotypes were grouped into 350, 140, and 140 clusters for White, Asian, and Black populations, respectively, balancing per-cluster sample size with clustering stability (fig. S9). Disease enrichment for each cluster was evaluated using Fisher’s exact test, and P values were adjusted for multiple testing.

Significant disease associations of specific HLA clusters were identified in all tested populations, for both class I and class II HLAs (Fig. 5, B to D, and fig. S8, B to D). For example, samples in cluster 118 exhibited a higher risk of celiac disease (CD) compared to those outside the cluster (Fig. 5B). All significant class I and class II HLA cluster disease associations for populations with different ethnic backgrounds are provided in the Supplementary Materials. The dataset included 416,967 White samples, but only 5615 Asian and 3751 Black samples, leading to some notably high OR in smaller populations, such as the association of cluster 134 with bone cancer in Black British samples (OR = 748.4; Fig. 5D). Because of the larger sample size, OR estimates for the White population are likely more robust and reflective of true disease associations. Consequently, most downstream analyses were focused on the White population.

We examined whether the HLA clusters were confounded by HLA haplotypes, where certain HLA pairs in linkage disequilibrium (LD) tend to be inherited together more frequently than expected. Our results indicate that nearly all tested individuals carry at least one HLA haplotype (98.8% for HLA class I and 100% for HLA class II loci; fig. S10A; see Materials and Methods). However, fewer clusters are predominantly composed of HLA alleles in LD (26.0% for class I and 46.6% for class II loci; fig. S10A). Among the disease-associated HLA clusters, 29.1% of class I HLA clusters and 65.5% of class II HLA clusters are dominated by HLA haplotypes in LD (fig. S10B). Notably, even within these LD-dominated disease-associated clusters, disease enrichment exhibited significantly higher OR compared to using HLA haplotypes alone (fig. S10C). These results suggested that our TCR-based distance measure captures functional HLA similarities despite the presence of haplotypes.

We next defined two alleles to have high disease-association similarity if they share significant associations with more than five diseases. The distances between these HLA pairs were significantly shorter compared to pairs with low disease-association overlap (Fig. 5E). In addition, individuals in the clusters with HLA genotypes closer to disease-risk alleles exhibited higher risks for the associated diseases, where a “disease-risk HLA allele” was defined as the allele most significantly associated with a disease in the dataset. The distance between an HLA cluster and a risk HLA allele was calculated as the mean weighted sum of pairwise distances defined by THNet (see Materials and Methods). Clusters in closer proximity to risk HLA alleles, such as HLA-B*08:01 for CD and HLA-DRB1*04:04 for polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR), tended to show higher disease enrichment compared to more distant clusters (Fig. 5F). This observation was generally true to both class I and class II alleles, suggesting that THNet-derived HLA similarity captures meaningful patterns of HLA-disease association (Fig. 5G).

Furthermore, we found that advantage of HLA cluster over single allele in disease association was more obvious among the Black population. While the P values of single-HLA enrichment and HLA cluster–based approaches showed similar distributions in White and Asian populations, cluster-based approach identified more significant HLA-disease associations in Black people (Fig. 5H). This may be attributed to the shorter LD blocks in individuals of African ancestry (58), making cluster-based approaches more effective in grouping functionally relevant allelic combinations and capturing disease associations. For example, the HLA class I cluster 59 and class II cluster 72 exhibited the strongest disease associations in the Black population (Fig. 5, I and J). They were enriched for samples with hemoglobinopathy and lupus, respectively. Notably, none of the composing HLA alleles within these clusters showed significant disease enrichment (Fig. 5, I and J). In summary, the use of HLA clusters defined by the THNet enables the identification of disease associations at the whole HLA genotype level, uncovering unique HLA disease susceptibilities that cannot be detected through single-allele analyses.

HLA mismatch score as an indicator of transplantation outcome

We next extended the HLA similarity analysis to quantitatively assess HLA mismatches during transplantation. Fewer HLA mismatches between donor and recipient are generally associated with better transplantation outcomes and longer survival (59–61). Furthermore, the clinical impact of mismatches is depending on the similarity between HLA alleles, where mismatches involving more similar HLA alleles are typically more tolerable than those between highly distinctive alleles (61, 62). Eplet score is the most commonly used tool for HLA similarity evaluation, which quantifies differences in surface amino acids of HLA molecules between donors and recipients (63, 64). It assumed that mismatched HLA alleles with greater structural differences are more likely to elicit donor-specific B cell responses that may lead to graft failure (64, 65). In contrast, HLA similarity defined in our study is based on its associated TCR repertoire, which might complement the B cell–focused eplet method, and provide an orthogonal perspective on the role of HLA mismatch in transplantation outcomes.

To explore, we analyzed the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (SRTR) database (66), which has 923,533 recorded entries on donors and recipients of organ transplantation in the United States since 1987. A total of 84,816 entries with HLA genotypes of both donors and recipients, as well as transplantation outcomes, was included in the analysis. Because high-resolution HLA alleles were unavailable for most individuals in this dataset, we imputed four-digit HLA alleles from two-digit records within specific populations (see Materials and Methods). Samples containing unpredictable HLA alleles were excluded from subsequent analysis. Because patient outcomes were significantly associated with the race of both donors and recipients (fig. S11), we focused our analysis on the White population due to its largest sample size.

We first studied kidney transplant cases given their highest abundance among transplantation types, with 3234 passed-filter entries. The THNet mismatch score (MS) was calculated as the sum of the mean pairwise distances against recipient for each donor-specific HLA allele, separately for class I and class II (Fig. 6A; see Materials and Methods). THNet MS was independent of the eplet score when conditioned on the number of HLA mismatches (fig. S12). Class I MS demonstrated superior predictive power for kidney graft failure compared to the eplet, while the case was the opposite for class II (Fig. 6B). We thus combined the THNet class I MS and the eplet class II score by selecting the greater value of the two, assuming a competitive relationship between T cell– and B cell–mediated rejection (67). This predictor provided optimal stratification of patients based on the timing of graft failure (Fig. 6C). In addition, the combined score displayed a higher hazard ratio (HR = 1.33; P < 0.001) compared to eplet alone (HR = 1.04; P = 0.005) when adjusted for potential confounders such as HLA mismatch number (MN), age, body mass index (BMI), and gender (Fig. 6D).

We then assessed whether the THNet MS could be applied to other types of organ transplantation. Lung and liver transplant data were combined into a single cohort of 1612 pairs from the SRTR dataset to increase power. Consistent with kidney transplant findings, the THNet class I MS displayed significantly better predictive power for graft failure than eplet score (Fig. 6E), while neither approach’s class II MS was significant (fig. S13C). To this end, THNet class I MS was the only indicator consistently associated with patients’ survival across all organ transplantation types investigated (fig. S13, A and B). Notably, the combined MS failed to improve predictive value in this setting, likely because the eplet-based class II MS lacked predictive power for lung and liver transplants.

In addition, we tested the applicability of the THNet in hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) using a public cohort in the Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research (CIBMTR) database (68), which contained high-resolution HLA typing for both donors and recipients. A total 594 transplantation entries passed our selection criteria (see Materials and Methods). THNet class II MS (P = 0.0145) was a better predictor of graft rejection compared to the class II eplet score (P = 0.2631; Fig. 6F), while the class I score of neither method demonstrated an association with graft rejection rates (fig. S13D). Overall, these findings highlight the utility of the THNet MS as a complementary tool to eplet, providing improved stratification of patients and enabling a more comprehensive assessment of donor-recipient immune compatibility across different transplantation settings.

Last, we evaluated the performance of an alternative THNet construction and the peptide-centric HLA functional similarity network MHCcluster for the same applications above. Specifically, we redefined THNet distances using the Jaccard index between associated HA TCRs of each HLA allele (hereafter referred to as Jaccard THNet; tables S4 and S5). Unlike embedding-based Materials and Methods, the Jaccard index considers only exact TCR overlaps, which yields lower sensitivity in low cross-reactivity settings but may increase the specificity in other scenarios. Specifically, 56.9% of class II HLA allele pairs share no HA TCRs (Jaccard distance = 1), rendering them indistinguishable under this metric. Meanwhile, MHCcluster is based on NetMHCpan-predicted peptide-binding profiles, which are optimized for class I alleles but show reduced accuracy for class II molecules (69). Because of these limitations, all comparisons involving Jaccard THNet and MHCcluster were restricted to class I HLA alleles. Jaccard THNet showed moderate to high correlation with the original THNet, whereas MHCcluster displayed low concordance (fig. S14, A and B). In disease-association analyses using the UK Biobank database, class I Jaccard THNet distances demonstrated stronger relevance to HLA-disease associations compared to the original THNet, while MHCcluster-derived distances showed no significant association (fig. S14, C and D). For transplantation outcomes, both Jaccard THNet and MHCcluster showed lower predictive power for rejection rates and posttransplant survival across different transplant types compared with the original THNet (fig. S14, E to H). These findings suggest that while alternative THNet constructions emphasize different aspects of HLA similarity, the overall TCR-based HLA similarity network may better reflect T cell–driven immune outcomes than peptide-centric approaches such as MHCcluster.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we conducted a large-scale investigation of HLA cross-reactivity of TCRs by leveraging an extensive dataset of more than 9 million TCR-HLA pairs. The reproducibility of the TCR cross-reactivity was successfully validated in unseen samples. On the basis of this result, we quantitatively estimated the shared TCR repertoires of different HLA alleles and defined the HLA similarity network. This network enabled the discovery of unique HLA-disease susceptibilities in the UK Biobank database. Moreover, MS defined by HLA distances was shown to be a predictor of graft rejection in both organ transplantation and HSCT.

Defining the HLA restriction of a given TCR is challenging because TCR recognition involves both HLA and the antigen peptide. Conventionally, the assessment of HLA restriction for a given TCR would require binding experiments against all available HLA alleles and the entire peptide pool, which is nearly impossible. Here, we defined TCR-HLA associations based on the significant co-occurrence patterns of TCR clusters among individuals with particular HLA alleles, akin to taking a glimpse at the results of “binding test” performed by the immune system in vivo. The resulting TCR-HLA associations do not represent the absolute HLA restriction of each TCR but rather reflect the outcomes of immune selection shaped by real-life antigen encounters. This approach may provide even more relevant insights into disease contexts, as it emphasizes functionally relevant immune interactions over theoretical HLA restrictions.

Different distance metrics highlight different aspects of TCR repertoire similarity and might offer advantages in different application scenarios when constructing HLA functional similarity networks. The Hausdorff metric measures the maximal-minimal distance between two sets of TCRs, emphasizing the degree of disagreement between HLA alleles. This makes it particularly suitable for applications such as predicting transplantation outcomes, where the extent of HLA mismatch plays a critical role. In contrast, the Jaccard index focuses on the overlapping part of TCR repertoires and may therefore be more relevant for capturing shared disease associations between HLA alleles. In addition, we assessed the performance of the peptide-centric network MHCcluster, which showed limited utility across the applications examined in this study. This may, in part, be due to its reliance on an outdated version of NetMHCpan, given that MHCcluster was originally developed more than a decade ago (36, 39). Updating MHCcluster with more recent versions of NetMHCpan could potentially improve its performance in future work.

In our analysis, we observed distinct performance between class I and class II THNet MS across organ transplantation and HSCT, likely due to the differing mechanisms of graft rejection. In organ transplantation, T cell–mediated rejection is primarily driven by a host-versus-graft response, where mature CD8+ T cells serve as the key effectors attacking the transplanted organ (70, 71). In contrast, HSCT rejection is predominantly a graft-versus-host response, in which donor CD4+ T cells must first be educated by the host’s thymic cells before initiating a broader immune response against host tissues (72, 73). As a result, class I mismatches may have a more important impact in organ transplantation, whereas class II mismatches could be more detrimental in HSCT, particularly in the context of T cell–mediated rejection.

There are several limitations of this work. First, the number of predictable HLA alleles remains limited compared with the vast allelic diversity identified to date. In addition, the accuracy for rare alleles is constrained by the small number of positive samples. Expanding TCR datasets with high-resolution HLA genotypes will be essential to enlarge the pool of predictable alleles and improve model performance. Second, the identified TCR-HLA pairs may be influenced by the presence of HLA haplotypes. While the observed association is actually driven by one HLA allele, and the other alleles in LD may also be labeled as associated with the same TCR. Last, this study was conducted using bulk TCRβ chain sequencing data without knowledge of the paired α chains, which limits the ability to fully characterize TCR-HLA interactions. While our results in Fig. 2 (D and E) help mitigate this concerns, single-cell TCR sequencing (scTCR-seq) remains the most ideal data source for capturing complete TCR-HLA interactions. However, current scTCR-seq datasets are still limited in size and coverage for large-scale population-level analyses. Continued advances in scTCR-seq technology with enhanced scalability and cost efficiency will be essential to overcoming this barrier and further refining our understanding of TCR-HLA interactions.

In summary, we presented THNet, a TCR-based HLA similarity network built upon HA TCR clusters, which reveals previously unrecognized cross-reactivity patterns across HLA alleles. By leveraging TCR-HLA functional relationships, this approach enhanced our understanding of immune cross-reactivity, shedding light on disease susceptibility and graft rejection. Our findings underscore the potential of THNet to advance precision medicine by elucidating the role of HLA alleles in shaping clinical outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental design

In the first step, we complied five publicly available dataset with high-resolution HLA information, totaling 1603 samples. Among them, 1405 samples contained complete class I and class II HLA data, while 198 samples had only class I HLA data (29, 41–44). Quality control (QC) was performed on the TCR sequences based on two criteria: (i) The TCR length must fall between 9 and 23 amino acids, and (ii) the TCR CDR3 regions must start with cysteine (C) and end with phenylalanine (F). Following QC, we selected the top 30,000 most highly expanded T cell clones, as determined by clonal frequency, to reduce the computational burden of TCR clustering. The resulting TCR sequences were aggregated into a TCR pool comprising 46,963,799 unique sequences. To further reduce computational complexity, the TCR pool was randomly divided into 12 subgroups, each containing fewer than 4 million TCR reads. GIANA TCR clustering was then performed on each subgroup (40). To enhance the number of clustered TCRs, the TCR pool was reshuffled three times, and the clustering process was repeated for each iteration. All resulting TCR clusters were subsequently integrated. To ensure the validity of downstream analyses, we applied additional filtering steps. Only clusters with fewer than 2000 TCRs were retained, under the rationale that larger clusters are less likely to adhere to the assumption that TCRs within the same cluster share the same HLA restriction. Clusters that appeared in fewer than five samples were also excluded because of insufficient statistical power for HLA enrichment analysis. After filtering, 456,315 TCR clusters were retained.

HLA enrichment analysis

We performed HLA enrichment analysis at the TCR-cluster level. A cluster was considered present in a sample if at least one TCR within the cluster was detected in that sample. If multiple TCRs from the same cluster appeared in a single sample, then each occurrence was counted separately. The total occurrences for each cluster in relation to each HLA allele were then summed to represent the overall association value of the cluster with that allele. By aggregating all clusters, we constructed a matrix where the rows represented clusters, the columns represented HLA alleles, and each cell contained the occurrence value for a specific cluster-HLA allele pair. Next, we applied Fisher’s exact test to assess the enrichment of each cluster-HLA allele pair, using “greater” as the alternative hypothesis. The resulting P values for all cluster-HLA allele pairs were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate (FDR) correction, performed separately for each HLA allele. Cluster-HLA allele pairs with an adjusted P value less than 0.05 were considered significantly associated.

The constriction of HLA typing inference models

The significantly associated TCRs for each HLA allele were used to build a separate logistic regression model with L1 regularization for each HLA allele. In these models, the presence of each TCR was assigned a weight, indicating how much the occurrence of that TCR contributes to the likelihood of the corresponding HLA allele being present. The rationale behind these models is to predict whether a sample contains a specific HLA allele based on the presence of its corresponding TCRs in the TCR repertoire. The probability formula for each HLA allele is as followsP(samplehas this HLAallele)=11+e−(wTx+b)where w is the vector of learned coefficients for each TCR feature, x is the input feature vector ( x = 1 if the feature TCR is present; x = 0 otherwise), and b is the bias term learned during training. The combined dataset was split into 80% for training and 20% for testing. For the final HLA inference model, the model was trained on 80% of the data by maximizing the log likelihood, with the L1 regularization strength selected on the basis of performance on the remaining 20% testing set. The decision thresholds were determined by maximizing the F score on the combined training and testing data.

Simulation-based assessment of factors affecting HLA typing prediction

To investigate how key variables influence the accuracy of HLA inference, we designed a series of simulation experiments focusing on class I HLA alleles. These simulations aimed to evaluate the impact of two major factors: the total number of HA TCRs used for training and their degree of cross-reactivity, i.e., the number of distinct HLA alleles each TCR is associated with.

In each simulation, we began by specifying a total number of HA TCRs to insert into the training set, which consisted of 1603 individuals with known HLA genotypes. For each HA TCR, we sampled an HLA assignment pattern according to a predefined cross-reactivity distribution and randomly selected the corresponding HLA alleles. We then simulated pseudo-TCR repertoires by inserting these HA TCRs into individual samples with the following rule: If the sample carried the associated HLA allele, then the TCR was inserted with a 10% probability; otherwise, it would be inserted with a 5% probability. This procedure captures the modest enrichment signal typically observed in real TCR-HLA data, where TCRs associated with a given HLA tend to appear more frequently in relevant samples, but some background noise remains.

We evaluated two cross-reactivity conditions. The high cross-reactivity distribution, estimated from THNet-derived HA TCRs, consisted of 44.3% of TCRs associated with one HLA allele, 21.3% with two, 10.9% with three, 6.2% with four, 4.0% with five, 2.9% with six, and 10.4% with more than six. The low cross-reactivity distribution, based on HLAGuessr, had 68.7% of TCRs associated with one allele, 24.6% with two, 6.7% with three, and only 0.1% with more than three. These distributions were derived from the respective HA TCR sets identified by each model.

We carried out three simulation experiments. First, we fixed the number of HA TCRs at 10,000 and compared model performance under the two cross-reactivity levels. Second, we simulated pseudo-repertoires using method-specific HA TCR counts, 5653 for HLAGuessr and 1,000,000 for THNet, combined with their corresponding cross-reactivity distributions, to reflect the theoretical model performance of each framework. Third, we examined how increasing the number of HA TCRs affects model performance under a fixed THNet-derived cross-reactivity distribution using HA TCR counts of 100, 1000, 10,000, 100,000, and 1,000,000.

In all experiments, we trained logistic regression models with L1 regularization (penalty = “l1,” solver = “saga,” max_iter = 1000) on 80% of the simulated data and evaluated performance on the remaining 20%, using per-allele model AUC as the evaluation metric.

The enrichment of HLA-restricted antigen-specific TCRs among HA TCRs

To test the association between HA TCRs and HLA-restricted antigen-specific TCRs, we analyzed severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2)–specific TCRs from the Nolan cohort (50). The sample source of the TCRs used in the multiplexed identification experiment was available in the original study (50). All analyses in this section were conducted in an HLA allele-specific manner, with HLA-A*01:01 used here as an illustrative example. HLA-A*01:01–restricted antigen-specific TCRs were defined on the basis of two criteria: (i) The TCRs originated from samples carrying the HLA-A*01:01 allele, and (ii) the TCRs recognized peptides presented by HLA-A*01:01 molecules as predicted by NetMHCpan-4.1. We then applied Fisher’s exact test to assess whether HLA-A*01:01–restricted antigen-specific TCRs were significantly enriched among HLA-A*01:01–associated TCRs compared to non–HLA-A*01:01–associated TCRs in each sample. Furthermore, we used THNet to infer the presence of the HLA-A*01:01 allele in samples, selecting only those with high-confidence predictions (probability < 0.01 as HLA-A*01:01–negative and probability > 0.99 as HLA-A*01:01–positive). Last, Welch’s t test was used to compare the OR of HLA-A*01:01–restricted antigen-specific TCR enrichment between HLA-A*01:01–positive and HLA-A*01:01–negative samples.

The HLA similarity network by Hausdorff distance

The HA TCRs were first converted into a numeric embedding using a method similar to the repertoire functional unit approach (52), incorporating additional CDR1, CDR2, and CDR2.5 regions. The combined CDR1/2/2.5 region along with the CDR3 regions was each embedded into a 500-dimensional vector, which were then concatenated to form the final embedding for a TCR sequence. Each HLA allele was then represented by an n*1000 matrix, where n is the number of HA TCRs associated with that HLA allele and 1000 is the vector length of each TCR read. To quantify the distance between two HLA alleles, we used the Hausdorff distance (74), which is defined asdH(HLA1,HLA2)=max{h(HLA1,HLA2),h(HLA2,HLA1)}where h(HLA1,HLA2) computes the maximum of the shortest distances from each HLA1-associated TCR to its nearest HLA2-associated TCR in the embedding space and vice versa for h(HLA2,HLA1) . The Hausdorff distance between two HLA alleles is given by the maximum of these two values. The distances between all tested HLA alleles were computed separately for HLA class I and HLA class II. The resulting distance values were then normalized to the range of 0 to 1 using min-max normalization.

The construction of Jaccard THNet

We defined the Jaccard THNet by computing the Jaccard index for each pair of alleles HLA1 and HLA2 representing the size of the intersection of their HA TCR sets divided by the size of their unionJ(HLA1,HLA2)=∣S1∩S2∣∣S1∪S2∣

Where S1 and S2 denote their respective sets of HA TCRs. This yielded a symmetric HLA-HLA similarity matrix, in which each entry reflected the proportion of shared HA TCRs between two alleles.

Peptide-binding pattern between HLA pairs with different distance

To compare the peptide-binding patterns of HLA pairs with different distances, we used a dataset containing experimentally validated peptide binders for various HLA molecules. For each known HLA-peptide pair, we substituted the original HLA allele with a closely or distantly related counterpart and assessed its binding affinity to the original peptide using NetMHCpan-4.1 (53). The rank scores (Rnk_EL) were used to quantify the binding affinity of the tested HLA-peptide interactions, with a binding threshold set at 2% to classify peptides as HLA binders. Because of the limited predictive accuracy of NetMHCpan for HLA class II alleles, this analysis was conducted exclusively for HLA class I alleles.

Population clustering based on HLA genotype

To cluster the population into smaller subgroups based on their HLA genotypes, we first computed a sample distance matrix, which is an n-by-n matrix, where n is the number of samples. Each value in the matrix represents the distance between a given sample and another sample. The sample-wise distance between two samples, Sa and Sb, is calculated asD(Sa,Sb)=∑i=1n1∑j=1n1d(Hai,Hbj)where D(Sa,Sb) denotes the overall distance between sample Sa and sample Sb; n1 represents the number of HLA alleles per sample ( n1 = 6 for class I HLA alleles and n1 = 10 for class II HLA alleles); and d(Hai,Hbj) is the distance between HLA allele Hai of sample Sa and HLA allele Hbj of sample Sb , as defined by THNet. Thus, the sample-wise distance is computed as the sum of all pairwise distances between HLA alleles of the two samples. Once the sample distance matrix was obtained, we applied agglomerative hierarchical clustering to group similar samples. Each sample initially formed its own cluster, and clusters were iteratively merged on the basis of their closest neighbors until the final clustering structure was achieved. If a genotype was shared by samples from different racial backgrounds, then the color in the Fig. 5A and fig. S5A was assigned on the basis of the most frequent race within that genotype.

Identification of HLA haplotypes in LD

HLA haplotypes in LD were identified on the basis of two criteria: (i) a “D” metric greater than 0.5 and (ii) an adjusted P value less than 0.05. The D value quantifies LD by measuring the nonrandom co-occurrence of two HLA alleles (75). It is calculated asD=PAB−PAPBwhere PAB is the observed frequency of the two HLA alleles A and B occurring together and PA and PB are their respective marginal frequencies. To standardize D , we compute D′ asD′=DDmax,Dmax={min(PAPb,PaPB),D>0min(PAPB,PaPb),D<0where Pa = 1 – PA and Pb = 1 − PB are the complementary allele frequencies. The D value ranges from −1 to 1, with positive values indicating an increased co-occurrence of alleles A and B and negative values indicating lower-than-expected co-occurrence. To ensure the robustness of identified HLA pairs in LD, we applied Fisher’s exact test, followed by FDR correction. Only allele pairs with a D value greater than 0.5 and a significant adjusted P value were classified as HLA pairs in LD, and allele pairs with co-occurrence in fewer than five samples were excluded from the analysis. We defined an HLA cluster as LD dominated if the two most frequent HLA alleles within the cluster are HLA haplotypes in LD.

Distance calculation between HLA clusters and disease-risk HLA alleles

To quantify the distance between each HLA cluster and a disease-associated HLA allele, we first analyzed the disease association of all HLA alleles using Fisher’s exact test. After applying FDR correction, for each tested disease, the most significantly enriched HLA allele with an OR > 2 was defined as a risk HLA allele. Next, we computed the distance between each HLA cluster and the identified risk HLA allele using the following formulaD(Ca,Hb)=∑i=1n1wai·d(Hai,Hb),Hai≠Hbwhere D(Ca,Hb) denotes the overall distance between HLA cluster Ca and risk HLA allele Hb, n1 represents the number of HLA alleles per cluster (only HLA alleles that appear in at least 25% of class I clusters and 50% for class II clusters were included), d(Hai,Hb) is the HLA allele distance between HLA allele Hai in cluster Ca and the risk HLA allele Hb , and wai represents the frequency of allele Hai within cluster Ca, serving as a weight in the calculation. If the risk HLA allele Hb is already present in HLA cluster Ca , then it would be excluded from the computation. Thus, the final distance between an HLA cluster and a disease-associated HLA allele is obtained as the weighted sum of pairwise distances between all HLA alleles in the cluster and the risk HLA allele.

The definition of the THNet MS score

The THNet MS quantifies the HLA distance between a donor and a recipient by evaluating the donor-specific HLA alleles, which are alleles that appear only in the donor but are absent in the recipient. If no donor-specific HLA alleles exist, then the THNet MS is set to zero. For class I HLA alleles, HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C are considered, while for class II HLA alleles, only HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQB1 are included, as these have been shown to be most associated with transplantation outcomes in previous studies (3). The distance between the donor-specific HLA allele set D and the recipient HLA allele set R is calculated asD(R,D)=∑Hd∈Dd¯(Hd,R)=∑Hd∈D1∣R∣∑Hr∈Rd(Hd,Hr)where D(R,D) represents the overall HLA distance between the donor D and the recipient R , d¯(Hd,R) represents the mean distance of each donor-specific HLA allele Hd to all recipient HLA alleles, d(Hd,Hr) is the pairwise HLA distance between donor-specific allele Hd and recipient allele Hr , and ∣R∣ is the number of HLA alleles in the recipient. Thus, THNet MS is defined as the sum of the average distances of all donor-specific HLA alleles to recipient HLA alleles.

Eplet score and combined score

The eplet scores of the organ transplantation data were calculated using the HLAMatchmaker service (76), specifically the ABC Eplet Matching Program V4.0 for class I HLA alleles and the DRDQDP Eplet Matching Program V3.1 for class II HLA alleles. The number of antibody-verified eplets was used as the eplet score for both class I and class II HLA loci. For HSCT transplantation, the eplet scores were obtained directly from their original study (68). To facilitate comparison, both the MS score and the eplet score were normalized to the same scale using z-score transformation. The combined score was then defined as the maximum of the two scores for each transplantation entry, based on the assumption that T cell– and B cell–mediated graft rejection operate in a competitive relationship.

Transplantation data

This study used data from the SRTR (66). The SRTR data system includes data on all donor, wait-listed candidates, and transplant recipients in the US, submitted by the members of the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN). The Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA), US Department of Health and Human Services, provides oversight to the activities of the OPTN and SRTR contractors. The CIBMTR dataset is primarily funded by the Public Health Service (U24CA076518) from the National Cancer Institute, as well as grants from the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute; the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases; HRSA (75R60222C00011); the Office of Naval Research (N00014-23-1-2057 and N00014-24-1-2507); the National Marrow Donor Program; and the Medical College of Wisconsin. In addition, for the SRTR dataset, four-digit HLA alleles were inferred on the basis of the most frequent allele within each corresponding two-digit HLA superfamily in the White population (77).

Statistical analysis

All significance assessments in the box plots throughout the paper were performed using Welch’s t test, which assumes normality but does not require equal variance between the two comparison groups. In addition, all significance assessments in the scatterplots were calculated using Pearson correlation. All multiple testing in this study was corrected using the BH FDR procedure.
