A minimalist [2]rotaxane with orthogonal Li+ and F− binding sites and differential fluorescence response
Authors: Hazem Amarne, Eyad A. Younes, Fatemeh Salami, Stephen J. Loeb, Yuming Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A minimalist [2]rotaxane, comprising a benzimidazole-dimesitylborane axle mechanically interlocked with a crown ether macrocycle, was designed and synthesized as a dual-responsive heteroditopic receptor. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction unambiguously confirmed the interlocked architecture, revealing hydrogen-bonding interactions between the imidazolyl N–H donor and the crown ether oxygen atoms. 1H NMR binding studies demonstrated that the rotaxane binds Li+ ions at a cooperative binding pocket formed by the crown ether and the imidazole unit, while F− ions bind selectively at the Lewis acidic boron center via formation of a stable fluoroborate species under a slow-exchange regime. Competition experiments established that Li+ binding thermodynamically prevails over F− binding, leading to quantitative displacement of fluoride upon addition of a lithium salt. Density functional theory (DFT) and molecular dynamics (MD) calculations were performed to further examine the 1 : 1 binding complexes for both ionic guests. Fluorescence spectroscopy revealed markedly distinct signal responses: Li+ binding induced a 4.6-fold fluorescence enhancement at 456 nm attributable to rigidification of the molecular framework, whereas F− binding resulted in appreciable quenching observed upon addition of a stoichiometric amount of F− ion. Importantly, subsequent addition of Li+ to the pre-formed [1 + F−] adduct restored the fluorescence. The mutual exclusivity of the two binding events and the differential fluorescence outputs render this rotaxane a functional prototype for molecular logic operations, demonstrating that sophisticated dual responsiveness can be realized within a structurally concise mechanically interlocked framework.

Introduction

The active pursuit of miniaturization and efficiency in information processing has driven scientific exploration from the silicon chip to the molecular scale. Molecular electronics and computing promise to overcome the physical limitations of conventional semiconductors by harnessing molecules as active components for logic operations, data storage, and signal transduction.1–3 Among various candidates, mechanically interlocked molecules (MIMs), such as rotaxanes and catenanes,4–8 stand out due to their inherent nanoscale moving parts—the ability of their components to undergo large-amplitude, stimuli-responsive mechanical motions.9–12 This makes them ideal prototypes for developing molecular switches and machines, where the controlled shuttling of a macrocycle along a molecular axle can be coupled to readable optical or electronic outputs.13,14

The topological constraints and associated structural and dynamic properties of MIMs are particularly useful for developing functional molecular devices, ranging from molecular machines and motors to stimuli-responsive materials and sensors.15–18 In molecular recognition and sensing, the mechanical bonds in MIMs facilitate the preorganization of binding sites while enabling adaptive conformational dynamics through unconstrained motion of the interlocked components.19 For example, rotaxane-based hosts have been shown to exhibit enhanced binding affinities and selectivities compared to their non-interlocked analogues.20–23 Moreover, the binding of molecular guests to MIMs can trigger distinct changes in electrochemical and photophysical properties, providing signaling mechanisms that enable the design of more sophisticated devices.13,24–27 A prominent example is molecular logic systems, in which responsiveness to multiple, orthogonal stimuli supports complex binary operations that mimic the function of semiconductor logic gates.28

Despite more than two decades of study, the number of molecular logic gates based on fluorescence-responsive rotaxanes remains limited.26,29–32 Most reported systems require complex structures and multiple control inputs. From a practical perspective, a fundamental question naturally arises: can such systems be built following a minimalist design principle?33 To address this, we herein report our study of a benzimidazole-triarylborane-based rotaxane (1, Fig. 1A). The target compound is synthetically concise, containing the minimal number of components essential for a rotaxane to respond to two distinct external stimuli. The axle unit of rotaxane 1 features two relatively bulky stoppers—a dibromobenzimidazole and a dimesitylborane unit—covalently linked via a p-phenylene bridge. The macrocyclic component is a large crown ether that engages with the benzimidazole moiety through hydrogen bonding. Previous work demonstrated that the dibromobenzimidazole stopper pairs effectively with a 24-crown-8 ether macrocycle to afford stable rotaxane systems, which exhibit no evidence of de-threading.34 This relatively simple rotaxane possesses two active, orthogonal binding sites: (i) the crown ether, in cooperation with the imidazole group, serves as a ligand for alkali metal ions. We anticipated that the lithium cation (Li+), given its size and coordination geometry, would be particularly well matched to this system. (ii) The boron center exhibits strong Lewis acidity. Although embedded in a sterically hindered environment, it can still readily complex with a small Lewis base such as fluoride anion (F−).35–39

Fig. 1B and C depict the binding modes of 1 with Li+ and F− ions, respectively. In the first binding scenario, synergistic coordination of Li+ with the crown ether and imidazole restricts the rotary dynamics of the macrocyclic component. In the second binding event, the sp2-hybridized boron center is converted into a more congested sp3-hybridized center, pushing the two bulky mesityl groups toward the p-phenylene linker. This has two consequences. First, the electronic effect of the borane unit switches from electron-withdrawing to electron-donating. Second, the increased steric hindrance also restricts the rotational freedom of the phenylene ring. At the molecular level, these structural and electronic changes should produce distinct variations in the photophysical properties (e.g., fluorescence) of the rotaxane system. If so, 1 would function as a molecular logic gate, exhibiting fluorescence modulation (enhancement or quenching) in response to two external inputs (Li+ and F−). Herein, we describe our detailed experimental and computational studies on this system.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and structural properties

Scheme 1 shows the synthetic route to rotaxane 1. First, a Zr(iv)-catalyzed condensation reaction between 3,6-dibromobenzene-1,2-diamine (2) and a formyl-substituted triarylborane 3 was conducted. The reaction proceeded under mild conditions to afford a benzimidazole–triarylborane system 4, which served as the axle unit of the rotaxane. Compound 4 was then subjected to protonation with a strong acid, HBF4·Et2O, yielding the corresponding benzimidazolium salt 5. Compound 5 was used as a template in the ring-closing metathesis (RCM) of 6,9,12,15,18,21-hexaoxahexacosa-1,25-diene (6) under the catalysis of the first-generation Grubbs catalyst. The reaction was allowed to proceed for two days at 45 °C, affording rotaxane 7 in 68% yield. Finally, 7 underwent catalytic hydrogenation with Pd/C to give the target rotaxane 1 in 44% yield.

The molecular structures of compound 1 and its precursors were unambiguously confirmed by NMR and MS analyses (see the SI for details). Furthermore, rotaxanes 1 and 7 were subjected to single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) analysis to elucidate their structural and conformational features in the crystalline state. The X-ray structure of 7 reveals double hydrogen-bonding interactions, wherein two imidazolium N–H bonds act as donors to engage closely with two oxygen atoms of the crown ether moieties (see Fig. 2A), a binding motif characteristic of benzimidazolium–crown ether templated rotaxanes.40 The two hydrogen bonds exhibit N–H⋯O distances of 2.02 Å and 2.11 Å, respectively, with N–H⋯O angles of 174.5° and 172.8°. Notably, the central phenylene ring adopts a torsion angle of ca. 41° relative to the benzimidazole unit. This rotation partially reduces π-electron delocalization between the benzimidazole and borane moieties. The X-ray structure of rotaxane 1 shows only one hydrogen bond between the imidazolyl N–H and one of the crown ether oxygen atoms, with an N–H⋯O distance of 2.16 Å, which is slightly longer than the hydrogen bonds observed in 7. The hydrogen bonding interaction revealed by X-ray analysis is consistent with the 1H NMR data; the imidazolyl N–H proton of rotaxane 1 resonates as a sharp singlet at 12.14 ppm, which is significantly downfield-shifted relative to that of the free axle 4 (9.78 ppm). On the opposite side of this hydrogen bond, the crown ether creates sufficient empty space for binding small metal ions such as Li+. In contrast to 7, the central phenylene ring of 1 adopts a nearly coplanar orientation with respect to the benzimidazole group, which in turn maximizes π-electron communication between the borane and the benzimidazole.

Binding properties of 1 with Li+ and F− ions

The binding properties of rotaxane 1 with Li+ were investigated through 1H NMR analysis. Lithium tetrakis(pentafluorophenyl)borate ethyl etherate (LiBArF) was chosen as the lithium ion source, given that the bulky BArF counterion would have negligible effects on the resulting [1 + Li+] complex beyond charge balancing.43Fig. 4A shows the aromatic region of rotaxane 1 upon interaction with approximately one equivalent of Li+ in CD2Cl2. Compared with the spectrum of pristine 1 (Fig. 4C), the imidazolyl N–H signal of the [1 + Li+] complex exhibits a pronounced downfield shift from 12.14 ppm to 12.87 ppm. The two benzo C–H signals of 1 appear as two doublets at 8.32 ppm and 7.24 ppm (labeled a and b in Fig. 4C). After complexation with Li+, these peaks both shift downfield to 7.46 ppm and 7.43 ppm, respectively. The four C–H groups belonging to the central phenylene ring of 1 give a typical AA′XX′ spin pattern with two pseudo-doublets at 8.63 and 7.58 ppm (labeled d/d′ and e/e′, respectively, in Fig. 4C). After interaction with Li+, the pseudo-doublet at 8.63 ppm shifts upfield to 7.37 ppm, while the one at 7.58 ppm shifts downfield to 7.76 ppm. The aromatic protons on the mesityl rings (labeled f in Fig. 4C) appear as a singlet at 6.87 ppm, and this signal shows no shift before or after interaction with Li+. Collectively, the variations in the aromatic proton signals suggest that the imidazolyl unit is the dominant ligand for Li+ binding by rotaxane 1. The binding also modulates the chemical environment of the phenylene linker but exerts insignificant effects on the dimesitylboryl end group.

Fig. 4D shows the aliphatic region of the 1H NMR spectrum of rotaxane 1, reflecting the features of the crown ether unit. Distinct signals in the range of 3.76–2.89 ppm can be assigned to the C–H groups of the ethylene glycol ether unit (labeled as g–l, Fig. 4D). In the upfield region, three relatively broad signals are observed at 1.26, 1.10, and 0.92 ppm, corresponding to the alkyl C–H groups (labeled as m–o). The methyl groups attached to the mesityl rings give rise to two distinct singlets at 2.33 and 2.03 ppm. Upon interaction with Li+, the crown ether signals become broader and shift notably. Because the quadrupolar relaxation effect of lithium is known to cause broadening of coupled proton signals, the observed changes in the crown ether signals suggest that the crown ether unit is directly involved in binding Li+, most likely through coordination by its oxygen atoms.

Based on the NMR results, a 1 : 1 binding model was proposed between rotaxane 1 and Li+, in which the Li+ ion occupies a cavity jointly created by the crown ether ring and the imidazole axle. Refinement of this model by density functional theory (DFT) calculations yielded the optimized geometry shown in Fig. 3A. In this structure, the imidazolyl N–H group remains a hydrogen bond donor to the crown ether ring. The other imidazolyl nitrogen atom, together with two crown ether oxygen atoms, coordinates to the Li+ ion, forming short N–Li (1.98 Å) and O–Li (1.92–1.94 Å) bonds.45,46 One of the bromo groups is positioned 2.85 Å from the Li+ center. Although this distance is longer than a typical Li–Br bond (ca. 2.72 Å), the bromine atom is believed to play a contributing role in coordinating the Li+ ion, thereby stabilizing the binding with 1.

Fig. 5 shows the NMR analysis of rotaxane 1 interacting with F−. The interaction of 1 with F− appears to be a slow-exchange process. In the aromatic region (Fig. 5A), distinct signals due to the [1 + F−] complex are clearly observed (highlighted in red and blue) alongside those of pristine 1. Among these, the imidazolyl N–H signal (labeled c) resonates at 11.98 ppm, which is upfield-shifted by 0.15 ppm relative to that of 1. The two benzo protons (labeled a and b) appear as two doublets at 7.24 and 7.14 ppm, also upfield-shifted relative to their positions before binding with F− by ca. 0.10 ppm. The most significant shift occurs for the mesityl proton signal (labeled f), which shifts from 6.87 ppm to 6.55 ppm upon binding with F−. For the protons on the central phenylene unit (labeled d,d′ and e,e′), the spectral pattern changes dramatically from an AA′XX′ spin system to four relatively broader and separate peaks at 8.36, 8.11, 7.88, and 7.12 ppm (highlighted in blue, Fig. 5A). In the aliphatic region (Fig. 5B), the C–H groups of the ethylene glycol ether unit (3.77–2.88 ppm) do not show significant shifts after binding with F−. The three aliphatic signals (1.28, 1.01, and 0.93 ppm), corresponding to protons labeled m–o, are downfield-shifted to 1.53, 1.38, and 1.08 ppm, respectively.

Expanded 1H NMR (500 MHz, CD2Cl2) spectra of (A) and (B): rotaxane 1 mixed with TBAF (1.0 equiv.) in comparison with the spectra of (C) and (D): rotaxane 1.

These observations reveal the following features of the F− binding event. (i) The binding of 1 with F− forms a stable fluoroborate complex that does not dissociate readily (slow exchange) in solution.47,48 (ii) The formation of the fluoroborate quenches the Lewis acidity of the boron center, leading to increased electron density across the conjugated aromatic backbone, as reflected by the upfield shifts of the aromatic signals. (iii) The formation of the fluoroborate also alters the orientation of the bulky mesityl groups, forcing them to clash with the central phenylene ring. The increased steric crowding reduces the rotational freedom of the phenylene ring, desymmetrizing its NMR pattern.

Based on the NMR results, the binding mode for [1 + F−] was proposed as a fluoroborate system, which was computationally modeled by DFT calculations (Fig. 3B). In this optimized structure, the hydrogen bond between the imidazolyl N–H and one of the crown ether oxygen atoms is retained. The N–H⋯O bond length is 1.99 Å, which is slightly longer than that in the [1 + Li+] complex. On the fluoroborate side, one of the mesityl groups adopts a conformation with close proximity to the central phenylene ring. This geometric feature aligns with the NMR observation, where the central phenylene ring showed restricted rotation. Overall, NMR analysis in combination with DFT modeling provides a clear depiction of the structural changes accompanying the binding of Li+ or F− ions with rotaxane 1 in solution.

The structure of the [1 + F−] complex in the solid state was further confirmed by single-crystal X-ray analysis. As shown in Fig. 6, the binding of F− with rotaxane 1 results in the formation of a B–F covalent bond and converts the boron center to a tetrahedral sp3 geometry. This change forces the two mesityl rings to move closer to the central phenylene ring, creating a congested environment that restricts its rotation. The benzimidazole unit shows a torsion angle of 31.6° relative to the central phenylene moiety. Two intermolecular N–H⋯O hydrogen bonds are observed between the imidazole and crown ether units, with bond distances of 2.33 and 2.20 Å, respectively. The X-ray structure is in good agreement with the DFT-predicted geometry of the [1 + F−] complex.

Li+ ion-induced B–F bond dissociation

To investigate whether rotaxane 1 can synergistically bind both Li+ and F− ions, we examined a mixture of 1 with one equivalent of LiF in CD2Cl2 using 1H NMR. The spectrum of this mixture showed little change compared to that of pristine 1 (see Fig. S16, SI). While this observation suggests the absence of synergistic dual binding, the evidence is not conclusive given the limited solubility of LiF in CD2Cl2. Subsequently, we monitored the NMR changes upon the addition of one equivalent of LiBArF to the [1 + F−] complex. The results clearly demonstrated the conversion of the [1 + F−] complex into the [1 + Li+] complex (Fig. S13 and S14, SI). This result indicates that rotaxane 1 possesses a higher binding affinity for Li+ than for F−, effectively displacing the anion in the presence of the cation.

To gain a mechanistic understanding of the exchange reaction between the [1 + F−] and [1 + Li+] complexes at the molecular level, we performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations using the semiempirical GFN2-xtb method.49 In our simulation setup, we first placed a Li+ ion randomly around the [1 + F−] complex at a distance beyond van der Waals contact, as depicted in Fig. 7B. The system was allowed to evolve at 298.5 K for about 8.2 ps. Analysis of the MD trajectory (Fig. 7A) reveals the following features of the reaction dynamics. In the initial time domain (0 to 44 fs), the Li–F distance rapidly decreases to ca. 1.6 Å and thereafter fluctuates between 1.4 and 1.7 Å. This trajectory feature indicates the rapid formation of a stably bonded Li–F ion pair, which in turn cleaves the B–F bond of the fluoroborate group. Subsequently, the Li+ ion is attracted to the benzimidazole and crown ether moieties. After 0.70 ps, one of the imidazolyl nitrogens (labeled N1, Fig. 7) closely engages with the Li+ ion, with the Li–N1 bond distance fluctuating between 1.7 and 2.7 Å for the remainder of the simulation. Additionally, the Li+ ion gradually coordinates with two oxygen atoms in the crown ether (labeled O1 and O2, Fig. 7), and their interactions reach equilibrium after ca. 4 ps. This MD outcome indicates that the imidazolyl nitrogen (N1) is the primary binding site for the Li+ ion, while the crown ether serves as a synergistic chelating ligand to further stabilize the binding. Moreover, another adjacent oxygen atom (labeled O3) in the crown ether moiety also participates in this binding, but its contribution is secondary, as it maintains relatively long distances with the Li+ ion when the system equilibrates. Fig. 7C shows a snapshot from the MD simulation at 5.0 ps, depicting the synergistic binding of Li+ with the benzimidazole and crown ether groups.

Fluorescence responses to Li+ and F− binding

Having established the binding properties through NMR analysis, we subsequently investigated the fluorescence response of rotaxane 1 to these binding events. Fig. 8 depicts the UV-vis absorption and fluorescence spectra monitored during the titration of 1 with LiBArF in CH2Cl2. In the UV-vis spectra (Fig. 8A), the π → π* absorption bands spanning 325 to 375 nm exhibited a decreasing trend upon the addition of LiBArF (0.00 to 1.07 equiv.). Beyond this point, the absorption remained unchanged, indicating the formation of a saturated complex. In the fluorescence spectra (Fig. 8B), rotaxane 1 displayed an emission peak at 402 nm. The titration of LiBArF induced the growth of a long-wavelength emission band at 456 nm, which reached saturation after the addition of one equivalent of LiBArF. At the saturation point, the fluorescence intensity at 456 nm had increased 4.6-fold compared to that of pristine 1, indicating a notable fluorescence enhancement effect exerted by Li+ binding. The UV-vis and fluorescence data are consistent with the 1 : 1 binding mode revealed by NMR and DFT studies. Specifically, the fluorescence enhancement can be attributed to the rigidification of the molecular framework, which attenuates rotation-induced non-radiative decay pathways.50,51

(C) UV-vis and (D) fluorescence (λex = 340 nm) spectra monitoring the titration of 1 (7.80 × 10−5 M, CH2Cl2) with TBAF and then LiBArF. Arrows indicate the trends of spectral changes.

The UV-vis spectra recorded during the titration of 1 with TBAF in CH2Cl2 display a clear isosbestic point at 330 nm (Fig. 8C). The low-energy absorption band of 1 at 341 nm steadily decreases in intensity with increasing TBAF concentration, while two prominent high-energy bands at 321 and 259 nm emerge. These spectral changes can be attributed to the binding of 1 to the F− ion, which attenuates the push–pull interaction between the benzimidazole (donor) and borane (acceptor) groups, resulting in a blue-shift of the π → π* transitions on the axle unit. The fluorescence spectra of 1 exhibit a steady decrease in emission intensity upon addition of TBAF (Fig. 8D). When one equiv. of F− ion was added, the emission band of 1 at 402 nm became substantially quenched, indicating a full conversion of 1 into a stable 1 : 1 [1 + F−] complex. To this mixture was then gradually added one equiv. of LiBArF. This resulted in the revival of the fluorescence peak at 402 nm to the level observed for rotaxane 1 before TBAF titration. Addition of excess LiBArF led to the significant growth of a long-wavelength emission band around 456 nm, which is similar to that observed during the binding of 1 to Li+. The results shown in Fig. 8D indicate that the fluorescence of 1 can be quenched, revived, and amplified through sequential addition of F− and Li+ ions.

The results in Table 1 indicate an INHIBIT gate behavior in terms of fluorescence, meaning that the addition of Li+ ions (Input 2) suppresses the effect of F− ions (Input 1). Regarding the rotational output, only the addition of F− ions triggers restricted rotation of the central phenyl unit in 1. As a result, rotaxane 1 can simultaneously perform an INHIBIT operation on F− and Li+ ions via fluorescence turn-on and a YES/BUFFER operation on restricted intramolecular rotation. It is noteworthy that the fluorescence of 1 can be precisely tuned into three distinct states: (i) quenched, (ii) restored, and (iii) amplified, through a specific sequence of ion addition: F− (1 equiv.) → Li+ (1 equiv.) → Li (excess). This sequence-dependent fluorescence response suggests potential for designing molecular encryption devices, such as a molecular “padlock”.

Conclusions

In summary, we have designed and synthesized a mechanically interlocked heteroditopic receptor (1) based on a [2]rotaxane architecture. The concise synthetic route involved Zr(iv)-catalysed condensation to form the benzimidazole-triarylborane axle, followed by ring-closing metathesis and hydrogenation to afford the interlocked structure in good overall yield. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction unambiguously confirmed the interlocked architecture and revealed a hydrogen–bonding interaction between the imidazolyl N–H donor and a crown ether oxygen atom, with the macrocycle creating a well-defined cavity suitable for guest binding.

The rotaxane possesses two orthogonal recognition sites: (i) a Li+-selective pocket formed cooperatively by the crown ether and the imidazole unit, and (ii) a sterically shielded dimesitylborane terminus that selectively binds F−via formation of a covalent B–F bond. Detailed 1H NMR spectroscopic investigations, corroborated by DFT and MD calculations, established the 1 : 1 binding modes for both ionic guests. Competition experiments revealed that Li+ binding thermodynamically prevails over F− binding, with quantitative displacement of fluoride upon addition of a lithium salt to the pre-formed fluoroborate complex.

The fluorescence responses to the two inputs are distinctly different: Li+ coordination triggers a 4.6-fold fluorescence enhancement at 456 nm by rigidifying the molecular framework and suppressing non-radiative decay, whereas F− binding produces negligible spectral perturbation at stoichiometric concentrations, with appreciable quenching observed only upon addition of excess TBAF. Remarkably, subsequent addition of Li+ to the pre-formed [1+F−] adduct restores and amplifies the fluorescence, confirming reversible switching behaviour. This differential behaviour arises from the spatial separation of the two binding sites and their distinct electronic coupling to the benzimidazole fluorophore.

The orthogonal Li+ and F− binding events and the resulting fluorescence and rotational modulation establish rotaxane 1 as a functional prototype for molecular logic operations; specifically, a two-input signalling system with fluorescence and intramolecular rotation as the readable outputs. More broadly, this work demonstrates that sophisticated dual-responsive behaviour can be realised within a structurally concise mechanically interlocked framework, offering a design principle that may be extended to other MIM-based sensing and logic devices. Future efforts will focus on improving the fluorescence switching contrast, extending this approach to receptors for different alkali metal and halide ion pairs, and integrating the system into solid-state device architectures.
