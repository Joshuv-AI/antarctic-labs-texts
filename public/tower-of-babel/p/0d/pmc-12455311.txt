A Case Cluster of Aseptic Meningitis Associated With a Newly Identified Recombinant Echovirus 6/Coxsackievirus B1 Enterovirus
Authors: Kartikeya Cherabuddi, Massimiliano S Tagliamonte, John A Lednicky, David A Ostrov, Tracey L Moquin, Vishal Kaushik Thoomkuntla, Brian Bourgeois, Vidya Sagar Kollu, Nicole M Iovine, Paul D Myers, Kuttichantran Subramaniam, Marco Salemi, J Glenn Morris
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA day care teacher presented with complaints of headache, neck stiffness, and fever. Because of initial concerns about meningococcal meningitis, families of day care attendees were notified, and 10 children from the day care presented for evaluation. Cerebrospinal fluid from the teacher and nasal swabs from 4 children who were febrile were positive for enterovirus on reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction. A novel recombinant enterovirus was cultured from the teacher's cerebrospinal fluid and from 2 of the nasal swabs. The amino-terminal portion of the recombinant virus was derived from echovirus 6, with the carboxy-terminal portion originating from coxsackievirus B1; recombinant segments were most closely related to similar segments from strains isolated in France. Recombination occurred within the C2 gene associated with virus replication and virion morphogenesis. Structural modeling predicted that the recombinant protein was capable of forming hexameric and heptameric assemblies. Our data highlight the potential for recombination among enteroviruses, leading to modifications within viral proteins that may affect virulence.

The genus Enterovirus consists of small single-stranded positive-sense RNA viruses, which are generally transmitted via a respiratory route or through the gastrointestinal tract. Enteroviruses are within the Picornavirus family and are responsible for a range of illnesses in humans, including upper respiratory infections, meningitis/encephalitis, myopericarditis, and poliomyelitis, as well as hand, foot, and mouth disease [1]. Enteroviruses were previously classified into 4 major groups: polioviruses, Coxsackie A viruses, Coxsackie B viruses, and echoviruses. Current nomenclature is based on genetic characteristics, with identification of 15 enterovirus/rhinovirus species, which may contain echovirus and coxsackievirus serotypes [2–4].

Reporting of enterovirus infections is not mandatory at a national level in the United States. As noted by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the US laboratory capacity for enterovirus serotyping is limited, and serotyping is rarely done in clinical cases [5]. In 2024, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s National Enterovirus Surveillance System received reports of enteroviral infections from laboratories in only 7 states with enterovirus D68 the most commonly identified serotype, accounting for 62% of cases. In a recent European study of 2018–2023 enterovirus surveillance data from 28 institutions in 16 countries [1], 11 605 confirmed enterovirus cases were identified; the most frequent types were echovirus 30 (1440 cases), coxsackievirus A6 (1341 cases), enterovirus D68 (1261 cases), and echovirus 9 (1004 cases). Evidence of neurologic infection was reported in 40.5% of the European cases for which clinical data were available, with neurologic infections more common in certain serotypes: enterovirus A71 (neurologic infections in 73% of cases), coxsackievirus B5 (72.9%), echovirus 30 (71.6%), and echovirus 6 (E6; 56.6%) [1].

RNA recombination occurs frequently within enteroviral groups and is a major driving force in the evolution of the species [6–9], with recent studies exploring the genetic processes by which such recombinations occur [6, 10]. We report here a cluster of enterovirus cases associated with a childcare facility; the virus responsible for the outbreak was a novel recombinant of E6 and coxsackievirus B1 (CVB1).

METHODS

Clinical and epidemiologic data were obtained through the university referral hospital infection control program and the county department of health. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and nasal swab samples were tested by polymerase chain reaction/reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (PCR/RT-PCR) via the BioFire meningitis/encephalitis panel and the respiratory 2.1 panel (bioMérieux), respectively. With these platforms, enteroviruses are reported as “enterovirus” or “rhinovirus/enterovirus”; serotypes covered by these panels are listed in their instructions for use [11, 12]. The study was reviewed and approved by the University of Florida institutional review board; the patient serving as the index case provided written consent for inclusion of data in the report.

Viral Culture/Sequencing

The following cell lines obtained from the American Type Culture Collection were chosen for virus isolation attempts, as previously described [13]: A549 (CCL-185), LLC-MK2 (CCL-7), and MRC-5 (CCL-171). All the cell lines were propagated as monolayers at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in advanced Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated, gamma-irradiated, low-antibody fetal bovine serum (SH30070.03IH; GE Healthcare Life Sciences), 2mM L-alanyl-L-glutamine (GlutaMAX; Invitrogen Corp), and antibiotics (50 μg/mL, penicillin; 50 μg/mL, streptomycin; 100 μg/mL, neomycin; Invitrogen Corp). RNA was extracted from virions in the spent cell media of A549 cells with a QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. A cDNA library was generated by a NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs) and the library sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 1000 sequencer. De novo assembly of the paired-end reads was performed in MEGAHIT version 1.1.4 [14].

Phylogenetic Studies

By using BLAST, matches to the full genome sequences were obtained and filtered based on ≥70% identity. Noncoding untranslated regions were trimmed out, as they were missing from many of the sequences; only the polyprotein segment was kept for downstream analyses. The alignment was subsampled by country, taking into consideration genetic diversity and temporal sampling with TARDIS [15, 16] and keeping a maximum of 5 sequences per country. To the dataset we added sequences that showed close genetic relation to the major fragments at a preliminary analysis (OR840837.1, OR840843.1, OR840844.1) and 4 more distantly related strains as the outgroup (JX982254.1, MF678294.1, JN542510.1, JX174176.1). The final dataset resulted in 133 isolates, including those from the present study. Recombination analyses and phylogenetic signal assessment were performed as previously described [17, 18]. The recombinant origin of the new isolates was assessed with Simplot++ version 1.3 by comparing the three 2024 Florida sequences (query) with E6 OR840837.1, CVB1 OR840843.1, and CVB1 OR840844.1 (putative parental strains) [19]. Phylogenetic analysis was performed by inferring an initial neighbor-joining tree with Mega version 11 by using the best-fitting nucleotide substitution model and 1000 bootstrap replicates [20]. A relaxed molecular clock for each recombinant segment was calibrated with dated tips and the bayesian coalescent framework implemented in BEAST [21] by running a Markov chain Monte Carlo for 50 million generations with a sampling frequency of 50 000. A final maximum clade credibility tree was calculated with TreAnnotator and displayed in FigTree (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/). The effective sample sizes of all estimated parameters were >200, showing proper mixing of the Markov chain.

Modeling of the Protein Structure

Translated virus sequences were used as input for structural prediction in AlphaFold 3 [22]. The resulting predicted structures were aligned and superimposed by secondary structure matching in Coot [23]. Structural analyses, including atomic distance measurements, were conducted, and visual representations were generated with PyMol [24].

RESULTS

Outbreak

A teacher aged 25 to 30 years at a community daycare presented to a local emergency department (ED) with complaints of severe headache, neck stiffness, and high-grade fevers; the teacher also reported photophobia, nausea, and an episode of vomiting. A lumbar puncture was performed, and CSF showed a white blood cell count >500 cells/mL with 98% polymorphonuclear neutrophils. A Gram stain was read initially by the night laboratory technician as showing gram-negative cocci in pairs; on repeat evaluation of the sample the following morning, it was determined that this was an artifact, and the final official report of the Gram stain reported no bacteria. However, based on the incorrect initial report, there were concerns that the patient had meningococcal or other bacterial meningitis, and the patient was empirically prescribed vancomycin and ceftriaxone by the ED team.

In the setting of a possible meningococcal meningitis case, the ED notified the county health department, and per protocol the health department contacted the daycare and advised that if parents had concerns about possible exposure, they should bring their children to a university referral hospital for further evaluation. Ten children came to the hospital for evaluation, including daycare attendees and their siblings; because the daycare had been closed for the holidays, exposures ranged from 5 to 8 days prior to the hospital visit. Four children were symptomatic, 3 of whom underwent lumbar puncture; symptoms and laboratory results are shown in Table 1. Based on the report of the teacher's CSF pleocytosis, all 4 were empirically prescribed vancomycin and ceftriaxone. Potential adverse reactions to the antimicrobial therapy were noted in 3 children, resulting in administration of diphenhydramine and, in 1 case, methylprednisolone and epinephrine.

Virus Culture

CSF and nasal swab samples were sent to the University of Florida Emerging Pathogens Institute for virus culture. Virions in the CSF sample from the teacher and nasal swabs from 2 of the children caused virus-induced cytopathic effects (CPEs) by 3 days postinoculation of A549 cells and 4 days postinoculation of LLC-MK2 cells (Figure 1). The CPE consisted of swelling of the cells, followed by their detachment from the growing surface. By 5 days postinoculation, much of A549 cell culture had detached from the growing surface, whereas only about 30% of the LLC-MK2 cells displayed CPE. No CPEs were detected in MRC-5 cells at 5 days postinoculation; however, CPE was observed at 10 days postinoculation, with almost complete destruction of the monolayer. This pattern was somewhat unexpected: our experience with other echovirus strains had been that the relative magnitude of the CPE responses were LLC-MK2 = MRC5 > A549 cells. No CPEs were observed in mock-inoculated cells maintained in parallel. All 3 virus strains cultured were sequenced, and sequences were submitted to GenBank (adult case, PP873675; children, PQ273607 and PQ273608 [Table 1]).

Phylogenetic Analysis

The 3 clinical virus strains isolated from culture had essentially identical sequences, consistent with clonal spread of the virus within the daycare/home setting. On further analysis, we found that the amino-terminal portion of the virus showed homology with E6, while the carboxy-terminal portion appears to have originated from a CVB1, with a junction in the nonstructural gene 2C (Figure 2). Figure 3C and 3D show maximum clade credibility trees for these 2 segments. For both, segments cluster most closely to segments from French strains, suggesting that the recombination event occurred in France, possibly in or around 2019. The recombinant origin of the new Florida isolates is also evident in the similarity plot showing a clear breakpoint in the 2C gene (Figure 4). Modeling of Protein Structure

AlphaFold 3 was employed to generate 3-dimensional structural models of the 2C protein from the French E6 and CVB1 strains and the recombinant Florida viruses. The Florida viruses were found to contain a chimeric 2C protein, with the amino-terminal portion derived from E6 and the carboxy-terminal region originating from CVB1. Mutational analysis revealed that changes in the Florida viruses were distributed across the amino- and carboxy-terminal regions of the 2C protein (Figure 5). Structural predictions by AlphaFold 3 indicated a rearrangement of the first α-helix in the 2C protein of the Florida viruses, introducing a break near the mutated position 23. This structural rearrangement, with additional alterations in the 2C protein, was predicted to influence subunit assembly.

AlphaFold 3 further predicted distinct oligomerization patterns for the 2C protein. Specifically, E6 2C was modeled as forming a hexameric assembly (Figure 6A and 6E), consistent with previous experimental observations [25]. In contrast, CVB1 2C was predicted to form heptameric assemblies (Figure 6B and 6F). Interestingly, the recombinant Florida viruses were predicted to adopt hexameric (Figure 6C and 6G) and heptameric (Figure 6D and 6H) assemblies, suggesting that recombination and mutation enable these viruses to mimic molecular assemblies characteristic of both parental viruses. Arg at position 280 of the Florida viruses has the potential to mediate intermolecular contacts with neighboring chains in the hexameric and heptameric assemblies (Supplementary Figure 1). These findings highlight the structural plasticity imparted by recombination and mutation in the evolution of viral proteins.

DISCUSSION

Clinically, this outbreak is consistent with what has been previously described in association with enteroviruses. Our data document transmission of the virus within a daycare setting and within households, in keeping with the “transmission clusters” reported from Europe and other parts of the world [3, 26]. While the CSF neutrophil pleocytosis seen in our adult patient raised concerns about bacterial infection, in this instance the positive RT-PCR and virus culture results for enterovirus and negative bacterial culture and repeat Gram stain results are consistent with an enteroviral etiology; they also underscore the possibility of an initial polymorphonuclear neutrophil response in a viral infection and the importance of PCR/RT-PCR and culture in identification of an etiologic agent. This report raises the issue of a shortage of well-trained clinical laboratory workers, including microbiology technologists [27], limiting performance and interpretation of basic laboratory tests such as Gram strains, especially on night shifts. Given the adverse reactions to antimicrobial therapy seen in 3 of the children in the outbreak (in 1 case, necessitating administration of solumedrol and epinephrine), the stress in parents with concern for meningitis exposure to their children, and the strain on a pediatric ED receiving multiple concerned families in a short period, the availability of rapid RT-PCR testing of CSF and nasopharyngeal swabs for agents such as enterovirus is of particular importance in an ED setting.

E6 and CVB1 have been placed within the enterovirus species Enterovirus betacoxackie [4], previously referred to as enterovirus B. E6 is well recognized as one of the most common causes of aseptic meningitis and has been implicated in meningitis outbreaks [3, 26]; in the European data reported by de Schrijver et al [1], 67% of E6 cases were linked with neurologic infections. CVB1 has also been linked with aseptic meningitis, as well as myocarditis, pancreatitis, and hand, foot, and mouth disease, and has been implicated as a possible trigger for the development of type 1 diabetes [28]. Interestingly, work by Pu et al [9] suggests that echovirus plays a major role in natural recombination in the coxsackievirus B group; however, in the studies that they report, they did not identify any recombinants involving E6 and CVB1.

The recombination site for the recombinant virus that we identified in this outbreak was within the nonstructural protein 2C, which encodes a RNA-stimulated ATPase [29] associated with virus uncoating and host cell membrane rearrangements; RNA replication, encapsidation, and morphogenesis; and ATPase, helicase, and chaperoning activities.

The structural changes in the 2C protein of the Florida viruses, particularly the rearrangement of the first α-helix and the ability to form hexameric and heptameric assemblies, may have significant functional and evolutionary implications. The α-helix break near position 23, with mutations in the amino- and carboxy-terminal regions, likely alters the stability and dynamics of the 2C protein, potentially influencing its role in viral replication and assembly. The capacity to adopt multiple oligomeric states could enhance the flexibility of the Florida viruses in adapting to different host environments or cellular conditions, providing a potential selective advantage. Moreover, the ability to emulate structural features of E6 and CVB1 suggests that recombination and mutation have conferred novel functional properties, which may affect virus-host interactions, immune evasion, or pathogenesis. These structural modifications could thus play a critical role in the emergence and dissemination of our Florida viruses, making them a unique model for studying the effects of structural diversity on viral fitness and evolution. Further work, potentially including that with chimeric strains, will be necessary to confirm and more fully elucidate the impact of the predicted changes on transmission and virulence of the virus.

In summary, this report highlights the potential for enterovirus case clusters and the need for rapid diagnostics capable of identifying enterovirus infections. While the propensity for enteroviruses to recombine is well recognized, the 2C gene is reported to have a low frequency of recombination, and recombination between groups (in this case, echovirus and coxsackievirus) is not well described. While we do not have a complete understanding of the significance of this particular recombination and its potential for increasing virulence, the adult case in this outbreak was indeed severe, with the E6 gene segment most closely related to the segment in our Florida strains also coming from a strain isolated from CSF (Figure 3A and 3C). Given that viruses associated with aseptic meningitis are seldom cultured or sequenced, with only limited enterovirus sequence data available at a national level in the United States, we have no way of estimating the degree of spread of this specific recombinant virus; this lack of data also limits our ability to reconstruct the genetic steps underlying the recombination, as has been done by Muslin et al for other recombinant enterovirus strains [6, 10]. However, the recognition that such recombinants are relatively common and are continuing to emerge [1, 7, 8] underscores the need for ongoing surveillance of enteroviral infections with sequence analysis, particularly in instances involving more severe illness or case clusters.
