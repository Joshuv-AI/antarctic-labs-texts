An Equine Protein Atlas Highlights Synovial Fluid Proteome Dynamics during Experimentally LPS-Induced Arthritis
Authors: Louise Bundgaard, Filip Årman, Emma Åhrman, Marie Walters, Ulrich auf dem Keller, Johan Malmström, Stine Jacobsen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn human proteomics, substantial efforts are ongoing to leverage large collections of mass spectrometry (MS) fragment ion spectra into extensive spectral libraries (SL) as a resource for data independent acquisition (DIA) analysis. Currently, such initiatives in equine research are still missing. Here we present a large-scale equine SL, comprising 6394 canonical proteins and 89,329 unique peptides, based on data dependent acquisition analysis of 75 tissue and body fluid samples from horses. The SL enabled large-scale DIA-MS based quantification of the same samples to generate a quantitative equine protein distribution atlas to infer dominant proteins in different organs and body fluids. Data mining revealed 163 proteins uniquely identified in a specific type of tissue or body fluid, serving as a starting point to determine tissue-specific or tissue-type-specific proteins. We showcase the SL by highlighting proteome dynamics in equine synovial fluid samples during experimental lipopolysaccharide-induced arthritis. A fuzzy c-means cluster analysis pinpointed SERPINB1, ATRN, NGAL, LTF, MMP1, and LBP as putative biomarkers for joint inflammation. This SL provides an extendable resource for future equine studies employing DIA-MS.Keywords: SWATH-MS, DIA, equine, tissue-specific proteins, synovial fluid, LPS-induced arthritis, biomarkers

Introduction

In mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics, data independent acquisition (DIA) has developed into a compelling alternative to data dependent acquisition (DDA) for identification and quantification of proteins and peptides in complex biological samples.1 In DIA, the mass spectrometer cycles over user-defined isolation windows to analyze all precursor ions across the liquid chromatography (LC) gradient. In this way, DIA overcomes the stochastic nature of the traditional DDA approach, where precursor ions are selected for analysis based on abundance, leading to a more complete and reliable coverage of the proteome.1 Following data acquisition, peptide and protein identifications are extracted via targeted data analysis, using a reference spectral library (SL) containing annotated sets of spectra required for protein identification. The SL is usually generated from previously acquired DDA and DIA-MS data to establish a nonredundant, curated, and searchable compendium of spectra. An alternative analysis strategy is the library-free approach, but this has in some instances been shown to be less sensitive than the SL-based strategy.2 It is consequently of significant importance to construct SLs with high proteome coverage while maintaining strict control of the false discovery rate (FDR) to reach the highest possible depth and quantification accuracy in DIA.3 In the proteomics research community, billions of fragment ions are acquired during MS studies. To advance the field in human research, initiatives have been formed with the aim to leverage these data sets into a SL resource for the processing of all new DIA data acquired.4 Currently, there is still no such initiative in equine research, but the use of SLs are emerging.5−8

In recent years, the horse has been increasingly used as an animal model for various diseases including osteoarthritis (OA),9,10 atrial fibrillation,11 and infectious and allergic diseases,12 where size, physiology, anatomy, and other aspects confer specific validity to the equine model. The increasing traction of research involving horses engender a growing demand for technologies such as MS-based proteomics, since validated heterologous antibodies for immunoassays are a scarce resource13 and there has been limited financial incentive to raise equine-specific antibodies. As an alternative strategy, MS-based proteomics has been successfully adopted to quantify, e.g., acute phase proteins in wound healing,14 septic articular pathologies,15 the peritoneal fluid composition following intestinal strangulation,16 and the abundance of proteins in saliva in response to systemic inflammation.17 Besides being a good model for joint diseases in humans,9,10 the equine joint is a compartment undergoing intensive investigation as equine joint diseases is one of the major causes of equine morbidity and poor welfare.21,22 Essential insight into the pathology of equine joint diseases, e.g., osteoarthritis and septic arthritis, has been obtained using proteomics,7,15,23 but further proteomics studies are required.23 Early diagnosis of joint pathology is of particular importance for early intervention, and the initial pathology of joint inflammation can be studied by the use of an equine experimental model of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced arthritis.24−26

In contrast to collective efforts in other animal model systems, the absence of large-scale cataloging of the equine organ proteome landscape has precluded the construction of a high-quality SL that could serve as a starting point to further advance targeted proteomics (SRM/PRM/MRM) and DIA approaches in the horse. Another shortage in equine proteome research is the poor annotation of the genome and proteome. Of the 44,484 Uniprot entries, only 291 proteins are reviewed (Uniprot entry 2023-03-06), and it is unknown which proteins are expressed and how proteins distribute across tissues and body fluids. Extensive SLs exist for both human18 and mouse,19,20 and besides providing the foundation for publicly available SLs, these efforts have catalyzed large-scale protein abundance and protein distribution analysis in health and disease.

Here we present an equine SL based on DDA analysis of 15 different tissues and four body fluids from the horse. Based on DIA-MS protein abundance profiles across the different types of samples, we constructed a protein distribution map of the horse proteome that can be used to infer dominant proteins in different organs and body compartments. We showcase the resource by analyzing proteome dynamics in synovial fluid (SF) samples sequentially during experimental LPS-induced arthritis, with the purpose of obtaining new insights into LPS-induced joint inflammation.

Experimental Section

Study Design and Analysis Rationale

In this study, we used DDA-MS to profile the proteome of 75 samples from 15 different tissue types and four body fluids obtained from 12 individual horses and used the data to generate an SL. The tissue and body fluid samples were collected in at least three healthy biological replicates, except for synovial membrane (one replicate), to measure the biological variation. The horses were considered healthy based on clinical assessment and routine blood analyses obtained before euthanasia, and for joint tissue and SF an additional macroscopic inspection of the joint cavity was performed post mortem. Inflammation is a cardinal symptom in many diseases, leading to substantial reorganization of the tissue and cellular proteomes. To increase the coverage of the SL, we included tissue and fluid samples representing an inflammatory state, both acute (strangulated jejunum, periphlebitis, and SF from joints with LPS-induced arthritis) and chronic (SF and tissue from joints with OA and tissue from a limb wound 14 days post wounding). Next, samples were analyzed in DIA-MS mode, and the SL was used to generate a quantitative equine protein distribution atlas.

To showcase the developed SL, SF samples from three horses with highly inflammatory LPS-induced experimental arthritis were sequentially sampled at five different time points (0, 1, 16, 24, and 48 h) and evaluated with DIA analysis and processed with the SL as the reference library. A fuzzy c-means cluster analysis (clustering method where every data point is assigned a cluster and a probability of it being in the cluster) was used to get insight into the clustering of the quantified proteins based on the protein dynamics.

Samples

Samples Included in the SL

Seventy-five samples from 12 horses representing 15 different tissues and four different body fluids were included. They are termed the “Tissue–Fluid samples”. The types of samples were specifically selected to cover the major vascularized organs (liver, spleen, kidney, and lymph nodes), the internal organs most often involved in morbidity in horses: digestive system (jejunum, cecum, and colon), joint compartment (synovial membrane, cartilage, and subchondral bone), respiratory system (lung),27 ubiquitous tissues (adipose tissue and muscle), and the body fluids most commonly used for diagnostics (serum, plasma, SF, and peritoneal fluid). The Tissue–Fluid samples were isolated from healthy horses along with additional specific samples from horses with local inflammatory condition. The horses were Standardbred trotters and Cobs, mares, and geldings, 3–14 years old. Supplementary File 1 provides an overview of the samples included in the protein atlas.

Specific samples were obtained with permission from the Danish Animal Experiments Inspectorate (License 2017-15-0201-01314 (horse IDs H5, H9, H10, H11, and H12) and 2011/561-1996 (horse IDs H8 and SF used to validate the SL)). Samples from horse IDs H6 and H7 were leftover SF after synoviocenteses from client-owned horses, stored for research purposes with the owners’ consent. The remaining samples were obtained from euthanized research horses (horse IDs H1, H2, H3, and H4) from unrelated terminal studies. All sampling procedures were approved by the ethics and welfare committee of the Department of Veterinary Clinical Sciences, University of Copenhagen, and procedures were carried out according to the Danish Act on animal experiments.

Samples with horse IDs H5, H9, H10, H11, and H12 originated from an experimental equine OA model described in previous studies.28 In short, OA was induced by creating an 8 mm osteochondral fragment on the radial carpal bone under arthroscopic guidance. From day 14 and until euthanasia at day 71, horses underwent exercise on a treadmill 5 days/week. The contralateral joint underwent sham surgery and was sampled similarly to the operated joint, thus serving as a healthy control. SF was sampled regularly over the study period from both OA and healthy control joints.

Synovial Fluid Samples from Horses with Experimental LPS-Induced Arthritis (LPS-SF)

The SF used to validate the SL (and the Tissue–Fluid samples with horse ID H8) originated from a study aimed at characterizing intra-articular hemostatic and inflammatory responses in experimental LPS-induced acute arthritis as described in detail previously.25 In short, acute arthritis was induced by intra-articular injection of LPS in one antebrachiocarpal joint. SF was aspirated aseptically from the joint at regular intervals over the next 24 h and transferred to citrate-coated tubes. The study included determination of the concentration of the inflammatory acute phase proteins serum amyloid A (SAA), fibrinogen (FIB), and haptoglobin (HP) in the SF samples using commercial assays.

Sample Processing

All Tissue–Fluid samples obtained post mortem were sampled within 5 h after euthanasia. Tissue samples were immediately washed in PBS to remove blood/SF, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. All tissue samples were digested within 4 months from sampling.

To ensure that the SL covered a broad spectrum of proteins and embracing proteins potentially favored by various preparation steps, several different processing methods were used for body fluids. Blood was collected in tubes with clot activator (serum) or EDTA-coated tubes (plasma), peritoneal fluid in EDTA-coated tubes, and SF in plain tubes, EDTA-coated tubes, or citrate-coated tubes, all based on the initial purpose for which the sample was obtained. Protease inhibitor (cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, Roche) (25x) was added to a number of plasma, peritoneal fluid, and SF samples (Supplementary File 1). Blood samples in tubes with a clot activator were centrifuged at 1800 g for 10 min at RT after a clot was established. Blood and SF samples in EDTA coated tubes were centrifuged at 1000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. SF samples in citrate-coated tubes were centrifuged at 1500 g for 15 min at RT, and SF in plain tubes were centrifuged at 2500 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Two plasma samples and three SF samples (Supplementary File 1) were enriched for lower abundant proteins by the use of ProteoMiner (large capacity kit) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (http://www.bio-rad.com/webroot/web/pdf/lsr/literature/10010636D.PDF), followed by buffer exchange (2.5 M GuHcl, 250 mM HEPES, pH 7.8) in a 3 kDa cut off spin filter (Merck Millipore). All body fluid samples were digested within 6 months upon sampling except SF in plain tubes (digested within a year from sampling) and SF in citrate-coated tubes (digested after ∼3 years from sampling).

The LPS-SF were sampled in citrate-coated tubes and processed as already described for the SF samples in citrate-coated tubes. The samples were digested ∼3 years from sampling.

Digest and Desalting

Tissue–Fluid samples and LPS-SF samples ((6–8) × 1 mm3 cubes of each tissue, fluids ∼100 μg protein) were dissolved in 8 M urea (Sigma-Aldrich) in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate and processed by ultrasonication (Bioruptor Pico, Diagenode) for 10 min at 4 °C, 20 cycles, 15 s ON and 15 s OFF. Protein disulfide bonds were reduced with 5 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (Sigma-Aldrich) for 60 min at 37 °C and alkylated with 10 mM iodoacetamide for 30 min at RT in the dark, followed by addition of 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate to a final urea concentration below 1.5 M. Proteins were digested with trypsin (min. 1:100 w/w) at 37 °C for an hour followed by another addition of trypsin (min. 1:100 w/w) and digestion overnight at 37 °C. The digestion was quenched by the addition of 10% formic acid to pH ≈ 3. The samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 14,000 g and the supernatant was desalted using the Thermo Scientific SOLAμ vacuum manifold and solid phase extraction micro elution as follows: The columns were conditioned with 200 μL acetonitrile, equilibrated with 400 μL 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, and 250 μL of the sample was loaded and diluted in 250 μL 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. The sample on the column was washed with 500 μL of 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid then 200 μL of 0.1% formic acid and eluted with 50 μL of 50% acetonitrile and 0.2% formic acid two times. The samples were vacuum-dried and resuspended in 50 μL of HPLC water with 2% acetonitrile, 0.2% formic acid, and synthetic peptides (1:20 v/v) (iRT peptide kit, Biognosys AG) for retention time calibration. The peptide concentration was measured by use of the Pierce Quantitative Colorimetric Peptide Assay (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (https://assets.thermofisher.com/TFS-Assets/LSG/manuals/23275_quantpeptide_color_UG.pdf).

MS Analysis

Samples were separated on a PepMap RSLC C18 analytical column (75 μm × 50 cm, 2 μm, 100 Å, nanoViper, Thermo Scientific) using the EASY-nanoLC system (Thermo Scientific) coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X Hybrid Quadropole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Separation of 1 μg of peptide mixture was achieved by running a constant flow rate of 350 nL/min in 0.1% formic acid/99.9% water and a 120 min gradient from 10% to 90% (10% for 90 min, 30% for 20 min, 45% for 1 min, and 90% for 10 min) elution buffer (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid, and 19.9% water) (Supplementary File 2).

For DDA analysis, Xcalibur 4.1.31.9 June 2017 was applied to record the spectra. The full scan MS spectra (350–1400 m/z) were acquired with a resolution of 60,000 after accumulation to a target value of 3e6. Higher-energy collisional-induced dissociation MS/MS spectra with fixed first mass of 100 m/z were recorded from the 15 most intense signals with a resolution of 15,000 applying an automatic gain control of 1e5 and a maximum injection time of 60 ms. Precursors were isolated using a window of 1.3 m/z and fragmented with a normalized collision energy of 28%. The intensity threshold for precursor ion selection was 1.7e4. Precursor ions with an unassigned or single charge state were rejected, and precursor masses already selected for MS/MS were excluded for further selection for 10 s. DDA data for Tissue–Fluid samples were acquired in two different batches. Blanks were included after every fourth sample.

For DIA, the full scan MS spectra (340–1410 m/z) were acquired with a resolution of 60,000 after accumulation to a target value of 3e6 and a maximum injection time of 100 ms. The MS/MS data was recorded by 32 full fragmentation scans, using an isolation window adjusted to the number of peaks (Supplementary File 2). The precursor ions within each isolation window were fragmented using higher-energy collisional-induced dissociation with a resolution of 30,000 applying an automatic gain control of 1e6 and a maximum injection time of 120 ms. DIA data for Tissue–Fluid samples were acquired in two different batches. Blanks were included after every fourth sample.

Data Analysis

Building the Spectral Library

DDA files from the 75 Tissue–Fluid samples were compiled into an SL using the pulsar search engine in the commercial software Spectronaut v. 16.2.220903.5300029 on an Equus caballus reference proteome FASTA database with reviewed and unreviewed (TrEMBL) proteins from Uniprot release 22_05, totaling 44,484 entries using default Biognosys (BGS) factory settings. Trypsin (P) was specified as the enzyme with up to two missed cleavages, allowing a minimum of five and maximum of 50 amino acids and including proteins identified with only one peptide to present the global proteome. Mass tolerance intervals for fragment ions were set to 200–3000 m/z.

Searching DIA Data

A database search of the 75 DIA files from the Tissue–Fluid samples was carried out in Spectronaut, using the previously built SL as a search database in library mode. The search was configured using the default BGS factory settings with FDR set to 0.01 at both the peptide and protein level. The DIA files from the SF samples from the LPS study were searched using the same library and configuration settings as above.

Protein Quantification

For the DIA search result of the Tissue–Fluid samples, the peptides were inferred to protein quantities using the Spectronaut built-in maxLFQ algorithm. For the LPS-SF, the DIA search result file was processed using the software MapDIA v.3.1.030 inferring peptides to protein quantities given that the peptides are unique to the protein, with a minimum of one unique peptide per inferred protein.

Imputation

Protein quantification data sets from Tissue–Fluid samples and LPS-SF samples contained cases where proteins were quantified in some of the samples, and the other part resulted in NA values due to insufficient quantifiable peptides. The assumption is that the samples with NA values are manifestations of a very low concentration or total absence of the protein and therefore not detected in the MS. To simulate this, every sample with a protein NA value was imputed by a random draw from a uniform distribution ranging from 5% to 50% of the minimum observed quantified value of the protein.

In eq 1, the imputed sample protein value (ISPv) is obtained by a random draw from a uniform distribution (U) based on a range of 0.05 to 0.5 times the minimum observed quantified protein value (OQPvmin). For the calculation of the CV, missing values were not imputed but proteins with missing values in one or more samples were simply left out.

Normalization

For global comparison of DIA-SL samples, a VSN strategy was used to bring the quantitative data from heterogeneous samples onto a same scale, making the sample variance nondependent from the mean intensity.31 The LPS-SF data were median normalized and log 2 transformed. Median normalization was also applied for the calculation of CVs.

Analysis of the SF Data from Horses with Experimental LPS-Induced Arthritis

Only proteins identified with at least two unique peptides and identified in at least five samples were included in the analysis, and the mean intensity signal per time point was used in the fuzzy c-means cluster analysis. The number of clusters was set to four, based on the point of inflection when imaging the within cluster sum of squared error and a correlation between clusters below 0.75. Only proteins with a membership score >0.5 were considered true members of the designated cluster. Median-normalized and nonimputed data from analysis of DIA-SL tissue samples from healthy individuals were used to uncover the potential tissue origin of proteins identified in LPS-SF. The protein abundance in the different tissues was scaled to the sum of all the abundances for the protein.

R (v. 4.1.2) was used for imputation, normalization, and analysis. Metascape32 was used for enrichment analysis of GO biological processes with min overlap at five and min enrichment at two. Protein intersection analysis was performed using Venny v. 2.1 and UpSet.33 Additional graphics were created in GraphPad Prism v. 9.3.0, Adobe Illustrator and BioRender. The MS data has been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the data set identifier PXD044741 and is publicly available.

Results and Discussion

Protein Profiling Across Tissues and Body Fluids

The equine SL was generated by combining data from 75 nonfractionated samples from 19 different tissues and body fluids originating from 12 different healthy and diseased horses. All tissue samples were processed for MS analysis and analyzed in DDA-MS mode and the resulting data used to generate an SL encompassing 6394 unique proteins and 89,329 peptides. Only 291 proteins of the 44,484 entries in the Uniprot equine proteome database were marked as reviewed, and 181 of these proteins were identified in the equine SL presented here. This means that the equine SL provided evidence for 6213 unreviewed proteins (14%) in the equine proteome in Uniprot (more details on protein and peptide identification can be found in Supplementary File 3).

This is the most extensive equine SL presented to date, and our hope is that the comprehensive data set presented here provides a valuable resource for future equine studies employing the DIA-MS approach and may act as a general and publicly available equine SL that can be further expanded in the future. Development of project-specific SLs requires a considerable investment of time in sample preparation and analysis,34 but with large community-shared SLs as presented here, a common set of reference spectra ready to apply is provided, often resulting in higher protein coverage compared to project-specific SLs.34 Such extensive libraries are required to quantitatively compare heterogeneous samples in larger-sized cohorts such as clinical studies.18,34,35

In the next step, all samples were analyzed using DIA and the SL was used to infer dominant proteins in the different organs and body compartments by targeted data extraction to generate a quantitative equine protein distribution atlas. In total, this analysis generated quantitative protein profiles for 5934 canonical proteins and 83,728 unique peptides. The average number of proteins was 3123 for tissue samples and 448 for body fluids (Supplementary File 1).

A Pearson correlation of the median normalized log transformed protein abundances was used to compare the correlation between the biological replicates within the sample type and condition. The correlation coefficients were between 0.67 and 0.95 for tissue and 0.76 and 0.97 for body fluids (Figure 1). The distribution profiles obtained with the Pearson correlation analysis revealed three major distinct groups: one group with body fluids, a group with cartilage and subchondral bone, and a group encompassing the rest of the tissues included in the correlation analysis, where the correlation was more diverse. Some samples had a negative correlation, which was caused by proteins with high abundance in one tissue/body fluid and low abundance in another tissue/body fluid (Supplementary File 4).

In conclusion, we identified and quantified the proteome from 15 different tissue types and four body fluids obtained from 12 individual horses. The results provided evidence for 14% of the unreviewed uniprot entries and was used to generate the most extensive equine SL to date. The proteomes from biological replicates of the same sample type and condition showed a high degree of correlation, and the proteins with high abundance levels in distinct samples mapped to familiar biological processes for the given organ.

Protein Distribution and Dynamics of the Equine Protein Tissue Atlas

Measuring the protein dynamics in tissues and body fluids is fundamental to understanding biology and disease mechanisms. Mining of the equine protein distribution atlas generated new insights into the protein distribution in different tissues, fluids, and body compartments and enabled the identification of tissue-specific proteins of potential relevance when studying different disease mechanisms. Figure 3 shows how the protein distribution in different types of samples (excluding wound and inflamed vein) intersected. Interestingly, 163 proteins were only identified in one specific type of tissue or body fluid, serving as a starting point to determine tissue-specific or tissue-type-specific proteins (Supplementary File 5). As examples observed in both the DDA and DIA data set, three Paneth-cell-specific α-defensins (DEFA) not detected in other mammalian species, e.g., cattle, pig, or dog,36,37 were uniquely identified in equine jejunum. They have been shown to be important in the defense mechanism of the small bowel mucosa toward prominent human and equine pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus, Salmonella typhimurium, and Escherichia coli. Their presence and tissue specificity make the horse an obvious species to study biological properties of α-defensins.38 Uromodulin (UMOD), dendrin (DDN), and 12 solute carrier family (SLC) members were identified only in the kidney tissue samples. They are known to be kidney-specific proteins and to be of major physiological importance in humans but are also involved in various kidney disorders.39,40 These have not previously been investigated in horses. Furthermore, SLC22A6, a transporter of drugs, is believed to facilitate interorgan communication around the gut–liver–kidney axis, and studies involving SLC22A6 have the potential to provide knowledge about altered drug disposition pathways in different organs in kidney diseases.41 Other examples of tissue-specific proteins were solute carrier organic anion transporter family member (SLCO1B3), tyrosine aminotransferase (TAT), and bile salt export pump (ABCB11), all three of which were exclusively identified in liver samples and pulmonary surfactant-associated protein C (SFTPC) unique to samples from the lung. Various proteins involved in striated muscle contraction and muscle system processes were exclusively identified in muscle. In lymph nodes, the protein linker for activation of T-cell family member (LAT) 1, LAT2, and signaling lymphocytic activation molecule family member 1 (SLAMF1) were uniquely identified. Investigation of such tissue-specific proteins in both health and disease might reveal tissue-specific events of relevance for both horses and humans.

Articular joints constitute a semiclosed environment, which is subject to intense investigation in many diseases, e.g., OA. To understand the pathogenesis of joint diseases and to develop disease-specific diagnostics and therapeutics, it is important to have an insight into the tissue of origin of identified proteins. Both SF, synovial membrane, cartilage, and subchondral bone samples were included here, and Figure 4A shows the relationship of the identified proteins in the DIA data in the different sample types.

The proteins exclusively identified in the synovial membrane and cartilage were related to intracellular processes. Some cartilage matrix proteins have been investigated in OA, but little information is available regarding the disturbances in subchondral bone.42 In accordance with a recently published study,5 osteocalcin (BGLAP), Indian hedgehog (IHH), and calsyntenin 1 (CTNNBIP1) were only identified in subchondral bone both in the DDA and the DIA data sets and the tissue specificity of these proteins might be of particular interest when studying pathological events in joint diseases. Research in joint diseases often focuses on identification of biomarkers in SF reflecting pathological events in the osteochondral unit. Figure 4B shows a list of intersecting proteins involved in skeletal system development and ossification identified in SF and in cartilage and/or subchondral bone but not in synovial membrane samples. These proteins could be potential biomarkers for degradation of tissues in the osteochondral unit, which may be of interest for biomarker studies in the joint disease research community. Cellular communication factor 3 (CCN3) was identified in subchondral bone, cartilage, and SF. In a previous study it has been shown that, after completion of endochondral ossification, CCN3 was observed in few cell types in bone marrow, with expression being dramatically enhanced after bone injury.43 Intra-articular administration of CCN3 has been reported to have a favorable treatment effect in murine OA models,43,44 but it has recently been found to enhance cartilage degeneration when used for longer periods.45 Its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target is still under investigation,46 and OA research in horse models might contribute to establishing evidence. Chordin-like protein 2 (CHRDL2) was identified in cartilage and SF and has previously been found to be downregulated on the gene level in cartilage from joints with OA and suggested as a biomarker for this condition.47 In conclusion, the SL confers knowledge about specific proteins that might be central in specific joint pathologies or potential biomarkers of interest for the research community within joint diseases.

Synovial Fluid from Experimental LPS Arthritis Study

Diagnosis and monitoring of septic arthritis can be challenging, and early diagnosis is crucial for a successful outcome.48 Insight into the protein dynamics during the initial hours of disease progression can give important knowledge about the disease pathology and identify a panel of potential biomarkers for early diagnosis. To demonstrate that the SL can facilitate the identification of putative biomarker candidates, we applied the SL as a database to search DIA data from analysis of SF sequentially sampled during the initial hours of experimental LPS-induced arthritis, a model of septic arthritis, in three horses. Time dependent DIA-MS analysis using the established SL demonstrated that dynamic changes of SAA1, FIB, and HP (Figure 5) were in line with a previous analysis of the same samples using commercial assays.25 Currently, global analysis of the dynamic changes in protein expression during inflammatory arthritis in horses has only been scarcely investigated,15,49 and the SL is thus expected to significantly advance this research topic.

We used fuzzy c-means cluster analysis of the protein expression profiles to study the SF proteome dynamics during LPS-induced acute arthritis in more detail. Based on the time-resolved protein fluctuations, most of the identified proteins could be divided into four distinct clusters (Figure 6 and Supplementary File 6).

The proteins in cluster 1 showed a marked increase in abundance toward 8 h followed by a steady level toward 48 h. The most enriched biological processes were Regulation of cell substrate adhesion, Actin filament organization, Inflammatory response, and Positive regulation of cell adhesion. Proteins with a prompt and marked change in abundance, such as the proteins mapping to cluster 1, are of relevance as early disease biomarkers. The cluster included SERPINB1, which has previously been shown to have an important role in the resolution of inflammatory diseases and in the host response to some infections,50 attractin (ATRN), which is involved in immune cell clustering in the initial phases of inflammation,51 and l-selectin (SELL), essential for lymphocyte homing and leukocyte trafficking.52 Also, inter-α-trypsin inhibitor 1 (ITIH1), ITIH2, ITIH3, and ITIH4 and lipocalin 2 (LCN2), also called neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), mapped to cluster 1. Using an ELISA validated for equine samples,53 two recent studies have investigated the biomarker potential of NGAL in equine inflammatory arthritis. One study evaluated NGAL in clinical cases with septic arthritis and found nearly perfect discriminatory ability.54 In another study evaluating NGAL in horses with LPS-induced arthritis, concentrations increased within a few hours of LPS injection and peaked between 6 and 9 h, thus confirming that NGAL is an early marker of inflammatory arthritis in horses.55 The acute phase proteins SAA1, FIB, and HP also mapped to cluster 1.

The cluster 2 proteins showed a prompt increase in abundance toward 8 h followed by a decrease to an abundance above the starting point at 48 h. The most enriched biological processes were Humoral immune response, Blood coagulation, Actin-filament-based processes, and Regulation of defense response. This cluster included the cytokine interleukin (IL)-6, a potent inducer of the acute phase response, which was expected,56 but this low-abundant protein was only identified in eight of the 15 samples. In addition, neutrophil elastase (ELANE) secreted by neutrophils during inflammation, lactotransferrin (LTF), matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) 8, and tissue inhibitor of MMP (TIMP1) mapped to cluster 2. Lactotransferrin (LTF) has previously been identified in higher concentration compared to the control in SF in clinical cases of septic arthritis in the horse15 and might be considered as a diagnostic or prognostic biomarker (Figure 6).

Proteins in cluster 3 decreased markedly in abundance toward 16 h followed by an increase in abundance toward the 48 h but never reached the initial level. Ossification, Regulation of insulin-like growth factor receptor signaling pathway, Extracellular matrix organization, and Skeletal system development were the most enriched biological processes. The proteins mapping to extracellular matrix organization included fibulin 1 (FBLN1), fibromodulin (FMOD), lumican (LUM), MMP9, cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP), and transforming growth factor β-induced protein (TGFBI). These proteins are likely involved in or are a product of cartilage degradation triggered by LPS as previously shown in in vitro studies on human chondrocytes.57

The abundance of proteins in cluster 4 showed a steady increase over time. Negative regulation of proteolysis, Acute phase response, Positive regulation of cytokine production, and Regulation of cellular response to growth factor stimulus were the most enriched biological processes and included an extrahepatic isoform of serum amyloid A (SAA2),58 lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) and LDHB, lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP), and MMP1, MMP2, and MMP19 known to degrade components of the cartilage extracellular matrix in arthritic disease.59−61 Nine members of the SERPIN family also mapped to cluster 4; SERPINA3 (chymotrypsin), SERPINA6 (hormone binding), SERPINA7 (antitrypsin-like), SERPINA10 (inhibitor of activated coagulation factor Z and XI), SERPINB10 (inhibitory protein involved in hematopoietic and myeloid development), SERPINC1 (antithrombin III), SERPIND1 (thrombin inhibitor), SERPINF2 (involved in fibrinolysis), and SERPING1 (C1 inhibitor).62 Even with their slower increase in abundance, proteins in cluster 4 could also be of value for diagnosis of joint disease.

A protein that might be of interest in septic arthritis caused by Gram-negative bacteria is the protein LBP (Figure 6). LBP is an LPS receptor and the LPS–LBP complex interacts with CD14 and triggers monocyte secretion of cytokines. This interaction could also be a therapeutic target, because LPS binding of the bactericidal permeability increasing protein (BPI) hinders the binding of LBP and antagonizes the proinflammatory effect of LBP.63 A therapeutic agent with the capacity to block LBP binding could potentially mitigate the inflammatory response.

Based on the change in concentration and/or known relation to the inflammatory response, as well as the consistency of quantification at most of the time points, the findings in the present study support SERPINB1, ATRN, NGAL, LTF, MMP1, and LBP as putative biomarkers for joint inflammation (Figure 7).

The equine septic arthritis proteome has been studied in a few previous studies. In one study, the time-resolved changes in protein composition focusing on potential biomarkers of eradication of infection was analyzed using label-free DDA-MS. Arthritis was experimentally induced in horses by intra-articular injection of either very low amounts of LPS or a clinical strain of E. coli,49 and it was shown that SF levels of the sensitive inflammatory biomarker SAA increased in response to bacterial infection and peaked at day 4, which corresponds to how naturally occurring septic arthritis evolves in horses. High concentrations of SAA determined by immunoassays has been demonstrated in several studies on equine septic arthritis.25,64,65 In another study, the SF proteome of horses with clinical septic arthritis was characterized by the use of MS but with no time-resolved observations,15 and similar to our results, LTF was identified to be a potentially useful biomarker in equine arthritis.

The SF proteome is maintained by influx and efflux of proteins from the surrounding tissues, and it is known that the permeability of the synovial membrane increases markedly in response to septic arthritis and results in a more systemic inflammatory response affecting many organs in the body.48 But a detailed understanding of the factors behind the control of the SF proteome in health and disease is still missing. Previously, the dynamics in the blood plasma proteome were investigated in mice, which unraveled the extent to which various tissues contributed to alterations in plasma protein composition in sepsis.19 We used the protein–organ distribution atlas generated here to unravel the potential tissue distribution of proteins identified in SF from joints with LPS-induced arthritis, which is important information to strengthen our knowledge of the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Another benefit of knowing the protein origin is that tissue-specific biomarkers can be of great value. The role and involvement of subchondral bone in joint pathology is becoming increasingly recognized, and specific biomarkers from cartilage and subchondral bone are important to better understand the pathophysiology of joint tissue degeneration and staging joint pathology. Several proteins identified in SF from joints with LPS-induced arthritis were also predominantly identified in healthy cartilage and/or subchondral bone samples, thus suggesting these tissues as the potential origin of the protein (Supplementary File 7). As an example, matrix Gla protein (MGP) was found in SF from horses with LPS-induced arthritis. MGP is bone-specific and involved in ossification,66 and the tissue in which it was predominantly identified in our sample material was subchondral bone. Other proteins identified in SF also originated from the articular tissues. Thrombospondin 1 (THBS1) was more abundant in cartilage, and the cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP) and chondroadherin (CHAD) were more abundant in cartilage and subchondral bone. CHAD is involved in collagen organization and has previously been identified in the equine distal radius zone of Ranvier during normal chondrocyte differentiation.67 Based on results from in vitro studies, CHAD has been proposed to be involved in chronic inflammatory joint diseases in humans,68 but its role in equine arthritis and value as a specific biomarker will need further investigation.

Some proteins identified in the SF from joints with LPS-induced arthritis might originate from tissues outside the semiclosed joint environment. As examples, adiponectin D had a high abundance in adipose tissue, SERPINA7 displayed the highest abundance in the spleen, and the scavenger receptor cysteine rich family member with five domains (SSC5D) involved in the innate defense mechanisms was primarily identified in the intestine (jejunum, cecum, and colon). These proteins may accumulate in SF because of the inflammation-induced increases in the synovial membrane permeability. The concentrations of these proteins might also increase systemically upon release from the organs of origin and serve as systemic markers for joint inflammation.

Limitations of This Study

There are some limitations to our study. First, the study does not cover all very low abundance potentially tissue-specific proteins. This could have been achieved by the use of enrichment/depletion strategies, longer LC gradients, and/or by the use of fractionation but at the cost of more instrument time. Previous studies have shown that fractionation might benefit identification of low abundant proteins, but the number of identified peptide and protein groups was lower compared to the smaller nonfractionated libraries.1,69 Second, we observed many duplicate gene names. Although redundant sequences were deleted from the reference proteome before data analysis, many related, although not identical, sequences were identified and mapped to the same gene name (Supplementary File 5). These may reflect either different protein isoforms, potential point mutations, or lack of consensus about the sequence. The quality of the equine reference database is constantly improving and more proteins might become annotated and provide certainty about the protein sequences. Reanalysis of the data in some years might provide an even more extensive insight into the horse proteome and tissue distribution. The MS instrument selected for analysis also imposes a limitation. The hybrid quadrupole–orbitrap instrument Q Exactive HF-X used for analysis is less sensitive and has lower selectivity than the updated version, Orbitrap Exploris 480, and use of the latter might have increased the number of proteins identified. Employing a type of MS instrument that combines a linear ion trap with an orbitrap analyzer could also have affected both the number and type of proteins identified.70 The rapid advancements in MS technology keep improving the sensitivity and resolution of the instruments. At the time of sample analysis, the Q Exactive HF-X instrument used was state of the art and the instrument is still a valuable tool for high-throughput screening in proteomics.70 Another limitation of this study was the long storage time (∼3 years) at −80 °C for the SF samples from joints with LPS-induced experimental arthritis. This could have caused degradation of some proteins and potentially affected the results. However, optimal storage conditions (−80 °C) and avoidance of repeated freeze–thaw cycles has been shown to preserve the proteins in bodily fluids intact for years.71 Due to the low number of individuals (n = 3), significance analyses were not performed. Yet, our results may still have biological relevance, and future studies may build on the proteomes identified and proteins highlighted as potential markers for disease. Lastly, we used a normalization method for global analysis that is known to reduce variation significantly. Data from many different tissues are very diverse in terms of protein numbers identified and abundance level, and VSN normalization is a statistical method aimed at making the sample variances nondependent from their mean intensities and bringing the samples onto a same scale, but with the consequence that log FCs are consistently underestimated.31 This means that the protein dynamics for global analysis might be compromised. On the other hand, use of nonimputed and median-normalized data as done in the other analyses included in this study might skew the protein abundance when comparing across different types of samples. To mitigate bias as much as possible, samples were prepared by the same person following the exact same protocol, and we trust that the findings highlighted are of biological relevance.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we present an extensive equine SL publicly available to enhance DIA-based data analysis in equine research. We demonstrated how the SL can advance our understanding of the tissue origin of proteins and how the different tissues influence the SF proteome in health and disease. The capability of the library to support the specific application for which the data set was intended and identification of potential biomarkers for tissue-specific diseases such as arthritis showcase the utility of the SL in equine research.
