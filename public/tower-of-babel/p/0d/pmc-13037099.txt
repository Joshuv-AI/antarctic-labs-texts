Unraveling the microbiomes contributing to biodeterioration dynamics of limestone heritage at the Longmen Grottoes archeological site
Authors: Xiaoying Zhang, Chaolong Ma, Fasi Wu, Xiaobo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Longmen Grottoes archeological site is a representative of the UNESCO World Heritage. Unfortunately, the long-term exposure to the outdoor environment has caused severe damage to the limestone heritage at this archeological site due to microbial colonization and biodeterioration. However, a lack of understanding of the microbiomes and mechanisms involved in biodeterioration processes has largely restricted the development of sustainable conservation of the heritage there.ResultsHere, we systematically compared physicochemical characteristics between the low and high biodeterioration caves, identified the keystone microbial communities and functions that shape the biodeterioration dynamics, and explored the biogeochemical cycles of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur that drive the biodeterioration divergence. As a result, physicochemical parameters of the bio-deteriorated rocks of the caves suggest a substantial divergence of biodeterioration. Microbial community structures and functions revealed that the metabolic potential of carbon fixation, nitrification, and denitrification processes shape the biodeterioration dynamics. The results strongly suggest that nitrification is a major contributor to the observed biodeterioration divergence.ConclusionsWe revealed that cyanobacteria, as the main organic carbon producer, support the development of microbiomes that drive the biogeochemical cycles of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur. Importantly, corrosion of minerals by microbial acids through ammonia oxidation and nitrification is the main consequence of the biodeterioration dynamics. Our findings will provide a basis for sustainable conservation of outdoor stone heritage from microbially induced biodeterioration. Introduction

The Longmen Grottoes archeological site comprises 2345 caves and over 110,000 Buddhist statues, which is one of the most influential repositories of Buddhist art in China. Spanning over 1400 years, the site showcases exquisite sculptural craftsmanship and a unique artistic style that reflects the integration of multiple cultural elements. This has established the Longmen Grottoes as a distinctive representation of Central Plains Buddhist cave art and an essential landmark in the history of global art. In 2000, the site was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

The carvings at the Longmen Grottoes were executed primarily on limestone, which is a representative building stone material widely used in Chinese cave temples and carvings. Due to its high porosity and chemical reactivity, it is particularly susceptible to weathering and erosion, making it a typical model for studying the deterioration and decay mechanisms of stone cultural heritage. The archeological site is in the Central Plains region of China, where a temperate continental monsoon climate prevails. Combined with the hydrological influence of the Yi River, the site experiences continuous water seepage and fluctuations in temperature and humidity [1]. These environmental factors lead to substantial microbial colonization, which results in severe biodeterioration of the statues, such as discoloration, crust formation, cracking, and flaking [2]. Therefore, a thorough diagnosis of microbiomes at the archeological site is crucial for the development and implementation of effective and sustainable conservation strategies [3].

The instability and biodeterioration of stone monuments are often the result of multiple interacting factors [4]. As early as 1945, Polynov proposed that the presence of lichens and fungal hyphae is a key driver of biological weathering in rocks [5]. Previous studies have confirmed that microorganisms colonizing stone surfaces are the major contributors to the deterioration of stone cultural heritage [6]. Although microorganisms may not cause immediate or large-scale damage to rocks, their colonization and metabolic activities often result in long-term impacts, which therefore pose a great challenge to the sustainable conservation of stone heritage [7, 8]. Microorganisms attach to and entrap communities of different functions, including bacteria, fungi, algae, and lichens, gradually forming complex biofilm structures on rock surfaces and influencing the chemical properties of the rock surface [3, 9]. Specifically, phototrophic and/or chemolithotrophic organisms first provide organic substances for heterotrophic colonizers, forming a consortium through synergistically driving biogeochemical cycles of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur [10–13]. Epilithic biofilms produce inorganic and organic acids that dissolve minerals, resulting in the formation of secondary minerals and the destabilization of mineral crystal structures [14, 15]. In turn, the acidolysis may increase rock porosity and microfractures, offering more niches for microbial colonization and growth. This process has been reported as the main mechanism of the stone biodeterioration [16]. However, the microbiomes on the stone monuments of the Longmen Grottoes and the impact of their activity on biodeterioration remain unclear. Thus, exploring the functional microorganisms and their metabolisms will help elucidate the biodeterioration processes taking place on stone monuments.

This study aims to examine the microbial community composition in the Longmen Grottoes and explore how microbially driven carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur biogeochemical cycles contribute to the biodeterioration dynamics in an outdoor environment. We first compared physiochemical characteristics between low and high bio-deteriorated rocks, subsequently explored the microbial community structures and functions that shape the biodeterioration divergence, and finally elucidated the key microbial metabolisms that conduce to the biodeterioration dynamics. Our findings will provide a basis for sustainable conservation of outdoor stone heritage from microbially induced biodeterioration.

Methods

Sample collection and climatic characteristics

In July of 2023, about 15 g of bio-deteriorated rocks were collected in three replicates from the surfaces of each stone statue in seven caves (i.e., C1381, C404, C1609, C1787, C1744, C712, and C159, as shown in Fig. S1). To ensure no destruction to the cultural heritage materials, the rock samples were gently collected into sterile sampling bottles using sterile tweezers and packaged with ice bags before being transported to the laboratory for further analyses. Considering the local climate characteristics, which are crucial for understanding the mechanisms and trends of biodeterioration of stone cultural heritage in open environments, we compiled climate data from the month before sampling (data provided by the Dongshan Meteorological Station of the Longmen Grottoes). The data included daily average maximum and minimum temperatures as well as relative humidity (Fig. S2).

Physicochemical analysis

Cave dripping water collected as the control sample was first filtered using 0.22-μm pore size membranes, and the filtrate was collected and stored at −20 °C for chemical analysis. The bio-deteriorated rocks were ground into powder before being fully mixed with distilled water in a 1:5 ratio by weight. After being centrifuged at 5000 g for 5 min, the supernatant was used for subsequent physicochemical analysis, and the pellets were collected for DNA extraction [17].

To observe microbial colonization of the stone materials, rock fragments that had detached from the surfaces of statues were collected and thinly sliced for further microscopy analysis. First, the rock fragments were embedded using epoxy resin and hardener (Epo Thin TM 2). Once solidified, the samples were polished using a grinding and polishing machine (MetaServ 250 Grinder-Polisher, Buehler, USA). The embedded sample cross-sections were then observed using a polarizing microscope (CH-9435, Leica Microsystems, Germany). Following this, new samples in a dried state were selected, fixed onto a two-sided conductive adhesive carbon tape, and spray-gold coated for 60 s. High-resolution images were then obtained using a JEOL JSM-6610LV Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM, Japan). The working parameters were: 15 kV accelerating voltage, 25 mm electro focusing, 1 Na current probe, and 60–300 s spectral collection [18].

For physicochemical analysis, the concentrations of Cl−, NO2−, NO3−, and SO42− of the samples were detected using a Thermo Fisher ICS-900 anion chromatograph equipped with an AS19 anion exchange column using KOH solution as the eluent, whereas the concentrations of Na+, NH4+, K+, Mg2+, and Ca2+ were determined by a Thermo Fisher ICS-600 cation chromatograph equipped with a Dionex IonPac CS12A cation exchange column using 20 mM methanesulfonic acid as the eluent. Additionally, the contents of Fe and Al in all the samples were analyzed using an inductively coupled plasma (Thermo Scientific™ iCAP™ PRO Series ICP-OES). The pH value was simultaneously determined with a pH meter (Sartorius PB-10, Germany).

Extensive research has confirmed that microbial activities are the main reason for the biodeterioration of limestone, mainly through the dissolution of minerals. Based on the physicochemical characteristics, the samples were classified into high and low biodeterioration categories (as shown in Fig. 1). Specifically, high/low status was assigned based on visual characterization of biofilms and then substantiated based on calcium content (above/below ~ 500 mg/L). To initially identify the biodeterioration levels of the samples, microscopy analysis was conducted. As a result, different microstructures, such as irregular particles, textures, and spot-like penetrations, were clearly profiled on the samples (Fig. 1a−f). These profiles may be traces of biodeterioration processes [19]. Based on these differences in microstructures, we generally classified the samples into low (i.e., C1381, C1609, and C404) and high (C1787, C1744, C712, and C159) biodeterioration for further analysis (Fig. S1).

DNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing

For each sample, genomic DNA was extracted from 0.25 g of the centrifuged pellets using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (QIAGEN, GER), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA concentrations were used to estimate the biomass in the samples (Fig. S3a) [20]. The purity and concentration of the DNA extracts were measured using the Thermo NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Specific primers were used for PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA gene (V4-V5 region) with a 12 bp barcode for Bacteria (515F: 5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′ and 907R: 5′-CCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT-3′) and Archaea (524F10extF: 5′-TGYCAGCCGCCGCGGTAA-3′ and Arch958RmodR: 5′-YCCGGCGTTGAVTCCAATT-3′) [21, 22]. The PCR reaction system (50 μL) for bacterial amplification was composed of 25 μL of TAKARA TAP polymerase (Takara, Japan), 1 μL of each primer, 50 ng of DNA template, and 1 μL of bovine serum albumin (BSA). For archaeal amplification, the reaction system was the same, except that 2.5 μL of each primer was used and no BSA was added. PCR amplification was performed under the following thermal cycling conditions: an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 52 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. To avoid the bias of primer dimers or other non-specific bands/products, the PCR products were examined with 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and purified using the E.Z.N.A. ® Gel Extraction Kit (Omega, USA). Library construction was performed according to the NEBNext ® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina ® (New England Biolabs, USA), followed by high-throughput sequencing of the constructed amplicon library on the Illumina Nova 6000 (PE250) platform (Guangdong Magigene Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). All raw sequencing data were available in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) under the accession number PRJNA1273662.

qPCR analysis of functional genes responsible for C, N, and S biogeochemical cycles

Microbial colonizers on the stone statues could cause biodeterioration of surfaces by driving biogeochemical cycles. To provide genetic evidence for the metabolic processes involved in biodeterioration, high-throughput qPCR functional gene chip (QMEC) reactions were conducted on the samples, which contained a total of 72 primer pairs [23, 24]. The chip mainly targeted key functional genes involved in the carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and sulfur (S) biogeochemical cycles (Table S1). qPCR reactions and fluorescence signal detection were performed using the SmartChip Real-Time PCR System. Quality control and gene statistics were performed based on the Ct values for each gene in the samples provided by the SmartChip Real-Time PCR System. In any case, a gene was considered positive only if detected in all three technical replicates. The absolute quantification of the 16S rRNA gene was obtained using the Roche LightCycler480II. Based on the absolute quantification of the 16S rRNA gene, the absolute quantities (copies/ng DNA) of each functional gene in the samples were calculated (Supporting Appendix 1). Gene abundance heatmaps and gene family stacking plots for each sample were generated based on the absolute quantitative data [25].

Identification of keystone taxa and construction of core functional gene network

A molecular ecological network was constructed based on the relative abundance of bacterial and archaeal OTUs in the samples (http://ieg2.ou.edu/MENA/) [26]. The network construction referred to the methodology of Zhou et al. [27], which included data preprocessing, similarity matrix construction, and conversion of the adjacency matrix based on random matrix theory (RMT). In this study, only OTUs that appeared in at least four of the seven samples were retained [28]. More topological features of the cooccurrence networks could be found in Supporting Appendix 2. The constructed network was visualized using Gephi-0.10.1. Based on network modularity and modularity calculations, the module intra-connectivity (Zi) and inter-connectivity (Pi) of each node were determined. OTUs were classified into four roles based on different combinations of Zi and Pi, such as Peripheral nodes, Connectors, Module hubs, and Network hubs, from which key OTUs (or keystone taxa) in the samples were selected if Zi > 2.5 and Pi > 0.62. More information on the classification of key OTUs can be found in Supporting Appendix 2.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and visualization were performed using R version 4.3.2, Origin version 2019, and GraphPad Prism version 9.5. Physicochemical analysis data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three replicates. The significance of differences between samples was tested using IBM SPSS Statistics 25 (ANOVA). Metabolic pathway diagrams were created using Adobe Illustrator CC 2018.

The raw paired-end sequencing data of bacterial/archaeal samples were quality trimmed using sliding window quality trimming (-W 4 -M 20) with FASTQ (version 0.14.1, https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp). Primers at both ends of the sequences were removed using Cutadapt (https://github.com/marcelm/cutadapt/) based on primer information, yielding quality-controlled paired-end Clean Reads. Subsequently, paired-end Clean Reads were merged using usearch -fastq_mergepairs (V10, http://www.drive5.com/usearch/) based on overlap between the PE reads, with default parameters including a minimum overlap length of 16 bp and a maximum mismatch of 5 bp in the overlapping region. Tags that did not meet these criteria were filtered out to obtain Raw Tags. Finally, FASTQ was used for quality filtering of the Raw Tags to obtain valid Clean Tags. OTU clustering (at 97% similarity) and taxonomic annotation of the Clean Tags were performed using the SILVA 138 database and the bioinformatics software QIIME 2. Finally, a total of 622,051 sequence count reads were obtained. To minimize biases introduced by sequencing depth differences, the samples were rarefied before subsequent ecological analysis (bacteria were standardized to at least 41,076 Feature Counts; archaea were standardized to at least 37,997 Feature Counts).

Results

Physiochemical parameters suggest a substantial divergence of biodeterioration

The archeological site prevailed with a temperate continental monsoon climate with distinct seasonal characteristics. We compiled a short-term snapshot of the local climate data from the month before sampling. Generally, the local average temperatures range from 23 to 33 °C. The highest temperature during the month exceeded 40 °C, while the lowest temperature was only 20 °C, with relative humidity fluctuating between 48 and 90% (Fig. S2). These climate conditions favor microbial colonization and metabolic activity on the stone heritage at this site.

Consistently, the concentrations of dissolved metal ions (e.g., Ca2+ and Mg2+) in the high biodeterioration samples were significantly higher than those in the low biodeterioration samples (Fig. 1g, h). Moreover, the dissolved metal levels in both the high and low biodeterioration samples were significantly higher than those in the cave dripping water (as the control). Beyond the contents of Al and Fe, the concentrations of Na+, K+, and Cl− were consistent with this pattern (Fig. S4). Dissolved minerals could be another trace of biodeterioration of stone [29], and these differences between the two types of samples suggest a substantial divergence of biodeterioration.

The dissolution of minerals is mainly attributed to corrosive acids released by microorganisms. We then examined the concentrations of NO3− and SO42− in the samples. The concentration of SO42− in the high biodeterioration samples was threefold that in the low biodeterioration ones, with the cave dripping water having the lowest SO42− concentration (Fig. 1i). Like SO42−, the concentration of NO3− or NO2− in the low biodeterioration samples was significantly lower than that in the high biodeterioration ones but not significantly lower than that in the cave dripping water (Fig. 1j). The higher concentrations of NH4+ in the high biodeterioration samples indicated potential higher contents of nitrogen source for nitrification process in these caves. These differences in the concentrations of NO3− and SO42− between the high and low biodeterioration samples suggested that the potential of biogenic inorganic acids of microbial activity could be a consequence of mineral dissolution. The pH values of all samples remained within the neutral or mildly alkaline range (Fig. S3b), suggesting that rock minerals may play a significant role in neutralizing acidic metabolites. Although microbial metabolic activity generates acidic substances, we hypothesize that this process may be coupled with the dissolution of alkaline minerals in the rock. This observation supports the hypothesis of microbially driven rock particle dissolution.

Although the statues of the Longmen Grottoes are consistent in location, rock composition, surface roughness, and orientation relative to the sun, some micro-environmental factors (e.g., water availability) might contribute to the divergence of geomicrobiological impact [30, 31]. Thus, we conclude that the difference in microbiomes is one of the main reasons for substantial divergence in biodeterioration processes taking place on the statues of the Longmen Grottoes.

Microbial community structures and functions support the biodeterioration divergence

Generally, the diversity and richness between the two types of samples indicated no significant difference (Fig. S5a and b). However, the shared OTUs between the two types of samples accounted for only 16% in bacterial communities but more than half in archaeal communities (Fig. S5c and d). Moreover, the abundance distribution of the top 20 most abundant bacterial and archaeal OTUs also showed significant divergence between the two types of samples (Fig. S5e and f). Taken together, such information indicated a significant difference in microbial community structures between the two types of samples.

Bacterial communities were mainly composed of Cyanobacteria, Actinobacteria, Chloroflexi, Proteobacteria, and Firmicutes (Fig. 2a). The relative abundance of phototrophic communities (e.g., Cyanobacteria and Chloroflexi) in the high biodeterioration samples was significantly higher than that in the low biodeterioration samples. In contrast, the relative abundance of Actinobacteria was significantly lower than that in the low biodeterioration samples. More specifically, the genus Crossiella accounted for almost 20% of the bacterial populations in the low biodeterioration samples, followed by Bacillus and Nitrospira (Fig. 2b). Differentially, the pigmented bacteria, such as Scytonema, Rubrobacter, and Chroococcidiopsis were predominated in the high biodeterioration samples. Archaeal populations in the low biodeterioration samples were mainly composed of three phyla Crenarchaeota (90%), Halobacterota (7%), and Thermoplasmatota (3%), whereas Crenarchaeota (accounting for about 75%) and Halobacterota (the remaining 25%) predominated in the high biodeterioration samples (Fig. 2c). At the genus level, the relative abundance of Candidatus_Nitrocosmicus, Candidatus_Nitrososphaera, Halococcus and Halalkalicoccus in the low biodeterioration samples were significantly higher than that in the high biodeterioration samples, whereas Nitrososphaeraceae and Halorussus were more abundant in the high biodeterioration ones (Fig. 2d). Overall, the differences of bacterial and archaeal communities between the low and high biodeterioration samples could explain the divergence of biodeterioration.

To explore further evidence on microbial community functions, we investigated the relationships between the predominant genera and the functional genes responsible for carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur metabolisms (Supporting Appendix 1).

In carbon metabolisms, the genes related to carbon fixation (e.g., pccA, frdA, accA, mct, smtA, acsABE, rbcL, and aclB) exhibited significant correlations with two cyanobacterial genera Chalicogloea_CCALA_975 and Scytonema_UTEX_2349 (Fig. 3a) and three genera of ammonia-oxidizing archaea (AOA) Candidatus_Nitrocosmicus, Nitrososphaeraceae, and Candidatus_Nitrososphaera (Fig. 3b). However, the genes involved in carbon degradation (e.g., cdh, mnp, sga, amyA, chiA, glx, manB, lig, and xylA) were mainly associated with the bacterial genera Microvirga, WD2101_soil_group, Tumebacillus, JG30-KF-CM45, and Blastocatella (Fig. 3a). Notably, the strong positive correlation between archaeal genera (Nitrososphaeraceae and Candidatus_Nitrososphaera) and the mct gene suggests that AOA colonizing oligotrophic stone statues may couple energy metabolism with carbon fixation to provide themselves with organic carbon sources (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, these three archaeal genera exhibited strong positive correlations with carbon degradation genes, such as amyA, naglu, sga, mnp, xylA, and cdh, speculating that AOA might obtain carbon skeletons by degrading complex organic carbons in oligotrophic stone environments [32, 33].

In nitrogen metabolisms, the cyanobacterial Scytonema_UTEX_2349 exhibited significantly positive correlations with the genes (e.g., nifH) involved in nitrogen fixation (Fig. 3c). The genus Nitrospira was correlated with the genes hao and nxrA responsible for the conversion of NO2− to NO3−. Moreover, strong oligotrophic adaptation and Comammox capability of Nitrospira provide it with an irreplaceable ecological niche in the nitrogen cycle [34]. In contrast, two bacterial genera, Bacillus and Microvirga, were positively correlated with the functional genes nosZ and nirK involved in denitrification, respectively. As mentioned above, three AOA genera Candidatus_Nitrocosmicus, Nitrososphaeraceae, and Candidatus_Nitrososphaera also mediated the nitrification and denitrification processes (Fig. 3d). For example, Candidatus_Nitrocosmicus was significantly positively correlated with the amoB gene responsible for ammonia oxidation [35]. Beyond them, Halococcus, Halorussus, and Marine_Group_II also showed significant correlation with the amoA gene.

In sulfur metabolism, the core gene soxY involved in sulfur oxidation, was significantly correlated with WD2101_soil_group, whereas the gene apsA in AKIW781 showed a significant correlation with sulfate reduction (Fig. 3c). However, few archaeal communities were observed to be associated with sulfur mechanisms. Given the differences in relative abundance of the functional communities between the low and high biodeterioration samples (Fig. 2), it is concluded that microbial community structures and functions support the divergence of biodeterioration.

Keystone communities are involved in the biodeterioration dynamics

To reveal the interactions within the microbial communities and identify keystone taxa, we constructed a bacterial-archaeal co-occurrence network (R2 = 0.818), which consists of 215 nodes and 464 edges, exhibiting strong modularity in community structure with 13 modules (Fig. 4a). In the 13 modules, any taxa with an abundance of less than 5% was assigned to the mini_model, such as Halobacterota and Myxococcota. The predominant Cyanobacteria were present in multiple modules (module_0/1/2/3/7), of which the members Chroococcidiopsis and Chalicogloea functioned as the core OTUs in correlation with the archaeal keystone taxa Candidatus_Nitrocosmicus and Candidatus_Nitrososphaera. This suggests the fact that Cyanobacteria work as the pioneers of microbial colonizers on stone monuments [36]. In contrast, the bacterial genus Nitrospira was negatively correlated with the genus WD2101_soil_group but positively correlated with certain genera of the phylum Cyanobacteria in module_2. Notably, Sporacetigenium, Bacillus, and Candidatus_Nitrososphaera were the core taxa with high connectivity in module_3. However, the three AOA genera Candidatus_Nitrocosmicus, Nitrososphaeraceae, and Candidatus_Nitrososphaera were dispersed across different modules in the network, showing intertwined positive and negative correlations. This distribution pattern suggests that ammonia-oxidizing communities maintain the stability of the nitrification process through spatial isolation and potential cross-group cooperation [37]. Also, the cross-module distribution, competition, and cooperation between taxa are crucial for maintaining the community stability, with the spatial intertwining of positive and negative correlations ensuring the homeostasis of key functions (e.g., carbon fixation and nitrification) within the community [38, 39].

Module hubs, connectors, and network hubs typically play ecological roles in maintaining module structural stability, facilitating material/energy flow between modules, and acting as “super hubs” in the network. Further intra and inter-module connectivity analysis confirmed the keystone taxa roles of Halobacterota, Crenarchaeota, Thermoplasmatota, Cyanobacteria, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteriota (Fig. 4b). Importantly, four out of six archaeal OTUs belong to the family Nitrososphaeraceae, suggesting that ammonia-oxidizing archaea may play a key role in driving the biodeterioration of the statues (Supporting Appendix 2). Overall, the network indicates that cyanobacteria and ammonia-oxidizing archaea form a core consortium likely supporting nitrification-driven acid production in high-deterioration caves.

Nitrification other than sulfur oxidation accounts for the biodeterioration divergence

To further explore metabolic evidence, we examined the differences in the abundance of functional genes responsible for carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling between the high and low-biodeterioration samples in the Longmen Grottoes (Table S1 and Supporting Appendix 1). The absolute quantitative values of each functional gene in the samples were derived from the absolute quantification of the 16S rRNA gene. Overall, the genes involved in carbon fixation exhibited the highest abundance in both low biodeterioration (21,524.86 copies/ng) and high biodeterioration (13,628.10 copies/ng) samples (Fig. S6a). This may be attributed to the low organic carbon and nutrient-poor environment on the Longmen Grottoes caves, where microbial communities often rely on carbon fixation to autonomously acquire carbon sources for growth [40]. As mentioned above, Cyanobacteria, as the keystone taxa responsible for carbon fixation among the samples, further support this hypothesis.

Microbial-driven carbon cycling and metabolic regulation are fundamental to the survival of microorganisms [41]. Generally, heatmaps of the abundance of key genes in carbon metabolism indicated that carbon fixation and partial carbon degradation in the low biodeterioration samples were more intense than in the high biodeterioration samples (Fig. S6b). Specifically, the acsA gene that encodes carbon monoxide dehydrogenase showed the highest abundance. The acsA gene participates in the reductive acetyl-CoA pathway, by which CO2 is reduced to acetyl-CoA, an important intermediate in the carbon cycle. Based on this, we focused on six key genes responsible for acetyl-CoA metabolism, including accA, acsA, aclB, acsE, and acsB (Fig. 5a). Consistently, the abundance of these genes in the low biodeterioration samples was higher than in the higher biodeterioration samples. Similarly, the key genes pccA and rbcL, which separately fix CO2 in carboxylation metabolism and the Calvin cycle [42–44], were more abundant in the low biodeterioration samples. In contrast, the genes (e.g., pmoA, mmoX, and mxaF) responsible for methane oxidation were rare in the low biodeterioration samples (Fig. S6b). These findings suggest that microbial communities in the Longmen Grottoes caves might adopt multiple metabolic strategies to adapt to the oligotrophic niches on stone [45, 46]. Moreover, dependent on the carbon fixation of autotrophic microorganisms, heterotrophic microorganisms secrete a variety of enzymes to degrade organic matter, facilitating the biogeochemical cycling of carbon. Typically, the genes abfA (encoding α-L-arabinofuranosidase) and sga (encoding β-glucosidase), which are responsible for cellulose decomposition, were much more abundant in the high biodeterioration samples. By activating these genes, microorganisms have adopted effective substrate utilization strategies, which are crucial for survival in the carbon-limited cave environment [47, 48].

In nitrogen cycle, six major transformation processes [35], such as assimilation, ammonification, nitrification, denitrification, anammox, and nitrogen fixation were discussed (Fig. 5a). Generally, the abundance of the genes responsible for nitrogen fixation, anammox and nitrification in the high biodeterioration samples were significantly higher than the low biodeterioration samples, whereas the genes responsible for denitrification, assimilation, and ammonification were more abundant in the low biodeterioration samples (Fig. S6c). The nifH gene, as a marker for detecting nitrogen-fixing microorganisms (encoding the iron protein subunit of nitrogenase), had a significantly higher abundance in the high biodeterioration samples (1532.71 copies/ng), compared to the low biodeterioration ones, indicating more nitrogen fixation present in the high biodeterioration caves. In contrast, the low biodeterioration samples showed higher abundances of the ureC and gdhA genes, suggesting that microbial communities in the low biodeterioration caves mainly rely on heterotrophic nitrogen fixation and ammonium assimilation. Importantly, nitrification-related genes (amoA, amoB, hao, nxrA) were generally more abundant in the high biodeterioration samples, especially amoA, which encodes ammonia monooxygenase (AMO) that catalyzes the oxidation of ammonia to hydroxylamine, a reaction considered the rate-limiting step in the entire nitrification process [49]. Therefore, it can be inferred that the potential of ammonia oxidation is higher in the high biodeterioration samples, suggesting the resulting nitric and/or nitrous acids might contribute to the acidic corrosion in the Longmen Grottoes. However, denitrification-related genes showed a higher abundance in the low biodeterioration samples, especially the nosZ gene that encodes nitrous oxide reductase, responsible for the final step of the denitrification process [50]. Additionally, the genes encoding copper-containing and heme-containing cd1 nitrite reductases (nirK and nirS) also showed higher abundance in the low biodeterioration samples, suggesting that denitrification reduces the accumulation of nitric and/or nitrous acids, thereby slowing down the biodeterioration caused by acidic corrosion.

In the sulfur cycling (Fig. 5c), although the genes soxY and yedZ responsible for sulfur oxidation were slightly higher in the low biodeterioration samples, there was no significant difference (Fig. S6d). Similarly, the genes associated with sulfate reduction also had no significant difference in abundance between the two types of samples. This finding suggests that sulfur cycling is not the main reason for the biodeterioration divergence.

Discussion

Stone cultural heritage in outdoor environments is usually colonized by microbial communities. These epilithic microorganisms can grow on stone heritage, which is a typical oligotrophic environment of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur sources, through synergistic cooperation of metabolisms. The diversified composition of microbial communities on the surface may be due to the complementary metabolic activities of different microbial species [51]. This phenomenon is particularly evident in the complex interactions between phototrophic and heterotrophic microorganisms. They could cooperate to drive the key biogeochemical cycles, which are crucial for the deterioration of stone surfaces [52]. To unravel the microbiomes driving biodeterioration dynamics of stone heritage at the Longmen Grottoes archeological site, we first compared physiochemical characteristics between low and high bio-deteriorated rocks, subsequently explored the microbial community structures and functions that shape the biodeterioration divergence, and finally elucidated the key microbial metabolisms that contribute to the biodeterioration dynamics. Our findings will provide a basis for sustainable conservation of outdoor stone heritage from microbially induced biodeterioration.

The microbial community works as a whole, which leads to the biodeterioration of stone cultural heritage. High concentrations of nitrate produced by ammonia-oxidizing bacteria and archaea have caused biodeterioration of sandstone monuments at the Angkor Wat in Cambodia [53]. It is revealed that microbial communities on stone monuments transform limestone into gypsum through sulfur metabolism, and filamentous cyanobacteria and fungi cause biomechanical damage [54]. These diverse microbial interactions create a dynamic and interconnected community, leading to an increased rate of biodeterioration. Comparative investigation of the low and high bio-deteriorated stone monuments will greatly help to determine the predominant microbial players of the biodeterioration processes and elucidate biochemical mechanisms underpinning the processes [55]. Our study has systematically investigated microbial communities and functions involved in the biodeterioration of the limestone monuments in the Longmen Grottoes, which will promote the development of practices for stone heritage conservation.

Corrosion of minerals by biogenic acids is one of the main mechanisms underpinning the biodeterioration of stone cultural heritage. On the sandstone of the Antarctic Dry Valleys, endolithic microbial communities accumulate oxalate that leads to crustal flaking, a cyclical process that repeats every 10,000 years [56]. Our previous study on the Leizhou Stone Dog indicated that sulfur-oxidizing bacteria (e.g., Sulfurovum and Acidiphilium) predominated in the microbiomes and generated gypsum as a consequence of blackening of the basalt sculptures [57]. In this study, the high biodeterioration and low biodeterioration samples from the Longmen Grottoes have accumulated more NO3− and SO42− than the dripping water inside the caves, especially in the high biodeterioration caves (Fig. 1), which will undoubtedly lead to more intense dissolution of the limestone. Together with Spearman correlation analysis between functional genes and physicochemical characteristics (Fig. S7), we conclude microbial metabolic activity of acid production could be one of the reasons for biodeterioration of the limestone monuments in the Longmen Grottoes. Despite this, qualitative (e.g., visual or microscopy observations) and quantitative (e.g., crust thickness, microfractures, and mineral loss) analyses could be systematically considered to standardize the biodeterioration levels of stone heritage, which will eventually generate a simple index or scoring system to justify the binary categories.

Microorganisms colonizing stone monuments participate in biodeterioration processes by driving biogeochemical cycles of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur [58]. It is well known that cyanobacteria and other microalgae are pioneer colonizers that create an organic environment on stone monuments. Beyond carbon fixation, cyanobacteria can penetrate carbonates and absorb Ca2+ to promote the dissolution of calcium carbonate [59]. Consistently, cyanobacteria in our study are the predominant taxa, which are associated with carbon and nitrogen fixation in cave ecosystems, influencing nutrient allocation and driving community succession as the primary producers [60]. Interestingly, the detection of the functional genes responsible for methane oxidation suggested that formic acid might be another case of acidic corrosion. This finding will provide new insights for the study of biological corrosion mechanisms of stone monuments. We are particularly excited to have detected the presence of Nitrospirota, which are typical nitrifying bacteria in the deterioration of building stones [7]. Moreover, co-occurrence network topology analysis revealed that archaeal members of the family Nitrososphaeraceae responsible for ammonia oxidation and nitrification processes were the keystone taxa, consistent with our previous studies at Angkor Wat [61]. Nitrification rather than sulfur oxidation predominated in the high biodeterioration caves and resulted in the higher concentration of nitrate, suggesting that nitrification is the main factor driving the biodeterioration dynamics. However, the contribution of nitrification to biodeterioration still needs to be further validated through direct experimental confirmation (e.g., controlled pH or mineral loss assays).

In conclusion, the biodeterioration of stone monuments in the Longmen Grottoes is the result of microbial synergistic metabolisms of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling. Importantly, the keystone nitrifying bacteria and archaea determined the biodeterioration dynamics on the monuments. Although we have provided a comprehensive explanation of the microbiomes driving the biodeterioration dynamics of limestone heritage at the Longmen Grottoes, further confirmation of transcriptomic, metabolomic, or chemical evidence is required to expand our understanding of how microbial activity specifically influences the biodeterioration of limestone monuments. Metatranscriptomics can directly reveal the expression of relevant acid-producing pathways in the metabolically active microbial communities involved in the carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles, addressing the limitations of relying solely on community structure-based assessments of “potential”. Furthermore, stable isotope probing (SIP) techniques can be employed to trace specific metabolic pathways, providing additional insights into the molecular mechanisms of microbial interactions with the stone materials. Such efforts will ultimately confirm the proposed mechanisms by integrating multidimensional evidence.
