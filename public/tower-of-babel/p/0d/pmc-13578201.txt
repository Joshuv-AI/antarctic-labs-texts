Development and validation of a novel T cell exhaustion-related signature to predict prognosis in patients with breast cancer
Authors: Lianhe Guo, Xiangjin Chen, Fan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer is the most prevalent malignant tumor in women globally, with its prognosis linked to immune responses, especially CD8 + and CD4 + T cell infiltration. T cell exhaustion, an immune dysfunction seen in chronic infections and cancers, is not well understood in breast cancer. This study seeks to investigate the role and prognostic significance of genes related to T cell exhaustion in breast cancer through bioinformatics, offering insights into the mechanisms of T cell exhaustion in this disease.MethodsBreast cancer sample data from UCSC Xena and GEO databases underwent differential gene expression analysis with DESeq2. ssGSEA and WGCNA assessed T cell exhaustion-related genes. Key prognostic genes were identified through GO and KEGG analyses and PPI network construction. A prognostic model was developed using univariate Cox, Lasso regression, and multivariate Cox analyses, and its predictive performance was validated with an external dataset. Functional and immune infiltration characteristics of the prognostic genes were explored using GSEA and CIBERSORT.ResultsThe study identified 2,989 differentially expressed genes and 832 key module genes. Enrichment analysis indicated that these genes were associated with immune dysfunction and T cell exhaustion‑related pathways. A risk model was developed incorporating six prognostic genes: S100B, BCL2A1, RSPH1, KCNJ10, ZMYND10, and MOB3B. Using an appropriate cutoff, patients were stratified into low-risk and high-risk groups, with the overall survival (OS) curves of these groups exhibiting significant differences. The model’s efficacy was validated using external datasets. Furthermore, the estimated IC50 values of multiple anticancer drugs showed differences between the two risk groups, warranting further investigation in the context of breast cancer therapy.ConclusionThis study uses bioinformatic analyses to highlight the prognostic importance and mechanisms of T cell exhaustion-related genes in breast cancer, offering insights for therapeutic targets that could enhance clinical management and immunotherapy strategies.

Introduction

Breast cancer is a prevalent malignancy among women globally, with rising incidence and ranking as the second leading cause of cancer deaths in women [1]. It is a heterogeneous disease with varied causes and manifestations, classified by the expression of specific receptors [2]. Risk factors include age, family history, obesity, smoking, alcohol, and lifestyle. Despite advances in treatment, such as surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, hormone therapy, and immunotherapy, there is a need for innovative methods to identify high-risk patients and personalize treatment strategies [3].

Breast cancer prognosis is closely tied to immune activity, with significant inflammatory cell infiltration observed in tumor tissues [4]. Research indicates that CD8⁺ T cell density correlates with immune evasion in BRCA, while both CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cell infiltration is linked to patient outcomes [5]. Thus, identifying reliable immune-related predictors and prognostic biomarkers is essential for enhancing outcomes in high-risk patients and tailoring treatment strategies.

One of the most transformative advances in cancer research over recent decades has been the application of DNA microarray technology and next-generation sequencing [6]. Rapid advances in high throughput sequencing have made bioinformatics indispensable in cancer research. Multi step bioinformatics metheds integrating differential expression analysis, WGCNA, machine learning, and PPI networks have been widely used to identify novel biomarkers and elucidate disease mechanisms across biomedical fields [7–9]. These technologies provide insights into individual genetic profiles beyond clinicopathological features, enabling more precise and personalized treatment approaches. T cells are categorized into CD4⁺ helper and CD8⁺ cytotoxic subsets. T cell exhaustion, initially observed in chronic viral infections in mice, occurs due to persistent antigen stimulation, inhibitory signaling, and chronic inflammation [10]. This dysfunctional state is common in chronic infections and cancer, characterized by prolonged antigen exposure and increased inhibitory receptor expression [11].

Local inflammation is mainly caused by infections or environmental factors, while systemic inflammation is linked to obesity and metabolic issues. In tumor development, these inflammatory responses interact, leading to a shift from acute to chronic inflammation and causing T cell exhaustion. Chronic inflammation is crucial in tumor growth and spread, involving immune cells and cytokines that promote T cell exhaustion [12]. The exact mechanisms of T cell exhaustion in breast cancer (BRCA) are not well understood. This study uses bioinformatics to analyze T cell exhaustion-related genes in breast cancer, aiming to uncover their prognostic value and underlying mechanisms, potentially enhancing our understanding of T cell exhaustion in BRCA.

To construct a robust and biologically interpretable prognostic model, we adopted a multi‑step screening strategy. First, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between breast cancer and normal tissues were identified to ensure that candidate genes were associated with disease status. Second, weighted gene co‑expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed to identify gene modules closely correlated with the T cell exhaustion (TCE) score, thereby focusing the analysis on co‑expression networks relevant to our core phenotype. The intersection of DEGs and WGCNA‑derived module genes retained both disease‑associated genes and genes functionally linked to TCE, reducing false positives. Third, functional enrichment analysis (GO/KEGG) and protein‑protein interaction (PPI) network analysis were conducted on the 222 intersecting candidates to preliminarily assess their biological relevance. Finally, univariate Cox regression, Lasso regression, and multivariate Cox regression were sequentially applied to screen prognostic genes and construct the risk model.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and processing

We acquired the TCGA-BRCA dataset from UCSC Xena (as of July 19, 2024), containing expression matrices and clinical data for breast cancer (BRCA) and adjacent normal samples. Gene expression was normalized using TPM and log2-transformed for analysis. We included 1,171 samples (1,072 BRCA and 99 normal) after screening, with 1,050 BRCA samples with complete survival data used for training. The GSE42568 dataset from GEO, with 121 samples (104 BRCA and 17 normal), served as an external validation set.

The 36 T cell exhaustion‑related genes (TCE‑RGs, listed in Table 1) used in this study were identified through a systematic literature review, primarily referring to experimentally validated T cell exhaustion markers in chronic viral infection and tumor immunity [13–15]. Although this gene set may not cover all genes related to T cell exhaustion, it includes widely recognized core exhaustion‑associated molecules (e.g., PDCD1/PD‑1, CTLA4, LAG3, HAVCR2/TIM‑3, TIGIT) and key transcriptional regulators (e.g., TBX21, EOMES, PRDM1). This gene set has been extensively used for T cell exhaustion enrichment analyses and thus has good representativeness and acceptance in the field.

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis between breast cancer (1,072 samples) and adjacent normal (99 samples) was performed using DESeq2 in R. Raw count data were used as input, and the default internal normalization was applied. Genes with an absolute log₂ fold change over 1 and an adjusted p-value below 0.05 were identified as differentially expressed. Results were visualized with a volcano plot via ggplot2 and a heatmap using ComplexHeatmap to show expression patterns.

ssGSEA analysis

The ssGSEA enrichment score was calculated using the full set of 36 TCE‑RGs (as listed in Table 1). All 36 genes were present in the TCGA‑BRCA expression matrix; therefore, no gene was omitted from the analysis. The ‘GSVA’ R package was used with default parameters (weight exponent = 0.25), and the resulting enrichment scores were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank‑sum test to explore TCE-RGs’ role in BRCA pathogenesis. To examine the link between the risk score and breast cancer survival, we divided BRCA samples in the training cohort into high‑ and low‑risk groups using the optimal cutoff determined by the surv_cutpoint function from the ‘survminer’ R package with minprop = 0.45 to ensure that neither group contained less than 45% of the total samples. Kaplan–Meier curves were then generated to compare survival differences between the two groups.

WGCNA analysis and identification of key module genes

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was conducted using the all 1,072 BRCA samples by “WGCNA” R package. An optimal soft‑thresholding power (β = 7) was selected to achieve a scale‑free network topology, yielding a scale‑free fit R² of 0.90. An unsigned network was constructed to preserve both positive and negative correlations. Gene similarity was calculated, and a co-expression network with a hierarchical clustering tree was generated. Key gene modules were identified using a dynamic tree-cutting algorithm with deepSplit = 2 and a minimum module size of 200 genes. Pearson correlation analysis was used to identify modules significantly associated with TCE-RGs scores, with thresholds of |correlation coefficient| > 0.3 and p-value < 0.05. Module membership (MM) and gene significance (GS) were calculated, and genes with |MM| > 0.6 and |GS| > 0.2 were identified as key TCE-RGs-related module genes.

Construction and validation of a prognostic model based on candidate genes

Candidate genes were identified by intersecting DEGs with key module genes from WGCNA and visualized using “ggvenn.” Functional enrichment analysis for GO and KEGG pathways was performed with “clusterProfiler” (P < 0.05), and results were visualized using “ggpubr” and “treemap.” A PPI network was created using the STRING database and visualized in Cytoscape. Univariate Cox regression on TCGA-BRCA data was conducted using “survival” R package, filtering for HR ≠ 1 and P < 0.05. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption was tested for each gene using Schoenfeld residual tests via the cox.zph() function. Genes with P > 0.05 were considered to satisfy the PH assumption and were retained for subsequent Lasso regression and multivariate Cox regression, developing a prognostic risk model using 1,050 BRCA samples with complete survival data. The model’s evaluation involved plotting ROC curves with the “survivalROC” R package and determining the optimal risk cutoff using the surv_cutpoint function from “survminer.” BRCA patients were then categorized into high- and low-risk groups, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated. The model’s generalizability was validated using the GSE42568 dataset. An independent prognostic analysis with clinical data was conducted, and a nomogram was created to predict patient outcomes.

Enrichment analysis based on prognostic genes and tumor immune microenvironment characterization

Spearman correlation between prognostic and expressed genes was analyzed with the “psych” R package. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) used the “clusterProfiler” R package and c2.cp.kegg.v7.0.symbols.gmt file, identifying pathways with |NES| > 1 and P.adjust < 0.05. Prognostic gene similarity was assessed via “GOSemSim.” CIBERSORT with LM22 estimated 22 immune cell types in BRCA samples, and “ggplot2” visualized infiltration differences between risk groups. Correlations between prognostic genes and immune cells were analyzed with “psych” and visualized with “ggcor.” ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATEScore were calculated using “estimate,” compared with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and shown in violin plots. Tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion were assessed in BRCA subtypes using the TIDE database to measure immune escape activity.

Analysis of cancer-related pathways and drug sensitivity based on prognostic genes

Using the GSCALite database, we examined the link between prognostic genes and 10 common cancer pathways in the TCGA-BRCA dataset, visualizing results with a pie chart. The Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database provided detailed information on drug sensitivity and molecular markers in cancer cells, integrating cellular, pharmacological, and molecular data. Utilizing data from 198 chemotherapeutic and targeted agents, the “oncoPredict” R package estimated the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values for each drug in BRCA samples based on risk group.

Statistical analysis

In this study, data analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.2.2). Variables with normal distribution were assessed using the unpaired Student’s t-test, while those with non-normal distribution were examined using the Wilcoxon test. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated through both univariable and multivariate Cox regression models. For differential expression analysis using DESeq2, the Benjamini‑Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method was applied for multiple testing correction, and an adjusted p‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For other analyses (e.g., GSEA), the same BH method was used where applicable. Significance levels are denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, **p < 0.001.

The key gene module identified by WGCNA

Using the BRCA samples from the TCGA-BRCA dataset for WGCNA analysis, we performed clustering to remove outliers, ensuring accurate results. Figure 2A shows no outliers, allowing us to proceed with WGCNA. To ensure gene interactions fit a scale-free distribution, we used the gene expression matrix to determine the soft threshold. As depicted in Fig. 2B, when the scale-free R² approaches 0.9 and mean connectivity nears 0, the network aligns with a scale-free distribution. Thus, we selected a soft threshold value (β) of 7.

To construct a co-expression matrix, genes from the input expression matrix are grouped into several modules. Gene similarity is determined by calculating adjacency, which is then used to derive a dissimilarity coefficient and create a hierarchical cluster tree. Using dynamic tree cutting with a minimum of 200 genes per module and deepSplit set to 2, eight modules are identified (Fig. 2C).

We analyzed the Pearson correlation between the TCE-RGs score and eight WGCNA-derived modules. As shown in Fig. 2D, using thresholds of |cor| > 0.3 and P < 0.05, the green module exhibited the strongest positive correlation (correlation coefficient of 0.9, P < 0.001), while the brown module showed the strongest negative correlation (correlation coefficient of −0.34, P < 0.001). Although the black module also met the threshold (cor = 0.31, P < 0.05), but its correlation strength was substantially weaker than green module. So we focused on the green and brown modules as they exhibited the strongest positive and negative correlations, respectively, with the TCE‑RG score. The green and brown modules, containing 1,368 and 1,885 genes respectively, are key modules to this study. To identify genes closely linked to TCE-RGs scores in the green and brown modules, we screened module genes based on GS (gene-trait correlation) and MM (gene-module correlation) using thresholds of |MM|>0.6 and |GS|>0.2 (Fig. 2E). This process identified 560 genes in the green module and 272 in the brown module. After merging, 832 key module genes related to TCE-RGs were identified.

Screening and enrichment analysis of candidate genes

We identified 222 candidate genes by intersecting key module genes from differential gene expression (DEGs) and WGCNA, as illustrated in Fig. 3A. To explore their molecular functions and mechanisms, we conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analyses using the “Cluster Profiler” R package. This revealed a total of 925 enriched entries: 816 in biological processes (BP), 37 in cellular components (CC), and 72 in molecular functions (MF). Key enriched pathways include T cell differentiation, immune response activation, T cell activation regulation, immune synapse formation, and immune receptor activity, as shown in Fig. 3B.

The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) is widely used for pathway research. KEGG pathway analysis annotates candidate genes and identifies key metabolic and signaling pathways they influence. In this study, a P-value threshold of < 0.05 revealed 30 significantly enriched candidate pathways, primarily related to Primary Immunodeficiency, Cell Adhesion Molecules, and Cancer Misregulation, suggesting their role in tumor induction. Figure 3C illustrates these enrichment results. Above GO and KEGG analyses indicate a strong correlation between the candidate genes and immune disorders in the tumor microenvironment, as well as their involvement in breast cancer development.

The Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network examines protein interactions to pinpoint crucial genes. We built a PPI network for 222 candidate genes using STRING and visualized it with Cytoscape. Figure 3D shows the network with 221 nodes and 1,144 interactions, indicating a broad interactive network among these genes.

Construction and validation of prognostic model

To screen survival-related genes, we used the “Survival‌ " R package for single-factor Cox regression on candidate genes in the TCGA-BRCA dataset. We identified 70 significant genes based on HR ≠ 1 and P < 0.05. To ensure the Cox model’s assumptions were met, we tested the PH hypothesis for these genes. Sixty-five genes had P > 0.05, confirming the hypothesis, and were retained for further analysis. The five genes that failed the PH assumption were: CCL19 (P = 0.0142), CD79A (P = 0.0235), IL18 (P = 0.0316), STX11 (P = 0.0408), and SFRP1 (P = 0.0463).

To further identify prognostic genes, the 65 genes previously screened were analyzed using Lasso regression with the “GLMNET” R package. In Fig. 4A, the left dotted line indicates the position with the smallest cross-validation error (lambda.min). This position determines the log(Lambda) value, showing the number of prognostic genes on the upper axis. The optimal log(Lambda) value identifies the relevant genes and their coefficients, along with the model’s explained residual proportion. The Lasso model with the smallest error, at Lambda.min, revealed 13 genes: CCL19, CD79A, IL18, S100B, STX11, BCL2A1, SPIB, RSPH1, KCNJ10, SFRP1, ZMYND10, RIBC1, and MOB3B (Fig. 4B).

Thirteen genes identified through Lasso regression were further analyzed using multivariate Cox regression to enhance predictive accuracy with minimal variables. Based on HR ≠ 1 and P < 0.2, six prognostic genes were selected as prognostic markers (Fig. 4C). A prognostic risk model was developed using these genes’ expression levels and OS data from the TCGA-BRCA dataset (Fig. 4D). The risk score formula is: riskScore = (−0.147) * S100B + (−0.183) * BCL2A1 + (−0.231) * RSPH1 + (−0.499) * KCNJ10 + (−0.269) * ZMYND10 + (−0.204) * MOB3B.

Risk scores were ranked, and BRCA patients were split into high and low-risk groups using the surv_cutpoint function from the “survminer” R package (minprop = 0.45). The Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curve was plotted to compare survival differences between these groups, with a log-rank test showing significant differences (p < 0.0001). Figure 4E illustrates that high-risk patients have a worse prognosis, indicating that higher risk scores correlate with poorer survival.

Rank the risk scores from low to high and plot a risk curve (Fig. 4F), showing more deaths in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group. This indicates a lower mortality rate for the low-risk BRCA group and poorer survival for the high-risk group. Additionally, Fig. 4G displays the expression levels of six prognostic genes, all of which are down-regulated in the high-risk group.

To assess the risk model’s effectiveness, the AUC of the ROC curve was calculated using the “Survival‌” R package. Figure 4H shows AUC values of 0.684, 0.663, and 0.714 for 3, 5, and 7 years, respectively, all above 0.65, indicating the model’s high ability to distinguish survival outcomes. Thus, the prognosis risk model using six genes effectively predicts BRCA patient survival.

Independent Prognostic analysis and nomogram development

To develop a more accurate prognostic model, we analyzed five clinical features—Age, Stage, T, N, and M—along with riskScore using univariate Cox regression on TCGA‑BRCA patient samples (HR ≠ 1, P < 0.05). As shown in Fig. 7A, all six features were significantly associated with survival. Subsequently, the proportional hazards (PH) assumption was tested for each variable using the cox.zph() function. Age, N, M, and riskScore satisfied the PH assumption (P > 0.05), whereas Stage (P = 0.00156) and T (P = 0.00085) violated the assumption. Therefore, only the four PH‑compliant variables were included in the multivariate Cox regression model (HR ≠ 1, P < 0.2). As shown in Fig. 7B, M, N, riskScore, and Age were identified as independent risk factors, consistent with the univariate findings.

We constructed a Nomogram model using M, N, riskScore, and Age as independent prognostic factors. To visualize the survival analysis, we employed the “RMS” R package to create the nomogram (Fig. 7C), which predicts 3-, 5-, and 7-year survival rates based on these factors; higher scores indicate lower survival rates. We also generated a calibration curve (Fig. 7D) to assess the model’s accuracy, with the x-axis representing predicted probabilities and the y-axis representing actual probabilities. A slope close to 1 indicates good predictive ability, and the curve’s proximity to 1 for 3-, 5-, and 7-year survival rates confirms the model’s effectiveness.

To confirm the prediction model’s validity, we plotted the Nomogram model’s ROC curve. Figure 7E shows that its AUC values for 3, 5, and 7 years exceed 0.6, outperforming the prognostic risk model and demonstrating strong predictive capability.

Immune microenvironment analysis

To analyze immune cell infiltration in high- and low-risk groups, the CIBERSORT algorithm and LM22 gene set were used to determine the proportion of 22 immune cells in BRCA samples. After excluding 194 samples (P > 0.05), 856 samples remained. Using the “ggplot 2” R package, a bar chart (Fig. 8A) illustrated the infiltration abundance of these immune cells, and a box chart with a Wilcoxon rank-sum test compared the two groups. Figure 8B shows significant differences in the infiltration of 11 immune cell types, including naive B cells, CD8 T cells, activated CD4 memory T cells, helper T cells, gamma delta T cells, resting and activated NK cells, monocytes, M0 and M2 macrophages, and resting dendritic cells. To examine the relationship between six prognostic genes and 11 immune cells, we used the “psych” R package for Spearman correlation analysis and the “GGCOR” R package to create a correlation map (Fig. 8C). The BCL2A1 gene showed the strongest positive correlation with activated CD4 memory T cells (correlation coefficient 0.505, P < 0.001), while the S100B gene had the strongest negative correlation with M2 macrophages (correlation coefficient − 0.360, P < 0.001).

ESTIMATE is a method that uses cancer transcriptome data to evaluate stromal and immune cell presence in tumors. It assigns scores to these cells via single sample genome enrichment analysis and predicts their infiltration. TIDE assesses the potential for tumor immune escape based on gene expression. To compare ImmuneScore and StromalScore between high- and low-risk groups, the “Estimate” R package calculates ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and a combined Estimate score. Differences are analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test, with results shown in a violin plot. Figure 8D illustrates that high-risk groups have notably lower ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and Estimate scores than low-risk groups.

TIDE analysis revealed distinct immune evasion mechanisms between risk groups (Fig. 8E). In all breast cancer subtypes, the Exclusion score—reflecting physical barring of T cells from infiltrating the tumor parenchyma—was significantly higher in the high‑risk group (positive correlation with risk score, P < 0.05). Conversely, the Dysfunction score—reflecting intrinsic T cell exhaustion—was significantly lower in the high‑risk group (negative correlation with risk score, P < 0.05). The composite TIDE score, which combines both mechanisms, showed no significant difference between risk groups across all subtypes. These findings indicate that immune evasion in high‑risk patients is driven primarily by T cell exclusion rather than T cell exhaustion.

Cancer-related pathways and drug sensitivity analysis

Discussion

Understanding breast cancer’s heterogeneity and the interaction between immune and tumor cells is crucial for advancing tumor biology and treatment strategies. With advancements in high-throughput quantitative technologies and data sharing, tumor research has embraced big data, particularly in immunotherapy. A key issue is preventing T cell function failure under prolonged tumor load, as T cell exhaustion weakens immune checkpoint inhibitors [16]. This is significant in triple negative breast cancer (TNBC), where ICIs show potential due to high tumor mutation and immune cell infiltration, though not all patients benefit. Studying genes related to T cell exhaustion aids in understanding breast cancer’s immune escape and supports developing new immune typing and prognosis models. Moreover, bioinformatics approaches have been successfully extended to immunopharmacology [17, 18] and central nervous system diseases [19], underscoring the broad applicability of data‑driven strategies. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that integrating multi‑omics data, focusing on specific biological mechanisms, and incorporating immune microenvironment analysis represent a core paradigm in contemporary bioinformatics research.

This study analyzed the bioinformatics of breast cancer (BRCA) patients using TCGA and GEO databases to investigate the role of T cell exhaustion-related genes (TCE-RGs). A total of 2,989 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, and TCE-RGs scores were calculated. Significant survival differences were observed between the control and BRCA groups, as well as between high and low TCE-RGs scores. Additionally, 832 key module genes were identified through WGCNA in relation to TCE-RGs scores. Intersection analysis with DEGs yielded 222 candidate genes involved in T cell differentiation, immune response regulation, and linked to pathways such as primary immunodeficiency, cancer transcription disorders, and the PD-1 checkpoint.

These findings align with previous research. Natalija et al. [20] demonstrated that PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint blockade can reverse CD8 + T cell exhaustion, while Daniel et al. [21] showed that prolonged antigen exposure can exhaust T cells. This study’s gene enrichment results confirmed these genes’ roles in immune regulation and cancer. Additionally, protein interaction network analysis highlighted the extensive connections between candidate and other genes, offering insights into the breast cancer immune microenvironment and potential therapeutic targets.

In a detailed analysis of the TCGA-BRCA breast cancer genome data set, six genes linked to disease prognosis were identified using single-factor Cox proportional hazard model, Lasso regression, and multi-factor Cox model. These genes are crucial in breast cancer’s biological mechanisms. Notably, S100B is closely associated with breast cancer development and metastasis [22, 23]. BCL2A1, a key apoptosis regulator, shows therapeutic potential despite limited breast cancer research [24]. BCL2A1/bfl-1 protein inhibitors could be promising treatments [25]. ZMYND10’s functional inactivation through promoter methylation affects tumor progression, underscoring gene regulation’s role in cancer. These insights enhance breast cancer research and offer potential molecular targets for future therapies [26].

This study identified specific gene combinations as biomarkers for breast cancer prognosis and developed a prognostic risk model. The model demonstrated strong predictive ability at 3, 5, and 7 years, with ROC AUC values over 0.65 and significant survival differences between high-risk and low-risk BRCA patients (p < 0.0001). BRCA patients from TCGA were categorized into four subtypes: TNBC, HER2, Luminal A, and Luminal B. High-risk patients in TNBC, HER2, and Luminal A had notably lower survival rates than low-risk patients, while Luminal B showed a similar trend without significant differences. Although subtype analysis had lower AUC values than the overall cohort, external validation with GSE42568 confirmed the model’s universality and potential for subtype-specific applications. These results highlight the prognostic risk model’s potential for evaluating breast cancer outcomes across various patient subtypes.

Advances in bioinformatics have enabled diverse strategies for prognostic biomarker discovery. For instance, FUNMarker integrates multi‑source biological information using K‑means clustering and label propagation to identify breast cancer biomarkers with strong discriminative power [27]. This highlights the value of integrating heterogeneous data and accounting for patient heterogeneity. In our multivariate Cox regression analysis, we considered riskScore along with Age, tumor Stage, T stage, N stage, and M stage. The findings identified M-stage, N-stage, riskScore, and age as independent prognostic factors. Using these, we developed a Nomogram. The calibration curve showed a slope near 1 for 3-, 5-, and 7-year survival predictions, indicating strong predictive performance. Additionally, the Nomogram’s AUC values for these timeframes all exceeded 0.7, confirming the model’s accuracy and reliability.

The TME is a complex ecosystem comprising immune cells, cytokines, stromal cells, and tumor‑associated factors that critically modulate immunotherapy responses. In our study, CIBERSORT revealed significant differences in 11 immune cell types between risk groups, and TIDE analysis further indicated that high‑risk patients evade immune attack primarily through physical T cell exclusion rather than intrinsic T cell exhaustion. Emerging studies support this interpretation. For example, Wang et al. showed that antibody‑drug conjugates can convert immunologically “cold” tumors to “hot” by inducing immunogenic cell death in colorectal cancer [28]. Our TIDE results suggest that high‑risk breast cancer corresponds to a “cold” phenotype characterized by T cell exclusion. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, PAK2 was found to negatively regulate immune cell infiltration via multi‑omics approaches [29], implying that our six genes (e.g., S100B, BCL2A1) might similarly affect chemokine or adhesion molecule expression to influence T cell migration. Furthermore, a disulfidptosis‑related signature (DFRS) was recently shown to correlate with immune exclusion pathways, consistent with our TIDE findings [30]. From a broader perspective, chronic inflammation can activate cancer‑associated fibroblasts (CAFs) to deposit extracellular matrix components, forming physical barriers that exclude T cells [31], a process that aligns with the elevated Exclusion score we observed in high‑risk patients. We also note that while TIDE and risk scores were negatively correlated in HER2 and Luminal A subtypes, no significant difference across all subtypes was observed, likely due to small sample size. While overlapping mechanisms can’t be entirely ruled out, the role of immune cell rejection in patient prognosis appears more significant, as confirmed across all subtypes, suggesting it as a crucial survival strategy for breast cancer cells.

Pathway analysis revealed that BCL2A1 and KCNJ10 genes effectively promote apoptosis, aligning with previous findings. In GDSC drug sensitivity analysis, significant IC50 differences were observed for 190 drugs between high- and low-risk groups. It should be noted that most of these 190 drugs are not first‑line or standard treatments for breast cancer. The top six drugs, ordered by P value, are Syk inhibitor (PRT062607), Teniposide, insulin receptor inhibitor (BMS − 754807), Mitoxantrone, Topotecan, and Irinotecan, suggesting their potential in low-risk groups [32]. In breast cancer, an RCT found that combining mitoxantrone with trastuzumab significantly improved the objective remission rate and median progression-free survival in HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer (MBC) without increasing adverse reactions, indicating its effectiveness and safety [33]. Although Irinotecan has been tested for metastatic breast cancer, it is not yet a standard treatment [34]. Future studies should focus on drugs that have shown potential in breast cancer (e.g., mitoxantrone, irinotecan, topotecan) and validate the predictive value of the risk grouping for drug sensitivity in preclinical models.

Some recent bioinformatics studies have adopted similar analytical methods to construct prediction models. For breast cancer, Xu et al. constructed a three‑gene signature (IGHA2, SLC35A2, BST2) achieving 3‑ and 5‑year AUCs of 0.744 and 0.765 [35]. Compared to that model, our six‑gene T cell exhaustion‑focused signature provides broader subtype (TNBC, HER2, Luminal) and TIDE immune evasion analyses. Another Xu et al. study developed a six‑gene model for lung adenocarcinoma integrating single‑cell RNA‑seq and ten machine learning algorithms, with validation in immunotherapy cohorts [36]. Their finding that high‑risk patients responded better to immunotherapy complements our TIDE results, which suggest immune exclusion drives evasion in high‑risk breast cancer. Together, these comparisons indicate that future breast cancer models should incorporate single‑cell resolution and ensemble machine learning to enhance interpretability and generalizability.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, the transcriptomic data are derived from bulk RNA‑seq, which cannot resolve cellular heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment. It remains unclear whether the six prognostic genes are primarily expressed in tumor cells or specific immune subsets, nor how their spatial distribution influences T cell exhaustion. Future studies should adopt single‑cell and spatial transcriptomics [37] to validate their expression patterns at high resolution. Second, after obtaining 222 candidate genes, we performed no systematic functional prioritization before regression modeling, which may increase overfitting risk. Future work should integrate prior functional knowledge or employ regularization methods such as elastic net to further control overfitting. Third, the optimal risk score cutoffs were inconsistent between the training and validation cohorts, affecting model portability. Using fixed percentile‑based cutoffs or continuous risk scoring systems [38] could improve generalizability. Prospective validation with unified criteria is needed. Fourth, the model relies on absolute gene expression levels, which are sensitive to batch effects. Converting the six‑gene signature into a gene‑pair framework based on relative expression ranks [39] may enhance cross‑dataset robustness. Fifth, the six prognostic genes have a limited evidence base in T cell exhaustion. The observed correlations do not prove causality. Thus, mechanistic interpretations remain hypothesis‑generating. Future studies should combine Mendelian randomization with multi‑omics [40] and CRISPR‑based functional assays to establish causal roles. Finally, this study was based solely on bulk RNA‑seq data, and it is well recognized that mRNA levels do not always correlate with protein abundance. Therefore, quantitative immunoassays [41] should be employed in future studies to validate the six genes at the protein level in breast cancer tissues or serum, thereby bridging the gap between transcriptomic findings and clinical application.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study established an innovative prognostic model comprising six genes associated with T cell exhaustion. This model demonstrated an independent correlation with overall survival (OS) in both the derivation and validation cohorts, thereby enhancing the understanding of prognostic predictions in breast cancer (BRCA). However, the fundamental mechanisms linking T cell exhaustion-related genes to tumor immunity in BRCA are not yet fully elucidated and require further exploration.
