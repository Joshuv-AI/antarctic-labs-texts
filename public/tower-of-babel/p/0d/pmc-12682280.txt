A network of Bayesian agents for reward prediction and noise tolerance
Authors: Daniela Gandolfi, Mirco Tincani, Giulia Maria Boiani, Lorenzo Benatti, Tommaso Zanotti, Giovanni Pezzulo, Giuseppe Pagnoni, Francesco Maria Puglisi, Jonathan Mapelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe development of computational models capable of performing biologically relevant tasks is essential for real-world applications. This work introduces a network of single neurons modeled as independent agents, each optimizing its own cost function. This optimization governs the firing probability, resulting in spike generation—the core unit of neural communication. The network is hierarchically structured to reflect the connectivity of the dopaminergic reward system. Neurons adapt their synaptic weights to improve input prediction, leading to the self-organization of circuit activity. While the network performs well on classical reward-based tasks, its performance degrades when noise is introduced at the level of individual neurons, affecting spike generation. In line with biological systems, spike generation is inherently noisy, yet the brain achieves reliable computation through evolved mechanisms. Similarly, increasing the network size restores performance. By incorporating strategies to counteract intrinsic noise, this model lays the foundation for robust, energy-efficient, scalable, and noise-tolerant neuromorphic architectures.

Introduction

According to the Bayesian Brain theory, the central nervous system can be viewed as a prediction machine that continuously generates expectations about upcoming sensory data based on an internal model of the world.1,2 The discrepancy between expected and actual sensory data acts as a teaching signal for model optimization, thus allowing the adaptation to mutated environmental conditions. In neural circuits, such as the minimization of prediction error is the driving principle for self-organization, shaping both neural dynamics and synaptic plasticity at the level of individual neurons.3 This proactive mode of operation offers unique performance advantages for biological agents compared to simple feedback control systems, such as enhanced reactivity and computational efficiency. Interestingly, it has been recently shown that single cortical neurons can learn temporal information and generate prediction4 through local mechanisms based on error computations. In this context, the Active Inference approach suggests that the processes of Bayesian updating occur by minimizing a tractable proxy for surprise – called variational free energy (VFE)5 – which scores the discrepancy between expected and actual observations. This theory has been extensively applied to cognitive and motor functions by simulating circuits at the mesoscopic and macroscopic scales, and only recently, its validity has been demonstrated at the microscopic scale through models of bio-inspired neuronal networks, the Multi-Agent Bayesian (MAB) networks.6,7 Importantly, the active inference paradigm has been also implemented in a closed-loop system based on electrophysiological stimulation and recording that allowed a neuronal cultures embedded in a simulated game-world to rapidly learn playing.8 The implementation of the free energy minimization at the level of single neurons in a given circuit requires tracking the probability distribution of synaptic weights within the network and adjusting learning rates according to Bayesian inference. This, in turn, allows the network to perform specific cognitive tasks.9

Dynamic changes in synaptic weights underpin most brain computations, and the consolidation of these changes forms the basis of learning processes. Among areas exploiting this capability, the basal ganglia are involved in several behavioral tasks. A key role is played by the dopamine (DA) reward circuit, one of the most phylogenetically conserved pathways in the brain and consisting in neuronal groups that implement reward-predictive tasks through long-term plasticity mechanisms occurring at different sites and that can be estimated both in vivo and in silico. The reward circuit includes several brain regions, with a key role played by dopaminergic neurons in the Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA) and in the Nucleus Accumbens (Nac), as evidenced by behavioral, pharmacological and physiological studies.10 In particular, these DA neurons are classically thought to be involved in the computation of reward prediction error, crucial in various behavioral functions, such as fear learning, addiction, habit learning and motivation.11,12 In all cases, the reward paradigm is expressed by enabling neurons to predict salient outcomes, which are classically represented as single scalar quantities, which support learning about the mean of stochastic outcomes. Recently, this general framework has been questioned by computational models and experimental recordings in single neurons showing that reward is represented as a probability distribution corresponding to multiple outcomes simultaneously and in parallel.13 In this perspective, individual dopaminergic neurons report reward prediction error for various aspects of moment-to-moment situation14 and support the evidence of pattern heterogeneity in action responses among dopaminergic neurons. From a broader perspective, the role of midbrain dopamine neurons (DAs) in encoding reward prediction error (RPE) is one of the most debated topics in computational neuroscience, and alternative computational paradigms could help shed light on the overall system.

Although the specific task can be different, the implementation of an RPE requires the expression of long-term plasticity15,16 to take place. In the dopamine reward circuit various synaptic contacts have been shown to undergo long-term changes during the consolidation of the task, both in the form of long-term potentiation (LTP)17 and Long-Term Depression (LTD),18 requiring a variety of mechanisms ranging from the classical NMDA dependent excitatory LTP19 to heterosynaptic plasticity at GABAergic synapses.20

Regardless of the chosen computational framework, the implementation of bio-inspired networks must account for the intrinsic noise embedded in biological networks. The performance of biological circuits in fact is intrinsically affected by noise originating from multiple sources across different spatiotemporal scales,21 including stochastic ion channel openings, synaptic vesicle release, and external sensory inputs.22 It is therefore crucial to investigate the effects of noise on neuronal computation, if we want ultimately to build a network that preserve its performance when exposed to external perturbation.

While the impact of different sources of noise to signal transmission is essential for comprehending the physiology and replicating neuronal functions in hardware, quantifying it is particularly challenging. The effect of noise on the activity of neuronal circuit is not yet fully understood and extant hypotheses are generally situated between two opposite theoretical positions: (i) noise limits information encoding,23 and (ii) brain functions actually benefit from the random dynamics introduced by intrinsic noise.24 However, most quantitative evaluations of the role of noise in brain activity are based on system-level experimental recordings, such as EEG or fMRI, which do not capture the dynamics at the level of individual cells and synapses.23 The idea behind the hypothesis of a positive impact of noise on signal transmission is that noise increases behavioral variability, an emergent property with crucial adaptive value that can be observed in both perception and action, even under constant external conditions.24 Since variability first emerges at the neuronal and synaptic level, it is crucial to explore how neuronal networks compensate for this variability to prevent communication failures, while also leveraging this variability to enhance the overall capacity to process information. The impact of detrimental noise in automatic systems can be reduced by exploiting strategies such as parallelization25 and redundancy.26 To this day, however, practical applications in simulated neuronal networks or even in physical environments are scarce.

In this study, we present a Multi-Agent Bayesian (MAB) network where every neuron behaves as an independent agent minimizing its own cost function—namely the variational free energy. This minimization process determines the neuron’s firing probability, ultimately leading to spike generation. The resulting spiking-based computational framework is evaluated on an associative learning task inspired by the brain’s dopaminergic reward system, a biological mechanism that has long provided the conceptual bases for numerous computational models. Among these, reinforcement learning stands out as one of the most widely adopted paradigms in artificial intelligence.27

Our results demonstrate that the MAB network is capable of associative learning within just a few training cycles, using the Nucleus Accumbens (NAC) as the readout layer for network’s predictive performance. Task performance is evaluated based on the anticipatory spiking activity of the NAC, which reflects the network’s ability to associate temporally correlated events. Drawing a parallel with the brain, we pose the following questions: What are the potential sources of error that could impair the predictive capability of the network? To what extent does variability in spike generation influence task performance? What mechanisms does the brain employ to mitigate such variability? And which of these mechanisms could inspire the development of more efficient learning algorithms or higher-performance neuromorphic hardware?

Notably, we demonstrate that systematic errors, whether introduced in the sensory input or in the variational minimization process in individual neurons, can degrade— or in extreme cases, completely disrupt— the network’s ability to perform the task. This progressive decline in performance, however, can be mitigated through redundancy in the circuit and parallelized computation, echoing strategies observed in biological systems. Such approaches may also be applicable to the design of neuromorphic hardware, where fabrication imperfections often introduce mismatches and non-idealities that affect the dependability of the hardware computation. Unlike traditional AI algorithms, we explicitly incorporate noise in the decision-making process—reflecting the stochastic nature of biological circuits. Investigating strategies to mitigate the impact of noise in such systems offers valuable insights for the development of neuromorphic devices, whether in analog or digital form.

Results

Recent studies have shown that networks composed of individual agents, each one operating under the principles of Bayesian inference, can successfully perform specific tasks, such as network synchronization6 or associative learning.7 Such neuronal networks strongly rely on a recognized organizational principle: the hierarchical structure of neural circuits. We have recently tested in a cerebellar circuit7 that changes in the representativeness of hierarchical layers, obtained by varying the number of neurons in each layer, affected task performance. Alternatively, rescaling the network did not impair the performance if the hierarchy was preserved.7

We extended this approach to develop a reward prediction error network implementing a simplified version of the basal ganglia mesolimbic dopaminergic circuit (Figures 1A–1C).28 In the biological circuit (Figure 1B), neurons in the Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA) are gradually activated by sensory inputs such as the ones transmitted via the Prefrontal cortex (PFC - CS) with their activity modulated by dopaminergic and GABAergic neurons. In line with the known physiology, the simulated network was organized into two distinct pathways processing conditioned (CS) and unconditioned (US) stimuli to implement the reward paradigm (Figure 1C). In our configuration, the main neuronal populations involved in the reward task were arranged hierarchically and connected respecting the known convergence/divergence ratio among neuronal types. The computational load was reduced by minimizing the network size to 132 neurons and 795 connections whilst preserving the circuit architecture. The general framework of Active Inference2 was then independently applied to each neuron to construct an MAB circuit. Within this computational paradigm, the probability that a single neuron will fire an action potential is directly linked to the current value of a cost function known as variational free energy (VFE; see6,7 and STAR Methods for a detailed explanation). VFE quantifies the divergence between predicted and actual input to the neuron, thereby guiding neuronal activity.

The VFE of each neuron depends on the neuron’s generative model of its surrounding environment, encoding expectations about the hidden causes of its synaptic inputs and the current synaptic inputs. Crucially, the same principle governs the gradual adaptation of synaptic connection strengths, effectively simulating synaptic plasticity. During this process, the weights of both excitatory and inhibitory synapses are automatically adjusted through the minimization of free energy. The reward paradigm was implemented by sequentially activating the pathways associated with conditioned (CS) and unconditioned (US) stimuli, following a temporal scheme in which the CS precedes the US (see STAR Methods). Both stimuli, when presented individually, are sufficient to elicit a minimal response in the readout neurons located in the Nucleus Accumbens (NAc; Figure 1C).

Network performance was evaluated by assessing the presence of firing activity in NAc neurons during the combined presentation of the conditioned and unconditioned stimuli (USCS), specifically within the time window corresponding to the CS (Figures 2A and 2B). This anticipatory activation, compared to the responses evoked by the CS and US presented separately, mirrors the behavior observed in biological circuits (see STAR Methods). Spikes generated by NAc neurons in response to the combined stimuli (USCS) were counted during the first time window and compared to those elicited by the CS or US presented individually, within the same time window. To allow comparison across experimental conditions, spike counts were normalized by the number of neurons (see STAR Methods). Neural responses to the US, CS, and USCS were labeled as unconditioned (UR), conditioned (CR), and combined responses (URCR), respectively. Reward-predictive behavior was assessed as a binary outcome, based on the presence of anticipatory firing activity in NAc neurons during the first time window of the URCR condition (Figure 2C). This anticipatory activity was absent in both the UR and CR conditions when presented alone.

We investigated the ability of the MAB circuit to perform a reward-predictive task by delivering ten stimulation cycles and monitoring neuronal activity. Anticipatory firing in the NAc emerged during the fourth cycle in the URCR condition (Figures 2A–2C), while activity in response to the CS and US alone (CR and UR conditions) appeared only in the fifth cycle (Figure 2C, red arrow). Given that reward-related behavior is believed to depend on dopamine release from the VTA to PFC neurons, we simulated dopaminergic inhibition by removing the feedback loop from the VTA to the excitatory PFC population (PFCe, Figure 2D; left panel). This manipulation led to a general reduction in NAc neuronal activity and delayed the emergence of reward-predictive firing to the fifth cycle. Moreover, the anticipatory response was significantly attenuated, showing a 45% reduction in spike count compared to the intact feedback condition (compare Figure 2D left and right panels). To further characterize network dynamics, we examined the persistence and extinction of the reward-predictive response by removing the US and tracking NAc activity during the first time window, when only the CS was presented. Interestingly, spike counts declined rapidly, mirroring extinction patterns observed in vivo29,30,31 (Figure 2E).

Emergence of synaptic plasticity

The present network exhibited the ability to perform a complex biological task that required the dynamic adaptation of its internal parameters. In biological systems, such capabilities are the hallmark of learning and are typically attributed to enduring modifications in synaptic weights—i.e. manifestations of long-term plasticity.32,33 According to established learning rules, mechanisms such as long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD) enable neuronal circuits to alter their performance and adapt to specific tasks over time. In the MAB network, synaptic precision reflects the modulation of synaptic weights. To assess whether the emergence of reward predictive behavior was driven by changes in synaptic precision, we tracked its evolution over the course of the stimulation. Interestingly, the dopaminergic MAB circuit exhibited a significant and sustained increase in synaptic precision at specific excitatory connections projecting from dopaminergic neurons to both PFCe and NAc neurons during the URCR condition (yellow traces in Figure 3A). At the same time, other excitatory synapses—such as those from the Pedunculopontine Nucleus (PPT) to dopaminergic neurons—showed a persistent decrease in precision throughout the training phase, which extended well beyond the end of the stimulation cycles (Figure 3B). Overall, 38.9% of the excitatory synapses in the network underwent lasting modifications, either in the form of LTP or LTD (Figure 3C). Given that synaptic inhibition plays a central role in shaping circuit dynamics34—and that inhibitory plasticity has been observed in various brain regions20,35—we also tracked changes in synaptic precision at inhibitory synapses throughout the simulations. Notably, a subset of inhibitory connections displayed increased precision following repeated stimulation, while none exhibited signs of LTD (Figure 3D). Since the plastic behavior of the biological dopaminergic circuit involved in reward-related tasks has been extensively studied, we have analyzed topologically the distribution of plasticity in the MAB network. Synaptic changes were predominantly distributed among excitatory connections from PCFe neurons (21 LTP out of 40 connections; 52.5%) to dopaminergic and GABAergic VTA neurons (4 LTP out of 20; 20%), as well as to PFCi neurons (8 LTP out of 45; 18%). Additionally, excitatory synapses from dopaminergic neurons displayed sustained potentiation toward both PFCe (17 LTP out of 45; 37.8%) and NAc neurons (2 LTP out of 6; 3%), along with synapses from PPT to dopaminergic neurons (8 LTP out of 100%; 8%). Similarly, LTP at inhibitory synapses was mainly observed in connections from GABAergic dopaminergic VTA neurons (7 LTP out of 30; 23.3%) and from PFCi to PFCe neurons (3 LTP out of 45; 6.7%). The impact of input noise on the MAB network

One of the most striking features of biological neural circuits is their ability to face perturbations, which can arise from various sources of noise. Evaluating the performance of a network under noisy conditions is essential to assessing the ability of an artificial circuit to adapt to environmental variability. This adaptive capacity, referred to as resilience, can be measured in our network by two main indicators: the presence of anticipatory firing in the NAc population and the number of stimulation cycles required to complete the task. To assess network resilience, we first applied stimulus trains with varying temporal patterns while monitoring NAc activity. Specifically, we increased the duration of the interstimulus interval between two consecutive trials (Figure 4A), keeping the internal structure of the stimulation sequence (US, CS, USCS) unchanged. Remarkably, as in biological systems, task performance was abolished when the delay between successive cycles exceeded 250 time-steps (Figure 4A). Next, we introduced variability in the timing of inputs by allowing the interstimulus interval to fluctuate randomly around a mean value of 240 time-steps, a condition that typically elicits reward-predictive behavior (see third point of the black curve in Figure 4A). Although the average timing remained favorable, increasing the variance progressively disrupted performance, despite the preservation of the internal US-CS sequence (Figure 4B). This growing variability quickly degraded network behavior, as shown by the contrast between Figures 4C, 4D, 4E and 4F indicating that, much like in biological circuits, precise temporal dynamics are critical. Robustness of the network to noise during inference

In biological neural circuits, noise can arise from various internal sources, such as the stochastic nature of synaptic transmission and fluctuations in cellular excitability, which affect the process of spike generation. We thus investigated the effects of noise on several key internal parameters of the network: (i) the prior belief of a neuron’s hidden state (D), (ii) the corresponding inference or the neuron’s firing probability (X), (iii) the likelihood matrix encoding synaptic weights (A), and (iv) synaptic precision (p). To evaluate the impact of noise, we initially introduced a spatially uniform error into the X variable. At each iteration, a single random value —positive or negative—was drawn from a Gaussian distribution and uniformly added to the X value of every neuron in the network (see STAR Methods). This spatially homogeneous perturbation systematically altered the neuron’s firing thresholds (see STAR Methods). We explored different noise configurations by manipulating the properties of the random distribution used to introduce stationary noise. First, we varied the full width at half maximum (FWHM, denoted as σ) of the Gaussian distribution, gradually increasing it from 0 to 0.7 while keeping the mean fixed at the maximum firing probability (Figure 5A; see STAR Methods). Even at low σ values, this spatially uniform noise significantly influenced neuronal firing across all populations within the MAB network (Figure 5A), leading to a rapid degradation of task performance (Figure 5B). Next, we fixed σ at 0.04—an intermediate level that still allowed for the expression of reward-predictive behavior— and systematically varied the mean (μ) of the distribution. Lowering μ further increased the overall firing probability across neuronal populations (Figure 5C), which again disrupted task performance, abolishing reward-predictive behavior (Figure 5D).Figure 5MAB network and stationary noise(A) Firing activity of the circuit obtained by increasing the variance of the error distribution. Since noise in biological systems is typically dynamic and spatially non-uniform, we conducted a second set of simulations in which errors were introduced in a non-stationary manner at random steps during the iterative free energy minimization process (Figure 5; see STAR Methods). Briefly, network activity was monitored while random values were sampled from Gaussian distributions centered on the current value of each variable as determined by the inferential process. The full width at half maximum (FWHM, σ) of these distributions was systematically varied, ranging from 0.01 to 0.1. We began by perturbing the prior firing probability (D). Task performance remained stable until σ exceeded 0.05, beyond which it rapidly deteriorated (Figure 6A). Similar results were observed when perturbing the X and A variables: random noise sampled from Gaussian distributions (again centered on the current inferred values and with increasing FWHM was added, and network performance declined markedly once σ surpassed 0.05 (Figures 6A–6C). Moreover, when noise was simultaneously applied to multiple variables within the same simulation, specifically X and D, task performance deteriorated even at σ values below 0.05, indicating a cumulative effect of distributed noise sources (data not shown). To assess the effective resilience of the MAB circuit to such perturbations, we explored a strategy based on upscaling the network size. Specifically, the number of neurons in each population was increased by discrete scaling factors, while maintaining the original connectivity ratios between groups. To evaluate whether performance could be recovered under disruptive noise conditions, we introduced non-stationary, variable errors to network parameters (X, D, and A), using Gaussian distributions centered on their actual values with σ = 0.1— a level previously shown to impair the task execution. Noise was simultaneously applied to pairs of parameters (X and A, X and D, D and A), and network performance was re-evaluated. While visual inspection suggested that the combined firing probability (URCR) remained relatively stable across conditions (data not shown), quantitative analysis revealed that anticipatory spiking activity— and thus task performance— was not preserved (Figures 6D–6F, first data points). Interestingly, increasing the network size enabled recovery of reward-predictive behavior, although with a notable reduction in the normalized number of emitted spikes. When noise was introduced to X and A (Figure 6A), doubling the network size was sufficient to restore task execution, albeit with a reduced level of anticipatory firing compared to baseline (see black dashed line). Similarly, for noise applied to X and D (Figure 6E) or D and A (Figure 6F), task performance was rescued only when the network was scaled up by at least a factor of three. The impact of noise on synaptic precision

When the task performance was compromised by noise, none of the synaptic contacts underwent persistent changes (Figure 7). Importantly, when the network size was increased and the task performance gradually recovered, LTP and LTD also progressively recovered (Figures 7C and 7D). To further assess the resilience of the proposed network to noise, we examined its ability to perform the reward-predictive task under constraints on synaptic precision. In this set of simulations, the dynamic range of synaptic precision was limited by imposing fixed upper and lower bounds, effectively clipping its values. Notably, even when synaptic precision was prevented from exceeding a maximum threshold, the network consistently maintained task performance, reinforcing the robustness of the MAB architecture (data not shown). In biological systems, the ability to adapt to changing environ-mental conditions often relies on an enhanced sensitivity to individual sensory inputs in response to background noise.36 Furthermore, the contribution of each sensory modality to overall perception is inversely related to the level of noise affecting that modality.37 Building on this principle, we conduct-ed an additional set of simulations to determine whether the MAB network would adjust the synaptic strength of specific input pathways when selectively exposed to noise. Gaussian, non-uniform noise was applied to the D parameter of neurons receiving either CS inputs (PFCe) or US inputs (PPT), while the alternate input remained unperturbed. In both conditions, the network successfully retained its ability to perform the reward-predictive task, though with a reduced intensity of anticipatory firing (Figures 8A and 8B, first data points). As anticipated, increasing the network size progressively improved task performance, as evidenced by a greater number of anticipatory spikes generated by NAc neurons (Figures 8A and 8B). Interestingly, synaptic precision showed an inverse relationship with the level of input noise. Synapses associated with the noisy pathway exhibited minimal changes in efficacy, while those in the non-noisy pathway underwent significant long-term modifications. Furthermore, the number of synapses undergoing persistent changes increased in proportion to network size (Figures 8C and 8D). Taken to-gether, these findings highlight the remarkable resilience of the MAB network in performing reward-predictive tasks under noisy conditions. Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that a network composed of individual agents each functioning as an independent predictive unit can dynamically adapt its behavior to solve a reward-predictive task. This extends beyond predictive coding, positioning neurons as agents that act to shape future inputs, not just encode them. Using past input-output pairs, neurons implicitly learn environment dynamics and infer latent states to generate control signals (spikes) accordingly.

Analogously to what has been recently proposed by,38 the model naturally gives rise to the potentiation and depression time windows observed in spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP), as neurons adjust to discrepancies between intended and actual outcomes. Furthermore, it captures the biological variability associated with imprecise neuronal firing under constant input—an aspect that standard predictive models often struggle to reproduce. The model’s resilience to internal randomness or noise also aligns with the variability commonly observed in biological neurons, for instance, in the process of spike generation. Despite exposure to variable stochastic noise impairs network performance—reflecting the sensitivity observed in the biological dopaminergic reward circuit— the gradual increase of the network’s size restored its functionality, mirroring the resilience mechanisms found in biological systems.

The proposed MAB network reproduces the hierarchical organization of the dopaminergic system, enabling reward-based learning in the basal ganglia. The network allows for a scalable, minimal canonical representation of the different neuronal layers involved in the task execution. The MAB network was also able to generate anticipatory responses after learning, through mechanisms analogous to reward prediction error (RPE), a concept attributed to the activity of dopaminergic neurons in predictive learning models.39 Dopaminergic neurons play a central role in regulating a wide range of behavioral functions11,12 where the reward-based framework operates by enabling neurons to learn and predict salient outcomes, whether appetitive or aversive. As with other biological learning systems, long-term synaptic plasticity15,16 is essential for the implementation of dopaminergic prediction mechanisms. Interestingly, various synaptic connections—both excitatory and inhibitory—within the network exhibited long-term changes during the acquisition of reward-predictive behavior. It is important to note that the current model adopts a reductionist approach, which preserves the architecture and the hierarchical organization of the circuit while distinguishing only between excitatory and inhibitory synapses and therefore not considering neurotransmitter and receptor heterogeneity. Although the specific molecular mechanisms underlying plasticity induction and expression were not explicitly modeled, the observed patterns of LTP and LTD can be mapped onto known anatomical pathways. For instance, the connection from the VTA to PFCe—known to undergo LTP during goal-directed learning via D1 and D2 dopamine receptor activation40—was similarly potentiated in the MAB network, supporting the idea that reward-predictive learning can emerge from circuit-level organization alone. A recent hypothesis41 proposes that reward prediction may arise from neurons learning to integrate diverse synaptic input dynamics through optimization at the single-neuron level, a process thought to be governed by Spike-Time Dependent Plasticity (STDP). In line with this proposal, the MAB network implements a reward-predictive mechanism by minimizing free energy as a local cost function, consistent with in vivo experimental observations. Remarkably, the predictive properties displayed by the network resemble those observed in biological systems, including isolated neuronal cultures8 and in cortical in vitro circuits.4

In a wider perspective, recent evidence13 suggests that dopaminergic signaling is far more complex and multidimensional than previously assumed. The traditional scalar model posits that dopamine neurons encode a single, linear RPE, whereby dopamine release is relatively straightforward, unchanging, and proportional to the discrepancy between expected and actual reward. According to this view, all dopamine neurons respond uniformly, encoding RPE in a consistent, context-independent manner. While this framework is consistent with basic reinforcement learning models, it oversimplifies the diverse functions of dopamine in the brain, and in fact, recent findings suggest that this model is insufficient to explain the full complexity of dopamine’s role in the brain. In contrast, the emerging multidimensional model proposes that dopamine encodes a richer array of signals beyond a scalar RPE.13,14 These include contextual and task-dependent variables such as reward magnitude, uncertainty, effort, motivation, temporal dynamics, and goal relevance. This more nuanced framework better accounts for the variability observed in dopaminergic activity across different behavioral and environmental conditions. Within this broader perspective, the MAB network presented here can be viewed as one of the computational expressions of dopaminergic multidimensionality. By integrating context-dependent variables and adapting its behavior through internal plasticity and predictive inference, the network reflects key features of the emerging model of dopamine function. Further investigations with different context dependent inputs could be performed to test this hypothesis and eventually demonstrate the multidimensionality of the predictive reward dopamine circuit.

Importantly, the MAB network can be scaled by changing the number of neurons and if the hierarchy is preserved, the network can effectively perform the task.7 In the context of spiking neuronal networks (SNNs), both the number of neurons and the connectivity patterns, though inspired by biological networks, are not directly mapped onto their biological counterparts. Instead, they replicate key organizational principles. We have already demonstrated7 that the MAB networks, by simply exploiting their architecture, can outperform traditional SNNs, requiring fewer learning epochs and fewer neurons to accomplish the functional task (e.g., cerebellar associative learning).7

The active inference framework is inherently biologically inspired since it is grounded in a fundamental principle: the hierarchical organization of biological microcircuits. In the present work, the network is designed to perform a reward-based task, drawing direct inspiration from the hierarchical architecture of dopaminergic microcircuits within the basal ganglia, structures that are essential for associative learning between temporally correlated sensory inputs. To reflect the biological plausibility, the excitatory and inhibitory populations within the network were rescaled according to the relative representativeness of each layer and accounting for the hierarchical organization. Importantly, we have demonstrated in a different network7 reproducing the cerebellar microcircuitry that minor variations in the number of neurons per layer did not affect task performance, whereas disrupting the hierarchical structure resulted in a failure to successfully perform the task. Similarly, the principle of “rewarding” associated with the free-energy minimization, which was used here in place of explicitly embedded bio-inspired synaptic learning rules, led to the emergence of synaptic plasticity patterns that aligned with the ones observed in biological circuits. The present network, although idealized, can exhibit autonomous learning, ultimately leading to the emergence of predictive capabilities.

The reward prediction error is thought to arise from dopamine release in the NAc, which signals the discrepancy between expected and actual reward, providing a biological foundation for reinforcement learning.42 Extensive research on reward prediction by NAc neurons, and more broadly within the striatal circuitry, has yielded a wide range of findings (Table 1). Experimental evidence has confirmed the role of NAc neurons in encoding reward-related taste stimuli.43,44,45 At the circuit level, NAc neuronal activity is typically assessed through in vivo experiments in which animals are trained to perform associative tasks, while the firing rates of individual neurons are recorded.43,45,46 In most of these experimental paradigms, the training period required for an animal to learn a specific task ranges from a few to several dozen trials, depending on the complexity of the behavioral task. Real-world data comparison

In our simulations, we could not directly map behavioral tasks at the system level. Therefore, we monitored changes in the cumulative firing of NAc neurons in the anticipation of the unconditioned stimulus, as an indirect measure of the network’s ability to perform an associative task. The MAB network demonstrated performance consistent with that of biological circuits: the associative behavior emerged within the first 3–5 stimulation cycles and led to cumulative firing levels reaching approximately 70% of the maximum NAc activity by the end of the training session. Additionally, reward prediction error tasks can be explored using abstract models and artificial neural networks. Our computational framework can be compared to these systems in terms of task execution. In agreement with our findings, a recent computational modeling e with our results, a recent computational model—composed of two coupled recurrent neuronal networks of integrate and firing neurons with plastic synapses—was able to develop reward prediction error-like dopaminergic activity starting from the tenth trial. Task learning in this model was quantified as an increase in the average firing rate.

Similarly, various models of reward-modulated plasticity have been proposed. In most cases, spiking neurons are connected via dynamic synapses that implement STDP mechanisms.51,53 In line with this, the MAB network adjusts synaptic weights through a free energy minimization process, and interestingly, the resulting plasticity patterns can be mapped onto known anatomical pathways. Finally, more abstract models designed to perform error-prediction-based associative tasks are often based on mathematical kernels that minimize prediction error (see54). These models typically converge to stable solutions within a limited number of cycles. However, they are difficult to compare directly with biologically grounded, cell-level computational models such as the MAB network.

Moreover, unlike biologically inspired systems, these abstract models generally do not account for the presence of noise.

The brain is renowned for its exceptional computational capabilities, including the execution of complex operations,55,56 noise tolerance,52 and remarkable energy efficiency.57,58 However, increasing the size of a neural circuit may introduce energetic constraints,59 creating a trade-off between enhanced information transfer and metabolic costs required to sustain such a system. Comparative studies have shown that as we move from rodents to primates and humans, white matter connectivity becomes increasingly parallelized, with brain regions communicating multiple redundant pathways.60 This parallel organization appears to offer a functional advantage: it mitigates the amplification of noise within the system. Our findings demonstrate that such parallelization can confer robustness to neural circuits exposed to noisy activity.

Unlike individual neurons, where noise can be amplified through nonlinear mechanisms such as thresholding or dendritic integration9,61—parallel connectivity enables distributed processing, which helps to buffer against perturbations. In biological systems, average firing rates are stabilized through plastic mechanisms,62 such as the homeostatic regulation of synaptic weights,35,63 the dynamic expression of ion channels,64 and quantal release adjustment,65 all of which contribute to dampening noise effects. Nonetheless, the inherently distributed and highly parallel architecture of neural circuits is likely a fundamental factor in limiting noise propagation.66 In our current work, we show that a network of inferential (Bayesian) neurons becomes increasingly resilient to noise-induced perturbations as its size grows, underscoring the protective role of redundancy. Intriguingly, there are scenarios in which noise may enhance, rather than degrade, network performance. For instance, introducing stochastic noise during training can accelerate learning and improve working in recurrent neural networks.67 Additionally, incorporating computational imprecision in line with psychophysical Weber-Fechner law has been shown to yield near-optimal adaptive behavior, even under volatile environmental conditions.68 An important avenue for future research will be to determine under what conditions, and in which tasks, the MAB network proposed here can actively leverage noise to improve computational performance.

The MAB network exhibits remarkable resilience to noise, positing it as a promising candidate for a compact, efficient, and robust alternative to conventional spiking neural networks.69 When translated into hardware, it could serve as a foundational neuromorphic computing platform. In the pursuit of increasingly energy-efficient computational architectures and neuromorphic chips, the distinctive noise tolerance of the MAB network may help address the well-known trade-off between energy efficiency and noise, an inherent limitation in electronic devices and circuits affected by both spatially stationary variability and spatiotemporal dynamic noise.70 Within this context, the MAB network could be implemented using either digital or analog hardware paradigms. However, only an analog implementation is likely to deliver the necessary performance in terms of energy efficiency, noise robustness, and silicon area occupation, while preserving the overall computational capabilities of the model. Notably, adopting a slightly less compact neuron design or increasing the overall network size is expected to exponentially enhance noise resilience, while the associated increase in area usage and energy consumption would scale linearly. These characteristics suggest that the MAB network could enable highly optimized neuromorphic circuits capable of approximating the computational complexity and efficiency of biological systems,71 offering a compelling direction for the next generation of low-power, noise-tolerant artificial intelligence hardware.

Limitations of the study

Demonstrating that a neuronal network with single predictive elements can effectively perform reward-based tasks, resist noise is significant, as growing evidence suggests that biological circuits learn temporal patterns and generate predictions by tuning synaptic weights and neuronal excitability4. While the present model is in line with the predictive coding framework, different tasks and architectures must be explored to demonstrate the solidity of the computational paradigm. Moreover, the computational load of simulations with large networks has limited the possibility to investigate the full scalability of the network and, therefore, assess the resilience to noise at larger network sizes. Finally, we have investigated only one of the possible strategies to limit the impact of the noise on MAB network performance, whereas alternative solutions inspired by biological circuits such as synaptic filters72 or contrast enhancement73 could be explored.

Method details

We designed and examined the behavior of a network composed by neurons performing Bayesian inference (called MAB) that self-organizes through free energy minimization. Under this framework the excitability of the biological neuron can be conceived as an optimization process based on the minimization of a cost function, defined as variational free energy (VFE), through a variational message passing (VMP) algorithm.6

The VFE is defined as:Equation 1VFE=DKL(q(st)‖p(st|o))−lnp(ot)where DKL denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence operator, and q and p are the approximate and the exact probability distributions, respectively. ot represents the synaptic inputs received by the neuron, and st the external hidden states (i.e., the state of activity of the other neurons afferent to it), such that q expresses the probability that a hidden state st has generated the input received by the neuron. The VMP algorithm iteratively converges to an approximate solution of the variational problem. In this context, the generative model of a single agent “world” is a joint probability of the external causes to the agent and the observables they generate (i.e., the synaptic input to the agent). This can be decomposed into the product of a likelihood matrix A, probabilistically relating external causes to current observables (ot) at a given time point, and a prior (Bayesian) belief about the external states, which is parameterized by the agent’s internal states (st). This Bayesian process enables decision-making even under conditions of limited information or uncertainty. This implies that the inferred value of the internal state may lead the neuron to one of two alternative states: active (on) or inactive (off) according to its prediction about received synaptic inputs. Adopting such an inferential model thus allows the excitability of a single neuron to be conceived as a stochastic event, whose posterior probability distribution is updated based on the inputs it receives.

In particular, the inference can be reformulated as an optimization process, where the updating of beliefs (computation of the posterior distribution) corresponds to the convergence of the variational free energy (VFE) toward a local minimum. The neuron thus functions as an inferential unit, and at each time point, the internal states are updated through an error term (εt). The internal states are associated with the neuron’s membrane potential (vt) as follows:Equation 2vt=ln(st)In the specific case of the VMP algorithm applied to the neuron, the mathematical formulation of the problem is given by the following equation, whose solution provides an estimate of the internal states (st):Equation 3ln(st)=ln(Bt−1st−1)+ln(BtTst+1)+∑j=1mln(Ajoj,t−1)where B is the transition matrix (potentially also a function of time), which indicates the probability that the internal state evolves into another state, given its state at previous time point. The solution consists in estimating the value of the internal states (st) that satisfies the equation above. This can be expressed in variational terms by rewriting the equation through the introduction of the state prediction error (εt), which corresponds to the gradient of the free energy.Equation 4εt=ln(Bt−1st−1)+ln(BtTst+1)+∑j=1mln(AjToj,t−1)−ln(st)Equation 5vt˙=εt=−∂FE∂stEquation 6st=σ(vt)In the equation above, the softmax operator (σ) is a sigmoidal activation function that mimics the depolarization responsible for the neuronal action potential and also serves the purpose of making the posterior distribution over st a proper probability (i.e., integrating to unity). This describes both the updating of beliefs and the neuronal dynamics at each time step—that is, the inference process.

It was recently shown that a network composed by single inferential neurons, modeled according to,6,7 and organized to map the hierarchical organization of real brain circuits can adapt its behavior to implement a functional task. In this study, we adopt the same inferential approach to solve a reward-predictive task. For this, we adapt the network structure to mimic the dopaminergic circuit that solves the same task in biological organisms. The circuit used in this study comprised 132 neurons with the synaptic connectivity illustrated in Figure 1C. The network activity evolves with a time step accounting for synaptic integration and the action potential refractory period. The network contains both excitatory and inhibitory synapses. Each neuron’s hidden state is represented by a one-dimensional quantity (s(t-1) ∈ {0,1}), that can be either “off”, i.e., non-firing (0) or “on”, i.e., firing (1). Depending on the connectivity matrix, if the neuron is firing, at the subsequent time step it will cause an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP) or an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP) in its post-synaptic neurons. The EPSPs or IPSPs represent the observation or sensory input ot for the postsynaptic neuron and are one-dimensional quantities (ot ∈ {0,1}). According to,6 the generative model is described by a likelihood matrix (A) mapping hidden states to sensory input, and prior beliefs over hidden states. Thus, each synaptic connection is represented by a likelihood matrix which encodes the probability of a specific ot given s(t-1). In our setup, the A matrices for excitatory synapses are initialized as follows:Equation 7Aexc=σ(log[1001]+e−12)while the A matrices for the inhibitory synapse are initialized as:Equation 8Ainh=σ(log[1100]+e−2)

The σ denotes the softmax operator (normalized exponential) and the quantities of e−1/2 and e−2 are weights added to differentiate the strength of inhibitory and excitatory connections. From the perspective of the generative model, these weights control the precision of the likelihood (i.e., the sensitivity to pre-synaptic inputs). In likelihood matrices such as the above, the element Ai,j represents the probability that the hidden state st-1[j] will induce the postsynaptic outcome ot[i]. Ainh features higher precision compared to Aexc in order to mimic the higher efficacy of inhibitory synapses compared to excitatory synapses. Ainh are responsible for decreasing the firing probability of the postsynaptic neuron. In addition to the generative process of encoding the output (EPSPs) generated by latent hidden causes (presynaptic firing), each neuron encodes, in terms of firing probability (X), an inference about the hidden states generating its inputs through an iterative minimization of variational free energy. The firing probability X encodes the update of the prior beliefs about the presynaptic hidden states. The generative model requires the initialization of the prior belief about hidden states (D). This parameter represents the firing probability of a neuron which corresponds to its “belief” about the state of its presynaptic neurons at the beginning of each exchange of spikes or messages (see below). D is updated at time t + 1 to the posterior expectation of hidden state at the end of the iterative free energy minimization in the variational Bayes inference process describing neuronal dynamics.

Finally, the conditional dependence of st on s(t-1) is parametrized by a probability transition matrix (B). In our model matrix B is set equal to the softmax of the identity matrix [1,0;0,1], since the circuit is not equipped with specific temporal dynamics, such as oscillations, that are prescribed by a conditional dependence between states. Intuitively, this means each neuron “expects” that the hidden or latent states generating its observations do not change during its period of belief updating.

Finally, the generative model requires the initialization of the prior belief about hidden states (D).

The reward predictive task

The dynamics of the system was simulated in time-steps. The reward predictive task (Figure 1) was implemented by inducing a generic sensory stimulus (the conditioned stimulus - CS) temporally paired with a reward stimulus (the unconditioned stimulus - US). The former was produced by a repetitive stimulation (45 stimuli in 60 time-steps) repeated 10 times every 240 time-steps, while the latter was conveyed as a repetitive stimulation (12 stimuli in 15 time-steps) repeated every 180 time-steps. The US was always delivered within the CS stimulation window and more precisely the ends of the two stimulations window coincided. Each simulation comprised the subsequent and independent repetition of three conditions: US, CS and combined USCS.

Network performance

The reward dopaminergic circuit is known to be involved in the prediction of rewarding stimuli. In the MAB network simulations, this predictive feature was quantified by the number of spikes emitted by NAc neurons in the first-time window of the USCS stimulus, while NAc neurons remained silent during the CR and UR conditions separately. The number of spikes associated with a specific time-steps was obtained by introducing an upper threshold of 90% for the firing probability of NAc neurons output. To compare different network configurations, this value was normalized to the maximum possible number of spikes generated by NAc neurons in the US window (1 spike every two simulation steps, accounting for the refractory period) and then scaled according to the network size. For example, in a time window of 20 cycles, 3 NAc neurons can generate a maximum of 30 spikes, whereas in a network with 6 NAc neurons, the maximum number of spikes would be 60, and so on. Therefore, if the original network performed the task with 15 spikes emitted by NAc neurons, the performance would be considered the same if, in a doubled MAB network, NAc neurons emitted 30 spikes. Network performance was evaluated based on the first stimulation cycle in which the predictive behavior appeared.

Input noise

The input noise was introduced by randomly varying the interval between two consecutive sets of stimuli (either CS, US or USCS). The maximum time window between consecutive sets of stimuli required for the associative behavior to take place was assessed by progressively increasing the interval of a fixed duration (80 simulation steps), from the original 80 time-steps per cycle to 560. Once assessed the maximum interstimulus interval, the noisy input patterns were generated by randomizing the interval between two consecutive stimuli while preserving the average interval (240 time-steps). The stimulation pattern was varied by randomly sampling the interstimulus interval from a Gaussian distribution centered on 240 time-steps and with increasing variance. The network performance was measured while changing the variance of the distribution adopted to sample the interstimulus interval.

Internal noise

The internal noise was introduced at each cycle of Bayesian free energy minimization by adding a non-stationary value to the operating variable (X, D, A). This value was randomly sampled from a uniform distribution within a symmetric range between a negative and a positive integer (e.g., [-a, +a]), or alternatively, it was sampled from a Gaussian distribution centered around the true value of the operating variable, with a standard deviation proportional to the error (e.g., 1/3 of the percentage error). In the latter case the value was extracted from a normal distribution with variable parameters: average value (m) and Full-Width at Half Maximum (s). The number extracted from the normal distribution was then added to parameters updated during every iteration cycle and required to calculate the free-energy minimization. Furthermore, errors were added either homogeneously to all the neurons in the network or non-homogeneously by picking different numbers from the distribution for every neuron in the network.

To investigate the impact of noise at various stages of the simulation, we systematically perturbed the pristine values x of the simulation with an additive relative Gaussian noise, defined as:Equation 9x′=x+σ·N(0,1)

Here, N(0,1) is a random variable drawn from a Gaussian distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1. For this purpose, we used the MATLAB R2022b randn function. We defined the standard deviation σ of the applied noise as:Equation 10σ=x·err_rel3where err_rel represents the relative error, expressed as a number between 0 and 1. This relation evaluated the maximum possible error and ensures that 99.7% of the times error takes a value that falls within ±err_rel of the original value, given the assumption that the values follow a normal distribution (empirical 3σ rule).

To fit the simulation constrains we clipped the values of x’ in the range [0,1]. Since the variables are 2D vectors and represent probability distribution, we normalized each element by their sum. This was achieved by applying the function spm_norm() from the MATLAB Library SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm-statistical-parametric-mapping/).

The assumption of a Gaussian (normal) distribution to describe noise in neural transmission is based on both mathematical and biological/statistical reasons. Neural transmission involves many independent stochastic processes: neurotransmitter release, ion channel fluctuations, thermal noise, etc. According to the Central Limit Theorem, the sum of many small, independent random variables tends toward a normal distribution, even if the individual variables are not normally distributed. This makes it natural to model the resulting noise as Gaussian.

Relative error

To investigate the degree of resilience of the network, we applied small perturbations on different variables and stages of the computational simulation. We designed the perturbation to be a relative additive term to the true outcomes of the variables:Equation 11J′=J+σerrrwhere J and J′ are respectively the true and the perturbed outcome of the specific algorithmic step, r is a random value sampled from a standard normal distribution. σerr is the factor used to rescale the distribution standard deviation which is obtained by imposing the following condition:Equation 123σerr=Jerelwith erel being the relative error. We used MATLAB R2022b built-in pseudo-random number generator randn().

Spatial error

To investigate the impact of mismatch between network elements, we assigned a steady error, different for each neuron of the network, but stationary during the entire simulation. The set of errors are generated at the beginning of the simulations and each time a neuron i performed a computation, its firing probability D was perturbed by the corresponding error term erri:Equation 13D′(erri)=max⁡(0,min⁡(1,Fp)/erri)where the combination of max and min functions is used to clip the value of Fp_i to the range [0,1]. This perturbation reflects the success rate of spike generation through the function:Equation 14APi=binornd(1,Fpi′(erri))with binornd() being the MATLAB R2022b binomial distribution function used to output the outcome of a success/fail trial with probability of success Fpi (erri).

Each error term was generated by sampling a pseudo-random value from a normal distribution with mean μ and standard deviation σ. This task was performed by exploiting the built-in MATLAB R2022b function normrnd(μ,σ,#neu), with #neu being the total number of neurons in the network.

Synaptic precision

Neural plasticity was estimated by monitoring synaptic precision during all the simulation and calculating the relative variation of every contact in respect to pre-stimulus average value. Long-term changes were considered stable only if a variation of at least 20% could be measured and persisted until the end of the simulation.
