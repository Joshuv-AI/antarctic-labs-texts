DeNovoSeer:a deep learning framework for pathogenicity prediction of De novo mutations
Authors: Rong Qiu, Xiyu Rao, Hong Jiang, Jinchen Li, Guihu Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDe novo mutations (DNMs) play a crucial role in the pathogenesis and clinical interpretation of genetic diseases. However, existing pathogenicity prediction methods either uniformly handle all variants or focus on specific variant types, lacking a systematic prediction framework for DNMs and failing to effectively integrate functional annotation with clinical evidence. In this study, we present DeNovoSeer, a deep learning pathogenicity prediction framework for coding-region DNMs. Built upon a labeling system that combines high-confidence ClinVar annotations with complementary phenotype information from Gene4Denovo, the method integrates multi-source functional annotations and clinical evidence derived from the ACMG/AMP guidelines. It employs a semi-supervised convolutional-dilated convolution hybrid network architecture, enabling joint representation learning across multiple tasks under limited labeled data. On the Gene4Denovo test set, DeNovoSeer demonstrated stable performance across 10 independent random splits, achieving an AUC of 0.876 ± 0.007 and an AP of 0.881 ± 0.008, while outperforming existing tools. SHAP-based analysis provides feature-level attribution of model predictions, revealing biologically and clinically meaningful evidence patterns and offering interpretable support for variant assessment. This study proposes a systematic framework for pathogenicity prediction of coding-region DNMs, integrating robust label construction, semi-supervised representation learning, and clinical evidence integration. It provides new methodological support for molecular diagnosis and the interpretation of disease mechanisms.Keywords: de novo mutations, deep learning, pathogenicity prediction, clinical interpretation, semi-supervised learning, ACMG/AMP guidelines

Introduction

De novo mutations (DNMs) are DNA sequence alterations that arise spontaneously in germ cells or during early embryonic development [1]. DNMs play a crucial pathogenic role in a broad spectrum of genetic and complex diseases, particularly in early-onset neurological disorders such as autism spectrum disorder [2], intellectual disability [3], early-onset Parkinson's disease [4], and early-onset Alzheimer’s disease [5], more precisely, coding-region DNMs contributed approximately 30% of the diagnosis rate of individual cases and explained pathogenic mutations in 45% of female patients [2]. In autism, DNMs can account for 52% to 67% of low-risk families and 30% to 39% of the pathogenic sources of all cases [6]. In severe developmental disorders (DD), Pathogenic DNMs are significantly enriched in development-related genes, resulting in a birth prevalence ranging from 1/213 to 1/448 [7]. The widespread adoption of high-throughput sequencing technologies [8] has greatly expanded the discovery of DNMs; however, the functional and pathogenic significance of most variants remains elusive. Therefore, accurately assessing the pathogenic potential of DNMs is essential for molecular diagnosis, etiological interpretation, and personalized medicine.

In recent years, the rapid advancement of machine learning and deep learning has led to the development of numerous variant pathogenicity prediction tools. SpliceAI [9] employs deep neural networks to capture genomic splicing signals; CADD [10] integrates conservation metrics, regulatory annotations, and transcript information.

More recent studies have further advanced the field toward more expressive and biologically informed models. For example, SVPath [11] demonstrates the importance of variant-type-specific modeling for exon structural variants; V2P [12] introduces phenotype-conditioned multi-task learning for joint prediction of pathogenicity and disease outcomes; GNN-based approaches such as GNN-MAP [13] leverage graph representations to integrate multimodal annotations; and popEVE [14] combines deep evolutionary signals with human population variation to improve calibration of variant effect prediction. These developments highlight a shift toward phenotype-aware, multimodal, and better calibrated pathogenicity prediction frameworks.

Despite these advances, existing tools remain limited when applied to DNMs, particularly in coding regions. First, widely used predictors (e.g., CADD, DANN [15]) provide general functional scores but are not specifically designed for DNMs and do not explicitly encode de novo evidence such as ACMG PS2/PM6. Other methods focus on specialized variant classes—for example, ReVe [16] targets missense variants; CancerVar [17] is tailored to somatic mutations; other approaches, such as ECSWalk [18] and MSINGB [19], focus on cancer-specific tasks including driver gene prioritization and microsatellite instability detection —and are therefore not optimized for germline DNMs. These mutations are characterized by extremely low population frequency, strong individual specificity, parental validation, and limited labeled data, making unified modeling challenging.

Second, most existing tools prioritize functional annotations while integrating limited or no clinical evidence, leading to a disconnect between functional prediction and clinical interpretation. In addition, many high-performing models remain difficult to interpret, as their predictions lack transparent feature-level explanations that can be related to clinically relevant evidence.

Taken together, no existing framework simultaneously provides (i) DNM-specific labeling and explicit PS2/PM6-based de novo evidence, (ii) comprehensive functional annotation for coding variants, and (iii) a semi-supervised learning paradigm capable of leveraging large unlabeled DNM datasets while maintaining clinical interpretability.

To address these limitations, we propose DeNovoSeer, a pathogenicity prediction framework specifically designed for coding-region DNMs. The framework systematically integrates multi-source functional annotations and clinical evidence under a labeling system grounded in the principle of high-confidence ClinVar annotations complemented by Gene4Denovo phenotype information. It employs a semi-supervised convolutional–dilated convolution hybrid network architecture to jointly model functional and clinical signals, while interpretability analysis bridges model outputs with clinical evidence [20, 21].

The integrated feature set encompasses six major categories: evolutionary conservation, coding-region and cross-regional functional prediction, splicing effects, population allele frequencies, and ACMG/AMP-based [22] clinical evidence scores. The model is trained within a semi-supervised deep learning framework that jointly optimizes supervised classification, self-supervised reconstruction, and contrastive learning tasks. This approach leverages both labeled and unlabeled variants, enhancing generalization under limited labeled data. Furthermore, SHAP [23] analysis provides feature-level attribution of model predictions, enabling a more interpretable and clinically meaningful assessment of variant pathogenicity, transforming the prediction process from a "black box” into a traceable and explainable framework.

Materials and methods

Datasets

The training and validation datasets used in this study were derived from the Gene4Denovo database [24], which we previously constructed and made publicly available. This database systematically integrates 741,866 records of DNMs, providing a comprehensive and reliable foundation for this work. During data preprocessing, we removed entries lacking annotation information and excluded duplicate variants reported in multiple publications, resulting in a final set of 70,415 coding-region variants.

To evaluate independent generalization, we employed the Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) cohort from the SPARK project [25] as an external test set. Following the DNM identification framework proposed by Samocha et al. [26], we implemented a standardized pipeline comprising: (i) identifying trio structures (proband–parent combinations) based on the VCF, family structure (.fam), and variant list files; (ii) applying strict quality control, including genotype quality (GQ) thresholds, Mendelian consistency checks, and additional exclusion of false-positive sites using mutation frequency, parent–offspring depth ratio, and allele balance; and (iii) performing de novo mutation calling to validate true DNM status through probabilistic modeling.

This rigorous process effectively distinguishes genuine DNMs from sequencing or genotyping artifacts, ensuring the high reliability of the resulting variant set. After filtering, 33,948 coding-region variants were retained.

Variant and label selection criteria

Given the rarity of DNMs, existing databases provide limited annotation of their functional and pathogenic characteristics. In particular, only a small fraction of DNMs are recorded in authoritative resources such as ClinVar [27]. To construct a standardized labeling system, A hierarchical labeling framework was designed to combine high-confidence ClinVar evidence with phenotype-based statistics from Gene4Denovo. The overall labeling workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Hierarchical Labeling framework for coding-region DNMs. An overview of the variant and label selection workflow.

ClinVar-based labeling. Variants were first annotated using high-confidence ClinVar entries (≥2-star). Variants meeting these criteria were directly assigned ClinVar pathogenic or benign labels, and in cases of conflict with Gene4Denovo annotations, ClinVar labels were prioritized.

Variants without ClinVar annotations were labeled based on the Phenotype field and variant source information within Gene4Denovo, following these rules: (i) Benign (label = 0) : All reported phenotypes were controls. (ii) Pathogenic (label = 1) : All reported phenotypes were disease-related (non-control) and observed in at least two independent studies or in two or more affected individuals. (iii) Unknown (label = -1) : Variants that did not meet either of the above criteria.

Applying this hierarchical labeling framework, the dataset was divided into clinically interpretable categories while retaining ambiguous variants for semi-supervised modeling. In the Gene4Denovo dataset, 63,494 variants were labeled as unknown, 3,956 as benign (813 from ClinVar and 3,143 from Gene4Denovo), and 2,965 as pathogenic (1,704 from ClinVar and 1,261 from Gene4Denovo). In the independent SPARK cohort, the distribution was similarly imbalanced: 24,817 variants were labeled as unknown, 8,614 as benign (260 from ClinVar and 8,354 from Gene4Denovo), and 517 as pathogenic (264 from ClinVar and 253 from Gene4Denovo).

This strategy enabled the construction of a clinically grounded label system that balances high-confidence expert annotation with statistical evidence from large-scale phenotypic observations, forming a robust foundation for semi-supervised learning and cross-cohort generalization in real-world clinical settings.

Dataset independence and similarity analysis

To ensure strict independence between the SPARK test set and the Gene4Denovo training set, we evaluated feature-space cosine similarity across cross-cohort and within-dataset contrasts.

To avoid redundancy and potential information leakage, SPARK variants with cosine similarity > 0.95 to any Gene4Denovo pathogenic variant were removed. This threshold was selected to exclude only variants with highly similar feature representations, while retaining the majority of the independent SPARK cohort. After filtering, the final SPARK test set contained 27,266 variants (19,418 unknown, 7,489 benign, and 359 pathogenic), with no overlapping variants between the two datasets.

After filtering, as shown in Supplementary Table S1 and Figures S1–S3, cross-cohort pathogenic variants exhibited moderate positive similarity (mean ≈ 0.10), whereas benign pairs were centered near zero. In contrast, pathogenic–benign comparisons within each dataset shifted toward lower or negative values, indicating class separation in feature space. These results support effective feature-space discrimination and confirm cohort independence. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis using stricter cosine similarity thresholds showed consistent performance trends across methods (Supplementary Table S16).

Feature annotation

Candidate DNMs were first annotated with basic functional information using ANNOVAR (version 2024.09.24) [28], including transcript position, mutation type, and amino acid change. To characterize variant effects, multiple predictive tools were integrated through the VarCards2 framework [29], including MutPred2 [30], ReVe, and SpliceAI, capturing functional impact and splicing alterations. All scores were normalized and combined into a unified feature matrix.

In addition to functional annotations, clinical evidence features were incorporated from ClinVar and the automated ACMG/AMP scoring tool GeneBe [31], enabling integration of expert-curated evidence and standardized clinical interpretation.

For modeling convenience, all features were grouped into functional features and clinical evidence features. Functional features describe molecular and evolutionary properties, while evidence-based features encode ACMG/AMP guideline interpretations. It is noteworthy that clinical evidence features (e.g., PP3, BP4) are conceptually related to functional features, as the ACMG PP3 criterion itself is derived from functional prediction outcomes such as conservation or damage scores. Incorporating both enables dual benefits: functional features retain raw quantitative descriptors for data-driven pattern learning, while evidence scores encode domain knowledge from expert guidelines—allowing model predictions to align naturally with ACMG reasoning logic while maintaining flexibility and transparency.

Functional features

Functional features encompass five categories: evolutionary conservation, coding-region functional prediction, cross-region functional features, splicing effects, and population allele frequencies. Evolutionary conservation was quantified using GERP++ [32], phyloP [33], phastCons [33], SiPhy [34], and LINSIGHT [35]; Coding-region functional prediction features included 52 widely used tools (e.g., SIFT [36], PolyPhen-2 [37], MutationTaster2 [38], REVEL [39], MutPred2, AlphaMissense [40], and EVE [41]), capturing diverse aspects of protein-level impact; Cross-region prediction models such as Eigen [42] and GenoCanyon [43] were incorporated to capture regulatory and epigenetic context that can influence gene function and expression, thereby providing complementary signals beyond protein-centric effects rather than serving as proxies for missing coding features; Splicing effects were evaluated using SpliceAI, which provides four delta scores (DS_AG, DS_AL, DS_DG, DS_DL) and corresponding positional annotations; Population allele frequency features were obtained from gnomAD v4.1 (exome and genome) [44], ExAC [45], and ESP [46], resulting in 63 features describing global, population-specific, and rare variant frequencies.

Clinical evidence features

Clinical evidence features were constructed in two stages. First, ACMG/AMP interpretations from GeneBe were used to extract general evidence items and an aggregated clinical score, providing baseline clinical evidence for each variant.

As GeneBe does not include PS2/PM6 evidence, these were modeled separately following ClinGen SVI recommendations [47]. Given that Gene4Denovo primarily comprises trio-based studies with confirmed parental validation, all variants were treated as “de novo with confirmed parentage” and assigned proband-level scores (2, 1, 0.5, or 0) based on phenotypic consistency. Scores were summed and mapped to ACMG evidence strength levels (Supporting, Moderate, Strong, Very Strong).

In the model, PS2/PM6 were encoded directly as ACMG evidence levels rather than intermediate scores. Following Tavtigian et al. [48], evidence strengths were numerically represented as Supporting = 1, Moderate = 2, Strong = 4, and Very Strong = 8, with benign evidence assigned negative values and absence encoded as 0.

Finally, PS2/PM6 evidence is integrated with GeneBe-derived evidence at two levels: (i) as ACMG evidence items, and (ii) in the calculation of the final clinical score through summation. Together, the evidence items and the aggregated clinical score constitute the clinical evidence feature set used in the model.

Notably, as all variants are de novo, PS2 is present at least at supporting level with variable strength, whereas PM6 is consistently encoded as 0 due to the absence of unverified cases.

Feature extraction and processing

Evidence-based features were one-hot encoded, preserving the original structure of ACMG evidence while enabling the model to learn relative feature weights. Combined with SHAP analysis, this design enables traceable, clause-level interpretability in prediction outcomes.

Model construction

Framework architecture

To address label scarcity and class imbalance among candidate DNMs, we developed DeNovoSeer, a semi-supervised deep learning framework.

The DeNovoSeer framework consists of three main components: a feature extractor that encodes variant annotation vectors into high-dimensional latent representations, a supervised classification head that learns discriminative decision boundaries from labeled variants, and a self-supervised learning module composed of a reconstruction head and a contrastive learning head, designed to capture intrinsic structural information from unlabeled data.

Feature extraction network

The feature extractor adopts a hybrid CNN–IDCNN (Iterated Dilated Convolutional Neural Network) architecture to capture both local and long-range dependencies across heterogeneous features. The CNN module includes two one-dimensional convolutional layers (64 and 128 channels, kernel size = 3), each followed by batch normalization, ReLU activation, and max pooling.

The IDCNN module comprises four dilated convolutional layers (256 channels, kernel size = 3) with dilation rates of 1, 2, 4, and 8, expanding the receptive field without reducing feature resolution. An adaptive average pooling layer and a fully connected layer project features into a shared 128-dimensional latent representation.

Multi-task learning module

The supervised classification head consists of a dropout layer (rate = 0.5) followed by a fully connected layer that outputs pathogenicity probabilities. The self-supervised module includes a reconstruction head (two-layer MLP optimized with mean squared error) and a contrastive projection head following a SimCLR-style design, mapping features into a 64-dimensional embedding space.

For contrastive learning, feature perturbation-based augmentation was used to generate multiple views. Specifically, Gaussian noise and random feature masking were applied, which were selected based on ablation results (Supplementary Table S5). These perturbations mimic measurement noise and incomplete annotations while preserving the underlying biological semantics. The resulting representations are optimized using the NT-Xent (normalized temperature-scaled cross-entropy) loss.

Further architectural and implementation details are provided in Supplementary Note S1.

Activation functions

The classification output layer uses the Softmax activation function [50] to normalize the model output into a binary classification probability, directly reflecting the pathogenic risk of the variant. The hidden layers use the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) function to alleviate gradient vanishing while maintaining computational efficiency [51].

Loss functions

The model is trained with a joint loss comprising three components: Focal Loss [52] for supervised classification to address class imbalance, mean squared error (MSE) [53] for reconstruction to preserve latent feature structure, and NT-Xent loss [54] for contrastive learning to enforce representation consistency. These losses are combined with weights (λ_recon = 1.0, λ_contrast = 0.5), selected via grid search on the validation set (seed = 42).

Model training and optimization

Model training was conducted under a semi-supervised multi-task framework that jointly optimizes supervised and self-supervised objectives.

During preprocessing, all variant features were standardized [55] to ensure comparability across heterogeneous data sources. The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets in a 6:2:2 ratio. To assess robustness, ten independent splits were generated using random seeds from 42 to 51. In each split, labeled variants were partitioned using stratified sampling to preserve class distributions, while unlabeled variants were randomly assigned. Final performance was reported as mean ± standard deviation across the ten runs.

During training, labeled and unlabeled variants were organized into separate data loaders and sampled at an approximate 1:8 ratio per mini-batch, consistent with the training data composition after labeled oversampling, thereby preserving the intrinsic data structure while stabilizing semi-supervised optimization at the batch level. Random oversampling (RandomOverSampler [56]) was applied only to labeled training data to mitigate class imbalance, while validation and test sets retained their original distributions. Focal loss was used to address class imbalance within labeled samples only.

For each mini-batch, latent representations were generated by the shared feature extractor. Labeled samples were used to compute classification loss, while unlabeled samples contributed reconstruction and contrastive losses. The total loss was defined as a weighted sum of these components and optimized jointly via backpropagation.

Model parameters were optimized using Adam [57] (β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, learning rate = 0.001), with ReduceLROnPlateau scheduling, gradient clipping (threshold = 1.0) [58], and early stopping based on validation AUC. The model with the best validation performance was retained.

The weights of the self-supervised losses were selected via grid search on the validation set (seed = 42), with λ_recon ∈ {0.5, 1.0, 2.0} and λ_contrast ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 1.0}. The final configuration (λ_recon = 1.0, λ_contrast = 0.5) achieved the best performance and was used in all experiments. Performance was stable across settings (Supplementary Table S6), indicating low sensitivity to these hyperparameters.

During inference, only the feature extractor and classification head were retained to produce final pathogenicity probabilities. Unless otherwise specified, a default decision threshold of 0.5 was used to derive binary classifications.

Feature importance and model interpretability

To enhance model transparency in clinical applications, we incorporated SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to quantify the contribution of each input feature to pathogenicity predictions. SHAP estimates feature importance by measuring the change in model output when individual features are perturbed relative to a baseline, enabling both global and instance-level interpretation.

At the global level, SHAP identifies the most influential features driving model predictions, reflecting the relative importance of functional annotations, computational scores, and population-based signals. At the individual level, SHAP decomposes each prediction into positive and negative feature contributions, providing insight into the factors supporting or opposing a pathogenic classification.

Notably, many input features are conceptually related to ACMG evidence categories. For example, computational prediction scores correspond to PP3-like evidence, loss-of-function indicators relate to PVS1, and population frequency features reflect PM2 or benign evidence such as BS2. Although SHAP does not provide a direct one-to-one mapping to ACMG criteria, this alignment enables model predictions to be interpreted in terms of clinically relevant evidence types, facilitating transparent and clinically meaningful interpretation.

Module-level ablation

We evaluated four configurations: full model, w/o contrastive, w/o reconstruction, and supervised-only (i.e., w/o contrastive+reconstruction). As shown in Figure 3, removing either the contrastive or reconstruction module consistently degraded performance, indicating that contrastive learning enhances representation discriminability while reconstruction preserves feature structure. Robustness under feature similarity-based partitioning

To further assess model robustness under distribution shift, DNMs from the Gene4Denovo dataset were partitioned into 10 clusters based on feature-level similarity using MiniBatch K-means. Each cluster was iteratively used as an independent test set, while the remaining clusters were used for training and validation.

Under this more stringent setting, DeNovoSeer still achieved strong performance (e.g., AUC > 0.80) on several clusters, indicating that the model can generalize well when evaluated on feature-similar subsets. At the same time, lower performance was observed in certain clusters, likely reflecting increased difficulty associated with specific feature patterns rather than overfitting under random partitioning. These results suggest that the model retains meaningful predictive ability under feature-based partitioning, supporting its robustness beyond random splits. Detailed per-cluster performance statistics are provided in Supplementary Table S10.

To explore factors driving cluster-level performance variation, we analyzed intrinsic cluster characteristics. A strong positive correlation was observed between cluster pathogenic ratio and predictive performance (AUC: R=0.83, p=0.003; AP: R=0.95, p<0.001; Supplementary Figure S4). While minor deviations exist, the overall pattern remains statistically robust. All baseline methods showed relatively consistent performance patterns across clusters (Supplementary Table S15), confirming challenging subsets reflect intrinsic dataset properties rather than model-specific limitations.

SHAP interpretability results analysis

Global SHAP consistency

To assess model interpretability, SHAP analysis was performed on the Gene4Denovo test set, the independent SPARK cohort, and a feature-similarity-based clustering setting using representative splits (seed = 42; cluster 0).

As shown in Fig. 7A, the most influential features are consistently dominated by computational pathogenicity scores (e.g., MPC, M-CAP, AlphaMissense) and ACMG-related evidence features (e.g., PVS1, PM2, PP2), indicating reliance on biologically and clinically relevant signals.

(A–C) SHAP summary plots for (A) Gene4Denovo test set, (B) independent SPARK cohort, and (C) feature-similarity-based cluster partition (cluster 0). All results are based on representative splits (seed = 42 for Gene4Denovo and SPARK). Across settings, top-ranked features are consistently dominated by computational pathogenicity scores and ACMG-related evidence features, indicating stable and biologically meaningful attribution patterns.

Similar patterns were observed in the SPARK cohort (Fig. 7B), suggesting that feature attribution is generalizable beyond the training distribution. Under cluster-based evaluation (Fig. 7C), SHAP importance patterns remained largely stable, with only minor ranking variations, demonstrating robustness to partitioning strategies. Cross-region features (e.g., VARA_score [59]) also contributed consistently, indicating that they capture biologically relevant regulatory signals rather than merely acting as proxies for coding-region features.

Notably, SHAP provides feature-level attribution rather than a direct one-to-one mapping to ACMG criteria. However, many of the input features are designed to correspond to ACMG evidence categories. For example, computational prediction scores (e.g., MPC, M-CAP, AlphaMissense) are conceptually aligned with PP3, loss-of-function indicators correspond to PVS1, and population frequency features relate to PM2 or benign evidence such as BS2. Therefore, SHAP contributions can be interpreted as reflecting the relative influence of different ACMG-relevant evidence types in the model’s decision-making process.

This framework provides a clinically meaningful link between model predictions and ACMG-guided reasoning. In particular, for variants with ambiguous predictions, SHAP enables decomposition into supporting and opposing evidence, offering insight into VUS-like cases and the evidence required to shift classification.

Together, these results suggest that DeNovoSeer produces stable and clinically meaningful feature attribution patterns across datasets and evaluation settings.

Case study

To further illustrate the clinical interpretability of DeNovoSeer, we analyzed representative variants with distinct prediction outcomes using SHAP-based decomposition (Fig. 8). Detailed metadata for these variants are provided in Supplementary Table S11.

For a highly pathogenic variant (Fig. 8A), the prediction is primarily driven by strong positive contributions from loss-of-function-related features (e.g., PVS1) and deleterious computational scores (e.g., MPC, M-CAP), which are consistent with ACMG pathogenic evidence categories. In contrast, for a highly benign variant (Fig. 8B), negative contributions from benign evidence and population-related features (e.g., BS2-related signals) dominate the prediction, leading to a confident benign classification.

More importantly, for borderline variants (Fig. 8C–D), the model output lies near the decision threshold due to competing contributions from pathogenic and benign evidence. In these cases, SHAP provides a decomposition of the prediction into supporting and opposing evidence components, revealing the underlying reasons for uncertainty. Such interpretability is particularly valuable for variants of uncertain significance (VUS), as it highlights which types of evidence contribute to ambiguity and suggests what additional evidence may be required to shift the classification.

Overall, these case studies demonstrate that SHAP-based explanations enable variant-level interpretation that is consistent with ACMG reasoning principles, providing a transparent and clinically meaningful framework for understanding both confident and ambiguous predictions.

Discussion

This study proposed and systematically evaluated a deep learning prediction framework for coding regions, DeNovoSeer, targeting DNMs with real-world challenges such as label scarcity, complex features, and extremely imbalanced classes. Unlike previous methods that mostly focused on specific variant types or single evidence dimensions, this study decoupled label construction and evidence integration: a labeling system was constructed at the data level (high-confidence ClinVar labels + phenotype supplementation from Gene4Denovo); and ACMG clinical evidence criteria were quantified and integrated with multi-source functional annotations as inputs to the semi-supervised hybrid convolutional model. This strategy achieved stable gains in both the Gene4Denovo test set and the independent SPARK cohort, especially in real clinical scenarios with extremely imbalanced label distributions, maintaining superior overall discriminative ability and generalization performance compared to existing tools.

This study provides several key advances. First, a high-quality labeling strategy was established by prioritizing ClinVar expert-reviewed labels and supplementing them with Gene4Denovo phenotype evidence, balancing reliability and scale. Second, by explicitly encoding ACMG criteria (e.g., PVS1, PM1, PP3, BS1), the model bridges functional annotations and clinical interpretation, enabling predictions that are both quantitatively robust and clinically interpretable. Third, the semi-supervised framework jointly optimizes classification, reconstruction, and contrastive learning, allowing effective utilization of unlabeled variants and improving robustness under limited supervision. Finally, SHAP analysis demonstrates that model decisions are primarily driven by biologically and clinically meaningful features and can be interpreted in a manner consistent with ACMG reasoning.

Additionally, full-coverage prediction is critical for clinical applicability. Many existing tools are restricted to specific variant types and may fail to provide predictions for a substantial proportion of variants. In contrast, DeNovoSeer maintains complete coverage, reducing the risk of variant exclusion and supporting end-to-end clinical workflows. On the independent SPARK cohort, despite severe class imbalance, the model retains competitive ranking performance, suggesting its potential utility in realistic diagnostic scenarios.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, although DeNovoSeer demonstrates stable performance across datasets, a performance drop was observed on the independent SPARK cohort, which may reflect differences in cohort characteristics beyond class imbalance and label noise. Further validation on additional independent cohorts and stratified analyses by variant type remain important for future work.

Second, population and cohort bias remain potential concerns. The training data derived from Gene4Denovo may reflect specific population compositions, disease spectra, and sequencing protocols, which could affect model calibration when applied to other populations. Future work will benefit from incorporating larger, multi-center and multi-ethnic datasets, as well as exploring domain adaptation or transfer learning strategies to improve cross-population robustness and fairness.

Third, the current model does not incorporate structural or dynamic biological information, such as protein 3D structure, tissue-specific expression, or interaction networks. These factors may provide additional mechanistic insights, particularly for variants affecting protein stability or regulatory interactions.Within this context, the limited contribution of PS2/PM6 features may reflect the current dataset composition and the relatively homogeneous de novo structure of the cohort, and may become more informative as larger and more diverse datasets become available.

Fourth, although DeNovoSeer provides interpretable probability outputs and SHAP-based evidence decomposition, its integration into clinical workflows remains to be further developed. In practice, model outputs should be interpreted in conjunction with ACMG guidelines rather than used as standalone diagnostic criteria. Furthermore, while SHAP analysis maps feature contributions to ACMG-related evidence categories, a systematic quantitative comparison between SHAP-derived evidence attribution and manual ACMG classification by clinical experts has not yet been conducted. Such validation—using expert-curated variants with consensus classifications—would directly substantiate the clinical traceability of model predictions and remains an important direction for future work. Future work will also focus on improving usability and facilitating broader accessibility in clinical and research settings through user-friendly interfaces such as web servers or APIs.

In summary, DeNovoSeer provides a unified framework for DNM pathogenicity prediction by integrating high-quality labels, multi-source features, semi-supervised learning, and interpretable outputs. We do not advocate replacing human interpretation with the model but rather enhancing the efficiency and consistency of clinical and research workflows through standardized, verifiable evidence outputs, providing an expandable technical path for the molecular diagnosis and mechanism study of genetic diseases.

Author Summary.

In this study, we developed a new artificial intelligence tool called DeNovoSeer to help determine whether de novo mutations are likely to cause disease. These mutations are often linked to conditions like autism and developmental disorders, but accurately predicting their impact has been challenging. Existing methods either overlook clinical evidence or are not designed specifically for these rare mutations. Our approach combines multiple types of biological and clinical information into a unified model that learns from both labeled and unlabeled data, making it especially useful when expert-curated examples are scarce. DeNovoSeer not only achieves high accuracy but also provides interpretable predictions through feature-level explanations, enabling results to be understood in a clinically meaningful context. Importantly, the model can generate predictions for all evaluated variants without missing outputs, improving its practical applicability in real-world analysis. By providing interpretable predictions, we aim to facilitate more informed interpretation of genetic variants in research and clinical contexts. This work supports the integration of computational prediction with clinically relevant interpretation, offering a more trustworthy and accessible tool for personalized medicine.
