Role of Systemic Inflammatory Markers in the Differential Diagnosis of Organizing Pneumonia and Lung Cancer
Authors: Hilal Erinanc, Yıldız Uçar, Keziban Ucar Karabulut, Aynur Yonar, Ayse Gülhan Kanat Unler, Sevsen Kulaksizoglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo investigate novel inflammatory indexes including neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), systemic immune inflammation index (SII), systemic immune response index (SIRI), and pan‐immune‐inflammation value (PIV) in the diagnosis of organizing pneumonia (OP) and distinguishing OP from lung cancer (LC). The study included 125 patients with LC and 21 patients with OP. Retrospectively, we compared the values of SII, SIRI, PIV, NLR, and C‐reactive protein (CRP) and evaluated correlations between the patient groups. The CRP levels in patients with OP (median: 45.2 mg/dL) were lower than those in patients with LC (median: 64 mg/dL). However, no differences were observed in systemic inflammatory indexes between the groups. Statistically significant positive correlations were found between neutrophils and NLR (r = 0.63), SIRI (r = 0.52), and SII (r = 0.72). In addition, significant negative correlations were observed between lymphocytes and NLR (r = −0.67), SIRI (r = −0.53), SII (r = −0.27), and PIV (r = −0.37). Increased CRP levels may indicate lung carcinoma risk and CRP measurements may be predictive in distinguishing between OP and LC. Additionally, high neutrophil counts, thrombocyte counts and/or lymphocytopenia might lead to a poor immune response and are linked to higher systemic inflammatory indices.

Introduction

Organizing pneumonia (OP) is a rare disease, with an annual incidence of 1.1 per 100,000 [1]. Cryptogenic pneumonia is the term used to refer to pneumonia for which no definite cause can be identified. Systemic disorders such as vasculitis, collagen vascular diseases, interstitial lung disease, and secondary causes such as drug toxicity, infections, and radiation treatment may also lead to OP [2].

Chronic lung inflammation resulting in long‐standing injury can damage the alveolar walls, bronchioles, segmental bronchi, and the interstitium. Lesions range from bronchiolitis obliterans with organizing pneumonia (BOOP) to severe interstitial fibrosis with remodeling of lung parenchyma. Histopathologically, it is characterized by the presence of connective tissue components, fibrin, and fibroblasts within the alveolar spaces [3].

Computed tomography (CT) findings of OP may be similar to those of lung cancer (LC), which are variable such as nodules or multiple masses, cavitary lesions, bronchocentric distribution, inverted halo pattern, band‐like opacities, pneumatoceles, and pleural involvement [4]. This resemblance can lead to missed diagnoses, misdiagnoses [5, 6], or delays in treatment, particularly in early‐stage LC such as bronchioloalveolar carcinoma (BAC) [7]. Furthermore, OP foci may coexist with LC and have been reported in cases of lung adenocarcinoma [8], endobronchial squamous cell carcinoma [9], and bronchogenic carcinoma [10].

In terms of diagnosis, biopsy is considered the gold standard; however, due to similarities in radiologic and clinical features of OP and LC–such as fever, cough, sputum, hemoptysis, weight loss, anorexia‐, additional tests which are simple, cost‐effective, and rapidly accessible may be necessary to make an accurate diagnosis. The systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), the systemic immune‐response index (SIRI), pan inflammatory value (PIV), and neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio (NLR) are novel indicators that reflect the systemic immune and inflammatory status of the body. They are based on the peripheral blood cell counts (lymphocytes, neutrophils, platelets, and monocytes).

Recent studies have demonstrated that the NLR, SII, SIRI, and PIV have a potential role in assessing the risk and prognosis of diseases. The association between the systemic inflammatory markers and the prognosis of various malignancies has also been evaluated in many studies [11, 12, 13, 14].

In this study, we aimed to investigate whether NLR, SII, SIRI, PIV, and C‐reactive protein (CRP) can serve as novel diagnostic markers in differentiating OP from LC.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was a single‐center retrospective study and this study was approved by Baskent University Institutional Review Board (Project No: KA25/23).

The pathology reports of all patients who underwent lung biopsy at Baskent University Konya Hospital from January 2016 to October 2022 were retrospectively reviewed. Slides were re‐evaluated and OP diagnoses were approved histopathologically. The presence of fibrin exudates and neutrophils in the infiltrates and intraluminal granulation tissue in the alveolar ducts, alveoli, and bronchiolar lumen was the histologic criterion that supported OP (Figure 1).

Fibrin and fibroblast plug composed of spindle cell proliferation in the alveolar spaces HEX40.

A total of 21 patients with a diagnosis of OP were identified histologically. During the same period, 125 patients who underwent lung biopsy and were diagnosed as having LC were included in the study. The distribution of LC types was as follows: adenocarcinoma (41.6%, n = 52), squamous cell carcinoma (32%, n = 40), small cell carcinoma (20.8%, n = 26), non‐small cell carcinoma (3.2%, n = 4), large cell carcinoma (1.6%, n = 2), and malignant mesothelioma (0.8%, n = 1), respectively. All included cases were histopathologically confirmed based on lung biopsy specimens.

The clinical, laboratory, and radiologic findings of the patients were obtained from the patients' medical records. The recent routine hemogram analyzes and biochemical parameters of the patients at the time of first admission were used in the study. The counts of neutrophils (K/μL), lymphocytes (K/μL), thrombocytes (K/μL), eosinophils (K/μL), and mean platelet volume (MPV) (fL) values were obtained from the hemogram analyzes. Neutrophil to lymphocyte ratios (NLR) were calculated. CRP (mg/dL) levels were obtained from the electronic archive of the Hospital Information System. The following formulas were used in the index calculations: SII: (neutrophil count × platelet count)/lymphocyte count; SIRI: (neutrophil count × monocyte count)/lymphocyte count; PIV: (neutrophil count × platelet count × monocyte count)/lymphocyte count.

In addition, the CT findings of the patients were also recorded from the electronic archive of the Hospital Information System. As the majority of LC patients were referred to our hospital from external centers for diagnostic biopsy, comprehensive radiological reports were not available. Bronchioloalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid tests, culture findings, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) results were also examined. Mycobacterium tuberculosis , fungus, and other aerobic organisms were not detected in sputum stains or cultures.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyzes were conducted using the IBM SPSS Statistics 22 and R 4.1.3 software packages (https://www.r‐project.org/). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests were used to test the normality assumption and Levene's test was used to check the homogeneity assumption of the groups. The median, standard error, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and interquartile range (IQR) of the variables are given. The independent two‐sample t‐test and Mann–Whitney U test were performed to reveal the differences between the groups according to the variables and these differences were examined using boxplots. Moreover, correlations between the variables were analyzed using Spearman's correlation analysis. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The present study involved 21 patients with OP (47.6% male, n = 10 and 52.4% female, n = 11) and 125 patients with LC (83.2% male, n = 104 and 16.8% female, n = 21), who underwent lung biopsy at Baskent University Konya Medical Hospital between 2016 and 2022. The male ratio was significantly higher in the LC group (83.2%), while the genders were approximately equal in the OP group (p = 0.0018). The mean ages of the patients with LC and OP were 65 and 69 years, respectively. The demographic and laboratory finding of the patients for each group are given in Table 1 and Figure 2. The median CRP level in the OP group was lower than that in the LC group (45.2 vs. 64 mg/dL, respectively).

No significant differences were found between the groups for the systemic inflammatory marker levels: NLR (p = 0.662), SIRI (p = 0.715), SII (p = 0.411), and PIV (p = 0.131). Hemoglobin (Hb) levels were similar between the two groups. In this study, Hb levels ≤ 12 g/dL were defined as anemic, and levels ≥ 12 g/dL were considered normal.

Leukocytosis (white blood cell [WBC] count > 10,000/mL) was not observed in either group. The mean WBC count was 8.36 × 103/mL in the OP group and 8.79 × 103/mL in the LC group.

SIRI showed positive correlations with WBC (0.57) and NLR (0.69), and SII was positively correlated with PIV (0.55).

Radiologic findings indicated that peripheral consolidation and ground‐glass opacity were the most common patterns in patients with OP (52.4%, n = 11), followed by nodules and mass lesions (33.3%, n = 7). Other radiologic manifestations included cavitation (4.8%, n = 1), abscess (4.8%, n = 1), and peripheral irregular subpleural bands (4.8%, n = 1).

In the LC group, the most common radiologic finding was nodules or mass lesions with irregular margins, observed in 35.71% of cases (n = 25). This was followed by peribronchial masses with obliteration in 18.58% (n = 13) and cavitating nodules in 14.28% of cases (n = 10). Other findings included ground‐glass opacities (11.43%, n = 8), pleural involvement (11.43%, n = 8), atelectasis (7.14%, n = 5), and bronchiectasis (1.43%, n = 1). No cases of abscess were reported in this group.

Discussion

In this study, we assessed the potential diagnostic utility of certain hematological indices (NLR, SII, SIRI, and PIV) in distinguishing between OP and LC. Although a few studies have investigated the relationship between these systemic inflammatory markers and LC, to our knowledge, no studies have investigated their diagnostic value in differentiating OP from LC.

Numerous previous investigations have demonstrated that neutrophils, platelets, and lymphocytes in peripheral blood play a critical role in initiating systemic immuno‐inflammatory responses by secreting various enzymes, chemokines, cytokines, and growth factors.

In lung tissue, the systemic inflammatory‐induced pulmonary dysfunction is primarily driven by leukocyte activation and the subsequent release of pro‐inflammatory mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α). During the inflammatory processes, platelets are also activated, and bind to leukocytes, forming platelet–leukocyte complexes that contribute to tissue damage.

Elevated WBC counts (leucocyte count: neutrophil and lymphocyte count) in plasma have been negatively correlated with pulmonary function in patients with asthma [15]. Additionally, tumor‐associated leukocytosis was reported in nearly all cases of non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [16] and has been found to be related to a poorer prognosis [17]. Leukocytosis (leucocyte count > 10,000/mL) has been reported in approximately 50% of OP cases [18] and 15% of LC cases [19].

Our findings demonstrated that WBC count exhibited the strongest correlation with neutrophil count (89%), and a statistically significant positive correlation was observed between neutrophil and platelet levels. This might be evidence that the aforementioned mechanism of lung damage is mediated by neutrophil and platelet activation.

Beside correlations between neutrophils and thrombocytes, statistically significant positive correlations were also present between neutrophils and, SII (72%), NLR (63%), and SIRI (52%). Conversely, lymphocyte counts were negatively correlated with NLR, SII, SIRI, and PIV.

Based on our results, we thought that high neutrophil counts and thrombocyte counts and/or lymphocytopenia might lead to a poor immune response and an increased SII index could indicate this situation more than others.

In previous reports, NLR was one of the most extensively studied biomarkers for assessing prognosis and risk in lung diseases. It has been suggested as a prognostic indicator in bronchiectasis [20]. A positive association between elevated NLR levels and disease severity has been identified in patients with COVID‐19, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and community‐acquired pneumonia [21, 22, 23, 24]. In lung cancer (LC), high NLR levels were also linked to lower overall survival rates [25].

Similarly, SII has been associated with an increased risk of hospitalized exacerbations in bronchiectasis [26]. In COPD patients, higher SII levels were significantly related to increased risks of respiratory failure and mortality [27]. Li et al. reported that SII could effectively predict the response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy in patients with non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [28].

When comparing the prognostic value of SII and NLR, the superiority of SII over NLR was demonstrated in LC [29]. In addition, Cheng et al. found that SIRI had a better predictive value for stroke prevalence than SII in asthma patients [30]. A newer, inflammatory biomarker, PIV that includes all peripheral blood cells (neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets, and monocytes), has been suggested to outperform other inflammatory indices in risk stratification [31].

In many studies, different optimal cut‐off values for systemic inflammatory indices have been reported in predicting complications in lung diseases. These differences may be due to variations in sample size, study design, disease duration, or characteristics of the patient population. For instance, Ruta et al. reported that SII values over 500 might be associated with interstitial lung involvement in patients with connective tissue diseases [32].

Ke et al. reported that SII (1.988), SIRI (3.208), and NLR (2.622), were associated with an increased risk of mortality, while Cheng et al. found that higher SII (cut‐off 609) and SIRI (cut‐off 1.12) levels were strongly associated with increased risk of stroke in the asthmatic population [30, 33]. Another study determined that higher SII (≥ 580.67), SIRI (≥ 0.705), and NLR (≥ 2.606) indicated poor prognosis in the early stages of LC [34]. In healthy individuals, normal NLR levels have been reported to range between 0.78 and 3.53 [35]. Yu et al. used an NLR cut‐off of 4 in cases of small‐cell lung cancer, while other studies adopted a cut‐off of 5 for identifying infection or sepsis [25, 36].

In our study, the values of NLR, SII, and SIRI were 3.85, 1049.73, and 2.462 in patients with OP, and 3.66, 880.4, and 2.309 in patients with LC, respectively. Our results demonstrated that an increase in inflammatory markers occurred in both OP and LC patient groups; however, this increase was not statistically significant between the two groups. Despite the absence of intergroup differences, we observed statistically significant positive correlations between SII, SIRI, and NLR. We thought that elevated levels of systemic inflammatory markers may indicate the presence of chronic damage and inflammation in both OP and LC. Autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, or chronic infections (e.g., tuberculosis, COPD) may independently elevate NLR, SII, and SIRI [37]. Therefore, clinicians should consider the possibility of underlying systemic inflammatory conditions when interpreting elevated values of systemic inflammatory indices. Furthermore, these increases in NLR, SII, and SIRI may also have prognostic implications, suggested in previous studies [25, 26, 27, 28, 30, 33, 34]. Nevertheless, since only a single blood sample from each patient was analyzed, our results may not fully reflect the prognostic significance of these markers. Therefore, monitoring of NLR, SII, SIRI, and PIV during disease progression may provide more comprehensive insights into systemic inflammatory and immune responses, as well as potential diagnostic benefits.

In the present study, although CRP levels showed a statistically significant difference (p = 0.033), they did not remain significant after Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment (adjusted p = 0.429). However, the median CRP levels were higher in patients with LC compared to those with OP (64 vs. 45.2 mg/dL), which may still be clinically informative in the differential diagnosis. In literature, authors demonstrated that serum CRP levels higher than 40 mg/L were associated with metastatic disease in LC [38]. In that study, increased CRP values were associated with disease severity. CRP is an acute‐phase reactant and a systemic marker of chronic inflammation. Significant tissue injury, such as trauma or malignant tumors (without concomitant bacterial or viral infections) causes increased CRP levels. The complement system, apoptosis, phagocytosis, nitric oxide release, and the synthesis of cytokines, including interleukin‐6 and TNF‐α, play a significant role in host responses to infection and increased CRP values.

Based on our results, we thought that the higher CRP value in the LC group compared with the OP group could be a guide for physicians in the differential diagnosis, especially in patients with diagnostic difficulties. The present study also showed that there was a positive correlation between CRP and NLR; however this relationship was not statistically significant. Authors emphasized that an increase in NLR was closely related to an increased risk of LC, and this correlation was more pronounced within 1 year before the diagnosis of LC [39, 40]. On the basis of this literature, we hypothesized that evaluating increased CRP and NLR levels together may be helpful in diagnosis.

Our study has limitations. First, the study was designed as single‐centered and retrospective and a limited number of patients were included. There was a marked imbalance in the sample sizes between the OP and LC groups (21 vs. 125 patients, respectively). Although appropriate statistical methods were employed to account for unequal group sizes, the small sample size of the OP group may have limited the statistical power to detect significant differences. Second, systemic inflammatory indices were calculated by using initial measurements of hemogram values because of the retrospective nature of the study. Changes in the concentrations of peripheral blood cells during the disease progression were not included; therefore, the effect on prognostication was not investigated. Future prospective and more balanced patient cohort studies focusing on the dynamic changes of systemic inflammatory markers during disease progression are needed to validate these results. Finally, the primary reasons for OP were not known exactly; individuals with any systemic inflammatory diseases such as rheumatic and autoimmune diseases, which may independently elevate the laboratory parameters, were not excluded.

Third, individuals with systemic inflammatory diseases such as rheumatic and autoimmune disorders—which are known to independently alter systemic inflammatory markers—were not excluded. This represents a significant limitation, as such conditions may confound the levels of biomarkers like NLR, SII, and CRP. Future studies should more rigorously control for these potential confounders to isolate the inflammatory response specific to OP and LC.

Conclusion

Based on our results, although the CRP value was not statistically significant after multiple comparison correction, it showed a trend toward being higher in the LC group. This finding suggests that increased CRP concentrations may still be clinically relevant in differentiating between OP and LC. Physicians should be aware of the potential associations between elevated CRP levels and cancer risk. Understanding the correlations between systemic inflammatory biomarkers may aid in evaluating disease risk and prognosis; however, more comprehensive prospective studies with larger patient populations are needed to confirm these associations.
