Nanomaterial-driven spatiotemporal autophagy modulation: The dual-edged sword in precision cancer therapy
Authors: Linghuan Xu, Jintao Hao, Yueyang Zhao, Yanshu Wang, Yuetong Yu, Chaoxing He, Zhiyun Niu, Defang Ouyang, Bai Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autophagy-modulating nanomaterials have emerged as a promising therapeutic paradigm in oncology, offering precise intervention in tumor progression, metastasis, and treatment resistance. The therapeutic efficacy of these platforms is largely attributed to their context-dependent dual functionality. On one hand, they are capable of inducing cytotoxic autophagy, such as through the activation of oxidative stress or endoplasmic reticulum stress, leading to tumor cell death. On the other hand, they can inhibit cytoprotective autophagy, a crucial survival mechanism exploited by tumor cells, by impairing lysosomal function or perturbing cellular energy metabolism. A key advancement in this field is the ability of nanomaterials to achieve spatiotemporal control over autophagy modulation, allowing targeted regulation at specific tumor sites and disease stages. This review systematically summarizes the molecular mechanisms underlying these bidirectional effects, with particular emphasis on the modulation of selective autophagy pathways including mitophagy, and discusses how rational nanomaterial design adapted to tumor stage and microenvironment dictates therapeutic outcomes. Finally, we highlight major translational challenges, especially the lack of standardized characterization and the difficulty in precisely controlling autophagic responses, which must be addressed to facilitate the clinical translation of these innovative nanotherapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of death and a major threat and burden to global health1,2. While conventional tri-modality therapies comprising surgical resection, cytotoxic chemotherapy, and ionizing radiation continue to form the cornerstone of oncologic practice3,4, their clinical efficacy is constrained by inherent limitations. These include non-selective cytotoxicity leading to systemic toxicity, radioresistance in hypoxic tumor microenvironments, and the emergence of multidrug-resistant cancer stem cell populations4,5. The development of molecular biotechnology has made immunotherapy a viable and accessible option, emerging as a promising method for future cancer treatment6. Recent research on immunotherapy has focused on new targets to strengthen therapeutic outcomes7, mechanism of immunotherapy8, and new compounds9. The clinical efficiency of immunotherapy has been demonstrated in various studies10,11. However, current immunotherapeutic regimens demonstrate heterogeneity in clinical responses across patient populations. Factors such as primary resistance mechanisms, immune-related adverse events (irAEs), and the dynamic immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment have significantly hampered translation to clinical practice12. Therefore, the challenges we face today in cancer treatment call for new, effective therapeutic solutions.

In recent years, biomedical research has been developing at an unparalleled speed, providing new alternatives for cancer therapy. Among the available strategies, macroautophagy/autophagy has emerged as a critical target in cancer therapy (Fig. 1). It is a conserved cellular mechanism responsible for delivering cytoplasmic constituents—originating both intracellularly and extracellularly—to the lysosome for degradation and subsequent recycling. This process facilitates the re-utilization of vital cellular components, thereby supporting cell survival under conditions of stress or nutrient deprivation and maintaining cellular homeostasis. Regulated by approximately 16–20 core autophagy-related genes (ATGs), autophagy proceeds through a series of coordinated steps, including initiation, membrane elongation, fusion with lysosomes, and degradation13 (Fig. 2). Recent studies have further revealed that the autophagy-initiating Atg1 complex can undergo phase separation to form preautophagosomal structures, which serve as dynamic sites facilitating Atg8 lipidation and driving autophagosome biogenesis14. It plays an important role not only in responding to cellular stress, energy deprivation, and pathogen invasion but also in maintaining cellular metabolic balance during normal cellular processes15. In cancer therapy, autophagy plays a paradoxical role, with both cytotoxic and cytoprotective functions. Through the precise modulation of autophagy, either the drug resistance and survival of tumor cells can be promoted, or therapeutic efficacy can be enhanced16. Nanotechnology, which involves the rational design of materials at the atomic or molecular level within the 1 to 100 nm range17, offers great advantages in precision drug delivery and advanced diagnostic imaging, and is increasingly being recognized for its potential in cancer treatment18. Nanomaterials possess distinctive physicochemical characteristics, such as their diminutive scales, specific surface area, and programmable biointerfacial interactions with cells through nonspecific or specific pathways19,20. These properties can be designed for tumor-selective drug accumulation in cancer therapy. By improving the bioavailability and stability of anticancer drugs or modulators, the targeted delivery ability of nanomaterials can strengthen the therapeutic efficacy21, and reduce the off-target effects and systemic toxicity22. Notably, nanomaterials can be engineered for on-demand drug release when exposed to specific stimuli within the tumor environment, such as cancer related-miRNA23, laser and acid24, reactive oxygen species (ROS)25, thereby improving therapeutic precision. Additionally, nanotherapeutics targeting autophagy often use nanomaterials as delivery vehicles, with some possessing intrinsic catalytic properties. For instance, silver nanoparticles26 and quantum dots27 can act as autophagy inducers, while gold nanoparticles28 and iron oxide nanoparticles29 can function as autophagy inhibitors. Consequently, the integration of bio-nanotechnology with autophagy-focused processes presents unique advantages in cancer treatment30.

Nanoparticles provide a unique and rational framework for autophagy regulation owing to the intrinsic complexity and context-dependent nature of autophagic responses in cancer. Autophagy is not a linear or binary process but a highly dynamic and spatially organized pathway that is tightly coupled to intracellular organelles, redox homeostasis, metabolic status, and stress-adaptive signaling19. Nanoparticles, by virtue of their tunable size, surface chemistry, and intracellular trafficking behaviors, are well suited to interface with these multilevel regulatory nodes simultaneously31. In particular, nanoparticles can act as integrative modulators that spatially confine autophagy regulation to specific subcellular compartments, synchronize autophagic modulation with tumor-associated stress signals, and coordinate autophagy with other cell-death or survival pathways31,32. Such capabilities are difficult to achieve with conventional small-molecule modulators, which often suffer from systemic exposure and lack spatiotemporal control33. Recent advances in autophagy-targeting nanomedicine (ApT-NM) indicate that nanoparticles are not only carriers of autophagy modulators, but can also precisely bias autophagic outcomes toward either inhibition of cytoprotective autophagy to overcome therapy resistance or promotion of autophagy to trigger type II programmed cell death34. Moreover, multifunctional nanoplatforms integrating autophagy modulation with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or phototherapy have emerged as rational strategies to spatially and temporally coordinate autophagy regulation with tumor stress responses, amplifying therapeutic efficacy as well as limiting off-target effects34. Therefore, the use of nanoparticles is not merely a delivery strategy, but a rational approach to harness and reprogram autophagy in a context-aware manner for improved cancer therapeutic outcomes.

Bio-nanotechnology targeting autophagy for cancer treatment has received unprecedented attention with several notable achievements. Existing reviews have illustrated the mechanisms and the therapeutic effects of different nanomaterials that play important roles in tumor treatment by regulating autophagy35, 36, 37. Nevertheless, the association among nanomaterials, autophagy and tumors remain highly intricate. Nanoparticles can regulate autophagy in tumors either positively or negatively, and the effects of autophagy depend on the cancer type and stage. Previous reviews only focused on the singular effect (inhibition or promotion) that nanomaterials have on autophagy, neglecting the dualistic effects in regulating autophagy for cancer treatment. Building upon these insights and based on the latest advances in autophagy-mediated cancer treatment using nanomaterials, this article reviews the mechanisms and systematic impacts of both promoting and inhibiting autophagy by nanomaterials in cancer cells, hoping to help researchers have a complete view of the complicated and delicate regulation of nanotherapeutics targeting autophagy in tumors.

Molecular machinery and context-dependent biology of autophagy

Core molecular machinery of autophagy

Molecular machinery of autophagy

Driven by ATGs, autophagy is a highly conserved cellular recycling process that involves the formation of double-membrane vesicles called autophagosomes to degrade and recycle unwanted materials to maintain homeostasis within cells38. The process of autophagy can typically be divided into four stages, with each stage relying on the specific ATGs network and the modulation of molecules such as AMPK and mTOR39. The initiation stage is driven by Unc-51-like kinase 1 (ULK1) complex (ULK1–ATG13–FIP200–ATG101)40. This stage decides whether autophagy will be initiated. The second stage is nucleation. The class III PI3K complex (VPS34–Beclin1–ATG14L/PI3KC3–C1) is brought to the phagophore through extensive contacts between FIP200 and VPS15/ATG14/BECN1 after the ULK1 complex is activated, leading to the rearrangement of ULK1C stoichiometry from 2:1:1 to 2:2:241. Membrane recruitment stimulates VPS34 kinase activity, in part via myristate release from the VPS15 pseudokinase domain, ultimately leading to increased PI3P generation42. The PI3P produced at this site subsequently engages downstream effectors, such as WIPI2, either directly or through the WIPI–ATG13 complex43. This step lays the structural groundwork for autophagic membrane formation and is central to the canonical autophagy pathway44,45.

The third stage is elongation and maturation. The expansion of the isolation membrane and the formation of autophagosomes rely on two ubiquitin-like conjugation cascades: the ATG12–ATG5–ATG16L1 complex and the LC3 (ATG8) lipidation system46. Within this machinery, ATG7 and ATG3 catalyze the conversion of LC3-I to the membrane-bound LC3-II, a modification essential for membrane extension, cargo selection, and the execution of selective autophagy14. This phase constitutes the most ATG-intensive step of autophagosome biogenesis, where the coordinated actions of these proteins are most critical. The fourth stage is fusion and degradation. Mature autophagosomes merge with lysosomes through the coordinated action of SNARE proteins (STX17, SNAP29, and VAMP8) and Rab7 GTPases, giving rise to autolysosomes47. Within these compartments, lysosomal hydrolases break down the sequestered organelles and proteins48, and the resulting metabolites, such as amino acids and fatty acids, are recycled to support cellular homeostasis49, 50, 51.

Within the autophagy regulatory network, mTOR and AMPK function as central integrators of nutrient, energy, and stress signals. Generally, mTORC1 acts as a negative regulator under nutrient-replete conditions, where it inhibits autophagy initiation by phosphorylating ULK1 at Ser75752 and by amplifying the nuclear translocation of TFEB, thereby strengthening both autophagosome formation and lysosome-related gene transcription53. Importantly, mTORC1 also participates in feedback regulation by coordinating mitochondrial biogenesis and metabolic reprogramming, which in turn reshapes cellular energy status and secondarily influences autophagy dependency, highlighting that mTORC1-autophagy regulation is embedded within a self-adjusting metabolic loop rather than a unidirectional cascade54. In contrast, the role of AMPK is context dependent and characterized by bidirectional regulation. Under acute glucose deprivation, AMPK can transiently inhibit autophagy initiation by modulating ULK1 phosphorylation at distinct sites and dampening the ULK1–Atg14–Vps34 signaling cascade, which is a response to prevent excessive energy expenditure55. This transient inhibition represents a compensatory mechanism through which AMPK prioritizes metabolic homeostasis over catabolic flux, underscoring its dual role as both an autophagy modulator and a metabolic safeguard55. Concurrently, AMPK preserves the integrity of the ULK1 complex by protecting it from caspase-mediated degradation, maintaining the capacity for autophagy reactivation when metabolic stress is alleviated55. Such preservation enables rapid autophagy rebound once energetic conditions improve, forming a reversible feedback control over autophagy amplitude. During prolonged amino acid deprivation, AMPK deficiency impairs mTORC1 reactivation and elevates autophagy56. By contrast, pharmacological AMPK activation or glucose deprivation paradoxically attenuates autophagy induced by amino acid starvation, suggesting the non-linear nature of AMPK signaling55,56. These seemingly paradoxical outcomes reflect the coexistence of feedforward and feedback circuits within the AMPK–mTOR axis, rather than conflicting experimental observations. Beyond direct ULK1–mTOR interactions, AMPK influences autophagy through various intersecting pathways, including integrin αVβ5/AMPK/mTOR57 and AMPK–FOXO3a–SPK2–CARM1 axis58, with dynamic regulation of TFEB59 serving as a common convergence point. Besides, TFEB-mediated lysosomal and mitochondrial transcriptional programs provide an additional layer of feedback regulation, coupling autophagic capacity with organelle biogenesis and stress adaptation60. Moreover, the impact of the AMPK–mTOR axis has shown tissue specificity and therapeutic relevance, indicating the potential as well as the contextual risks of targeting this axis in disease intervention61,62.

Pathways shared by nanomaterials

Accumulating evidence indicates that diverse nanomaterials regulate autophagy through a set of convergent molecular pathways rather than entirely distinct mechanisms. Despite differences in composition, size, and surface chemistry, many nanomaterials commonly modulate canonical autophagy-related signaling axes, including the mTOR pathway63, immunology-related pathways, mitochondria-related pathways64,65, and the activation of selective autophagy mediated by ATGs66,31. These shared routes reflect the central regulatory nodes governing autophagy and highlight that nanomaterials often converge on similar upstream triggers while exerting context-dependent effects on downstream autophagic outcomes.

Nanomaterials can regulate autophagy largely by suppressing the mTOR pathway, with the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway being the most frequently targeted. In most cases, the drugs carried by nanoparticles function as autophagy modulators. Glutathione-sensitive nanoparticles (PSMA-NP/BEZ) were developed to carry BEZ235, and BEZ235 inhibits the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway to promote autophagy, thereby enhancing immunotherapy efficiency67. In glioblastoma (GBM), a scorpion venom-derived decapeptide Ctir9495 (C9) was conjugated on a polyamidoamine (PAMAM) dendrimer (the nanoparticle was named as G5C9) used to disturb mTORC1 activity and suppress the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, and blocked GBM cell growth by boosting autophagosome lysosome-based apoptotic GBM cell death68. A self-assembled nanodrug (PLC-NP) enables precise delivery of celastrol to tumor sites through its conjugation with the bioactive material LMWH and a P-selection-targeting peptide. Once internalized, celastrol suppresses the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and induces ROS-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby activating autophagy to inhibit both primary and metastatic tumors69. Additionally, various upstream axes can also regulate mTOR, and in some contexts, this regulation enhances autophagy, underscoring the mechanistic diversity through which nanoparticles modulate autophagy. The accumulation of WSGC peptide-loaded porous MS (WSGC@MS) in gastric adenocarcinoma can stimulate autophagy mediated by the ROS/AMPK/mTOR pathway, preventing tumor cells from multiplying and metastasising70. The phosphorylation of mTOR is blocked and ULK1 is activated by Mo-POMs to promote mTOR-dependent autophagy, thereby achieving antitumor effects71.

Various nanoparticles target immune-related pathways to modulate autophagy. Inflammatory factors are considered excellent targets. The novel polymer-lipid manganese dioxide nanoparticles (PLMDs) induce autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress in castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) cells, which promotes the secretion of damage-associated molecular patterns, remodels the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and enhancing therapeutic efficacy72. By inhibiting autophagy in cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF), more IL6 (interleukin 6) is released, ultimately improving immunochemotherapy effects. Chloroquine diphosphate-loaded mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-liposomes can target CAF accurately in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and chloroquine was used to initiate the above process73. In some cases74,75, inflammatory factors and autophagy are both regulated to achieve the best outcomes. Interestingly, upregulating or downregulating MHC-I caused by autophagy can both be used to treat cancer. High expression of MHC-I on cancer cells increases immune recognition, and the process is triggered by the MOF nanozymes in iron–copper metal-organic frameworks (Fe-Cu MOFs), which selectively inhibit autophagy76. Conversely, to prevent cancer immune evasion, MHC-I on tumor cells is reduced by the autophagic inhibition induced by a therapeutic tumor vaccine utilizing cationic liposomes for the co-encapsulation of MC38 total RNA and PDL1 siRNA (siPDL1)77. Furthermore, in recent years, PDL1 has emerged as a promising regulatory target78,79. Some nanomaterials facilitate immunogenic cell death (ICD)80,81, and the connection between autophagy and ICD will be discussed in other parts.

Dual roles of autophagy in cancer

The dual role of autophagy has been a focus on treating cancer82. The effects of autophagy on tumors not only relate to certain mechanisms mentioned below, but also have connections with the stage of the tumor. Promotion of tumor by autophagy

Autophagy can boost tumor growth through diverse pathways and functions as an adaptive response for tumor survival84,85. Autophagy is critical for cancer development by creating the tumor microenvironment (TME) supporting its growth86,87, elevating the immune escape of tumor cells88, maintaining the metabolic demands and stress resistance ability of cancer stem cells (CSCs)89 and strengthening tumor drug resistance90. Thus, autophagy is essential in cancer development91.

Firstly, autophagy contributes to anti-tumor immunity92. In PDAC, autophagy engages in the upregulation of major histocompatibility complex class 1 (MHC-1)93, which is beneficial to the functional response of CD8+ T lymphocytes. A separate investigation suggested that protein kinase 2 (RIPK2), inherent in cancer cells, disturbs autophagy to downregulate the MHC-1 level on the cell membrane, helping PDAC cells avoid the killing effect from cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs)94. Besides, autophagy works as a bridge for molecules like Interferon-α to upregulate PDL1 (an immune checkpoint), and eventually interfere with CD8+ T cells’ function of eliminating tumor cells95. Therefore, the impacts of autophagy on cancer cell immunity can be very complicated.

CSCs, present in small numbers inside the tumor, are pivotal in driving its growth, functioning similarly to normal stem cells that maintain tissue regeneration in other healthy organs96. When autolysosome formation is induced by a certain gene, autophagy is promoted, inducing head and neck squamous cell carcinoma to keep its stemness, making it easier to grow97. Hypoxia leads to the promotion of autophagy in glioma stem cells, supporting the self-regeneration of glioblastoma98. In leukemic stem cells (LSCs) of the acute myeloid leukemia, autophagy maintains the cancer cells’ viability and self-renewal ability and is significantly involved in both cancer development and resistance to therapy99.

Furthermore, autophagy, especially autophagy dysregulation, strengthens drug resistance in neoplasms100. When cancers enter the late stage, autophagy can sustain survival under stress conditions through mechanisms like eliminating impaired proteins, preserving mitochondrial function, and supporting tumor metabolism101. In addition, the tumor metastasis process is facilitated by autophagy, enhancing the aggressiveness of tumors, and in some cases, the inhibition of autophagy will limit tumors’ metastasis ability102.

Inhibition of tumor by autophagy

Autophagy strengthens immune surveillance through varied ways. Firstly, it helps the antigen presentation process, especially by regulating the MHC class II pathway. Autophagy promotes the modification of arginine into citrulline and the citrullination of glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78), leading to major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC II) structural changes, and promoting the activation of CD4+ T cells103. Autophagy not only alters the composition of MHC II, but also increases the expression of MHC II on cellular membranes to promote antigen presentation104. Secondly, autophagy induces ICD via ways such as producing proinflammatory cytokines to stimulate antigen-presenting cell (APC) maturation105. In summary, autophagy exerts multifaceted influences on anti-tumor immunity, and its impact is shaped by the microenvironment.

Additionally, autophagy regulates cell metabolism and suppresses tumor growth. Keeping genomic stability is essential for cells, and DNA damage is common in a variety of cancers106. Autophagy is capable of degrading and recycling damaged organelles and intracellular components, and during the repair of DNA damage, proteins involved in autophagy, such as Beclin1 and FIP200 also play significant roles107. Moreover, autophagy inhibits tumor development by reducing the levels of oncogenic mediators like the ACR protein P62, whose existence is frequently upregulated in various cancer types91.

In short, autophagy exhibits a context-dependent dual function in cancer, which can be employed for therapeutic purposes. In different situations and environments, autophagy will trigger or be induced by different molecular pathways and other cells, promoting or inhibiting cancer progression. Although the interaction between cancer and autophagy remains complex and nuanced, it provides potential opportunities for cancer therapy.

Modulation of autophagy across different tumor stages

Autophagy plays different roles in different tumor stages. In the initiation stages of tumor, autophagy mainly inhibits tumor growth, while in advanced cancer, autophagy promotes tumor growth101,108. Autophagy restrains tumor initiation by cooperating with DNA damage response to alleviate early replication stress and DNA damage, suggesting an antagonistic pleiotropy entanglement in premalignant stages109. The constant activation of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in hepatocytes partly leads to hepatocellular carcinoma in HBV-transgenic mice, and hyaluronan-mediated motility receptor (HMMR) could foster autophagy to lessen endoplasmic reticulum stress (ER stress), which attenuated carcinogenesis110. In gastric cancer, during H. pylori infection, a functional ATG16L1 polymorphism (rs2241880 G-allele) causes autophagy impairment, triggering unresolved ER stress and dysregulated inflammation, and ultimately propelling the progression from gastric premalignant lesions to cancer111. Conversely, in solid tumors that have already formed, autophagy facilitates tumor progression, recurrence, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Details on how autophagy promotes drug resistance have been discussed in “2.2.1 Promotion of tumor by autophagy”. The overexpression of voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1) in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) boosted autophagy mediated by mTOR, and stimulated the metastasis and radioresistance of NPC112. CD147 was shown to drive non-canonical autophagy by facilitating autophagosome and amphisome biogenesis while blocking autolysosome maturation, thus promoting crinophagy-dependent exosome secretion, accelerating metastasis and progression of non-small-cell lung cancer113. Influenced by exosomes as well, dormant salivary gland adenoid cystic carcinoma cells were found to have elevated ER stress-related autophagy and lipophagy levels after interacting with fibroblast-derived extracellular vesicles carrying PLIN2, and autophagy and lipophagy supply metabolic energy for tumor reactivation, proliferation, and eventual recurrence and metastasis114. In contrast, inhibition of autophagy can restrain tumor progression. Genetic ablation of the autophagy initiator ULK1 in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma impaired autophagy, delayed tumor growth, and remodeled the immune microenvironment toward antitumor immunity, underscoring autophagy as a critical driver of tumor progression at advanced stages115.

Hence, the modulation of autophagy in cancer depends on tumor stage markedly, highlighting the temporal regulatory role of autophagy in cancer therapy.

Modulation of autophagy across different tumor types

The types of tumors determine the regulation of autophagy. Therefore, the role of autophagy in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), lung cancer, and breast cancer is discussed as follows to fully understand its dual role in cancer.

In HCC, autophagy acts as a double-edged sword, which means promoting or inhibiting autophagy in different situations can both attain therapeutic effects. Mediated by ROS, the overexpression of glutaryl-CoA dehydrogenase (GCDH) stimulated autophagy and DNA repair dysfunction to limit HCC progression116. In vivo, the promotion of mitophagy by EGR1 mRNA also exhibited impaired xenograft tumor growth117. Interestingly, autophagy facilitation can harm tumor treatment in some cases, and this type of autophagy should be hindered. The protective autophagy in HCC can be induced by Annexin A2 (ANXA2) deacetylation through an mTOR-related pathway, leading to HCC progression and chemotherapy resistance to donafenib118. Tumor cell extravasation and metastasis have been proved to be enhanced after autophagy promotion via the upregulation of the assembly of the STX17–SNAP29–VAMP8 complex119. Hence, autophagy inhibition—here referring to a global reduction in autophagic activity without specification of the affected mechanistic step—has emerged as a therapeutic strategy in HCC. An arsenic trioxide (ATO) nanomedicine delivery system delivers a BECN1-targeting DNA enzyme to inhibit autophagy, and redirects ATO-induced cell death from apoptosis to caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis, so that both direct tumor killing and antitumor immunity are enhanced120. However, conversely, the existence of mixed lineage kinase domain-like (MLKL) impaired autophagy and drove hepatocarcinogenesis, indicating that autophagy plays different roles in tumorigenesis and development121.

Lung cancer is broadly subdivided into non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC), and the two exhibit distinct autophagy-dependent responses. Generally, limiting autophagy has been reported to inhibit lung cancer progression122, as well as reduced drug resistance to docetaxel123 and doxycycline124, while stimulating autophagy has also been shown to hinder lung cancer progression after 6-h time-restricted feeding in mice125. In NSCLC, inhibiting autophagy usually obtains the goal of tumor suppression, but facilitating autophagy is still a strategy in some cases. Cooperating with other cell death pathways like ferroptosis, the autophagy triggered by Fuzheng Kang'ai Formula did cause extensive cell death in NSCLC126. In contrast, multiple studies consistently indicate that autophagy functions as a pro-survival mechanism and inhibiting it can trigger tumor cell death and overcome therapeutic resistance. Natural small molecules such as aloperine and baicalin have been shown to block late-stage autophagic flux by disrupting autophagosome-lysosome fusion or autolysosome degradation, respectively, leading to SQSTM1 accumulation, ROS overproduction, and ultimately apoptotic cell death in NSCLC models, making autophagy inhibition as a direct cytotoxic strategy127,128. Beyond primary tumor suppression, autophagy also plays a critical role in chemotherapy resistance. LncRNA16-mediated autophagy inhibition paradoxically protects NSCLC cells from platinum-induced oxidative stress by stabilizing mitochondrial HBB/NDUFAF5, so targeted silencing of lncRNA16 restores chemosensitivity129. Similarly, YBX1-driven activation of the p110B/Vps34/Beclin1 axis strengthens autophagy and induces cisplatin resistance, whereas YBX1 knockdown hinders autophagy and improves drug responsiveness both in vitro and in vivo130. As for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), a subtype of NSCLC, autophagy plays a context-dependent role in shaping tumor fate, as exemplified by TRAF3IP3, which functions as a tumor suppressor by activating cytoprotective autophagy through STRN3 recruitment that partially buffers cell death but does not prevent overall apoptotic elimination in LUAD cells131. On the contrary, malignant LUAD progression can be driven by autophagy inhibition, as m6A-modified circRAPGEF5 binds IGF2BP2 to stabilize NUP160, resulting in inhibited autophagic flux and strengthened proliferation and metastasis132. Moreover, SCLC appears to rely more uniformly on autophagy as a survival mechanism underlying chemoresistance. In SCLC models, CDK4/6 inhibitors such as palbociclib or ribociclib synergize with chemotherapy by impairing autophagy through the AMBRA1–lysosome–TFEB/TFE3 axis, leading to enhanced chemosensitivity, and suppressed tumor growth independent of canonical RB signaling133. Consistently, chemoresistant SCLC exhibits metabolic dependence, and inhibition of the GGPS1–RAB7A–autophagy axis induces oxidative stress and apoptosis, effectively overcoming both intrinsic and acquired chemoresistance134.

In short, autophagy dependency is highly tumor-type- and context-specific, acting as either a tumor-suppressive or tumor-supportive process across HCC, NSCLC/LUAD, and SCLC, underscoring the necessity of tailoring autophagy activation or inhibition strategies according to distinct oncogenic states and therapeutic settings.

Autophagy and programmed cell death crosstalk

Emerging evidence indicates that autophagy is intricately interconnected with multiple regulated cell death pathways, rather than functioning as an isolated catabolic process. This crosstalk is particularly evident in contexts such as ferroptosis, apoptosis, and immune-mediated death, where autophagy not only intersects with but actively shapes these outcomes.

Recent work has indeed identified a distinct lytic cell death program arising from the convergence of innate immune signaling and metabolic disruption, further underscoring the dynamic and evolving nature of regulated cell death networks. Termed mitoxyperiosis, this cell death process is different from pyroptosis, necroptosis or oxeiptosis. It is characterized by BAX/BAK1/BID-dependent mitochondrial oxidative stress, sustained mitochondria-plasma membrane contacts, and localized oxidative membrane damage, leading to cell lysis ultimately135. Interestingly, this process is regulated by mTORC2 signaling, and disruption of mTORC2 is also known to impair autophagy136, which raises the possibility of a previously unappreciated interplay between autophagic regulation and this newly described cell death modality.

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is a novel programmed cell death (PCD) triggered by iron accumulation and peroxidation associated with intracellular iron metabolism disorders137. Ferroptosis is driven by the intracellular accumulation of redox-active Fe2+ and the oxidative deterioration of polyunsaturated phospholipids (PUFA-PLs)138, a process facilitated by lipoxygenase activity139 and Fenton chemistry140. The execution of ferroptosis is predominantly governed by the functional integrity of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), which detoxifies lipid hydroperoxides to maintain membrane redox homeostasis141. In parallel, cystine uptake through the system Xc– antiporter is indispensable for sustaining glutathione biosynthesis and thereby modulating ferroptotic sensitivity142,143. Besides, NCOA4 is an important regulator of ferroptosis. NCOA4 mediates ferritinophagy by selectively delivering ferritin to lysosomes for degradation, thereby increasing the intracellular labile iron pool144. The resulting iron overload promotes lipid peroxidation and drives ferroptotic cell death145. Moreover, cellular lipid metabolic pathways, including PUFA incorporation146, phospholipid remodeling147, and lipid peroxidation dynamics148, collectively shape the susceptibility and progression of ferroptotic cell death.

Autophagy and ferroptosis intersect at several regulatory nodes, and NCOA4 plays an important role in the interaction. Accumulating evidence identifies NCOA4 as a pivotal molecular hub linking autophagy to ferroptosis by governing ferritinophagy and intracellular iron homeostasis under pathological stress149. In glioblastoma, the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM7 inhibits ferroptosis by inducing K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of NCOA4, thereby limiting ferritinophagy-driven iron release and promoting tumor cell survival and therapy resistance150. Similarly, under high-salt conditions, fibronectin 1 sustains NCOA4 expression in cardiac fibroblasts, and its loss reduces autophagy-dependent ferritinophagy, consequently attenuating ferroptosis and fibrotic progression151. Environmental stressors further engage this axis through redox-sensitive signaling, as maternal exposure to polystyrene nanoplastics enhances ROS-driven TP53 activation, amplifying NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy and precipitating ferroptosis-associated neurodevelopmental impairment in offspring152. Conversely, therapeutic regulation of ferritin stability can uncouple autophagy from ferroptosis, as pharmacological preservation of ferritin integrity restricts iron release and confers protection against doxorubicin-induced ferroptotic cardiomyocyte injury153. Beyond NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy, autophagy regulates ferroptosis through additional signaling and metabolic pathways. In diabetic kidney disease, the downregulation of autophagy and inactivation of AMPK increase mitochondrial ROS and lipid peroxidation, amplifying ferroptotic susceptibility in renal tubular epithelial cells154. Aldolase A suppression alters cellular metabolism and enhances autophagy-dependent degradation of lipid-modifying enzymes, thereby expediting ferroptosis sensitivity in cancer therapy155. At the machinery level, TMEM164 is specifically required for ATG5-dependent autophagosome formation during ferroptosis; the degradation of ferritin, GPX4, and lipid droplets is stimulated through autophagy, consequently enhancing iron accumulation and propelling ferroptosis156.

Apoptosis

Apoptosis is a genetically programmed cell death mechanism that eliminates obsolete, damaged, infected, and self-reactive cells in a manner that limits immune cell activation157. It is characterized by distinct morphological and biochemical features, including cell shrinkage, chromatin condensation, DNA fragmentation, and the formation of apoptotic bodies, which are subsequently cleared by phagocytes without provoking inflammation158,159. Mechanistically, apoptosis is precisely regulated by the caspase cascade160 and can be initiated through two major pathways: the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway160,161 and the extrinsic (death receptor-mediated) pathway162. Triggered by intracellular stress signals such as DNA damage or oxidative stress, the intrinsic pathway is controlled by the BCL2 family proteins, which regulate the permeabilization of the mitochondrial outer membrane and the release of cytochrome c163,164. The engagement of death receptors activates the extrinsic pathway, which includes Fas and TNF receptor, and leads to the formation of death-inducing signaling complexes165,166. Both pathways converge on executioner caspase activation, leading to the orderly elimination of cells in a controlled manner167.

The complex interactions between autophagy and apoptosis determine the fate of cells. In some cases, autophagy and apoptosis contribute to cell death together. The knockdown of tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) enhanced both apoptosis and autophagy in melanoma cells168. Ceramide-based metabolic stress induces DRP1-dependent mitophagy that cooperates with pro-apoptotic signaling to suppress tumor growth, illustrating the coordinated contribution of autophagy and apoptosis to cell death169. They can both be triggered by sonogenically activated malate depletion modulator (MDM) and work with antitumor immune response to increase therapeutic effects170. Notably, there exists a delicate balance between autophagy and apoptosis. The promotion of autophagy will inhibit apoptosis, while autophagy inhibition leads to apoptosis enhancement. After spinal cord injury, the overexpression of actin-like protein 6A (Actl6a) strengthens neurological function by amplifying autophagy and limiting apoptosis171. However, when the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes is impeded by SM15, leading to blocked autophagy, ROS-related apoptosis is spurred in cancer cells172. In bladder cancer, cisplatin can inhibit CCPG1 expression to curtail reticulophagy (a selective autophagy), and apoptosis is enhanced, causing tumor cells death as a result173. Additionally, autophagy and apoptosis are coordinated through shared regulatory molecules, reflecting extensive crosstalk between these processes, with BCL2 acting as a pivotal molecular hub. When BCL2 is overexpressed in osteoarthritic chondrocytes, autophagic flux was impaired and apoptosis was increased, and these effects can be influenced by exosomes174.

Immune-mediated death

Immune-mediated cell death is commonly used to describe cell death processes that include the engagement of the immune system either as a direct executor or as a critical downstream mediator. However, this term's scope is broad and its systematic mechanisms remain loosely defined across the literature. Within this category, ICD represents one of the most extensively studied and conceptually established forms, particularly in oncological settings175. By rendering dying cancer cells immunologically visible, ICD stimulates antitumor immune responses176. This process is accompanied by the release and exposure of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as surface translocation of calreticulin, extracellular ATP secretion, and passive release of high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1)177,178. These signals facilitate dendritic cell activation and antigen presentation, thereby promoting the priming of tumor-specific cytotoxic T cells and linking cell death induction to adaptive immune involvement179,180.

It is widely recognized that autophagy represents one of the key pathways involved in the induction of ICD, with certain chemotherapeutic agents and molecular targets facilitating this process. Autophagy is capable of inducing DAMP release via specific targets such as cathepsin D inhibition and the activation of the LKB1–AMPK–ULK1 signaling axis, causing ICD directly181. The effects of boosting DAMPs release by autophagy also strengthen chemotherapy-induced antitumor immunity and propel effective T-cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment182. Therapeutically, both autophagy activation and inhibition have been shown to strengthen ICD, underscoring the complex and context-dependent relationship between autophagy and ICD. While chemotherapeutic agents can lead to ICD by promoting autophagy, the potency of this type of ICD is limited and still requires an autophagy inducer such as the polymeric nanoparticles STF@AHPPE to amplify ICD efficacy and antitumor immune activation183. In lung adenocarcinoma cells, mitophagy can be stimulated by BPQDs@Lipo-YSA via the PRKN/AKT1 pathway, which increased its cytotoxic effect and triggered ICD in animal models184. Conversely, a redox-responsive RNA interference nanoplatform (which co-delivered Nrf2 siRNA, the mitophagy inhibitor 3-Methyladenine, and the sonosensitizer purpurin-18) inhibited mitophagy, causing the accumulation of ROS and thereby inducing ICD185. A novel Prz-loaded liposome hybrid nanovesicle (Prz@LINV) system that delivered prazosin (Prz) precisely to the tumor sites greatly inhibited autophagy in tumor cells, facilitating ICD as well as the activation of antitumor immune responses186. Furthermore, autophagy and ICD can be ignited together in non-small cell lung cancer to stimulate αPD1 efficacy187.

Nanomaterials modulating microenvironment targeting autophagy

Physicochemical properties of nanomaterials

Nanomaterial-mediated autophagy regulation should be reconsidered from a materials science perspective. Emerging evidence indicates that autophagy outcomes arise from structured interactions between material physicochemical properties and the tumor microenvironment, rather than from isolated effects.

At the compositional level, different classes of biomaterials establish distinct biochemical foundations for autophagy regulation. Metal-based nanomaterials can function as redox-sensitive regulators of autophagy, initiating early autophagic responses through ROS-dependent signaling while, at later stages, impairing autophagic flux, inducing lysosomal dysfunction—defined as a loss of lysosomal acidity, enzymatic activity, or membrane integrity—which in turn impairs autophagic flux188. The balance between pro-survival and pro-death outcomes is consequently shaped by material dose, size, surface chemistry, and cellular context188. For instance, macrophages have been shown to utilize the intrinsic oxidative lability of Ti3C2 MXene as an endogenous detoxification signal, coupling CYP450/ROS-mediated particle oxidation with PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy to neutralize the material and mitigate secondary mitochondrial damage189. Besides, carbon-based nanoparticles regulate autophagy in a context-dependent and bidirectional manner. Low-dose or surface-engineered carbon quantum dots activate cytoprotective autophagy, while high-dose or oxidized carbon materials impair autophagic flux through lysosomal dysfunction, turning cell fate from survival to immunogenic or apoptotic death190. Consistent with this behavior, flavonoid-derived carbon dots (FIS-CDs) scavenge ROS and activate SIRT3-dependent mitophagy, promoting oxidative stress resolution and pro-osteogenic survival191. Polymeric materials, including polydopamine-based photothermal nanomaterials exert antitumor effects by integrating mitochondrial damage, lysosomal dysfunction, and autophagy modulation192. Specifically, branched Au-Ag nanoparticles coated with polydopamine (Au-Ag@PDA) exhibit high photothermal conversion efficiency and suppress colorectal tumor growth by inducing multiple cell death pathways including autophagy in vitro and in vivo193. In a specific tumor type, inorganic nanomaterials can serve as smart drug carriers, external-field sensitizers, and tumor-microenvironment responders to markedly improve renal cell carcinoma therapy while minimizing systemic toxicity194. Furthermore, liposomal delivery systems carrying autophagy regulators enable precise modulation of tumor autophagic flux to overcome therapeutic resistance and boost chemotherapy as well as immunotherapy efficacy195. Targeting PDL1, lipid nanoparticles delivering PTEN mRNA have been shown to selectively restore PTEN expression in triple-negative breast cancer, causing ICD associated with autophagy196.

Structural features constitute the critical interface through which material composition is translated into microenvironmental sensing and cellular interaction. Parameters such as particle size, shape, surface roughness, and charge distribution determine tumor penetration, endocytic pathways, and intracellular trafficking, shaping autophagy activation and autophagosome formation indirectly197. For instance, particle size influences not only tissue permeability, but also the preference for clathrin-mediated endocytosis versus macropinocytosis, and surface topography and non-spherical geometries can trigger pathways related to autophagy198. Matrix stiffness and mechanical properties further influence autophagy through cytoskeletal reorganization and altered LC3 expression, linking material mechanics to drug resistance mediated by autophagy86,199. Advanced structural designs, such as oxygen carriers, and oxygen-generating materials, allow nanoplatforms to actively remodel the tumor microenvironment and establish conditions that bias autophagy toward specific functional outcomes76,200.

In summary, cancer treatment targeting autophagy by nanomaterials emerges from coordinated interactions between material physicochemical properties and the tumor microenvironment, which collectively dictate autophagy directionality and functional consequences instead of isolated promotion or inhibition effects.

Microenvironmental determinants

Oxidative stress induction

Oxidative stress, defined as the result of an imbalance between cellular oxidants and antioxidants201, is important in the occurrence of autophagy. Mainly produced in the inner mitochondrial membrane, ROS are generated when electrons leak from the mitochondrial respiratory chain. These ROS can usually cause oxidative stress, which is regarded as one of the main causes of cytotoxicity induced by nanomaterials32,202. Oxidative stress serves as the initiating event of autophagy and induces autophagy through various pathways, including mTOR and AMPK. As for the PI3K–AKT–mTOR pathway, ROS lead to a decrease in AKT and mTOR phosphorylation activity203, indicating that ROS induce AMPK activation while downregulating the mTOR signaling pathway concurrently204. Moreover, ROS has a direct impact on ATG4 by inhibiting its deconjugating activity without affecting its protease activity through the oxidative modification of specific cysteine residues205. ROS can also stimulate Beclin1 expression to facilitate autophagy206.

Recently, a variety of nanomaterials targeting oxidative stress to trigger autophagy in the treatment of cancer have been reported. Despite differences in composition, these nanomaterials can be broadly categorized into metal-based, carbon-based, and biomimetic systems, which converge on excessive ROS generation as a shared upstream trigger of autophagy19. As a representative metal-based nanomaterial, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have been widely studied to promote autophagy through ROS-related pathways, and they mainly target the liver207, with studies indicating that they can lead to apoptosis in HepG2 cells through autophagy. AgNPs induce oxidative stress by prompting the generation of ROS208. A subsequent study revealed that the mechanism involves the inhibition of AMPK, which finally results in the suppression of the mTOR mechanism, thus triggering autophagy209. In cutaneous melanoma, another metal-based piezo-catalytic nanosystems, Fe-doped ultrathin Bi4Ti3O12 nanosheets (Fe-UBTO NSs) induces ROS production by enhanced piezo-chemocatalytic activity and therefore amplifying autophagy, improving tumor therapy and wound healing210. Biomimetic nanovesicles, such as hybrid systems composed of red blood cell (RBC) membranes and liposomes, synergize with short-term fasting, induce ROS overproduction and the formation of autophagosomes, thereby suppressing triple-negative breast cancer metastasis211. Carbon-based nanomaterials, exemplified by fluorescent metformin-derived carbon dots (M-CDs), modulate intracellular redox balance and autophagy-related signaling by downregulating the expression levels of mTOR, AMPK, and P62 while simultaneously enhancing the activation of p-AMPK and increasing the expression of autophagy-related markers such as LC3-I, LC3-II, and BECN1. Thus, autophagy in cells exposed to M-CDs is promoted, impeding the growth of breast cancer cells212. Although these nanomaterials differ in physicochemical properties and tumor models, they share a common regulatory pattern in which ROS overproduction serves as a central upstream signal that converges on AMPK-mTOR and related pathways, ultimately modulating autophagy toward a therapeutically beneficial outcome.

Endoplasmic reticulum stress

ER stress is a pathophysiological process caused by stresses that perturb ER function, leading to diseases like neurodegenerative disorders and cancer. During this process, protein folding is disrupted, which can result in the accumulation of inactive and chemically aggressive proteins in the ER213. Nanomaterials can trigger ER stress through many routes that induce autophagy. ER stress initiates the unfolded protein response (UPR), a cellular mechanism aimed at restoring homeostasis. This response is orchestrated through three essential signaling pathways mediated by inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), PKR-like ER kinase (PERK), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6)214 (Fig. 4). The three signaling molecules can modulate the expression of autophagy-related genes directly to promote autophagy215, 216, 217. The heavy-chain binding protein (BiP), also functioning as an ER stress detector, can interact with IRE1 and PERK through its nucleotide-binding domain upon its dissociation, thus promoting the UPR218. Importantly, autophagy activated downstream of UPR in turn serves as a compensatory feedback mechanism that alleviates ER stress by selectively removing misfolded proteins and damaged ER membranes, thereby contributing to the attenuation or termination of prolonged UPR signaling219. Besides, a new study supports that the damage of mitochondria-associated membrane integrity can induce ER-phagy in vitro and in vivo220. ER-phagy represents a selective form of autophagy that directly remodels ER structure and function, further reinforcing the feedback loop between ER stress sensing and autophagic resolution221. ER stress can also lead to ICD which can help kill tumor cells222. In recent years, a number of nanoparticles have been discovered to be capable of prompting autophagy through ER stress, which is promising for the treatment of cancer. In spite of their diverse compositions, these nanomaterials commonly disrupt ER homeostasis or protein-folding capacity, activating UPR signaling that links ER stress to autophagy induction. As for metal-associated systems, the disulfiram/copper complex (DSF/Cu) or nano-Cu has been extensively investigated for its anticancer effects. In oral squamous cell carcinoma224 and HCC cells225, DSF/Cu robustly induces ER stress, leading to the facilitation of autophagy and apoptosis and thereby promoting cancer cell clearance. Mechanistically, DSF/Cu-induced autophagy has been shown to involve multiple branches of the UPR. These include activation of the IRE1α pathway in breast and pancreatic cancer cells226, upregulation of the PERK/eIF2α axis in meningioma adhesion cells227, and ER stress-associated ICD that coordinates immune activation in HCC228. Beyond metal-based systems, self-assembled supramolecular drugs on the basis of small-molecule anion transporters, can also provoke ER stress in osteosarcoma cells, ultimately resulting in cell death associated with autophagy229. Similarly, ER-targeting nanoparticles (TSE-CEL/NP) preferentially accumulate in the ER, where they induce ER stress and calreticulin (CRT) exposure, thereby eliciting a strong antitumor immune response against melanoma230. Collectively, these studies indicate that nanomaterials capable of perturbing ER homeostasis, either through metal-associated stress, supramolecular assembly, or ER-targeted delivery, tend to converge on UPR activation, which serves as a common molecular hub linking ER stress to autophagy induction and antitumor immunity.

Lysosome damage

Given their central role in autophagy, lysosomes are often functionally disrupted by various nanomaterials, which act as powerful modulators of autophagic flux in cancer therapeutics231. The whole process of autophagy includes the merging of the autophagosome with the lysosome to form an autolysosome232,233. Autophagic flux can be impaired if lysosomal function is compromised, and maintaining an acidic environment in lysosomes is critical for their functions. Some nanoformulations are capable of disrupting the acidic environment of lysosomes, leading to autophagic flux blockade—here referring to impairment of the late stages of autophagy, in which autophagosome-lysosome fusion or intralysosomal degradation is obstructed—and consequently preventing the breakdown of damaged organelles and proteins within cells234. For example, changes in ionic levels in the cell, especially calcium ion levels, can affect the acidic environment in lysosomes, inhibiting their function in the autophagy process235. Classically, in autophagy, SNAREs are the key proteins that mediate the homotypic fusion of phagophore precursors and are believed to include the STX17–SNAP47–VAMP7/VAMP8 complex236. Recently, new SNAREs have been identified, like a subset of SNB6, VAMP7, USO1 and SYX17 (which work as a tethering factor)237. SNAREs often gather together to form a complex, such as the STX17–SNAP29–VAMP8 complex238, and STX17 attracts SNAP29 to autophagosomes, where SNAP29 binds with VAMP8 on lysosomes subsequently to form a trans-SNARE complex essential for the fusion process239. Thus, interfering with the interaction between SNAP29 and VAMP8 can block autophagosome-lysosome fusion240.

A large number of nanomaterials have been developed to target lysosome and then hinder autophagy in tumor treatment (Fig. 5). Dendrimer-based proton sponge nanocomposites, including proton sponge G5AcP dendrimer, Fenton-like agent Cu (II), and doxorubicin, which can upregulate the pH in the lysosome, inhibiting cytoprotective autophagy to accelerate tumor cell death241. Also, through attenuating intralysosomal acidity and inducing lysosomal dysfunction, a nanomaterial named bisaminoquinoline-derived one-component new-chemical-entity nanomedicine (BAQ ONNs) has demonstrated potent anti-tumor effect by inhibiting autophagy231. Some nanoparticles can interfere with autophagosome-lysosome fusion. It has been found that surface-modified mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) modified with polyethylenimine are capable of preventing STX17 incorporation into autophagosomes and the trafficking between autophagosome and lysosome is inhibited242. MNPs@NO-Gel, constructed by loading the ferrimagnetic Zn0.5Fe2.5O4 magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) onto an NO-Gel, enables a steady release of NO, and NO successfully inhibits the formation of autophagosomes while damaging lysosomes at the same time to block autophagy243. Nanomaterials like Ag nanoparticles (Cy3.5@Ag NPs) that gathered in lysosomes will permeate the lysosomal membrane, resulting in lysosomal deactivation244. In hepatocellular carcinoma, calcium phosphate-based nanoparticles incorporating poly (acrylic acid) as a pH-sensitive component (PAA/CaP NPs) can increase Ca2+ content in HCC cells, which restrains autophagy and then boosts EPI's cytotoxicity, promoting the treatment effect on HCC greatly245. Also in hepatic carcinoma, encapsulation of doxorubicin and an MRI contrast compound in silica nanoparticles affects TFEB translocation to the nucleus and attenuates autophagy by disrupting lysosomal function, resulting in enhanced tumoricidal effects246. Collectively, these studies illustrate the autophagosome-lysosome axis as an effective intervention point to strengthen anticancer efficacy. Inhibition of energy metabolism-related autophagy

Energy metabolism is a key factor for the initiation and sustenance of autophagy in cancer cells, and it has been widely accepted that interfering with metabolism is a viable way to treat cancer247. In the special environment in tumors, extreme conditions such as hypoxia248, lack of glucose86 and so on can promote autophagy to cope with nutrient and energy deficiency. Impaired mitochondria produce less ATP, and this low energy level activates the AMPK–mTOR pathway and ignites autophagy, thus leading to the formation of dormant polyploid giant cancer cells249. These all indicate that inhibiting energy-related autophagy can potentially achieve good cancer therapeutic effects. Besides, glutamine metabolism plays an important role in cell energy metabolism. Apart from serving as a key metabolic fuel250, glutamine also modulates autophagy-related signaling pathways in cancer cells. Glutaminolysis triggers the AMPK–mTORC1 pathway and then leads to the inhibition of autophagy, which provides a promising therapeutic candidate in oncology251. On the contrary, the glutamylation of TAB1 makes it possible for signals to pass through the GFAT1–TAB1–p38 pathway and ultimately promotes autophagy, protecting neoplastic cells to survive in the absence of glucose252.

Hence, disturbing cancer metabolism is a promising target for nanomaterials’ design and optimization. Tumor repair can be interfered with through a division of functional roles within nanoplatform-based systems. In such systems, autophagy modulation is primarily mediated by the loaded therapeutic agents, and nanomaterials mainly function as delivery and coordination platforms instead of regulating autophagy directly. In the CQ@MIL-GOX@PB platform, chloroquine (CQ) directly inhibits autophagy-dependent cellular repair, while glucose oxidase (GOx) blocks energy metabolism, whereas the nanoplatform mainly enables their co-delivery and coordinated action at tumor sites253. Similarly, in the Co-MOF-based nanoplatform co-loaded with anethole trithione (ADT) and modified with GOx and hyaluronic acid (HA), GOx suppresses glycolysis and ADT disrupts oxidative phosphorylation, while the nanocarrier facilitates spatial coupling and synergistic metabolic interference254. Dual restriction can also be achieved with liposomes loaded with glucose oxidase and telaglenastat in breast cancer, through which both the glutamine and glycolysis metabolism are impeded255. In a sequencing study, this dual restriction, mediated by mTOR and HIF-1, was further explored with a cascade-boosting nanomedicine designed to suppress glucose and glutamine metabolism and then inhibit autophagy, providing a detailed mechanism in the treatment of advanced breast cancer256. The integration of metabolic intervention with autophagy inhibition represents a rational strategy to overcome tumor metabolic plasticity. We believe that future nanoplatforms should explore coordinated regulation instead of isolated metabolic blockade to modulate the complex tumor microenvironment with higher efficiency.

Temporal-spatial issues in autophagy regulation

A defining characteristic of nanoparticles is their capacity for precise disease treatment, achieved by preferential accumulation at designated sites and controlled drug release at specific time points, a property collectively referred to as the spatiotemporal regulation of nanoparticles.

pH-responsive (acid-triggered) nanoparticles provide an intrinsic spatiotemporal control over autophagy regulation, as drug release is spatially confined to acidic tumor tissues or subcellular compartments and temporally initiated only after nanoparticle accumulation and environmental sensing, rather than immediately upon systemic administration. The nanoparticle PDGL-GEM@CAP/CQ, assembled from PDGL-GEM, CQ, and calcium phosphate, can release CQ in an environment of pH lower than 6.5 because of the dissolution of calcium phosphate, downregulating autophagy to hinder pancreatic cancer progression and metastasis257. Aiming at regulating the autophagy-lysosome pathway and TFEB nuclear translocation, FA-Gd2O3@MSN-DOX carrying doxorubicin (DOX) and MRI contrast agent Gd2O3 can be controlled to release DOX when dissociating in acidic tumor microenvironment (pH 6.0) to inhibit autophagy, amplifying tumor cell death246. The nanoplatform HSA-MnO2-CQ NPs has stricter environment requirements to be dissociated, which requires sufficient H2O2 concentration and proper pH (pH 6.5), and autophagy is inhibited after CQ release, modulating the microenvironment and sensitizing radiation in bladder cancer treatment258. Beyond autophagy inhibition, controlled activation of autophagy under acidic environment has also emerged as a complementary strategy, reflecting its dual role in cancer therapy. Zn2+-doped, disulfiram-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles (DSF@Zn-DMSNs) undergo pH-sensitive degradation in the mildly acidic tumor microenvironment (pH < 6.5), co-releasing Zn2+ and DSF to generate toxic in situ chelates that simultaneously induce apoptosis and boost autophagy-mediated DAMP release, enhancing ICD and T-cell infiltration182. These pH-triggered nanoplatforms exemplify how spatiotemporal control enables bidirectional manipulation of autophagy according to therapeutic context, so the synergistic anticancer efficacy is maximized.

Sonodynamic-responsive nanoplatforms make precise spatiotemporal regulation of autophagy possible by allowing ultrasound-controlled drug release under demand, while spatial specificity is achieved through preferential nanoparticle aggregation within tumor tissues. Interestingly, most of the nanoparticles responding to ultrasound inhibit autophagy to achieve therapeutic effects. A biomimetic nanoplatform composed of cancer cell membrane-coated hollow mesoporous TiO2 nanoparticles loaded with HCQ (CCM-HMTNPs/HCQ) was designed for breast cancer, in which homologous tumor targeting enabled preferential accumulation at tumor sites, while ultrasound irradiation temporally triggered HCQ release to block SDT-induced protective autophagy, sensitizing tumor cells to sonodynamic apoptosis259. Similarly, a phase-change liposomal nanodroplet platform SP@Lip-PEG carrying autophagy inhibitor SAR405 targets tumor sites selectively, and low-intensity focused ultrasound initiated acoustic droplet vaporization to achieve controlled drug release, limiting autophagy and enhancing immune activation to improve checkpoint blockade efficacy in melanoma260. In colon cancer, stress-induced autophagy is blocked by the CQ in a homologous targeting hollow polydopamine nanocarrier (CCC@HP@M), which is capable of responding to ultrasound-activated SDT and cooperating with calcium overload to secure combined therapeutic efficacy261. Focusing on mitochondrial quality control, an angiopep-2-modified liposomal nanosystem (ACHL) integrating Ce6 and HCQ was developed for glioma, in which the first ultrasound exposure induced transient Blood-Brain Barrier opening to permit tumor-selective accumulation, followed by a second ultrasonic stimulation that temporally triggered ROS generation and HCQ release to hinder mitophagy via the MAPK/p38-PINK1-PRKN pathway262. Also targeting mitophagy, ROS production is controlled by ultrasound, as a sonoactivated Z-scheme heterojunction nanosystem constructed from PtCu3 nanocages and black phosphorus nanosheets enables tumor-localized aggregation and ultrasound-triggered ROS release, while lysosomal alkalization mediated by Cu2+ prevents mitophagic clearance of impaired mitochondria, so the mitophagy-driven resistance is surmounted in triple-negative breast cancer263. Ultrasound-responsive nanomaterials provide a versatile spatiotemporal framework for context-dependent autophagy regulation, making sonodynamic strategies more precise in cancer treatment.

Beyond pH-responsive and sonodynamic-responsive nanoparticles, there exist multiple mechanisms to achieve spatiotemporal modulation of autophagy by nanoparticles. It has been reported that nanoparticles can respond to autophagy directly. Due to the higher levels of autophagy in deep tumor cells, autophagy-responsive multifunctional nanoparticles (PGN) were designed, and the CQ loaded on it can be released when the nanoparticle reached deep tumor cell and cationic autophagy-responsive cell-penetrating peptide (GR9) was cut off by an autophagy-associated protease, inhibiting autophagy to enhance antitumor efficacy199. Additionally, the azocalix [4]arene-modified supramolecular albumin nanoparticle accomplishes spatiotemporal inhibition of mitophagy by selectively responding to the hypoxic microenvironment, thereby strengthening therapeutic efficacy against hypoxic cancers264. Besides endogenous stimuli-responsive strategies, light has emerged as a powerful external trigger to attain precise spatiotemporal regulation of nanoparticle-mediated autophagy in tumors. UV-activated DNA nanoparticles that sequentially disassemble and reassemble in lysosomes via an i-motif-driven AND gate have been reported to spur autophagy, and the spatial specificity (in lysosome) and temporal specificity (activated by UV) offered the precise spatiotemporal modulation of the autophagic flux in cancer cells265. Through laser-induced localized heating, a photothermal-responsive DNA nanosphere obtains spatiotemporally controlled activation of autophagy following tumor-selective intracellular disassembly, propelling autophagy-lysosomal degradation of target proteins such as PDL1 for enhanced antitumor efficacy266. Although the strategies mentioned above all promote autophagy, autophagy inhibition remains a viable solution in phototherapy. By integrating photodynamic and photothermal effects with real-time imaging, light irradiation triggers autophagy inhibition in pyropheophorbide a-bisaminoquinoline conjugate lipid nanoparticles, which results in sustained tumor retention and efficient bladder cancer ablation267.

Overall, spatiotemporally programmable nanoparticles exploit both tumor-intrinsic signals and externally applied stimuli to dynamically regulate autophagy in space and time, strengthening the precision and therapeutic flexibility of cancer nanomedicine as a result.

Selective autophagy and its relevance to cancer

Selective autophagy refers to a highly regulated lysosome-dependent degradation pathway in which defined cellular substrates, such as damaged or surplus organelles, protein aggregates, or intracellular pathogens, which are specifically recognized by cargo receptors and selectively sequestered into autophagosomes for subsequent degradation, and contributing to cellular quality control, metabolic adaptation, and genome stability maintenance268. Depending on the nature of cargo, selective autophagy encompasses various specialized forms, including organelle-specific pathways such as mitophagy, ER-phagy, lysophagy, ribophagy, and nucleophagy, as well as processes targeting protein aggregates (aggrephagy), lipid droplets (lipophagy), and invading pathogens (xenophagy)269.

Mitophagy

Mitophagy is a selective form of macroautophagy that mediates the targeted degradation of damaged or superfluous mitochondria. This process is essential for mitochondrial quality control and cellular homeostasis270. The role of mitophagy in tumorigenesis depends on its multiple effects on cellular metabolism, apoptosis regulation, and adaptation to the microenvironment. The dual role of mitophagy in cancer stems from its ability to maintain mitochondrial quality control to downgrade tumor progression, while also upgrading tumor development by enabling cancer cells to adapt to hypoxic conditions and resist therapy271. Mitochondria-targeting nanoparticles have been developed in recent years272.

Currently, the mechanism of mitophagy has two main classifications, ubiquitin (Ub)-dependent and Ub-independent pathways271. In the Ub-dependent pathway, the PINK1–Parkin pathway is the most important and complicated one, but there still exist other pathways that exclude Parkin, and some receptors are essential in mediating mitophagy273. Encoded by PINK1/PARK6, PINK1 is a mitochondrial serine/threonine kinase and recruits Parkin to the impaired mitochondria from the cytoplasm274. After ΔΨm depolarization, PINK1 will recruit Parkin, which has E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. Parkin can associate with the outer mitochondrial membrane and then trigger a cascade of signaling events that ultimately lead to the sequestration of damaged mitochondria within lysosomes275,276. Besides Parkin, RBX2 regulates PINK1's steadiness without depending on Parkin277. BNIP3 and NIX, recognized as important receptors on the outer mitochondrial membrane, interact with a protein called LC3 in hypoxia, play key roles in many pathways that promote mitophagy278,279, and their expression is elevated by transcription280. In cancer cells, the normal pathway can encounter some problems, and both suppressing and boosting tumor growth can be achieved through different mechanisms. In colorectal cancer, PINK1 functions as a tumor suppressor through promoting mitophagy, decreasing glycolysis, and reducing acetyl-CoA production, so the tumor growth is inhibited. Conversely, a low level of Parkin causes mitochondrial dysfunction and the accumulation of ROS, contributing to tumor progression and chemotherapy resistance32,281. Besides, mesencephalic astrocyte derived neurotrophic factor (MANF) triggers mitophagy in breast cancer cells by interacting with Parkin RBR E3 ubiquitin protein ligase, helping cancer cells survive in the condition of glucose-starvation; thus inhibiting mitophagy can interfere with cancer growth282. When experiencing hypoxia, the expression of BNIP3 and NIX's is enhanced283, which lowers mitochondrial quantity and curbs the generation of ROS284. On the contrary, knocking down BNIP3 and NIX leads to mitophagy deficiency and the over production of ROS, eventually causing cell death285.

In diverse cell environments, Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) are capable of treating tumor via the regulation of mitophagy. In most cases, ZnO NPs often stimulate mitophagy. By promoting the expression of PINK1 and Parkin in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, ZnO NPs will stimulate the PINK1–Parkin signaling pathway to cause mitophagy, eliciting cytotoxic effects in cancer cells286. Similarly, the increased ROS level in CAL 27 human tongue cancer cells caused by ZnO NPs will also trigger mitophagy mediated by PINK1 and Parkin287. Many studies have proved that the application of ZnO NPs can lead to the accumulation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF1A) protein in tumor cells, and subsequently affect the amount of BNIP3 and LC3 in cells, which means mitophagy is induced through HIF1A–BNIP3–LC3B pathway coupled with other experiments, thereby killing the abnormal cells288,289. Overall, current findings support ZnO nanoparticle-induced mitophagy as an effective tumoricidal mechanism in vitro. Future studies should focus on the tumor-stage dependence and durability of this response to determine whether mitophagy induction can be harnessed as a predictable therapeutic strategy. In pancreatic cancer, a nanoparticle named miriplatin (MPt)-loaded liposome (LMPt), has been developed and is found to target mitochondria, lowering the level of POLG and TFAM, and then promoting mitophagy through the PINK1–Parkin pathway because of mitochondrial DNA replication's inhibition caused by the degradation of POLG and TFAM290 (Fig. 6).Figure 6Mechanistic illustration of the liposomal miriplatin formulation LMPt in regulating mitophagy. However, it has been reported in recent literature that in some cases, the inhibition of mitophagy can also help suppress the growth of cancer. The nanoparticle cuproptotic nanoinducer (PCM) facilitates the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and the activation of the cGAS–STING signaling pathway by inducing mitochondrial proteotoxic stress and inhibiting mitophagy by carrying mitophagy-inhibiting Mdivi-1, thereby suppressing tumor growth291. The removal of damaged mitochondria can be achieved through mitophagy, and a new nanomaterial called "iron-phagy" nanoparticle (Dp44mT@HTH) has been developed to suppress the clearance of malfunctioning mitochondria, triggering thorough destruction of mitochondria, and causing the death of cancer cells292. KCKT, an in situ transformable nanoparticle, restrains mitophagy by blocking its protective mechanism, so drug delivery efficiency was enhanced and bladder cancer was treated more efficaciously293. A drug-loaded nanocarrier formulation engineered for targeted delivery was composed of calcium phosphate and glucose oxidase (Ca@GOx) cloaked with a combination of homologous cell membranes and mitochondrial membranes. However, the activation of mitophagy hindered its treatment effect on cancer cells. So chloroquine was added into the nanoparticle as a mitophagy inhibitor; this blocked mitophagy and achieved the aim of killing cancer cells294.

Ferroptosis-related autophagy

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent cell death pathway that is influenced by autophagy through regulation of iron metabolism, redox balance, and lipid peroxidation295. Among these mechanisms, ferritinophagy represents the most well-characterized axis linking autophagy and ferroptosis. Mediated by NCOA4, ferritinophagy delivers ferritin to lysosomes selectively, promoting ferritin degradation and expanding the labile iron pool, and the availability of redox-active Fe2+ to fuel Fenton reactions is amplified as a result144,296. At the molecular level, NCOA4 acts as a selective autophagy receptor that interacts with ferritin in an iron-dependent manner to regulate ferritin degradation99. Moroever, iron homeostasis is altered and ferroptosis sensitivity is enhanced in cancer cells when NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy is disrupted296.

Beyond ferritinophagy, increasing evidence suggests that ferroptotic sensitivity is further modulated by autophagic pathways that do not target iron storage proteins directly. Both chaperone-mediated autophagy and macroautophagy can influence the stability of key antioxidant components, with GPX4 as the most notable molecule, and autophagy-dependent depletion of GPX4 has been associated with impaired detoxification of lipid hydroperoxides and a lowered threshold for ferroptosis297,298. Additionally, autophagic regulation of cystine transport and glutathione metabolism is capable of reshaping intracellular GSH pools indirectly, weakening GPX4-centered antioxidant defenses and intensifying ferroptotic stress299. Beyond redox enzymes, selective autophagy pathways regulate lipid metabolism and organelle quality control, which in turn reshape the cellular redox environment and promote persistent lipid peroxidation in iron-replete conditions295,300. These ferroptosis-related autophagic mechanisms function as upstream regulatory nodes that integrate iron homeostasis, antioxidant capacity, as well as oxidative stress to fine-tune ferroptotic cell fate301.

In most cases, nanoparticles designed to target ferroptosis-related autophagy promote autophagy or ferritinophagy to treat cancer. As an advanced type of selective autophagy, ferritinophagy is a promising target. Sorafenib (SRF) and indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase inhibitor (NLG919) are loaded on nanoparticles containing ferritin and a pH-sensitive molecular-switch (FPBC@SN), which can disintegrate in acidic cytoplasm. SRF not only upregulates NCOA4 to gain iron ions by ferritinophagy to participate in the Fenton reaction, but also suppresses the synthesis of glutathione and downregulates GPX4 to boost ferroptosis by clearing LPO generated from Fenton reaction in tumor cells302. The GSH-responsive nanoparticle CSAA/Fe@PPI was developed to carry Polyphyllin I (PPI) and ferric ion (Fe3+), and the material itself is capable of scavenging the aberrant GSH in cells to inhibit GPX4 activity so LPO will accumulate in cells, cooperating with ferritinophagy triggered by PPI and Fe3+ which increased intracellular iron level, thereby enhancing ferroptosis-based anti-cancer treatment efficiency303. Equipped with pH/glutathione-dependent degradability to inhibit GPX4, iron (II)-based metal-organic framework (FessMOF) nanoparticles loading Actinomycin D (ActD) can suppress Xc– system and ignite ferritinophagy, achieving excellent ferrotherapeutic efficacy304. Beyond GSH or GPX4 related pathways, ferritinophagy also works together with other mechanisms including tumor immune microenvironment modulation and ROS. Fucoidan-decorated Fe-TA-Zol supramolecular assembled nanoparticles (FTZ@Fu) enable acidity-responsive zoledronic acid release and promoted iron redox cycling, leading to activation of ferritinophagy that drives ferroptosis and immunogenic cell death while remodeling the metastatic tumor immune microenvironment305. Assembled from oleate-conjugated ruthenium complexes, a ferroptosis-inducing nanoplatform based on liposome-like ruthenosomes preferentially gather in mitochondria to strengthen ROS production, disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis, and activate ferritinophagy, ultimately sensitizing tumor cells to ferroptotic death306.

While ferritinophagy provides a direct link between autophagy and iron release, emerging studies suggest that ferroptosis is further shaped by autophagy-dependent regulation, and autophagy is usually boosted by nanomaterials. One representative strategy exploits autophagy to amplify ferroptosis by suppressing the GPX4–GSH axis and promoting ferritin degradation, thereby upregulating labile iron availability and lipid peroxidation in lung adenocarcinoma using FIN56-based silk fibroin nanoparticles307. At the molecular level, autophagy can function as a direct facilitator of ferroptosis, as exemplified by ELAVL1-mediated stabilization of BECN1 mRNA, which facilitates autophagic flux, activates ferritinophagy, and is required for efficient ferroptotic execution in tissues of advanced fibrotic patients with hepatocellular carcinoma308. In addition to ferritin turnover, autophagy-associated iron mobilization can be promoted by ferritin metabolic reprogramming, where the suppression of ferritin O-GlcNAcylation catalyzes autophagy, expands the labile iron pool, and reinforces lipid peroxidation in chemodynamic therapy309. However, excessive autophagy sometimes needs to be inhibited to enhance ferroptosis. GSH depletion-related ferroptosis has been shown to synergize with iron-mediated ROS production, and tumor cells counterbalance this stress through autophagy activation, necessitating deliberate autophagy inhibition to enhance combined apoptosis-ferroptosis therapy310.

Lipophagy

Lipophagy, a selective form of autophagy, targets lipid droplets for lysosomal degradation specifically, thereby mobilizing stored neutral lipids to support energy production and cholesterol efflux311. Lipophagy releases free fatty acids that can be reutilized for mitochondrial β-oxidation, membrane repair, and cellular growth, and thus represents a key regulatory axis connecting autophagy and lipid metabolism312. At the mechanistic level, lipophagy proceeds through a series of steps coordinately, including the selective recognition of LDs by autophagy receptors, such as SQSTM1/p62, NBR1, and OPTN, followed by ubiquitination-dependent sequestration that propels their delivery to the lysosomal compartment313,314. The lipid degradation during lipophagy appears to proceed in an organized manner, with lipid hydrolysis being initiated at lipid droplets before lysosomal fusion315. Vacuolar-protein-sorting-associated protein 4A (VPS4A) has been identified as a selective receptor for lipophagy, and silencing VPS4A hinders lipophagy without influencing other forms of selective autophagy316. Multiple signaling pathways control lipophagy, and most notably networks including AMPK–mTORC1317,318, as well as transcriptional regulators such as TFEB, which couples lipophagy with lysosomal biogenesis319. Importantly, lipophagy exhibits pronounced tissue-specific regulation313 and its dysregulation has been implicated in a range of pathological conditions, which makes it a promising target for disease treatment in the future.

Lipophagy plays a context-dependent role in tumor biology by maintaining membrane homeostasis, metabolic flexibility, and fostering tumor metastasis312. In glioblastoma, activation of autophagy regulated by SREBP-1 promotes the lipophagic degradation of cholesteryl ester-enriched lipid droplets, which helps maintain plasma membrane homeostasis and supports tumor cell growth under cholesterol limited conditions320. By sustaining lipophagic activity, C/EBPα helps preserve mitochondrial function and boosts the ability of cancer cells to withstand metabolic stress, including starvation and hypoxia321. Metastatic ovarian cancer spheroids display extensive lipid droplet accumulation and rely on lipophagy to sustain fatty acid utilization and β-oxidation, an adaptation that is critical for survival in the malignant ascites microenvironment322. Although lipophagy helps tumor cells survive through various mechanisms, it can still combat tumor growth in some specific circumstances. The activation of lipophagy depending on ATG14 has been reported to spur free fatty acid amassment, causing endoplasmic reticulum stress and mitochondrial apoptosis, ultimately reducing tumor cell viability323. In triple-negative breast cancer cells, docosahexaenoic acid can amplify lipophagy, and the effect will be enhanced when combined with delta-tocotrienol. This kind of lipophagy reduces lipid droplet accumulation and is associated with decreased malignant features, demonstrating the tumor-suppressive role of lipophagy324.

Recent research has begun to explore the therapeutic value of lipophagy. The nanomaterial EFP@MNs was developed to trigger lipophagy by disturbing metabolism, and FFA was released following the degradation of lipid droplets. Therefore, lipid peroxide production was increased, stimulating ferroptosis to facilitate cancer treatment325. Although studies on nanomaterials targeting lipophagy remain limited, they provide important evidence that lipophagy can be selectively harnessed to amplify lipid peroxidation and overcome metabolic constraints, underscoring lipophagy as a promising yet underexplored target for nanomedicine-assisted cancer treatment.

Nucleophagy

Nucleophagy is a form of selective autophagy that targets discrete nuclear components for degradation rather than eliminating the entire nucleus, thereby preserving nuclear integrity and homeostasis. Through nucleophagy, cells selectively remove non-essential or damaged nuclear constituents, including aberrant nuclear proteins, chromatin fragments, micronuclei, and DNA-protein crosslinks, thus maintaining nuclear and cellular homeostasis during nitrogen starvation326,327. Two major steps are involved in nucleophagy: the formation of an autophagosome and the recruitment of autophagy receptors that link nuclear components to the phagophore membrane. Encoded by LMNA, the nuclear envelope protein lamin A/C (LMNA) plays a key role in the regulation of nucleophagy. Modulators of autophagy328 and an mTOR inhibitor329 are given to LMNA-related cardiomyopathy mice, which delays cardiac dysfunction and prolongs survival, indicating that the disruption of autophagy is a pathogenic factor in the disease. The LMNA mutation leads to abnormal Ca2+ processing and triggers arrhythmias in cardiomyocytes, accompanied by nuclear envelope deformation and SUN1 accumulation, indicative of nucleophagy disruption330. In addition, Atg39 has emerged as an important regulator of nucleophagy. Studies have demonstrated that Atg39 functions as a selective nucleophagy receptor that accumulates at defined nuclear envelope sites and coordinates sequential inner and outer nuclear membrane fission, enabling the removal of nuclear cargo from an intact nucleus through Dnm1-dependent membrane remodeling331. Under nutrient stress, Atg39-mediated macronucleophagy acts as a regulatory checkpoint that restrains excessive micronucleophagy at nucleus-vacuole junctions, thereby preventing uncontrolled loss of nuclear components327. Furthermore, additional molecules have been identified to participate in the regulation of nucleophagy. Members of the REEP protein family, including Hva22/Yep1, are capable of facilitating autophagic membrane remodeling and the sequestration of nuclear cargo, suggesting a conserved mechanism underlying selective nuclear degradation332.

In cancer, nucleophagy does not exert a uniformly pro- or anti-tumorigenic function; instead, both the promotion and inhibition of nucleophagy can achieve therapeutic benefits, depending on tumor context as well as treatment pressure. Genomic instability is a defining feature of malignant cells and underlies the genetic diversity commonly observed during tumor development333. Genomic stability can be preserved in lung cancer by targeting USP24, which ignites autophagy-dependent pathways, reduces PDL1 expression, and suppresses drug resistance, indicating that nuclear genome maintenance through autophagy is a critical determinant of therapeutic response124. Supraphysiologic testosterone promotes nucleophagy in prostate cancer cells, so the nuclear DNA is released, activating cytosolic nucleic acid sensors and NF-κB related immune signaling pathways, and the tumor growth is hindered because of enhanced antitumor immune responses334. Conversely, in some cases, nucleophagy is conducive to tumor growth; hence, this kind of nucleophagy ought to be inhibited. In small cell lung cancer, high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) strengthens chemoresistance by promoting PARP1 PARylation and propelling PARP1–LC3 interactions, which facilitates nucleophagy to promote PARP1 removal from DNA damage sites and sustain genome maintenance under therapeutic stress335. Under genotoxic stress, selective autophagy has been reported to participate in DNA damage processing directly by mediating the nuclear export and lysosomal degradation of TOP1–DNA cleavage complexes, revealing that a nucleophagy-related mechanism supports DNA repair and cancer cell survival336.

The mechanisms mentioned above are all potential therapeutic targets for cancer therapy in the future. However, despite the growing mechanistic understanding of nucleophagy in tumor progression and treatment response, strategies that exploit nanotechnology to selectively modulate nucleophagy in tumors remain largely unexplored. Therefore, the development of nanoparticles to precisely target nucleophagy represents an advanced direction for future cancer treatment.

Targeting autophagy with nanoparticles to overcome tumor resistance

Tumor drug resistance is the ability of cancer cells to adapt and survive despite the presence of therapeutic agents through genetic mutations or non-mutational mechanisms. This resistance often leads to treatment failure and potential disease relapse337. Nanomaterials are able to modulate autophagy through many pathways to suppress cancer growth.

Protective autophagy-driven resistance: Lysosome-dependent survival and nanoparticle-mediated autophagy inhibition

Autophagy is crucial in tumor resistance, as it can clean damaged organelles, recycle necessary substances, and maintain cellular homeostasis to enhance cancer cells’ adaptation and survival under stressful conditions such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy338. As mentioned above, autophagy does promote tumor resistance through many mechanisms. Inhibition of lysosomal function is widely recognized to assist cancer therapy, which means the lysosomal degradation stage of autophagy contributes to tumor survival, especially drug resistance339. On the contrary, the inhibition of autophagy-related genes including TFEB and ATG5, cooperating with the disruption of autophagosome-lysosome fusion, contributes to the reduction of therapeutic resistance in glioblastoma340.

Since autophagy can induce tumor drug resistance, inhibiting this type of autophagy is a useful strategy to promote cancer therapy sensitivity. Some nanoparticles aim to interfere with autophagy-lysosome fusion in the autophagy process. A glutathione (GSH)-responsive nanoplatform (C-A-D NPs) competes with tumor cells to keep them sensitive to cisplatin by impeding the autophagy–lysosome pathway, thus preventing tumor cells from entering a reduced-energy hibernation phase341. By increasing lysosomal pH, a targeted gene/drug co-delivery nanoplatform also hinders autophagosome-lysosome fusion (causing autophagic flux blockade), hampering autophagy and then curtailing the growth of Taxol-resistant lung cancer cells (A549/T)342. Additionally, under physiological pH, a small-molecule conjugate PBC targeting the lysosome can spontaneously organize into nanoparticles and intelligently convert into nanofibrils within lysosomes, causing autophagy inhibition and lysosomal impairment. The activation of PBC by light enables the inhibition of autophagy-driven resistance commonly present in phototherapy343. Activated by light, pyropheophorbide a-bisaminoquinoline conjugate lipid nanoparticles (PPBC LNPs) accumulate in lysosomes, trigger lysosomal membrane permeabilization, and inhibit autophagy, which helps overcome therapy resistance and significantly suppresses tumor growth in bladder cancer267. Autophagy, as a cause of oxaliplatin (OXA) resistance in colorectal cancer (CRC), is inhibited by a nanodelivery system consisting of oxaliplatin/berbamine-coloaded and ATB0,+-targeted nanoparticles ((O + B)@Trp-NPs) also by restraining the fusion process to overcome resistance to OXA344.

Autophagy-deficient and immune-evasive resistance: Restoring autophagic flux and immunogenic cell death

The functional involvement of autophagy in cellular processes is intricate, and the effects are determined by the tumor type and the cellular environment. For example, although autophagy can cause immune escape in some cases if controlled properly in specific situations, it can still cooperate with the immune system. Autophagy, working together with the CD98-mTOR axis, is able to strengthen CD8+ T cells’ function against immunotherapy resistance in mice345. In myeloma, the reduction of autophagy and changes in Golgi morphology caused by GABARAP deficiency can block the ICD signal transduction, leading to bortezomib (a proteasome inhibitor) resistance. This means autophagy is crucial to avoid resistance in myeloma346. Inhibitor of DNA binding 1 (ID1), a stemness regulator, induces drug resistance in some tumors347,348. Furthermore, Etk and Src are promising targets to overcome drug resistance in prostate cancer, and a dual inhibitor of the two enzymes named CTA095 can stimulate autophagy to induce cell death349.

Under the complicated environment inside and outside cancer cells, some nanoparticles aim to induce autophagy and successfully tackle the problem of tumor resistance. A large number of nanoparticles induce autophagy and immune responses72,350. The augmentation of autophagy caused by zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 nanoparticles carrying STF62247 (STF62247@ZIF-8/PEG-FA) promotes ICD in remaining cancer cells after incomplete radiofrequency ablation (IRFA) and suppresses residual tumors from growing80 (Fig. 7). Besides immune mechanisms, the promotion of autophagy can conquer tumor resistance via other ways. A bimetallic metal-organic framework (MOF)-based biomimetic nanoplatform (AFMMB) also triggers various reactions, such as suppressing DNA methylation and activating RNA methylation-mediated degradation of programmed cell death protein ligand transcripts after autophagy is induced to interfere with the growth and metastasis of leukemic blasts (LBs) that are resistant to immunotherapy351. The accumulation of the natural nanocarrier apoferritin (AFt-Cu1-4), especially AFt-Cu1 and AFt-Cu3 promote autophagy, while AFt-Cu2 and AFt-Cu4 can cause oncosis after entering the nucleus, which all have significant therapeutic outcomes in multidrug-resistant colon cancer352. Also, bortezomib (BTZ) encapsulated nanoparticles, functionalized on the surface with BCMA antibodies (BCMA-BTZ-NPs), can selectively accumulate in tumor sites, triggering autophagy and ultimately decreasing tumor cells in multiple myeloma resistant to BTZ353. Non-coding RNAs are also considered important regulators of autophagy, and nanomaterials designed to modulate ncRNAs have emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy356. The role of various non-coding RNAs in autophagy modulation varies across cancer types.

Challenges in clinical transition

Nevertheless, challenges still exist in the clinical transition of nanoparticles. The potential toxicity that nanoparticles exert on organs cannot be ignored. The liver is the most targeted organ of AgNPs. In mice fed a high-fat diet, exposure to AgNPs intensified hepatic lipid accumulation and inflammatory responses tremendously, accompanied by elevated oxidative stress and alterations in epigenetic regulation369. AgNPs exposure also leads to ultrastructural changes in hepatocytes370, as well as the elevation of serum hepatic injury biomarkers371. The damage to the liver mainly results from phagocytosis by mononuclear phagocyte system cells, and the nanoparticles need to be designed elaborately to conquer this barrier372. The toxicities of nanoparticles originate from the disruption of the microbiota-gut-brain axis373 and lysosomal dysfunction374 as well. Beyond toxicity, the delivery of nanomaterials relies heavily on the microenvironment, while the conditions are changing in human bodies, making it harder for nanoparticles to target precisely regardless of their potential to reach specific tissues. In vivo, the complex biological environments, including electrolytes and proteins, as well as the specificity of the tumor microenvironment, lower the efficiency of nanoparticles to accumulate and penetrate into action sites375,376. Though breakthroughs have been made, the existence of physiological and pathological barriers is still a core challenge for the effective utilization of nanomaterials. For instance, the blood-brain barrier remains a major obstruction and requires special designs of nanoparticles377. In cancer, the vascular basement membrane appears as a harsh mechanical barrier, which can trap nanoparticles in the subendothelial void378. Strategies like using hyperthermia to promote the aggregation and inflammation of platelets378 and utilizing nitric oxide to trigger the opening of endothelial junctions379 are possible solutions but require further confirmation. Additional intratumoral barriers, including abnormal tumor vasculature, dense stroma, and abundant stromal cells, further impede the efficient delivery of nanoparticles380.

One of the biggest barriers to the clinical translation of nanomaterial-based autophagy regulation strategies is the highly complex and dual nature of their autophagy-modulating effects, which constrains the realization of precise, safe, and reproducible therapeutic outcomes in humans. The efficacy of nanomaterials targeting autophagy is coupled to the dynamic behaviors in vivo, including biodistribution, cellular uptake routes, and subcellular trafficking, all of which can differ substantially across physiological and pathological contexts in different patients and disease conditions31. From a translational perspective, maintaining robust batch-to-batch consistency is a fundamental requirement. However, the minor variations in particle size, surface chemistry, or assembly architecture may translate into disproportionate changes in the magnitude or direction of autophagy regulation381. Moreover, inter-individual heterogeneity further complicates therapeutic predictability, as variations in metabolic state, immune background, and disease stage can elicit markedly different biological responses to the same nanomaterial-based autophagy strategy382.

Among the clinical trials conducted each year, failed studies remind researchers that the clinical translation of nanoparticles still has a long way to go. As mentioned above, safety issues are important barriers to tackle. MRX34, a microRNA miR-34 liposomal injection, was developed to treat hepatocellular carcinoma primarily (NCT01829971)368. However, the trial (Phase I) was halted because one-fifth of the patients experienced severe immune-related adverse events in 2016383. Low efficiency is another main challenge. In spite of the success gained in prostate cancer (Phase II)384, BIND-014 (docetaxel nanoparticles for injectable suspension) failed in head and neck cancer (NCT02479178) in the Phase II trial368. The failure of BIND-014 was attributed to an inappropriate choice of cytotoxic payload, which limited therapeutic efficacy despite effective nanoparticle-mediated tumor targeting, ultimately falling short of the clinical benefits385. Therefore, efforts should be paid before the successful clinical utilization of nanomaterials.

Conclusions and perspectives

Autophagy is considered a unique cell survival pathway, with complex but subtle regulation mechanisms, maintaining a delicate balance in normal cells. In recent years, treating cancer through modulating autophagy has been studied deeply386, 387, 388. However, according to the existing literature, the effects of treating cancer via a single pathway are not satisfactory, so the combination of autophagy and other therapeutic methods has become a trend when it comes to application. Regulating autophagy through nanotherapeutic strategies offers a promising approach for cancer treatment. With emphasis on the therapeutic potential, this review explores how nanomaterials exert bidirectional effects on autophagy. The underlying mechanisms by which nanoparticles modulate autophagy nanoparticles modulate autophagy to either facilitate or suppress tumor cell growth, proliferation, and drug resistance, underscoring their complex and diverse role in clinical applications. Among the many ways to regulate autophagy, autophagy can be promoted through oxidative stress and endoplasmic reticulum stress, while it can be inhibited in energy-related metabolism and through lysosomal damage. Due to the complexity of autophagy, some mechanisms can both promote and inhibit autophagy in different tumors and situations. So far, although nanoparticles targeting autophagy can function effectively in some circumstances, no clinical application is available, and no standardized application and therapeutic protocol has been approved for nanotechnology for cancer treatment, partly because of the intricacy of autophagy. Beyond biological complexity, real-world barriers also substantially limit the clinical transition. In particular, the large-scale manufacturing of nanomaterials capable of modulating autophagy faces persistent challenges in ensuring reproducibility, batch-to-batch consistency, and long-term physicochemical stability. Minor variations in particle size, surface chemistry, or assembly architecture may lead to disproportionate changes in autophagy-regulatory outcomes, making quality control more complicated. Moreover, regulatory approval of nanomedicines remains demanding, as standardized frameworks for evaluating safety, pharmacokinetics, as well as the efficacy of multifunctional nanoplatforms remain to be further evolved.

The integration of autophagy modulation and nanotechnology provides innovative strategies for cancer treatment. Nevertheless, challenges remain in the precise design of nanoparticles, the dynamic autophagic regulation, and the translation of these approaches into clinical applications. A central unresolved question lies in whether nanomaterials should function as autophagy ‘drivers’ or ‘brakes’. This decision must be guided by contextual precision, which demands rigorous stratification of patients through multimodal biomarkers, including tumor staging profiles (e.g., TNM classification) and real-time autophagy flux quantitation (e.g., LC3-II/p62 ratio). First, the main challenge that remains in the area is the clinical translation of nanomaterials in autophagy modulation for cancer treatment. In particular, although circRNAs have been proved to engage in autophagy regulation132,389 and nanocarrier-based circRNA delivery has been increasingly explored390,391, nanomaterial-mediated delivery of circRNAs specifically targeting autophagy for cancer therapy remains at a very early stage, with only limited direct evidence currently available. This gap further underscores the translational challenges in this field. Further investigations are required for a deeper understanding of certain issues, including nanoparticle stability, targeted delivery efficiency, potential off-target toxicity, and nanoparticles' exact pharmacokinetics inside the body. Besides, the synthesis of nanoparticles and the optimization of dosage lack standardized protocols, restricting their large-scale clinical application. Only by overcoming these challenges can the potential of nanomaterial-mediated autophagy modulation in clinical applications be released. Second, with the research on the spatiotemporal effect getting deeper, more attention should be paid to the spatiotemporal effect when using nanomaterials to regulate autophagy. Different tumor stages (temporal factor) and tumor microenvironments (spatial factor) can affect the role autophagy plays in cancer progression. Therefore, at what stage of cancer and at what site in cancer the nanoparticles are delivered can result in enhancing or inhibiting autophagy, influencing cancer therapy outcomes, which calls for detailed studies on the complex mechanisms of the whole process and probable therapeutic effects. Third, combination therapy is the future trend of cancer treatment. Although nanomaterials targeting autophagy cooperating with other therapeutic methods like phototherapy, chemodynamic therapy and immunotherapy have achieved some effects, the future of combinatorial therapies lies in their ability to synergistically address multiple aspects of tumor biology. Furthermore, the relationships between autophagy-based nanoparticle modulation and other advanced therapeutic methods need to be illustrated more clearly.

The fast development of artificial intelligence (AI) casts new light on the research392,393. Equipped with the robust ability to process large amounts of data quickly, identifying complex patterns and other advantages, AI will accelerate the development speed of nanotechnology's usage in cancer treatment by analyzing vast tumor datasets to optimize nanomaterial design and clinical strategies based on autophagy. In particular, AI-driven models can integrate multi-omics data, live-cell imaging, and temporal treatment-response profiles to characterize autophagy flux rather than relying solely on static autophagy markers, thereby distinguishing between protective and cytotoxic autophagy under different therapeutic contexts394. Such data-guided analyses may further enable the rational optimization of nanomaterial design, including drug loading, release kinetics, and lysosome-targeting properties, in accordance with tumor-specific autophagy dependencies. In addition, AI-assisted modeling could facilitate the rational design of sequential or combinational therapeutic strategies, for example by determining whether transient autophagy inhibition should precede immune activation or whether controlled autophagy induction is required to sensitize tumors prior to cytotoxic intervention394. Besides the application of AI in future research, another noteworthy consideration is that current research predominantly focuses on only a single pathway, like autophagy regulation, with limited attention given to their impact on other signaling pathways in one study. Multiple pathways and axes are involved in cancer treatment, and they cooperate and interact with each other to prevent cancer development. Future studies should not only explore how nanoparticles modulate autophagy, but also pay more attention to the influence of other critical pathways during the same therapeutic process, such as ferroptosis, apoptosis, and calcium overload. Additionally, whether nanoparticles exert their effects on cells via the same molecules and signaling pathways needs further discussion.

In conclusion, although the regulation of autophagy through nanomaterials faces several challenges, as our understanding of the complexity of autophagy and its interactions with nanomaterials deepens, and as integrative strategies incorporating rational nanomaterial design, predictive modeling, and data-driven approaches continue to advance, clinical applications may eventually become feasible, ultimately transforming the landscape of cancer treatment.
