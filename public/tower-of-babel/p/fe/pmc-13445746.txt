Purple sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas L. Lam) leaves extract prevents weight gain and lipotoxicity in mice fed a high-fat diet by increasing metabolic flexibility and oxidative metabolism in skeletal muscle and brown adipose tissue
Authors: Claudia Delgadillo-Puga, Lilia G Noriega, Yonatan Y Cariño-Cervantes, Claudia Tovar-Palacio, Luis Cisneros-Zevallos, Jorge Barrios-Payan, Iván Torre-Villalvazo, Yesica R Cruz-Martínez, Mario Cuchillo-Hilario, Andrea Torres, Arturo Navarro-Ocaña

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPurple sweet potato leaves are a rich source of caffeoylquinic acids (CQA) and related compounds with potential metabolic benefits. Our previous research demonstrated that 5-CQA and 3,4-diCQA increase mitochondrial respiration in primary hepatocytes. To explore the translational relevance of purple sweet potato leaves extract (PSPLE) in the management and treatment of obesity, we evaluated the effect of PSPLE in C57BL/6 mice fed a high-fat (HF) diet supplemented with either 1% or 3% PSPLE (w/w) and assessed insulin secretion in INS-1E cells.MethodsPSPLE phenolic compounds were identified by LC-MS. Male C57BL/6J mice were fed (1) a Control, (2) high-fat (HF) diet, (3) HF diet supplemented with 1% and (4) HF diet supplemented with 3% of PSPLE for 15 weeks. Body composition, energy expenditure, glucose and insulin tolerance, serum metabolites, and tissue morphology were evaluated. AMPK activation was analyzed in the liver and skeletal muscle. Complementary, β-cell function was assessed in vitro.ResultsLC–MS revealed that PSPLE contained abundant CQA derivatives (5-CQA, caffeic acid, 3,4-di-CQA, 3,5-di-CQA, 4,5-di-CQA, 4 F-5CQA, and 3,4,5-tri-CQA). The HF + 1% PSPLE diet attenuated weight gain and adipocyte hypertrophy, increased brown adipose UCP-1 expression, and prevented hepatic steatosis. AMPK phosphorylation was enhanced in the liver and muscle, paralleling higher oxygen consumption and energy expenditure. Although PSPLE stimulated β-cell metabolism and insulin secretion in vitro, in vivo glucose tolerance showed only modest improvement, likely reflecting the multiple regulatory inputs on insulin secretion under physiological conditions.ConclusionsPSPLE enhanced metabolic flexibility and oxidative capacity in HF-fed mice by activating AMPK-dependent pathways in muscle, liver, and adipose tissue. The higher efficacy of the 1% PSPLE indicates a hormetic response, where low polyphenol exposure triggers adaptive mitochondrial and metabolic activation, on the contrary, higher doses offer no further benefits. These results present PSPLE as a sustainable, polyphenol-rich food ingredient with potential to combat obesity-related metabolic dysfunction.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-026-01144-x.Keywords: Ipomoea batatas, LC-MS, Caffeoylquinic acids, Metabolic flexibility, INS-1E

Background

According to the World Health Organization (WHO 2024), over 2.5 billion adults worldwide are overweight, including about 890 million living with obesity, a prevalence that has nearly doubled since 1990. This increase is no longer limited to high-income regions; low-and middle-income countries now experience the fastest prevalence growth rates [1]. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO 2024) also warns that global obesity could reach 1.2 billion adults by 2030. Meanwhile, the number of people with diabetes exceeds 800 million, most of whom live in countries experiencing rapid nutritional transition [2]. These current trends emphasize the urgent need for sustainable nutritional strategies capable of improving metabolic health and preventing obesity-related disorders. Global food and nutrition systems face mounting challenges due to climate change, population growth, and changing dietary preferences. Consequently, there is increasing interest in exploring alternative and sustainable food sources such as underutilized crops and undervalued plant species.

The sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas L.) is native to southeastern Mexico and belongs to the Convolvulaceae family [3]. Due to its great plasticity, it has adapted well all over the world: Asia, Oceania, Africa and recently in the United States and Europe [4]. The leading producers and consumers are Asian and African countries [5]. Today, sweet potato is the seventh most important crop worldwide due to its exceptional nutritional properties and health-promoting compounds; e.g. carotenoids, peptides, and polyphenols [6]. It is one of the foods richest in β-carotene and chlorogenic acids. The root pulp contains β-carotene, acylated anthocyanins, and some flavonoids, depending on the color. The leaves contain the same components, but in higher amounts, except for carbohydrates [7]. Sweet potato leaves contain a total phenol content of 27.45 to 243.82 mg chlorogenic acid equivalents (CAE)/g DW [8], and approximately 70% of these are derivatives of caffeoylquinic acid [9]. Among the caffeoylquinic acid derivatives (CQAs) isolated from sweet potato leaves are chlorogenic acid, 3,4-di-CQA, 3,5-di-CQA, 4,5-di-CQA, 3,4,5-tri-CQA, in addition to feruloyl-caffeoylquinic acid derivatives [10]. These bioactive compounds have numerous biological properties, including antioxidants, antimutagenic, antihypertensive, anti-inflammatory, anti-obesogenic, and antidiabetic effects. Previous studies have described that CQAs derivatives influence disorders of glucose and lipid metabolism, since these compounds have been shown to have a modulatory role in hepatic peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR-α) and in turn stimulate AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activity, reducing fatty acids, triglycerides, cholesterol, and steatosis in the liver, in addition to the secretion of adipocytes and the suppression of glucose-6-phosphatase (G-6-pase) [11].

Other studies indicate that CQAs stimulate the protein kinase B (AKT) and AMPK pathways in skeletal muscle, leading to increased glucose uptake via glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) translocation and improved oxidative metabolism. These actions converge to improve metabolic flexibility and prevent lipid accumulation, underscoring the relevance of CQA-rich foods, such as sweet potato leaves, as potential dietary interventions for obesity-related insulin resistance and dyslipidemia.

We recently investigated how 5-CQA, 3,4-diCQA, and 4,5-diCQA affect mitochondrial activity in primary hepatocytes, using an extracellular flux analyzer. Our results showed that maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity were highest when cells were treated with 5-CQA and 3,4-diCQA, indicating improved mitochondrial function. Moreover, 3,4-diCQA notably increased glycolytic reserve [12]. To explore the translational relevance of PSPLE in the management and treatment of obesity, we evaluated the effect of PSPLE on metabolic flexibility in C57BL/6 mice fed a high-fat diet supplemented with 1% or 3% PSPLE (w/w) and assessed insulin secretion in INS-1E cells. We hypothesize that PSPLE improves mitochondrial activity in metabolic tissues such as the liver, skeletal muscle, and brown adipose tissue (BAT) in a live organism, preventing obesity-related metabolic alterations caused by a high-fat (HF) diet, and in insulin-releasing INS-1E cells, promoting oxidative metabolism and insulin secretion.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The purple sweet potato accession with the highest caffeoylquinic acid derivative profile was previously selected [13]. These were obtained from the Center for the Conservation of Subtropical Species of the National Plant Genetic Resources System (SINAREFI), SNICS-SAGARPA, located in Harinas de Coatepec, State of Mexico.

Extraction of phenolic compounds from purple sweet potato leaves (PSPL)

The extraction of purple sweet potato leaves (PSPL) was obtained from a single batch, in which 800 g of lyophilized PSPL were suspended in 5 L of a mixture of acetone: water (40:60) containing 1% lactic acid (> 98%) and sonicated at 40 °C for 20 min. Later, the extract was concentrated under reduced pressure and resuspended in water for a liquid-liquid extraction with n-hexane (6 L) and ethyl acetate (6 L). The organic fraction was recovered and concentrated under reduced pressure. The extract was stored at 4 °C until use.

LC-MS analysis of purple sweet potato leaves extract (PSPLE)

The phenolic composition of the purple sweet potato leaves extract (PSPLE) was characterized using an Agilent 6410 Triple Quadrupole Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS) system with electrospray ionization (ESI). Separation was performed on a Hypersil-Gold column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) at 25 °C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) water/acetonitrile/formic acid in a ratio of 89:10:1 v/v and (B) acetonitrile with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The elution gradient consisted of using the mobile phase A from 97% to 75% over 45 min, followed by 70% of A for 1 min, then returning to 97% of A after 9 min. MS detection was carried out in negative ion mode, scanning m/z 200–2000. The MS parameters included a capillary voltage of 4000 V, nitrogen nebulizer gas at 50 psi, a dry gas flow of 12 L/min, and a source heater temperature of 350 °C. Full scan MS and zoom scans of specific m/z ions (179, 353, 515, 529, 677) were performed with a collision energy of 15 eV.

Evaluation of insulin secretion in INS-1E cells

INS-1E cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, 2 mM glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 10 mM HEPES, 50 µM 2-mercaptoethanol, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin, in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. To evaluate insulin secretion, INS-1E cells were seeded on 24-well poly-L-ornithine-coated plates. After 48 h, cells were treated with 5 or 10 µM chlorogenic acid equivalents (CAE) of the PSPLE for two hours, as previously reported [14, 15]. Insulin secretion was reported as the percentage of insulin released into the media relative to the total insulin content.

Analysis of glycolytic rate in INS-1E cells

We assessed the effect of the PSPLE on the glycolytic rate of INS-1E cells using an induced glycolytic rate assay (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with a Seahorse XF96 extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Briefly, INS-1E cells were seeded on a 96-well Seahorse XF96 plate and treated with the extract as previously described. Afterwards, the cells were washed with XF RPMI medium (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) supplemented with 2.5 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, and 2 mM glutamine (pH = 7.4) and then incubated in this medium for 1 h at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator. During the experiment, glucose (2.5 or 25 mM), rotenone plus antimycin A (0.9 µM, inhibitors of complex I and III), and 2-deoxyglucose (50 mM), a non-metabolizable glucose analogue that inhibits the initial step of glycolysis, were sequentially injected. Three measurements of the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were performed under basal conditions and following the addition of the extract. Basal, induced, and compensatory glycolysis rates were calculated from the report generated by Wave software 2.6.1 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). ECAR data were normalized by counting nuclei stained with Hoechst 33,342 using the Cytation 1 platform with Gen5 software (Bio Tek, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Animals, experimental design and diets

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals [16] and complied with Institutional and National guidelines for the ethical use of animals for experimentation. The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Committee for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (CICUAL) of the Instituto Nacional de Ciencias Médicas y Nutrición Salvador Zubirán (INCMNSZ) under the registration number CICUAL-NAN-2037-21-23-1. Experimental procedures were conducted to minimize animal discomfort and to use the minimum number of animals required to achieve scientific validity. Twenty-four 5-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (21–23 g), obtained from the Department of Experimental Research and Animal Care of the INCMNSZ, were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6). Group sizes were established using power calculations informed by prior metabolic studies under comparable conditions. These analyses indicated that the selected sample size achieves sufficient power (> 0.8 at α = 0.05) to detect 20–25% differences in physiological and molecular parameters. Each group received one of the following dietary interventions for 15 weeks: (1) Control diet, (2) high-fat (HF) diet, (3) HF + 1% PSPLE, and (4) HF + 3% PSPLE (diet composition was based on the AIN guidelines [17] and included in the Supplementary Table 1. The PSPLE was homogeneously mixed into the diets at 1% and 3% (w/w). Diets were provided in dry form. Mice were housed at 23 °C with a 12/12 h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. Food intake was recorded every other day.

Evaluation of body composition and energy expenditure

Lean and fat mass were measured at week 13 using magnetic resonance imaging (EchoMRI; Echo Medical Systems, Houston, TX, USA). Energy expenditure and substrate utilization were assessed at week 14 by indirect calorimetry in an Oxymax Comprehensive Laboratory Animal Monitoring System (CLAMS), (Columbus, OH, USA) as described in Delgadillo-Puga et al. [18]. Briefly, mice were acclimatized for six hours in the CLAMS cages before data collection. The animals were food deprived for eight hours for fasting recordings and then fed their respective diets for the following 18 h. Oxygen (O2) consumption and carbon dioxide (CO2) production were continuously measured throughout the test. The respiratory exchange ratio (RER) was calculated as the volume of CO2 exhaled (VCO2, mL·kg − 1·h − 1) divided by the volume of O2 consumed (VO2, mL·kg − 1·h − 1).

Evaluation of glucose and insulin tolerance

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance tests (ipGTT) and insulin tolerance tests (ipITT) were conducted during weeks 11 and 12, respectively. Mice were deprived of food for six hours before each test. The ipGTT was started with an intraperitoneal injection of 2 g/kg of glucose, and the ipITT with an intraperitoneal injection of 0.5 UI/kg of insulin. Blood glucose levels were measured from the lateral tail veins at 0, 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min after injection using a portable FreeStyle glucometer (Abbott, Mexico City, Mexico), following the method described by Delgadillo-Puga et al. [18].

Euthanasia, tissue, and blood collection

Following a 15-week study period, mice underwent a 6-hour food deprivation before euthanasia by sevoflurane overdose (Fluoromethyl-2,2,2-trifluoro-1-(trifluoromethyl) ethyl ether). Blood was collected via portal venipuncture into serum-separating tubes and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting serum was stored at − 70 °C. Liver, pancreas, skeletal muscle (gastrocnemius and soleus), and subcutaneous, visceral, and brown adipose tissues (SAT, VAT, and BAT, respectively) were rapidly excised and divided into two fractions. One fraction was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 70 °C for protein abundance analysis. The other fraction was fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for histopathological examination.

Biochemical parameters

Serum glucose, triglycerides, and total cholesterol concentrations were measured using a COBAS C111 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics Ltd., Switzerland). Serum insulin levels were determined by ELISA (ALPCO, Salem, NH, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histological analysis of liver, pancreas, white and brown adipose tissue

Samples of liver, pancreas, and adipose tissues were fixed in absolute alcohol, dehydrated in xylene, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined under a Q-win Leica 500 morphometry microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). The areas of adipocytes and pancreatic islets were analyzed using Adiposoft software for ImageJ (ImageJ 1.52 K, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, ML, USA). The number of adipocytes and islets was counted in two sections of each tissue at different depths. Digital photographs of each section were captured at 20X magnification.

Abundance of mitochondria and lipid content in skeletal muscle

The abundance of mitochondria and lipid content in skeletal muscle was assessed as previously described by our group [19]. The gastrocnemius and soleus muscles were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT) and stored at −80 °C. Succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) activity was used to visualize the mitochondrial abundance. Briefly, frozen 12 μm sections were mounted onto positively charged slides and incubated with SDH staining solution containing nitro-blue tetrazolium and sodium succinate at 37 °C for 60 min. The slides were washed with deionized water and dehydrated sequentially in 30%, 60%, and 90% acetone for 2 min each, then rehydrated with 60% and 30% acetone in deionized water for 2 min each. Lipid infiltrates in myofibers were evaluated by Oil Red O (ORO) staining. Briefly, 9 μm frozen sections were mounted on positively charged slides, fixed in 10% cold formalin, dehydrated in propylene glycol, stained with 0.7% ORO in propylene glycol for 20 min, washed in deionized water, and counterstained with hematoxylin. Stained slides were mounted with aqueous mounting media. Digital images were captured at 20X magnification using a Leica DM750 microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Staining intensity was quantified with ImageJ by converting the image to 8-bit grayscale and measuring the grey intensity of each fibre.

Immunoblotting

Skeletal muscle and brown adipose tissue samples were homogenized with RIPA buffer (containing 1% IGEPAL, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 1 mM sodium fluoride, 2 mM sodium orthovanadate in phosphate-buffered saline) at 4 °C, supplemented with a tablet of phosphatase and protease inhibitors (PHOStop, Complete Mini, Roche Diagnostics, South San Francisco, CA, USA) using a TissueLyser II (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA). Subsequently, the samples were incubated on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 14, 000 rpm at 4 ° C for 15 min. The supernatants were aliquoted and stored at −70 °C until use. Total protein quantification was conducted using the Lowry method with the DC Protein Assay kit (Bio- Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Forty micrograms of protein were separated by electrophoresis on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels (SDS-PAGE) within an electrophoresis chamber (Bio- Rad) and then transferred onto 45 μm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Hybond- P, Amersham, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) using the Mini Trans- Blot cell (Bio- Rad). The membranes were blocked with 5% non- fat dry milk (Bio- Rad) for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in TBS-Tween. The primary antibodies included: PGC-1α (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA, Cat: ab191838, dilution 1: 750), AMPK (Abcam, Cat: ab23875, dilution 1: 750), p- AMPK (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA, Cat: 07–681, dilution 1: 1000), as well as the structural protein GAPDH (Abcam, Cat. ab181602, dilution 1: 20000) and UCP-1 (Abcam, Cat. ab155117, dilution 1: 1000). After incubation, the membranes were washed three times for 10 min with TBS-Tween and then incubated with the secondary antibody (anti- rabbit, Abcam, Cat. ab6721, dilution 1: 10000) for 1 h. The membranes were washed three times with TBS-Tween and finally visualized using a 1:1 mixture of substrate peroxidase solution and substrate luminol reagent (Millipore, Cat. WBKLS 0500). Images were acquired with the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System and processed with Image Lab 6.1 software (Bio-Rad). Results are expressed as a ratio of the protein of interest to the housekeeping protein (GAPDH), and for AMPK, as the ratio of p-AMPK to total AMPK. The Control group was arbitrarily assigned a value of 1 to serve as a reference for comparison with other conditions.

Statistical analyses

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. For normally distributed data, differences among groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. When data did not meet the assumptions of normality, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test. In vitro data were analyzed using unpaired two-tailed t-tests. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05. Different letters in figures indicates significant differences. All analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism version 9.0 GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA.

Results

Phenolic compounds characterization in purple sweet potato leaf extract (PSPLE)

The LC-MS report (Fig. 1, Supplementary Tables 1, and Supplementary Figs. 1–7) demonstrates that the PSPLE consists of 5-caffeoylquinic acid (5-CQA), caffeic acid (CA), 3,4-di-CQA (3,4-dicaffeoylquinic acid), 3,5-di-CQA (3,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid), 4,5-di-CQA (4,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid), 4 F-5CQA (4-feruloyl-5-caffeoylquinic acid), and 3,4,5-tri-CQA (3,4,5-tri-caffeoylquinic acid).

Effect of PSPLE on body mass, food intake, and body composition

Figure 2 shows the changes in body weight of mice fed different dietary treatments over 15 weeks. At the beginning of the study, all mice weighed approximately 25 ± 2 g. As expected, mice on a high-fat (HF) diet gained significantly more weight than Control (Fig. 2A), but the supplementation with either 1% or 3% PSPLE to the HF diet prevented the excess increase in body mass (p < 0.05, Fig. 2B). Control mice consumed about 3.7 g of food per day throughout the study period, which was significantly more than the HF (3.17 g/day) and HF + 3% PSPLE groups (3.10 g/day, Fig. 2C), respectively. However, when these results were expressed as energy intake, there was no statistical difference among groups (Fig. 2D). To assess whether differences in weight were due to increased body fat or lean mass, body composition was measured in all mice at week 13. Fat mass in HF + 1% PSPLE mice was similar to Control and lower than the HF or HF + 3% PSPLE groups (p < 0.05, Fig. 2E). Lean mass was also highest in HF + 1% PSPLE mice, comparable to all other groups (Fig. 2F). These results indicate that the HF diet increased fat mass in mice, and the reduced body weight of mice supplemented with 1% PSPLE was not due to decreased skeletal muscle mass but rather fat mass. These weight changes were unlikely due to differences in energy intake but perhaps due to improved energy expenditure.

Serum biochemical parameters, liver H&E staining and hepatic immunoblotting in mice fed with a high-fat diet supplemented with PSPLE

The effect of PSPLE on fasting glucose, cholesterol, and triglyceride serum levels is shown in Fig. 3A, C, D. High-fat diet consumption significantly increased (p < 0.05), these biochemical parameters compared to the Control group, including insulin levels (Fig. 3B). Notably, mice fed a high-fat (HF) diet supplemented with either 1% or 3% PSPLE showed values similar to the Control group. Effect of PSPLE on glycolytic rate and insulin secretion in vitro, and on glucose tolerance and pancreatic islet size in vivo

To evaluate whether the phenolics of the PSPLE influence β-cell bioenergetics and insulin secretion, we assayed glycolytic metabolism and glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) in INS-1E cells. The results showed that during the glycolytic rate assay (INS-1E), the PSPLE independently increased both basal and induced glycolysis in a dose-dependent manner under low glucose conditions (Fig. 4H-K). Furthermore, PSPLE significantly increased basal, induced, and compensatory glycolysis at both doses (1% and 3%) under high glucose conditions (Fig. 4H-K). Consequently, PSPLE enhanced insulin release dose-dependently under low glucose conditions and under high glucose conditions (Fig. 4G). These results suggest that PSPLE modulates β-cell glycolysis, thereby modulating GSIS.

To assess whether the PLSPLE also influences insulin secretion and sensitivity in vivo, we examined glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in mice fed a HF diet supplemented with 1% and 3% PSPLE. Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance tests (ipGTT) and insulin tolerance tests (ipITT) were performed during weeks 11 and 12, respectively. The results revealed a significant (p < 0.05) increase in the area under the curve (AUC) in high-fat-fed mice compared to Control in both ipGTT (Fig. 4A, B) and ipITT (Fig. 4C, D), indicating pronounced insulin resistance. Unexpectedly, adding 1% or 3% PSPLE to the high-fat diet did not improve glucose tolerance (Fig. 4B). However, mice supplemented with 1% PSPLE showed a slight reduction in AUC during the ipITT (Fig. 4D). Notably, we also observed an increase in pancreatic islet area in HF-fed mice (Fig. 4E, F), and supplementation with 1% or 3% PSPLE resulted in a non-significant reduction in islet size. These findings suggest that 1% PSPLE supplementation marginally enhances insulin sensitivity in mice fed a high-fat diet, highlighting that although in vitro increased GSIS occurs, the in vivo mechanisms of insulin secretion involve many other factors that diminish the effect of PSLP (Fig. 4H).

Effect of the PSPLE on energy expenditure and mitochondrial activity in skeletal muscle

To evaluate whether the reduced body weight of the mice supplemented with 1% PSPLE fed a HF diet was due to increased energy expenditure, we assessed oxygen consumption and the respiratory exchange ratio (RER) at the 13th week. Mice fed the HF diet had lower oxygen consumption than Control, indicating reduced mitochondrial activity. Interestingly, mice fed the HF diet supplemented with 1% PSPLE increased oxygen consumption by approximately 6% (p < 0.05, Fig. 5A, C) compared to those fed solely the HF. The HF diet also reduced the RER during feeding, indicating a decreased capacity and plasticity to switch energy substrates from fat to glucose during the feeding period. Again, the PSPLE supplementation elevated it by approximately 4% (p < 0.05, Fig. 5D, E), suggesting improved metabolic flexibility. To evaluate the muscle fiber oxidative capacity, we measured mitochondrial activity using SDH staining. Results showed a significant (p < 0.05) increase of approximately 30 relative units (RU) in oxidative capacity when 1% PSPLE was added to HF-fed mice compared to the HF-fed group (Fig. 5G, H). To understand the molecular mechanisms behind this increase in mitochondrial activity, we examined AMPK and PGC-1α protein levels. As shown in Fig. 5J, PGC-1α levels were lower in HF-fed mice compared to the Control. Supplementation with PSPLE was not statistically different (p > 0.05) from the HF group. However, both treatments increased AMPK phosphorylation, showing more favorable outcomes than the HF diet alone. The increase in mitochondrial activity was accompanied by a reduction in lipid content when 1% or 3% PSPLE were added to HF diets (Fig. 5I, L), indicating enhanced skeletal muscle oxidative capacity. This effect seems to result from the activation of AMPK by phenolic compounds present in the PSPLE, as previously reported [20].

Effect of the PSPLE on subcutaneous, visceral and brown adipose tissue cytoarchitecture and UCP-1 protein abundance in brown adipose tissue

Adipose tissue actively participates in energy homeostasis regulation by storing surplus calories as triglycerides in their lipid vacuoles within subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissue (SAT and VAT, respectively), and by oxidizing excess lipids through brown adipose tissue (BAT). However, chronic consumption of high-fat (HF) diets impairs WAT and BAT functions, causing adipocyte hypertrophy and hypoplasia, which leads to dysregulated energy metabolism. Consistently, H&E staining showed enlarged adipocytes on SAT and VAT of mice fed with HF diet (Fig. 6A–F). Interestingly, PSPLE supplementation significantly reduced adipocyte sizes in both depots compared to HF (p < 0.05). HF diets also reduce brown adipose tissue (BAT) function, as evidenced by enlarged lipid vacuoles. In contrast, PSPLE supplementation decreased BAT vacuole size while increasing UCP-1 expression (Fig. 6J, K), indicating heightened uncoupled lipid oxidation (p < 0.05, Fig. 6G–I). Notably, the higher effect was observed with the 1% PSPLE dose, indicating a dose-dependent response, where the lower dose provided better metabolic protection against HF diet-induced metabolic derangements. These results suggest that the phenolic compounds in PSPLE can prevent white adipocyte hypertrophy and browning of brown adipocytes, maintain adequate lipid oxidation while promoting whole-body energy balance, thereby preventing obesity and metabolic disturbances.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the metabolic effects of a purple sweet potato leaves extract (PSPLE) in a preclinical obesity model. PSPLE, rich in caffeoylquinic acid derivatives (CQAs), prevented excessive weight gain, decreased adiposity, enhanced hepatic lipid metabolism, and boosted whole-body oxidative capacity without effect on food intake. These effects were linked to AMPK activation in skeletal muscle and liver, increased UCP-1 expression in brown adipose tissue (BAT), and improved mitochondrial function, supporting higher energy expenditure and improving metabolic flexibility.

The LC–MS analysis showed that PSPLE contains many CQAs, including 3,4-diCQA, 3,5-diCQA, 4,5-diCQA, and 3,4,5-triCQA, along with chlorogenic acid and minor flavonoids such as luteolin-7-O-glucoside. Our findings agree with those reported by Zhang et al. [21], who identified 11 compounds in an ethyl acetate leaves extract (with a polarity similar to that of the extract used in this study). Specifically, 3,4-diCQA, 3,5-diCQA, 4,5-diCQA, and 3,4,5-triCQA were found at concentrations of 22.89, 229.1, 16.51, and 21.62 mg/g of dried extract, respectively. These compounds are produced by plant species from the Solanaceae, Asteraceae, Rubiaceae, and Convolvulaceae families, with the latter including Ipomoea batatas L. Lam. These compounds participate in plant biological functions, such as resistance to pests and diseases, and responses to biotic and abiotic stress [22]. CQAs are well-known activators of energy-sensing and antioxidant pathways, enhancing insulin sensitivity through AMPK and AKT activation, increasing glucose uptake via GLUT4 translocation, and downregulating hepatic lipogenic genes (SREBP1c, FASN) [23]. They also promote β-oxidation and mitochondrial biogenesis. These mechanisms, demonstrated in different models [12, 24–26], provide a solid biochemical basis for the metabolic benefits observed on PSPLE. To assess the metabolic effect of this phenolic extract, we selected two concentrations based on previous studies involving phenolic-rich plant extracts fed to high-fat diet mice, where 1–3% dietary inclusion delivered bioactive yet non-toxic polyphenol levels while effectively modulating metabolism. These doses result in an approximate daily intake of 1–3 g extract per kg of body weight, corresponding to plasma concentrations in the low micromolar range like the 5–10 µM used in vitro [18, 19, 27, 28].

Despite the changes in body weight, PSPLE supplementation elicited significant improvements in adiposity, adipocyte morphology, and markers of thermogenic activity. The 1% PSPLE diet reduced visceral and subcutaneous fat mass, decreased adipocyte hypertrophy, and increased UCP-1 expression in brown adipose tissue (BAT), consistent with enhanced lipid turnover and thermogenesis (Fig. 6J, C). These findings align with previous reports from Ju et al. [29], who demonstrated that purple sweet potato extract (PSPE) increased hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) activity, promoting triglyceride hydrolysis to release free fatty acids and glycerol from adipocytes. Our results also agree with the reports from Lee et al. [27], which showed that oral administration of PSPE (100 mg/kg/day) in HF-fed mice promoted energy expenditure through adipose browning, upregulating PGC1-α and UCP-1 in white adipose tissue (WAT) while reducing adipocyte hypertrophy and hepatic steatosis. Our data demonstrate increased UCP-1 expression in BAT and reduced adipocyte hypertrophy in visceral adipose tissue (VAT) with 1% PSPLE supplementation. This may partly explain why higher PSPLE supplementation (3% versus 1%) was less effective at preventing excessive fat deposition in subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) and VAT (Fig. 6A, B, D, E). Together, these effects indicate that PSPLE improves lipid utilization and storage efficiency, preventing ectopic lipid accumulation and contributing to overall metabolic flexibility.

Chlorogenic acid has been shown to reduce body weight and adiposity without altering food intake, potentially through increased postprandial energy expenditure and decreased respiratory quotient [29]. These results align with our observations (Figs. 2A-E), where 1% PSPLE decreased body weight and fat deposition in HF-fed mice without affecting food intake.

Skeletal muscle is a major factor in maintaining systemic energy balance. The primary regulator of skeletal muscle energy metabolism is AMPK, which, upon phosphorylation, stimulates energy-generating processes such as glucose uptake and metabolism, mitochondrial biogenesis, and activity, resulting in enhanced oxidative metabolism [30]. Several phenolic compounds can activate and phosphorylate AMPK in skeletal muscle, boosting glucose uptake and oxidation [31]. AMPK activation occurs after translation and can enhance oxidation independently of PGC-1α transcription. Our current results show that caffeoylquinic acid derivatives from PSPLE can also boost AMPK phosphorylation in skeletal muscle, enhancing overall energy expenditure and metabolic flexibility. PSPLE increased SDH activity and the pAMPK/AMPK ratio in muscle, indicating improved oxidative metabolism and mitochondrial function. These molecular effects were accompanied by higher oxygen consumption and energy expenditure (Fig. 5G), suggesting better substrate utilization and flexibility between carbohydrate and lipid oxidation. These findings align with the known actions of CQAs on mitochondrial regulation. CQAs, especially chlorogenic acid and its derivatives, activate the AMPK–SIRT1–PGC-1α pathway, thereby promoting mitochondrial biogenesis, fatty acid oxidation, and antioxidant defenses [23–26]. These pathways also support the dynamic remodeling of mitochondria, promoting fission and mitophagy to uphold mitochondrial quality Control. Such coordinated effects enhance respiratory efficiency and energy homeostasis, providing a mechanistic basis for the improved metabolic flexibility observed in skeletal muscle and BAT of PSPLE-treated mice.

Chronic HF diet intake induces insulin resistance by promoting lipid accumulation in the liver and muscle, triggering lipotoxic mediators that impair insulin signaling and glucose metabolism [32]. In vitro, PSPLE and its phenolic components enhanced β-cell oxidative metabolism and insulin secretion, probably through AMPK activation and increased mitochondrial activity, as observed with other CQA-rich extracts. However, in vivo glucose (ipGTT) and insulin (ipITT) tolerance tests showed only modest improvements. This difference likely reflects the complex regulatory environment of insulin secretion in vivo, where multiple stimuli—including incretins, neural input, and nutrient flux collectively regulate β-cell activity [33]. Thus, the in vitro results demonstrate the inherent insulinotropic potential of PSPLE, while the in vivo data emphasize its broader role in enhancing systemic energy metabolism rather than functioning as a direct insulin secretagogue. Insulin resistance also hampers the anabolic effect of insulin on skeletal muscle protein synthesis and muscle mass accretion, leading to sarcopenia [34]. Our findings revealed that the increase in insulin sensitivity exerted by the 1% PSPLE was linked to the prevention of skeletal muscle mass loss and the enhancement of skeletal muscle oxidative capacity, despite chronic intake of a HF diet.

PSPLE supplementation prevented hepatic steatosis and normalized serum triglyceride levels that were elevated by high-fat feeding. Although serum triglycerides in PSPLE-supplemented groups appeared slightly lower than in Control, these values were statistically similar, indicating that PSPLE effectively normalized triglyceride levels that would otherwise be elevated by the high-fat diet. This normalization suggests improved lipid handling through enhanced lipid oxidation and storage. Therefore, PSPLE supplementation restored triglyceride concentrations to the physiological range observed in Control mice, consistent with its capacity to promote efficient lipid utilization and prevent hepatic lipid accumulation, in line with AMPK activation and the downregulation of lipogenic pathways. CQAs and related phenolics have been shown to suppress SREBP1c and ACC, stimulate β-oxidation, and improve mitochondrial function in hepatocytes [35]. The restoration of hepatic architecture and lipid homeostasis in PSPLE-fed mice indicates improved metabolic signaling and reduced lipotoxic stress.

Our chemical analysis of PSPLE identified chlorogenic acid and several caffeoylquinic acid derivatives (3,4-diCQA, 3,5-diCQA, 4,5-diCQA, and 3,4,5-triCQA), compounds known to increase gut microbial diversity, which metabolize bile acid into secondary bile acids, thereby modulating FXR–FGF15 signaling. This, in turn, maintains whole body lipid and cholesterol homeostasis. Previous studies showed in diabetic mice that di-CQAs (200 or 400 mg/kg) enhance intestinal barrier integrity and enterohepatic bile acid circulation [36], preventing intestinal permeability, bacterial translocation, and systemic inflammation. These important activities elicited by symbiotic bacteria in the intestine are impaired due to host factors such as stress, the intake of HF diets, and excessive sugars, which disrupt the composition of the gut microbiota, leading to dysbiosis that results in fat deposition [37], intestinal barrier dysfunction, and chronic low-grade inflammation [38]. These interactions between the host and its environment highlight the importance of gut microbiota in modulating polyphenol bioactivity and metabolic health. Given that gut microbiota is central to polyphenol metabolism, excessive polyphenol intake may disrupt symbiotic bacterial populations, particularly under high-fat feeding, attenuating the metabolic benefits observed at lower doses. This mechanism could explain why the 3% PSPLE supplementation produced weaker therapeutic effects than the 1% dose, suggesting that optimal modulation of host–microbiota interactions contributes to the hormetic response facilitated by moderate PSPLE intake.

These findings demonstrate that PSPLE offers multi-organ metabolic benefits by activating energy-sensing and oxidative pathways. Although changes in body weight and composition were modest, consistent and statistically significant shifts in biochemical and molecular parameters suggest that even slight improvements in energy balance and mitochondrial function can produce measurable metabolic benefits. The PSPLE rich caffeoylquinic acids appear to improve mitochondrial function, promote lipid oxidation, and support thermogenesis while maintaining β-cell capacity and reducing hepatic lipid content. The overall enhancement in metabolic flexibility particularly with 1% supplementation indicates that moderate, clinically relevant doses of PSPLE can optimize energy efficiency without adverse effects. This study employed an integrated approach, combining physiological, cellular, biochemical, and molecular analyses to thoroughly evaluate the metabolic effects of PSPLE.

Importantly, the higher effectiveness of the lower dose supports the concept of hormesis, where mild bioactive or metabolic stress caused by low polyphenol levels triggers adaptive cellular responses—such as AMPK signaling and mitochondrial biogenesis—that enhance resilience and metabolic efficiency [39]. Conversely, larger doses may exceed this adaptive limit, decreasing absorption or activating compensatory mechanisms that diminish metabolic advantages [40]. This hormetic reaction might therefore account for the better results seen with 1% PSPLE.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that PSPLE rich in caffeoylquinic acid derivatives improves metabolic flexibility and oxidative metabolism in mice fed a HF diet through coordinated physiological and molecular adaptations. The stronger effects observed with the 1% dose reflect a hormetic response, where moderate polyphenol exposure triggers beneficial adaptive signaling without eliciting inhibitory or compensatory effects associated with higher doses. Our findings highlight PSPLE as a promising functional food ingredient capable of mitigating obesity-related metabolic disturbances via mechanisms centered on mitochondrial activation, metabolic flexibility, and adaptive hormetic regulation of energy homeostasis.
