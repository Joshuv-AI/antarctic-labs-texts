Efficacy of pharmacotherapies in improving liver fibrosis among patients with MASLD and fibrosis stages of F1-F3: systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Xin-Yue Sun, Hua-Ling Jiang, Yu-Tong Ma, Tian-Qi Xiong, Xie-Yuan Leng, Yi-Fan Zhao, Xian-Feng Shen, Chao Zhang, Yi-Jun Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolism-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is one of the most prevalent chronic liver diseases worldwide. In recent years, although a network meta-analysis evaluated the efficacy of different drugs for patients with MASLD, it did not provide the specific dosage of the drugs and could not be accurately applied to the specific population for MASLD and fibrosis stages F1–F3, which greatly affected the extensibility and accuracy of the evidence. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of various pharmacotherapies with different dosages in improving liver fibrosis among MASLD patients with fibrosis stages F1–F3 through a network meta-analysis.MethodsRandomized controlled trials involving adults with MASLD and fibrosis stages of F1–F3 who received pharmacological interventions for improving liver fibrosis from three databases were identified from inception to July 21, 2025.ResultsThirteen RCTs involving 3,871 patients with 12 pharmacological interventions were involved. Compared with placebo in this network meta-analysis, 6 active pharmacological interventions, including resmetirom 80 mg/day (RR = 2.56, 95%CI: 1.09, 6.02), resmetirom 100 mg/day (RR = 3.07, 95%CI: 1.31, 7.16), survodutide 6 mg/week (RR = 7.25, 95%CI: 2.07, 25.36), tirzepatide 5 mg/week (RR = 4.29, 95%CI: 1.33, 13.82), tirzepatide 10 mg/week (RR = 5.31, 95%CI: 1.67, 16.85), and tirzepatide 15 mg/week (RR = 6.00, 95%CI: 1.91, 18.89), were significantly more effective for achieving NASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis. In direct comparisons, 6 active interventions, including resmetirom 80 mg/day (RR = 1.70, 95%CI: 1.22, 2.37), survodutide 6 mg/week (RR = 7.25, 95%CI: 2.68, 19.60), tirzepatide 5 mg/week (RR = 4.29, 95%CI: 1.76, 10.43), tirzepatide 10 mg/week (RR = 5.31, 95%CI: 2.23, 12.66), tirzepatide 15 mg/week (RR = 6.00, 95%CI: 2.54, 14.15), and denifanstat 50 mg/day (RR = 2.46, 95%CI: 1.18, 5.13), were significantly more effective than placebo. The ranking based SUCRA showed that survodutide 6 mg/week (92.0%) demonstrated the best efficacy, followed by tirzepatide 15 mg/week (89.9%), whereas emricasan 10 mg/day had the poorest efficacy (8.1%).ConclusionsThis study supported the use of survodutide (4.8 mg/week), efruxiferimin (28 mg/week), resmetirom (80 mg/day), and denifanstat (50 mg/day) as an recommended regimen in adults with MASLD and fibrosis stages of F1–F3. However, these findings did not support the clinical use of emricasan because of its poor efficacy. Concurrently, multiple pharmacological agents also exhibited efficacy at other relevant clinical endpoints.Trial registrationThe protocol of NMA was available at the Open Science Framework (OSF: 10.17605/OSF.IO/DM2NC).Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08219-6.

Background

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), which is the leading cause of chronic liver issues, is common worldwide, affecting approximately 25% of Westerners [1–4]. As the research on its mechanism continues to improve, MASLD previously referred to as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), has been performed, whereas nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) was redefined as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) [5, 6]. The pathological progression of MASLD leads to liver fibrosis, the most significant predictor of mortality in this disease [4], which advances through progressive architectural alterations including patocellular ballooning and lobular inflammation., culminating in cirrhosis [7, 8]，The METAVIR system classifies fibrosis from F0 (none) to F4 (cirrhosis) [9]. Hepatic fibrosis demonstrated histological reversibility, whereas cirrhosis reversal had proven substantially more challenging [10]. These findings necessitated precise pharmacological intervention during fibrosis stages F1–F3 of MASLD, prior to cirrhosis development. Such targeted therapy enables early disease intervention and optimized management, thereby reducing the risk of end-stage liver disease.

Although both the 2024 European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) clinical guidelines for liver disease [11] and the 2025 Korean Association for the Study of the Liver (KASL) clinical guidelines [12] only recommended resmetirom for MASH patients with liver fibrosis, regulatory approval from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) had been granted only to resmetirom [13] and semaglutide [14] for noncirrhotic MASH with moderate to severe fibrosis as of August 2025. Concurrently, while multiple clinical studies had evaluated various pharmacotherapies in MASLD patients with F1–F3 fibrosis in recent years, the absence of comprehensive comparative efficacy data for this specific population hindered the development of reliable clinical guidance. Furthermore, although the earlier study by Souza [15] evaluated the effects of various pharmacotherapies on liver fibrosis improvement in MASLD patients, the study population was not stratified by fibrosis stage. This was especially pertinent for fibrosis stage F4, which was characterized by established cirrhosis characterized by increased pathophysiological complexity that may obscure the evaluation of therapeutic interventions [10]. Moreover, the analysis did not yield specific therapeutic recommendations or explore dosage regimens, and its limited scope, which relied solely on two outcomes, compromised the comprehensiveness of the assessment.

This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of various pharmacotherapies at different dosages for improving liver fibrosis among patients with MASLD and fibrosis stages of F1–F3, and to explore the optimal pharmacotherapy strategies through network meta-analysis.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA extension statement for reporting systematic reviews incorporating network meta-analyses, ensuring compliance with all required elements of the reporting guidelines [16].

Research registration

The protocol of network meta-analysis was available at the Open Science Framework (OSF: 10.17605/OSF.IO/DM2NC).

Search strategy

A systematic and comprehensive search was conducted on three databases of PubMed, Embase and Cochrane Library to obtain the relevant literature and references published before July 21, 2025. The literature search strategy and databases used are described in Supplementary Method 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study adopted the following inclusion criteria: (i) Study design: Randomized controlled trials (RCTs); (ii) Participants: Adult patients (≥18 years old) were diagnosed by biopsy to had MASLD and fibrosis stages of F1 -F3. NASH histologic activity was quantified by the NAFLD activity score (NAS) and fibrosis was staged according to the NASH Clinical Research Network (CRN) criteria [17]; (iii) Interventions: Resmetirom, semaglutide, obeticholic acid, cenicriviroc, emricasan, efruxifermin, PXL065, denifanstat, tirzepatide, pegbelfermin, survodutide, and icosabutate; (iv) Comparisions: Placebo or other active interventions; (v) Outcomes: At least one of the following outcomes: NASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis, fibrosis regression by ≥ 1 stage without worsening of NASH, patients with ≥2 point reduction in NASH without worsening of fibrosis, fibrosis regression by ≥ 1 stage, fibrosis regression by ≥ 2 without worsening of NASH, patients with ≥2 point reduction in NASH, lobular inflammation ≥ 1 point reduction, ballooning ≥ 1 point reduction, MRI-PDFF 30% reduction or more. NASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis and fibrosis regression by ≥ 1 stage without worsening of NASH were defined as primary outcomes, and other outcomes were defined as secondary outcomes.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) Combination with unincluded liver-related non-pharmacological interventions such as exercise interventions, bariatric surgery, or specific dietary interventions; (ii) Patients with cirrhosis were excluded; (iii) Unclear intervention dosages of pharmacological interventions; (iv) No available data in the original research or the data that could not be converted; and (v) Duplicate studies.

Data extraction

Data extraction primarily involved several aspects, including study characteristics (first author, publication year, region where the study was conducted, and follow-up periods), participant characteristics (author, year, region, sex, age, sample size, fibrosis stage, NASH stage, follow-up), interventions and control (specific intervention details), and outcome measures (post-intervention assessments).

Quality assessment

To determine the validity of included trials, two independent evaluators utilized the Risk of Bias 2.0 (ROB 2.0) instrument [18, 19] for risk of bias assessment in RCTs. The bias risk associated with the included RCTs was evaluated on the basis of seven aspects, including random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and study personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective outcome reporting, and other potential sources of bias. Risk levels were categorized as low risk, some concerns, or high risk in accordance with the ROB 2.0 criteria. The overall bias risk was classified into three grades, including low risk of bias, some concerns, and high risk of bias. If there were any discrepancies, a third reviewer provided a comprehensive judgment.

Statistical analysis

For dichotomous outcomes, the relative risk (RR) with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated as an effect measure [20]. A p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant. Moreover, I2 was used to detect the magnitude of heterogeneity, where I2 values of ≥40% indicated significant heterogeneity [21]. A multivariate frequentist random-effects meta-analysis method using mvmeta package were performed for network meta-analysis [22]. According to the underlying assumption of transitivity within the network, conflicts might exist between pairwise comparisons and the distribution of effect modifiers [23]. This network meta-analysis generated robust evidence for both direct and indirect comparisons. The node-splitting approach was employed to test consistency equations when assessing treatment effects around a loop [24]. Treatment hierarchies were visualized through the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) values [25], where 100% represented the optimal intervention and higher values indicated superior efficacy.

Considering the variation in follow-up duration across the included studies, a bayesian network meta-regression analysis was performed to investigate whether treatment duration modified the intervention effects based on exchangeable and related treatment-specific interactions model [26]. In this study, safety concerns related to hepatotoxicity and pruritus prevented the approval of obeticholic acid for the treatment of NASH-induced liver fibrosis, and the sensitivity analysis using frequentist method, excluding data from obeticholic acid studies, was employed to verify the robustness of all network comparisons [27]. Additionally, to address potential heterogeneity arising from inconsistent fibrosis stage inclusion criteria, sensitivity analysis using frequentist method was performed by excluding patients with F2–F3 fibrosis to ensure the robustness of the findings.

To examined the dose-response relationships, we employed a bayesian model-based meta-analysis approach that fitted the dose-specific effect estimates derived from a network meta-analysis to an Emax dose-response model [28, 29]. The Emax model, which represented the maximum theoretical drug effect, was used to characterize pharmacological dose-response relationships [30]. The log maximum odds ratio (ORmax) with 95% credible intervals (CrIs) was the maximum effect of each pharmacotherapy on all outcomes compared with placebo, which was the logit form of the estimated parameter. All statistical analyses were conducted via STATA 15.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) and R 4.2.2 (Vienna, Austria), with valid copyright licences obtained.

Results

Literature screening

A total of 12,209 records were retrieved from three databases, including 1,880 from PubMed, 3,979 from EMBASE, and 6,350 from the Cochrane Library. Before screening, 2531 duplicate records were deleted. Additionally, 432 records were marked as ineligible by automation tools, and 57 records were removed for other reasons. After the initial deletion, 9,189 records were selected for further processing. As a result, 9,146 records were excluded during this screening stage. After screening based on the titles and abstracts, a total of 43 articles advanced to the full-text review stage, and all of these articles could be retrieved. Thirty studies, including discrepancies in population (n = 13), discrepancies in outcomes (n = 14), and studies with unavailable or unextractable key data (n = 3), were excluded in Supplementary Table 1. Ultimately, 13 RCTs [31–43] met the inclusion criteria. Quality assessment of included studies

Among these studies, 6 RCTs [37–42] had potential implementation bias. As a result, the overall risk of bias in the trial was determined to be of some concern. The overall risk of bias in the remaining studies was at a low level, the methodology was rigorous, and the results were highly reliable. Supplementary Table 2 summarized the risk of bias assessment.

The network dose-response meta-analyses were employed for all outcomes (Supplementary Table 10). Compared with placebo, significant dose-response relationships were detected for semaglutide (LogORmax = 40.56, 95%CrI: 5.58, 112.73) and tirzepatide (LogORmax = 16.66, 95%CrI: 2.93, 52.09) for NASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis, and semaglutide (LogORmax = 29.30, 95%CrI: 2.72, 86.83) for fibrosis regression by ≥ 1 stage without worsening of NASH. In the secondary outcomes of patients with ≥2 point reduction in NASH without worsening of fibrosis, compared with placebo, a significant dose-response relationship was detected for resmetirom (LogORmax = 3.17, 95%CrI: 1.20, 6.74). No significant dose-response relationships were detected for other active drugs or for other outcomes in Supplementary Table 10. Emax models were constructed on the basis of estimates of dose specificity for drugs used in patients with MASLD and fibrosis stages F1–F3. However, these estimates were imprecise, as indicated by their wide 95%CrI (Supplementary Figures 8–16).

Inconsistent test

The results of node-splitting approach showed that it were not statistically significant (all p > 0.05), indicating no significant inconsistency, and the network evidences had good internal consistency (Supplementary Tables 20–28).

Sensitivity analysis

Owing to safety concerns related to hepatotoxicity and skin itching, the FDA refused to approve the obeticholic acid as a therapeutic agent for liver fibrosis caused by NASH. In the sensitivity analysis, we excluded the study data involving obeticholic acid to verify the robustness of the four outcomes, namely NASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis, fibrosis regression by ≥ 1 stage without worsening of NASH, patients with ≥2 point reduction in NASH without worsening of fibrosis, lobular inflammation ≥ 1 point reduction and ballooning ≥ 1 point reduction. Pooled data showed no substantial change in outcomes when enrollment was changed, and the sensitivity analyses of all outcomes were shown in Supplementary Tables 29–33.

Sensitivity analysis after excluding participants with F2–F3 fibrosis were performed, and it generally yielded results similar to those of the primary analysis. However, for the outcome of NASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis, the effect estimate of semaglutide 0.1 mg/day versus placebo changed from (RR = 2.12, 95%CI: 0.81, 5.55) to (RR = 2.12, 95%CI: 1.18, 3.82), and the result became statistically significant after the exclusion of F2–F3 fibrosis patients. For all other outcomes, the pooled estimates remained largely unchanged, and the sensitivity analyses of all outcomes were shown in Supplementary Tables 34–39.

Publication bias

Publication bias was not found in any of the network funnel plots for any of the outcomes (Supplementary Figures 26–34).

Discussion

Key findings

A total of 13 RCTs were involved in this study, and we compared the therapeutic effects of various drugs in patients with MASH and fibrosis stages of F1- F3 to identify the most efficacious therapy for this population. First, with respect to NASH resolution without worsening fibrosis, survodutide had the best efficacy, followed by tirzepatide, with emricasan having the poorest efficacy. Second, regarding “fibrosis improvement of ≥ 1 stage without worsening of steatohepatitis”, survodutide still performed the best, accounting for the second highest percentage of patients, and emricasan still had the poorest efficacy.

Survodutide and tirzepatide are glucagon receptor/GLP-1 receptor dual agonists. They ameliorate MASH through multiple mechanisms, including a reduction in inflammatory responses, lipid accumulation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress damage, as well as the promotion of mitochondrial biogenesis and liver tissue repair [44]. The study revealed that survodutide was the optimal drug for treating patients with MASLD and fibrosis stages of F1–F3 [41, 45] especially in NASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis, fibrosis regression by ≥ 1 stage without worsening of NASH, and MRI-PDFF 30% reduction. Moreover, tirzepatide also demonstrated considerable efficacy for the primary outcome of NASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis, which was second only to survodutide. It thus represents a suitable alternative for patients who are intolerant to survodutide. In accordance with the 2024 EASL clinical guidelines [11], the efficacy of survodutide and tirzepatide in patients with MASLD and fibrosis validated the emphasis on glucagon receptor/GLP-1 receptor dual agonists as a promising therapeutic class. In the drug efficacy study, the 4.8 mg per week dose of survodutide had a greater effect than the 6.0 mg and 2.4 mg per week doses did on the outcome of fibrosis improvement in patients in the ≥1 stage without worsening of NASH. From a clinical perspective, the medium dose not only ensured efficacy but also reduced potential side effects, offering a superior benefit risk profile. Although the 15 mg dose of tirzepatide demonstrated superior efficacy in alleviating NASH, the 10 mg dose was better tolerated and exhibited comparable therapeutic effects, avoiding gastrointestinal discomfort or metabolic disorders that may be caused by high doses [38]. Consequently, the 10 mg dose is considered more suitable for patients who cannot tolerate the higher dose.

Our findings differ from those of an earlier study by Sousa et al. [15], which provided an efficacy ranking of the drug treatment plan in terms of fibrosis regression and MASH alleviation. In contrast, our specific focus on fibrosis stages F1–F3 enabled more precise identification of the most effective treatments for this patient subgroup, and it provided scientific evidence to support the promotion and application of these treatments. These stages, including fibrosis stages of F1–F3, generally represented early to intermediate disease, characterized by relative clinical stability and significant reversibility potential. In contrast, the F4 stage reflected progression to cirrhosis, where increased pathological complexity potentially obscured the true effects of interventions. Furthermore, the analysis did not yield specific therapeutic recommendations or explore dosage regimens, and its limited scope-relying solely on two outcomes-compromised the comprehensiveness of the assessment. To address these limitations, the present study optimized medication dosages, expanded the clinical evidence base, and incorporated comprehensive outcome indicators, thereby enabling a multidimensional comparison of drug efficacy, and the provision of specific therapeutic recommendations offered direct guidance for clinical decision-making.

In March 2024, resmetirom, a liver-directed, thyroid hormone receptor beta–selective agonist, was the first drug conditionally approved by the FDA for treating adults with noncirrhotic MASH and moderate-to-advanced fibrosis [46]. In this study, no significant difference in efficacy was observed between resmetirom 80 mg/day and resmetirom 100 mg/day. Considering its the safety profile and potential adverse effects, resmetirom 80 mg/day was recommended as the preferred therapeutic option [43]. However, when compared with survodutide 4.8 mg/week, resmetirom 80 mg/day still showed insufficient efficacy. These findings suggested that for patients with fibrosis stages of F1–F3, medium-dose dual-receptor agonists may offer greater advantages than high-dose single-target drugs. In the research conducted by Hazem Ayesh’s team [47], the efficacy of resmetirom, fibroblast growth factor 21 analogues, and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists was compared for the treatment of MASLD. The results showed that FGF21 analogues were most effective for both MASLD resolution and fibrosis improvement. Furthermore, resmetirom also demonstrated considerable efficacy, particularly in reducing hepatic fat content, which underscored its potential role in regulating lipid accumulation and inflammation. In contrast, although GLP-1 receptor agonists effectively reduced liver fat, their ability to improve fibrosis was relatively limited. A notable difference emerged between the findings of prior research and this study, where survodutide demonstrated the best overall efficacy. The divergence was largely attributable to differing eligibility criteria. The current analysis was restricted to MASLD patients with fibrosis stages F1–F3, whereas Hazem Ayesh’s study employed broader enrollment criteria. This difference suggested that baseline fibrosis stage was a key effect modifier.

Efruxifermin and pegbelfermin were analogs of fibroblast growth factor 21, which act as a key regulator for all organs in the body [48, 49]. In this study, no significant differences in therapeutic efficacy were observed among the 28 mg, 50 mg, and 70 mg doses of efruxifermin. Considering the drug safety profile, the 28 mg dose was identified as the preferred treatment option [34]. However, the overall efficacy of efruxifermin was superior, especially in the two key outcomes of fibrosis regression by ≥ 1 stage and lobular inflammation ≥ 1 point reduction. Its performance was significantly better than that of pegbelfermin, highlighting the prominent advantages of this drug in improving liver fibrosis and inflammatory conditions in MASH patients. The superior duration of action of efruxifermin is greater than that of most FGF21 analogues [34, 50]. This protein potentially stemmed from its nature as an Fc-fusion protein that was created through the molecular modification of human FGF21 and its linkage to the human IgG1 Fc domain [51].

Denifanstat, an oral fatty acid synthase (FASN) inhibitor that reduces damage to proinflammatory and fibrotic pathways in MASH [52], demonstrated significant efficacy in a phase 2b trial conducted by Rohit Loomba and colleagues [37]. On the basis of this study, denifanstat at 50 mg/day demonstrated the best efficacy for fibrosis regression by ≥ 2 stages without worsening of NASH. This drug significantly reduced hepatic fat content by targeting the regulating of de novo lipogenesis [53].

Emricasan, a pan-caspase inhibitor, was found to reduce caspase-3/7 activity along with levels of cytokeratin-18 and serum alanine aminotransferase in patients with NASH [54]. However, its limited efficacy observed in this population indicated an insufficient inhibition of cysteine proteases, likely attributable to its predominant action on inflammatory pathways rather than the core metabolic disturbances driving MASLD. As a result, emricasan failed to produce significant improve in fibrosis or NASH [33, 54].

This study evaluated the potential of various other drugs for the treatment of MASLD. In August 2025, semaglutide became the second drug to receive conditional FDA approval for treating non-cirrhotic MASH with moderate to severe fibrosis in adults [14]. Incretin-based poly-agonists, including semaglutide, demonstrated hepatoprotective effects in both MASLD and MASH, with evidence indicating that these benefits emerged early in the disease spectrum, particularly at the MASLD stage. Accordingly, incretin-based therapies represented a promising treatment strategies for these conditions [55]. Cenicriviroc, a dual CCR2/CCR5 antagonist [56], exerted antifibrotic effects by significantly reducing the recruitment of mononuclear phagocytes. PXL065 was a novel chemical entity generated through deuterium modification of (R)-pioglitazone. Its parent drug, pioglitazone, was an approved insulin-sensitizing thiazolidinedione that had been extensively investigated in NASH [57]. Among the evaluated therapies, PXL065 demonstrated optimal efficacy for patients with ≥2 point reduction in NASH.

Obeticholic acid was a farnesoid X receptor agonist that can also reduce liver fibrosis and inflammation [31, 58–61]. It performed best on the outcome measure of ballooning ≥ 1 point reduction. However, the FDA declined to approve the drug because of safety concerns regarding hepatotoxicity and pruritus. To verify the reliability of the study results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis, which indicated that none of the outcome measures were significantly influenced by potential confounding factors, thereby demonstrating good robustness.

Clinical significance

For adult patients with MASLD and fibrosis stages of F1–F3, this study addressed a gap in the literature by providing a comprehensive network meta-analysis that directly compared the efficacy of different drugs and dosages. Unlike previous studies that enrolled patients across a broader spectrum of fibrosis stages, this analysis strictly limited its population to stages F1–F3. This narrower focus enabled a more targeted comparison of drug efficacy and yielded precise evidence for treating this specific patient subgroup. This study employed stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria, with defined key elements including the study population, interventional strategies, and outcome measures, to ensure the accuracy and scientific rigor of the research. This study employed a systematic approach to identify relevant literature and implemented targeted strategies to minimize the risk of omission, thereby ensuring the comprehensiveness and representativeness of the final dataset. Furthermore, the patient group with MASLD and fibrosis stages of F1–F3 represented a common and prognostically crucial population that previously lacked clear guidance for drug selection. This study provided reliable evidence to inform the FDA’s regulatory decisions on therapies for MASLD patients with fibrosis, thereby offering evidence-based references to assist clinicians in selecting individualized treatments for those at stages of F1–F3.

Limitations

This study had severa limitations. Firstly, the included studies and samples were unevenly distributed, only 13 RCTs were incorporated. For drugs such as cenicriviroc and PXL065 lacked sufficient direct comparison data, which limited the reliability of their efficacy evaluations. Secondly, certain dosage groups had small sample sizes or insufficient subgroup data to inform personalized treatment, which highlighted the need for more well-designed RCTs. Thirdly, Most of the included studies had a relatively short follow-up duration, and there was still a lack of long-term data support exceeding two years. Fourthly we could not fully address potential confounding factors in the included RCTs like baseline concurrent medications or lifestyle habits. Finally, We also did not fully assess comorbidities beyond MASLD and fibrosis, which might conceal the true effects of the drugs, so targeted clinical guidance is limited.

Future research directions

Future research directions should focus on the following aspects. Firstly, long-term head-to-head RCTs with follow-up periods of 2–5 years are needed to directly compare drugs such as survodutide, efruxifermin, and resmetirom. These trials should assess their ability to sustain fibrosis improvement over time and evaluate their long-term safety profiles, particularly with regard to late-onset adverse events relevant to real-world clinical decision-making. Secondly, a fibrosis stage-stratified analysis was needed. This involved evaluating treatment responses across subgroups (F1, F2, F3) to determine how well each drug matched different disease severities. For instance, it was necessary to clarify whether survodutide 4.8 mg had superior efficacy in F3 patients and whether denifanstat 50 mg offered a more favorable cost-benefit ratio for those at the F1 stage. Finally, dosage optimization remained a critical gap in the evidence base and needed to be addressed through further controlled studies. Large-scale clinical studies are needed to evaluate the efficacy of formulations, such as semaglutide 1.0 mg/day and efruxifermin 70 mg/week. Addressing these evidence gaps would help determine whether dose adjustments could enhance treatment responses without compromising safety, thereby informing optimal dosing strategies in clinical practice. Future studies should proactively incorporate these factors into RCT designs for stratified analyses, with particular attention given to the influence of comorbidities on drug effectiveness, ultimately improving the precision of clinical guidance.

Conclusion

The study supported the use of survodutide (4.8 mg/week), efruxiferimin (28 mg/week), resmetirom (80 mg/day), and denifanstat (50 mg/day) as an recommended regimens in adults with MASLD and fibrosis stages of F1–F3. However, these findings did not support the clinical use of emricasan because of its poor efficacy. Concurrently, multiple pharmacological agents also exhibited efficacy at other relevant clinical endpoints. These data provided more precise and direct evidence-based support for the development of personalized clinical treatment strategies. In the future, the therapeutic efficacy and safety profile required additional validation through rigorously designed RCTs.
