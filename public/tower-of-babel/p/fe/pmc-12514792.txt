Modelling the determinants of uptake of complete immunization of children in Ghana
Authors: Daudi Yeboah, Gideon Mensah Engmann, Dioggban Jakperik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmunization remains a vital public health initiative for children since it reduces the risk of morbidity, mortality, and long-term complications associated with vaccine-preventable diseases. The study sought to model the determinants of uptake of complete immunization among children in Ghana using data from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS). The study used a sample of 4,961 women with children under 5 years and employed Poisson regression, and Negative Binomial models. Results from the study showed that about 51.28% of the children had completely received all the childhood vaccines but 3.79% of the children had never received any of the vaccines in the country. The study found that Negative Binomial regression was the best-fitted model for modelling complete immunization of children under 5 years in Ghana. Respondents who have primary and secondary education were 3.20% and 4.00% times more likely to completely immunize their children as compared to those with no education. Respondents who are currently working are 7.20% more likely to completely immunize children as compared to those not currently working (IRR = 1.072; 95% CI: 1.042, 1.104). Women who have been exposed to mass media were 3.50% more likely to completely immunize their children as compared to those not exposed to mass media (IRR = 1.035; 95% CI: 1.000, 1.071). Women who made 8 or more number of ANC visits were 5.30% more likely to have children completely immunized compared to those made 1–3 ANC visits. We therefore encourage the use of mass media to educate women on the need to ensure complete immunization of their children. The study also recommends that women should be encouraged to improve their ANC visits (8 or more) to be fully made aware of childcare-related issues, such as the significance of immunizing children completely.Keywords: Immunization, Complete immunization coverage, Vaccine, Children, Ghana

Introduction

Childhood immunization is a key aspect of public health interventions for preventing morbidity, mortality, and long-term complications associated with vaccine-preventable diseases. The World Health Organization (WHO) recognizes vaccines as a highly cost-effective investment in public health [1]. Complete coverage implies that all recommended vaccines are received within the appropriate timeframes, ensuring maximum protection against vaccine-preventable diseases. Incomplete coverage can leave children vulnerable to infections, leading to illness, complications, and even death [2]. As a result of the undesirable health outcomes of children who are not immunized, there is a need to ensure improvement in the coverage of vaccination among all children [3]. The Global Vaccine Action Plan (GVAP) was formed by the World Health Organization (WHO) in May 2012 as an instrument to avoid mortality in children by improving access to equitable vaccines. To this end, Olorunsaiye et al. highlighted the need for strengthened health systems and improved service delivery to enhance immunization coverage [4].

Approximately 50% of children in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) have access to basic immunization, and only one in twenty children in the poorer remote areas of low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have access to the necessary immunizations [5]. In LMICs each year, more than 10 million mortalities in children occur as a result of lack of access to efficient interventions like immunization [6]. Vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) still cause around 17% of all under-five deaths worldwide each year, despite improvements in child health and rising child survival rates as a result of numerous public health efforts [7]. Specific challenges must be overcome for children in Sub-Saharan Africa to receive all necessary vaccinations. Low immunization rates are a result of the region’s weak health services, poor infrastructure, and scarce resources [4].

Ghana has seen a noticeable improvement in immunization coverage; however, discrepancies still exist between various socioeconomic categories and geographical areas [8]. However, several children never received any vaccinations at all [9]; others are unable to receive all the recommended vaccines [10]. Over the years, few studies on immunization have been undertaken in Ghana. Most of which [9, 11–13] focused on specific vaccine types while others are location-specific [10, 14]. However, the focus of some nationwide studies was found to be on inequalities in child immunization [15] and the effects of childhood immunization [16]. This study therefore seeks to model the determinants of uptake of complete childhood immunization in Ghana using nationally representative data.

Methods

Data source, population and sample

The study used data from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS). The study used the children’s recode file of the 2014 GDHS dataset.

The study population consists of women with children under 5 years. Women who responded as “don’t know” or could not recall that the child was vaccinated were excluded from the study. When it comes to whether answers of “no” and “don’t know” indicate the same thing, the literature is inconsistent. Some believe they are similar and can be analyzed together [17, 18], whilst another school of thought claims that “no” and “don’t know” indicate different things and deserve to be separately analyzed [19]. The second strain of thought, according to which “no” and “don’t know” are distinct responses is adopted in this study. The study ensured that all participants had information on the variables used in the analysis. The study included a sample of 4,961 women after the data cleaning.

Study variables

Dependent variables

The study focuses on identifying the extent of complete immunization in Ghana, which served as the main outcome measure. Data on immunization coverage were collected either from vaccination cards or through the verbal responses of mothers during the survey. The response options provided to the mothers included “No,” “Yes,” and “Don’t Know,” which were assigned numerical codes: “No” was coded as 0, “Yes” as 1, and “Don’t Know” as 8. Only women who provided definitive responses (“No” or “Yes”) were included in the analysis.

In accordance with the 2017 guidelines from the World Health Organization, “complete or full vaccination” refers to a child who has received specific doses of various vaccines. This includes one dose of Bacillus Chalmette Guerin (BCG), three doses each of pneumococcal vaccine (PCV), oral polio vaccines (OPV), Pentavalent vaccine, and Diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis (DPT), two doses of Rotavirus vaccine, one dose of yellow fever vaccine and one dose of measles vaccine. To simplify the analysis, the responses were recoded as “0 = No” for children who did not receive the recommended doses and “1 = Yes” for those who did. A composite variable representing complete immunization was created by combining the responses on all the administered vaccines. The complete immunization variable consists of counts from 0 to 8, a count of 0 means no vaccine was taken and a count of 8 means all vaccines were taken.

Independent variables

The study included 14 independent variables. These variables consisted of maternal age, educational level, marital status, region, number of children, place of residence, wealth index, religion, ethnicity, number of ANC visits, partner’s educational level, respondent currently working, sex of a child, and exposure to mass media. The respondents’ ages were divided into three categories: 15–24, 25–34, and 35–49. The educational level variable was categorized as no education, primary, secondary, and higher education. The region variable encompassed the Upper East, Central, Brong Ahafo, Greater Accra, Volta, Ashanti, Eastern, Northern, Western, and Upper West Regions. The place of residence was classified as either rural or urban.

The number of children was divided into three groups: 1–2 births, 3–4 births, and 5 or more births. The variable indicating whether the respondent was currently working was recoded as “Yes” or “No.” Marital status was recoded as either not married or married. Partner’s educational level was categorized as no education, primary, secondary, and higher education. The number of ANC visits was recoded as 1–3 visits, 4–7 visits and 8 and above visits. Exposure to mass media was recoded as “Yes” or “No. The exposure to mass media was a composite variable generated from the use of print media (newspapers/magazines), listening to the radio and watching television. The religion variable was also recoded as Christian, Muslim, Traditionalist/spiritualist and no religion.

Ethnicity was recoded as Ewe, Gruma, Mole-Dagbani, Guan, Akan, Ga-Adangbe, Grussi, and Others. The sex of the child was coded as female and male. Wealth status was grouped into five categories: poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest.

Data analysis method

In this study, we used Stata SE version 15 to analyze the data. The study employed both descriptive and inferential statistics. The variables were described using frequencies and percentages. The study utilized three models including Poisson, and Negative Binomial models to examine the effect of the independent variables on the complete immunization of children in Ghana. Statistical analyses were controlled at a 0.05 alpha level. The risk of achieving complete immunization was measured using the incidence risk ratio (IRR). IRR measures the incidence rate of a health event in an exposed group to an unexposed group [20].

Results

Sociodemographic and economic characteristics

Women between the ages of 25 and 34 years made up the majority of respondents (48.07%), followed by those between the ages of 15–24 (26.33%). The educational level of the respondents was mainly secondary education (47.69%) and no education (28.91%). The number of children for these respondents ranges from 1 to 2 (47.35%), 3–4 (29.73%) and 5 or more (22.92%). Most of these women were married (86.17%) and Christians (62.81%). About 58.00% of the women were living in rural areas in the country, while 42.00% of them lived in urban areas.

The highest number of women in the study was found to be from Northern (10.38%), North East (9.29%), and Savannah (8.35%) regions, whilst the Eastern region had the least number of women in the study. Most of the women were found to be in the poorest (31.30%) category of wealth status in the study. However, about 76.23% of them are working. During pregnancy, the majority of the respondents made between 4 and 7 ANC visits (53.78%), whereas 35.76% of them made 8 or more ANC visits. Most of the women were found to have been exposed to mass media (78.88%). Most of the children were males (51.60%). The children were aged 0 year (37.15%), 1 year (36.81%) and 2 years (26.04%). The mean age of the children is 0.89 year. These children were delivered at Home (14.57%), Hospital (49.49%), Clinic (26.75%), and CHPS (9.19%) (Table 1).

Children who were delivered at a clinic were 6.10% more likely to be completely immunized as compared to those who were delivered at home. Discussion

Vaccination is among the three utmost effective public health efforts since it helped prevent almost two million deaths annually worldwide [2]. However, achieving complete coverage of childhood immunization has been a major challenge in Ghana [21]. This study looked at examining the complete immunization coverage of children in Ghana using data that nationally represents the entire population. From the results, the most used vaccine among children in the country was the BCG vaccine. Adokiya et al.. also reported a similar finding in the Techiman municipality of Ghana [10]. Other studies outside the country had reported similar findings [22, 23]. This is so because BCG is a single-dose vaccine given during the first clinical contact or at birth to protect against tuberculosis [24]. Another reason for the increased BCG coverage may have resulted from more institutional delivery [23]. On the other hand, immunization with rotavirus vaccine was the lowest vaccine received among the children. This finding is not different from the report of the 2022 GDHS [21] and a study in Ethiopia [25].

The study again found that about 2.33% of the children had never received any of the vaccines in the country whilst 40.94% of the children had completely received all the recommended childhood vaccines. However, those who have completely received all the recommended childhood vaccines in the study were about 37% lower than the 2022 GDHS report [21]. This study envisaged that enhanced health service delivery, including the establishment of additional sites for outreach immunization under the Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) and stationed centers in the country, may contribute to high immunization coverage. Additionally, BCG and OPV0 vaccines are currently administered by private healthcare facilities to ensure greater coverage of vaccination services in the country [10]. Other studies have reported that the unknown location of vaccinations, issues associated with the timing of vaccinations, the need to prioritize other chores, illness of the child, the fear of adverse vaccination reactions or effects, and the lack of vaccines at health facilities are some of the justifications given for not receiving all recommended vaccinations [26].

Moreover, the study found that maternal age, number of children, ethnicity, region, place of residence, number of ANC visits, and respondent currently working were significantly associated with complete immunization coverage of children in Ghana. It was identified that children of mothers with primary and secondary education had a higher likelihood of being fully immunized than children of mothers with no formal education, suggesting that the degree of education of mothers was also linked to the child’s level of immunization. According to earlier research, children of secondary-educated mothers were more likely than those of non-formal-educated mothers to be fully immunized [27, 28].

Ethnicity continues to play a significant role in the use and acceptability of vaccinations. The study realized that women who belong to Grusi, Gurma and Mande ethnic groups are less likely to completely immunize their children as compared to those who belong to the Akan ethnic group. Other studies also found significant associations between ethnicity and complete immunization [10, 29, 30]. Shibre et al. indicated that there exist inequalities in immunization coverage between rural and urban residents and requires careful consideration [31]. Again, this study found that women residing in rural areas are less likely to have their children completely immunized. This might be apparently due to access, awareness and availability of resources [25]. Similar studies reported that women in urban areas completely immunize their children [10, 32]. However, in places where there are functional outreach services and availability of vaccinators, mothers are more likely to vaccinate their child up-to-date [3, 33]. This could be explained by the fact that, in comparison to parents in rural regions, parents in urban areas were more likely to have an education, which could have increased their knowledge about the advantages of childhood vaccination uptake [34]. This finding may be attributed to the fact that Ghana has made significant efforts to improve the delivery of health services, including through the formation of new locations for outreach immunization under the Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS). Despite the health services are more readily available and better in urban places than in rural ones, the severity of urban poverty is probably concealed by this fact [35, 36]. Following a study, one-third of the urban population in developing nations resides in slums with low quality of life and inadequate access to healthcare [37]. However, other studies reported that women in urban areas completely immunize their children [10, 32].

Furthermore, there was a significant association between complete vaccination coverage and region. Women who live in the Western North and Upper West regions were more likely to have complete immunization as compared to those from the Western region. Similar findings were found in other studies [7, 38, 39]. A possible reason is that certain regions are underserved in several critical services, especially in developing nations where spatial disparities in access to and availability of health facilities and information are widespread. Some settings have highlighted obstacles to providing all children with the full complement of immunizations, such as low service quality, insufficient vaccines, distance to facilities, and a lack of motivated and skilled human resources [24]. Given the existence of these disparities, further research needs to be done to determine the degree and causes of regional disparities in childhood vaccination coverage.

The time spent by the mothers and the opportunity cost of waiting at health and immunization centers are represented by the respondent who is now employed. Respondents who are currently working are more likely to have their children completely immunized in the study area. This corresponds to a study conducted in Ghana [7] and in the North of Nigeria [40] which states that women who work had the highest proportion of children who received the vaccination. Compared to non-salaried workers or women with limited income, women who work for a salary have more influence and decision-making capacity. This might be the cause of the improvement in immunization rates of children. The maternity leave policy in Ghana allows salaried workers in the formal sector to take time out of work to care for the baby. This time off may have been utilized for infant vaccinations as well as child health care.

Women who made 8 or more ANC visits were more likely to have their children completely immunized. The reason is that women who frequently go for ANC visits get familiar with health care including immunization programs [41]. Also, women are made aware of childcare-related issues during ANC visits, such as the significance of immunizing children, which might be responsible for complete immunization [42]. As a result, the Ministry of Health and the Health Directorates should develop a strategy for carrying out continuous health education on the need to completely immunize children against childhood diseases and as well ensure that pregnant women make the WHO-recommended number of 8 or more ANC visits to be fully made aware of child care-related issues such as the significance of immunizing children completely.

Furthermore, the place of birth of the child influences whether or not the infant is completely immunized. Our findings indicate that children who were delivered at a clinic were more likely to be completely immunized as compared to those who were delivered at home. Hospital birth has been associated with timely uptake of vaccines [43–45].

Children who are 1 and 2 years old were more likely to have children completely immunized as compared to those who are 0 years. Similarly, a study in Nigeria also found that children who are 1 year and above were more likely to completely immunize their children [46]. Children under one year old in Angola whose mothers were aware of the immunization services had higher vaccination program compliance [47]. Parents of older children may be more aware of the importance of vaccinations and more likely to follow recommended vaccination schedules [48].

Strengths and limitations of the current study

The study’s main strengths were that we used nationally representative population-based data and robust modelling techniques to model the uptake of complete childhood immunization coverage and therefore could make the results be applied to women aged 15 to 49 in the study context. However, this study does not establish a causal relationship because the findings are based on a cross-sectional survey. Furthermore, the selection of variables in the study was constrained by the analysis’ use of secondary data.

Conclusions

The study found that about half of the children had complete immunization coverage. This is less than the 80% national targets by GAVI and UNICEF.

The study found that sociodemographic and economic factors such as educational level, ethnicity, region, respondent currently working, and number of ANC visits significantly impact the complete immunization coverage of children in Ghana.

Factors like working class of women, and higher number of ANC visits (8 or more visits) were more likely to aid in complete immunization coverage.

The study therefore recommends the use of mass media to educate women on the need to ensure complete immunization of their children. Women should be encouraged to improve their ANC visits (8 or more) to be fully made aware of childcare-related issues such as the significance of immunizing children completely.
