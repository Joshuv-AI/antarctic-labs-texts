Development and validation of a risk models for gout in patients with diabetic kidney disease
Authors: Ningyu Cai, Mengdie Chen, Yiyun Wang, Ping Feng, Xianping Zhu, Qidong Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with diabetic kidney disease (DKD) exhibit a markedly elevated risk of developing gout. The coexistence of these conditions severely compromises health. Consequently, risk for gout necessitates evaluation within this patient cohort. This study aimed to identify risk factoes of gout among DKD patients and to establish and validate a clinically usable nomogram for estimating individual gout risk.MethodsA two-center, retrospective analysis enrolling DKD patients managed at the Metabolic Management Centers of Taizhou Central Hospital and Yuhuan Second People’s Hospital between September 2017 and February 2025 was conducted. Key risk factors were selected using an integrated approach combining Best Subset Regression and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator analysis, followed by multivariate logistic regression to identify independent correlates of gout. A risk nomogram was then generated. Model performance was assessed using Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) values, calibration plots, Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit tests, and a decision curve analysis (DCA) approach.ResultsOverall, 5,670 DKD patients were analyzed. Of these, 4,617 participants from Yuhuan Second People’s Hospital were divided into training and internal validation cohorts, while 1,088 patients from Taizhou Central Hospital formed an external validation cohort. Variables incorporated into the final model included sex, body mass index, fasting C-peptide, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and serum urate. The nomogram displayed strong discrimination with respective AUCs of 0.784, 0.782, and 0.770 in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts. Calibration plots showed close alignment between predicted and observed outcomes (Hosmer-Lemeshow p-values 0.769, 0.332, and 0.520 for the respective cohorts). DCA demonstrated net benefits across various threshold probabilities deemed clinically useful.ConclusionsThe prognostic nomogram developed in validated in this study has been specifically tailored to estimate gout risk in DKD. The tool can help clinicians identify high-risk individuals and implement early, targeted preventive strategies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02389-6.Keywords: Gout, Diabetic kidney disease, Risk model, Nomogram, Risk factors

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD), a major microvascular diabetes complication, is one of the principal causes of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) worldwide, with incidence rates continuing to rise [1]. In China, demographic aging combined with the escalating prevalence of diabetes has produced a steady year-on-year increase in DKD cases [2]. Epidemiological studies estimate that approximately 20–40% of individuals with diabetes develop DKD, with this risk being particularly prominent in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [3].

In parallel, the prevalence of gout, an inflammatory arthritis predominantly driven by hyperuricemia, has increased globally [4–5]. T2DM confers a heightened risk for gout [6]. For example, a large retrospective analysis using the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink found a 48% higher incidence of gout in T2DM patients compared with age- and sex-matched non-diabetic controls [7]. Reduced renal excretion of serum urate (SU) in DKD further amplifies the risk of hyperuricemia and subsequent gout attacks [8–9]. The coexistence of gout and DKD has been linked to elevated cardiovascular event occurence and all-cause mortality [10], underscoring the clinical need for proactive risk assessment and early intervention.

Although recent studies have attempted to develop risk assessment tools for gout, most were constructed for general or hyperuricemic populations and seldom validated in independent cohorts. For instance, Liu et al. used machine learning to model gout risk within a Taiwanese biobank [11], while another nationwide investigation created risk calculators for hyperuricemia [12]. However, no dedicated risk model has been tailored specifically to DKD populations, and rigorous external validation efforts remain insufficient for those models that do exist.

The present study therefore utilized data from two independent medical centers to construct and externally validate a simple, clinically applicable nomogram for gout risk in DKD patients. Such a tool could facilitate early detection of high-risk individuals, promote individualized screening, and inform preventative or therapeutic decisions.

Materials and methods

Study design

In total, 14,367 individuals with T2DM recorded at the Metabolic Management Centers (MMC) of Taizhou Central Hospital and Yuhuan Second People’s Hospital between September 2017 and February 2025 were identified. Among these, 8,658 did not meet the criteria for DKD and were excluded. Of the remaining 5,709 with established DKD, eight patients under 18 years of age and thirty-one with missing baseline data on systolic or diastolic blood pressure, fasting glucose, body mass index, SU, or estimated glomerular filtration rate were also excluded. The final analytic sample comprised 5,670 DKD patients. Participants from Yuhuan Second People’s Hospital were randomized at a 7:3 ratio into training (n = 3,208) and internal validation (n = 1,374) cohorts. An independent external validation cohort of 1,088 DKD patients from Taizhou Central Hospital was then assembled. Figure 1 details the participant flow for this study.

The investigation was undertaken according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All procedures were approved by the institutional review boards of both hospitals, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Clinical data collection

The selection of a broad range of variables potentially linked to gout risk was performed based on prior research and clinical expertise. These included demographic, anthropometric, and biochemical measures: gender, age, educational level, diastolic and systolic blood pressure (DBP, SBP), weight, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), disease duration, hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking status, alcohol intake, fasting blood glucose (FBG), fasting C-peptide (FCP), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (Scr), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), SU, total cholesterol (TC), high- and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, LDL-C), triglycerides (TG), and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR). Gout status was determined by a self-report of a prior diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R v4.2.1 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing; http://www.R-project.org) and Free Statistics Software version 2.2.0. Data distributions were examined visually using histograms to determine normality. Continuous variables exhibiting normal distributions were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas skewed variables were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical data were expressed as absolute numbers and percentages. Between-group differences were tested with chi-square tests for categorical variables, one-way ANOVAs for normally distributed continuous variables, and Kruskal-Wallis H tests when analyzing skewed variables.

Initial screening of candidate risk factors was carried out using univariate logistic regression 1n the training set to estimate unadjusted associations with incident gout. Variables achieving significance at p < 0.05 were retained for further analysis. Correlation matrices were then generated to identify strongly collinear variables; where collinearity was observed, clinically representative variables were selected to prevent model instability.

To balance model simplicity with discriminatory ability and the minimization of over-/under-fitting two complementary feature-selection techniques were applied in the training cohort: best subset regression (BSR) using the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation. Both methods converged on the same subset of risk factors, which were subsequently entered into a multivariable binary logistic regression to produce the final prognostic model. Based on this model, an easily interpretable nomogram was constructed to estimate gout risk in DKD patients.

Model discrimination in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts was quantified based on area under the curve (AUC) values for the receiver operating characteristic curve. Calibration was assessed visually with calibration plots and statistically with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, with no cohort exhibiting a significant deviation from perfect fit (all p > 0.05). Decision curve analysis (DCA) was also undertaken to estimate net clinical benefit across a spectrum of threshold probabilities. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Candidate risk factor selection

Within the training cohort, participants were categorized as incident-gout or non-gout cases. Univariate comparisons showed that male sex, higher BMI, waist circumference, diastolic blood pressure, body weight, FCP, UA, triglycerides, ALT, BUN, and serum creatinine were each associated with higher odds of gout. Hypertension, dyslipidemia, current smoking, and alcohol use were also positively associated. In contrast, elevated HDL-C and HbA1c demonstrated inverse associations with gout risk (all p < 0.05; Supplementary Table 1). Correlation analyses revealed that body weight and waist circumference were strongly collinear with BMI, and BUN with serum creatinine (Fig. 2). Accordingly, weight, waist circumference, and BUN were excluded to minimize multicollinearity.

Correlation matrix of variables in the training cohort

Both BSR and LASSO approaches were used for variable selection to enhance reproducibility and parsimony. Starting from 16 clinically relevant variables, BSR identified the model with the lowest BIC (-220; Fig. 3A, B), while LASSO regression with ten-fold cross-validation produced an optimal λ of 0.018, retaining five nonzero coefficients (Fig. 3C, D). The two approaches yielded an identical core set of variables (sex, BMI, FCP, eGFR, and UA) which consistently showed significant differences across the training and validation cohorts (all p < 0.05; Table 2). These variables were incorporated into the final risk multivariable logistic regression model.

Development and validation of a risk nomogram

A nomogram was developed using the five significant risk factors (Fig. 4; Supplementary Fig. 1). Its performance was evaluated in all three cohorts. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses showed strong and consistent discriminative capacity, with AUCs of 0.784 (95% CI 0.755–0.813), 0.782 (95% CI 0.741–0.824), and 0.770 (95% CI 0.725–0.815) for the training, internal validation, and external validation sets, respectively (Fig. 5A-C). Calibration plots demonstrated close alignment between predicted and observed probabilities (Fig. 5D-F), and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test confirmed acceptable goodness-of-fit in all cohorts (p = 0.769, 0.332, and 0.520, respectively). Decision curve analysis further showed that the model offered superior net benefit compared with both “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies across a clinically meaningful probability range of 1–50% (Fig. 5G-I).

Discussion

In this study, a risk assessment tool for estimating incident gout in patients with DKD was developed and externally validated. Using a combined BSR and LASSO approach, five key risk factors were identified and incorporated into a nomogram, including sex, BMI, FCP, eGFR, and UA. The model demonstrated robust discrimination across both internal and external validation cohorts, exhibited excellent calibration, and delivered tangible net benefit in decision curve analyses. Collectively, these findings underscore the nomogram’s potential as a practical instrument for individualized risk assessment, enabling clinicians to quantify the contribution of each risk factors and address modifiable risk factors in a proactive fashion.

Hyperuricemia has long been recognized as a a pre-requisite for of gout. Many reports corroborate the close positive association between serum urate concentration and gout onset [13, 14]. When serum urate exceeds its solubility threshold, monosodium urate crystals form and can accumulate in joints, soft tissues, and kidneys, initiating inflammatory cascades [15]. Activation of innate immune pathways, including NLRP3 inflammasome signaling, further amplifies gout pathogenesis [16]. Consequently, interventions that lower serum urate remain fundamental to both prevention and treatment of gout.

Our analysis identified male sex as an independent and robust risk factors of new-onset gout in patients with DKD, a finding that echoes earlier epidemiological research. Large-scale population studies consistently report markedly higher gout prevalence among men than women [17, 18]. For example, the NHANES 2007–2008 data showed a consistently greater prevalence of gout across all male age categories compared with females [19]. More recent NHANES data from 2015 to 2016 likewise demonstrated a prevalence of 5.2% in men versus 2.7% in women [20], while a Taiwanese population-based cohort revealed a similar pattern, with prevalence rates of 9.3% in men and 3.2% in women [21]. This male predisposition is thought to reflect sex hormone-related differences in urate metabolism. Estradiol, for instance, enhances urate clearance by stimulating the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway and increasing intestinal ABCG2 expression, thereby lowering circulating urate concentrations [22]. Experimental models further support this mechanism: ovariectomized female rats display elevated serum urate levels that normalize with estrogen replacement, whereas androgen exposure has been shown to upregulate xanthine oxidase (XOD), which is a key enzyme involved in urate production [23]. In addition, sex-dependent differences in renal urate handling have been documented. Men exhibit greater tubular urate reabsorption and consequently lower fractional excretion at comparable serum urate levels [24, 25]. This functional disparity correlates with sex-specific transporter expression: male kidneys display higher levels of the reabsorptive transporter URAT1 and reduced levels of the excretory transporter GLUT9 [26]. Moreover, sex differences in gout between men and women also relate to cytokine production capacicity [27]. By incorporating sex into the risk model developed herein, this study highlights the clinical importance of developing tailored preventive and therapeutic strategies for gout in DKD populations, taking into account biological differences between men and women.

Our prior work has demonstrated a strong link between elevated BMI and heightened gout risk among individuals with T2DM [28]. Using data from NHANES (2001–2018), Mao et al. reported a 5% increase in gout incidence for each unit rise in BMI [29]. In a large prospective cohort, baseline obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) conferred a 2.4-fold greater likelihood of developing gout in women relative to their non-obese peers, while obesity during early adulthood was associated with an even larger 2.8-fold risk elevation [30]. A systematic review of longitudinal studies by Nielsen et al. further underscored that weight reduction meaningfully lowers serum urate concentrations and decreases the frequency of gout flares [31]. Notably, modest weight loss of 5–10% appears sufficient to produce clinically relevant benefits. Several mechanisms explain how excess adiposity fosters gout pathogenesis: increased urate synthesis, reduced renal urate elimination, chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, greater adherence to pro-gout dietary patterns (such as high-purine or high-fructose intake), and promotion of sedentary behavior [32]. Accordingly, weight control, lifestyle modification (including physical activity), and dietary interventions (such as purine restriction) form central pillars of evidence-based strategies for both prevention and management of gout.

C-peptide, a proinsulin cleavage product released by pancreatic β-cells, is secreted into circulation at levels comparable to those for insulin but exhibits a longer plasma half-life, leading to higher measured concentrations than insulin itself [33]. Importantly, C-peptide measurement remains unaffected by anti-insulin antibodies or exogenous insulin administration. Although traditionally considered biologically inert, circulating C-peptide serves as a robust surrogate for endogenous insulin secretion and is therefore a valuable marker of β-cell function and insulin resistance (IR) [34]. A substantial body of evidence links elevated C-peptide concentrations to more severe IR [35, 36]. This relationship is clinically relevant because IR is now recognized as a contributor to gout pathophysiology. Gout patients typically show higher fasting insulin levels and greater IR than non-gout controls [37], and Mendelian randomization analyses indicate that hyperinsulinemia drives hyperuricemia, whereas improved insulin sensitivity leads to lower serum urate levels and reduced gout risk [38]. Mechanistically, IR promotes gout via multiple pathways. Hyperinsulinemia suppresses renal urate transporters such as URAT1 and GLUT9, thereby diminishing urate clearance and elevating circulating levels of serum urate [39]. In parallel, IR may amplify NLRP3 inflammasome activity, encouraging monosodium urate crystal deposition and precipitating gout flares [40, 41]. These observations suggest that insulin-sensitizing interventions, including metformin therapy, may indirectly attenuate hyperuricemia and gout burden alongside their established effects on glycemic control [42, 43].

eGFR is a critical index of renal function, and a growing body of evidence highlights its association with gout risk. chronic kidney disease (CKD) is consistently linked with substantially higher gout prevalence compared with the general population, and progressive eGFR decline corresponds to rising gout incidence [44]. Analyses of NHANES data have further established reduced eGFR as an independent predictor of new-onset gout. Among diagnosed gout patients, an eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 is associated with a 1.5-fold increase in recurrence risk, and declining kidney function predicts development of treatment-refractory disease [45]. Moreover, synergistic effects emerge when reduced eGFR co-occurs with other metabolic risk factors. For example, individuals with both low eGFR and elevated body roundness index demonstrate markedly greater gout risk than those with only one of these risk factors [45].

There are key strengths to this study. First, the large-scale, multi-cohort design enhances both the statistical power and the external validity of our findings. Second, our dual-pronged feature-selection strategy combining BSR and LASSO minimizes overfitting and selection bias, thereby improving model robustness. Third, the risk model underwent stringent internal and external validation, confirming its reliability and performance across diverse cohorts. Finally, this study represents the first attempt to create a clinically applicable nomogram specifically for assessing gout risk in patients with DKD, thereby filling a key knowledge gap. The inclusion of a broad range of clinical and laboratory variables not only facilitated identification of key determinants but also supported the development of a practical, evidence-based risk assessment tool for use in real-world clinical settings.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The cohort’s predominantly Chinese composition may restrict the applicability of our findings to populations with different genetic and environmental backgrounds. The gout outcome was defined based on patient self-report rather than confirmed by gold-standard methods such as joint aspiration for urate crystal detection or imaging. Although self-report is widely used in large-scale epidemiological studies and has demonstrated reasonable reliability, it may still be subject to recall bias or misdiagnosis, potentially leading to outcome misclassification. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and future prospective or randomized studies are required to clarify causal pathways. Finally, some variables with the potential to have influenced these results such as physical activity patterns or detailed medication histories (e.g., urate-lowering medications or antidiabetic drugs) were not captured, which may limit the comprehensiveness of the established model. Future work should incorporate these parameters to enhance risk assessment accuracy and clinical relevance.

Conclusions

In summary, a robust risk model for gout risk in DKD patients was herein developed and validated, identifying gender, BMI, FCP, eGFR, and UA as central determinants of individual risk. The model demonstrated strong discrimination and calibration across multiple validation cohorts, offering a clinically valuable tool for individualized risk stratification and informed management decisions.
