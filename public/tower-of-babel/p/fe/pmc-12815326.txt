Significance of Yoga for the Management of Dyslipidemia: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials
Authors: Palash Pramanik, Sridip Chatterjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The present study aims to critically analyze the effects of yogic interventions on dyslipidemia compared with distinct control conditions. We systematically searched the relevant randomized controlled trials published from January 2001 to January 2025 in electronic databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. Additionally, we conducted a manual search in a peer-reviewed journal. We used a random-effects model based on the inverse variance method to calculate weighted mean differences. We also assessed the risk of bias, heterogeneity, sensitivity analysis, and reporting bias. A total of 58 records were included in this systematic review, and 53 were considered for meta-analysis. In the group analysis, yoga was compared to control (no intervention), walking/aerobic exercise, and dietary restriction groups, respectively. As a result of the overall comparison between groups, people practicing yoga showed lower levels of total cholesterol (MD: -10.99, 95% CI: -15.19 to -6.78, I2 = 97%, P < 0.001), triglycerides (MD: -17.17, 95% CI: -22.00 to -12.34, I2 = 93%, P < 0.001), low-density lipoprotein (MD: -10.71, 95% CI: -13.75 to -7.68, I2 = 96%, P < 0.001), very low-density lipoprotein (MD: -278, 95% CI: -5.33 to 0.15, I2 = 99%, P = 0.06) and higher high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (MD: 2.44, 95% CI: 1.69 to 3.19, l2 = 93%, P < 0.001). These findings suggest that yoga as an intervention has a greater impact on controlling lipid profiles. Medical professionals should recommend yoga as a complementary and alternative therapy for their patients under safe guidance, for better and integrated healthcare.

BACKGROUND

Dyslipidemia is a major public health problem in both developed and developing countries. It is a leading risk factor for stroke and coronary artery disease (CAD). It refers to abnormal levels of fat content in the blood. It is characterized by elevated total cholesterol (TC ≥200 mg/dL), triglycerides (TG ≥150 mg/dL), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C ≥130 mg/dL), as well as a decrease in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C <40 mg/dL for men; and <50 mg/dL for women) in the bloodstream.[12] In 40%–60% of cases, dyslipidemia may be caused by genetic alterations; however, lifestyle-related factors are crucial in elevating lipid levels.[34]

A substantial number of studies have reported that dyslipidemia is highly prevalent in Europe (53.7%),[5] America (47.7%),[5] Jordan (81.6%),[6] Northern Ethiopia (66.7%),[7] South Africa (76.71%),[8] Iran (73.33%),[9] Saudi Arabia (25.5%),[10] Pakistan (96%),[11] and China (33.8%).[12] In India, the prevalence of dyslipidemia is 25%–30% in urban and 15%–20% in rural populations due to lifestyle changes.[13]

A fast lifestyle, motivated by consumerism, along with poor environmental conditions, calls for a holistic approach to health. Yoga is an ancient health science that originated in India around 5,000 years ago and takes a wide perspective towards wellbeing. Yoga emphasizes on healthy lifestyle by promoting a moderate diet (mitahara), sufficient physical activities (vyayama), along with conscious living (yama and niyama), and adequate sleep. Yoga also proposes the practice of physical postures (asanas), a variety of breathing techniques (pranayamas), relaxation methods, and meditation (dhyana).[14]

An ample number of systematic reviews and meta-analyses have been published previously evaluating the effects of yogic interventions on lipid profiles, but their findings have been inconclusive.[151617] Many previous studies[181920] have compared yogic interventions with mixed control conditions, making it difficult to isolate the specific impact of different control groups on lipid outcomes. In addition, these studies have exclusively examined data up to 2022, leaving room for including recently published randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that could contribute to the existing body of evidence. Given these gaps, a comprehensive analysis is required to assess the efficacy of yogic interventions on dyslipidemia by systematically comparing it with distinct control conditions. Therefore, the present systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effects of yogic interventions on dyslipidemia among healthy adults and individuals with prediabetes, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), metabolic syndrome (MetS), and CAD while accounting for the influence of different comparator groups separately.

METHODS

This study was performed according to the Cochrane guidelines[21] and adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist.[22] This study was also registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), with the registration number CRD42023391855.

Literature search strategy

Four electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library) were systematically searched to find related articles published from January 2001 to January 2025. Boolean operators (“OR” and “AND”) were utilized to search for articles in databases by using the following keywords: [Yoga OR yogic OR asana OR pranayama OR “surya namaskara” OR dhyana OR meditation OR shatkarma OR shatkriya OR “cleansing process” OR “mind-body medicine” OR “mind-body therapies” OR “complementary therapies” OR “mindfulness-based interventions”] AND [“lipid profile” OR “blood lipids” OR “serum lipids” OR “lipid markers” OR hyperlipidemia OR hypercholesterolemia OR hypertriglyceridemia OR hypoalphalipoproteinemia OR dyslipidemia OR “total cholesterol” OR triglyceride OR “high density lipoprotein” OR “low density lipoprotein” OR “very low density lipoprotein”]. A combination of Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms and titles and abstracts, along with specific filters such as age ≥18 years, RCT, and human subjects, was used for conducting the literature search. In addition, a manual search was conducted in “Yoga Mimamsa,” a peer-reviewed multidisciplinary research journal on yoga. The online search strategy is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Studies focusing on other conditions or comorbidities such as renal diseases, cancer, pregnancy, adrenal or endocrine tumors, or a personal history of psychosis were excluded.

Study selection

Two investigators (PP and SC) retrieved studies from online databases and imported them into Mendeley reference management software to remove duplicate trials. Then, a manual screening process was conducted in two stages: (1) original records were uploaded to the Covidence web-based tool (www.covidence.org) for title and abstract screening based on the inclusion criteria; (2) studies that met or nearly met the inclusion criteria were obtained with the full text in Mendeley. Any disagreements between the two authors regarding study selection were resolved during the screening process. The final selection of studies was made through a consensus between the reviewers.

Data extraction

The records from each study were extracted into a data extraction form with the following study characteristics: first author’s surname and year of publication with study location, population (sample size, age, and gender), intervention (type, intensity, duration, and components), comparison, outcomes, study design, and results. Data were collected by two independent reviewers (PP and SC). Disagreements were solved by consensus between the two reviewers.

Quality assessment

We assessed the risk of bias (ROB) of individual studies concerning seven domains: random sequence generation (selection bias), allocation concealment (selection bias), blinding of patients and personnel (performance bias), blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias), incomplete outcome data (attrition bias), selection reporting (reporting bias) and other potential sources of bias. Each of these domains was evaluated as low risk (0 points), high risk (2 points), or unclear risk (1 point) of bias based on the quality of evidence and availability of information.[24] Studies scoring ≥ 7 points were considered to have a high risk of bias, 3-6 points having a moderate risk, and 0-2 points, a low risk of bias. All disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Statistical analysis

We performed the meta-analyses using the RevMan statistical software (version 5.4.1). A random-effects model, based on the inverse variance method, was applied to determine the pooled effect size. The weighted mean difference and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the differences in means and standard deviations (SDs) for all continuous outcomes across both intervention and control groups. If the standard deviation (SD) was not reported but the 95% confidence interval was provided, the SD was estimated using the Meta-analysis Accelerator tool.[28]

Heterogeneity within the studies was evaluated using the I2 statistic and Cochran’s q-test. I2 values less than 25% were mild, 25%–75% were moderate, and more than 75% were considered as substantial heterogeneity.[21] Subgroup analysis was carried out when I² was more than 50%. However, subgroup analysis was done in two steps. First, subgroup analysis was executed based on the nature and characteristics of the control group, where they received usual care/no intervention, walking/aerobic exercise, or dietary restriction. Second, subgroup analysis was possible only on the health conditions of the populations in the initial yoga versus control groups. Sensitivity analysis was performed using the leave-one-out method by excluding individual studies one by one, and a meta-regression test was conducted to evaluate the influence of age as a confounding factor. Publication bias was examined visually through funnel plots and statistically using Egger’s test. A P value < 0.05 was considered to have statistical significance.

Effects of yoga on dyslipidemia

Total cholesterol

Data from 56 trials involving 12,527 participants were analyzed to evaluate the impact of yogic intervention on TC. Overall, yoga-based intervention significantly reduced the total cholesterol levels in adult populations (MD: −10.99, 95% CI: −15.19 to − 6.78, I2 = 97%, P < 0.001) [Figure 2]. On subgroup analysis, it was found that yogic practice significantly decreased TC levels when compared to control (no intervention) (MD: −14.13, 95% CI: −18.51 to − 9.75, I2 = 96%, P < 0.001), and yogic practice did not significantly reduce TC levels when compared to both waking/aerobic exercise (MD: −2.19, 95% CI: −5.97 to 1.59, I2 = 51%, P = 0.26) as well the dietary restriction group (MD: −3.22, 95% CI: −11.48 to 5.05, I2 = 47%, P = 0.45). However, a statistically significant difference was found among these three groups [Figure 2]. In addition, while performing subgroup analysis on the health condition of participants, it was found that yogic practice lowered TC levels in healthy adults (MD: −10.44, 95% CI: −19.86 to − 1.02, I2 = 90%, P = 0.03), adults with pre-diabetes or T2DM (MD: −18.11, 95% CI: −23.35 to − 12.87, I2 = 96%, P < 0.001), and patients with MetS (MD: −12.95, 95% CI: −19.82 to − 6.08, I2 = 54%, P = 0.002). No positive effect of yogic practice was observed in patients with CAD (MD: 8.33, 95% CI: −53.90 to 70.56, I2 = 97%, P = 0.79) [Supplementary Figure 1].

Forest plot of the effect of yoga on TC levels compared to exercise and dietary restriction

Meta-regression analysis revealed no significant effect of mean age on the effect size (regression coefficient = 0.116; 95% CI: −0.289 to 0.520; P = 0.575) [Supplementary Figure 2]. Additionally, Egger’s test and the funnel plot indicated no signs of publication bias (P = 0.216) [Supplementary Figure 3].

The results of the sensitivity analysis showed that none of the studies influenced the overall pooled effect of TC. This indicates that the results of this meta-analysis are reliable. However, three studies[608082] significantly influenced the effect estimate of TC when comparing yoga and control (MD: -15.66, 95% CI: -19.63 to -11.69, I² = 91, P < 0.001). The effect estimate of TC was also affected by the omission of two studies[4850] when comparing yoga versus walking/aerobic exercise (MD: -0.73, 95% CI: -1.99 to 0.53, I² = 0%, P = 0.26). Similarly, in the yoga versus dietary restriction group, two studies[7886] potentially influenced the effect estimate of TC (MD: -3.16, 95% CI: -5.98 to -0.33, I² = 0%, P = 0.03).

Triglycerides

Data from 61 trials involving 13,314 participants were analyzed to evaluate the impact of yogic intervention on TG. Overall, yoga-based intervention significantly decreased the triglyceride levels (MD: −17.17, 95% CI: −22.00 to − 12.34, I2 = 93%, P < 0.001) [Figure 3]. On subgroup analysis, it was found that yogic practice significantly reduced TG levels when compared to control (no intervention) (MD: −22.73, 95% CI: −27.52 to − 17.93, I2 = 88%, P < 0.001) and dietary restriction group (MD: −4.61, 95% CI: −6.02 to − 3.19, I2 = 0%, P < 0.001), where yogic practice did not significantly reduce TG levels when compared to waking/aerobic exercise (MD: −0.88, 95% CI: −5.35 to 3.58, I2 = 0%, P = 0.70). However, a statistically significant difference was observed among these three groups [Figure 3]. In addition, while performing subgroup analysis on the health condition of participants, it was found that yogic practice significantly lowered TG levels in healthy adults (MD: −18.24, 95% CI: −26.17 to − 10.32, I2 = 77%, P < 0.001), adults with pre-diabetes or T2DM (MD: −28.20, 95% CI: −36.09 to − 20.31, I2 = 91%, P < 0.001), and patients with CAD (MD: −30.41, 95% CI: −41.32 to − 19.50, I2 = 0%, P < 0.001). TG levels were also decreased in patients with MetS after doing yoga, although the results were not statistically significant (MD: −13.08, 95% CI: −20.80 to − 5.36, I2 = 34%, P = 0.009) [Supplementary Figure 4].

Forest plot of the effect of yoga on TG levels compared to exercise and dietary restriction

Meta-regression analysis revealed no significant effect of mean age on the effect size (regression coefficient = 0.174; 95% CI: −0.293 to 0.641; I2 = 0.466; P = 0.466) [Supplementary Figure 5]. Additionally, Egger’s test and the funnel plot indicated no signs of publication bias (P = 0.245) [Supplementary Figure 6].

The results of the sensitivity analysis showed that the overall effect of TG was significantly changed after eliminating single studies one by one (MD: -14.75, 95% CI: -19.53 to -9.96, I2 = 61, P <0.001). In the yoga versus control group, 10 studies [29334954555760648081] significantly influenced the effect estimate (MD: -20.70, 95% CI: -25.84 to -15.56, I2 = 49%, P < 0.001) of TG.

Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol

Data from 54 trials involving 12,421 participants were analyzed to evaluate the impact of yogic intervention on LDL. Overall, yoga-based intervention significantly decreased the low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels (MD: −10.71, 95% CI: −13.75 to −7.68, I2 = 96%, P < 0.001) [Figure 4]. On subgroup analysis, it was found that yogic practice significantly reduced LDL levels when compared to control (no intervention) (MD: −13.62, 95% CI: −18.02 to − 9.21, l2 = 97%, P < 0.001) and dietary restriction group (MD: −5.64, 95% CI: −9.35 to −1.92, I2 = 17%, P = 0.003), where yogic practice lowered LDL levels when compared to waking/aerobic exercise but not statistically significant (MD: −2.83, 95% CI: −7.93 to 2.26, I2 = 78%, P = 0.28). However, a statistically significant difference was found among these three groups [Figure 4]. In addition, while performing subgroup analysis on the health condition of participants, it was found that yogic practice lowered LDL levels in healthy adults (MD: −7.02, 95% CI: −12.61 to −1.44, I2 = 78%, P = 0.01), adults with prediabetes or T2DM (MD: −18.54, 95% CI: −24.61 to − 12.47, I2 = 98%, P < 0.001), and patients with MetS (MD: −12.61, 95% CI: −22.11 to −3.11, I2 = 65%, P = 0.009), and no positive effect was observed in patients with CAD (MD: 16.66, 95% CI: −48.21 to 81.52, I2 = 97%, P = 0.61) [Supplementary Figure 7].

Forest plot of the effect of yoga on LDL levels compared to exercise and dietary restriction

Meta-regression analysis revealed no significant effect of mean age on the effect size (regression coefficient = 0.039; 95% CI: −0.378 to 0.455; P = 0.855) [Supplementary Figure 8]. Additionally, Egger’s test and the funnel plot indicated no signs of publication bias (P = 0.0446) [Supplementary Figure 9].

The results of the sensitivity analysis showed that the overall pooled effect of LDL was significantly changed after excluding any of the studies (MD: -12.99, 95% CI: -15.95 to -10.2, I2 = 93%, P < 0.001). However, three studies[426480] significantly influenced the effect estimate of LDL when comparing yoga versus control (MD: -14.84, 95% CI: -18.77 to -10.91, I2 = 93, P < 0.001). The effect estimate of LDL was also affected by the omission of four studies[37486365] when comparing yoga versus walking/aerobic exercise (MD: -6.39, 95% CI: -10.12 to -2.66, I2 = 0%, P = 0.008). Similarly, in the yoga versus dietary restriction group, one study[86] potentially influenced the effect estimate of LDL (MD: -5.95, 95% CI: -6.87 to -5.04, I2= 0%, P <0.001).

High-density lipoprotein cholesterol

Data from 59 trials involving 13,235 participants were analyzed to evaluate the impact of yogic intervention on HDL. Overall, yoga-based intervention significantly increased the high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels (MD: 2.44, 95% CI: 1.69 to 3.19, I2 = 93%, P < 0.001) [Figure 5]. On subgroup analysis, it was found that yoga significantly improved HDL levels when compared to control (no intervention) (MD: 2.77, 95% CI: 1.87 to 3.68, I2 = 89%, P < 0.001) and walking/aerobic exercise group (MD: 2.11, 95% CI: 0.69 to 3.52, I2 = 72%, P = 0.004), where yogic practice enhanced HDL levels when compared to dietary restriction group (MD: 0.61, 95% CI: −0.70 to 1.93, I2 = 85%, P = 0.36). However, a statistically significant difference was observed among these three groups [Figure 5]. In addition, while performing subgroup analysis on the health condition of participants, it was found that yogic practice significantly increased HDL levels in healthy adults (MD: 1.11, 95% CI: 0.24 to 1.98, I2 = 14%, P = 0.01), adults with pre-diabetes or T2DM (MD: 3.56, 95% CI: 2.42 to 4.70, I2 = 92%, P < 0.001), and patients with CAD (MD: 4.25, 95% CI: 2.45 to 6.06, I2 = 0%, P < 0.001), and no significant improvement was observed in patients with MetS (MD: 2.34, 95% CI: −0.31 to 4.99, I2 = 67%, P = 0.08) [Supplementary Figure 10].

Meta-regression analysis revealed no significant effect of mean age on the effect size (regression coefficient = 0.056; 95% CI: −0.024 to 0.136; P = 0.168) [Supplementary Figure 11]. Additionally, Egger’s test and the funnel plot indicated no signs of publication bias (P = 0.3857) [Supplementary Figure 12].

The results of the sensitivity analysis showed that the overall effect of HDL was significantly changed after excluding any of the studies (MD: 1.83, 95% CI: 1.32 to 2.34, I2 = 47%, P < 0.001). However, nine studies[296064697476808182] significantly influenced the effect estimate of HDL when comparing yoga versus control (MD: 2.41, 95% CI: 1.62 to 3.20, I2 = 38, P < 0.001). The effect estimate of HDL was also affected by the omission of three studies[506177] when comparing yoga versus walking/aerobic exercise (MD: 0.93, 95% CI: 0.56 to 1.30, I2 = 0%, P < 0.001). Similarly, in the yoga versus dietary restriction group, three studies[667886] potentially influenced the effect estimate of HDL (MD: -0.97, 95% CI: 0.73 to 1.21, I2 = 0%, P < 0.001).

Very-low-density lipoprotein cholesterol

Data from 24 trials involving 2505 participants were analyzed to evaluate the impact of yogic intervention on VLDL. Overall, yoga-based interventions lowered the VLDL levels (MD: −278, 95% CI: −5.33 to 0.15, I2 = 99%, P = 0.06) [Supplementary Figure 13]. On subgroup analysis, yogic practice group decreased the VLDL levels when compared to control (no intervention) (MD: −4.50, 95% CI: −9.49 to 0.49, I2 = 98%, P = 0.08) and dietary restriction group (MD: −0.12, 95% CI: −0.42 to 0.17, I2 = 0%, P = 0.4), where yogic practice group was found to have no positive effect on VLDL when compared to waking/aerobic exercise groups (MD: 0.71, 95% CI: −5.47 to 6.88, I2 = 95%, P = 0.82). It was also found that these three groups had no statistically significant difference in VLDL levels [Supplementary Figure 13]. In addition, while performing subgroup analysis on the health condition of participants, yogic practice did not significantly reduce the VLDL levels in healthy adults (MD: −5.96, 95% CI: −10.29 to − 1.64, I2 = 49%, P = 0.007), adults with pre-diabetes or T2DM (MD: −3.77, 95% CI: −9.80 to 2.26, I2 = 98%, P = 0.22), and patients with MetS (MD: −7.61, 95% CI: −29.29 to 14.07, I2 = 94%, P = 0.49) [Supplementary Figure 14].

Meta-regression analysis revealed a significant effect of mean age on the effect size (regression coefficient = 0.295; 95% CI: -0.004 to 0.593; P = 0.053) [Supplementary Figure 15]. Additionally, Egger’s test and the funnel plot indicated no signs of publication bias (P = 0.448) [Supplementary Figure 16].

The results of the sensitivity analysis showed that the overall effect of VLDL was significantly changed after excluding any of the studies (MD: -0.20, 95% CI: -0.48 to 0.08, I2 = 0%, P = 0.16). However, six studies[313738496976] significantly influenced the effect estimate of VLDL when comparing yoga versus control (MD: -1.45, 95% CI: -2.74 to -0.15, I2 = 0, P = 0.03). Similarly, in the yoga versus walking/aerobic exercise group, three studies[374861] potentially influenced the effect estimate of VLDL (MD: -1.26, 95% CI: -3.90, 1.38, I2 = 0%, P = 0.35).

DISCUSSION

This meta-analysis of 53 studies found yoga effectively reduced total cholesterol, triglycerides, and low-density lipoprotein while increasing high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. In subgroup analysis, yoga vs. control (no intervention) groups revealed significant improvement in lipid parameters. Similar effects for TC, TG, and LDL were found in the yoga versus walking/aerobic exercise group, but yoga significantly increased HDL more than aerobic exercise. Dietary restriction performed better in some lipid parameters, but yoga showed additional benefits for LDL and TG. Subgroup analysis on the participants’ health conditions showed that yogic practice is more effective for controlling dyslipidemia in individuals with T2DM and CAD than in patients with MetS and healthy adults. Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of these findings with minor variations. Meta-regression showed no significant age-related effects, and publication bias was not detected.

Numerous RCTs showed that people doing yoga had significantly lower TC, TG, LDL, and significantly higher HDL cholesterol.[3246525455] It was also observed that yoga intervention is more beneficial to lipid profiles than walking or other physical exercise.[6065] In contrast, a 12-month exercise intervention resulted in a more significant improvement in lipid profiles than a yoga intervention.[48] According to Saboo et al. (2024),[66] those performing yoga had a significantly higher reduction in TC, TG, and LDL compared to the dietary intervention group and a non-significant decline in HDL, whereas Yazdanparast et al. (2020)[86] reported contrary results. However, our research findings are consistent with the previous meta-analysis conducted by Cui et al. (2016).[87]

However, there are probable mechanisms behind the effects of yoga on dyslipidemia management. Yogic practices, including suksma vyayama, Suryanamaskar, and various asanas, significantly influence the function of endocrine and exocrine glands. These practices lead to lower blood sugar levels by facilitating the absorption of excess glucose by muscles through insulin released by pancreatic ß-cells.[8889] This process enhances insulin sensitivity. Furthermore, yogic intervention promotes lipolysis, resulting in increased fatty acid levels in the bloodstream, subsequently elevating heart rate and blood flow, leading to enhanced energy production and reduced fatty acid accumulation in bodily organs.[90] This translates to an increase in HDL levels and a decrease in TC, TG, LDL, and VLDL-C levels, as observed in yoga practitioners.[4449] This improvement in lipid profile may be attributed to the upregulation of hepatic lipase and lipoprotein lipase at the cellular level, increasing triglyceride uptake by adipose tissue and affecting lipoprotein metabolism.[91] Yoga may also positively influence growth hormone and sex steroid hormone levels.[92] This hormonal regulation helps to normalize LDL and HDL levels by reducing excessive hepatic TC accumulation. The decrease in LDL-C is influenced by various factors, including weight loss, changes in body composition, increased muscle mass, and reduced body fat.[6290] Yoga helps to alleviate insulin resistance by reducing fat deposition in the waist region and converting central obesity into peripheral obesity, resulting in increased lean body mass and a decrease in BMI. The practice of Kriyas or cleansing processes significantly improves lipid profiles by reducing body fat.[93] Stress-induced cortisol overproduction is associated with hypertension and dyslipidemia. However, pranayama practice effectively reduces cortisol and sympathetic hormone levels by enhancing parasympathetic (vagal) activity and decreasing sympathetic tone, which helps to manage stress.[94] The practice of meditation induces a hypometabolic state. It reduces stress-induced sympathetic activity,[95] thereby boosting stress-coping abilities, and reduces cortisol levels, contributing to an improved lipid profile in yoga practitioners. Oxidative stress is a major factor contributing to lipid disorders. Combined yoga practices reduce oxidative stress and enhance antioxidant status, particularly in individuals with T2DM.[378296] Finally, high levels of inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-1, and interleukin-6) are associated with lipid abnormalities linked to insulin resistance via various molecular mechanisms. Regular yogic practice can reduce the levels of these inflammatory markers, promoting improved lipid profiles.[337082]

This review represents the first comprehensive evaluation of the impact of yogic practice on dyslipidemia in healthy adults, individuals with pre-diabetes or T2DM, patients with MetS, and CAD. Additionally, our study systematically compares yoga with different control conditions, i.e., control (no intervention), walking/aerobic exercise, and dietary restriction. We also included several new RCTs published after the meta-analysis by Ghazvineh et al.[20] This meta-analysis was aligned with our study; however, the control group in this study was heterogeneous, which may have influenced the original effects of yogic interventions. Furthermore, we exclusively searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library to find relevant scholarly articles, imposing no language restrictions. These aspects highlight the strengths of our study. However, there remain a number of limitations to this review work. First, the lack of blinding in many studies could weaken the reliability of conclusions. Second, most meta-analyses exhibited substantial to considerable heterogeneity due to small sample sizes in some trials and regional bias. Third, our meta-analysis did not evaluate differences in yoga practice, which can vary in terms of intensity and frequency.

Due to the limitations of this study, there is still a need for more high-quality RCTs using the CONSORT paradigm to assess the efficacy of yoga for controlling dyslipidemia and associated risk factors. Further studies are needed to determine whether yoga combined with walking/aerobic exercise or dietary restriction is equally or more beneficial than yoga alone.

Yoga is a simple practice to learn and understand. The modern scientific community has considered yoga as a non-pharmacologic and integrative medicine to address various health conditions. Therefore, it might be a choice for everyone to enhance their health and overall well-being through regular yoga practice. Based on the included studies, we suggest that practicing yoga for 45–60 minutes 5 days a week for 12 weeks may effectively manage dyslipidemia and its associated health conditions.

CONCLUSION

The present systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs clearly give an indication that yoga is an effective mind-body practice for the management of dyslipidemia. However, further experimental research is needed to find out the significant mechanism for more scientific clarity.
