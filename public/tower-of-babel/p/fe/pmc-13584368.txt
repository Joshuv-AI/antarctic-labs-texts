Toward maternal health equity in Iran: nationwide analysis of spatial and resource distribution in primary healthcare
Authors: Efat Mohamadi, Fatemeh Ghavidel, Ardeshir Khosravi, Elham Ebrahimi, Jawad Jafarzadeh, Hakimeh Mostafavi, Ahad Bakhtiari, Fateme Yaftian, Sara Mohamadi, Amirhossein Takian, Alireza Olyaeemanesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEquity in maternal health is critical to achieving Sustainable Development Goals and Universal Health Coverage. Unequal distribution of maternal health resources threatens access and outcomes, particularly in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) like Iran. This study assesses spatial and socioeconomic inequalities in the availability of human and physical maternal health resources across Iranian provinces in 2023.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed data from 20 maternal health resource indicators collected in 2023 across all 31 provinces of Iran. Among these, 7 variables represented human resources and 13 represented physical resources. Data aggregation was performed at multiple levels, including provincial boundaries, geographic regions, deprivation categories, and classification of provinces as border or central, to comprehensively assess spatial and socioeconomic disparities. Composite indices for human and physical resources were constructed using min-max normalization followed by averaging relevant variables. Inequality was quantified using the Gini coefficient across provinces and data processing and statistical computations were performed using R software.ResultsFindings reveal generally moderate inequalities in maternal health resource distribution (Gini mostly below 0.5) with advantaged and border provinces showing higher resource availability. Specialized facilities and supervisory staff remain concentrated in select regions, while frontline workers show more equitable distribution. The concentration of border‑specific facilities in a limited number of provinces explains part of the observed regional disparities.ConclusionsWhile important progress has been made in expanding maternal health services, spatial and socioeconomic inequities in the distribution of resources persist across provinces in Iran. Policy efforts should prioritize equity-oriented resource allocation, strengthen services in deprived provinces, and implement continuous inequality monitoring to support more equitable maternal health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13690-026-02001-2.

Background

Achieving equity in maternal health is a central objective of global health policy and a key component of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 3), particularly targets 3.1 and 3.7, which aim to reduce maternal mortality and ensure universal access to reproductive and maternal health services [1, 2]. Within the framework of Universal Health Coverage (UHC), equitable access to essential maternal health services is recognized as a critical requirement for improving maternal health outcomes and reducing preventable maternal deaths [3].

In many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Iran, access to maternal health services depends not only on the availability of healthcare facilities but also on the equitable distribution of human and physical resources across regions. Iran’s Primary Health Care (PHC) system plays a central role in delivering maternal health services through an extensive national health network that includes Health Houses (HH), rural and urban health centers, and Community Health Workers (CHW) [4–6].

Globally, a growing body of evidence highlights persistent inequalities in maternal health resource allocation. Studies from China [7], India [8], and several Sub-Saharan Africa [9] countries have shown that despite expansions in healthcare infrastructure, inequities remain in both human resources and physical facilities. Studies show that although some countries have improved maternal health equity, significant disparities persist. In China, resources were more fairly distributed by population than by geography (Gini 0.13–0.29) [10]. In Ethiopia and Kenya, inequalities stemmed from workforce shortages and urban–rural gaps. Ghana and Mozambique addressed these through community programs and training mid-level providers, yet regional gaps remained [11]. Overall, reviews from LMICs suggest that financial incentives can boost service use but cannot by themselves resolve structural imbalances in health resource distribution.

Iran’s PHC system has historically been recognized as one of the most extensive primary care networks in the region and has played a crucial role in improving maternal and child health indicators over the past decades [12]. The maternal mortality rate in Iran has markedly improved over the past three decades, declining from over 107 deaths per 100,000 live births in 1990 to approximately 16 by 2023 [13, 14].

However, national evidence suggests that Iran’s overall progress in maternal health has not been uniform across provinces. More deprived provinces continue to experience poorer maternal health outcomes, partly because of unequal access to skilled personnel and essential obstetric services, compounded by geographic, financial, quality-of-care, and health-system constraints [15, 16].

Despite reforms, i.e. service integration, referral strengthening, and staff training, province-level inequalities linked to the uneven distribution of maternal health resources persist [17]. Most national data sources emphasize service coverage or mortality but rarely examine whether health resources are distributed equitably across different provinces and population groups. Furthermore, existing studies are often local or limited in scope [18–20]. This critical knowledge gap poses a significant challenge, as equitable resource allocation is essential for both reducing provincial disparities and fulfilling Iran’s commitment to UHC by 2030. By systematically quantifying provincial inequalities in maternal health resource distribution within primary care, this study provides urgently needed evidence to guide targeted policies and promote health equity at the national level. This study therefore aimed to evaluate spatial and socioeconomic inequalities in the distribution of maternal health human and physical resources across the provinces of Iran in 2023 as an indicator of equity in access to care, to quantify disparities within the primary healthcare system. By identifying where disparities persist, this research offers new empirical evidence to guide provincial and national planning focused on equity, enabling more informed resource allocation in line with UHC and SDG commitments.

Methods

This descriptive analytical cross-sectional study assessed the distribution and inequality of maternal health resources across the provinces of Iran in 2023. The analysis used provincial level data covering the entire public primary healthcare system.

Sample, sample size, and sampling method

Given the use of registered secondary data in this study, the sample comprises the entire population of maternal health-related resources allocated within the primary healthcare sector of the public health system across all 31 provinces of Iran. Thus, the study employed a census sampling method, including all available data without selection or omission.

Variables and data sources

Data were obtained from official administrative registries of the Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MOHME), the Statistical Center of Iran, and the National Administrative Division Dataset of the Ministry of Interior (Table 1).

Data gathering and quality assurance

Data for this study were obtained entirely from secondary official sources. A researcher-developed data extraction checklist was designed to systematically collect relevant indicators on human and physical resources related to maternal health within the PHC system. The data collection process involved four sequential stages. First, all relevant national registries were systematically reviewed to identify variables consistent with the study framework. Second, authorized data custodians from the MOHME provided official provincial datasets, which were extracted in standardized formats and compiled into a consolidated national database. Third, the dataset underwent rigorous quality control procedures, including duplicate removal, variable harmonization, and consistency checks across different data sources. To further ensure reliability, data from three randomly selected provinces were independently cross-validated with corresponding provincial universities of medical sciences. Finally, where overlapping information existed among registries, data reconciliation was carried out through comparison of metadata and official reference values to achieve consistency and accuracy across provinces. All data were recorded in a structured Excel-based checklist specifically developed for this project, containing variables on human resources, physical facilities and demographic indicators.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, ranges) were calculated to characterize resource distribution across provinces, followed by composite index construction and inequality measurement to comprehensively assess spatial and socioeconomic disparities in resource allocation.

To summarize the overall distribution of maternal health resources, two composite indices were constructed: the Composite Human Resources Index (CHRI) and the Composite Physical Resources Index (CPRI). For each province, the CHRI was calculated using seven human‑resource variables, and the CPRI using 13 physical‑resource variables, with all indicators normalized using min–max scaling to range from 0 (minimum) to 1 (maximum). The full list of variables included in each composite index is provided in Table 1. For each index, the normalized values for a given province were then averaged to create a single composite score per domain, with higher values representing greater resource availability. These indices were then used as the primary variables for subsequent inequality analyses, including comparisons across deprivation levels and border versus central provinces, employing appropriate statistical and graphical methods.

Inequality in resource distribution was quantified using the Gini coefficient, a widely used measure for assessing disparities in health resource allocation and a proxy indicator for evaluating equity. The Gini coefficient, which ranges from 0 (complete equality) to 1 (maximum inequality), is widely applied in health resource distribution studies to summarize differences in access and allocation. Gini values were interpreted using conventional benchmarks: <0.2 (low), 0.2–0.4 (moderate), and > 0.4 (high inequality), consistent with health resource distribution literature [21–23]. The values were estimated using a geometric approach, as described below [24]:

Stratified analyses compared resource availability across deprivation levels and geographic categories (border vs. central provinces). Data processing and statistical computations were performed using R (version 4.3.1), with the ‘ineq’, ‘sf’, and ‘spdep’ packages applied for equity measurement and spatial analysis.

Results

Descriptive analysis of the provinces reveals that the total number of counties across all 31 provinces is 482, with an average of approximately 15.6 counties per province, ranging from 3 to 37 counties. The total population across all provinces is nearly 86 million, with urban residents constituting about 66 million and rural residents about 19.8 million. Fertility rates vary between 1.06 and 3.48, averaging 1.75 across provinces. Sixteen provinces are classified as border, and 15 as central. Regarding deprivation levels, provinces are nearly evenly split among advantaged, less advantaged, and deprived categories (Table 2). Patterns among physical health infrastructure indicators, however, revealed mixed trends. While deprived provinces had a higher number of Rural Health Posts (RHP = 29.27) compared to advantaged ones (17.39), the number of Urban Health Posts (UHP) and Comprehensive Health Centers (UCHC_R, URCHC, and their 24-hour equivalents) was lower in deprived areas than in advantaged or less-advantaged provinces. This suggests that although deprived regions may benefit from greater outreach at the rural level, urban-based health infrastructure remains relatively limited. Similarly, border provinces demonstrated greater availability of Health Houses (HH = 1700.70) and Border Health Facilities (BHC = 2.19, BHP = 13.38) but slightly lower densities for Urban Comprehensive Centers (UCHC) compared with central provinces. In terms of specialized maternal care facilities, e.g. MCF and BDCC, the differences between groups were modest. Maternity Care Facilities (MCF) showed comparable densities across deprivation categories (deprived: 7.41, less-advantaged: 7.50) and geographic regions (border: 14.72, central: 14.42), with differences < 8% indicating no substantial disparities (Table 4).

The CHRI and CPRI were calculated to synthesize the provincial distribution of maternal health resources. Across all provinces, the mean CHRI was 0.303 (SD: 0.221), ranging from a minimum of 0.020 to a maximum of 0.854. The average CPRI across provinces was 0.246 (SD: 0.163), with a minimum of 0.013 and a maximum of 0.588. Stratification by deprivation levels revealed that advantaged provinces had higher mean indices (CHRI: 0.411, CPRI: 0.330) compared to less advantaged (CHRI: 0.292, CPRI: 0.229) and deprived provinces (CHRI: 0.215, CPRI: 0.186). Furthermore, border provinces demonstrated higher composite indices (CHRI: 0.365, CPRI: 0.298) relative to central provinces (CHRI: 0.237, CPRI: 0.191) (Fig. 1, Additional files 2 &3).

The findings indicate moderate to high inequality in the distribution of maternal health resources across provinces, with notable variation among indicators. Nationally, Gini coefficients ranged from 0.131 for female Community Health Workers (FBHZ) to 0.328 for supervising health workers, showing a more equitable distribution of frontline staff than supervisory or professional cadres. Physical infrastructure displayed moderate inequality overall, though disparities were pronounced for specialized facilities such as 24-hour Comprehensive Urban Health Centers (0.760) and Border Health Centers (0.759). When analyzed by geography, inequality levels were low, mostly below 0.15, suggesting relatively balanced distribution between central and border provinces. However, by deprivation level, inequality was higher in deprived regions, particularly for midwives (0.218), supervising health workers (0.249), and Rural Health Posts (0.216), indicating uneven access to skilled care and rural infrastructure. In contrast, basic PHC resources such as female Community Health Workers (FBHZ) and Health Houses showed more equitable distribution. Overall, the most unequal access was observed for advanced and 24-hour maternal health facilities, highlighting a gap between basic and comprehensive service availability across provinces (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study revealed measurable disparities in the distribution of maternal health resources across Iran’s provinces, reflecting spatial and socioeconomic inequalities in access to essential human and physical health resources. Although the Gini coefficients for most indicators were below 0.5, indicating moderate inequality in resource distribution, these findings suggest persistent inequities in access to maternal health resources across provinces.

The concentration of these border‑specific facilities reflects their functional mandate and naturally results in distributional clustering, which appears as higher inequality when comparing border and central regions.

Together, these figures suggest that while basic human resources, e.g. Community Health Workers, are relatively evenly distributed, supervisory staff and specialized facilities remain concentrated primarily in advantaged or central provinces. This points to ongoing challenges in ensuring equitable distribution of advanced maternal health services, especially in deprived and border regions. The differential distribution of human resources, where frontline Community Health Workers are more evenly spread while supervisory and specialized cadres cluster in certain areas, reflects systemic challenges in workforce planning and retention. Similarly, physical infrastructure disparities, particularly the scarcity of comprehensive and 24-hour Urban Centers in deprived provinces, signal gaps in equitable health service provision.

One plausible explanation for the high inequality in BHC and BHP lies in their inherently location-specific mandate. These facilities are concentrated in a few border provinces to serve nearby populations. This geographic focus raises national inequality indices, though their placement reflects health and security priorities rather than unfair resource distribution. Similarly, the marked inequality in UCHC_24 and URCHC_24, may reflect an intentional policy response to geographic and service accessibility gaps. Provinces with limited hospital coverage or low private sector presence, typically deprived or remote areas, are more likely to have such centers to ensure continuous service provision. Consequently, their distribution may appear uneven nationwide, not because of under-allocation, but due to targeted deployment where hospital access is lacking. In contrast, high inequality in RHP may be linked to demographic and settlement patterns. Provinces with extensive rural populations and dispersed villages, i.e. Sistan and Baluchestan or Kohgiluyeh and Boyer-Ahmad, require a denser network of Rural Health Posts, whereas urbanized provinces, i.e. Tehran or Alborz, have fewer. This variation in population geography naturally produces disparities in density-based indices. For MCF, inequality may stem from uneven integration of obstetric units within the PHC system and differing levels of hospital dependency across provinces. Urban centers with hospital-based maternity care tend to have fewer PHC-linked maternity facilities, while rural or deprived provinces rely more heavily on PHC-based maternal services. Thus, resource distribution reflects a functional substitution pattern rather than a straightforward inequity. Finally, the concentration of SUPCHW and MID in specific regions may indicate workforce retention challenges and differential recruitment capacities among provinces. Better-resourced or centrally located provinces can attract and retain skilled staff through better working conditions and training opportunities, whereas deprived or remote provinces often face higher turnover and vacancy rates. Table 4 shows a somewhat unexpected pattern, whereby some deprived and border provinces have higher per-capita densities of physicians, midwives, and Community Health Workers than advantaged central provinces. This likely reflects deliberate policies to deploy health workers to underserved areas, including compulsory service for newly graduated physicians and midwives and targeted recruitment of Community Health Workers from within local communities. However, part of this pattern could also be a measurement artifact: in sparsely populated provinces with large territories, provider-to-population ratios may look favorable even when facilities are far from where people live and geographic access remains constrained. Comparable pro-poor distributions of health workers have been reported in a few middle-income settings implementing rural service schemes, but evidence on sustained success is still limited, suggesting that Iran’s experience may offer relevant policy lessons.

Our findings align with national evidence showing persistent geographic disparities in the distribution of the health workforce and infrastructure. Previous studies in Iran have reported Gini coefficients exceeding 0.4 for specialist physicians and notable regional gaps in the allocation of midwives, confirming the concentration of skilled personnel in more developed areas [25]. Similarly, studies from Tehran and other provinces have highlighted unequal access to health facilities between urban and rural settings, emphasizing that the expansion of services has not been accompanied by equitable spatial distribution [26–28].

Comparable patterns have been observed internationally. Evidence from Brazil [29] and Vietnam [30] indicates that despite overall workforce expansion, remote and deprived areas continue to face shortages of skilled providers. Likewise, studies from India and South Korea have documented that infrastructure inequalities persist even under supportive national programs [31–33]. Together, this body of evidence reinforces our results, suggesting that inequities in maternal health service provision are not solely a matter of resource quantity but of their spatial and structural distribution.

The findings highlight spatial and socioeconomic disparities in the distribution of maternal health resources across provinces in Iran, indicating the need for equity-oriented policy responses. Strengthening maternal health services in deprived regions, improving the distribution of midwives and Community Health Workers, and expanding access to comprehensive maternal care should be prioritized. In addition, routine monitoring of resource distribution using inequality measures, e.g. the Gini coefficient, can support more responsive and evidence-based health system planning. Resource allocation frameworks would benefit from incorporating need‑based indicators and measures of geographic accessibility, ensuring that higher workforce densities translate into meaningful access.

This study benefits from comprehensive national datasets and robust composite and inequality metrics to evaluate spatial health disparities. The systematic normalization and aggregation procedures present transparent and reproducible indices. However, limitations include incomplete data coverage in some provinces, variability in data recording systems, and an inability to capture within-province disparities or service quality aspects. Our provincial‑level analysis cannot capture within‑province disparities, and substantial differences in access may still exist between urban centers and remote rural areas. Efforts to harmonize definitions and validate data reporting partially mitigate these constraints, but residual biases may remain. Although most indicators were standardized by population size, the inequality analysis was conducted at the provincial level and did not fully incorporate need-based allocation models that consider demographic or epidemiological differences across regions.

Future studies should employ mixed-methods approaches to explore underlying causes of observed inequalities, including budget allocation policies, human resource constraints, and local health governance structures. Longitudinal analyses tracking changes over time will elucidate policy impact and inform sustainable interventions. Investigating patient perspectives through qualitative studies can reveal access barriers beyond infrastructure and workforce counts. Modeling demand-driven resource allocation tailored to population fertility rates, deprivation levels, and geographic characteristics will further optimize equity. Additionally, assessing health outcomes linked to resource distribution disparities will reinforce the evidence base for policy reforms.

Conclusion

This study provides a nationwide assessment of the distribution of maternal health resources within Iran’s primary health care system. The results indicate notable geographic and socioeconomic differences in the availability of maternal health resources across provinces, with more pronounced gaps in deprived and border provinces. These results emphasize the importance of equity-oriented resource allocation and strengthened maternal health services in underserved areas. Addressing these disparities through targeted and evidence-based policies is essential for improving maternal health outcomes and advancing health equity in Iran. These findings point to the need for targeted deployment of key workforce cadres, improved measurement of geographic accessibility, and greater use of need‑based criteria in allocating maternal health resources.
