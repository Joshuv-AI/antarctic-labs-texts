Feasibility and acceptability of an unsupervised and adapted physical activity program (PACE tool) as pragmatic care in a geriatric outpatient clinic
Authors: Ruiz Fabien Jean Manuel, Kergoat Marie-Jeanne, Buckinx Fanny, Di Lemme Stephanie, Beauchet Olivier, Brodeur Catherine, Andriamampionona Francis Andria, Tannou Thomas, Aubertin-Leheudre Mylène
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical inactivity accelerates the ageing process, leading functional incapacities and physical dependence. Although physical activity (PA) is known to counteract these effects, its integration as a prescription in healthcare is uncommon due to the lack of pragmatic solutions.AimsThis study aimed to assess the feasibility and acceptability of implementing a pragmatic, unsupervised and adapted PA program in geriatric outpatient clinics.MethodsIn a 4-month pilot study, a 12-week adapted unsupervised PA program was prescribed using the Promote the Autonomy through exerCisE (PACE) tool. Feasibility was assessed by measuring patient inclusion, program adherence, healthcare professional workload and the tool usability score. Acceptability (Likert scale) was assessed through healthcare professional and patient satisfaction, as well as reported pain, falls and feelings of safety during program practice. Pre- and post-intervention tests were conducted to explore adaptations in physical performance adaptations.ResultsOut of the 250 eligible patients, 76 were included, with 67 consenting to the prescribed PA intervention. Ultimately, 37 participants completed the full 12-week program, averaging 3.3 weekly sessions. Notably, 70.2% adhered to the daily walking prescription. Physicians found the PACE tool ‘highly relevant’ and non-disruptive. Beliefs in PA benefits and kinesiologist support facilitated adoption, whilst existing pain and lack of motivation hindered adoption. No meaningful change in functional performance was noted post-intervention.ConclusionThe results suggest that implementing PACE as a prescription exercise care regimen is feasible and acceptable to both healthcare professionals and patients. However, further randomised controlled trials are needed to evaluate its health effects compared to usual care.

Background

Physical inactivity exacerbates the ageing process, particularly impacting physical health by contributing to the decline in muscle function, physical performance and functional capacities [1–3]. The World Health Organization recognises physical inactivity as the fourth leading cause of death [4].

However, integrating physical activity (PA) into the standard care of older patients with health issues presents a challenge. This occurs from the shortage of PA specialists in these contexts, the need for specific equipment or transport, and the challenges faced by certain training modalities (unsupervised and/or home-based) [5]. Short home-based exercise programs have been developed to better engage older adults in exercise practice. These programs provide a safe and effective training modality without the need of specific equipment or memberships [6]. Furthermore, the simplicity of such exercise programs can empower and boost the self-efficacy of older adults to undertake regular strength and balance training [6].

Drawing from existing literature and our prior research [7–9], we co-designed an innovative PA program called Promote the Autonomy through exerCisE (PACE). The PACE tool prescribes adapted unsupervised multimodal exercises and walking programs based on a decisional tree assessing the individual’s mobility profile, whilst remaining aligned with geriatric patients’ limitations and realities (cognitive impairment, loss of autonomy, etc.). PACE aims to offer versatile implementation modalities for the practical integration of PA in today’s diverse clinical outpatient environments.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the feasibility and acceptability of the PACE tool in geriatric outpatient clinics, and explore its impact on physical performance. We hypothesised that our co-designed approach, based on previous studies [7, 8], would render PACE feasible and acceptable for both geriatricians and patients. Additionally, we anticipated that PACE would, at a minimum, maintain physical performance levels, as demonstrated in previous literature [5].

Methods

Design

A single-arm feasibility and acceptability study (Phase II) was conducted in an outpatient geriatric clinic. Approval was obtained from an institutional research ethics committee (#CER VN 20-21-06).

Population

Volunteers were screened and recruited based on the geriatricians’ consultation schedule between March and June 2021. Eligible patients provided verbal informed consent to participate in the PA care program.

Inclusion criteria: (i) over age 60; (ii) able to perform PA (sitting or standing); (iii) able to read and communicate in French or English; (iv) able to attend appointments at the geriatric outpatient clinic or access to videoconferencing technology (e.g. Zoom©).

Exclusion criteria: (i) individuals with terminal illness or unstable cardiovascular disease; (ii) those with severe cognitive impairment [mini-mental state examination (MMSE) <18/30]; (iii) individuals undergoing a rehabilitation program.

Based on the methodology described by Lewis et al. [10] and considering our single-arm group design, we used the following hypothesis testing parameters: 0 1—for two criteria—adherence of ≥50% (defined as practicing at least three times per week) and drop-out rate of ≤25%. A minimum of 42 participants were required to validate our main outcomes.

Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the study’s recruitment process was interrupted due to sanitary restrictions [11].

Promote the Autonomy through exerCisE tool and intervention

PACE exercise programs (n = 35) were categorised into seven colours that each focused on one or more physical parameters such as balance, mobility and muscle function through functional exercises and walking (programs available at: https://www.trainingrecommend.com). The various PACE programs included five levels of intensity, and were co-designed to be performed safely at home and unsupervised. To avoid ageism or other human biases [12], two decisional trees were co-designed to prescribe an adapted PA program to the patient’s care.

During medical consultation, the geriatrician first administered a subjective decisional tree (SDT) consisting of 12 questions from three validated questionnaires (FRAIL [13], FIND [14] and SARC-F [15]; see Supplementary material 1). This SDT aimed to assess the mobility profile [(lower limbs endurance (n = 3/12; score A:x/5), balance (n = 3/12; score B: x/5), trunk flexibility (n = 3/12; score C: x/5)], and walking ability (n = 3/12; score D: x/5) of the patient. The prescribed walking time was determined by the obtained score D (0–1: 10-min; 2: 15-min; 3: 20-min; 4–5: 30-min). The prescribed exercise program was based on two scores obtained using the decisional tree. Specifically, the total score (A + B + C = x/15) determined the difficulty level (5 levels = I: 0–5; II: 6–7; III: 8–10; IV: 11–13; V: 14–15), whilst the lowest score obtained in the three mobility subcategories (A–B–C) determined the program focus [7 focus colours: Blue = A (muscular endurance)/Yellow = B (balance)/Red = C (flexibility)/Green = A + B/Purple = A + C/Orange = B + C:/Brown = A + B + C] (Supplementary material 2).

Subsequently, to ensure safety, the patient underwent an in-person or remote session with a kinesiologist to perform the objective decisional tree (ODT; see Supplementary material 3), which included four validated geriatric tests: (i) 30-s sit-to-stand (STS; [16, 17]; score A:x/5), (ii) side-by-side, semi-tandem, tandem and unipodal balance ([18, 19]; score B:x/5), (iii) FRT ([20, 21]; score C:x/5), and (iv) 4-metre walking speed ([22]; score A:x/5). Score interpretation for exercise prescription was the same as in the SDT. This session also aimed to teach the prescribed PA program and explain the relevant documents (follow-up, booklet, website, etc.) to the participant. All participants were instructed to perform their PACE program every day for 12 consecutive weeks (Supplementary material 4), with a minimum of three sessions per week (based on the 150 min/week of PA to be considered physically active). Follow-up telephone calls were made weeks 1, 3, 6 and 9, during which the kinesiologist assessed exercise-practice (volume, intensity), ensured practice safety and provided adjustments to adapt or progress exercise difficulty.

Measures

General characteristics

Age, sex, depression [Geriatric Depression Scale 4 items (GDS4): total score from 0 (no-risk) to 4 (high-risk)] [23] and cognition [MMSE: total score from 0 (high-risk) to 30 (no-risk)] [24, 25] status, body mass index (BMI; kg/m2) and education level were collected.

Promote the Autonomy through exerCisE tool implementation

The implementation of PACE was estimated using the ratio of eligible patients to the total number of patients evaluated by the geriatrician (eligibility rate; %), and the ratio between the number of included patients and the number of eligible patients (inclusion rate; %).

Promote the Autonomy through exerCisE tool feasibility

Participants were instructed to record each completed session in a logbook. PA adherence (frequency and exercise+walking volume) was assessed using the PACE logbook and information collected during the follow-up phone calls. Feasibility was calculated based on adherence throughout the intervention [ratio between the number of completed exercise sessions and the number of prescribed exercise sessions (%) + ratio between walking time performed/walking time prescribed (%)] amongst participants who completed the evaluation (‘per-protocol’ analysis).

PACE tool acceptability

Healthcare team: Acceptability amongst the healthcare team was assessed using three Likert questions on satisfaction, perceived burden and workload of tool implementation. The system usability scale (SUS) questionnaire (10 questions with a total score from 0 to 100 [27]) was also used, with a score ≥ 70/100 considered acceptable [26]. Barriers and facilitators were further evaluated through open-ended questions.

Patient: Patient acceptability was determined using Likert scale questions assessing four items (enjoyment/satisfaction, pain, safety, perceived difficulty), in addition to the SUS questionnaire. The level of perceived difficulty was normalised using the validated POMP (percent of maximum possible score) equation [27]: (((observed—minimum)/(maximum—minimum)) × 100), where observed = the observed score for a single case, minimum = the minimum possible score on the scale, and maximum = the maximum possible score on the scale]. Barriers and facilitators were also evaluated through open-ended questions.

For the open-ended questions, an inductive coding method was employed [28]. Three investigators ensured the reliability of this process. Following grounded theory principles, coding included: (i) collecting and transcribing participants’ terms and phrases; (ii) meticulous coding to generate specific codes representing different facets of the data, which were harmonised by the reviewers; (iii) categorisation to group similar codes into broader, meaningful themes, with reference to existing theoretical concepts [29].

Promote the Autonomy through exerCisE health effects

Objective and validated tests [30-s STS (repetitions [30–32]); STS 5-repetitions (seconds, risk of falls ≥12 s [31]); normal walking speed (m.s−1, risk of loss of mobility <0.8 m.s−1 [33]), timed-up and go (TUG: seconds, risk of falls ≥14 s [34]), short physical performance battery (SPPB: x/12, 6 < x < 10 = pre-disabled [35])] were used.

Additionally, validated questionnaires [physical independence level (Barthel index: x/100 [36] and Lawton index: x/8 [37]), sarcopenia risk (SARC-F: x/10), fear of falling (short FES-I: x/28 [38]), mobility area (LSA: x/120 [39]), PA level (RAPA: x/10 [40]) and quality of life (SF-12v2 [41]) were also administrated.

To explore the potential health adaptations following the PACE prescription, physical performance tests were performed before and after the 12-week intervention.

Statistics

Continuous variables were presented as means [standard deviation (SD)] and categorical variables as percentages. Normality of the data was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test and homoscedasticity using Levene’s test.

A per-protocol analysis was used to assess the feasibility and acceptability of implementing PACE, as well as to explore potential health benefits. Baseline participant characteristics were analysed collectively and by subsamples (those who completed the program vs. those who dropped out). Non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon and McNemar’s) were used due to the abnormal distribution of the data and the small sample size. The effect size for quantitative data was calculated using Wilcoxon effect size coefficient (r) and can be interpreted as follows: small (0.1–0.3), medium (0.3–0.5) or large (>0.5). For categorical data, the effect size was calculated using the Phi coefficients (φ) and can be interpreted as follows: small (0.1), medium (0.3) or large (0.5). All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). A P-value <.05 was considered significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

At baseline, PACE participants had a mean age of 81 years (women: 61.7%). The majority were referred to the outpatient clinic for cognitive assessment (70.2%; MMSE: 25.4 ± 2.9/30) and had a family caregiver (72.9%). Participants exhibited suboptimal levels of PA (RAPA:3.6 ± 1.5/10), mild dependence in activities of daily living (Barthel index = 96.5 ± 6.2/100) and instrumental activities of daily living (Lawton index = 6.0 ± 2.0/8). They were at risk of falls (STS-5rep:12.2 ± 3.6 sec; 3 m-TUG:14.1 ± 4.9 sec) and pre-disabled (SPPB:8.5 ± 2.4/12) (Table 1). Implementation/feasibility

During the study, 250 patients received an outpatient geriatric consultation, of which 76 (30.4%) were eligible. Amongst eligible patients, 88.2% (n = 67/76) consented to PA care during the geriatrician consultation. After the consultation, the kinesiologist taught the PACE exercise program to 61.8% (n = 47/76) of them, as some refused the PACE care. Ultimately, 50.0% (n = 38/76) completed the 12-week program, however one participant did not complete the post-intervention evaluation. During the 12-week intervention, dropouts (n = 9/10) occurred due to health conditions or lack of motivation of the patient or caregiver (Figure 1). Based on the ODT, the focus of the prescribed exercise programs were mainly blue, for lower extremity muscle function (n = 29), followed by yellow (n = 6), green (n = 5), red (n = 4), purple (n = 2) and brown (n = 1). The difficulty levels of the exercise programs prescribed were as follows: level IV (n = 22), level III (n = 16), level II (n = 8), and level V (n = 1). The prescribed walking durations were 30 min (n = 28), 20 min (n = 12), 15 min (n = 5) and 10 min (n = 2). The average follow-up time with the kinesiologist during the 12-week program was 280 minutes. Participants who completed the intervention (see Table 2), averaged 3.3 ± 2.5 exercise sessions per week and achieved 70.2% of the prescribed walking duration. Program adherence and walking time tended to decrease over the 12 weeks.

Healthcare team acceptability

The geriatricians (n = 4/6) who participated in the study rated the tool as ‘very relevant’, with three of them expressing confidence in its use. The average SUS score for the PACE tool was 69.4% (±11.4), indicating an ‘acceptable’ level of usability. They found that the tool added ‘minimal work’ and was ‘tolerable’ in their practice. Two geriatricians expressed a desire to involve other professionals such as kinesiologists into their practice, to better integrate the PA programs into usual care.

Patient acceptability

Participants reported a high level of safety throughout the intervention (first week: 97.3%; 100% thereafter). Two participants reported pain during the first week, but the pain did not persist in subsequent follow-ups (100% pain-free rate in the following weeks). The average difficulty of the exercise program was 41.6%, indicating a level of difficulty slightly below ‘moderate.’ Additionally, participants reported a usability index (SUS) of 70.8% (±16.1), indicating that the PACE tool was considered ‘acceptable’.

Barriers and facilitators

The Hierarchical Ascending Classification diagram (Figure 2) illustrates that most participants identified awareness of the benefits of PA (24.3%) and awareness of the challenges of ageing (18.9%) as key facilitators. PACE tool modalities, such as kinesiologist supervision (16.2%) and independence [choice of location, time and ability to exercise with a caregiver; (13.5%)], were also noted as important facilitators. 18.9% of participants cited physical pain unrelated to the exercise program as a limiting factor. Additionally, 16.2% felt there was inadequate follow-up by the kinesiologist, 13.5% reported a lack of initial motivation, and 10.8% reported a progressive loss of motivation during the 12-week intervention.

Discussion

The primary aim of our study was to assess the feasibility and acceptability of prescribing PA using the PACE tool in a geriatric outpatient clinic. Our results indicate that implementing the PACE tool is feasible and acceptable for healthcare professionals and patients.

PACE adherence was acceptable, with 71.1% of participants engaging in daily walks and 59.6% regularly participating in the exercise program (three times per week or more). This adherence rate aligns with the literature on unsupervised exercise programs in older adults [6, 42, 43] and meets PA recommendations [44]. Although a gradual decrease in practice frequency was observed over the 12-week intervention, which is a common trend in unsupervised modalities, amongst other challenges [5], there is some room for improvement. Increasing awareness of the benefits of PA amongst patients and their caregivers could help prevent drop-outs [45]. Additionally, incorporating motivational tools or closer follow-up may reduce barriers and enhance adherence to PA [46].

We also observed that the dropouts mainly occurred between eligibility and the PA teaching session (66.6% of all dropouts). This rate could possibly be attributed to the ‘false consent’ phenomenon. The literature indicates low treatment adherence in older adults despite prior agreement, stemming from a conflict between administered care implications and perceived integration into daily life [47]. Furthermore, implementing PA prescription through geriatric outpatient consultations may have been overly restrictive for some patients transitioning from sedentary to active lifestyles [46]. Time constraints during outpatient geriatrics consultations [48], coupled with potentially uneven and sometimes incomplete knowledge of PA amongst physicians [49], might also contribute to patient misunderstanding regarding the importance of PA, thereby reducing motivational effectiveness [29]. However, the dropout rate from the beginning to the end of the 12-week intervention (19.1%) is considered satisfactory, aligning with the literature [42, 45]. Additionally, the inclusion rate could have been higher, as 10% of patients were ineligible due to ongoing physiotherapy follow-ups. This disrupted care rate has also been observed in previous literature [50, 51].

Furthermore, our findings indicate that implementation of the PACE tool did not impose a significant workload and was deemed acceptable (SUS ≈ 70/100) for clinicians. Therefore, given the potential barriers encountered by new care practices [52], PACE could be considered a satisfactory practice change. Another positive aspect of PACE implementation is the diverse profile of patients enrolled, ranging from physically resilient to frail patients. Specifically, two-thirds of the enrolled participants were from the memory clinic. It is often challenging to recruit patients from memory clinics, given the primary aim of these consultations being the diagnosis of cognitive disorders. Our results are therefore promising, considering the high proportion of patients stemming from the memory clinic. Furthermore, older adults with cognitive impairment are at higher risk of physical decline and falls [53, 54]. Older adults with dementia account for up to one-third of emergency hospital admissions, and over half of these admissions are fall related [55]. This finding underscores the value of recommending PA to patients who do not initially complain of or seek care for physical issues.

Moreover, PACE was well received by patients, as evidenced by their high level of satisfaction and perceived safety. These results, in addition to the absence of falls and additional pain are also promising as they represent crucial factors that encourage older adults to participate and remain motivated in PA [56]. Additionally, despite the diverse health profiles of the patients, the SUS score indicates an acceptable level of PACE tool usability (SUS > 70).

Finally, we observed a low effect size (r ≤ 0.3) of the PACE intervention on physical health. Several factors may explain these findings. First, the small sample size (phase II design) could contribute to the observed effects as the study was not primarily designed for this exploratory objective. Additionally, the heterogeneity of adherence amongst participants could also play a role, as a dose–response relationship is expected in unsupervised home exercises. An insufficient amount of exercise (volume*intensity) performed may result in a weak to moderate effect on physical performance [5, 6]. The decrease in training volume observed overall testifies to the need to optimise motivational strategies to ensure maximum adherence throughout the program. Furthermore, although the kinesiologist had the opportunity to propose specific adaptations or progressions of exercises during follow-up calls conducted during the first week and subsequently every 3 weeks, these exercise modifications were based on patient-reported difficulty and feedback. This potentially resulted in an insufficient progression in training stimulus over 12 weeks. The study took place during COVID-19 restrictions, a period linked to declining health in older adults [57]. Thus, stable physical health pre- and post-intervention may be seen as a positive outcome. Other limitations such as the lack of a control group and a short follow-up period hinders the ability to assess PACE’s effectiveness over the course of the study and its long-term impact on physical health and patient adherence. To address these limitations, randomised controlled trials (RCTs) including a control group (usual care without PACE) are needed. On another note, the pilot study demonstrates well-established implementation strategies tailored for pre-frail and frail older adults, that benefit from co-designed approaches and interdisciplinary expertise. Another strength worth noting is the study’s pioneering approach to assessing a PA prescription tool, taking into account perspectives from both users and prescribers, hence providing valuable insight into the feasibility and acceptability of PACE in geriatric outpatient clinics. Lastly, the versatility of the intervention is noteworthy as it allows application across various geriatric care settings. This flexibility enhances its potential for broader implementation and adoption in different healthcare contexts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our pilot study demonstrates that implementing the PACE tool in an outpatient geriatric clinic is feasible, safe and acceptable to patients and healthcare professionals. However, further RCTs are needed to examine health adaptations of the PACE intervention. This step is crucial before considering PACE as a potential preventive care strategy against physical deconditioning and premature ageing resulting from an inactive lifestyle.
