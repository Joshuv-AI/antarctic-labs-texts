Inflammation related proteins and PLCG1 may contribute to papillary thyroid carcinoma risk through a potential regulatory axis
Authors: Shi-Qi Wang, Miao Yang, De-Wei Rao, Yan-Jun Su, Ruo-Chuan Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPapillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is usually indolent, but a subset of patients shows aggressive behavior. Understanding protein-level mechanisms is essential for identifying new therapeutic targets.Materials and methodsWe integrated protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL)-based Mendelian randomization (MR), transcriptomic validation, and mediation analysis to investigate protein-level causal pathways in PTC. Genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics for PTC were obtained from the FinnGen cohort (2400 cases, 78,749 controls). Candidate proteins were selected based on prior evidence of involvement in pyroptosis-related pathways, including phospholipase C gamma 1 (PLCG1), which has been functionally linked to inflammation-associated cell death. pQTL data were sourced from the UK Biobank (UKB) and deCODE datasets. Two-sample MR was conducted using the inverse variance weighting (IVW) method, with sensitivity analyses for pleiotropy and heterogeneity. Mediation analysis was used to estimate indirect effects of upstream proteins on PTC risk via PLCG1. Differential expression was assessed using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project via the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis 2 (GEPIA2) platform.ResultsAmong 11 pyroptosis-related proteins with available pQTL instruments, the genetically predicted plasma level of phospholipase C gamma 1 (PLCG1) was significantly associated with reduced PTC risk (OR = 0.679, 95% CI 0.520–0.887, p = 0.004). Its downregulation in tumor tissues was confirmed by RNA-sequencing data from TCGA-THCA (p < 0.001). We next performed MR using data from the UKB-PPP, identifying 118 proteins with FDR-significant causal associations with PTC risk (FDR < 0.1), including 58 protective and 60 risk-enhancing proteins. Of the 118 proteins, 28 were causally associated with PLCG1 expression, with inflammation-related proteins comprising the largest proportion (46.4%). Mediation analysis revealed that SMPD3 (25.21%, p = 0.008), SIRT1 (22.70%, p = 0.007), and PPM1B (19.91%, p = 0.005) exerted significant indirect effects on PTC risk via PLCG1. These proteins were also downregulated in tumor tissues, with SMPD3 showing the most pronounced decrease (log₂FC = − 1.303, adjp = 3.63 × 10−127).ConclusionOur study findings suggest a potential regulatory axis in which inflammation-related proteins, such as SMPD3, may influence the risk of papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) through the pyroptosis-associated mediator PLCG1. These associations suggest a possible protein-level pathway in PTC, with SMPD3 and PLCG1 as promising molecular candidates, although further experimental validation is required to confirm their functional roles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03864-7.

Introduction

Thyroid cancer is the most common malignancy of the endocrine system, with PTC accounting for over 80% of all thyroid malignancies [1]. In recent years, thyroid cancer has emerged as one of the most commonly diagnosed malignancies worldwide, with its incidence ranking among the top ten cancers and approximately 800,000 new cases reported annually [2]. Although the overall mortality is relatively low compared to other solid tumors, the rapidly increasing incidence and the potential for aggressive clinical behavior in a subset of patients underscore the growing clinical burden of PTC [3].

PTC is typically characterized by indolent growth and favorable prognosis, with surgical resection being the primary treatment modality [4]. Most patients achieve excellent long-term survival; however, disease progression due to local invasion or distant metastasis (DM) still occurs in a subset of cases. In patients with DM, the 5-year survival rate can decline sharply to approximately 40% [5, 6]. For progressive or metastatic disease, especially in differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC) and medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), conventional chemotherapy has limited efficacy [7]. Targeted therapies, such as multikinase inhibitors, may improve progression-free survival but often fail to extend overall survival [8–10]. Moreover, once resistance emerges, disease progression tends to accelerate. Immunotherapy remains under clinical investigation, and external beam radiation offers minimal benefit [11, 12]. Radioiodine (131I) therapy is only effective in iodine-avid DTC, whereas patients with radioiodine-refractory disease have poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options [13]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify novel molecular biomarkers and therapeutic targets to improve outcomes in high-risk PTC populations.

Pyroptosis is a form of programmed cell death distinct from apoptosis and necrosis, characterized by membrane pore formation, cell swelling, and release of proinflammatory intracellular contents [14]. It is typically triggered by inflammasome activation and executed by gasdermin family proteins. While pyroptosis contributes to host defense, its dysregulation may lead to chronic inflammation and tumor-promoting conditions in certain cancers, such as colitis-associated colorectal cancer and asbestos-related mesothelioma [15, 16]. Importantly, as an immunogenic form of cell death, pyroptosis has also shown potential as a novel anticancer strategy through the activation of strong antitumor immunity [17]. These dual effects highlight its context-dependent role in tumor biology and suggest that pyroptosis-related pathways may serve as therapeutic targets in certain cancers.

Among pyroptosis-associated molecules, PLCG1 is a key mediator linking upstream immune signals to downstream execution of inflammatory cell death [18]. It acts at the intersection of pyroptosis-related pathways by regulating intracellular calcium flux and activating downstream effectors such as NF-κB, MAPK, and inflammasome components [19]. Notably, direct experimental evidence has shown that PLCG1 is required for gasdermin-dependent pyroptosis: in lethal polymicrobial sepsis models, macrophage pyroptosis driven by cleaved gasdermin D (GSDMD-NT) proceeds in a PLCG1-dependent manner, and perturbing PLCG1 attenuates GSDMD-mediated cell death [20]. Consistently, PLCγ1–calcium signaling has been demonstrated to contribute to endothelial GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, and inhibition of PLCγ1 or calcium chelation mitigates pyroptotic injury [21]. Mechanistically, PLCG1 hydrolyzes PIP2 to generate IP3, which mobilizes Ca²⁺ via IP3R; such Ca²⁺ mobilization is a recognized upstream trigger for NLRP3 inflammasome activation that culminates in caspase-1–dependent GSDMD cleavage and pore formation [20]. Prior studies have shown that aberrant activation of PLCG1 contributes to excessive cytokine release, T cell hyperactivation, and immune dysregulation, underscoring its role in inflammation-associated diseases [22]. In cancer contexts, PLCG1 has been implicated in tumor progression and immune evasion, although its functions appear to be highly context-dependent [23]. Together, these data place PLCG1 upstream of the gasdermin execution machinery for pyroptosis through Ca²⁺/inflammasome pathways; however, its thyroid-specific roles remain to be defined.

In the present study, we identified PLCG1 as a candidate effector through a pQTL-based MR screen focusing on pyroptosis-related proteins derived from previously published literature. We subsequently applied a multi-omics strategy to further investigate the potential mediating role of PLCG1 in linking upstream inflammation-related proteins to PTC risk. This work aims to uncover a protein-level regulatory axis that may contribute to thyroid cancer development.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Summary-level GWAS data for papillary thyroid carcinoma were obtained from the FinnGen Release 12 dataset. The specific phenotype used was “C3_THYROID_PAPILLARY_ADENO_EXALLC,” representing papillary adenocarcinoma of the thyroid gland, with controls excluding all cancers. This classification is based on the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3), under the disease category “Neoplasms, from cancer register.” The dataset comprised 2400 PTC cases and 78,749 controls and is publicly accessible at https://www.finngen.fi/en/access_results.

A total of 33 pyroptosis-related genes were curated based on two recent studies by Zhao et al. [24] and Li et al. [25] (see Supplementary Table S1). These genes are recurrently implicated across multiple cancer types and represent pivotal components of the pyroptotic signaling cascade, encompassing cytokine release (e.g., IL-1β, IL-18), immune cell recruitment, and epithelial cell death. To enhance the rigor of candidate gene selection, only genes consistently reported in both studies were prioritized.

Plasma pQTL data were sourced from two large-scale proteomic datasets. The first included 4907 pQTL summary files from the deCODE database, derived from an Icelandic population (https://www.decode.com/summarydata/). After applying standard clumping criteria (p-value < 5 × 10⁻⁸, linkage disequilibrium r² < 0.1, and clumping window of 10,000 kb), 4759 pQTL files were retained for downstream analysis. The second source was the UKB-PPP, comprising individuals of European ancestry (https://www.synapse.org/Synapse:syn51364943/wiki/622119). From a total of 2940 pQTL summary files, 2619 met the aforementioned filtering thresholds and were included in the analysis.

Transcriptomic data used for gene expression validation were obtained from TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), a comprehensive and publicly accessible resource for large-scale cancer genomic profiles. To enhance the robustness of differential expression analysis, normal thyroid tissue data were derived by integrating matched normal samples from TCGA (n = 59) with non-diseased thyroid tissue samples from the GTEx project (n = 278), yielding a total of 337 normal controls. A total of 512 tumor tissue samples from thyroid carcinoma (THCA) were included in the analysis.

All data used in this study were obtained from publicly available de-identified datasets; therefore, no additional ethical approval or informed consent was required.

Two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis

Two-sample MR analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.0) with the TwoSampleMR package. This study aimed to evaluate the causal effects of pQTL-encoded plasma proteins on PTC risk, as well as to explore potential causal relationships among pQTL-regulated proteins themselves. Instrumental variables (IVs) were selected based on genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10⁻⁸). Prior to clumping, SNPs with a MAF < 0.01 were excluded. LD clumping was performed using a threshold of r² < 0.1 and a window size of 10,000 kilobases. LD estimates were based on European ancestry samples from the 1000 Genomes Project (Phase 3). To ensure instrument strength, F-statistics were calculated for all SNPs, and only instruments with F > 10 were retained. When only a single instrumental SNP was available for a given exposure-outcome pair, the Wald ratio method was applied. For exposures with multiple instruments, several complementary MR methods were implemented, including IVW, MR-Egger regression, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode approaches. Among these, IVW was used as the primary method for causal effect estimation.

To assess the robustness of the causal inferences, heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q statistic, and horizontal pleiotropy was tested via the MR-Egger intercept. Additionally, leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to determine whether any single SNP disproportionately influenced the MR estimates. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to control for multiple testing, with a significance threshold set at FDR < 0.1.

Mediation analysis

To identify potential upstream regulators of the pyroptosis-associated molecule PLCG1, we performed mediation analysis using summary-level data derived from the UKB. Specifically, we first estimated the causal effect of each plasma protein on PLCG1 expression (β₁), followed by the effect of PLCG1 expression on PTC risk (β₂). The mediated (indirect) effect through PLCG1 (β₁₂) was subsequently calculated, along with the mediation proportion (β₁₂_p), representing the proportion of the total effect mediated by PLCG1. To statistically assess the significance of the indirect effect, we applied the Sobel test, a widely used method based on the delta approximation, which evaluates whether the product of the two path coefficients (β₁ × β₂) is significantly different from zero under the assumption of approximate normality. Associations with p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. This approach allowed us to evaluate whether inflammation-related proteins influence PTC risk via PLCG1 as an intermediate effector.

Differential gene expression analysis

To further validate the findings from MR analysis, we conducted differential gene expression analysis using transcriptomic data from TCGA. The analysis was performed through the GEPIA2 online platform (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn), utilizing the “Expression DIY” module. Differential expression was assessed using one-way ANOVA, with significance thresholds set at |log₂ fold change| > 1 and a q-value < 0.01. GEPIA2 is an updated web tool based on TCGA and GTEx data, offering user-friendly and robust functions for transcriptome-level analysis [26].

Results

Screening and identification of candidate proteins

To explore the potential relationship between pyroptosis-associated proteins and PTC, we intersected a curated set of pyroptosis-related genes with large-scale plasma pQTL datasets. This integrative approach aimed to identify proteins encoded by pyroptosis-related genes with available pQTL instruments suitable for MR analysis. A total of 11 such proteins were found to have corresponding pQTL data (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table S2). After applying instrumental variable selection criteria and IVW assumptions, six proteins remained eligible for MR analysis. Using the IVW method, we evaluated the causal effects of these genetically predicted plasma levels of these six proteins on PTC risk (Fig. 1B). Among them, the genetically predicted plasma level of PLCG1 showed a statistically significant association (OR = 0.679, 95% CI 0.520–0.887, p = 0.0044), suggesting a protective role against PTC development. Detailed information on the IVs is provided in Table S3, and the full MR results are presented in Table S4. To further support this finding, we performed gene expression validation using RNA-sequencing data from the TCGA-THCA cohort. The results demonstrated that PLCG1 was significantly downregulated in PTC tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (log₂FC = − 2.019, p < 0.001; Fig. 1C), consistent with the MR results. Therefore, PLCG1 was selected for subsequent analyses as candidate protein.

MR analysis identifies circulating proteins causally associated with PTC

To systematically evaluate the causal impact of circulating proteins on the risk of PTC, we performed MR analysis using pQTL data from the UKB-PPP. Initially, 344 proteins showed nominally significant associations with PTC risk. Details of the selected IVs and complete MR findings are summarized in Tables S3 and S4, respectively. We subsequently applied FDR correction to account for multiple testing, identifying 118 proteins that passed the significance threshold (FDR < 0.1). Of these, 58 proteins were negatively associated with PTC risk, while 60 were positively associated.

Notably, several protective proteins exhibited strong inverse associations with PTC risk, including PPM1B (OR = 0.363, 95% CI 0.188–0.703, FDR = 0.074), USP28 (OR = 0.364, 95% CI 0.216–0.614, FDR = 0.016), and CDC27 (OR = 0.370, 95% CI 0.201–0.683, FDR = 0.057) (Fig. 2A). In contrast, KLK3 (OR = 3.315, 95% CI 1.494–7.356, FDR = 0.082), EIF1AX (OR = 2.317, 95% CI 1.352–3.971, FDR = 0.068), and CHRD1L (OR = 2.197, 95% CI 1.444–3.342, FDR = 0.020) were identified as the most significantly risk-enhancing proteins (Fig. 2B). The complete list of 118 FDR-significant proteins is provided in Supplementary Table S6.

Identification of Circulating proteins causally associated with PLCG1 expression

To investigate upstream plasma proteins that may regulate PLCG1 expression, we conducted MR analyses using 118 pQTL-associated proteins. A total of 28 proteins showed statistically significant causal associations with PLCG1 expression, including 8 with negative and 20 with positive effects (Fig. 3A and B). The corresponding IVs and MR estimates for these proteins are detailed in Tables S3 and S4, respectively. These associations exhibited moderate to strong effect sizes, with ORs ranging from 0.915 to 1.684. Functionally, inflammation-related proteins accounted for the largest proportion (13/28, 46.4%), followed by those involved in metabolic regulation (7/28, 25.0%), oncogenic signaling (5/28, 17.9%), and other biological processes (3/28, 10.7%). Notably, inflammation-related proteins such as PPM1B (OR = 1.684, p < 0.001), SIRT1 (OR = 1.606, p < 0.001), and SMPD3 (OR = 1.492, p < 0.001) showed the strongest positive associations with PLCG1 expression, while IL10 (OR = 0.915, p = 0.012) and B2M (OR = 0.934, p = 0.001) represented the most prominent negative regulators.

Mediation and expression analysis of inflammation-related proteins in PTC

To explore the upstream regulatory mechanisms underlying the tumor-suppressive effects of the pyroptosis-associated gene PLCG1 in PTC, we first identified 28 proteins that were causally associated with PLCG1 expression. Among them, we selected those that also showed a significant causal association with PTC risk in our prior analysis, leading to the inclusion of SMPD3, SIRT1, and PPM1B for mediation analysis. Three proteins—SMPD3, SIRT1, and PPM1B—were found to influence PTC risk through significant mediation effects (p < 0.05), suggesting their potential involvement in regulating disease progression via PLCG1-dependent pathways. Specifically, the estimated mediation proportions were, 25.21% for SMPD3 (p = 0.008), 22.70% for SIRT1 (p = 0.007), and 19.91% for PPM1B (p = 0.005). Detailed results of the mediation analysis are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

To further assess the relevance of these proteins in PTC, we examined their mRNA expression levels in tumor versus normal tissues using the TCGA-THCA dataset. The differential expression analysis showed significant downregulation of SMPD3 (log₂FC = − 1.303, adjp = 3.63 × 10⁻¹²⁷), PPM1B (log₂FC = − 0.315, adjp = 1.21 × 10⁻²⁴), SIRT1 (log₂FC = − 0.047, adjp = 1.17 × 10⁻⁶). Among them, SMPD3 met the threshold of |log₂FC| > 1 and were markedly downregulated in tumor tissues, suggesting potential tumor-suppressive effects consistent with the findings of both MR and mediation analyses. The expression differences of these three genes between tumor and normal tissues are shown in box plots (Fig. 4A–C). These results support the hypothesis that SMPD3 may inhibit PTC progression through PLCG1-dependent mechanisms. Specifically, higher expression levels of SMPD3 were associated with increased PLCG1 expression, which in turn was linked to a reduced risk of PTC, indicating a negative regulatory pathway through which this upstream protein exerts its protective effect by enhancing PLCG1. The corresponding mediation pathway is illustrated in Fig. 4D.

Discussion

In this study, we applied an integrative multi-omics approach to explore the role of pyroptosis-related proteins in PTC. PLCG1 was identified as a protective effector through pQTL-based MR and validated as downregulated in tumor tissues. Further screening of circulating proteins revealed 118 candidates associated with PTC risk, among which 28 also influenced PLCG1 expression. Mediation analysis suggested that SMPD3, SIRT1, and PPM1B may function as upstream regulators potentially acting through PLCG1-dependent pathways. However, only SMPD3 showed consistent downregulation in tumor transcriptomes and was ultimately selected as the most plausible upstream regulator. These findings suggest a potential tumor-suppressive axis involving SMPD3 and PLCG1, providing new insights into PTC pathogenesis.

PLCG1 is a key signal transducer that connects upstream immune stimuli with downstream inflammatory and pyroptotic pathways. It regulates calcium flux and activates effectors such as NF-κB and MAPK, positioning it at the crossroads of inflammation and programmed cell death [18, 19]. Prior studies have shown that dysregulated PLCG1 activity can lead to excessive cytokine production and T cell hyperactivation [20], while in cancer, PLCG1 has been linked to tumor progression and immune evasion, particularly in glioma, pancreatic, and small-cell lung cancers [27–32]. However, its role appears highly context-dependent. In our study, PLCG1 was found to exhibit a tumor-suppressive role in PTC, which contrasts with its previously reported oncogenic behavior in other cancer types. This divergence suggests that PLCG1’s function may be influenced by the tissue-specific immune and metabolic landscape of the thyroid.

Among follicular-derived thyroid cancers, PTCs harboring BRAF^V600E mutations are particularly common and often display constitutive activation of both classical and alternative NF-κB signaling pathways, as supported by immunoblot and gel shift assays [33]. These pathways activate downstream transcription factors such as RelA and RelB, which can upregulate metastasis-associated genes like MMP1 and PLAU, thereby contributing to tumor invasion. Further, NF-κB-inducing kinase (NIK) has been implicated in sustaining this activation and may intersect with PLCG1-related cascades. Notably, oncogenic events such as BRAF^V600E and RET/PTC rearrangements have also been associated with elevated levels of inflammatory mediators, including IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and CCL chemokines, further enhancing the pro-inflammatory milieu [34].

These observations support a potential mechanistic link in which the mutation-driven inflammatory microenvironment in PTC may modulate the biological effects of PLCG1. Consistent with this, our findings showed that nearly half of the upstream proteins with significant causal associations with PLCG1 were inflammation-related, suggesting that PLCG1 may participate in immunomodulatory signaling within the thyroid tumor microenvironment.

This potential connection becomes even more relevant in the context of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT), a chronic autoimmune condition frequently co-occurring with PTC and characterized by lymphocytic infiltration [35]. HT has been reported to both increase the risk of PTC development and confer a more favorable prognosis in affected individuals [36–38], reflecting the dual roles of inflammation in promoting tumorigenesis while also enhancing antitumor immunity. Given PLCG1’s known roles in immune regulation and pyroptosis, it is plausible that PLCG1-mediated signaling may contribute to the dynamic immune processes underlying the HT–PTC relationship.

While our findings raise intriguing possibilities regarding this interplay, further functional studies are needed to directly evaluate the involvement of PLCG1 in modulating immune crosstalk between HT and PTC.

Crucially, our integrative analysis combining MR, mediation analysis, and transcriptomic validation suggested that SMPD3 may serve as a potential upstream regulator of PLCG1. SMPD3, an enzyme critical for ceramide metabolism, has been previously recognized as a tumor suppressor in hepatocellular carcinoma, where it exerts antitumor effects by modulating lipid metabolism and immune regulation, including influencing exosomal PD-L1 secretion and immune evasion mechanisms [39–41]. Consistent with these observations, SMPD3 was significantly downregulated in PTC tumor tissues and positively correlated with PLCG1 expression, suggesting that SMPD3 may enhance antitumor immunity via PLCG1-dependent signaling pathways. Given that PLCG1 is a signal integrator downstream of inflammatory triggers, its activation is highly sensitive to membrane lipid composition and upstream signaling cues. SMPD3, through its enzymatic hydrolysis of sphingomyelin to generate ceramide, not only influences membrane structure but also plays a pivotal role in organizing lipid rafts and immune synapse platforms [42]. These membrane domains are critical for the spatial localization and activation of proteins like PLCG1, particularly in T cell receptor and cytokine signaling contexts [43]. Studies in immune and cancer models have shown that ceramide-enriched microdomains can facilitate signalosome assembly, amplify receptor-induced calcium signaling, and modulate effector responses such as NF-κB and MAPK activation—all of which are downstream of PLCG1 [44]. Therefore, the observed causal relationship between SMPD3 and PLCG1 may reflect a coordinated lipid–signaling interface, wherein SMPD3 fine-tunes the biophysical environment necessary for PLCG1-mediated immune regulation and pyroptotic signaling. This model aligns with emerging evidence positioning sphingolipid metabolism as a central modulator of inflammation and cancer immunity. Furthermore, epigenetic silencing of SMPD3 via promoter hypermethylation observed in renal carcinoma provides a plausible explanation for its diminished expression in PTC and indicates potential therapeutic avenues such as DNA demethylating agents to restore SMPD3-PLCG1 mediated tumor suppression [45].

However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although Mendelian randomization offers a powerful approach for assessing potential causal relationships at the population level, it inherently relies on genetic proxies and does not account for post-transcriptional and post-translational modifications, tissue-specific effects, or temporal dynamics of protein regulation. Therefore, the observed associations may not fully reflect the biological complexity of protein function in vivo. Second, the pQTL datasets employed in our analysis were derived almost exclusively from individuals of European ancestry. This demographic restriction may affect the external validity of our findings and limit their applicability to non-European populations. Given the known inter-ethnic variability in genetic architecture and plasma protein levels, future studies incorporating diverse populations will be essential for confirming and extending our results. Third, while we validated the expression of PLCG1 and its upstream mediators using bulk transcriptomic data from TCGA), the lack of matched clinical covariates and the absence of cell-type resolution in these datasets pose additional constraints. In particular, the precise cellular origin of the expression signals—whether from malignant follicular cells, immune infiltrates, or stromal components—cannot be determined from bulk RNA-seq data alone. Finally, although our findings revealed statistically significant causal and mediating relationships, the biological mechanisms underlying these associations remain speculative. Experimental validation using in vitro and in vivo models is urgently needed to confirm the regulatory roles of SMPD3 and other upstream proteins on PLCG1 activity, as well as the involvement of PLCG1 in modulating pyroptosis pathways in thyroid epithelial cells. Functional assays, such as CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing, immunoprecipitation, and cytokine release assays, may help elucidate the mechanistic basis of these interactions and establish their relevance in thyroid carcinogenesis.

Collectively, these limitations underscore the exploratory nature of our current study and the need for further validation. While our integrative approach combining MR, mediation analysis, and transcriptomic profiling provides a promising framework for uncovering upstream regulators of PLCG1 in PTC, the findings should be interpreted with caution. In particular, we adopted a relatively liberal significance threshold (FDR < 0.1), which, although acceptable in exploratory proteomic MR analyses, warrants cautious interpretation of results, especially those with FDR values close to 0.1. To build upon this foundation, future research should prioritize mechanistic experiments in thyroid-specific models, leverage single-cell and spatial transcriptomic technologies, and incorporate pQTL datasets from diverse ancestral backgrounds. Such efforts will be essential not only to validate the causal roles proposed here but also to clarify the context-specific functions of inflammation-related proteins in the tumor immune microenvironment. Ultimately, translating these discoveries into clinically actionable targets will require bridging genetic evidence with molecular and cellular biology.

Conclusion

This study suggests potential causal pathways through which inflammation-related circulating proteins may influence PTC risk via the pyroptosis-associated effector PLCG1. In particular, SMPD3 emerged as a candidate upstream regulator that may reduce PTC risk by promoting PLCG1 expression, supported by suggestive mediation effects and consistent downregulation in tumor tissues. These findings highlight the possible biological relevance of PLCG1 in PTC pathogenesis and indicate that SMPD3 and PLCG1 represent promising biomarkers or candidate molecules warranting further investigation. However, given the nature of this population-based genetic analysis, future functional studies are necessary to confirm these relationships and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.
