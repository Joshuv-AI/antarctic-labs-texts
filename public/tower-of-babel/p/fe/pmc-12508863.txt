Coronary artery calcium burden across the pooled cohort equation versus the American Heart Association PREVENT risk calculator
Authors: Gabrielle S Gershon, Jaret R Barr, Alexander C Razavi, Yan Yang, Eshan Momin, Omar Dzaye, Seamus P Whelton, Michael J Blaha, Roger S Blumenthal, Laurence S Sperling, Carlo N De Cecco, Marly van Assen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Pooled Cohort Equation (PCE) and the Predicting Risk of cardiovascular disease EVENTs (PREVENT) calculator assess atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk. Coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring enhances ASCVD risk stratification beyond traditional risk factors, but CAC burden across PCE versus PREVENT risk groups remains unclear. This study evaluates the distribution of CAC burden across PCE and PREVENT risk groups.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted involving 7610 asymptomatic patients who underwent clinically indicated CAC scoring between 2010 and 2023. Ten-year ASCVD risk was calculated using PCE and PREVENT (low<5, borderline-intermediate 5–19, high≥20 %). CAC scores (0, 1–99, 100–299, ≥300) were compared across risk groups using Kendall Tau tests.ResultsThe mean age was 57±9 years; 41 % of patients were women and 11 % identified as Black. PCE and PREVENT classified 52.5 % and 72.6 % as low-risk, 41 % and 27.1 % as borderline-intermediate risk, and 6.5 % and 3.7 % as high-risk, respectively. Higher PCE/PREVENT risk groups had increased median CAC (p < 0.001). For borderline-intermediate risk, 71 % for PCE and 79 % for PREVENT had CAC>0, while for low risk, 45 % and 51 %. Within the borderline risk group (5–7.5 %), 20.7 % of PCE and 35 % of ASCVD PREVENT group had CAC>100.ConclusionsA significant number of patients had non-zero CAC in low-risk stratification groups for both PREVENT and PCE. CAC distribution was heterogeneous in the borderline-intermediate groups for both PREVENT and PCE. These results emphasize the significance of CAC in further stratifying risk beyond the PCE and PREVENT.Keywords: CAC, Calcium Score, CT, PCE, PREVENT

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death globally [1]. Estimates indicate that 57 to 90 % of CVD cases over a 10-year period can be attributed to modifiable risk factors, including tobacco use, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, physical inactivity, and obesity [2,3].

Cardiovascular disease prevention is guided by clinical risk stratification models to determine therapeutic approaches, including pharmacological interventions [4,5] such as lipid-lowering medications, antihypertensives, and aspirin therapy [6,7]. Validated risk stratification tools allow for informed communication between clinicians and patients to guide the initiation of pharmacologic primary prevention.

The Pooled Cohort Equation (PCE) was established for ASCVD risk stratification in 2013 by the American College of Cardiology (ACC) and American Heart Association (AHA) Task Force based on a systematic review of four large cohort studies [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. PCE incorporates risk factors for the development of ASCVD to generate a 10-year risk score of ASCVD. Most studies suggest that PCE has a tendency to overestimate risk, while other studies have validated its performance across many populations [10,12,13].

In 2023, PREVENT was developed and validated as a contemporary risk calculator considering the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic health paradigm while also facilitating risk assessment of total CVD and heart failure (HF). As compared to PCE, PREVENT incorporates BMI, eGFR, and statin use while omitting race [14]. While the PCE calculator was developed from a population of 26,000 people, the PREVENT calculator was developed from a population of 3.3 million people [14]. The PREVENT online calculator uses a base model to estimate risk, but additional models that include hemoglobin A1c, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and zip code as a surrogate for social deprivation index were also included [15,16].

Beyond clinical risk scores, cardiac imaging is a noninvasive approach to visualize and quantify subclinical atherosclerosis and has been playing an increasing role in risk stratification and guiding preventive strategies. Non-contrast cardiac-gated CT imaging can quantify coronary artery calcium (CAC) to refine risk stratification, particularly in patients with borderline to intermediate ASCVD risk as defined by their clinical risk score [6,[17], [18], [19], [20]]. American College of Cardiology (ACC) / American Heart Association 2019 primary prevention guidelines include CAC scoring as a validated tool for ASCVD risk assessment (Class of Recommendation Level IIa, Level of Evidence B-Nonrandomized) [6]. The combination of traditional risk prediction scores with the measurement of CAC has been shown to improve the accuracy of risk prediction [21,22] specifically using the Pooled Cohort Equation (PCE) via net reclassification improvement metrics [23]. Clinical risk scores with the additional guidance of CAC scores help clinicians to make better decisions and personalize treatment plans for each patient.

The primary objective of this study is to describe CAC distribution across PREVENT and PCE risk categories, specifically assessing the prevalence of subclinical atherosclerosis in low-risk patients and the potential for risk reclassification in borderline-intermediate risk groups. This knowledge could expand the use of CAC scoring beyond current clinical risk scores, potentially leading to more personalized and accurate cardiovascular disease prevention strategies.

Methods

A retrospective cohort was conducted using data from a diverse population from Emory Healthcare-affiliated facilities in Atlanta, GA, including both private and public hospitals, such as Grady Health System, was utilized. This study received ethical oversight from Emory University, and IRB approval was granted with a waiver for informed consent due to its retrospective nature.

Patient population

Patients were considered eligible if they underwent a cardiac CT examination that included a dedicated CAC acquisition between 2010 and 2023, met the age criteria for the relevant risk scores (40–79 years), had complete clinical documentation of input variables required to calculate the risk scores, had no prior history of ASCVD, and were asymptomatic at the time of the CT. The most prevalent indication of CAC CT was a wellness screening program for patients having one or more risk factors for cardiac disease. A total of 23,562 patients were deemed eligible in the initial search based on CT imaging criteria. After applying inclusion/exclusion criteria, the final study population comprised of 7610 patients, depicted in Fig. 1.

Diagram illustrating patient selection from 23,562 initial candidates to final study cohort of 7,610 patients, with reasons for exclusion at each step. CAC = coronary artery calcium; ASCVD = atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

Prior history of ASCVD and symptomatic screening were evaluated based on historical ICD9 and CPT codes per patient, as well as the CT order report.

PCE and prevent

PCE was originally designed for patients ages 40 to 79 years, whereas PREVENT was intended for patients ages 30 to 79 years [4]. To allow comparison, only patients ages 40 to 79 years were included. PCE and base PREVENT were retrospectively calculated for each patient. The baseline 10-year risk of ASCVD was calculated using PCE and PREVENT. Optional PREVENT risk enhancers including HbA1c, UACRR, and ZIP code were not included in this analysis. These variables had limited availability in our retrospective cohort (<5 % had complete data), making subset analysis underpowered for meaningful conclusions. The focus of our comparison was between PCE and baseline ASCVD PREVENT. CVD and HF PREVENT were incorporated as a secondary outcome for additional analysis. (Fig. 2)

Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC) burden was represented in four CAC levels: 0, 1-99, 100-299, and >300. Four clinical risk scores were calculated: 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) Pooled Cohort Equations (PCE); 10-year ASCVD Predicting Risk of cardiovascular disease EVENTs (PREVENT); 10-year CVD PREVENT; and 10-year heart failure (HF) PREVENT. The distribution of the CAC burden was described per each risk stratification (<5% = low risk; 5-19% = borderline-intermediate risk; >20% = high risk) per each risk score.

CAC scoring

ECG-gated non-contrast cardiac acquisitions included were acquired at 120kVp and reconstructed with a 3 mm slice thickness, according to clinical protocol. CAC scoring was performed per the Agatston method, defined as a weighted sum of lesions with a calcium density above 130 HU. All patients’ CT scans were analyzed using a well-validated AI algorithm to calculate the overall Agatston score by Siemens Healthineers [24,25].

Statistics

Normality was evaluated using a Q-Q plot. Normally distributed data were reported in mean (SD), while non-normally distributed data was reported as median (IQR). CAC scores across PREVENT and PCE groups were compared using Chi-square tests categorizing CAC as 0, 1–99, 100–299, or ≥300, and PCE/PREVENT 10-year risk as low (<5 %), borderline-intermediate (5–19 %), or high (≥20 %). Concordant and discordant groups were created according to agreement between PCE and PREVENT, using 7.5 % as a cutoff, as 7.5 % and above is used as the cutoff for statin eligibility by the 2018 AHA/ACC/Multisociety Cholesterol Guideline [26]. Sub-analysis of ASCVD PCE and ASCVD PREVENT risk scores within the borderline group, between 5–7.5 % risk, was performed. Within this group, the number of patients with CAC > 100 was determined, as it is recommended in the 2019 ACC/AHA Guidelines on the Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease that CAC > 100 in the borderline group would reclassify the patient into intermediate risk and clinically recommend statin therapy [6]. R (version 4.4.1) was used to perform the Kendall’s Tau, Mann-Whitney U, and Spearman tests. A threshold for significance of p < 0.05 was used.

Results

The final cohort included 7610 patients with a mean age of 57 ± 9 years; 41 % were women, and 22 % identified as non-White. Detailed demographics are shown in Table 1. Comparison of included (n = 7610) versus excluded patients (n = 15,952) revealed no significant differences in age (57±9 vs 58±10 years, p = 0.12), gender distribution (58 % vs 57 % male, p = 0.34), or racial composition (p = 0.08). Exclusions were primarily due to missing clinical data (42 %) rather than demographic factors, suggesting minimal selection bias and supporting the generalizability of our findings.

CAC burden across PCE/PREVENT scores

ASCVD PCE classified 53 % (n = 3997) as low-risk (<5 %), 41 % (n = 3118) as borderline-intermediate risk (5–19 %), and 7 % (n = 195) as high-risk (>20 %), whereas ASCVD PREVENT classified 73 % (n = 5523) low, 27 % (n = 2059) borderline-intermediate, and 4 % (n = 28) high-risk patients. Among all patients, 41 % (n = 2474) had CAC=0, 37 % (n = 2189) had CAC between 1–99, 11 % (n = 687) had CAC between 100–299, and 11 % (n = 653) had CAC ≥ 300.

For both PCE and PREVENT, higher-risk groups had higher median CAC compared to low-risk and borderline-intermediate groups. Additionally, CAC distribution was significantly different between risk groups for both ASCVD PCE and ASCVD PREVENT (p < 0.001). The smallest group (n = 27, 0.4 %) had discordant low PCE (< 7.5 %) and high PREVENT scores (> 7.5 %) with a median CAC score of 26.8 (IQR:0–190). The high PCE, high PREVENT concordant group (n = 931) had a median CAC of 85 (IQR: 6–358).

Sub-analysis on the theoretical use of CAC to re-classify risk

Among the concordant low-risk group (< 7.5 %), 13 % had CAC scores > 100, which would recalibrate risk categorization in favor of therapeutic intervention, following the aforementioned guidelines. A total of 1092 patients (14 %) fell into the borderline risk category between 5–7.4 % for ASCVD PCE and 1080 (14 %) for ASCVD PREVENT. Within this borderline group, 226 patients (21 %) from ASCVD PCE and 378 patients (35 %) from ASCVD PREVENT group had a CAC > 100 and were therefore recategorized as intermediate risk.

Discussion

This study investigated the distribution of CAC scores among PCE and PREVENT risk scores in a clinical cohort of asymptomatic patients. Higher-risk groups demonstrated higher median CAC scores for both PCE and all PREVENT clinical risk scores. As a newly available tool for CVD and HF risk stratification, PREVENT risk scores demonstrated similar CAC distributions for ASCVD, CVD, and HF.

Our analysis reveals a notable prevalence of non-zero CAC scores across all clinical risk score groups, indicating that even patients classified as low risk according to ASCVD PCE and PREVENT may exhibit significant underlying subclinical atherosclerosis.

The comparison of CAC distribution between high- and low- ASCVD risk groups using the PCE and PREVENT equations highlights significant differences in CAC burden. The findings of our study corroborate evidence revealed in similar studies, which note the tendency of the PREVENT equation to classify risk lower relative to PCE [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. PREVENT has notably improved discrimination and calibration, as determined by Khan et al [14]. However, it is important to note that the 10-year risk cutoffs for future guidelines may change for PREVENT, as compared to PCE, to optimize the use of PREVENT risk scores. Further investigation is warranted to assess the predictive accuracy of these equations as compared to ASCVD event outcomes.

The removal of race from the PREVENT calculator represents an important advancement toward more equitable risk assessment. However, our findings demonstrate persistent racial differences in CAC burden, with significant variations in CAC distribution among different racial groups even within similar risk categories. This highlights the continued importance of CAC scoring in providing objective atherosclerosis assessment that transcends social constructs while maintaining clinical relevance across diverse populations.

CAC scoring provides critical insight for clinical decision-making by stratifying ASCVD risk more accurately based on their CAC scores. Mahabadi et al [31]. found that among patients with PCE-derived risk scores deemed as eligible for statin therapy under ACC/AHA guidelines, 19 % had a CAC score of 0 while 38 % had a CAC score of >100. Our study found similar results.

Moreover, numerous studies have discussed the utility of CAC for decision-making in asymptomatic patients who fall into intermediate risk categories [19,22,31] Our results reinforce the study by Blaha et al., which illuminated the preeminence of CAC as a risk stratification tool, specifically in the intermediate (5–20 % 10-year ASCVD risk) category [32]. This is evident in the subset of patients whose risk prediction is substantially changed due to the addition of CAC. The reclassification of risk based on the addition of CAC indicates the impactful role of imaging.

CAC scoring has also emerged as a valuable tool for “de-risking" patients who may otherwise be recommended for statin therapy based on traditional ASCVD risk calculations. According to a study by Nasir et al., 41 % of patients recommended for statin therapy (7.5–20 % ASCVD risk as calculated by PCE) were found to have a CAC of zero [33]. This reclassified their mean 10-year ASCVD risk to 4.6 %, resulting in a change of stratification from to “not recommended”. Similarly, this study demonstrated the role of CAC in the “de-risking” of patients with a 10-year ASCVD risk of 5 % to 10 %, showing that up to 50 % of patients in this group have a CAC score of 0. Our study demonstrated similar findings. These results strengthen the evidence in favor of the utility of zero CAC for "de-risking” patients.

These findings not only highlight the potential impact of using CAC scoring to inform clinical decisions but also raise important questions about the implications of adopting newer risk prediction models, such as PREVENT, on treatment eligibility and health equity. Our findings align with those from Dio et al., who demonstrated that using PREVENT equations could significantly alter risk categorization and treatment eligibility in a nationally representative sample of US adults [27].

Lastly, the PREVENT risk score includes options to calculate risk for HF and CVD. Our results showed that 10-year HF and CVD PREVENT scores exhibit similar CAC burden distribution to ASCVD PREVENT. Due to the novelty of PREVENT, particularly in predicting CVD and HF, further investigation is needed to validate the effectiveness of risk scores for CVD and HF diverse patient populations. As these new models evolve, understanding their comparative accuracy and utility relative to traditional methods will be crucial for optimizing prevention strategies and improving patient outcomes in CVD and HF management.

These findings have important real-world clinical implications. By integrating CAC scoring with both PCE and PREVENT risk calculations, clinicians can enhance their decision-making processes and provide more personalized treatment recommendations. This approach allows for better identification of patients who may benefit from statin therapy despite lower traditional risk scores, as well as 'de-risking' of patients who may be spared unnecessary treatment. Our findings underscore the need for continued evaluation of PREVENT risk stratification models. As the medical community moves toward more personalized approaches to cardiovascular disease prevention, the incorporation of non-invasive imaging techniques like CAC scoring will be crucial in refining patient management strategies. By leveraging both clinical risk assessment tools and imaging data, clinicians can enhance their decision-making processes, ultimately leading to more tailored interventions and improved outcomes for patients at risk for cardiovascular events.

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first study that compares the CAC distribution across both PCE and PREVENT risk groups in a diverse cohort of patients receiving CAC while being asymptomatic. This study was limited in the sample size due to a lack of clinical data to calculate PREVENT and PCE scores. This is an inherent disadvantage in using a retrospective clinical cohort study and not a prospective screening cohort. Additionally, risk-enhancing variables like UACRR, HbA1c, and zip code were not included as optional variables in the PREVENT risk scoring, which may have underestimated ASCVD risk in high-risk groups thereby favoring the PCE model. Further research should be done investigating the difference in outcomes between the risk score classification groups versus CAC groups and the added value of the optional variables. Additionally, future studies should evaluate the cost-effectiveness of CAC screening across different risk strata to optimize resource allocation in cardiovascular disease prevention.

Conclusion

This study illustrates the comparable prevalence of CAC across clinical risk score categories provided by the PCE and the PREVENT risk calculator and highlights the importance of integrating CAC scoring into clinical practice, particularly among patients falling in borderline- to intermediate- risk categories, as it provides additional insights into the presence of atherosclerotic disease that may not be fully captured by traditional risk scores alone. This study demonstrates the clinical utility of CAC scoring in enhancing risk stratification beyond traditional clinical risk scores, with significant implications for personalized primary prevention strategies across both PCE and PREVENT risk categories.
