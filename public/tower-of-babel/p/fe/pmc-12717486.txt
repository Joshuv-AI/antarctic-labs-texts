The Pregnant Surgeon: Welcoming the Advanced Ergonomics of Robotic Surgery
Authors: Ashley Jenkin, Kim‐Chi Phan‐Thien, Carina Chow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe detrimental effect of poor operative ergonomics in surgery cannot be underestimated. It's associated with negative outcomes such as increased burn‐out, fatigue, clinically relevant pain, and early retirement. Pregnancy creates additional challenges for female surgeons to prevent injury while adjusting to anthropometric changes that are difficult to accommodate for using open and laparoscopic surgical techniques. The robotic platform has been associated with improved ergonomic capabilities while maintaining dexterity and access. Robotic surgery will likely chaperone in a new era of possibility for female surgeons in particular of career longevity, reduced leave requirements, and improved comfort while operating pre‐ and post‐partum.Keywords: ergonomic, occupational hazard, pregnancy, robot, surgery

Issues With Ergonomics in Surgery

The negative impact of suboptimal operative ergonomics on the longevity and well‐being of surgeons is well established [1, 2]. Poor ergonomics can result in musculoskeletal (MSK) injuries, clinically relevant pain, fatigue, and increased burn‐out, and result in early retirement [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. The combination of prolonged static postures and extended procedural time has resulted in an increase in neck and shoulder pain reported by minimally invasive surgeons compared to open [2], which is true across multiple subspecialties [1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9]. Lack of awareness, training, and uptake of available interventions likely contribute to the significant impact poor ergonomics can have on work‐related morbidity [3, 10, 11].

Targeted solutions to improve ergonomics in the operating environment, such as multiple adjustable monitors and anti‐fatigue mats, have become a ‘hot topic’ in the literature [1, 2, 12, 13], suggesting a positive shift in surgeons' perspective on self‐preservation and longevity compared to the traditional ‘surgical martyr’. Despite this, in a survey of experienced robotic surgeons, 10% reported making no adjustments at all to the ergonomic settings before or during a case [8]. The surgical environment cannot necessarily be standardised; patient size varies, surgical instruments are subject to regulation, and regular breaks during long procedures are not always an option [2]. Pregnancy, in particular, creates ergonomic challenges as the anthropometric differences between surgeons are rapidly changed over time [1]. The role of the robotic platform, where personalised ergonomics and customisability for a neutral posture are built into the console, may usher in a new era of surgeon‐welfare optimisation.

Pregnancy Risk in Surgery

Gender has been variably associated with differences in reported complications and ergonomic challenges [2, 4, 8, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. For example, smaller hand size often reported by women can negatively impact instrument use in laparoscopy, ureteroscopy, and endoscopy and result in an increased risk of upper‐extremity injury [15, 16, 17, 18]. More female trainees report equipment is not optimised for their use, increasing their vulnerability to occupational risk, particularly with repetitive use [15, 17]. Women generally have a smaller stature and reduced muscle mass compared to male colleagues, which increases the physical workload associated with performing similar tasks [15, 16, 17, 18, 19], but increased physical strain disparity between genders persists even when glove size and height are controlled for [15, 16].

Pregnancy exaggerates many of the physical risks associated with surgery, while also creating others. Prolonged standing, heavy lifting, long working hours, poor sleep, and psychological stress have all been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, while contributing to absenteeism and lack of motivation in healthcare workers during pregnancy [19, 20, 21, 22]. Hormonal changes result in joint laxity and hypermobility, which are associated with backache, an altered gait, and an increased risk of falls and repetitive strain injuries for the gravid surgeon [19, 23, 24, 25]. The physiological adaptations in pregnancy also result in an increased risk of anaemia, fluid retention, neuropraxias, and pain, particularly of the extremities, which may be aggravated by the prolonged standing, static postures, and the rigors of a surgical career [19, 25]. As the pregnancy progresses, the surgeon's ergonomic needs require further adaptation through time, for which traditional platforms may not be able to easily accommodate.

Special consideration also needs to be given to exposure to ionising radiation, as well as biological and chemical agents in theatre [19, 21, 26, 27, 28, 29]. These occupational risks have been associated with an increased risk of preterm birth, spontaneous abortion, birth defects, and low birth weight babies, which has further implications for the surgeon's psychological wellbeing as well as their ability to return to work [19, 21, 26, 27, 28, 30]. These factors can understandably worsen cognitive load and fatigue, which may negatively impact outcomes [6, 31, 32, 33]. Preterm birth has specifically been attributed to long working hours for surgeons, particularly when working more than 60 h per week [30, 34]. Many surgeons also choose to return to work early post‐partum, especially when completing training, which may further aggravate adverse outcomes from occupational hazards, cognitive load, and ergonomic risks [29, 30, 35]. The potential consequences of being pregnant in surgery are multifactorial, and any measures to mitigate the associated risks need to be considered in the context of the individual and their unique situation, but improvements in occupational safety and operative ergonomics will likely play an important role.

Laparoscopic Versus Robotic Ergonomics

The benefits of minimally‐invasive surgery for the patient in terms of reduced post‐operative pain and recovery time does not extend to the minimally‐invasive surgeon [2, 3, 4, 6, 14, 36, 37, 38]. Reduced freedom of movement and prolonged operative times result in more work‐related injuries for the contemporary surgeon [1, 2, 4]. In meta‐analysis, laparoscopic surgeons are three to five times more likely to experience clinically significant pain, numbness, and fatigue than with open surgery [2]. However, pain was only reported in 3% of robotic surgeons [2]. Muscle fatigue and fatigue recovery is also improved using a robotic platform compared to laparoscopy, with surgeons demonstrating more balanced muscle use that results in lower muscular demand [6, 36, 39]. This may facilitate a faster return to work post‐partum, as well as following non‐pregnancy associated procedures where the strain of recommencing surgery is lessened [2, 38]. Furthermore, poor ergonomics and its associated strain and discomfort may negatively impact the cognitive load required to perform the procedure. More complicated procedures require increased focus, and in combination with high physical demands can result in increased operative error, inadvertent injuries, and worsened patient outcomes [31, 32, 40, 41, 42].

Robotic platforms are touted to provide improved precision while supporting positioning and access [6, 8, 43, 44, 45]. Avoiding prolonged standing, particularly with forward neck flexion, has been associated with a significant reduction in associated operative discomfort [3, 4]. The robotic assistant or trainee can also often remain seated for the majority of the case as well (see Figure 1). Attention to appropriate console set‐up and positioning is still relevant for the surgeon at the console, but neutral posturing is easier to achieve and maintain than with other operative modalities (see Figure 2) [1, 8, 39]. Static head and neck postures at the console have also been more recently mitigated with open‐console designs, which may also limit associated MSK injuries (see Figure 3) [12]. The presence or lack of foot pedals at the console can also variably impact the physical strain associated with ergonomics in robotic surgeries [39]. The customisability of robotic consoles does however allow junior surgeons' ergonomic requirements to be catered to, whereas with more traditional platforms the room and positioning tends to suit the more senior surgeon [3].

Surgical assistants for the robotic platforms are often able to sit down throughout the majority of robotic procedures with modifiable screens, facilitating improved comfort and ergonomic advantage over other surgical modalities. The addition of lumbar support would improve positioning, but is not always feasible in practice.

In laparoscopic surgery, ergonomics focus is centred on appropriate instrument selection, table and monitor height, and avoiding extreme posturing [1]. However, the majority of these recommendations become obsolete in robotic procedures with a surgeon at an adjustable console for which the platform takes on the mechanical strains of achieving dexterity for the procedure. Robotic platforms likely mitigate some of the challenges and injuries associated with having small hands—which have previously been associated with an increased risk of upper extremity and hand injuries [12, 15, 17]. Stapling devices in particular can be difficult to utilise laparoscopically for female surgeons [15], while robotically they require similarly low effort to other equipment utilised.

Robotic surgeons still report MSK complications associated with the repetitive strain required to perform surgery, and awareness of how to apply the ergonomic settings and maximise the perceived benefit is required [8, 37, 38, 46, 47]. This is particularly true in pregnancy where access and comfortable positioning will require adjustments over time. For example, controlled reach, use of the clutching feature, more than 360‐degree rotation capabilities, and movement scaling can reduce awkward joint positioning and overexertion while operating [39, 44, 45]. Neutral upper‐limb positioning on the armrest may reduce the fulcrum effect and associated upper limb discomfort, but worsen neck strain and trapezius activation [6]. Static neck postures over prolonged periods are a particular hazard associated with robotic platforms [1, 8, 46], but are also seen in laparoscopy [37, 47]. High volume robotic surgeons also report more eye‐related symptoms, reduced by high‐definition systems, which suggests that three‐dimensional vision systems may still cause visual fatigue similar to the two‐dimensional screens in laparoscopy [8]. The static hand postures required for robotic surgery may also be associated with an ongoing increased risk of upper extremity pain, without the added breaks of instrument changes that may be seen in laparoscopic surgery [46]. Prolonged operative times may also contribute to increased fatigue associated with robotic platforms over time [1, 9, 14], which may be improved with the experience of the surgeon [48]. Experience may also reduce the cognitive workload associated with operating on a robotic platform [33, 40, 41, 49]. As aforementioned, the flexibility of the console does allow for regular breaks and position changes without significantly hindering dexterity or access in a way that may not be true of other platforms, which may alleviate some of these concerns [8, 50]. Most surgeons will also use multiple modalities throughout their practice, which can also create challenges in attributing symptoms to a single platform [8]. The true impact of robotic platforms on the longevity of the surgeon still needs to be established.

Optimising Robotic Ergonomics in Pregnancy

Maximising the potential use of ergonomic settings is critical to preventing injury, particularly for the pregnant surgeon. Wheeled chairs with lower back support that facilitate rotation are an important adjunct to facilitate micro‐adjustments in posture over time and reduce strain while operating [1, 8, 19]. Improved ability to customize the console set‐up also implies adjustments can be made throughout pregnancy as body shape changes, though with limitations [46]. Wearing lead aprons, loupes, and headlights is all associated with increased physical demand in surgery [2, 9, 13, 28]. These factors can be significantly minimised through the use of a robotic platform where the surgeon is not required to be scrubbed in throughout the case, and magnification can be enhanced through the visual settings [9, 45]. Brief breaks to facilitate stretching or rotational exercises are more easily facilitated for both the assistant and surgeon in robotic procedures, and have been associated with reduced work‐related risk [13]. For the pregnant assistant, robotic platforms offer more flexibility in terms of screen position and height without impacting the primary surgeon as well [45]. Robotic procedures in the pelvis can also avoid the need for an assistant to overcompensate and stretch over the patient while on a step. Further, leaning over the patient‐warmer for prolonged periods to gain laparoscopic views and access to the pelvis may precipitate presyncope in those with low blood pressure, venous pooling, or the mechanical vena caval compression seen in pregnancy [25, 30, 51, 52]. Since the surgeon is not reliant on the assistant to maintain visualisation, position changes, brief vacancies or breaks from strenuous application of torque on hard‐to‐reach ports are no longer relevant [45, 48, 50]. The impact of robotic platforms on thenar activation and strain is also worth considering in particular given the tendency to develop or exacerbate carpal tunnel syndrome in pregnancy [22, 23], but there is conflicting evidence as to whether robotic consoles help or hinder [43, 46]. Results may therefore be hard to compare between various hand‐control and console designs. Overall, robotic platforms appear to result in less physical demand than other operative modalities, making them an optimal tool for the pregnant surgeon [1, 6, 8, 36, 37, 39, 46, 47].

Conclusion

The use of robotic platforms is not universally applicable for all procedures and specialties, but where available may offer significant advantages for the pregnant surgeon. As more women choose surgical specialties, consideration of the impact of the technical demands and occupational risk associated with pregnancy is increasingly relevant. Reducing the associated work‐related risk, improving employment longevity, and reducing discomfort are all accessible goals for the pregnant (or otherwise) surgeon. Hopefully, robotic surgery will chaperone in a new era of possibility for female surgeons in particular of career longevity, reduced leave requirements, and improved comfort while operating pre‐ and post‐partum.
