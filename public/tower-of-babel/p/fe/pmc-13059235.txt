METTL13 mediated SAA1 translation maintains cancer stem cell behavior and promotes esophageal carcinoma progression
Authors: Yu Liang, Zhaoyu Wang, Rui Chen, Shaoqing Niu, Ruoxing Zhou, Chunlong Yang, Shuishen Zhang, Qianwen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMethyltransferase 13 (METTL13) is a methyltransferase involved in mRNA translation and could function as either oncogene or tumor suppressor in cancers, but its function and mechanism in esophageal cancer remain unknown. In this study, we revealed that METTL13 promotes esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) progression through methylation-dependent mRNA translation.MethodsMETTL13 expression was analyzed using clinical database The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and ESCC samples collected. In vitro assays including CCK-8, colony formation, transwell and sphere-forming were utilized to determine the oncogenic functions of METTL13. Polyribosome-bound mRNA sequencing was performed to evaluate mRNA translation efficiencies under regulation of METTL13, which were further confirmed by quantitative RT-PCR and western blot. Alterations of Oxygen Consumption Rate (OCR) and levels of metabolites involved in lipid metabolism by seahorse assay and specific kits were detected respectively in ESCCs under METTL13 knockdown. ESCC mouse models were established to evaluate the oncogenic functions of METTL13 in vivo.ResultsMETTL13 is significantly up-regulated in clinical ESCC tissues and its high expression is associated with poor patient prognosis. Gain-of-function and loss-of-function assays demonstrated the critical function of METTL13 in regulation of ESCC progression in vitro and in vivo. Further mechanistic exploration has shown that METTL13 regulates ESCC by mediating the expression of Serum amyloid A1 (SAA1) at the translation level, which leads to lipid metabolism alteration. Sphere-forming assay experiments demonstrated that METTL13 plays an essential role in CSC-like properties in ESCC.ConclusionMETTL13 plays the essential oncogenic role in ESCC by enhancing SAA1 expression at translational level, leading to aberrant lipid metabolism. Our study provides the molecular basis for development of effective therapeutic strategies for ESCC treatment.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07936-2.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer, including esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) and ESCC, is deadly tumor with high incidence and low patient survival rate [1, 2]. ESCC accounts for more than 90% of esophageal cancers and are usually diagnosed at advanced stages [3]. Although rapid progresses have been achieved in the past decades, the mechanisms underlying esophageal cancer oncogenesis still remain largely unknown. As a result, esophageal cancer leads to high mortality rate due to lack of effective diagnostic and treatment strategies [4, 5]. Therefore, it is urgently needed to explore the molecular mechanism underlying esophageal cancer pathogenesis to develop more effective clinical diagnosis and treatment strategies for patients with esophageal cancer.

Recent studies have demonstrated that methyltransferases play important functions in esophageal cancer pathogenesis. METTL13, also known as FEAT, is a methyltransferase protein initially identified an inhibitor of nuclear cell apoptosis [6]. Besides, other studies have uncovered that METTL13 regulates the occurrence of many diseases and directly affects the survival rate of patients [7–10]. METTL13 catalyzes methylation of eukaryotic elongation factor 1A (eEF1A) lysine 55 (eEF1AK55me2) to promote the protein synthesis by affecting various cellular signaling and metabolic pathways [11, 12]. Interestingly, METTL13 could play dual function in regulation of cancers, it could function as an oncogene in certain types of cancers but play tumor suppressive role in other cancers including bladder and clear cell renal cell carcinoma [8, 9], suggesting that METTL13 could play tissue or context specific functions in various conditions. Unfortunately, the functions and mechanisms of METTL13 in ESCC have not been fully investigated.

In this study, we aimed to study the functional roles and underlying mechanisms of METTL13 in ESCC. Immunohistochemical analysis showed that METTL13 expression was up-regulated in most ESCC tissues. Cell functional experiments have shown that METTL13 plays an oncogenic role in ESCC cell proliferation and migration. Through polyribosome associated mRNA sequencing (polysome-seq) analysis, we identified lipoprotein SAA1 as a key downstream target of METTL13 and demonstrated that METTL13 regulates the malignancy of esophageal cancer by increasing the translation efficiency of SAA1. Our data suggest that METTL13 mediated translation regulation of SAA1 is a key oncogenic event in ESCC, providing a prognostic marker and therapeutic target for esophageal cancer.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

Tissue chips from patients with esophageal cancer in this study were obtained from the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (Guangzhou, China). All patient-related studies were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the hospital (B2021-131–01).

Cell culture

The cell lines KYSE-30, KYSE-150 and KYSE-510 were obtained from Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), and cultured in 5% CO2 at 37 °C with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% streptomycin-penicillin.

Plasmid construction and lentiviral transduction

The synthetic METTL13 gene and control were introduced into lentiviral vector and co-transfected with pCMVDR8.9 and pCMV-VSVG by using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen). Collect the virus and infect cells with 8 mg/mL Polybrene (Solarbio), and 3 mg/mL puromycin (Solarbio) was used to selected successfully transfected cells for 48 h. METTL13 and SAA1 over expression plasmid was generated by cloning the full-length open reading frame (ORF) into pFLAG-CMV2 vector as previously described [13].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The thick slices were incubated in a 4 °C refrigerator overnight with antibodies, and the secondary antibody was incubated at room temperature for about 2 hours. Then the slides were stained following the SABC kit (BOSTER) protocol and observed by microscope as previously described [14].

Western blot

Tissue or cell samples were isolated and extracted with RIPA buffer (Bioss) and then separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% defatted milk in TBST buffer (pH 7.5). The PVDF membrane was incubation at 4 °C with primary antibody overnight. The secondary antibody was incubated at room temperature then the result was observed by using ECL detection system (Thermo).

qRT-PCR

We use TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) to obtained total RNA. The cDNAs were prepared by using PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara). TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq II (Takara) and StepOnePlus real-time PCR system (Thermo) was used for qRT-PCR analysis after reverse transcription.

RIP-real-time qPCR

ESCC cells (2 × 107 per IP condition) were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed in RIP lysis buffer (150 mM KCl, 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 5 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM DTT, 0.5% NP-40, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail, 100 U/mL RNase inhibitor) for 15 min on ice. Lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected. For each IP, 50 µL of protein A/G magnetic beads were incubated with 5 µg indicated antibodies or IgG for 4 h at 4 °C. Antibody-coupled beads were then added to pre-cleared lysates and incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Beads were washed five times with RIP wash buffer (150 mM KCl, 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 5 mM EDTA, 0.05% NP-40, 100 U/mL RNase inhibitor). 10% lysate was saved as input. Co-precipitated RNAs were extracted using TRIzol reagent according to manufacturer’s protocol, followed by qPCR.

Cell proliferation analysis

We analysis cell viability by using Cell counting kit-8 (Dojindo) and seeded 1000 cells/well into 96-well plates. Recorded the absorbance at 450 nm by a microplate reader (Bio-Tek). For colony formation assay, we seeded 500 cells/well into 6-well plates and cultured for two weeks. The Image J software was used for calculated the colonies number after fixed with 4% PFA and stained with 1% crystal violet.

Wound healing analysis

We seeded 1 million cells into 6-well plates and make a straight scratch in each well in DMEM medium without FBS. Count and record the width of the scratch under a microscope (Leica) as previously described [14].

Trans-well analysis

We seeded 1 hundred thousand cells into an upper Trans-well plates with 200 mL serum-free medium and 600 μL of medium with 20% FBS was applied to each bottom chamber. For an invasion assay, the upper chambers were pre-coated with 2% Matrigel. After cultured, the invaded cells were stained and counted under a microscope as described [14].

Polyribosome associated mRNA sequencing (polysome-seq) and polysome-qPCR

The polyribosome-bound mRNA extraction was performed as described [15]. The bound RNA was purified and constructed for sequencing library and qRT-PCR. The library was sequenced with the Illumina Next-Seq 500 sequencer and analyzed as previously described [13].

Seahorse assay for ESCC OCR detection

ESCC cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well in a Seahorse XF96 V3 PS cell culture microplate (Agilent, catalog number 101,085-004) one day prior to the Seahorse assay. Prior to the assay, cells were incubated in a 37 °C non-CO₂ incubator for 1 hour with assay medium consisting of XF DMEM supplemented with 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine, and 10 mM glucose. Standard Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test conditions were employed, with sequential injections of the following reagents: 1.5 mM oligomycin, 1 mM FCCP, 2 mM rotenone, and 4 mM antimycin A. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) values were determined using the Agilent Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Report Generator.

Animal studies

4-week-old BALB/C nude mice obtained from Center of Experimental Animal of Sun Yat-sen University were divided into two groups (n = 5) randomly. KYSE30 cells transfected with shMETTL13 or shGFP plasmid were subcutaneously injected into mice. After 3 weeks, all mice were sacrificed and tumors were removed.

For in vivo tumor study in physiological condition, C57BL/6 mice about 4 weeks old were obtained from Center of Experimental Animal of Sun Yat-sen University. 5 × 106 mEC25 cells with or without Mettl13 knockdown were subcutaneously injected into the right dorsal flank of each mouse. The tumor volume was measured every 5 days. After 3 weeks, mice were sacrificed and tumors were collected for subsequent analysis. All the mouse experiments prior to implementation were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee (approval number: 2022–005).

Statistical analysis

The experiment was repeated three times in this study. Data was shown as mean ±SD. GraphPad Prism is used for all mathematical analysis. ns denotes not significant, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Results

METTL13 is elevated in ESCC and associated with poor prognosis

To evaluate the roles of METTL13 in cancers, we analyzed its expression using TCGA database and found that METTL13 is up-regulated in various types of cancers including esophageal carcinoma (ESCA) (Figs. 1A–B). Moreover, analysis of published GEO data also confirmed the elevated expression of METTL13 in ESCC (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, the high METTL13 expression is significantly associated with poor disease-free survival status (Fig. 1D). We further verified the association between METTL13 and esophageal cancer progression using immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining of METTL13 on tumor tissues and confirmed that METTL13 expression is associated with advanced tumor grades and cancer stages (Figs. 1E–G). Knockdown of METTL13 inhibited malignant phenotypes of ESCCs

The observed upregulation of METTL13 in ESCC implies its potential oncogenic role in ESCC pathogenesis. To validate this hypothesis, METTL13 expression was first quantified across 2 non-ESCC control and 8 ESCC cell lines using western blot analysis and the result showed METTL13 protein level was significantly elevated in ESCC cell lines compared to control cell lines (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, two cell lines exhibiting elevated METTL13 expression (KYSE-30 and KYSE-150) were selected for functional interrogation through METTL13 knockdown. Stable METTL13-knockdown models were generated using shRNA-mediated silencing, with successful depletion confirmed through comprehensive analysis at both protein (Western blot) and transcriptional (qRT-PCR) levels (Figs. 2B–E). Functional interrogation revealed that METTL13 ablation significantly impaired cellular proliferation in both KYSE-30 and KYSE-150 cell lines (Figs. 2F–G). Complementary wound-healing assays demonstrated a substantial reduction in migratory capacity following METTL13 suppression (Figs. 2H–K). These observations were further corroborated by transwell chamber assays, which showed concurrent attenuation of both migratory and invasive potentials in METTL13-deficient cells compared to controls (Figs. 2L–S). Knockdown of METTL13 negatively correlates with cancer stem cell cellular behaviors

Cancer stem cell-like cells (CSCs), a tumorigenic subpopulation implicated in cancer recurrence and metastasis [16], are defined by their capacity for self-renewal, unlimited differentiation, and tumor initiation [17, 18]. Given these critical functions, we investigated whether METTL13 regulates CSC-like properties in ESCC. To assess self-renewal capacity, sphere formation assays were performed in KYSE-30 and KYSE-150 cells. METTL13 knockdown significantly reduced the size of tumor spheres compared to controls (Figs. 3A–3D), demonstrating its necessity for maintaining ESCC self-renewal. To further characterize CSC-associated phenotypes, we analyzed expression of canonical stemness markers (ALDH1, Bmi1, Oct4, SOX2) using qRT-PCR and Western blotting. qRT-PCR analyses revealed consistent downregulation of all four markers in METTL13-depleted cells (Figs. 3E–3F). Western blot validation confirmed significant reductions in Bmi1 and SOX2 protein levels following METTL13 ablation (Figs. 3G–3H). METTL13 regulates the malignancy of tumors by promoting the mRNA translation of SAA1 in ESCCs

METTL13 was reported to promote mRNA translation to regulate tumorigenesis [19]. To identify the potential targets of METTL13 in esophageal cancers, we performed polyribosome associated mRNA-sequencing (Polysome-seq) in METTL13-depleted and control ESCC cells (Fig. 5A). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the differentially translated mRNAs between the METTL13-depleted and control samples identified the key candidate pathways in esophageal cancers (Figs. 5B–C). And we showed the top 10 differentially translated transcripts using Venn Diagram, with serum amyloid A1 (SAA1) being the most prominent and significant (Fig. 5D). Our Polysome-seq showed that the peak value of SAA1 was significantly decreased in in METTL13-depleted group (Fig. 5E). Next, Western blot and qRT-PCR were applied to determine the expression of SAA1 in METTL13-depleted and control cells. Western blot results showed that METTL13 depletion significantly reduced the protein level of SAA1 (Figs. 5F–5G), but had little effect on its mRNA level (Figs. 5H–5I), suggesting that METTL13 could regulate the mRNA translation of SAA1. Moreover, we treated ESCCs with cycloheximide (CHX) which inhibits global translation at a serial of timepoints to detect SAA1 protein half-life. Western blot results showed that SAA1 protein degradation rate did not have pronounced difference between METTL13-depleted and control groups (Supplemental Figure S1A-1B), excluding the possibility that METTL13 regulates SAA1 abundance at post-translational level. To confirm our findings, we used polysome-qPCR to detect the translation efficiency of SAA1. The results showed the mRNA translation efficiency of SAA1 was significantly reduced in METTL13 depleted ESCC cells (Figs. 5J–5K), which leads to the decrease of SAA1 protein level. Furthermore, we performed RNA immunoprecipitation real-time qPCR (RIP-real-time qPCR) and found the interactions of METTL13 and dimethylated eEF1A (eEF1aK55 me2) with SAA1 mRNA (Supplemental Figure S1D). Dimethylation level of eEF1A and its interaction with SAA1 mRNA were significantly decreased under METTL13 knockdown (Supplemental Figure S1C and 1E), suggesting that METTL13 regulates SAA1 mRNA translation through dimethylating eEF1A. Overall, our results indicate METTL13 affects the expression of SAA1 at translation level in ESCC cells.

METTL13 regulates lipid metabolism and overexpression of the SAA1 rescues ESCC progression in METTL13-depleted ESCCs

Next, to determine the key role of SAA1 in ESCC progression, we overexpressed SAA1 in control and METTL13-depleted ESCC cells to interrogate whether it is able to rescue the ESCC malignant phenotypes upon METTL13 depletion. Our data revealed that overexpression of SAA1 successfully increased the SAA1 expression in indicated ESCC cells (Fig. 6A). We further demonstrated that overexpression of SAA1 rescued the growth capacities of the METTL13 knockdown ESCC cells, as shown by the CCK-8 proliferation assay (Fig. 6B). In addition, SAA1 overexpression could further rescue the migration and invasion capacities of METTL13-depleted ESCC cells, as demonstrated by wound healing (Figs. 6C–6D), and invasion (Figs. 6E–6F) assays. Our gene ontology analysis revealed that mRNAs with decreased translation efficiency mainly enriched in metabolic pathway and biological processes including aerobic respiration (Figs. 5B–5C), suggesting that cellular metabolism affecting mitochondrial ATP synthesis may reprogram under METTL13 depletion. To confirm this, we conducted Seahorse assay and observed METTL13 knockdown inhibited oxygen consumption rate (OCR), basal respiration and maximal respiration, which were partially recovered by forced SAA1 expression (Figs. 6G–6L). In addition, given that SAA1 is one of the top listed targets of METTL13 and it is previously reported to involve in high density lipoprotein metabolism and cholesterol homeostasis [20, 21], we therefore speculated that metabolic pathways related to lipid metabolism might be involved in METTL13-mediated ESCC progression. Further analysis showed that METTL13 knockdown significantly reduced the levels of cholesterol, intracellular free fatty acid and triglyceride, which were significantly rescued by SAA1 overexpression (Figs. 6M–6O), indicating METTL13 promotes lipogenesis to support ESCC metabolic requirement. Our data showed that METTL13 depletion is able to downregulate lipid molecules (Figs. 6M–6O), suggesting metabolic change of cellular lipid. Since lipid metabolism have been demonstrated to have strong impact on regulating tumor microenvironment that is crucial for cancer progression [22–24], we next investigated whether inhibiting METTL13 can suppress ESCC development in tumor microenvironment. A mouse ESCC cell line (mEC25) that can establish suitable syngeneic model for study of tumor progression in physiological condition [25] is utilized. We first established Mettl13-depleted mEC25 cells using short hairpin RNA (Fig. 7K). Consistent with above results, knockdown of Mettl13 significantly reduced the proliferation ability of mEC25 cells (Figs. 7L–7N) in vitro. Moreover, Mettl13 knockdown significantly inhibited tumor growth in C57BL/6 ESCC syngeneic model (Figs. 7O–7Q), indicating METTL13 may also regulate ESCC progression in alternative conditions. Collectively, these data demonstrated that METTL13 is required for in vivo tumorigenic ability of ESCC, suggesting that targeting METTL13 may be a promising strategy for ESCC treatment (Fig. 7R).

Discussion

Esophageal cancer remains a global health challenge due to the lack of effective therapeutic options, which leads to poor prognosis [26]. Although breakthroughs have been made in esophageal cancer in the past years, the molecular mechanism underlying esophageal cancer pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, underscoring an urgent need to identify novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets. METTL13, a methyltransferase implicated in multiple cancer types, exhibits context-dependent functional duality, acting as either an oncogene or a tumor suppressor depending on the tumor types [19, 27]. In the present study, we demonstrate that METTL13 functions as a critical oncogene in ESCC by orchestrating a previously unrecognized METTL13–SAA1-lipid metabolism axis through translational regulation, thereby advancing our understanding of epitranscriptomic control in esophageal cancer progression.

Our clinical data revealed that METTL13 is significantly upregulated in ESCC tissues and positively correlates with poor patient survival, positioning METTL13 as a potential diagnostic biomarker and prognostic indicator for ESCC. Through comprehensive gain-of-function and loss-of-function assays, we established that METTL13 is indispensable for ESCC cell proliferation, invasion, and cancer stem cell-like properties in vitro. These findings were corroborated by in vivo xenograft experiments, wherein METTL13 depletion markedly suppressed tumor growth, collectively justifying its role as an oncogene essential for ESCC progression. Notably, METTL13 knockdown impaired mitochondrial function and disrupted lipid metabolism, providing critical mechanistic clues that guided subsequent exploration.

While METTL13 has been primarily characterized as a regulator of global protein synthesis via eEF1A methylation [19], our polysome-bound mRNA sequencing unveiled METTL13 ablation selectively attenuated the translation efficiency of a subset of mRNAs, with SAA1 emerging as the most prominently affected target. This selectivity raises an important mechanistic question concerning how METTL13 exerts target specificity at the level of individual mRNA. Regarding translation initiation, METTL13 may directly methylate SAA1 mRNA in a region-specific manner. Since emerging evidence suggests that certain methyltransferases possess dual specificity for both protein and RNA substrates [28, 29], it is plausible that METTL13 binds to specific structural elements or sequence motifs within the 5’ untranslated region (UTR) or 3’UTR of SAA1 mRNA, where it catalyzes methylation of adenine or cytosine residues. Such modifications have been reported to enhance ribosome recruitment, facilitate scanning, or promote ribosome recycling [30], thereby potentially and selectively boosting SAA1 translation, which requires further investigation. More intriguingly, the observed translational selectivity may operate at the level of translation elongation—a dimension increasingly appreciated as a key regulatory node for fine-tuning gene expression in response to cellular states [31, 32]. METTL13’s canonical function involves dimethylating eEF1A at lysine 55 [19], a modification that enhances the fidelity and efficiency of aminoacyl-tRNA delivery to the ribosome. This raises the possibility that METTL13-mediated eEF1A dimethylation does not uniformly affect all transcripts but rather confers a codon-dependent translational advantage to specific mRNAs as our RIP-real-time qPCR data indicated that eEF1A-bound SAA1 mRNA level was significantly decreased following METTL13 ablation (Supplemental figure S1E). Elucidating the precise molecular mechanism by which METTL13 selectively enhances SAA1 translation—whether through initiation control, codon-dependent elongation regulation, or their interplay—not only will deepen our understanding of epitranscriptomic and translational control in cancer but may also reveal novel therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Recently more and more studies have shown that changes in mRNA translation play a crucial oncogenic role in promoting cancer progression [19, 33–37]. SAA1 was reported to be an important prognostic marker of tumor malignancy [38–41]. In esophageal cancer, we found that depletion of METTL13 profoundly reduced the protein level of SAA1 but had little effect on its mRNA level or degradation speed. More specifically, we demonstrated that METTL13 could enhance the translation efficiency of SAA1 to promote ESCC progression, uncovering a novel regulatory mechanism of SAA1 expression at the mRNA translation level. Moreover, our rescue experiment revealed that overexpression of SAA1 restored the lipid-related metabolites levels and cancer progression in METTL13 depleted ESCC cells. Thereby, our study indicates that SAA1 serves as a necessary effector for METTL13’s oncogenic function in ESCC.

In summary, this study uncovers a mechanism whereby METTL13 drives ESCC progression by translationally upregulating SAA1, thereby remodeling lipid metabolism and sustaining cancer stem cell-like properties. Our findings not only expand the current understanding of crosstalk between epitranscriptomic regulation and metabolic reprogramming in ESCC but also establish that targeting the METTL13–SAA1-lipid metabolism axis is a promising therapeutic strategy. METTL13 thus may serve as a compelling candidate for diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic applications in ESCC patients.
