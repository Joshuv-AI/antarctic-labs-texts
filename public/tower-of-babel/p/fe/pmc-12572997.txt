Progress on Emulsification, Viscosity, Phase Inversion Characteristics, and Prediction Methods of Crude Oil–Water Mixed System
Authors: Haijun Luo, Yuzhang Jia, Yongrui Lu, Jiangbo Wen, Yuxin Luo, Chuyu Wang, Xu Xiao, Zizhe He, Zhenwei Huang, Chuanlin You
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper provides the research progress on emulsification, viscosity, phase inversion characteristics, and prediction methods of crude oil–water mixed systems. In the development of onshore and offshore oilfields, oil and water are often transported through pipelines in a mixed state, and the shear strength within the pipelines promotes the formation of emulsions. Such emulsions significantly increase the effective viscosity of the mixture, thereby altering the flow characteristics and pressure drop of the pipelines. In-depth studies have revealed that the properties of this system are determined by the combined action of internal and external factors. Among the internal factors, the physical properties of crude oil and natural surface-active substances (such as asphaltenes) are core elements. They directly affect the stability of emulsions by influencing the interfacial tension, interfacial film strength, solid particle distribution, and electric double-layer status. External factors, represented by shear force, water content, and temperature, continuously regulate the degree of emulsification and the viscosity characteristics of the mixture. It is worth noting that the phase inversion point of crude oil emulsions, as a key parameter, holds significant guiding significance for oilfield production and pipeline oil–water mixed transportation processes. Based on this, future research should focus on three aspects: exploring the influence of aqueous phase properties on emulsification characteristics, establishing a unified viscosity prediction model that can integrate stable and unstable systems, and introducing big data technology to enhance the universality and applicability of research results. These efforts will provide strong support for optimizing process calculations in oilfield development and promote the in-depth integration of theoretical research and engineering practice.

Introduction

During oilfield development, engineers commonly face the challenge of transporting crude oil and water through pipelines. During this mixed transportation, crude oil and water are sheared by pumps, pipelines, elbows, valves, and other components, leading to their mutual mixing and generation of emulsions. The generation of crude oil emulsions raises the effective viscosity of the oil–water blend. This increase notably affects the flow behavior and pressure reduction profiles within pipelines used for mixed transportation. −

Crude oil is abundant in natural surface-active substances, a category that encompasses resins, asphaltenes, waxes, mechanical impurities, and acidic compounds. While these natural components inherently influence the oil–water interface behavior, the hydrophilic or lipophilic tendency of surface-active substances (including both natural and synthetic surfactants) is typically quantified by the hydrophilic–lipophilic balance (HLB) value. Specifically, this value serves as a key indicator for predicting emulsion types: surfactants with lower HLB values tend to facilitate the formation of water-in-oil (W/O) emulsions, whereas those with higher HLB values are more prone to forming oil-in-water (O/W) emulsions. During the mixing process of crude oil and water, these substances attach to the oil–water interface, reduce interfacial tension, and form a film with a certain strength, thereby stabilizing the dispersed droplets in the mixed systemthis is the key internal factor for the formation of crude oil–water emulsions. Crude oils produced in different regions often exhibit notable distinctions in their inherent physical traits, which, in turn, lead to notable variations in their emulsification characteristics and the viscous nature of the formed crude oil emulsions. It is evident that the inherent physical attributes of crude oil significantly influence its emulsification characteristics as well as the viscous characteristics exhibited by crude oil emulsions. − In a crude oil–water mixture having a relatively low water content, the crude oil is capable of emulsifying the entire water phase to form a stable W/O crude oil emulsion. The viscosity of the W/O crude oil emulsion is considerably higher than that of pure crude oil, which results in an increase in frictional resistance in pipeline flow and, consequently, significantly affects the flow behavior exhibited by the mixed transportation pipeline. A stable W/O crude oil emulsion system, characterized by its stability, contains numerous emulsion droplets covering a broad spectrum of particle sizes. The dimensions and arrangement of these droplets have a substantial influence on the viscous properties of the crude oil emulsion, enhancing the flow retardation effect demonstrated by the emulsion system. When the crude oil–water mixture is in a high water content state, the crude oil lacks the capability to emulsify all water phases. Instead, the water phases encapsulate it, resulting in an unstable O/W crude oil–water mixture, with a sharp decrease in apparent viscosity. The morphological features suggesting an unstable state of the O/W crude oil–water mixture differ significantly from the characteristics found in the stable W/O crude oil emulsion. Specifically, under appropriate shear action, the crude oil–water mixture can maintain a uniform mixed state. However, once the external shear force is removed, the mixture will immediately undergo severe stratification and become uneven. Studying the viscous behavior of high-water-content, unstable O/W crude oil–water mixtures is crucial for guiding the intermediate to advanced phase development and gathering and transportation in oil fields. The inversion point at which the crude oil–water mixture transitions, shifting from the W/O to the O/W type, corresponds to the critical water content value at which the mixture experiences an abrupt change in its apparent viscosity. Once the crude oil–water mixture undergoes phase inversion, the viscosity of the system drops significantly, and the pressure drop gradient in the mixed transportation pipeline also decreases substantially, thereby altering the pipeline transportation behavior. The phase inversion point of the crude oil/water mixture is a crucial parameter that provides important guidance for oilfield production and mixed transportation in oil–water pipelines.

Research Status on Crude Oil Emulsification Characteristics

Mechanisms for Maintaining Stability in Crude Oil Emulsions

The emulsions formed by the mixing of crude oil and water under external shear forces are mainly of two types: One type is where the water phase is dispersed as tiny droplets in the crude oil, referred to as a W/O crude oil emulsion; the other type is where the crude oil is dispersed as tiny droplets in the water phase, termed as an O/W crude oil emulsion. The degree of stability exhibited by crude oil emulsions is a concept that is relative in nature, with separation between oil and water occurring gradually from the moment of their formation and is typically measured by the length of the demulsification time. Generally, the slower the rate of collision, flocculation, conglutination, and subsequent stratification takes place among the droplets of the dispersed phase, the higher the stability of the emulsion. The stability mechanism of crude oil emulsions is mainly embodied in the following aspects (1) For an emulsion to be formed between crude oil and water, it is essential that the oil–water interfacial tension is reduced. With the oil–water interfacial area remaining constant, a decrease in the interfacial tension decreases the interfacial free energy. This helps dispersed droplets maintain their shape without breaking, thereby facilitating the formation of emulsions. (2) The stability of the film at the interface. During the mixing of crude oil and water, asphaltenes, resins, and wax crystals, which act as natural surfactants, attach to the oil–water interface, creating an interfacial film. When the oil/water interfacial area is kept constant, lowering the interfacial tension reduces the interfacial free energy. In turn, this enables dispersed droplets to retain their shape without rupturing, which is conducive to emulsion formation. The dispersed phase droplets within the emulsion system are constantly in motion, and they collide with each other. An interfacial film with a certain mechanical strength can effectively prevent the coalescence of droplets due to collisions, maintaining their dispersed state within the system and thus ensuring the stability of the emulsion. (3) Solid particle stability. From a physical perspective, solid microparticles such as asphaltene particles, resin particles, wax crystals, and mechanical impurities in crude oil have surface properties such that some portions of them are wetted by the water phase and other portions by the oil phase, respectively. Therefore, they readily adsorb onto the oil–water interface, serving a crucial function in stabilizing crude oil emulsions. The well-known Pickering emulsion is an emulsion formed by using ultrafine solid particles as surface-active substances. These ultrasmall solid particles aggregate at the oil–water interface, which creates a high-strength interfacial film that hinders droplet coalescence and maintains the stability of the emulsion. (4) Electrical double layer stability. For O/W crude oil emulsions, natural interfacial active substances, including asphaltenes, resins, added surfactants, polymers attached to the oil–water interface, and clay particles, all carry negative charges. The dispersed oil droplets also carry negative charges. Consequently, an electrical dipole layer is established at the boundary of the oil droplets. The surfactants and polymers mentioned in the text refer specifically to anionic surface-active species that are either indigenous to the crude oil or externally added. Indigenous asphaltenes and resins, for example, contain carboxyl and phenolic hydroxyl groups that can dissociate and impart a negative charge under neutral or alkaline conditions; similarly, added anionic emulsifiers, such as sulfonates, also render the interface negatively charged. When oil droplets converge, the electrical double layer generates electrostatic repulsion to prevent droplet coalescence, thereby promoting the stability of the emulsion. Numerous experimentssuch as zeta-potential measurementsdemonstrate that crude oil droplets dispersed in the aqueous phase are typically negatively charged, primarily because naturally occurring anionic species (e.g., asphaltenate ions) adsorb at the interface and impart a negative surface charge to the droplets.

Effects of Crude Oil Properties on Emulsification

Effects of Asphaltenes and Resins on Emulsification

Crude oil is rich in natural surface-active substances, which encompass resins, asphaltenes, waxes, mechanical impurities, and acidic compounds. Asphaltenes are complex mixtures composed of nonhydrocarbon compounds with diverse molecular structures, exhibiting strong interfacial activity at the oil–water interface. , Recent research has highlighted that while asphaltenes play a crucial role in emulsion stabilization, not all molecules contribute equally; only specific chemical components and functional groups possess interfacial activity. − Due to their compositional and structural complexity, the precise chemical structures of polar asphaltene molecules responsible for emulsion stabilization remain incompletely understood.

Upon adsorbing onto the oil–water interface, asphaltenes give rise to the formation of an interfacial film that exhibits a specific degree of mechanical strength, which serves to hinder the dispersed droplets from merging. Ma et al. indicate that asphaltenes crucially stabilize heavy O/W crude oil emulsions. Asphaltenes can accumulate and aggregate spontaneously at the oil–water interface through π–π stacking interactions, hydrogen bonding (involving both intermolecular and intramolecular aspects), and other noncovalent interactions, forming a viscoelastic interfacial film. Gorbacheva and Ilyin highlighted that asphaltenes are of crucial significance in stabilizing emulsions by adsorbing and aggregating at the oil–water interface to create a resilient interfacial layer, which prevents flocculation and coalescence between droplets, thereby promoting the steadiness of the emulsion. Li et al. propose that asphaltenes can self-aggregate in solution and subsequently form a rigid “skin-like” layer at the oil–water interface. The interfacial rigidity of the asphaltene film arises from the cross-linking of asphaltene aggregates, thus reinforcing the emulsion’s stability. The research by Lu et al. indicates that as the mass fraction of asphaltenes increases, both the water separation rate and interfacial tension of the emulsion tend to decrease. Asphaltenes can also block the coalescence of dispersed droplets, preventing them from forming aggregates. Consequently, it becomes difficult to separate the oil and water phases, enhancing the stability of the crude oil emulsion.

Asphaltenes’ microscopic aggregation morphology and concentration in crude oil also exert a substantial influence on the emulsified stability of crude oil. Research by Kilpatrick, Verruto and Kilpatrick, Ismail et al., and Alves et al. indicates that in emulsions stabilized by asphaltenes as surface-active substances, the oil–water interfacial film is formed by asphaltene aggregates with sizes ranging from approximately 7 to 20 nm. Stark and Asomaning. and Angle et al. argue that asphaltene dispersion affects the stability of emulsions. Specifically, when asphaltenes are dispersed in crude oil in an aggregated form, the emulsifiability of the crude oil is increased. According to the research by Velayati and Nouri, asphaltene aggregates stabilize emulsions via steric hindrance, creating a rigid droplet interface. The greater the content of asphaltene aggregates in the simulated oil, the more stable the resulting emulsion is. Salmin et al. altered the aggregation state of asphaltene by mixing crude oil with a heptane–toluene mixture, thereby modifying its interfacial activity. They pointed out that the surfactant-like behavior of asphaltene is related to its aggregation state, with asphaltene acting as a strong emulsifier in the aggregated state but as a weak emulsifier in the precipitated or dissolved state. A strong emulsifier refers to a substance that can significantly reduce interfacial tension and effectively stabilize emulsions, while a weak emulsifier has a smaller impact on interfacial tension and a weaker stabilizing effect. Sun et al. examined the impacts of nanosilica and asphaltene on emulsion stability, pointing out that the impact of silica nanoparticles on emulsion stability could be either positive or negative, depending on the concentration and aggregation degree of asphaltene in the emulsion. In addition to nanosilica nanoparticles, other nanomaterials frequently employed in crude-oil emulsion studies include alumina, clays (e.g., montmorillonite), and graphene oxide. Owing to their differing surface chemistries, these materials exert distinct effects on emulsion stability: hydrophilic particles preferentially stabilize O/W crude oil emulsions, whereas hydrophobic ones favor W/O crude oil emulsions, and surface modification can be used to tune wettability and thereby alter the stabilizing efficacy.

The wettability, surface charge, and particle size of nanomaterials govern both the strength and the location of their adsorption at the oil–water interface. Hydrophobic particles tend to partition into the oil phase, hydrophilic ones tend to partition into the aqueous phase, and charged particles of the same sign as indigenous stabilizers (e.g., negatively charged asphaltenes) experience electrostatic repulsion that weakens interfacial adsorption. Conversely, surface-hydrophobized or appropriately sized nanoparticles can cooperate with asphaltenes, jointly reinforcing the interfacial film and enhancing the emulsion stability. For example, negatively charged nanosilica may repel negatively charged asphaltenes and thus diminish their interfacial accumulation, whereas hydrophobically modified nanoparticles can synergize with asphaltenes to strengthen the interfacial film. These mechanisms have been elaborated on in the revised manuscript and are supported by selected literature citations. Li et al. reported that increasing asphaltene concentration promotes its adsorption at the oil–water interface. Consequently, it brings about a decrease in the surface force between different phases and the dimensions of the dispersed particles and boosts the ability of the interface to resist deformation under dilation. As the asphaltene concentration increases, the stability of the emulsion improves. Liu et al. obtained asphaltene subfractions with different polarities by the solvent precipitation method, and investigated emulsion stability prepared with these subfractions at various concentrations. It was found that emulsion stability gradually increased as the concentration of asphaltene subfractions increased. Studies by Piroozian et al. and Ashoorian et al. demonstrated that when the asphaltene concentration is below or near the precipitation pointthe critical concentration or condition at which asphaltenes start to precipitate in the oil phaseits interfacial activity and ability to stabilize emulsions are relatively strong, and the formed emulsions are also more stable. However, when the asphaltene concentration exceeds the precipitation point, asphaltene will precipitate and settle, resulting in the formation of emulsions with poor stability.

Resins and asphaltenes belong to the same category of substances, with resins having a slightly smaller relative molecular mass and polarity compared to asphaltenes. There is no clear boundary between the resins and asphaltenes. Resins also have a significant effect on the stability of crude oil emulsions. The difference stems from the fact that the interfacial film strength formed by resins is generally weaker than that of asphaltene films. Therefore, resins’ stabilization capability for emulsions is also less stabilizing than that of asphaltenes. Zargar et al. prepared emulsions using asphaltene-containing model oil and found that the addition of resins increased the solubility of asphaltenes in the model oil, resulting in reduced emulsion stability. Fingas and Fieldhouse prepared emulsions using model oil and studied the effects of resins and asphaltenes on emulsion stability. They pointed out that there were significant differences in the stability of emulsions prepared from model oils with different resin/asphaltene ratios. Studies by Yonguep et al., Yudina et al., Koryakina et al., and Al-Sahhaf et al. indicated that resins have a solubilizing effect on asphaltenes, altering their aggregation state in the oil phase. Therefore, increasing the resin/aspiration ratio in model oil can destabilize the emulsion. Research by Alvarez demonstrated that the polarity of resin aggregates is weaker than that of asphaltene aggregates, and the stability of emulsions formed by resin aggregates is also poorer.

Supplementary Experimental Conditions for Asphaltene-Related Studies

Effects of Hydrocarbon Composition on Emulsification

Due to the property of asphaltenes being soluble in benzene but insoluble in nonpolar small-molecule n-alkanes, the hydrocarbon components in crude oil can affect the dissolution behavior and aggregate structure of asphaltenes. McLean and Kilpatrick conducted research on model oil emulsions and pointed out that there is a close relationship between the aromaticity of the model oil and the emulsified system’s resilience. This is mainly attributable to the aromaticity affecting the distribution pattern of asphaltenes and resins in the oil phase, thereby influencing the stability of the emulsion. The study by Elsharkawy et al. also revealed that the emulsion’s durability gradually decreases as the aromaticity of the model oil increases. Hirschberg et al. conducted precipitation experiments on asphaltenes employing n-alkanes of varying carbon chain lengths. The findings from the experiment indicated that an increase in the carbon number of the n-alkanes would lead to a decline in the solubility of asphaltenes in the oil phase, a gradual increase in the amount of precipitation, and subsequent sedimentation. The research conducted by Hammami et al. demonstrated that when alkanes (such as n-heptane) are added to model oils containing asphaltenes, the dissolvability of asphaltenes in the oil phase diminishes, promoting the precipitation of asphaltene molecules to form aggregates and enhancing the ability to stabilize emulsions. Conversely, when aromatic hydrocarbons (such as toluene) are added to the model oils, the solubility of asphaltenes in the oil phase increases, leading to a reduction in the number of asphaltene aggregates or even preventing their formation, thereby weakening the ability to stabilize emulsions. Crude oils with different physical properties have varying contents of hydrocarbon components, which results in different solubilities and aggregation morphologies of asphaltenes in different crude oils, thereby affecting the stability of crude oil emulsions.

Effects of Wax Crystals and Impurities on Emulsification

When the dissolved wax molecules in crude oil precipitate, they form tiny wax crystal particles, which possess strong interfacial activity and can adsorb at the oil/water interface, thereby enhancing the robustness of the interfacial layer. Although wax molecules themselves are nonpolar, precipitated wax crystal particles may acquire interfacial activity through surface defects and polar groups or dynamic adsorption behavior. The former occurs because wax crystals may form surface defects or adsorb polar components (such as resins and asphaltenes) in crude oil during their growth, endowing them with local amphiphilicity; the latter is because the adsorption of wax crystals at the oil–water interface is not static. Instead, they reduce the interfacial energy by dynamically adjusting their orientation. The research conducted by Li et al. − indicates that as the temperature decreases, wax molecules precipitate and transform into wax crystal particles. These tiny wax crystal particles desorb and aggregate at the droplet surfaces, forming a crystalline wax layer with a certain elasticity and strength. This film serves as an efficient barrier to the coalescence of droplets, thereby enhancing the stability of the crude oil emulsions. The research conducted by Hodge and Rousseau reveals that wax crystals formed through rapid cooling of wax-containing model oils are smaller in size compared to those formed through slow cooling. Emulsions prepared using model oils with rapidly crystallized wax exhibit higher stability than those prepared with slowly crystallized wax. The experimental results indicate that smaller wax crystal particles have a greater ability to stabilize the emulsions. The research by Zhang et al. indicates that wax crystals are dispersed in the oil phase and, through synergistic interactions with asphaltenes, adsorb and aggregate at the boundary between oil and water, cutting down interfacial tension and improving the emulsion’s stability performance. Umar et al. pointed out that the size of wax crystals plays a crucial role in the stability of oil–water emulsions. Wax crystals adsorb at the oil–water interface, forming a blockade that hinders the collision and coalescence of droplets. Additionally, the wax crystals behave in a way that they can create a three-dimensional network configuration, efficiently enclosing the dispersed phase, which is essential for the emulsion’s stability.

Solid particles and other mechanical impurities in crude oil have a similar effect on the oil–water interface as wax crystals. They can also adsorb and aggregate at the oil–water interface, thereby enhancing the interfacial film’s robustness. Tambe and Sharma pointed out that solid particles in the oil phase can function similarly to polymeric compounds, impeding the approach and coalescence of droplets and creating a spatial barrier effect, thereby enhancing the emulsion’s stable state. Gelot et al. investigated the emulsification characteristics of an oil–water system using clay particles as emulsifiers and found that the emulsion became significantly more stable as the clay particle content rose. Li et al. investigated the impact of laponite clay particles on the stability of emulsions and found that laponite clay particles can, on the one hand, increase the viscosity of the oil phase, and conversely, adhere to the oil–water interface, creating an effect that hinders droplet merging and boosts emulsion stability.

Effects of Acidic Compounds on Emulsification

Crude oil contains a large number of various acidic compounds, including straight-chain alkanoic acids, naphthenic acids, and asphaltenic acids. It is currently widely believed that these acidic compounds can react with other substances (such as basic substances) to form new substances with interfacial activity, which play a crucial role in determining the properties of the oil–water interface and the stability of emulsions. , In light of the research by Arla et al., acidic substances in crude oil not only promote the formation and stability of emulsions but also influence the type of emulsion formed. Alvarado et al. pointed out that acidic substances in crude oil can react with metal cations to form salts with certain interfacial activity, which can promote the stability of emulsions. The research conducted by Poteau et al. indicates that low-molecular-weight acidic compounds in crude oil, including naphthenic acids, can interact with asphaltenes, promoting their attachment and congregation at the oil–water phase interface. Zhang et al. conducted an investigation into dynamic interfacial tension characteristics of crude oil-alkaline water systems. The results showed that under appropriate ion concentration and pH conditions, the organic acid constituents within crude oil possess the capability to significantly decrease the interfacial tension.

The composition and architecture of acidic substances in crude oil are diverse, and the degree of their interfacial activity is intimately associated with the structure and attributes of the acidic compounds themselves. The research by Xu et al. indicates that the interfacial activity of acidic components in crude oil is closely related to their relative molecular mass. The acidic components with a lower relative molecular mass primarily have side chains composed of aliphatic hydrocarbons, and these types of acidic components exhibit strong interfacial activity. In contrast, acidic components with a higher relative molecular mass demonstrate weaker interfacial activity. Brandal et al. conducted experiments using a simulated oil-alkaline water system to investigate the influence of interactions between acidic substances and metal cations regarding the oil–water interfacial tension. The findings demonstrated that the oil–water interfacial tension undergoes variations in accordance with alterations in the structure and concentration of the acidic substances. Li et al. conducted research regarding the emulsification properties of crude oil and found that carboxylic acids and fatty acids in crude oil, which have relatively low molecular weights and more branched chains, primarily function to reduce interfacial tension. In contrast, the acidic substances in crude oil with relatively high molecular weights mainly play a role in stabilizing the emulsions. Kilpatrick also pointed out that carboxylic acids in crude oil can effectively diminish the oil–water interfacial tension and promote the stability of crude oil emulsions. The strength of their interfacial activity varies due to differences in molecular structure and relative molecular mass. Another type of acidic substance found in crude oil is called naphthenic acid. These acidic substances have at least one cycloalkane chemical moiety within the molecular structure, and these cycloalkane functional groups often carry alkane side chains. They also have a crucial impact on the emulsification characteristics in relation to crude oil. The research by Horvath-Szabo et al. indicates that sodium naphthenate aggregates at the oil–water interface, creating a liquid crystalline film that enhances the stability of the emulsion. The research by Ese and Kilpatrick demonstrates that cholinic acid, an isomer of naphthenic acid, exhibits strong interfacial activity and can stabilize both W/O and O/W crude oil emulsions.

Research Status on Droplet Size and Distribution in Crude Oil Emulsions

In oil–water mixed transportation pipelines, upon the mixing of crude oil and water to generate an emulsion, the system contains a large number of emulsion droplets of varying sizes. There are numerous factors that influence the size and distribution of these emulsion droplets, including shear intensity, temperature, proportion of the dispersed phase, oil properties, and flow time. Ward and Knudsen used photographic techniques to study the droplet size of oil–water two-phase dispersed flow in a vertical pipe, and found that the Sauter mean diameter of the droplets first increased and then decreased with an increase in flow velocity. According to the study carried out by Paul and Sleicher, the droplet size in oil–water two-phase dispersed flow exhibits a tendency to decrease as the flow velocity increases. The studies by Simmons et al. and Zhao et al. also demonstrate that as the flow velocity of the oil–water mixture in the pipeline increases, the average droplet size gradually decreases. The higher the flow velocity in the pipe, the greater the shear intensity that the oil–water mixture experiences from the flow, which intensifies droplet deformation and breakup, resulting in the generation of smaller droplets. Research also indicates that the temperature and the proportion of the dispersed phase significantly affect the size and distribution of the droplets. Wang pointed out in his research that the robustness of the interfacial film of droplets weakens with an elevation in temperature, which further leads to a higher likelihood of coalescence between droplets, resulting in the formation of larger droplets. Lv et al. conducted a study on various factors affecting droplet size, and the findings revealed that the Sauter average diameter of droplets increased with an increase in temperature and the proportion of the dispersed phase. The emulsification characteristics of crude oils with different physical properties vary significantly, leading to differences in the dimensions and dispersion characteristics of the droplets formed in the emulsions. Taking into account the influence of the diversity of crude oil properties on droplet size, establishing the association between droplet magnitude and crude oil properties will be an important direction in studying the size and distribution characteristics of droplets. API gravity reflects the “lightness” or “heaviness” of crude oil. Light crude oils (high API gravity) are low-viscosity fluids that produce large, unstable droplets and are readily demulsified; heavy crude oils (low API gravity) are rich in asphaltenes and other components, are highly viscous, and yield fine, stable droplets after emulsification. Crude oil viscosity directly governs how easily an emulsion forms and the viscosity of the resulting emulsion. High-viscosity crude oils are more likely to generate small, stable droplets under shear, yet the final emulsion viscosity is also higher. Interfacial tension between oil and water is a key determinant of emulsion stability: the lower the tension, the smaller the droplets formed and the more stable the emulsion. Indigenous surface-active species in crude oil (asphaltenes, resins, etc.) lower interfacial tension and thus facilitate emulsification. A large oil–water density difference accelerates droplet sedimentation and is detrimental to stability. Aqueous-phase pH influences the dissociation of indigenous acids, thereby altering the concentration of interfacially active species. Salinity destabilizes emulsions by compressing the electrical double layer, reducing interdroplet electrostatic repulsion, and changing the charge density of the interfacial film, ultimately promoting droplet coalescence and sedimentation.

Several models for droplet size in oil–water two-phase dispersed flows have already been published. Research by Kolmogorov, Hinze indicates that the external forces exerted by the continuous phase on the droplets of the dispersed phase cause deformation and even breakup of the droplets, while the interfacial tension and viscosity of the droplets maintain their shape. Subsequently, they established a predictive model for the maximum droplet size in turbulent flow fields through experimental research. Kubie and Gardner improved the Hinze model based on the interaction between individual droplets and the continuous phase, proposing an enhanced predictive model for the maximum droplet size. Angeli and Hewitt analyze d the motion of droplets and their interaction with eddies in turbulent flow fields, taking into account droplet breakup and coalescence. They studied the distribution of droplet sizes in the oil–water biphasic system dispersed flows and obtained experimental data on droplet sizes using endoscopy. Based on their research, they proposed the d max and d 32 models. Brauner and Ullmann developed an equation for computing droplet size in situations with high dispersed phase concentrations. Lv et al. formulated a prognostic model for the droplet size of the dispersed phase by studying the balance between turbulent pulsation kinetic energy and interfacial energy, the relationship between radial pulsation and friction in oil–water two-phase pipe flow, alongside the shear effect.

Research Status on Viscosity Characteristics of Crude Oil–Water Mixtures

Factors Affecting Viscosity of Crude Oil–Water Mixtures

Shear action, water content, and temperature are key external factors that affect the emulsification and viscosity of crude oil–water mixtures. Studies − indicate that elevating the shear intensity during the emulsification process intensifies the mixing of crude oil–water mixtures, rendering it more challenging for droplets to merge and sediment. This enhances the stability and viscosity of the mixture. Concerning the impact of water content, in instances where the water content within the system falls below the emulsion inversion point, the mixture typically constitutes a steady W/O crude oil emulsion, and the viscous property of the emulsion rises significantly with an increase in the water content of the system. − Nevertheless, once the water content within the system surpasses the inversion threshold of the emulsion, an unstable crude oil–water blend, characterized by an O/W crude oil emulsion structure, is brought into existence, and the viscosity of the mixture drops sharply. , Under the influence of the temperature, the stability and viscous nature of the crude oil–water mixture will rapidly decrease with an increase in temperature. The reasons indicated by studies − mainly include the following aspects: (1) The increase in temperature causes a reduction in the viscous nature of crude oil, resulting in reduced resistance to the movement of droplets. (2) The rise in temperature leads to a reduction in the density of crude oil, leading to a larger density difference between oil and water, which makes it easier for the water phase to settle. (3) With the elevation of temperature, the solubility of surface-active substances, including resins, asphaltenes, and waxes, in the oil phase is augmented, consequently impairing the interfacial film structure. (4) As the temperature rises, the frequency of collisions among droplets is heightened, thereby facilitating the breakdown of the emulsion.

The crude oil contains abundant natural surfactants, which mainly encompass resins, asphaltenes, waxes, mechanical impurities, and acidic compounds. These substances serve as essential internal determinants of emulsification and viscosity properties of crude oil–water mixtures. − Asphaltenes can effectively adsorb onto the oil–water interface film, decreasing the interfacial tension, thus enhancing the stability of the crude oil emulsion. Research conducted by Qiao et al., Subramanian et al., and Lashkarbolooki and Ayatollahi have shown that asphaltenes contain mainly polar subcomponents as their active ingredients, which easily adsorb at the oil–water interface to create a viscoelastic film. As asphaltene concentration increases, the magnitude of thickness and robustness of the oil–water interfacial film increases, leading to increased stability of the formed emulsion and higher viscosity. Additionally, aggregated asphaltenes have a more pronounced impact than molecular asphaltenes. Resins and asphaltenes are of the same substance category; the substances have lower relative molecular weights and polarities compared to asphaltenes. Research conducted by Liu et al., Cao et al., and Zhang et al. has revealed that during the emulsification process, resins demonstrate a cooperative interaction that enhances the effects of asphaltenes. A small amount of resins enhances the asphaltenes’ adsorption affinity for the interfacial film, whereas a larger amount of resins promotes the scattering of asphaltenes, thereby weakening the structure at the interface film’s boundary. The precipitated wax crystals are capable of assembling into a 3D network architecture, which serves as an efficient encapsulating matrix for the dispersed phase. Additionally, it is the aggregation of wax crystal particles at the interfacial film that hinders the coalescence of the droplets. A higher wax content in crude oil leads to greater stability and viscosity of the resulting crude oil emulsion. − Solid-particle mechanical impurities in crude oil show interfacial behavior resembling wax crystal particles. Micron or nanometer-sized inorganic solid particles both increase the viscosity of crude oil and enhance the composition and framework of the interfacial film, thus improving the robustness of the emulsion. , Crude oil contains a wide variety of acidic substances, which encompass linear alkanoic acids, carboxylic acids, naphthenic acids, and asphaltic acids. Research conducted by Andersen et al., Liu et al., and Saliu and Della Pergola has demonstrated that acidic compounds can adsorb onto the oil–water interface. Independently or reacting with bases to form new surface-active substances, exerting a significant influence on the interfacial film attributes, stability, and viscous properties of emulsions. Additionally, the molecular structure and relative molecular mass are key intrinsic factors that influence the surface-active behavior of acidic compounds at interfaces.

Research Status on Viscosity Models for Stable Crude Oil Emulsions/Unstable Crude Oil–Water Mixtures

Regarding the calculation of the viscous property of stable W/O crude oil emulsions formed under low water content conditions, predecessors have published numerous predictive models. Early on, Einstein proposed that the viscous property of an emulsion correlates with the viscous property of the oil phase and the water content, and established a model to compute the viscous property of emulsions applicable to low water content conditions. Vand enhanced the scope of application of the Einstein model by modifying the number of terms and coefficients in the polynomial. Taylor, assuming that the droplets of the dispersed phase exhibit a perfectly spherical shape and possess extremely small particle dimensions, considered the influence of the dispersed phase viscosity and established a model for viscosity prediction applicable to dilute emulsions. Richardson established an emulsion viscosity model that exhibits an exponential correlation with the volumetric proportion of the dispersed phase, based on a summary pertaining to experimental measurements of emulsion viscosity with higher water content. The aforementioned models represent the pioneering and most prevalent approaches in the petroleum industry to determine the viscosity of emulsions computationally.

In the later stages, Broughton and Squires, Mooney, Marsden, and others sequentially introduced enhanced models grounded in the Richardson equation, which are applicable to various emulsion systems. Broughton and Squires extended the Richardson power-law model by introducing a two-parameter form in which the logarithm of the viscosity ratio is linearly related to the dispersed-phase volume fraction, with A and B as fitting parameters. This modification allows the model to fit experimental data for a wider range of systems. The Mooney model incorporates the concept of a maximum packing fraction (φmax) into the Richardson framework via an exponential expression. It is applicable at higher dispersed-phase concentrations: when φ approaches φmax, the viscosity rises sharply, reflecting the behavior of concentrated emulsions. Marsden focused on high-water-cut emulsions; he augmented the Richardson model with additional parameters for temperature and shear rate, enabling predictions under nonisothermal and non-Newtonian conditions. Phan-Thien and Pham developed a new viscosity model for emulsions based on continuum mechanics theory and using the Taylor model as a foundation. Pal put forward an enhanced viscosity model that builds upon the Phan-Thien equation, taking into comprehensive consideration the impact of surfactants.

Moreover, the viscosity of crude oil emulsions is also influenced by factors such as shear rate and particle size, exhibiting non-Newtonian characteristics. At the current stage, Dan and Jing and Pal have successively proposed emulsion viscosity models that take into account the effects of interfacial rheology, shear rate, and other factors. Azodi and Nazar have developed a new emulsion viscosity model by comprehensively considering influencing factors such as oil content, temperature, shear rate, and emulsifier concentration. do Carmo et al. prepared emulsions using Brazilian crude oil and put forward an emulsion viscosity model grounded in fractional calculus theory, which considers the influence of droplets in the dispersed phase. Shi et al., based on improvements to the Taylor equation and the Richardson equation, have proposed a viscosity model applicable to both W/O and O/W crude oil emulsions through a thorough consideration of influencing factors such as shear rate, oil viscosity, temperature, and water content.

Viscosity Calculation Models for Stable Emulsion

Currently, most oilfields in China have advanced to the intermediate to advanced phases of development. Furthermore, the fluid extracted from oil wells frequently exhibits a significant water content. In gathering and transportation pipelines, they lack the ability to emulsify the entirety of the aqueous phase, but are instead enveloped by the aqueous phase, forming an unstable O/W crude oil–water mixture. At present, some scholars have proposed a limited number of viscosity calculation methods for this type of unstable crude oil–water mixture.

The initial approach utilized in engineering for determining the viscosity of crude oil–water blends commonly relied on the weighted average technique. However, this method is relatively crude and often results in significant calculation errors. Subsequent researchers conducted experiments using loop facilities and backcalculated the apparent viscosity of oil–water blends inferred from pressure drop measurements obtained in these loops. Pan utilized a loop apparatus to study stratified and dispersed oil–water flows and proposed a viscosity calculation model for oil–water mixtures that considers the influence of the degree of mixing. Cai et al. conducted laboratory experiments on oil–water two-phase flow in vertical pipelines and established an empirical viscosity model of oil–water mixtures based on pipeline pressure drop data. Wen et al. investigated the viscous properties of high-water-cut oil–water mixtures, taking into account the impact of emulsified water in the oil phase. They improved upon the Taylor model and developed a viscosity prediction model suitable for high-water-cut unstable emulsified oil–water mixtures.

Current viscosity models for crude oil–water systems exhibit significant limitations: most fail to incorporate oil-specific physical properties, leading to substantial errors across diverse oil–water systems; certain models incorporate undefined oil-related parameters without establishing quantitative relationships to physical properties, necessitating empirical calibration and restricting universal applicability; additionally, no universally validated predictive model exists for phase inversion points, which remain experimentally determined despite their intrinsic dependence on crude oil’s physical characteristics, collectively undermining the accuracy and adaptability of existing modeling approaches.

Research Status on Phase Inversion Characteristics of Crude Oil–Water Mixed Systems

During the global development of both offshore and onshore oilfields, the simultaneous transportation technology for crude oil and water is extensively utilized, and when the water cut surpasses a critical threshold (the phase inversion point), the initially stable W/O crude oil emulsion reverses into an unstable O/W mixture. After the phase inversion of the crude oil emulsion, the viscosity of the mixture system will significantly decrease, and there will be a significant decrease in the frictional resistance within the multiphase transportation pipeline, thereby altering the pipeline transportation characteristics. ,

The phase inversion point of crude oil emulsions is a crucial parameter that exerts a remarkable guiding influence on petroleum-field exploitation and the technology of oil–water mixed transportation in pipelines. In the process of heavy oil transportation with water blending, precisely ascertaining the phase inversion point of heavy oil emulsions is of the utmost importance. The key to designing the water blending process is to enable the generation of an O/W crude oil emulsion in the heavy-oil–water mixture, thereby achieving the objective of reducing the viscosity of the mixture and the frictional resistance in the pipeline. − In petroleum-field collection and conveyance procedures, the introduction of a high-molecular-weight polymer drag reducer can effectively decrease the phase inversion threshold of crude oil emulsions. Although high-molecular-weight polymeric drag reducerssuch as partially hydrolyzed polyacrylamide and other water-soluble long-chain polymersare not surface-active agents and do not directly participate in emulsification, they can diminish shear-induced disruption of emulsions by suppressing turbulent fluctuations. Consequently, stable O/W emulsions form at lower water cuts, effectively reducing the critical water content required for phase inversion. This allows the creation of an O/W crude oil emulsion even at lower water cuts within the mixture, thereby diminishing the viscosity of the blend and boosting the efficiency of collection and transportation. , In offshore petroleum-field exploitation, the occurrence of “emulsification impairment” frequently occurs. This refers to the situation where, when the water content of the mixture is less than the phase inversion value, the viscosity of the stable W/O crude oil emulsion blend formed by the oil–water system significantly rises in comparison to that of unadulterated crude oil. This leads to increased lifting resistance in oil tubes and transportation resistance in subsea pipelines, thereby affecting oilfield productivity. Understanding the phase inversion characteristics of the produced fluid can effectively reduce productivity losses caused by emulsification issues. − The shutdown and restart characteristics of polymer-flooded heavy oil produced fluids are closely related to the inversion points of crude oil emulsions. Studies − have shown that when the water content of the system is less than the phase inversion threshold, as the water cut increases, the complexity of restarting the pipeline also grows. However, when the water cut goes beyond the phase inversion threshold, the difficulty of restarting the pipeline significantly decreases. In the context of crude oil dehydration treatment, when the water cut in the system is below the phase inversion threshold, the water phase is encapsulated in the form of minute droplets of crude oil, forming a robust and stable structure that does not facilitate heating or electrical dehydration processes, often resulting in a lower dehydration rate. Conversely, when the water cut of the system surpasses the phase inversion threshold, the structure formed by the oil and water becomes loose and less stable, at which point the rates of heating and electrical dehydration significantly increase. ,

For different types of crude oil–water mixed transportation processes, the phase inversion point of crude oil emulsions is closely related to the physical properties of the crude oil. Generally speaking, the higher the content of natural surface-active substances such as resins, asphaltenes, waxes, mechanical impurities, and acidic compounds in the crude oil, the stronger the emulsifying capacity of crude oil results in a higher phase inversion threshold for the crude oil emulsion. ,

Research Prospects and Suggestions

Current research on stable W/O crude oil emulsions predominantly focuses on average droplet size, overlooking the impact of droplet quantity on dispersion characteristics, which leads to inherent limitations in traditional characterization methods. In oil–water mixed transportation engineering, accurately calculating the viscosity of crude oil–water mixtures is crucial: while classical viscosity prediction models such as the Vand model and the Richardson model are available for stable emulsions formed under low water cut conditions, unstable oil–water mixtures with significantly different dispersion and stability characteristics emerge under high water cut conditions, rendering traditional viscosity models inapplicable and conventional rheometers ineffective for measuring their viscosity. Currently, empirical methods like weighted averaging or inversion calculations based on loop test data are commonly employed to estimate the apparent viscosity of these unstable mixtures, yet these approaches suffer from substantial prediction errors. The main deficiencies of existing viscosity calculation methods lie in two aspects: first, most models fail to account for the influence of crude oil’s physical properties, resulting in poor applicability across different oil–water systems; second, although certain models (e.g., the Richardson model and the Azodi model) incorporate undefined parameters related to crude oil’s physical properties, they lack quantitative relationships linking these parameters to the oil’s physical characteristics, necessitating experimental determination of parameter values and thereby limiting their universal applicability. Additionally, the phase inversion point of crude oil/water mixtures is currently determined primarily through experimental measurements, and no universal predictive model applicable to various oil/water systems has been developed to date.

Based on the practical needs of engineering applications and the limitations of previous studies, a comprehensive investigation into the viscosity and phase inversion characteristics of crude oil–water mixed systems under low- and high-water-cut conditions is carried out. The objective is to develop predictive models that are quantitatively founded upon the physical characteristics of crude oil and universally adaptable to diverse crude oil varieties, including (1) a droplet size prediction model; (2) a viscosity prediction model for stable W/O crude oil emulsions; (3) a model for predicting viscosity in unstable O/W crude oil–water mixtures; and (4) a model for forecasting the phase inversion point in crude oil–water mixed systems. These models are of great significance in providing universally applicable theoretical methods and scientific guidance for process calculations in oilfield development.

People generally think the influence of water phase properties on the emulsification characteristics of the crude oil–water mixed system is less significant than that of crude oil properties. However, from the perspective of aligning more closely with actual oilfield production and further improving the calculation accuracy of various models, the effects of water phase mineralization, inorganic salt types, and pH pertaining to the emulsification properties of the crude oil–water mixture cannot be completely overlooked. The research in this paper temporarily does not consider the influence of water phase properties, and ultrapure water is used as the aqueous phase medium. Therefore, it is recommended that in future studies, the combined effects of crude oil properties and water phase properties on the emulsification, viscosity, and phase inversion characteristics of the crude oil–water mixed system should be investigated collaboratively, and relevant viscosity and phase inversion point models that consider a more comprehensive range of influencing factors should be established.2.In the practical application of viscosity models for crude oil–water mixed systems, it is invariably necessary to first determine the specific morphology of the crude oil–water mixed system in the pipeline before selecting the corresponding viscosity model for calculation.
