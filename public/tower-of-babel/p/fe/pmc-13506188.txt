Isolated Flexor Hallucis Longus Weakness Caused by a Popliteal Ganglion Cyst Compressing the Tibial Nerve
Authors: Yohei Yanagisawa, Yusuke Eda, Naoya Kikuchi, Tomoki Koide, Shiho Kajita, Chihana Takamori, Matsuoka Ryota, Yasuhiro Homma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case:A 43-year-old woman presented with left foot numbness and progressive difficulty flexing the great toe. Examination revealed isolated flexor hallucis longus (FHL) weakness. Plain radiographs of the left knee showed no degenerative changes. MRI showed a multiloculated ganglion cyst in the left popliteal fossa with increased FHL signal intensity, suggestive of denervation edema. Cyst aspiration resulted in immediate FHL recovery, but the cyst recurred 3 months later, and complete recovery was achieved after surgical excision. At the 3-year follow-up, the patient remained symptom-free with full FHL strength (MMT 5/5) and no cyst recurrence.Conclusion:An extraneural popliteal ganglion cyst can cause selective fascicular compression of the tibial nerve, resulting in isolated FHL weakness, with durable functional recovery achievable through surgical excision.

Ganglion cysts of the popliteal fossa are common, but tibial nerve compression resulting in motor deficits is rare. Popliteal masses include Baker cysts, which arise from a degenerated knee joint and communicate with the joint cavity through a synovial-lined tract, and ganglion cysts, which originate from mucinous degeneration of joint capsules or tendon sheaths and lack a synovial lining1. Popliteal ganglion cysts are further classified as extraneural cysts, which extrinsically compress the tibial nerve, or intraneural cysts, which extend within the epineurium through an articular branch, most often from the superior tibiofibular joint, in accordance with the unifying articular theory2. Previous reports of tibial neuropathy from popliteal cysts have typically described combined weakness of multiple tibial nerve–innervated muscles, including the flexor hallucis longus (FHL) and flexor digitorum longus (FDL), with plantar sensory disturbance3,4. We describe a case of an extraneural popliteal ganglion cyst causing isolated FHL weakness via selective fascicular compression of the tibial nerve.

The patient was informed that data concerning the case would be submitted for publication, and she provided consent.

Case Report

Presentation

A 43-year-old woman presented with left foot numbness and progressive difficulty flexing the left great toe. Discomfort in the left posterior lower leg and popliteal region preceded the great toe weakness. She was referred to our department approximately 1 month after symptom onset. Her medical history included lumbar spondylolisthesis and hip osteoarthritis.

Neurological examination revealed severe isolated weakness of the FHL (manual muscle testing [MMT] grade 0-1/5). Strength was preserved in the FDL, tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus, extensor digitorum longus, and gastrocnemius muscles (MMT 5/5). Sensory examination was unremarkable. Straight leg raise and femoral nerve stretch tests were negative bilaterally; patellar reflexes were normal and Achilles reflexes diminished bilaterally.

Imaging

Plain radiographs of the left knee revealed no joint-space narrowing, osteophytes, or other degenerative changes (Fig. 1), making a Baker cyst arising from a degenerated knee joint unlikely. MRI of the left foot and ankle ruled out FHL tendon rupture. Lumbar MRI revealed L4 anterior spondylolisthesis without nerve root compression or canal stenosis that could explain the clinical findings.

Anteroposterior (Fig. 1-A) and lateral (Fig. 1-B) plain radiographs of the left knee, showing preserved joint space, no osteophytes, and no other degenerative changes—findings inconsistent with the typical background pathology of a Baker cyst.

Bilateral lower extremity MRI revealed a multiloculated mass in the left popliteal fossa, dorsal to the popliteus muscle and ventral to the popliteal vessels (Fig. 2). The mass was located outside the typical site of a Baker cyst (between the medial gastrocnemius and semimembranosus tendons) and did not communicate with the knee joint cavity. It had a globular rather than tubular configuration, did not follow the course of the tibial nerve, and showed no signet-ring sign or intraepineurial extension, findings favoring an extraneural ganglion cyst rather than an intraneural ganglion or Baker cyst5. The differential diagnosis included extraneural ganglion cyst, intraneural ganglion, Baker cyst, and schwannoma. Short tau inversion recovery (STIR) sequences revealed increased signal intensity within the FHL muscle, suggestive of denervation edema (Fig. 3), whereas the FDL showed normal signal intensity.

The mass shows a globular configuration without a tubular tail along the tibial nerve and lies outside the typical site of a Baker cyst, consistent with an extraneural ganglion cyst.

Treatment

Ultrasound-guided aspiration of the popliteal mass was performed 3 months after the first visit. Approximately 8 mL of viscous, gelatinous fluid was aspirated; cytopathology was negative for neoplastic cells. The patient demonstrated immediate FHL recovery, with restoration of great toe flexion within minutes.

Approximately 3 months after aspiration, she presented with left knee swelling and lower extremity pain, without FHL weakness. Follow-up MRI confirmed cyst recurrence; the previously noted denervation signal within the FHL had resolved, indicating interval reinnervation.

She was admitted for surgical excision 1 year after the first visit. Examination revealed a soft, elastic mass on the medial popliteal fossa; Tinel sign was negative, and all muscles, including FHL and FDL, demonstrated full strength (MMT 5/5). Nerve conduction studies and needle electromyography performed on the day before surgery—when FHL strength had fully recovered—showed no side-to-side differences in conduction velocities, latencies, or amplitudes of the tibial and common peroneal nerves, with no active denervation in the FHL or other tibial-innervated muscles, consistent with complete electrophysiological recovery and supporting a neurapraxic rather than axonotmetic injury. Surgical excision was performed through a posterior approach (Fig. 4). Intraoperatively, the cyst was adjacent to but anatomically separate from the tibial nerve, with no intraepineurial extension and no articular pedicle connecting to the knee or superior tibiofibular joint, confirming its extraneural nature. Histopathological examination (Fig. 5) demonstrated a cystic structure with a fibrous wall lacking a true epithelial or synovial lining and containing mucinous material, confirming a ganglion cyst and definitively excluding a Baker cyst, which is characterized by a synovial lining.

Outcomes

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 3 and ambulated without restrictions. At the final follow-up (3 years postoperatively), she remained symptom-free with full FHL strength (MMT 5/5) and no evidence of cyst recurrence.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of isolated FHL weakness caused by an extraneural popliteal ganglion cyst. The diagnosis of an extraneural ganglion cyst was established by 3 lines of evidence: (1) absence of knee degenerative changes on radiographs and absence of communication with the knee joint on MRI, arguing against a Baker cyst; (2) intraoperative findings of an extraepineurial cyst without an articular pedicle, arguing against an intraneural ganglion; and (3) histopathology demonstrating a fibrous-walled cyst lacking a synovial lining, the defining feature distinguishing ganglion cysts from Baker cysts1,2. Tibial intraneural ganglia most commonly originate from the posterior superior tibiofibular joint through the articular branch along the popliteus muscle5. In our case, the cyst showed a globular configuration without tubular tail or signet-ring sign and a clearly extraepineurial location at surgery. Previous reports of tibial neuropathy from popliteal cysts have demonstrated broad rather than selective nerve involvement3,4,6. Morrison et al. reported vascular compression of a single tibial nerve branch, indicating that isolated mononeuropathy of a single tibial nerve branch is possible7.

Our case demonstrated isolated FHL involvement without FDL weakness, indicating selective fascicular compression of the tibial nerve. Peripheral nerves exhibit somatotopic organization, so partial focal lesions can produce restricted clinical deficits8. The FHL is consistently innervated by a single tibial nerve branch arising after the gastrocnemius and soleus branches9, and the branching pattern shows considerable variation in the popliteal fossa10,11. An extraneural ganglion cyst can therefore exert focal pressure at the popliteal level, selectively compressing the fascicle supplying the FHL while sparing those innervating the FDL and other muscles.

STIR sequences revealed increased FHL signal consistent with denervation edema, a recognized MRI sign of subacute denervation. West et al. reported that denervated muscles show increased STIR signal as early as 4 days after symptom onset, significantly earlier than EMG changes, and that STIR signal remains normal in neurapraxic injuries, helping distinguish neurapraxia from axonotmesis12. Fleckenstein et al. similarly characterized prolonged T1 and T2 in denervated muscles, producing conspicuous STIR hyperintensity13. The increased STIR signal confined to the FHL, with normal FDL signal, provided objective evidence of selective nerve involvement.

Rapid and complete recovery of FHL strength after aspiration and again after surgical excision is consistent with neurapraxia: relief of mechanical compression allowed immediate restoration of nerve conduction. Electrodiagnostic studies performed at the time of full clinical recovery showed no side-to-side abnormalities and no evidence of active or chronic denervation, providing electrophysiological confirmation that the injury was a reversible compression neuropathy without axonal loss. The reproducible course—weakness developing with cyst recurrence and resolving with cyst removal—provides strong evidence for the causal relationship between the cyst and selective FHL weakness.

Although initial aspiration produced prompt neurological recovery, the cyst recurred and required surgical excision. Reported recurrence rates after aspiration range from 20% to 58%, compared with 4% to 15% after surgical excision14. For intraneural cysts, failure to identify and disconnect the articular branch is the principal cause of recurrence, whereas for extraneural cysts complete excision of the cyst wall is generally curative5. When a popliteal ganglion cyst causes a neurological deficit, definitive surgical excision—with intraoperative confirmation of cyst type and, when intraneural, disconnection of the articular pedicle—should be considered to minimize recurrent compression and progression to axonal injury.

This case has limitations. Electrodiagnostic studies were performed only after full clinical recovery rather than during the symptomatic period, so the acute electrophysiological pattern of selective fascicular compression could not be documented. Future investigators encountering similar presentations are encouraged to obtain electrodiagnostic studies during the symptomatic period.

This case demonstrates that an extraneural popliteal ganglion cyst can cause isolated FHL weakness, highlighting the need to consider proximal tibial nerve compression in the differential diagnosis. MRI with STIR is valuable in identifying denervation edema as objective evidence of clinical deficit. Careful distinction among Baker cysts, extraneural ganglion cysts, and intraneural ganglion cysts on the basis of radiographs, MRI configuration, intraoperative findings, and histopathology is essential for appropriate surgical management. Surgical excision should be strongly considered to achieve lasting resolution when a popliteal ganglion cyst causing neurological deficits recurs after aspiration.
