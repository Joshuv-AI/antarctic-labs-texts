International comparison of medicines approvals in the UK: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Ayomikun Oluwaseun Ogunyemi, Cyril Onwuelazu Uteh, Ross Fairbairn, Nick Meader, Dawn Craig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo understand the regulatory landscape in the UK and globally before the introduction of the Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency’s (MHRA) International Recognition Procedure (IRP). This paper aims to inform on the IRP’s potential impact on access to innovative medicines, by comparing medicines approval in the UK with other international regulatory agencies.MethodsA retrospective analysis of 154 technologies (medicine(s) + studied indication(s)) was conducted based on the National Institute for Health and Care Research Innovation Observatory technology briefings submitted to the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in 2020. These briefings serve as the trigger for the NICE Technology Assessment process. Data on the medicines’ submission and approval dates were extracted across five IRP reference regulators, which are the regulators from the US (Food and Drug Administration (FDA)), European Union (EU) (European Medicines Agency (EMA)), Japan (Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA)), Australia (Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA)) and Singapore (Health Science Authority (HSA)). The dates were compared with data from the UK (MHRA).Results95 (62%) medicines were approved by at least one regulatory agency, while 59 (38%) medicines were not approved by any regulatory agency. The number of medicines approved by the following regulatory agencies is FDA (n=84, 55%), EMA (n=80, 52%), MHRA (n=71, 46%), TGA (n=51, 33%), HSA (n=41, 27%) and PMDA (n=38, 25%). The first submissions were primarily to the FDA (n=64) or the EMA (n=24). The FDA had the highest number of first approvals (n=70), followed by the EMA (n=17), PMDA (n=5) and the MHRA (n=1). The FDA used more expedited pathways than other regulators (n=61). Compared with the MHRA, FDA approvals were on average 360 days faster and EMA approvals 86 days faster.ConclusionThere were significant differences in market access timelines across the five reference regulators, with the FDA and EMA having the highest number of approved medicines, first submissions and first approvals. IRP applications with the FDA or EMA as reference regulators may expedite access to innovative medicines in the UK by reducing the approval dates gap between the FDA/EMA and the MHRA. This understanding of the regulatory landscape will help inform future planning to accommodate the disruption that the IRP may cause.

Introduction

Regulatory agencies and pharmaceutical companies globally are intensifying efforts to expedite the development and delivery of increasingly complex and innovative therapies to patients with significant unmet medical needs, thereby reducing the time to access innovative therapies.1 2 The UK government has supported the creation of an international recognition framework by funding the Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) with an initial sum of £10 million to fast-track patient access to the most innovative products. This funding supported the creation and implementation of the International Recognition Procedure (IRP), a thorough but expedited approval process for innovative medicines by collaborating with other international regulatory agencies.3 The aim is to allow accelerated access to potentially life-saving or life-enhancing medicines approved internationally, thus saving time and resources within MHRA.3 4

The MHRA is the UK regulatory body that grants product licences, also known as marketing authorisations (MA), before any medicine can be made available in the UK market. Before the implementation of the IRP on 1 January 2024, the MHRA previously collaborated with the European Medicines Agency (EMA) through the decentralised and mutual recognition reliance procedures (MRDCRP) and the European Commission decision reliance procedure (ECDRP).5 6 The MHRA also participates in international work-sharing collaboration through the Access Consortium and Project Orbis, enhancing cross-border collaboration among its regulatory partners.7 8

International collaboration of global regulatory agencies is not a new concept, as it accelerates the approval of medicines.9 The COVID-19 pandemic intensified regulatory agencies’ efforts to collaborate through various techniques, including reliance, convergence, harmonisation, work sharing and collaborative review.10 11 In 2019, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) launched Project Orbis, enabling the review of oncology product applications in collaboration with other national health regulators in Australia, Canada, Singapore, Switzerland, Brazil, Israel and the UK.8 12 Project Orbis regulatory partners collaborate on the evaluation of new active substances and new indications in oncology medicines, enhancing evaluations through a broader spectrum of technical contributions; however, countries are allowed to make independent decisions.13

In 2007, the national regulatory bodies of Australia, Canada, Singapore and Switzerland established the Access Consortium, formerly known as ‘ACSS’. The group was renamed ‘Access’ after the MHRA joined in October 2020; however, work-sharing applications with Access partners commenced on 1 January 2021.7 Access partners maximise international cooperation through information exchange and work-sharing across various product and technical areas, particularly in the approval of new active substances and the extension of indications for existing licenses. This reduces duplication and increases each agency’s ability to ensure patients have timely access to high-quality, effective and safe medicines.13 The EMA also collaborates with other international authorities through mutual reliance and work-sharing. EMA has mutual recognition agreements with national regulators in Australia, Canada, Japan, New Zealand, Switzerland, USA and Israel.14

An example of convergence collaboration is the International Pharmaceutical Regulators Programme (IPRP). IPRP is a collaborative entity comprising regulatory authorities and international organisations that aims to enhance global pharmaceutical regulatory cooperation by exchanging information on shared concerns and converging regulatory approaches for pharmaceutical medicinal products intended for human use. The programme has 26 members, including MHRA, FDA, EMA, TGA, HSA and PMDA.15 16

International Recognition Procedure

The IRP is a new MHRA expedited licensing route for medicines already approved by trusted regulatory agencies, known as reference regulators (RRs).17 The MHRA evaluates IRP applications in a focused manner, while considering the decisions of the RRs; however, the MHRA may still deny an application if the supporting evidence is deemed insufficient.1 The RRs are seven in number, selected from different regions and they include (1) TGA18 from Australia, (2) Health Canada (HC) from Canada, (3) SwissMedic (SM) from Switzerland, (4) HSA19 from Singapore, (5) PMDA20 from Japan, (6) FDA21 from the USA and (7) the EMA22 and Member State Competent Authorities from the EU and European Economic Area.3 17

Applicants who have previously obtained authorisation for the same product from one of the regulators designated by MHRA are eligible to apply through the IRP. Applicants are responsible for submitting all relevant regulatory documents as evidence of product approval.17 The IRP has two recognition timetables: Recognition A, for which the reference regulator’s approval must have been authorised within the preceding 2 years, and Recognition B, for which the reference regulator’s approval must have been given within the preceding 10 years.17 The anticipated benefits of the IRP include cost-effective processes for approvals, expedited licensing and accelerated patient access to novel medications through the National Health Service (NHS), with the expectation that this will make the UK a global leader in medical innovation.23

Study aims

This paper explores the regulation of innovative medicines in the UK and across five of the seven IRP’s RRs. The five RRs were selected to provide a comprehensive global coverage of recognised regulatory agencies present in different regions. HC and SM were exempted because the FDA represented North America, while the EMA represented the EU. Although Switzerland, a European country, is not part of the EU, it benchmarks its regulatory activities with those of the EMA and FDA.24 The introduction of the IRP may pose a novel challenge for horizon scanners of medicines, regulators, Health Technology Assessment (HTA) bodies and health systems in the UK. The expedited pathways and international partnerships afforded by the IRP can potentially disrupt the standard working practices of these organisations. As a result, it is essential to understand the current landscape of UK medicines, alongside the international landscape of regulation for medicines that could now become quickly available in the UK. This research is based on medicines collated by the Innovation Observatory (IO) in its internal Medicines Innovation Database. The IO is the national horizon scanning centre in the UK, initiating the HTA process by submitting technology briefings of innovative medicines to National Institute for Health and Care Research (NICE) about 24 months before MA approval. These technology briefings comprehensively summarise medicines in the later stages of development (phases II/III) that meet NICE criteria for promising innovative medicines and are therefore likely to be assessed by NICE.25

This study aims to inform the potential impact of the IRP on access to innovative medicines and supporting organisations by comparing the approval of medicines in the UK with that of other international regulatory agencies.

Methods

Search methods

The definition of innovative medicines in this paper aligns with the IO’s horizon scanning concept previously reported.26 It is summarised as medicines within phase I/II to phase III clinical trials in the UK, Europe, the USA, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Singapore and Japan. These medicines are either a new medicinal product, a new biosimilar, a new combination of approved or unapproved medicines, a new fixed-dose combination or a new treatment line. They are developed for new indications, new subgroups or mutations, new disease stages or new populations.26 These innovative medicines are identified and monitored by IO through its pipeline, and technology briefings are submitted to NICE to trigger the HTA process.

A retrospective analysis of innovative medicines (n=154), whose technology briefings were submitted to NICE from 1 January to 31 December 2020, was conducted. The MHRA marketing authorisations for these medicines were expected mainly between 2022 and 2023, close to the IRPs implementation date of January 2024.

The technology briefings were retrieved from the IO repository. The following data were extracted from the briefings: IO identification number, name and indication of medicines, company, category of medicine (new active/new combination/licence extension), disease area and supporting clinical trials.

The websites and databases of six regulators were searched for approval status, regulatory pathway, MA application/submission and MA/approval dates. The regulators were: MHRA (UK),27 FDA (USA),28 EMA (EU),29 PMDA (Japan),30 HSA (Singapore)31 and TGA (Australia).32 We manually searched for the eligible medicines for all regulators by matching details from briefings with those of regulatory submission and approval records.

The website of the Australian regulatory agency (TGA) was checked first, because if it included Public Assessment Reports, it also provided details on the MA application/submission and the MA/approval dates of other regulatory agencies that had already approved the medicine. The cut-off date for the regulatory approval dates was 31 July 2023, meaning that only medicines approved by this date were analysed across all six regulatory agencies. The searches started on 21 August 2023. The supporting clinical trials of the medicine in the briefings were matched with the clinical trials submitted to regulatory agencies, ensuring consistency when extracting data for the MA dates across the various regulatory agencies. Clinical trial registries were checked to collect the status of the trials. The company’s websites, particularly pipeline, news and press releases, were checked for information on the medicines. Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted in two stages. First, the following data were extracted: the medicine’s approval status for a specific indication, submission and approval dates, authorisation procedure (standard or expedited), type of medicine (new active substance or significant license extension) and disease area. Data were extracted for all five RRs and the MHRA. The authorisation procedure was not specified for some medicines. Two reviewers independently extracted all the relevant data required for this study manually, and a third reviewer verified the accuracy of the extracted data. The final data set was extracted and analysed in Microsoft Excel, and the results were presented visually using Microsoft Power BI. The results were analysed with descriptive statistics.

The average time in days between FDA, EMA and MHRA approval dates was calculated using Microsoft Excel, based on the number of working days elapsed between regulators’ approval dates across all eligible medicines. Review times were calculated for the medicines across the regulatory agencies using Microsoft Excel, based on the number of working days elapsed from the submission date to approval date, which varied depending on national regulatory circumstances and the type of application submitted (standard or expedited).

Results

Regulatory approvals

The IO submitted 154 technology briefings to NICE in 2020 to initiate the HTA process for the medicines in anticipation of the medicines receiving MA from MHRA.

As at 31 July 2023, 95 (62%) of the medicines for which a technology briefing was sent were approved by at least one regulator, and 22 (14%) were approved by all six studied regulators. 59 (38%) were not approved by any regulator.

The MHRA approved a total of 71 medicines, out of the 154 medicines evaluated. The MHRA approved more medicines (n=71, 46%) than TGA (n=51, 35%), HSA (n=41, 27%) and PMDA (n=38, 25%) but had fewer approvals than FDA (n=84, 55%) and EMA (n=80, 52%), as shown in figure 1.

There was considerable overlap between approvals, with the medicines approved by one regulator often being approved by other regulators as well.

The FDA had the highest number of first submissions of MA applications (n=64), followed by EMA (n=24), PMDA (n=7) and HSA (n=1). The FDA had the highest number of first approvals of MAs (n=70), followed by EMA (n=17), the PMDA (n=5) and the MHRA (n=1), as shown in table 2. Reasons identified for non-approval (either by rejection of application or not having an application submitted) included failure to meet the primary endpoint in clinical trials, discontinuation/suspension of the medicine’s development, termination of clinical trials and withdrawal of application during the process. Additionally, some clinical trials were ongoing at the time of data collection, with submissions not yet sent and some submissions under review by some regulators.

Regulatory pathways

Examining approved medicines for which information on the pathway used in regulatory submissions was available, we found that the FDA used expedited regulatory pathways more frequently than any other regulator. Of 84 FDA approvals, 54 (64%) used an expedited pathway. The HSA used expedited pathways the least (3 of 41 approvals, 7%). In contrast, the MHRA used expedited pathways 11% of the time (8 of 71 approvals).

Review time

Review time refers to the number of working days from submission of MA application to approval of MA. It was calculated only for medicines where both submission and approval dates were available, and it varied among the regulators. MHRA had the lowest median review time (170 days, IQR 162–236), although with only seven eligible medicines for analysis. This was followed by the FDA, with a median review time of 173 days (n=82, IQR 129–218). The regulator with the longest median review time was the EMA (296 days, n=77, IQR 247–341) (figure 2).

Discussion

The IRP aims to expedite the licensing of innovative medicines by collaborating with competent regulators to accelerate market access. An understanding of the current regulatory timelines in the UK and internationally is essential to explore the possible implications of the IRP on market access and supporting organisations. The study showed that MHRA approved more medicines than the TGA, HSA and PMDA but approved fewer medicines than the FDA and EMA. 62% of the medicines were approved by at least one regulatory agency, while 38% were not approved by any regulatory agency. Only 14% were approved by all six regulatory agencies. The FDA had more first submissions (n=64) and first approvals (n=70) and used more expedited pathways (73%) than the other RRs. The FDA and EMA approved medicines 360 days and 86 days faster, respectively, than MHRA. The MHRA had the shortest median review time, although with a small sample size, followed by FDA, while the EMA had the longest median review time. The MHRA relied on the EMA through the decentralised MRDCRP and ECDRP for most of its approvals prior to the implementation of the IRP, which contributed to the MHRA having the shortest median review time.5 6

Some important measures of successful regulatory processes include the number of approved medicines and the speed of approval. However, are expedited approval processes linked to better medicine regulation and improved patient outcomes? The overall therapeutic benefit of medicines approved through expedited pathways may be unclear if non-randomised data and/or surrogate endpoints are used. Additionally, medicines approved via expedited pathways may put patients at risk when clinicians are not fully informed about a drug’s overall safety and efficacy, particularly if it is licensed before the crucial registrational clinical trial is published, especially in terms of patient selection and risk management.36 To balance safety and efficacy with accelerated access to innovative medicines, when relying on a reference regulator’s decision, the MHRA should reject applications that have insufficient evidence, as stated in the IRP.3

This study provides a snapshot of the regulatory landscape in the UK before the initiation of the IRP using publicly available information. The study also used data from the internal IO’s database, regulatory agencies’ websites and other online data sources, providing a reliable highlight of the current regulatory landscape in the UK. However, the study did not consider other factors that may impact market access, such as the company’s marketing strategy, economics and size. Companies consider the potential market size, epidemiology, pricing, reimbursement options and current market landscape in the specific country before proceeding with licencing plans.37 Also, medicines produced by smaller companies may not make it into international markets, and companies may decide not to pursue approval in certain regions because of uncertainty.37 38 For example, uncertainty in the business, political and health policy environments of a region or country.39 40

The study is consistent with previous research, which showed that the FDA and EMA are the most influential regulatory agencies, with the FDA being the most influential overall.36 The FDA usually approves more medicines,4143 has more first submissions (no submission gap),41 first approvals44 45 and expedited pathways than other regulatory agencies.41 The term ‘submission gap’ describes the time interval between submitting an MA application to one regulatory agency and then subsequently submitting it to another regulatory agency.46 The study results showed a significant gap in approval dates between the MHRA and FDA/EMA regulatory decisions for medicines. The IRP may reduce the gap in approval dates between the MHRA and other RRs, especially the FDA and EMA, if companies apply to MHRA soon after receiving MA from the RRs.

The MHRA had the shortest median review time, followed by the FDA. There is a scarcity of evidence to support this finding, probably because the MHRA became a standalone regulator in January 2021.47 Second, the MHRA’s short median review time may not be representative of the real world because of the small sample size of medicines with both submission and approval dates. Evidence suggests that the FDA usually has the shortest review time compared with other regulators.38 42 45 48 Companies tend to submit MA applications first to the FDA and second to the EMA, more than to any other regulatory agency.41 This may be because the USA is the largest market for pharmaceutical products, spending approximately US$603 billion in 2021, while EU nations come in second place, collectively spending US$282.75 billion in 2020.49 In 2022, North America accounted for 52.3% of global pharmaceutical sales, compared with Europe, which had 22.4%.50 In addition, prescription medicine prices in the US averaged 2.56 times more than in other high-income countries, making it the most profitable region for pharmaceuticals manufacturers, among other reasons, including limited price controls, solid patent protection, a large patient base with substantial health insurance coverage and government funding for research and development.5153

Time to approval is not the only factor driving patient access to innovative medications. Therefore, there is a need for effective collaboration between various organisations within the UK healthcare ecosystem.10 54 Achieving the IRP’s potential to shorten timelines for patient access likely requires adaptation of current systems of operation for horizon scanners, NICE and the NHS. For example, reducing the average time from MHRA approval to NICE guidance would further speed up patient access to innovative medicines; however, the dissemination and implementation of NICE guidance in the NHS is another complexity.55 The IRP could potentially disrupt the NICE appraisal process, particularly if there are mismatches in evidence requirements between NICE and other regulatory agencies.56 57 To tackle this disruption, NICE has stated that companies considering the IRP should provide evidence of randomised controlled trials in a population similar to the UK demography, comparators aligning with the UK standard of care and a 24-month notification period in advance, while adhering to submission deadlines to use this pathway effectively.56

Horizon scanning plays a crucial role in providing timely and relevant intelligence on emerging medicines, which would assist the MHRA and NICE in preparing for innovative medicines. Given the IRP’s aim of expediting licensing timelines and the potential to increase the volume of medicines entering the UK market, early awareness of these medicines is critical. If the MHRA and NICE are to adapt to shorter process timelines and increased activity, they will require horizon scanning intelligence to plan, allocate resources and implement necessary changes to lower the likelihood of disruption and delays.

These data provide an early snapshot of regulatory timelines for innovative medicines before the implementation of the IRP. Future research can continue to monitor these processes as organisations adapt to this system change. This includes trends in approvals over a more extended period, examining the difference in market access in the UK before and after the implementation of the IRP, and evaluating the impact of the IRP on the submission/approval dates gap of medicines. How the industry and organisations respond to the IRP by changing (or not changing) their processes can also be evaluated.

Study limitations

The submission dates of some medicines were not readily available on the MHRA, PMDA or HSA websites. The median review time was calculated only for medicines with available submission and approval dates, which differed across the regulators. The submission and approval dates of licence extension medicines were more challenging to find and, in some cases, were unavailable online, compared with investigational new active medicines. Furthermore, the approval dates of a few medicines were also unavailable online.

The PMDA approvals may not be a complete reflection of the total number of licenced medicines because, at the time of the search, approval dates on the PMDA website were last updated in March 2023. Efforts were made to obtain missing data from alternative sources, such as company news and press releases, TGA website, company websites and manual searches, but some were still unavailable online.

Out of the seven RRs approved for the IRP by MHRA, we examined only five, excluding HC and SM, because North America and EU were well represented by the FDA and EMA. The results of this study are based on the five RRs; our conclusion may have been different if all seven regulators had been explored, particularly in terms of how the regulatory approvals and review times of the medicines approved by HC and SM compare with those of MHRA, TGA, HSA and PMDA.

Conclusion

There were differences in the percentage of approved medicines across the five RRs, with the FDA and EMA leading the way, which is consistent with their role as two of the most influential regulatory agencies globally.58

IRP applications with FDA or EMA as the RRs may expedite access to innovative medicines in the UK, mostly by reducing the approval dates gap between the FDA/EMA and the MHRA. This will ensure that the UK remains at the forefront of medical innovation.

The knowledge that medicines may be approved faster with IRP applications, using EMA or FDA as RRs, will also assist horizon scanners, the MHRA and NICE in adapting their processes and working practices to benefit from the shorter timelines. This would minimise the disruption the IRP may cause by expediting the licensing of innovative medicines. Robust horizon scanning intelligence will be vital in ensuring these changes do not cause significant disruption to health systems.

Future research can assess the IRP’s long-term impact on regulatory practices and patient access.
