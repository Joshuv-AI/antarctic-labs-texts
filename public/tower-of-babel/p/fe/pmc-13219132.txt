The association between CALLY index and obesity in adolescents: a comparative analysis with traditional inflammatory markers
Authors: Yanchao Li, Xiaoshan Shao, Xiaoyan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the association between the C-reactive protein-Albumin-lymphocyte (CALLY) index and obesity in adolescents, and evaluate its predictive performance compared to traditional inflammatory markers.MethodsData from NHANES 2015–2018 were analyzed, including 2188 adolescents aged 12–20 years. The CALLY index was calculated as (albumin × lymphocytes)/CRP. Multivariate weighted logistic regression was used to assess associations with obesity. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and Area Under the Curve (AUC) were computed to evaluate the predictive performance of the CALLY index compared to traditional inflammatory markers (NLR, PLR, SII, SIRI). Survey-weighted logistic regression assessed associations of CALLY with obesity; model fit (Nagelkerke R2) and discrimination were evaluated, with sensitivity analyses for model specifications.ResultsThe CALLY index was inversely associated with obesity (OR = 0.968, 95% CI 0.958–0.977, p < 0.001), with each 1-unit increase reducing obesity odds by 3.20%. This association remained significant after multivariate adjustment and varied by age, race, and physical activity. The standalone AUC of CALLY (0.743) significantly outperformed traditional markers (PLR: 0.515, NLR: 0.546, SII: 0.593, SIRI: 0.598). Combined with covariates, AUC increased to 0.774 (DeLong test p < 0.001). At the cutoff of 15.10, CALLY achieved > 80% sensitivity with a positive likelihood ratio near 2.0.ConclusionsThe CALLY index is a robust and independent predictor of obesity in adolescents, outperforming traditional inflammatory markers. Its integration with traditional covariates further enhances predictive accuracy, suggesting that the CALLY index could be a valuable tool for early screening and intervention in adolescent obesity.Keywords: Adolescent obesity, CALLY index, Logistic regression, ROC analysis, NHANES

Introduction

The prevalence of childhood obesity is rising globally. Given the increasing incidence of adult obesity and obesity-related complications among obese children and adolescents, childhood obesity has emerged as a significant public health concern [1]. Research indicates that unhealthy eating habits, characterized by high-calorie, high-fat, and high-sugar diets, coupled with insufficient physical activity, are the primary lifestyle factors contributing to obesity [2]. Furthermore, socioeconomic factors, such as family income and education level, significantly influence the risk of obesity among children and adolescents [3]. Obesity is associated with a range of metabolic abnormalities, particularly insulin resistance and inflammatory responses [4, 5].

Adolescent obesity is increasingly recognized as a complex metabolic disorder that is characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation [6]. Recent studies have highlighted that systemic inflammatory biomarker—such as the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI)—play a significant role in quantifying this inflammatory state and in predicting obesity-related complications [6, 7]. These four inflammatory composite indices (NLR, PLR, SII, SIRI), derived from routine blood tests, can effectively predict tumor prognosis, cardiovascular risk, and progression to severe infections [8–11]. By reflecting systemic immune-inflammatory status, they assist in disease risk stratification and serve as cost-effective clinical prognostic assessment tools. In recent years, an emerging biomarker known as CALLY, which reflects both inflammation and immune response, has garnered increasing attention from researchers. Previous retrospective studies have explored the relationship between the CALLY index and the prognosis of patients with colorectal cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma, demonstrating its superior efficacy compared to other inflammation-based biomarkers [12, 13]. However, research investigating the correlation between the CALLY index and adolescent obesity remains limited.

This study aims to explore the relationship between CALLY and adolescent obesity through systematic analysis and empirical research, revealing the mechanism of action and potential effects of CALLY in the intervention of adolescent obesity. This research not only contributes to enriching the theoretical framework of adolescent obesity intervention but also provides a scientific basis for the design and application of future intelligent health devices.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

This study utilizes data from the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) and employs a cross-sectional design to examine the association between the Controlling Nutritional Status (CALLY) index and obesity among U.S. adolescents aged 12–20 years. Data were drawn from the 2015–2018 cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), which employs a complex, multistage probability sampling design to represent the non-institutionalized civilian population of the United States [14, 15]. The NHANES 2015–2016 and 2017–2018 cycle data used in this study were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Ethics Review Board (Continuation of Protocol #2011–17). For participants aged 12–17 years, written informed assent was obtained from the adolescents, and written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians. For participants aged ≥ 18 years, written informed consent was obtained directly from the participants. As this research utilizes publicly available, anonymized secondary data, no additional ethical review was required.

This analysis combined data from two NHANES cycles (2015–2016 and 2017–2018), following NCHS recommendations, initially including 19,225 individuals (Fig. 1). The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) age under 12 years or over 20 years (16,556 cases excluded, n = 2669 remaining), (2) missing BMI data (27 cases excluded, n = 2642 remaining), (3) incomplete lymphocyte, albumin, or C-reactive protein data preventing calculation of the CALLY index (454 cases excluded, n = 2188 remaining). The initial dataset included 2,188 participants.

Flow chart of the study population identification from NHANES 2015–2018

Study population and age characteristics:

After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 2188 participants aged between 12 and 20 years were included in the final analysis.

Age characteristics:

This study presents the weighted baseline characteristics of 32.28 million U.S. adolescents aged 12–20 years, representing the NHANES 2015–2018 non-institutionalized civilian population. Overall, 1857 participants (84.87%) were classified as normal weight, whereas 331 (15.13%) met the criteria for obesity.

C-reactive protein-albumin-lymphocyte index (CALLY)

Blood samples were collected from participants at the mobile testing center and subsequently sent to the central laboratory for the measurement of lymphocyte, albumin, and CRP levels, following standardized protocols. Serum albumin concentration was measured using the Roche Cobas 6000 analyzer with bromocresol purple reagent. Blood cell counts were analyzed using the Coulter HMX Hematology Analyzer, with results for neutrophils, platelets, monocytes, and lymphocytes reported in standardized units of × 103 cells per microliter. Our primary focus was on the CALLY index, a novel metric derived from albumin levels, lymphocyte counts, and CRP values. Specifically, the CALLY index was calculated as [(Albumin in g/L) * (Lymphocyte count in 1000 cells/µL)] / (CRP in mg/dL), providing a comprehensive evaluation of inflammatory and nutritional status [16]. The CALLY index was divided into quartiles based on the following percentile cutoffs in the overall study sample: Q1 (lowest quartile, < 5.38), Q2 (5.38 to < 15.30), Q3 (15.30 to < 36.00), and Q4 (highest quartile, ≥ 36.00). These thresholds represent the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of the CALLY distribution, respectively.

Inflammatory markers

This study used systemic immune inflammation index (SII), systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI), platelet to lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR) as inflammation-related variables, where SII was counted by platelets × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count is obtained, and SIRI is obtained from monocyte count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count [17]. The above variables are calculated as × 103 cells/μL. PLR is derived from platelet count/lymphocyte count [18]. NLR is derived from neutrophil count/lymphocyte count [19]. Lymphocyte, neutrophil, monocyte, and platelet counts were performed using a fully automated blood analyzer (Kurt® DxH 800 analyzer) for complete blood counts.

Obesity

Body measurements were collected by trained health technicians at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC). During the physical examination, health technicians were supported by recorders. The examination protocol was determined by the participant's age at the time of the screening interview. Standing height was measured using a stadiometer equipped with a fixed vertical backboard and an adjustable headpiece. Participants' weights were recorded using a digital weighing scale. Health technicians underwent standardized training and were regularly assessed through intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC > 0.90) and technical error of measurement (TEM < 0.5 cm). Participants were required to remove shoes, wear light examination gowns, and void their bladder prior to measurement. For height measurement, the head was positioned in the Frankfurt horizontal plane; waist circumference was measured at the level of the iliac crest. To determine BMI of the subjects, the weight was divided by the square of the height, expressed in kilograms per square meter (kg/m2) [20]. For adolescents, BMI is categorized differently than for adults, utilizing age- and sex-specific percentiles derived from growth charts. These percentiles represent the distribution of BMI within a reference population of the same age and sex, thereby facilitating a more accurate assessment of weight status in pediatric populations (≥ 95th percentile) [21].

Covariate assessment

In our study, we analyzed several potential covariates, which included age categories (12–16 years, and 17–20 years), gender (boys and girls), race (Mexican American, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and Other), Poverty-Income Ratio (PIR, categorized as < 1, ≥ 1). Sedentary time was assessed using the NHANES Physical Activity Questionnaire: "Over the past 30 days, on average, how many hours per day did you sit and watch TV or videos?" and "Over the past 30 days, on average, how many hours per day did you use a computer or play computer games outside of school?" Total daily screen time was categorized as No (< 2 h/day) or Yes (≥ 2 h/day). Physical activity was assessed using the NHANES Physical Activity Questionnaire: "During the past 7 days, on how many days were you physically active for a total of at least 60 min per day?" Based on the frequency of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), participants were categorized into three groups: Low (0–2 days/week), Moderate (2–5 days/week), and Active (5–7 days/week). The data on sedentary was obtained from the Physical Activity dataset and was assessed using the following NHANES question: “Over the past 30 days, on average, how many hours per day did you use a computer or play computer games outside of school?" and "Over the past 30 days, on average, how many hours per day did you sit and watch TV or videos?" Blood specimens were processed and shipped to the University of Minnesota Advanced Research Diagnostics Laboratory (ARDL, Minneapolis, MN, USA) for analysis. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D was measured by ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS), while alkaline phosphatase (ALP), albumin, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and lipoproteins were quantified using Beckman Coulter UniCel DxC 800 Synchron and DxC 660i Synchron Access analyzers (Beckman Coulter Inc., Brea, California, USA) with standardized enzymatic methods: albumin by bromcresol purple dye-binding, ALP by p-nitrophenol phosphate (PNPP) kinetic assay (450 nm), and cholesterol/triglycerides by enzymatic methods with Trinder reaction. All assays adhered to CLIA (1988) mandates and Westgard quality control rules. NHANES laboratory tests strictly adhere to CLIA (1988) quality standards, conducted by the University of Minnesota ARDL Laboratory with quality control using Westgard rules. Accuracy is ensured through blind split samples and 2% random retesting. All technicians undergo annual competency evaluations and training, while NCHS continuously monitors data quality through quarterly report reviews. Following the cutoff values established in the report released by the Institute of Medicine, we classified serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] levels in adolescents into three categories: (i) adequate vitamin D concentrations, defined as serum 25(OH)D levels of 50 nmol/L or greater; (ii) insufficient vitamin D concentrations, defined as serum 25(OH)D levels ranging from 30 nmol/L to less than 50 nmol/L; and (iii) vitamin D deficiency, defined as serum 25(OH)D levels of less than 30 nmol/L [22, 23].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R 4.5.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). The 4-year examination weights (WTMEC2YR) and design variables (stratum SDMVSTRA, cluster SDMVPSU) from NHANES 2015–2018 were used throughout via the survey package to account for the complex, multistage sampling design. Continuous variables are presented as mean (SD) for normally distributed data and median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Beyond the CALLY components, covariate missingness ranged 0.10–5.60%. To avoid complete-case bias, missing values were imputed by chained equations (mice, 20 imputations). The imputation model contained all analytic variables, the design variables, and the auxiliary laboratory measures listed below. Estimates were pooled across imputations with Rubin’s rules; reported coefficients and 95% CIs reflect the combined, design-adjusted data. Obesity (BMI ≥ 95th percentile of the CDC 2000 reference) was the outcome. Survey-weighted logistic regression was fitted to estimate the odds of obesity per 1-SD increase in the CALLY index. The covariate set was fixed: age, sex, race, poverty-to-income ratio, sedentary time, physical activity, vitamins D, total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL-C, HDL-C and ALP. Effects are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals; two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To examine the influence of extreme values, observations outside the 1st and 99th percentiles of the CALLY index (≈2% of the weighted sample) were excluded and the same survey-weighted logistic model was refitted; estimates are presented alongside the primary results. Three predictive models were constructed to compare predictive performance: (i) individual inflammatory indices (NLR, PLR, SII, SIRI) alone, (ii) CALLY alone, and (iii) traditional inflammatory markers combined with CALLY. Model fit was assessed using Nagelkerke R2 (preferred for logistic regression as it ranges 0–1) and Cox & Snell R2. The baseline model explained 5.60% of the variance (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.056), increasing to 17.60% after adding CALLY (ΔR2 = 0.120). Survey-weighted Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and Area Under the Curve (AUC) were computed with the surveyROC package; pairwise AUC comparisons used the complex-sample adapted DeLong method. To assess robustness, we conducted sensitivity analyses: (1) using age as a categorical variable instead of continuous (agecat model); (2) winsorizing CALLY at the upper 99th percentile to minimize the influence of extreme values (trim99 model).

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1, with a p-value < 0.05 (two-tailed) regarded as statistically significant.

Results

This study included a total of 2188 U.S. adolescents, among whom 331 (15.13%) were classified as obese. As shown in Table 1, significant differences were observed in demographic characteristics between the two groups. Although the gender distribution was similar (males: 49.55% vs. females: 50.45%, p = 0.497), the obesity group was significantly younger, with 82.78% of participants aged 12–16 years compared to 56.27% in the normal-weight adolescents’ group (p < 0.001). The racial composition varied significantly (p = 0.005), with higher proportions of non-Hispanic Black individuals (26.28% vs. 20.19%) and Mexican American individuals (25.08% vs. 20.73%). No statistically significant differences were found between the two groups regarding PIR and sedentary (p > 0.05). In terms of clinical biochemical indicators, the obese group demonstrated significant metabolic abnormalities and an inflammatory state. The lipid profile indicated markedly elevated median TG levels (2.40 vs. 2.17 mmol/L) and significantly decreased HDL-C levels (0.90 vs. 0.66 mmol/L, all p < 0.001) in the obese group. Additionally, TC exhibited a mild increase (4.12 vs. 4.01 mmol/L, p = 0.016). Characteristic alterations in nutritional and inflammatory markers were observed. The obese group exhibited decreased serum albumin levels (42.70 vs. 44.30 g/L), significantly elevated CRP levels (1.90 vs. 0.50 mg/L), and increased ALP activity (123.00 vs. 94.00 U/L, all p < 0.001). Additionally, the obese group demonstrated higher rates of vitamin D3 insufficiency/deficiency (60.12% vs. 42.11%, p < 0.001) and a greater prevalence of physical inactivity (p = 0.047). In the adolescents with obesity group, all inflammatory indices were significantly elevated, apart from PLR (p = 0.373). Specifically, the counts of WBC, NEUT, LYMPH, and MONO were all significantly higher than those in the normal weight group (all p < 0.001). Among the composite inflammatory indices, NLR, SII, and SIRI were significantly elevated in the obesity group (all p < 0.01). Notably, CALLY exhibited an opposite distribution pattern; the median CALLY in the obesity group was significantly lower than that in the normal-weight group (5.38 vs. 19.40, p < 0.001), indicating a characteristic decrease of this index in obese adolescents.

To evaluate the population heterogeneity in the association between CALLY and obesity, we conducted stratified analyses based on demographic and clinical characteristics (Table 3). Overall, each 1-unit increase in CALLY was associated with a 3.20% reduction in the risk of obesity (OR = 0.968, 95% CI 0.958–0.977; p < 0.001). Age significantly modified this association (interaction p < 0.001). Among adolescents aged 12–16 years, the protective association of CALLY was significant, exhibiting a large effect size (OR = 0.948, 95% CI 0.934–0.961; p < 0.001). However, this association was not present among youths aged 17–20 years (OR = 0.993, 95% CI 0.982–1.004; p = 0.185). Stratification by race revealed a marginally significant interaction trend (p = 0.062), with slightly larger effect sizes observed in non-Hispanic Blacks (OR = 0.956) and Hispanics/other races (OR = 0.959–0.986) compared to non-Hispanic Whites (OR = 0.966). Notably, all racial subgroups exhibited significant negative associations. The associations between gender, PIR, sedentary, and physical activity with obesity were not significantly modified by CALLY, as indicated by interaction p-values of 0.844, 0.318, 0.970, and 0.612, respectively. Notably, while vitamin D did not demonstrate a statistically significant interaction (p = 0.119), the effect size of CALLY markedly increased within the vitamin D deficient subgroup (OR = 0.910, 95% CI 0.859–0.964; p = 0.001). This finding suggests that individuals with vitamin D deficiency may be at a heightened risk for inflammation-related obesity.

Table 4 and Fig. 2 illustrated the ROC characteristics of individual inflammatory indicators and the combined model for predicting obesity. Among the individual indicators, the CALLY exhibited the highest discriminative accuracy (AUC = 0.743, 95% CI 0.728–0.758), significantly surpassing SIRI (AUC = 0.598), SII (AUC = 0.593), NLR (AUC = 0.546), and PLR (AUC = 0.515) as determined by the DeLong test (p < 0.001). However, despite these findings, the specificity of these indicators remains exceptionally low (< 31.30%), with positive likelihood ratios ranging from 1.12 to 1.24, thus indicating limited clinical utility. Notably, PLR demonstrates negligible discriminative value, with an AUC approaching 0.5. Incorporating CALLY into the traditional model significantly enhanced predictive performance, with AUC increasing from 0.654 to 0.774 (ΔAUC = 0.120, p < 0.001). The combined model achieved an optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity, demonstrating 73.70% sensitivity compared to 69.60% specificity, along with a positive likelihood ratio of 2.43. DeLong's test showed the combined model (AUC = 0.774) significantly outperformed the traditional model (AUC = 0.654), with an AUC gain of 0.120 (95% CI 0.094–0.147: Z = −8.90, p < 0.001).

The standalone AUC of CALLY reached 0.743, significantly outperforming NLR, PLR, SII, and SIRI, which had AUC values ranging from 0.515 to 0.598. Sensitivity analyses demonstrated robustness of the observed associations. When age was modeled as categorical variable (agecat) or CALLY was winsorized at the 99th percentile (trim99), the inverse association between CALLY and obesity remained virtually unchanged (OR = 0.972, 95% CI 0.959–0.982 and OR = 0.967, 95% CI 0.955–0.978, respectively; main model: OR = 0.970, 95% CI 0.959–0.979).

Discussion

We found that obese adolescents exhibited a significantly lower CALLY index, accompanied by elevated systemic inflammatory markers and an unfavorable lipid profile. These cross-sectional patterns support the notion that early-onset obesity is associated with a chronic, low-grade inflammatory state and impaired nutritional immunity [24, 25]. The 12-point lower median CALLY value observed in individuals with obesity (5.38 vs 19.40) corroborates recent findings in adults, indicating an inverse correlation with CRP and a positive correlation with albumin [26]. These two components were significantly altered in our cohort. Furthermore, the simultaneous elevation of SIRI and SII suggests a neutrophil-driven inflammatory response [27, 28]. Additionally, the reduction in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and the elevation of triglycerides reflect the atherogenic lipid profile that has already been documented in pediatric metabolic syndrome [29, 30]. The younger age distribution of obese participants aligns with NHANES secular trends that indicate an earlier onset of adiposity rebound in the second decade of life [31]. However, it cannot be determined from our cross-sectional design whether the depressed CALLY index merely reflects acute-phase changes or contributes causally to subsequent cardio-metabolic risk; longitudinal studies are necessary to clarify the temporal order.

Our observation that a higher CALLY index is independently and linearly associated with lower odds of adolescent obesity contributes to the limited pediatric literature on this composite nutritional-inflammation marker. The magnitude of this gradient is clinically meaningful and aligns with findings in asthma, where the per-unit OR is 0.995 [32]. Three mechanisms may explain the inverse relationship observed. First, both albumin and lymphocyte counts—components of the numerator in the CALLY index—are diminished in conditions characterized by protein–energy wasting and chronic inflammation [33, 34]. Their relative preservation may therefore indicate improved nutritional immunity and reduced adipose tissue biotoxicity. Second, CRP, which serves as the denominator in the CALLY index, is synthesized in response to interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), cytokines that are often overexpressed in visceral adiposity [35, 36]. Consequently, a lower CALLY score may reflect an anti-inflammatory environment. Third, the suppression of CRP while increasing albumin and lymphocyte counts is negatively correlated with BMI z-score or obesity in adolescents [37–39]. This suggests that CALLY may partially mediate the effects of nutritional behaviors on adiposity. In comparison to single inflammatory indices such as SIRI, SII, NLR, and PLR, CALLY exhibited an effect size that was 2 to 4 times stronger. This finding underscores the additional value of integrating nutritional and inflammatory metrics. However, the cross-sectional design of this study limits our ability to draw conclusions about causality; it is plausible that obesity may reduce CALLY levels instead of the reverse. Therefore, longitudinal and intervention studies are necessary to determine whether CALLY can function as an early-warning indicator or a modifiable target within pediatric weight management programs.

In subgroup analyses, the negative correlation between CALLY and obesity remained robust across most stratifications except for age. Among younger adolescents (ages 12–16 years), each unit increase in CALLY was associated with a 3.20% reduction in obesity odds, while no significant reduction was observed in the 17–20 age group (OR ≈ 1.00). This age-related pattern is consistent with longitudinal evidence indicating that weight gain velocity and inflammatory surges peak between the ages of 10 and 15 years [40, 41]. Consequently, the nutritional-immune resilience captured by CALLY may exert a more pronounced influence during early adolescence. No heterogeneity was detected concerning sex, PIR, or vitamin D3, which supports the generalizability of the effect within the U.S. adolescent population. Collectively, these subgroup analyses indicate that CALLY may be especially effective in identifying younger or less active youth who are at a disproportionately high risk of obesity and who could benefit most from targeted nutritional interventions. Longitudinal studies are necessary to ascertain whether the age-related divergence signifies a critical window of causality or merely represents a cross-sectional snapshot.

Our head-to-head comparison demonstrates that the CALLY index—an integrative marker of nutritional immunity—significantly outperforms single cellular ratios and provides meaningful discriminative power when combined with routine metabolic covariates. In contrast, NLR, PLR, SII and SIRI clustered near the diagonal (AUC 0.515–0.598), thereby confirming their limited utility in adolescent populations. Adding CALLY to the traditional model significantly enhanced predictive accuracy, improving AUC from 0.654 to 0.774. The cutoff value of 15.10 determined by ROC analysis was situated between the distributions of the two groups, with a median of 5.38 in the obese group compared to 19.40 in the non-obese group. Given that CALLY exhibited a negative correlation with obesity (OR = 0.968), individuals with CALLY levels below 15.10 were classified as high-risk for obesity. This threshold accurately identified 82.80% of individuals who were truly obese (sensitivity), but it misclassified 42.90% of normal-weight individuals as high-risk, resulting in a specificity of 100%–57.1%. The combined model, which integrates traditional inflammatory markers with CALLY, achieved a sensitivity of 73.70% and a specificity of 69.60% at a cutoff value of 0.18. CALLY has garnered increasing attention from scholars, revealing a negative correlation with Kawasaki disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, and diabetes [32, 42–44]. Some researchers even propose that its predictive performance for disease onset may exceed that of NLR and PLR [43, 45]. Thus, CALLY may represent a novel biomarker for these conditions.

This represents a significant improvement over the traditional model alone, which exhibited a sensitivity of 60.10% and a specificity of 66.80%. Although some perspectives, as noted by Pencina et al., suggest that the AUC may be overly conservative when robust predictors are already included in the model [46], the substantial increase observed in this study far exceeds the minimum threshold of 2 percentage points, which is generally considered clinically significant. This strongly demonstrates the incremental value provided by CALLY. Mechanistically, the improvement reflects the complementary information captured by CALLY: albumin and lymphocytes serve as indicators of protein reserves and adaptive immunity, while CRP reflects the inflammatory drive of adipose tissue [47–49]. Traditional models lack this nutritional-immune dimension, thereby underscoring the clear added value of the current approach. From a translational perspective, CALLY can be derived from a standard Complete Blood Count and basic biochemistry at minimal additional cost. The more pronounced effect observed in younger and less active adolescents indicates that prioritizing CALLY-enhanced screening in early secondary school settings could yield the most significant lifetime benefits from preventive interventions. In summary, incorporating CALLY into existing metabolic panels provides a low-cost, well-calibrated tool that significantly improves the detection of obesity risk in U.S. adolescents and merits evaluation in prospective cohorts.

This study is based on single-visit physical examination data from NHANES 2015–2018 and does not establish a temporal relationship between CALLY and obesity, nor does it infer causality. The components of CALLY (albumin, CRP) may be influenced by recent infections, trauma, or laboratory measurement errors, which can lead to short-term fluctuations and potentially result in the overestimation or underestimation of discriminative ability. Although multiple imputation was employed to address missing covariates, the imputation model relies on the assumption of 'missing at random,' and unobserved confounding factors may still introduce bias. The model weights were derived from non-institutionalized civilian adolescents in the United States, and the applicability of these conclusions to other countries, ethnicities, or clinically high-risk populations requires external validation. The improvement in AUC indicates enhanced discriminative performance; however, it does not assess the modifiability of CALLY as an intervention target. Future longitudinal cohort studies are necessary to validate its predictive capability for disease risk and response to weight-loss interventions.

Conclusion

Among adolescents aged 12–20 in the United States, the CALLY index exhibited a significant independent and dose-dependent negative correlation with obesity. Its discriminative capability surpassed that of traditional inflammatory ratios, achieving an improvement to 0774 when integrated with conventional covariates, representing a net increase of 12%. The CALLY index can be swiftly calculated from routine blood tests, providing advantages such as low cost, high discriminative power, and quantifiability, thus making it suitable for inclusion in early obesity screening toolkits. Future longitudinal studies are required to validate its predictive value concerning disease onset and intervention response.

What is already known on this subject?

Chronic low-grade inflammation is a well-established feature of obesity, and inflammatory markers such as CRP, NLR, and PLR have been associated with adiposity in various populations. However, most existing studies have focused on adult populations, and the utility of composite inflammatory indices—particularly those incorporating nutritional and immune parameters—in adolescent obesity remains underexplored.

What your study adds?

This study demonstrates that the CALLY index, a novel composite marker combining CRP, albumin, and lymphocyte counts, is a robust and independent predictor of obesity in adolescents, significantly outperforming traditional inflammatory markers (NLR, PLR, SII, SIRI) in discriminative ability (AUC 0.743 vs. 0.515–0.598). These findings suggest that the CALLY index could serve as a valuable, easily accessible tool for early screening and targeted intervention in adolescent obesity, potentially informing clinical risk stratification and public health strategies.
