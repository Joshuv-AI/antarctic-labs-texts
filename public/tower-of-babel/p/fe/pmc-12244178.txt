Gender differences in the transactional associations between anxiety sensitivity and emotion regulation over time
Authors: Leah D Church, Danielle R Shayani, Anna Stumps, Nadia Bounoua, Kaleigh Wieand, Jeffrey M Spielberg
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough anxiety sensitivity (AS) and disturbances in emotion regulation (ER) are both transdiagnostic factors thought to predispose toward internalizing pathology, little is known about associations between these constructs over time. Further, no work has examined the modulating impact of gender on these associations, despite previous work identifying gender differences in both AS and ER. Data were collected from 275 adults (Mage = 35.7; 52% women) at two timepoints, six months apart (baseline=March 2021, follow-up=September 2021), using an online crowdsourcing platform. Multigroup cross-lagged analyses showed that, across genders, lower baseline ER predicted higher AS at follow-up (p < .001), over and above baseline AS, but the reverse was not true. Additionally, this association was moderated by gender (∆Wald’s χ2 =8.33, p < .01), such that lower baseline ER predicted higher follow-up AS in men (p < .001), but not women (p = .35). Our findings suggest the presence of gender differences in the longitudinal, transactional associations between transdiagnostic risk factors important for the maintenance and exacerbation of future internalizing pathology. For example, men may be engaging in less adaptive ER strategies (e.g., suppression), resulting in a failure to downregulate future AS. Present results inform etiological models of internalizing disorders.Keywords: Anxiety sensitivity, Emotion regulation, Gender differences, Longitudinal study

Introduction

Anxiety disorders are among the most widespread psychological disorders in the United States [20], the 6th leading cause of disability worldwide [4], and have become increasingly burdensome in recent years [31]. Mounting evidence suggests that transdiagnostic factors are key to understanding the maintenance and exacerbation of internalizing pathology [27], [28], [8]. Although several transdiagnostic factors have emerged as playing a key role in anxiety, little is known regarding how these different factors relate over time, which is critical to developing comprehensive etiological models of anxiety.

One key transdiagnostic factor is anxiety sensitivity (AS), which is the fear of sensations related to physiological arousal (e.g., increased heart rate, difficulty breathing) and the belief that such sensations may lead to negative physical (e.g., heart attack), social (e.g., embarrassment), and/or psychological (e.g., insanity) outcomes [29], [34], [36]. Numerous studies highlight relationships between AS and several anxiety-related disorders, including generalized anxiety disorder (GAD; [18]), panic disorder (PD; [16]), and social anxiety disorder (SAD; [18]). A separate, but related, transdiagnostic factor implicated across anxiety-related disorders is difficulties in emotion regulation (ER; see Cisler et al. [8] for a review). ER encompasses an individual’s ability to implement both psychological and behavioral strategies to identify, process, and respond to emotions [12], [8], [7]. Further, given the heterogeneity of difficulties in ER, more work is needed to determine how ER may be related to other transdiagnostic factors of anxiety, such as AS.

Examining how ER and AS may change together over time is critical in elucidating how transdiagnostic factors may maintain or exacerbate each other and future anxiety pathology. For example, previous work has shown that AS is relatively stable over time, but there remains important unexplained variation over time [15], [30], suggesting that there is some malleability in AS. In fact, it is this malleability that is targeted in treatments that focus on AS [33]. However, little is known about the individual difference factors that lead to variability of AS across time. Similarly, research to date has shown that ER capacity varies across the lifespan, particularly during certain periods (e.g., adolescence). Although ER is relatively stable over middle adulthood [42], there remain important individual difference-related changes in ER over time.

Although AS and ER are distinct transdiagnostic factors, previous work has identified unique interactive effects [1], [19]. Indeed, relationships between AS and ER have been highlighted in the development, maintenance, and treatment of anxiety-related symptoms and pathology [1], [19], [2]. For example, past work suggests that lower ER capacity may serve to exacerbate AS, subsequently influencing an array of additional anxiety outcomes (e.g., worry, panic; [1]). Established theoretical models and empirical work to date suggests AS and internalizing symptoms may partially depend upon, or be influenced by, an individual’s ER capacity [19], [23]. Conversely, exacerbated levels of AS may theoretically inhibit an individual’s ability to effectively engage in ER strategies needed to down-regulate symptoms. In clinical populations, previous work shows that changes in AS and ER mediate reductions in anxiety symptoms in a sample with anxiety-related disorders (e.g., generalized anxiety disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, social phobia; [2]). Together, these results highlight the role of emotion regulation in both transdiagnostic anxiety processes, such as anxiety sensitivity, and anxiety-related symptoms. Together, these studies highlight potentially unique risk factors for internalizing pathology based on the relationship between AS and ER. Indeed, relationships have been identified between body hypervigilance and emotion regulation capabilities in individuals with a history of panic attacks, further suggesting that deficits in emotion regulation may play a key role in the development/maintenance of internalizing [38]. Yet, the cross-sectional nature of these studies prohibits causal interpretations of these effects. Thus, it remains unclear how ER and AS influence each other over time, which is key to advancing etiological models of anxiety pathology.

Past research has also established sex and gender differences in both AS and ER. For example, Stewart et al. [35] found that females reported greater levels of AS compared to male counterparts. Indeed, profiles of AS are influenced by genetic, hormonal, and environmental factors in women [32], but men only show influences from environmental factors [37]. Past work has also identified unique differences between males and females in the use of ER strategies. For example, males showed greater engagement in distinct positive (e.g., refocus on planning) and negative (e.g., blaming others) ER strategies compared to their female counterparts [43]. However, women showed greater engagement in strategies such as rumination and putting experiences into perspective [43]. Past work has also highlighted gender differences in emotion regulation on a neural level, implicating the role of gender in emotional processing more broadly [22]. Thus, important gender differences are apparent in both AS and ER, but the impact of gender on interactive relationships between these factors has yet to be examined.

In summary, research is needed to understand the temporally interactive effects between AS and ER, and the impact of gender therein. Advancing our understanding of such effects between transdiagnostic factors may allow for greater elucidation of multidimensional models of anxiety vulnerability. Potential synergistic or antagonistic relationships between AS and ER may confer greater risk or protection for the development of internalizing pathology. Moreover, parsing potential transactional relationships between AS and ER may allow for greater tailoring of intervention targets. For example, understanding whether AS potentiates future ER, or vice versa, could provide significant insight into top-down or bottom-up regulatory processes of emotions in the context of anxiety symptomatology.

The aim of the present study was to address these significant gaps in the literature by examining longitudinal interactions between AS and ER using cross-lagged structural equation modeling (SEM). Building upon existing literature and theory, we predicted that longitudinal relationships between AS and ER would interact such that both (i) differences in AS at baseline would predict future ER, over and above baseline ER and (ii) baseline ER would predict future AS, over and above baseline AS. Finally, given past evidence of gender differences in AS and ER in isolation, we hypothesized gender would moderate the transactional relationships between AS and ER.

Methods

Participants

Participants between the ages of 18–65 were recruited through an online crowdsourcing platform (Prolific Academic; https://www.prolific.co) as part of a larger online study. For the baseline sample, only participants from countries in which English is a dominant language were allowed to participate, specifically the United Kingdom, United States, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand, Canada, Puerto Rico, and the Minor Outlying Islands of the United States. Inclusion criteria were: ages 18–65, first language was English, Prolific approval rating of 95% or better. A filter was also applied to ensure the study consisted of males and females equally. In order to ensure data quality, participants must have taken at least 20 min to complete the baseline study in order to participate in the follow up data collection, as anything below this duration is highly suggestive that the participant was not carefully attending to the material. Initial (Time 1) data were collected from 745 adults and follow-up data were collected from 310 individuals. Outliers on key study variables (n = 35) were removed. Participants were asked to report their gender identity, and responses including “male/men” and “female/woman” were used in present analyses. The final longitudinal sample consisted of 275 adults (Mage = 35.7; 52% women). Racial breakdowns of this sample were: 72.4% white, 9.5% black, 5.1% Asian, 7.3% mixed race, and 2.5% other. Race data were missing for 3.3% of the sample.

Procedure

Participants completed baseline data collection in March 2021. Follow-up (Time 2) was collected from participants six months later in September 2021. We a priori planned to collect follow-up data from only 300 individuals, and thus data were collected from the first ∼300 individuals who responded to the invitation to participate in the 6-month follow-up. The participants who did not complete the follow up were either too slow to respond to the invitation or declined to participate, and it is not possible to differentiate between the two. Thus, these data are not necessarily ‘missing’ in the typical usage of this word (i.e., researchers intended to collect this data, but were unable). The reason that the follow-up sample was (a priori) limited to only half the baseline sample is due to the needs of the parent study from which these data were drawn, and thus should not impact the inferences drawn in the present study. Participants provided written consent, obtained through Prolific Academic. All procedures were approved by the University of Delaware Institutional Review Board. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) tools, a secure, web-based software platform, hosted at the University of Delaware [13], [14].

Measures

The Anxiety Sensitivity Index-3 (ASI; [37]) is an 18-item self-report measure of anxiety sensitivity, or an individual’s propensity to interpret anxiety-related sensations as distressing. The ASI consists of three subscales which assess physical (e.g., “It scares me when I feel faint.”), cognitive (e.g., “It is important for me to stay in control of my emotions”), and social concerns (e.g., “Is it important not to appear nervous”) related to arousal. Items are rated using a five-point Likert scale and range from 0 (very little) to 4 (very much). The physical, cognitive, and social subscale scores were intercorrelated and positively correlated with mean ASI scores across both time points (rs >.44). To assess global dimensions of AS, mean scores were calculated by averaging across all 18 items of the ASI. The mean ASI scores in the present sample falls between those seen in previous community and clinical samples [37]. Skewness and kurtosis values at baseline (.51/−.52) and follow-up (.29/−.73) were within acceptable ranges. Internal consistencies (Cronbach’s alpha) in the current sample were good (Time 1 =.89; Time 2 =.88).

The Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale - Brief Version (DERS; [5]) is a 16-item measure designed to assess difficulties evaluating, responding to, and regulating one’s emotions. The DERS consists of 5 subscales: lack of emotional clarity (2 items; e.g., “I am confused about how I feel), difficulties engaging in goal-directed behavior (3 items; e.g., ”When I’m upset, I have difficulty getting work done.”), impulse control difficulties (3 items; e.g., “When I’m upset, I become out of control.”), limited access to effective emotion regulation strategies (5 items; e.g., “When I’m upset, my emotions feel overwhelming.”), and nonacceptance of emotional responses (3 items; e.g., “When I’m upset, I feel like I am weak.”). The subscales were intercorrelated and positively correlated with mean DERS scores (rs >.39). Mean scores were calculated by averaging across all 16 items of the DERS. Internal consistencies (Cronbach’s alpha) in the current sample were good (Time 1 = .93; Time 2 = .94). Higher mean scores reflect greater difficulty with emotion regulation. The mean, standard deviation, and range of the DERS scores in the present sample are consistent with those in previous community samples (e.g., [5]). Furthermore, skewness and kurtosis values at baseline (.65/−.40) and follow-up (.64/−.40) were within acceptable ranges.

Cross-lagged panel analyses

Multigroup cross-lagged structural equation modeling was conducted using MPlus v.8 [25] to examine (i) longitudinal associations between AS and ER and (ii) gender differences between these longitudinal associations. Age was included as a covariate of no interest.

The model allowed the variables at baseline to covary (e.g., Time 1 anxiety sensitivity with Time 1 emotion regulation; see Fig. 1, Fig. 2) and the error terms of the Time 2 variables to covary. The model included a path within each variable across time points (e.g., stability paths; Time 1 to Time 2 anxiety sensitivity) as well as a path between variables across time points (e.g., cross-lag paths; Time 1 anxiety sensitivity with Time 2 emotion regulation). Finally, multigroup analyses were performed to examine moderation by gender. In the first model, all variables were free to vary across gender, whereas in the second model, all variables were held equal across women and men. Gender differences in covariances, stability, and cross-lag paths were probed using the Wald χ2 statistic to determine whether any differences in the pathways were statistically significant. Cross-lagged panel analyses

Main effects revealed that, across genders, DERS at Time 1 predicted higher ASI at Time 2 (standardized β = .20; p < .001), over and above Time 1 ASI, but the reverse was not true (Fig. 1). The main effects model showed acceptable fit (CFI =.99; RMSEA =.04; 90% CI = [.00–.14]; SRMR =.04).

Additionally, cross-lagged association between ASI and DERS were moderated by gender (Fig. 2; ∆Wald’s χ2 =8.33, p < .01), and this model showed acceptable fit (CFI=.99; RMSEA=.08; 90% CI=[.00 − .16]; SRMR=.06). Probing the gender differences revealed that Time 1 DERS predicted higher Time 2 ASI in men (β = .35; p < .001), but not women (β = .06; p = .35). No significant group differences emerged in the association between Time 1 ASI and Time 2 DERS (∆Wald’s χ2 =.15, p = .71). No gender differences were found in the autoregressive (i.e., stability) paths across time for DERS (∆Wald’s χ2 =.41, p = .52) or ASI (∆Wald’s χ2 =3.22, p = .07). In order to ensure that the exclusion of those who were invited to complete the follow-up, but did not do so, did not bias our findings, we repeated our analyses with a sample that included the additional 258 participants, accounting for missing data via Full Information Maximum Likelihood in MPlus. Importantly, the effect of interest remained significant, such that the cross-lagged association between ASI and DERS was moderated by gender (∆Wald’s χ2 =8.34, p = .003). Probing the gender differences revealed that Time 1 DERS predicted higher Time 2 ASI in men (β = .36; p < .001), but not women (β = .04; p = .56).

To ensure the relationships were not driven by shared variance with clinical symptomology, cross-lagged analyses were rerun with the addition of a series of covariates that indexed potentially confounding constructs (baseline values for these covariates were used). Specifically, we covaried worry (assessed via the PSWQ; [24]), anxious arousal (assessed via the MASQ-AA; [41]), obsessive-compulsive symptoms (assessed via the OCI-R; [9]), and social anxiety symptoms (assessed via the FNE; [21]). Importantly, the relationship between AS and ER remained significant with the inclusion of each of these covariates, indicating that this relationship was not driven by shared variance with these factors. Finally, all analyses were rerun with the inclusion of the outliers and results remained significant, although model fit worsened. As such, the reported results are from the model in which outliers were removed.

Discussion

Extant literature points to key roles for transdiagnostic factors such as anxiety sensitivity (AS) and emotion regulation (ER) in the development and maintenance of anxiety disorders (Taylor, 1999; [12]). Further, previous work has highlighted the unique role of gender differences in such transdiagnostic factors. Yet, little work to date has explored longitudinal, interactive effects between transdiagnostic factors involved in anxiety pathology and how gender may moderate these relationships. We found partial support for our a priori hypotheses, such that baseline ER predicted future increases in AS, but not the reverse, revealing a transactional, but not reciprocal relationship. Moreover, we found that this relationship held true only for men, but not for women.

In a large sample of adults, we found an association between ER at Time 1 and AS six months later, such that worse baseline ER capacity predicted higher AS at follow-up, above and beyond baseline AS levels. This finding adds to prior work that has established a clear cross-sectional association between AS and ER [1], [19], [40] by clarifying the temporal precedence of this relationship, as baseline ER significantly predicted future AS, whereas the reverse was not true. Although the current study only examined the longitudinal associations between ER and AS, this work has broader implications for the development of internalizing pathology. Specifically, given that AS is a potent risk factor for anxiety pathology, our findings suggest emotion dysregulation plays a critical role in conferring risk for future anxiety pathology. Consequently, our results suggest that targeting emotion regulation skills may have downstream benefits on individuals’ fear of anxiety symptoms (i.e., anxiety sensitivity), which may in turn, reduce risk for the development of anxiety-related pathology. Future work should examine these potential mechanistic pathways as they relate to the development and maintenance of anxiety pathology over time.

Consistent with our hypotheses, we found that the association between ER at Time 1 and AS six months later varied as a function of gender. Specifically, ER was predictive of future increases in AS among men only, not women. One potential interpretation of this finding is that men may be failing to engage in adaptive emotion regulation strategies to the extent needed, which would leave AS under-regulated. This potential explanation is supported by previous findings by McRae and colleagues (2008) who found that men showed decreased activation in prefrontal brain regions related to cognitive regulation of emotion. Alternatively, men may engage in automatic, nonconscious ER strategies, which current measures (i.e., DERS) fail to capture, as suggested by theoretical models of gender differences in ER [26]. It is possible that ER strategies such as suppression and experiential avoidance may be utilized more by men, becoming automatic over time. Together, greater engagement in automatic suppression or avoidance in men may result in failure to downregulate future emotional experiences, leading to higher AS, consistent with work showing that repeated engagement in avoidance and suppression may lead to increased risk for internalizing symptoms [3], [6]. Finally, our findings are consistent with work by [17]) who found that environmental factors accounted for variability in AS only in men. Our findings build upon this work by identifying ER as a key transdiagnostic factor which may confer unique risk for future increases in AS in men. More work is needed to further explicate how distinct mechanisms of ER may serve to protect or exacerbate future anxiety symptoms in ways which are unique to men.

Clinical implications

The current study indicates that ER may play an important role in maintaining or exacerbating future AS. Existing research suggests that ER and AS are important transdiagnostic treatment targets across anxiety disorders [11], [33], [10]. Moreover, individuals with co-occurring elevated levels of ER and AS show the highest levels of anxiety symptoms [19], [40]. However, work examining the relationships between these transdiagnostic factors have yielded contradictory findings (e.g., ER may exacerbate or protect against AS; [40], [1]). One potential reason for these discrepancies could be the cross-sectional nature of the previous studies. Thus, the present study findings significantly extend the literature by delineating the relationship between AS and ER across time.

Importantly, the clinical implications of the present findings appear relevant to men, although they do suggest that ER may not be the most potent treatment target in women. Such implications may be particularly useful, given that gender role conflict and stigma play a key role in the discrepant rates at which men underutilize mental health services [39]. Clinical interventions may seek to intervene in men’s mental health through encouraging men to receive cognitive behavioral therapy addressing ER with the goal of minimizing internalizing disorders. The ongoing COVID-19 pandemic has resulted in a significant rise in cases of anxiety pathology and future clinical work highlighting cognitive ER strategies is of utmost importance.

Strengths and limitations

The current study collected survey responses from multiple time points, allowing for the establishment of temporal precedence of effects, which is an important step in determining causality. At the same time, future studies with more than two time points would allow for better evaluation of AS and ER as potential mediators in relationships with clinical pathology. Greater exploration of mediators could help to inform the etiology of distinct forms of psychopathology. Although the sample was diverse in terms of nation of origin, it was not racially or ethnically diverse. Thus, generalizations about our findings should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, the lack of certain demographic and clinical information (e.g., race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status) makes the generalizability of the present findings less clear. Another limitation was that the present study relies entirely on self-report data. Future work might build on current assessment methods through incorporating multimethod approaches. For instance, future research could induce and experimentally manipulate AS and ER in a laboratory setting using biological tasks such as carbon dioxide inhalation. Another consideration is that there was a large decrease in our sample size from Time 1 to Time 2. However, this was not due to dropout, but rather that we had different (cross-sectional) goals for baseline data, whereas longitudinal methods have significantly greater power and thus a smaller sample size was warranted. Although the distributions of AS and ER scores in the present sample were consistent with other community samples, an important next step in the field is to repeat our analyses in clinical samples to better understand the role of such transdiagnostic factors in the populations that are of most concern. Finally, participants were removed from analyses if they identified as gender nonconforming (n = 6) due to lack of statistical power among this group. An important future line of inquiry should consider how the interplay of AS and ER over time is moderated by gender in a sample that includes gender nonconforming individuals.

Taken together, present study findings highlight transactional associations between AS and ER and the fact that these associations vary as a function of gender. Our findings fill critical gaps in the current understanding of transdiagnostic processes of internalizing pathology. Indeed, we identified a unique translational relationship between ER and future increases in AS, particularly in men. These findings highlight the growing importance of understanding the interplay between transdiagnostic factors of anxiety and how such distinct underlying factors may maintain or exacerbate anxiety symptomatology.
