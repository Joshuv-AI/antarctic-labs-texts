The medical community must not remain silent about healthcare and water access denial in Palestine☆
Authors: Jade Pagkas-Bather, Sabrina Imam, Rachel Bender Ignacio, Asim Farooq, Baddr Shakhsheer, Feroze Sidhwa, Omar Al-Heeti, Thaer Ahmad, Tamathor Abughnaim, John Schneider
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Physicians have a duty to oppose the human suffering and human rights abuses occurring in Gaza, particularly the violations of international humanitarian law (IHL). We especially call attention to the scarcity of potable water in Gaza, which has fueled outbreaks of hepatitis A, polio, and skin infections and has created a public health crisis that is further exacerbated by the destruction of healthcare infrastructure and targeting of healthcare workers. The time is long overdue for physicians to affirm that IHL, including guaranteed access to healthcare and potable water, must be upheld to protect the health and safety of all Palestinians. Moreover, as violence against Palestinians and mass displacement campaigns are escalating in the West Bank, this is a pivotal moment for action to prevent further harms.

It is imperative and long overdue that all physicians object to the blockade and occupation of Gaza which has contributed to the morbidity and mortality of its civilians [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. As physicians, our Hippocratic Oath states “I will prevent disease whenever I can, for prevention is preferable to cure. I will remember that I remain a member of society, with special obligations to my fellow human beings, those sound of mind and body as well as the infirm.” [5] Silence and inaction in the face of human suffering is, indeed, harmful, and the suffering inflicted on Palestinians in Gaza has been well documented over the past 718 days (at the time of this writing) by the victims, the press, and the perpetrators of the violence [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Physicians, as advocates of human rights and dignity, have a moral obligation to call out the violence and dehumanization that is occurring in Gaza [[11], [12], [13]]. In this Letter to the Editor, we argue that the safety and security of human beings is central to our ethical calling as physicians and should not be abdicated during conflicts [12]. Numerous American physicians, including co-authors Drs. Tammy Abughnaim, Thaer Ahmad, and Feroze Sidhwa, have volunteered in Gaza since October 2023 and have witnessed firsthand the direct targeting of civilians and hospital infrastructure—acts explicitly prohibited under International Humanitarian Law (IHL) [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. Of note, during a humanitarian crisis, the earliest documentations of the crises tend to come from news outlets, with the important context that international journalists have been severely restricted from entry into Gaza by Israel during the ongoing crisis [[21], [22], [23]]. We have, where possible, referenced peer-reviewed sources in addition to sources from the news.

Background and context

Prior to October 7, 2023, Gaza suffered under a 16-year land, air, and sea blockade alongside periodic Israeli military assaults that had largely destroyed the water system [24]. Indeed, preventable waterborne illness was the leading cause of death among children in Gaza prior to 2023 [25]. Israel, as an occupying power, has a duty under IHL to provide potable water to Gaza, but it has almost entirely cut-off the supply of this vital resource to the entire population of Gaza since October 7, 2023 [26]. Likewise, Gaza's healthcare system, which had already faced extreme shortages of supplies and medications due to the long-standing blockade, has been systematically destroyed by Israel, with nearly all hospitals targeted by repeated Israeli attacks, and many subjected to direct military sieges, invasions, and forcible evictions of patients and healthcare workers [6,14,15,[27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. In one striking example, Dr. Hussam Abu Safiya, a pediatrician and the director of Kamal Adwan Hospital in northern Gaza, gained worldwide attention as he detailed the merciless siege of the last remaining pediatric hospital in northern Gaza; he was apprehended by the Israeli military in December 2024 and continues to be imprisoned without charges [28,32]. Between January 19 and March 1, 2025, a fragile ceasefire was declared, but this tenuous agreement was repeatedly violated by Israel [33]. Shortly afterward, renewed hostilities were accompanied by a complete blockade of humanitarian aid from entering Gaza, at a time when the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) projects the deaths of 14,000 infants due to starvation by March 2026 [34]. These are just a few of the ongoing human rights violations endured by the Palestinian people.

Water and health impacts

The United Nations General Assembly recognizes “the right to safe and clean drinking water and sanitation as a human right that is essential for the full enjoyment of life and all human rights” and under IHL, attacks on indispensable resources, such as water, are prohibited [35,36]. Access to water, potable or otherwise, is severely limited in Gaza. Prior to October 2023, the precarity of access to potable water forced Palestinians to utilize multiple water sources for their daily needs, such as bathing with water from municipal supplies and drinking from water supplied by tanker trucks, public taps, and municipal taps which were plagued with high rates of fecal contamination [37,38]. Furthermore, groundwater, previously the major source of fresh water in Gaza, was rendered unfit for human consumption due to pollution from sewage and agricultural chemicals well before the escalation of violence in 2023 [39]. Since October 2023, direct attacks by the Israeli military on Gaza's already severely damaged desalination and wastewater treatment facilities, along with closure of the few operational pipelines, have left Palestinians in Gaza to subsist on less than 5 l of water per person per day for all essential needs, including drinking, cooking, cleaning, and toileting; this is in sharp contrast to the average water use of 300 l per person per day in the United States and the recommendation from The Sphere Handbook that 15 l of water per person per day as the minimum acceptable emergency standard [26,[40], [41], [42]]. Moreover, the current severe scarcity of water in Gaza represents 20 % of the pre-conflict levels, and almost none of the water is suitable for human consumption [26,42]. Furthermore, water scarcity in Gaza is perpetuated by ongoing civilian casualties. In one instance, four Palestinian engineers tasked with repairing damaged water infrastructure were killed by Israeli soldiers despite traveling in a clearly-marked vehicle and coordinating their movements with Israeli authorities ahead of time [43].

Furthermore, the unprecedented destruction of civilian infrastructure and those who maintain it will have an enduring environmental impact on the water supply as heavy metals and explosive residue leach into the water sources and debris laden with asbestos and human remains are mobilized [39]. The resulting health consequences for the population of Gaza will persist for generations, even when accounting solely for the effects of water contamination [27,39,44]. These assaults on water quality and availability fly directly in the face of the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals, which aim to ensure good health and well-being in the setting of clean water and sanitation [45].

As Gaza grapples with overwhelming healthcare needs, its capacity to respond has been drastically undermined as Gaza's healthcare infrastructure has been systematically targeted and destroyed over the last 23 months, with almost two-thirds of all structures suffering damage and no fully-operational healthcare facility remaining [46,47]. This deliberate campaign of denying healthcare to an entire population functions as a type of collective punishment and has undoubtedly led to excess mortality not considered in estimates of the widely-quoted Gaza Ministry of Health death toll in this conflict [[48], [49], [50], [51], [52]].

Infectious disease outcomes in the setting of healthcare infrastructure destruction

At the confluence of water denial, displacement, and the elimination of healthcare, one finds unchecked infectious diseases [53]. The decimation of Gaza's entire healthcare infrastructure has had wide-reaching consequences, including a limitation of the ability to confirm the diagnosis of specific infectious diseases through laboratory testing. For example, cases of acute jaundice syndrome, ascribed to hepatitis A, have skyrocketed to over 40,000 cases (as of April 2024), compared to 85 cases reported between October 2022 to July 2023 [54,55]. Given the fecal-oral transmission of hepatitis A, the current prevalence of the disease is undoubtedly attributed to the ingestion of water contaminated by sewage and has been associated with significant mortality, particularly among children [1].

The world also bore witness as polio, a disease transmitted via fecal contamination of water systems, was reported in Gaza for the first time in 25 years [56]. This led the United Nations Relief and Works Agency (UNRWA) and World Health Organization (WHO) to conduct a mass vaccination campaign which claimed to have successfully vaccinated 560,000 children [56]. While this feat is remarkable, the need to urgently vaccinate over half a million children was necessitated by the interruption of routine vaccinations for 11 months at that point, the continued denial of clean water, and the destruction of Gaza's sewage system. Indeed, one must also consider the cruel irony of vaccination to prevent polio-induced paralysis in a conflict that has created the largest cohort of child amputees in the world [2,57].

The denial of water and destruction of sanitation and hygiene infrastructure in Gaza has also led to outbreaks of severe skin conditions, primarily in children [58]. The Israeli blockade of basic necessities, including soap, has made routine bathing and the appropriate care of wounds and skin conditions impossible. Inconsistent water sources, disrupted sanitation systems, displacement of civilians, and the ongoing blockade of medical supplies from entering Gaza, have significantly impeded any semblance of infection control in the Gaza strip, leading to the proliferation of surgical site and other infections in an already under-resourced setting [59]. The few surgeries that are able to be performed are done with little ability to maintain a sterile field or adequately sterilize instruments, and disposable surgical instruments are being re-used [60]. In a particularly gruesome episode, an orthopedic surgeon amputated his own niece's leg at the knee using kitchen tools and sewing thread because transportation to the hospital was considered even more dangerous than operating on his dining table [61]. Moreover, surgical site infections are ubiquitous and carry significant morbidity that is exacerbated by the restriction of medications from entering Gaza, including anesthetics to ease wound debridement and antibiotics to treat infections [1,16,44,58].

Ethical and legal imperatives

What will it take for the medical community to actively oppose this human-made humanitarian catastrophe? What will motivate organizations such as the American Medical Association, which fervently and appropriately opposed Russia's targeting of hospitals in Ukraine, to take a stance against the widespread destruction of the entire healthcare system in Gaza, and to acknowledge what several hundred scholars of genocide as well as Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, UN Special Rapporteur Francesca Albanese, and now, the UN Commission have concluded is, in fact, a genocide? [1,4,[62], [63], [64], [65], [66], [67]] How can we stand by and watch the intentional targeting of hospitals and medical personnel and the denial of healthcare to an entire population? [1,9,10,48] What makes us incapable of recognizing the rights of Palestinians in the same way that we appropriately recognize the rights of Ukrainians and so many other oppressed peoples? [68] The way we identify with victims of atrocities predicts our response to their suffering and perpetuates racial bias in medicine [69,70]. The failure of the healthcare community to speak out against the manmade suffering of the Palestinians is a dereliction of our duty to deploy equity in the face of suffering, evenness in medical care delivery and decision-making, and compassion that knows no geographic, racial/ethnic, or political bounds. Numerous physicians have spoken out about their experiences working in Gaza, and yet, the conflict has continued despite detailed accounts of intentional civilian harm [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20],71,72]. Palestinians in Gaza have even shared their experiences of conflict, death, and injury in real-time, through social media [73]. However, our profession has remained largely silent [74]. We, the authors, routinely care for patients with whom we have no group affiliation, because in our professional work, we recognize that all people are deserving of medical care. Taking a united front against the human suffering and genocide against the Palestinian people is a moral imperative for healthcare workers. Demanding basic human rights, access to water, the right to healthcare, and an immediate cessation of hostilities against civilians and the healthcare workers who are tending to them, should not be controversial. Indeed, it should be the common thread of decency that unites us. Destruction of healthcare infrastructure, targeting of healthcare workers, and access to potable water should never be normalized, regardless of the context.

Call to action

We demand the immediate and unrestricted entry of critical medical and hygiene supplies and adequate food into Gaza, in accordance with the UN's mandate that humanitarian relief be provided based on the principles of humanity, impartiality, neutrality, and independence [75]. Importantly, the recently-instituted Israel- and US-backed Gaza Humanitarian Foundation (GHF) has been widely criticized for failing to meet these principles, instead leading to conditions that heighten scarcity, devastation, and violence for Palestinians [[76], [77], [78], [79]]. The institutionalized harm of the GHF was further reified by the resignation of its executive director, who described the program as “militarized, privatized, politicized” and “not in conformity with neutrality” [80]. We vehemently reject any effort to weaponize humanitarian aid and condition its delivery on political objectives. Access to a potable and sufficient water supply must be restored, and there is absolutely no justification for the targeting of hospitals that are actively treating patients during times of war [81,82]. Furthermore, this is a pivotal moment in which we can still prevent further harms to Palestinians in the West Bank, where similar threats are encroaching [83]. We call on our fellow physicians and all healthcare workers to demand that IHL be upheld for the protection of all Palestinians.
