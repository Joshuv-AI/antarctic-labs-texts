Efficient fine-tuning of vision-language adapters in chemical VLMs for molecular image-text tasks
Authors: Hyukjun Choi, Donghyeon Lee, Juyoung Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVision–language models (VLMs) have recently shown strong potential for multimodal scientific applications, yet their direct application to molecular structure images remains challenging due to the need for precise visual–textual alignment. In this study, we investigate efficient fine-tuning strategies for adapting a large-scale VLM to molecular image captioning tasks. Our approach is built upon the Qwen3.5—9B architecture and focuses on lightweight parameter-efficient adaptation.Specifically, we apply Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to the language-side MLP layers and explore selective tuning of the visual merger module that bridges the vision encoder and the language model. This module compresses visual features and projects them into the language embedding space, making it a critical component for cross-modal alignment. Experimental results on molecular image captioning tasks show that the combined L-MLP + Visual Merger tuning strategy achieves the best performance while maintaining low computational overhead.In addition, we evaluate multiple decoding strategies, including greedy decoding and stochastic sampling methods, to identify the most suitable inference configuration for molecular image captioning. The results demonstrate that selective fine-tuning of the visual–language interface significantly improves caption accuracy while preserving efficiency. These findings highlight the importance of adapting cross-modal projection modules when deploying general-purpose VLMs in specialized scientific domains such as cheminformatics. Introduction

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated impressive capabilities across a wide range of tasks, including those within scientific domains such as biology, medicine, and chemistry. Artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning have emerged as powerful tools in molecular science, enhancing the speed, accuracy, and scalability of molecular discovery and understanding. Among these, models like MolT5 support text-based molecular generation and captioning, helping researchers describe molecules in natural language or generate molecular structures from text [1]. However, despite such progress, most studies applying LLMs to chemistry, including property prediction, retrosynthesis, and molecule captioning, have relied on general-purpose models and remained limited to isolated case studies without extensive domain adaptation [2]. Recent research has begun to incorporate visual modalities, particularly molecular structure images, into multimodal models. For example, ChemVLM combines a 6B image encoder with a 20B chemical language model and applies Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) based fine-tuning to improve molecular captioning performance [3]. However, such large-scale models often require significant computational resources, limiting their practical deployment in resource-constrained environments.

Existing pipelines for converting images to chemical structures, such as Image2SMILES and MolNexTR, focus primarily on format conversion (pixels to structural representations or strings). These methods often fall short in providing human-interpretable, contextual, and explanatory information, such as a molecule's meaning, purpose, or notable features [4, 5]. In contrast, Vision-Language Models (VLMs) combine image and text modalities to generate natural language captions, creating explanations that are directly useful to humans.

To enable meaningful text–structure associations in the chemical domain, a clear understanding of chemical representations is essential. The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) has established a systematic nomenclature based on the structure and substructures of chemical compounds, enabling consistent and interpretable naming across the scientific community [6]. In parallel, the Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) has become a widely adopted standard format in cheminformatics and molecular datasets. SMILES encodes molecular structures as text strings using grammatical rules to represent atoms and bonds, allowing straightforward generation from 2D chemical graphs. Unlike coordinate-based representations, SMILES offers a compact and flexible alternative for representing molecular systems and is extensively used in machine learning applications such as de novo drug design, data augmentation, and structural fingerprinting [7].

Despite these promising advances, fine-tuning vision-language models such as CLIP poses challenges, particularly the risk of catastrophic forgetting, which can degrade zero-shot performance and multimodal alignment [8]. Indiscriminate fine-tuning may cause the model to lose valuable pre-trained knowledge, underscoring the necessity for selective fine-tuning strategies. By updating only a subset of critical layers, these strategies strive to balance effective domain adaptation with preservation of prior knowledge.

Numerous fine-tuning strategies have been proposed in prior research, necessitating careful selection to achieve optimal performance. As pre-trained language models scale up, fine-tuning all parameters becomes prohibitively expensive and practically infeasible [9]. Furthermore, inappropriate selection of fine-tuning strategies may degrade zero-shot performance on unseen tasks [10] and incur unnecessary computational overhead. Moreover, growing environmental concerns regarding the energy demands of AI training, combined with the scarcity of domain-specific chemical datasets, underscore the importance of parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) for the sustainable adaptation of vision-language models (VLMs) to specialized scientific domains [11]. Accordingly, the AI research community increasingly advocates for sustainable AI practices, with an emphasis on both efficiency and scalability [12]. The increasing demand for lightweight and domain-specialized models has highlighted the importance of fine-tuning strategies that are both memory-efficient and performance-effective, enabling cost-efficient deployment and faster inference in constrained environments. In the chemistry domain specifically, compact and specialized models can help overcome the limitations of general-purpose models, such as linguistic inaccuracies and a lack of domain-specific knowledge [13]. Therefore, fine-tuning methods must be not only accurate but also modular, cost-effective, and deployable on limited computational infrastructure.

LayerNorm stabilizes and accelerates training by normalizing intermediate representations within each layer, improving generalization and robustness across tasks [14]. Recent findings show that output LayerNorm parameters change more than any other component during fine-tuning and that tuning only LayerNorm can achieve comparable—or even superior—performance to full fine-tuning or other parameter-efficient strategies [15].

While existing work like ChemVLM has explored LoRA-based fine-tuning on large-scale models, there remains a need to investigate alternative parameter-efficient approaches and their effectiveness on smaller, more deployable models. Specifically, examining how selective fine-tuning of key components such as visual merger and LayerNorm can enhance performance in chemical tasks while maintaining computational efficiency represents an underexplored research direction in the chemistry domain.

To overcome these challenges, we present a fine-tuning approach for VLMs specifically adapted for chemical tasks. By leveraging both molecular structure images and their corresponding textual descriptions, our method aims to enhance the model's ability to interpret, reason, and generate domain-specific chemical knowledge. Specifically, we focus on tasks such as predicting the IUPAC name and generating the SMILES representation from a given molecular structure image. This approach not only expands the capabilities of multimodal LLMs within scientific applications but also offers practical insights into how such models can support chemists in tasks that require integration of visual and textual information. Moreover, generative AI has shown promising potential in science and chemistry education by enabling personalized learning and providing immediate feedback, thereby enhancing students' understanding and problem-solving skills [16–20].

To address these limitations, we present a specialized approach for fine-tuning Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in the chemistry domain. Our key contributions are as follows:

Efficient fine-tuning strategy for chemical VLMs: We propose a domain-specific fine-tuning method that selectively updates key components (e.g., visual merger and LayerNorm) to achieve high performance with reduced computational cost, enabling practical adaptation of VLMs for chemistry-related tasks.

Related work

In this section, building on recent deep learning approaches for chemical structure recognition, we discuss efficient fine-tuning strategies for language models, focusing on strategies for selecting parameters to optimize memory efficiency during the tuning process, as well as methods to enhance generalization performance.

Deep learning approaches for image recognition

Recent developments in general image recognition have shifted toward efficient one-stage Transformer architectures. For instance, the Low-Rank Spatial Channel Transformer (LrSC-T) introduces a novel attention mechanism that combines cosine similarity and bilinear outer product projections. This approach effectively captures higher-order feature interactions by utilizing features directly from a pre-trained Swin Transformer, eliminating the need for separate region-based processing [21]. However, such models primarily optimized for general objects may not be fully suitable for specialized domains like molecular image recognition that require precise structural interpretation.

Another significant advancement is the Cross-Modal Spatial-Channel (CM-SC) attention network, which addresses the limitations of conventional first-order attention by capturing higher-order interactions across different modalities. By utilizing a flexible variant of cross-variance, the CM-SC mechanism effectively models intricate spatial and channel-wise relationships between visual and textual information without increasing the parameter count. This approach has demonstrated a 20.74% reduction in average computation time while maintaining competitive performance on standard datasets like MSCOCO and Flickr30K [22]. Despite these improvements in cross-modal efficiency, such models are primarily optimized for general-purpose captioning and may not fully account for the specific structural constraints and naming conventions inherent in chemical informatics.

Furthermore, the Geometric Information-driven Network (IGINet) enhances image captioning by incorporating spatial and positional relationships among visual objects through a novel attention mechanism called GeoAtt. By utilizing low-rank bilinear pooling, GeoAtt enables more effective multimodal reasoning and selectively harnesses visual features to capture intricate inter-modal interactions. A key contribution of IGINet is the integration of normalized geometric features directly into the attention mechanism, allowing the model to focus on the most relevant image regions [23]. While this approach achieved a state-of-the-art BLEU-4 score of 39.9 on the MSCOCO benchmark, its focus on geometric and positional relationships in general scenes may not directly translate to the specialized structural and hierarchical naming logic required for molecular informatics.

The extraction of chemical formulas from images has long posed significant challenges due to the diverse graphical representations of molecules, shifting the problem from simple classification to complex formula generation [24]. Recent studies in chemical structure recognition have employed various deep learning techniques with notable success. One approach utilizes a Visual geometry group-based segmentation model to accurately detect chemical structure regions within images, achieving a segmentation accuracy of 99.8%. This crucial step isolates relevant chemical diagrams from complex backgrounds [25]. Another prominent method, DECIMER 1.0, combines convolutional neural networks (InceptionV3 or EfficientNet-B3) as image encoders with a Transformer-based encoder-decoder architecture. It outputs molecular representations in the SELFIES format, enhancing chemical validity and achieving an average Tanimoto similarity of 85.38% between predicted and true molecular structures [26].

Expanding on these methods, other research has focused on improving the robustness of models to different image qualities and graphical styles. For example, a study on Atom-Level Optical Chemical Structure Recognition proposes reframing the task as an object detection problem, first identifying individual atoms and bonds, and then reconstructing the full molecular graph [27]. This approach addresses a key limitation of sequence-to-sequence models, which can sometimes produce invalid or chemically implausible outputs, by directly generating a graph representation that is inherently more structured and chemically sound. In a similar vein, Chemception adapts the Inception architecture, a widely used convolutional neural network, for the specific task of recognizing chemical structures [28]. By pre-training the model on a large dataset of chemical images and then fine-tuning it, Chemception achieves high accuracy, particularly in identifying common substructures and functional groups, which are critical for accurate formula generation. More recently, the MolScribe model has been introduced as an image-to-graph model that directly predicts a molecular graph from an image [29]. These diverse approaches reflect the vibrant and ongoing research in applying deep learning to chemical structure recognition.

Synthetic data and data augmentation

When training a Vision-Language model with a small amount of data, overfitting can occur. One of the representative approaches to mitigate this issue is data augmentation. By artificially expanding visual data through various methods such as image rotation, translation, and blur processing, the model's generalization ability can be improved [30]. Several prior studies have reported that these image data augmentation techniques increase visual diversity and enhance image classification performance [31–33].

High-quality, large-scale datasets are a critical component in the development of powerful natural language processing (NLP) models. However, collecting such datasets through manual annotation is often costly, labor-intensive, and constrained by issues such as data scarcity and privacy concerns. Moreover, human-generated data can introduce biases and inconsistencies, potentially undermining the robustness and fairness of the resulting models.

To overcome these limitations, synthetic data generated by large language models (LLMs) has emerged as a promising alternative. LLMs, trained on vast corpora, possess strong language understanding capabilities and can follow instructions with high fidelity, allowing for flexible and targeted data generation. As a result, LLM-generated synthetic data is increasingly being regarded as a reliable resource for training and fine-tuning NLP models [34].

Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of multimodal data augmentation approaches that leverage large language models for generating synthetic training data across image, text, and speech modalities. This multi-step process is designed to significantly enhance machine learning models by diversifying training datasets, particularly in scenarios where data scarcity is a limiting factor [35]. The integration of LLMs in data augmentation has shown promising results in healthcare applications, including patient-trial matching and privacy-preserving synthetic data generation, though challenges remain in balancing data quality with privacy constraints [36].

Vision language model fine tuning strategies

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that introduces trainable low-rank matrices into the weights of pre-trained models while keeping the original weights frozen. Instead of updating full weight matrices, LoRA decomposes the weight update into two smaller matrices, significantly reducing trainable parameters and memory usage. This approach enables efficient adaptation without compromising performance and incurs no additional inference latency, making it particularly suitable for fine-tuning large-scale models such as GPT-3 [37].

Recent studies on visual Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) have focused on reducing the number of trainable parameters by freezing most of the pre-trained model while adapting it to downstream tasks. To address the limitations of heuristic-based parameter allocation, a sensitivity-aware approach, SPT (Sensitivity-aware visual Parameter-efficient fine-Tuning), was introduced. SPT identifies task-specific sensitive parameters in a data-driven manner and selectively applies structured tuning methods such as LoRA or Adapters when sensitivity exceeds a predefined threshold. Experimental results demonstrate that SPT significantly improves the performance of existing PEFT techniques, achieving state-of-the-art results on benchmarks like FGVC and VTAB-1 k [38].

CLIPFit is another parameter-efficient tuning method designed for vision-language models. It updates only the bias terms in the projection layers of the text encoder and the LayerNorm components of the image encoder in CLIP. Unlike prompt tuning or adapter-based methods, CLIPFit introduces no external parameters. Experiments conducted on 11 benchmark datasets confirm its effectiveness, supported by analyses of internal parameter changes and representation shifts [39].

Finally, partial fine-tuning strategies focusing on the later layers of Vision Transformers (ViTs) have shown superior performance compared to tuning earlier layers. Specifically, fine-tuning the MLP and attention modules in the later stages was more effective than modifying those in the initial layers. These observations suggest that the depth of layer selection plays a critical role in the efficiency and effectiveness of fine-tuning [40]. Evaluation

To evaluate the quality of the generated molecular captions, we employ both traditional automatic metrics and LLM-based evaluation methods. Among the commonly used automatic metrics for text generation tasks, BLEU and ROUGE measure the similarity between generated text and reference descriptions by comparing n-gram overlaps. BLEU evaluates the precision of n-gram matches between generated outputs and reference texts, while ROUGE focuses on recall-oriented overlap and is widely used in summarization and captioning tasks.

In addition to these traditional metrics, recent studies have increasingly explored the use of large language models (LLMs) as evaluators of generated outputs. In this paradigm, often referred to as LLM-as-judge, powerful LLMs are used to assess the quality of generated text by providing scores or qualitative judgments. For example, G-Eval employs GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 with chain-of-thought prompting to evaluate outputs in tasks such as summarization and dialogue generation [41]. Similarly, GEMBA introduces a GPT-based evaluation framework for machine translation quality assessment using GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 [41].

LLM-based evaluation approaches have also begun to appear in scientific and technical domains. Recent work proposes combining human feedback with LLM-generated judgments through a control-variate framework, enabling efficient and scalable evaluation while maintaining consistency with human ratings [42]. Motivated by these developments, our study adopts an LLM-based evaluation framework alongside traditional metrics to comprehensively assess the quality of generated captions in the molecular image captioning task.

Model architecture

Our model is built upon the Qwen3.5—9B architecture. The framework consists of a vision encoder, a vision–language merger, and a large language model (LLM). The vision encoder extracts visual representations from the input molecular structure images, while the vision–language merger projects the visual features into the hidden embedding space of the language model. Specifically, the merger compresses spatial visual features in a 2 × 2 grouping and maps them into visual tokens aligned with the LLM’s hidden dimension through a two-layer MLP. These visual tokens are then processed by the language model to generate textual descriptions of the molecular structures [43]. Dataset preparation and annotation

We constructed a specialized dataset of organic compounds sourced from the PubChem database, specifically targeting the Literature, Safety and Hazards, and Use and Manufacturing categories to ensure a diverse representation of chemical properties. A total of 8822 unique compounds were collected. To ensure dataset integrity, we implemented a Python-based verification pipeline to confirm that there were no duplicate or missing entries across the train, validation, and test splits. As a secondary validation step, each compound’s IUPAC name was converted into a SMILES representation, and we verified that no duplicate SMILES strings existed across the dataset. Additionally, molecular validity was checked using RDKit to ensure that all compounds corresponded to chemically valid structures.

Decoding strategies

To investigate the effect of decoding strategies on molecular image captioning, we evaluate several token generation methods during inference using the best-performing fine-tuned model. Specifically, we compare deterministic and stochastic decoding approaches to identify which strategy is most suitable for generating accurate textual descriptions of molecular structures.

Greedy search is a deterministic decoding method that selects the token with the highest probability at each generation step. This approach produces consistent outputs because it always chooses the most likely token according to the model’s predicted distribution [44].

In contrast, stochastic decoding methods introduce randomness by sampling tokens from the predicted probability distribution. Temperature sampling controls the sharpness of this distribution using a temperature parameter τ. Lower temperature values make the distribution more concentrated on high-probability tokens, leading to more deterministic outputs, while higher values increase randomness and diversity in the generated text [44].

In addition, we employ min-p sampling, a dynamic token filtering method that adjusts the candidate token set based on the probability of the most likely token. Specifically, tokens whose probability is lower than a threshold determined by the base probability multiplied by the probability of the most probable token are filtered out before sampling. This strategy allows the model to maintain generation diversity while preventing extremely low-probability tokens from being selected [45].

Based on these methods, we evaluate three decoding configurations: (1) greedy search, (2) temperature sampling with (τ = 0.5) and min_p = 0.1, and (3) temperature sampling with (τ = 0.1) and min_p = 0.05. Through this comparison, we aim to identify the decoding strategy that produces the most accurate descriptions for the molecular image captioning task.

In this architecture, LayerNorm plays a crucial role beyond simple activation stabilization; it enhances the expressivity of attention layers by projecting inputs to a balanced hyperplane, ensuring that attention queries treat keys more equally and preventing certain features from being ignored [44]. Recent research has demonstrated that tuning only the LayerNorm modules in multimodal LLMs can effectively reduce cross-layer similarity, fostering richer representations and often outperforming full-model fine-tuning on downstream tasks [45].

Furthermore, we focus on the MLP layers within the vision encoder, which are critical for progressively projecting high-dimensional visual features into the shared embedding space. This process facilitates the precise alignment between the visual representations of molecular structures and their corresponding linguistic descriptors [47].

By building upon these insights—specifically the strategic importance of LayerNorm and vision-language alignment components—we explore efficient fine-tuning strategies for adapting Qwen3.5—9B to the chemical domain. Our approach aims to minimize computational overhead while maintaining the model’s robust STEM reasoning capabilities, ensuring high performance in both molecular image recognition and detailed IUPAC nomenclature tasks.

Fine-tuning configurations

We evaluate the performance of the model under the following fine-tuning configurations, all based on the Qwen3.5—9B architecture:

L-MLP Only: LoRA is applied solely to the Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) modules in the language model. All other parameters are frozen.

L-MLP Only: LoRA is applied solely to the Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) modules in the language model. All other parameters are frozen.

L-MLP + V-MLP: In addition to applying LoRA to the language MLP modules, we fine-tune the later-stage MLP layers of the vision encoder. The rest of the model remains frozen.

L-MLP + V-MLP: In addition to applying LoRA to the language MLP modules, we fine-tune the later-stage MLP layers of the vision encoder. The rest of the model remains frozen.

L-MLP + VM: LoRA is applied to the language MLP modules, and we simultaneously fine-tune the visual merger (the projection module responsible for bridging the vision encoder and the language model). All other layers are kept frozen.

Training argument

We fine-tuned the Qwen3.5—9B model, which demonstrates strong multimodal capabilities and serves as a robust alternative to self-attention-only architectures [46]. The fine-tuning was performed using a lightweight training configuration optimized for environments with limited GPU memory, specifically on an NVIDIA RTX5090 with 32 GB of VRAM. The LoRA technique was applied selectively to the language model’s MLP (Multi-Layer Perceptron) modules, while other layers were fine-tuned using standard parameter updates where appropriate.

For LoRA, we set the rank parameter to 32, with a scaling factor (LoRA alpha) of 64, and applied a dropout rate of 0.1 to prevent overfitting during adaptation. The base learning rate was set to 5e-5 and scheduled to follow a cosine decay policy, which smoothly reduces the learning rate over time and has been shown to enhance training stability and final performance [47, 48]. We used a batch size of 4 per device with gradient accumulation over 8 steps, yielding an effective batch size of 32, and trained the model for a total of 2 epochs. Evaluation framework with GPT-based prompting

To evaluate the performance of our fine-tuned vision–language model, we employed a hybrid evaluation strategy combining traditional automatic metrics and an LLM-based assessment using OpenAI’s GPT-4o-mini. Conventional text generation metrics, including BLEU and ROUGE, were used to measure lexical overlap between the generated outputs and the reference answers.

Because our dataset contains two distinct task types, we adopted different evaluation strategies depending on the task characteristics. For Task 1 (Simple IUPAC Naming), which requires the generation of an exact chemical name, we applied a Python-based string exact match evaluation to determine correctness. For Task 2 (Detailed Nomenclature Logic), which involves explanatory descriptions of IUPAC naming principles, we employed BLEU-1 F1 and ROUGE-1 F1 metrics to capture lexical similarity between the generated explanations and the reference annotations.

In addition to these automated metrics, we introduced an LLM-based evaluation to assess semantic correctness. GPT-4o-mini was prompted to compare each generated answer with the corresponding ground truth and determine whether their meanings were equivalent. Based on this judgment, we assigned a binary accuracy score: 1 for semantically correct outputs and 0 for incorrect ones. The final accuracy was computed as the proportion of correct outputs across the evaluation set. To ensure reliability, the GPT-based evaluations were further manually verified by the researchers.

Furthermore, incorrect model outputs were qualitatively analyzed by the researchers, and common error patterns were categorized to better understand the limitations of the model and the types of mistakes occurring in molecular image interpretation.

This hybrid evaluation framework allows traditional n-gram-based metrics to capture surface-level similarity, while the LLM-based judge provides an additional semantic verification layer. By combining these approaches, we aim to obtain a more reliable and context-aware assessment of model performance for the molecular image captioning task. Notably, the baseline zero-shot performance of the pre-trained model for Task 1 (Simple IUPAC Naming) was merely 15.88%. Applying LoRA solely to the language-side MLPs (L-MLP Only) yielded an accuracy of 68.89%, representing a massive absolute increase of over 50 percentage points. Among the evaluated strategies, L-MLP + VM (Visual Merger) achieved the highest performance for Task 1, reaching 70.42%. This demonstrates that selectively tuning the visual merger—the critical module responsible for projecting vision outputs into the language space—is highly effective for precise cross-modal alignment.

Furthermore, this study evaluated the model's ability to explain detailed IUPAC naming principles (Task 2). In terms of lexical overlap, the L-MLP + VM strategy consistently outperformed the others, recording the highest scores in both ROUGE-1 F1 and BLEU-1 F1 metrics. Surface-level text matching, an LLM-based evaluator was employed to measure the semantic equivalence between the predicted explanations and the ground truth. Interestingly, this semantic evaluation revealed that L-MLP Only, L-MLP + VM, and L-MLP + L-LN all achieved an identical score of 57.14%. While the L-MLP + L-LN strategy (tuning all language LayerNorms) slightly dropped to 68.50% in Task 1, it maintained baseline semantic reasoning capabilities in Task 2, indicating that additional LayerNorm tuning provided no substantial advantage for this specific domain adaptation.

Conversely, the L-MLP + V-LMLP strategy—which fine-tuned the later-stage MLPs of the vision encoder—exhibited a severe performance drop, achieving an accuracy of only 29.07% in Task 1. This significant degradation provides a critical insight: modifying the parameters of a well-trained vision encoder can disrupt its established, robust visual representations. Rather than adapting to chemical structures, altering the vision encoder hinders the learning process for highly fine-grained visual recognition tasks.

To better understand the remaining limitations, a qualitative error analysis was conducted on the predictions of the best-performing L-MLP + VM configuration. The distribution of the identified error categories is summarized in Table 6. The analysis revealed that locant (positional) errors were the most frequent, accounting for 56.3% of the incorrect outputs. This was followed by partial naming errors, which constituted 22.9%. These findings suggest that while the model successfully captures the main structural framework and functional groups of the molecules, precisely mapping the positional numbers of these components remains a primary challenge.

Conclusion

This study proposes an efficient fine-tuning strategy for vision-language models that significantly reduces memory usage and training time, enabling faster model development and deployment with lower infrastructure requirements. The research contributes to the field of multimodal learning in chemical domains by demonstrating that effective fine-tuning can be achieved even under limited computing resources. By developing a selective fine-tuning approach tailored to the fine-grained nature of chemical image recognition, the model achieved improved performance with significantly fewer trainable parameters. This makes the methodology accessible to researchers working with constrained computational budgets.

In addition, the integration of generative AI to create synthetic datasets provides a practical advancement for automating both data preparation and quality assurance. This approach helps address data scarcity in specialized domains such as chemistry and pharmaceuticals and offers a scalable framework for dataset augmentation that may be extended to various scientific fields.

Unlike many previous studies that focus primarily on CLIP-based architectures, this research explores fine-tuning strategies for the Qwen3.5—9B model. This demonstrates that efficient fine-tuning strategies can be successfully adapted to smaller and more accessible models, broadening the range of applicable architectures. Furthermore, a specialized evaluation method is introduced to overcome the limitations of conventional metrics such as BLEU when applied to chemical nomenclature tasks, offering a more accurate assessment framework for chemical structure recognition and description.

By extending vision-language learning to the domain of chemical structure images, this study expands the application scope of multimodal models beyond general-purpose image tasks. The proposed methods have the potential to support a wide range of scientific applications, particularly in chemistry and related fields.

The effectiveness of this approach, combined with its lower computational requirements, has important implications for democratizing access to advanced AI tools in scientific research. The use of synthetic data generation and augmentation is especially promising for automating data preparation in domains where such tasks are traditionally labor-intensive and require specialized expertise.

While our results are promising, the model's generalization performance can still be improved. To further enhance robustness, increasing the diversity and size of the dataset remains essential. A larger and more varied dataset would enable the model to learn more comprehensive chemical knowledge, improving its ability to handle unseen molecular structures and generate more accurate predictions.

Future work will focus on improving the scalability and robustness of the proposed framework, exploring further domain adaptation strategies, and expanding the model's capabilities to address more complex chemical tasks. The integration of additional data modalities and enhanced fine-tuning techniques could lead to even more powerful tools for advancing scientific research in chemistry and beyond.
