Electrospun PVA/PEO/β-CD nanofiber composites incorporating chemically and biologically synthesized silver nanoparticles for enhanced antimicrobial activity
Authors: Hany M. Abdelmoneim, Tarek H. Taha, Mohamed S. Elnouby, M. A. Abu-Saied, Abdulrahman Mohammed Alhudhaibi, Shaikha A. Albatli, Marwa Yousry A. Mohamed, Abdullah A. Faqihi, Feras M. Afifi, Hamdi Bendif, Sulaiman A. Alsalamah, Hala Mohamed AbuShady
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of functional nanomaterials with enhanced antimicrobial efficacy is critical for addressing the growing challenge of antimicrobial resistance. In this study, electrospun antimicrobial nanofiber composites were developed by integrating silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) into a polyvinyl alcohol/polyethylene oxide/β-cyclodextrin (PVA/PEO/β-CD) matrix. A comparative evaluation was conducted between chemically synthesized AgNPs (chem-AgNPs) and biologically synthesized AgNPs (bio-AgNPs). Characterization of chem-AgNPs confirmed a spherical morphology with an average diameter of 35.06 ± 8.41 nm and a crystalline structure. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of chem-AgNPs was 2000 µg/mL for most tested pathogens, while Vibrio cholerae exhibited higher susceptibility at 1000 µg/mL. Furthermore, both AgNP types were successfully embedded into the polymer matrix, yielding uniform, bead-free nanofibrous mats with preserved structural integrity. Importantly, disc diffusion assays revealed that AgNPs-loaded nanofibers exhibited significantly enhanced antimicrobial efficiency compared with the corresponding free nanoparticles, confirming the effectiveness of the nanofibrous delivery platform. Notably, bio-AgNPs-functionalized nanofibers consistently produced larger inhibition zones than chem-AgNPs-based nanofibers against all tested clinically relevant pathogens. This enhanced performance is attributed to the distinct surface chemistry and bioactive capping agents associated with bio-AgNPs, which promote improved nanoparticle dispersion and sustained antimicrobial action within the nanofibrous matrix. Overall, these findings demonstrate that electrospun PVA/PEO/β-CD nanofibers serve as an efficient antimicrobial delivery system, and highlight the clear advantage of bio-AgNPs-functionalized nanofibers for advanced antimicrobial and biomedical applications, particularly against multidrug-resistant pathogens.

Introduction

Nanotechnology is the science of manipulating matter on a very small scale, with a size in the range of 1–100 nm [1, 2]. It is one of the most vital research fields in materials science, focusing on the synthesis and applications of nanoparticles [3]. There has been considerable interest in composite nanomaterials due to their tunable physicochemical properties and broad technological relevance [3, 4]. Consequently, recent studies have demonstrated that polymeric nanofibers incorporating antibiotics, iodine, or inorganic antimicrobial components can significantly enhance localized infection control, particularly in wound and burn management, through sustained and site-specific release mechanisms [5–8]. When noble metal nanoparticles are dispersed in a polymer matrix, the resulting composites may display novel physical and chemical properties of great scientific and technological importance [9]. These nanoparticles are significant due to their potential applications in catalysis, photonics, biomedicine, antimicrobial activity, and optics [10]. Thus, a key determinant of their overall properties is their size, shape, and chemical composition [11]. Their high surface-area-to-volume ratio confers unique characteristics and enhances their catalytic, magnetic, mechanical, and optical properties, thereby expanding their potential biomedical applications [12, 13].

Among all the noble metals, silver has been used as an antimicrobial agent since ancient times [3]. It has gained substantial attention due to its medicinal, clinical, and culinary properties, with recently demonstrated effectiveness as an anticancer agent [14]. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are widely recognized in both clinical and scientific contexts for their potent, broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity [15]. This efficacy is largely attributed to the sustained release of silver ions (Ag+), which act through multiple complementary mechanisms to disrupt microbial function [15, 16]. Specifically, Ag+ disrupt membrane integrity, induce oxidative stress, and inhibit essential enzymatic pathways, collectively leading to microbial cell death [16–18]. The combined action of these mechanisms makes AgNPs highly effective against a wide range of microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, and viruses [15, 17]. AgNPs can be synthesized using various chemical, physical, and biological methods [19]. Thus, AgNPs have attracted sustained interest due to their broad biomedical applicability, including antimicrobial, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer functions [20–24].

While AgNPs exhibit constrained utility in isolation, their incorporation into a polymer matrix significantly broadens their potential applications [25]. The synergistic integration of nanofiber and nanoparticle technologies maximizes their structural characteristics, resulting in a composite material highly suitable for a diverse range of applications [26]. Polymeric nanofiber composites containing AgNPs are known to demonstrate potent antimicrobial efficacy against various pathogens [27]. This efficacy is enabled by the intrinsic features of nanofibers, such as their nanometric diameter, adjustable porosity, and high surface-area-to-volume ratio, which facilitate their use in numerous potential fields [26]. Moreover, nanofibers possess the capability to incorporate nanoparticles, either throughout their bulk or on their exterior surface, to yield nanofibrous composite materials [28]. Among fabrication methods, electrospinning remains one of the most widely adopted approaches for producing polymeric nanofibers [29].

Recent studies increasingly emphasize composite nanofibers produced by blending synthetic and natural polymers, highlighting the importance of nanofiber composition and carrier design in governing antimicrobial efficacy, stability, biocompatibility, and biodegradability [30–32]. A wide range of synthetic polymers, including polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), polyethylene oxide (PEO), polylactic acid (PLA), polycaprolactone (PCL) polylactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA), and other vinyl-based polymers, are frequently used in the preparation of electrospun nanofibers. Alongside these materials, naturally derived polymers such as chitosan, collagen, cellulose, starch, alginate, and hyaluronic acid have also attracted considerable attention due to their inherent biocompatibility and biofunctional properties [31, 33, 34]. Furthermore, multicomponent nanofibrous systems obtained by blending different polymer solutions have emerged as an effective strategy to tailor structural and functional properties, and such composites can be readily produced using single-nozzle electrospinning techniques [35–37]. PVA and PEO are hydrophilic polymers favored for their excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, mechanical robustness, and low toxicity. Their high molecular weight and solution viscosity also make them amenable to electrospinning processes [38]. PVA is a synthetic, water-soluble, and odorless polymer containing multiple hydroxyl groups and is well known for its excellent film-forming ability, hydrophilicity, structural stability, mechanical strength, controlled biodegradability, non-toxicity, and low cost [25, 39–41]. PEO is a hydrophilic, water-soluble, and biocompatible polymer widely employed in electrospun biomedical nanofibers due to its excellent electrospinnability, low toxicity, and ability to enhance fiber uniformity and flexibility [42, 43]. When blended with polymers such as PVA, PEO improves solution processability, promotes bead-free nanofiber formation, modulates porosity and mechanical performance, and facilitates homogeneous distribution of functional components within the composite matrix [44, 45].

Although not polymers themselves, beta-cyclodextrins (β-CDs) offer attractive properties when incorporated into electrospun fibers. These cyclic oligosaccharides can form inclusion complexes with a broad spectrum of molecules, thereby enhancing guest solubility and stability, and enabling controlled release of incorporated agents [46]. The incorporation of β-CD has been identified as a key feature that helps stabilize the nanoparticles and modulate the release of silver for controlled antimicrobial performance [47]. Accordingly, the addition of β-CD into polymer nanofibers represents an effective strategy through which the resulting composites acquire novel characteristics that can potentially expand the application scope of nanofibrous systems [9]. Given these complementary properties, the PVA/PEO/β-CD ternary system provides a versatile and transient nanofibrous support matrix well suited for the sustained local release of synthesized AgNPs, enabling effective nanoparticle dispersion and controlled antimicrobial activity. Recent studies have demonstrated that electrospun biomedical nanofibers provide an effective antimicrobial platform due to their high surface area, ability to enable controlled agent delivery, and favorable biocompatibility, which supports the material design strategy adopted in the current work [48, 49]. Moreover, electrospun nanofibers can be engineered into multifunctional platforms with enhanced interfacial activity, extending their utility beyond biomedical and antimicrobial applications to smart energy and environmental systems [50–52]. Among the various techniques for fabricating such nanofibers [53], electrospinning is a widely used method that employs high voltages to create an interconnected mesh of fine fibers, with diameters spanning the nano- to micro-scale [54]. Despite extensive research on AgNPs and antimicrobial electrospun nanofibers, studies that directly compare chemically synthesized AgNPs (chem-AgNPs) and biologically synthesized AgNPs (bio-AgNPs) within the same electrospun polymeric nanofiber platform under identical fabrication and testing conditions remain limited. Filling this knowledge gap is essential for the rational design of next-generation antimicrobial platforms with optimized functionality. While AgNPs-based materials are clinically used, their efficacy can be limited by nanoparticle aggregation, rapid or uncontrolled Ag+ release, and concerns regarding cytotoxicity associated with rapid burst-release systems [57–59]. In this context, the present PVA/PEO/β-CD/AgNPs nanofibrous mat offers distinct advantages by providing a biodegradable and transient carrier that enables sustained and localized AgNPs and/or Ag+ release, potentially prolonging antimicrobial activity while minimizing excessive silver exposure. Moreover, the incorporation of bio-AgNPs, together with the hosting and diffusion-modulating capability of β-CD, is expected to enhance nanoparticle stability and bioavailability within the polymer matrix [60, 61], supporting controlled antimicrobial performance without long-term material persistence. This combination offers a targeted, biocompatible, and clearance-oriented advancement over existing AgNPs delivery systems.

In this study, electrospun polymeric PVA/PEO/β-CD nanofiber composites containing AgNPs with considerable antimicrobial activity were developed for potential antimicrobial applications. To achieve this, AgNPs were synthesized using a chemical technique and characterized using a series of analytical methods. Their antimicrobial activities were compared to bio-AgNPs synthesized and characterized in our previous study [62]. Both chem-AgNPs and bio-AgNPs were incorporated into polymeric PVA/PEO/β-CD nanofibers via electrospinning. The resulting composite nanofibers were thoroughly characterized, and their antimicrobial efficacy was assessed against clinically relevant microbial pathogens.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of AgNPs

The chemical synthesis of AgNPs was performed using glucose as a reducing agent and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) as a stabilizing agent. An aqueous solution of AgNO3 (5 mM, 500 mL) was supplemented with glucose (1.0 g) and SDS (0.5 g). The role of SDS was to inhibit the agglomeration and precipitation of the forming nanoparticles [63]. This mixture was stirred continuously at a constant temperature of 50 °C to promote the reaction. Subsequently, 1.0 mL of 0.2 M NaOH was added to the mixture. The reaction was allowed to proceed under these conditions for 30 min. The formation of AgNPs was indicated by the development of a pale yellow to brown coloration, a characteristic feature of plasmonic resonance in AgNPs [63–65]. The resulting AgNPs were purified by centrifugation at 8500 xg for 20 min, followed by drying and storing for subsequent use. For comparative purposes, biologically synthesized AgNPs from a previous study were also utilized [62].

Characterization of the prepared AgNPs

The chem-AgNPs were initially confirmed by visual observation of the characteristic color change to yellow–brown. Further characterization was conducted using ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. The characterization data for the biosynthesized AgNPs (bio-AgNPs) have been reported previously [62].

Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectral analysis

The reduction of AgNO3 to form the colloidal AgNPs solution was monitored by UV–vis spectroscopy. The absorbance spectrum of the chem-AgNPs colloidal solution was scanned from 300 to 800 nm at a 0.5 nm resolution using a Double Beam Spectrophotometer 6800 (JENWAY, Japan) [66].

TEM analysis

The morphology and size of the chem-AgNPs were examined using a high-resolution TEM (HRTEM, JEOL-2010) operated at 200 kV. For sample preparation, a droplet of the nanoparticle suspension was deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid and dried under a lamp. Imaging was performed at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV [67]. The average particle diameter and size distribution were determined by analysis of the TEM micrographs with ImageJ software, and the results are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

FTIR spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy was used to identify the functional groups capping the chem-AgNPs. For analysis, the AgNPs sample was uniformly dispersed in dry KBr powder at a 2% (w/w) concentration and compressed into a pellet. The spectrum was acquired in the 400–4000 cm−1 range with a 4 cm−1 resolution using a Spectrum One Spectrophotometer (Perkin Elmer, USA) [68].

Determination of the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of the synthesized AgNPs

The MIC is defined as the lowest concentration of an antimicrobial agent required to prevent the visible proliferation of a microorganism. The capacity of varying concentrations of the chem-AgNPs to suppress the growth of the selected microbial pathogens was assessed. The microbial pathogens used in this test included Staphylococcus aureus ATCC6538, Bacillus cereus ATCC6633, Escherichia coli NCTC10418, Vibrio cholera ATCC700, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC9027, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC13883, and Candida albicans ATCC700. The broth microdilution method was employed using resazurin as a metabolic indicator [69]. This was achieved by monitoring the color change of the resazurin indicator, which shifts from a blue/non-fluorescent state to a pink/highly fluorescent state, a transformation that signifies microbial metabolic activity and, consequently, growth. The procedure began by dispensing 100 µL of the chem-AgNPs stock solution (2000 µg/mL) into the first column of a 96-well microtiter plate, while 50 µL of LB broth was added to the remaining columns. A two-fold serial dilution was then performed horizontally across the plate; 50 µL from the first well was transferred and mixed with the second well, and this process was continued up to the tenth column. Following the dilution series, 50 µL of a 0.5 McFarland standard suspension of each microbial strain was added to all wells within the same row, up to the tenth column, which was then followed by the addition of 100 µL of LB broth. Column 11 wells, serving as the positive control (medium with bacterial inoculum), were composed of 100 µL of the 0.5 McFarland standard of each microbial strain plus 100 µL of LB broth. Column 12 wells, serving as the negative control (only medium), were filled with 200 µL of LB broth. Consequently, the highest concentration of AgNPs was present in column 1, and the lowest concentration in column 10. The plate was incubated at 30 °C for 24 h, after which 40 µL of resazurin (0.015%) was added to every well. All wells were subsequently checked for color changes after 2 h of incubation at 30 °C. The MIC was recorded as the lowest concentration of AgNPs where the resazurin maintained its original blue color, indicating the absence of microbial growth [70]

Synthesis of polymeric PVA/PEO/β-CD/bio- and chem-AgNPs composite nanofibers

Preparation of polymeric electrospinning solutions

Prior to use, all glassware was rinsed with distilled water. An 8.0% (w/v) PVA solution was prepared by dissolving PVA in distilled water at 80 °C under constant stirring for 5 h. Then, a 0.05 wt% PEO solution was prepared in distilled water at 80 °C. To the PEO solution, 10.0 wt% (relative to the polymer weight) of β-CD was added and stirred for 1 h. The PVA and PEO/β-CD solutions were then combined. Finally, either the newly synthesized chem-AgNPs or the bio-AgNPs from our previous study [62] were incorporated separately into the polymer blend at a concentration of 1.0 wt%, by continuous stirring for 15 min [38, 71]. A control nanofiber mat without AgNPs was also prepared.

Electrospinning

Nanofibers were produced using a Nanospinner 24-XP (Inovenso) device, which employs a bottom-up spinning approach. The experimental setup was adapted from Ding et al. [72] with modifications. The polymer solution was loaded into a 10 mL syringe equipped with a 21-gauge stainless steel needle. A syringe pump delivered the solution at a constant flow rate of 0.5 mLh−1, which was optimized to match the rate of fiber solidification and prevent bead formation [73]. An applied voltage of 18 kV was used to provide a sufficient electric field for stable jet initiation [74], and a tip-to-collector distance of 15 cm was maintained to allow adequate solvent evaporation prior to fiber deposition [75]. The cylindrical collector rotated at 1200 rpm to promote fiber alignment and mat uniformity [76]. The electrospun nanofibrous mats were collected on an aluminum foil, air-dried at room temperature, and stored for further analysis. All electrospinning was conducted under ambient conditions.

Characterization of composite nanofibers

The morphological analysis of the electrospun nanofibrous mat materials was carried out using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Hitachi Su 66,000, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. The average diameter of nanofibers was determined by measuring randomly selected fibers from each sample. FTIR spectra were recorded using an IRPRESTIGE-21 FTIR spectrophotometer with a resolution of 4 cm−1 in the wave number range between 400 and 4000 cm−1. The samples were prepared in the form of nanofiber film.

Antimicrobial activity test of composite nanofibers

The antimicrobial activity of the PVA/PEO/β-CD nanofibers containing chem-AgNPs or bio-AgNPs was evaluated against seven microbial pathogens using a disc diffusion assay (a modified Kirby-Bauer method). The tested strains were the same as those listed in Sect. 2.6. Each strain was cultured overnight in LB broth at 30 °C with shaking (200 rpm). The microbial lawns were prepared by spreading 100 µL of a 0.5 McFarland standard suspension onto LB agar plates. The composite nanofibers were cut into 10 mm diameter discs, sterilized under UV light for 20 min, and aseptically placed onto the inoculated agar surfaces. The plates were refrigerated at 4 °C for one hour to allow for diffusion and then incubated at 30 °C for 24 h. A nanofiber disc without AgNPs served as the negative control. Antimicrobial activity was quantified by measuring the diameter of the clear inhibition zone around each disc. All tests were performed in triplicate, and inhibition zone diameters are reported as mean ± SD [77].

Statistical analysis

Inhibition zone diameters obtained from the disc diffusion assays were measured in triplicate and expressed as mean ± SD. To compare the antimicrobial activities of the chem-AgNPs and bio-AgNPs-containing nanofibrous composites for each microbial strain, pairwise statistical comparisons were performed using Welch’s two-sample t-test (unequal variances assumed). A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted to determine statistical significance. All statistical analyses were carried out using standard statistical software.

To characterize the chem-AgNPs, several analytical techniques were employed, including UV–vis spectroscopy, TEM, and FTIR [78, 79]. The initial confirmation of nanoparticle formation was a visual color change in the reaction mixture from colorless to brown (Fig. 2), a phenomenon attributed to surface plasmon resonance (SPR) of metallic nanoparticles in the nanoscale size range [80]. FTIR analysis

FTIR analysis was performed to identify functional groups involved in nanoparticle stabilization (Fig. 7). The spectrum of chem-AgNPs exhibited a sharp absorption band at 3321.47 cm−1 attributed to N–H/O–H stretching vibrations, along with bands at 2133.48 cm−1 and 1636.56 cm−1 corresponding to C≡C/C≡N stretching and C = O/C = C vibrations, respectively [87–90]. Notably, the close correspondence between the FTIR spectra of chem-AgNPs and previously reported bio-AgNPs [62] indicates a convergence in the surface chemistry of the nanoparticles despite the different synthesis routes [91]. This spectral similarity likely arises from shared functional groups, primarily hydroxyl, carboxyl/carboxylate, and amine/amide moieties that involved in AgNPs formation, capping, and stabilization in both systems [92]. In the chemical synthesis, glucose provides abundant –OH groups analogous to those present in biogenic extracts, while SDS contributes overlapping aliphatic C–H vibrations [93, 94]. A notable observation was the appearance of blue wells for P. aeruginosa at sub-inhibitory chem-AgNPs concentrations, indicating suppressed metabolic activity rather than complete growth inhibition. As resazurin reduction reflects cellular redox activity, this response likely arises from transient interference with respiratory enzymes and electron transport pathways without inducing cell death [96, 97]. This behavior is consistent with the metabolic adaptability and intrinsic resistance mechanisms of P. aeruginosa, enabling survival under sublethal antimicrobial stress [98, 99]. This effect may also reflect a hormetic response to low-dose AgNPs exposure, where adaptive metabolic modulation occurs without irreversible damage [100]. Although MIC determination was unaffected, this finding highlights the complex, non-linear interactions between AgNPs and resilient pathogens, warranting further investigation.

Comparative analysis of chem-AgNPs and bio-AgNPs

A comparative analysis of the chem-AgNPs and previously reported bio-AgNPs [62] (Table 2), reveals clear differences in physicochemical properties and antimicrobial efficacy. Both types exhibited a characteristic SPR band near 420 nm and predominantly spherical morphology; however, chem-AgNPs were significantly larger (~ 35.1 nm) than bio-AgNPs (~ 17.4 nm). FTIR spectra revealed characteristic functional groups corresponding to hydroxyl, amine, and carbonyl vibrations, implying surface capping by organic biomolecules in the biosynthesized formulation. This disparity in physical characteristics correlated with their biological activity. The bio-AgNPs demonstrated superior antimicrobial potency, exhibiting consistently lower MIC values (500 µg/mL) than chem-AgNPs (2000 µg/mL) across most tested pathogens. Strain-dependent variations among Gram-negative bacteria indicate that antimicrobial efficacy is influenced by factors extending beyond nanoparticle size or Gram classification alone. Specifically, Vibrio cholerae ATCC700 exhibited greater susceptible to chem-AgNPs, whereas Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC13883 showed increased resistance to bio-AgNPs. The enhanced resistance of K. pneumoniae ATCC13883 is likely attributable to its dense polysaccharide capsule and active efflux systems, which restrict nanoparticle penetration and limit intracellular Ag+ accumulation [101–104]. Conversely, the higher susceptibility of Vibrio cholerae ATCC700 to chem-AgNPs may reflect a less protective outer membrane and more favorable nanoparticle–cell interactions. Characterization of electrospun nanofibrous mats

Nanofibrous mats were successfully fabricated by electrospinning PVA/PEO/β-CD solutions, with and without the incorporation of chem-AgNPs and bio-AgNPs. The morphology and fiber diameter were characterized using FE-SEM [106]. Uniform, bead-free nanofibers were successfully fabricated in all formulations (Fig. 9). The blank (AgNPs-free) PVA/PEO/β-CD nanofibers (Fig. 9a) exhibited a smooth, porous, bead-free, and interconnected morphology, indicating good structural integrity. Incorporation of chem-AgNPs (Fig. 9b) and bio-AgNPs (Fig. 9c) preserved this morphology, with no visible phase separation, suggesting good compatibility and homogeneous dispersion of AgNPs within the polymer matrix.Fig. 9FE-SEM micrographs of electrospun nanofibrous composites and corresponding fiber diameter distribution histograms. a Blank PVA/PEO/β-CD nanofibers; b PVA/PEO/β-CD/chem-AgNPs composite; c PVA/PEO/β-CD/bio-AgNPs composite

FE-SEM micrographs of electrospun nanofibrous composites and corresponding fiber diameter distribution histograms. a Blank PVA/PEO/β-CD nanofibers; b PVA/PEO/β-CD/chem-AgNPs composite; c PVA/PEO/β-CD/bio-AgNPs composite

Notably, AgNPs were clearly identifiable within the composite nanofibers, confirming their successful incorporation. Localized nanoparticle aggregation of AgNPs was occasionally observed, likely due to migration within the electrospinning jet driven by the high electrical conductivity of AgNPs [38]. These morphological findings are consistent with previous reports on AgNPs-embedded electrospun nanofiber composites [38].

The average diameter of the nanofibers was determined by measuring 80 randomly selected fibers from FE-SEM images using ImageJ software (Fig. 9). The average diameter of blank PVA/PEO/β-CD nanofibers was 171.5 nm. Incorporation of nanoparticles led to a slight but distinct variation in fiber diameters. The average diameter reduced to 150.5 nm for the chem-AgNPs composite nanofibers, while slightly increased to 204.3 nm for the bio-AgNPs composite nanofibers. Despite these variations, the fundamental microstructure of the nanofibers remained intact.

The diameter reduction in the chem-AgNPs composites is likely attributed to enhanced solution conductivity, which increases electrostatic stretching force during electrospinning, yielding thinner fibers [38]. This phenomenon is consistent with findings in similar systems, such as AgNPs/β-CD/Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) nanofibrous mats [9]. Previous studies have demonstrated a clear link between increased solution conductivity and reduced fiber diameter [107]. However, we did not directly measure solution conductivity in the present work; therefore, this mechanistic explanation should be regarded as a plausible interpretation rather than a definitive conclusion. Future studies will include quantitative conductivity measurements to verify this relationship in our formulations. Conversely, the slight increase in diameter for the bio-AgNPs composite may be related to the presence of β-CD-associated biomolecules, consistent with reports obtained by Bai et al. [106], who observed that increasing the β-CD content from 0 to 20 wt.% in β-CD/PVP nanofibers led to a corresponding increase in average diameter from 498 to 621 nm. These findings indicate that nanoparticle properties, rather than their mere presence, govern fiber diameter modulation without compromising structural integrity.

Beyond morphology, FTIR analysis (Fig. 10), with major vibrational modes summarized in Table 3, was conducted to examine chemical interactions within the nanofibrous composites. The FTIR spectra of the blank (Fig. 10a) and bio-AgNPs composite nanofibers (Fig. 10b) were largely similar, indicating that no significant alteration of the polymer functional groups upon bio-AgNPs incorporation. The broad band at ~ 3334 cm−1 corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations from hydrogen bonding in PVA and β-CD [108–111]. Bands in the ~ 2925 cm−1 region were assigned to C–H stretching vibrations of alkyl groups in PVA and β-CD [56, 109, 112]. Bands at ~ 1706 cm−1 and ~ 1657 cm−1 were attributed to residual C = O and C–O stretches from PVA, respectively [108]. The characteristic C–O stretching vibration at ~ 1084 cm−1 further confirms the presence of PVA/β-CD [77, 113]. In contrast, the spectrum of the chem-AgNPs composite (Fig. 10c) showed reduced intensities of the O–H at ~ 3334 cm−1 and C–H at ~ 2925 cm−1 stretching bands, suggesting interactions between the chem-AgNPs and the polymer functional groups possibly through coordination or surface adsorption. The absence of peak shifts indicates physical interactions rather than chemical bond formation. On the other hand, FTIR spectra of the plain PVA/PEO/β-CD composite often show systematic shifts and broadening of characteristic peaks that reflect changes in the hydrogen-bonding environment and intermolecular interactions between the polymers. For example, in PVA/β-CD systems the broad O–H stretching band centered around ~ 3208 cm−1 in pure PVA shifts to lower wavenumbers (~ 3177 cm−1) upon addition of β-CD—a hypochromic shift attributed to increased hydrogen bonding between PVA and β-CD hydroxyl groups, evidencing stronger intermolecular interactions than in the pure components [116]. Similarly, in PVA/PEO blends the broad hydroxyl stretching band of PVA exhibits slight shifts in position and changes in intensity as PEO is incorporated, which has been interpreted in the literature as an indication of hydrogen bonds forming between the PVA –OH groups and the ether oxygens of PEO chains [117]. These spectral changes—especially shifts in the O–H stretching region and altered fingerprint region peaks—are consistent with the formation of a hydrogen-bonded network in the composite rather than simple physical superposition of the individual polymers’ spectra [118].

The blank PVA/PEO/β-CD nanofibrous mat showed no detectable antimicrobial activity, confirming that the polymer matrix itself is non-bioactive. However, a marginal inhibition zone was observed for Candida albicans ATCC 700, suggesting a potential interaction between the polymer matrix and β-CD. The precise mechanism responsible for this effect remains to be elucidated and merits further investigation. In contrast, both AgNPs-containing composite mats exhibited significant antimicrobial activity against all tested pathogens, confirming the successful incorporation and bioactivity of AgNPs. Comparative analysis of PVA/PEO/β-CD/chem-AgNPs and PVA/PEO/β-CD/bio-AgNPs composite mats (Table 4) revealed that the bio-AgNPs-based composites consistently produced larger inhibition zones than their chem-AgNPs counterparts, indicating overall greater antimicrobial potency. The antimicrobial activity was strain-dependent. For the chem-AgNPs composite mats, the largest inhibition zone was observed for Candida albicans ATCC 700 (16.0 ± 0.8 mm), while the smallest was recorded for Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC13883 (11.0 ± 0.7 mm). Similarly, the bio-AgNPs composite mats showed maximal activity against Vibrio cholerae ATCC700 (18.0 ± 0.6 mm), whereas with Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC13883 remaining the least susceptible strain (13.0 ± 0.6 mm). Statistical analysis revealed that the inhibition zones produced by bio-AgNPs nanofiber composites were significantly larger than those of chem-AgNPs nanofiber composites for Vibrio cholerae ATCC700, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC9027, and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC13883 (p < 0.05). For Staphylococcus aureus ATCC6538, Bacillus cereus ATCC6633, Escherichia coli NCTC10418, and Candida albicans ATCC700, the observed differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

The observed antimicrobial effects are attributed to the diffusion-driven release of AgNPs and/or Ag+ ions from the nanofibers into the agar medium, followed by direct interaction with microbial cells leading to growth inhibition [77]. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating negligible activity of the blank polymer composite mats and significant antimicrobial activity upon AgNPs incorporation [110, 122]. However, In the present study, Ag+ release kinetics were not experimentally quantified (e.g., by ICP-MS). Therefore, although the proposed mechanism is consistent with previous literature, the relationship between Ag+ release and antimicrobial efficacy in our system cannot be considered quantitative and remains inferential.

The superior antimicrobial performance of the bio-AgNPs composites is likely related to their unique surface chemistry derived from the biological synthesis route. In this approach, biomolecules such as proteins and polysaccharides act as natural capping agents, improving nanoparticle stability, dispersion within the polymer matrix, and controlled Ag+ release [18, 123, 124]. These biofunctional surfaces promote stronger interactions with microbial membranes, resulting in enhanced and sustained antimicrobial activity compared to chem-AgNPs [125]. Moreover, the PVA/PEO/β-CD nanofibrous matrix is designed as a transient, biodegradable carrier. Following the release of AgNPs and/or Ag+ ions, the polymer mat is expected to undergo gradual hydrolysis under physiological conditions, producing biocompatible byproducts. This degradable nature supports sustained antimicrobial action while minimizing long-term material accumulation, highlighting the potential suitability of these nanofibrous composites for short-term antimicrobial and biomedical applications.

Limitations and future perspective

This study provides a promising foundation for AgNPs-based nanofibrous antimicrobial platform; however, several aspects warrant further investigation. At this stage, comprehensive biological validation, including in vitro cytotoxicity testing, detailed Ag+ release kinetics, and systematic evaluation of polymer degradation under physiological conditions, has not yet been performed. Addressing these factors is essential to fully elucidate the safety, release behavior, and functional stability of the developed nanofibrous composites in biomedical environments. Although the nanofibrous composites exhibited strong antimicrobial activity, extending the evaluation to a broader range of clinically relevant and drug-resistant pathogens would further strengthen their translational relevance. These considerations should not be viewed as shortcomings, but as logical extensions of this proof-of-concept study.

Future work will focus on optimizing Ag+ release profile, elucidating the mechanisms responsible for the enhanced antimicrobial performance of bio-AgNPs-based systems, and evaluating long-term material stability. To directly link antimicrobial performance with silver release behavior, future work will incorporate AgNPs and/or Ag+ release profiling using techniques such as inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) or atomic absorption spectroscopy, coupled with time-resolved antimicrobial assays. In parallel, systematic in vitro biocompatibility and cytotoxicity studies using relevant healthy skin cell models will be performed to confirm the safety and suitability of these nanofibrous composites for prospective biomedical applications.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed electrospun PVA/PEO/β-CD nanofiber composites functionalized with chem-AgNPs and bio-AgNPs. Although these nanoparticles exhibited potent antimicrobial activity, their efficacy was significantly enhanced upon integration into the electrospun nanofibrous matrix, as evidenced by reproducible inhibition zones against multiple microbial strains. While both systems exhibited antimicrobial activity, bio-AgNPs-based nanofibers consistently demonstrated superior and more reproducible efficacy against all tested pathogens. Notably, this improvement is not solely attributable to particle size but is more likely associated with the distinct surface chemistry and bioactive capping agents from the biological synthesis route. These biomolecules may facilitate improved nanoparticle dispersion within the polymer matrix, more controlled and sustained Ag+ release, or synergistic interactions with microbial cell membranes, thereby enhancing antimicrobial efficacy. In addition, the developed bio-AgNPs-based nanofiber composites represent a promising, high-performance platform for advanced antimicrobial applications, including wound dressings, protective coatings, and filtration media. The PVA/PEO/β-CD nanofibrous matrix acts as a transient, biodegradable carrier, supporting its suitability for short-term antimicrobial and biomedical applications. Overall, this work highlights biological synthesis as a strategy not only for green fabrication but also for achieving functionally superior antimicrobial nanofiber platforms.
