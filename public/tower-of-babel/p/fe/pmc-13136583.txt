Uncover This Tech Term: Tumor Habitat Analysis
Authors: Ji Eun Park, Seong Ho Park
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Radiological imaging has long relied on the implicit assumption that a tumor can be meaningfully summarized as a single object. However, this assumption is increasingly at odds with the contemporary understanding of tumor biology, which emphasizes profound intratumoral heterogeneity as a driver of treatment resistance, recurrence, and poor prognosis.

Radiomics is based on the hypothesis that data-driven quantitative imaging features can be useful for capturing heterogenous tumor phenotypes [1]. Through mean attenuation, average signal intensity, or global texture features, radiomics enables the depiction of spatial complexity by extracting numerous quantitative features, which are subsequently reduced by machine learning into a smaller set of representative features. Despite this effort, the indirect relationships between radiomic features and underlying biology and the loss of spatial information have left the biological meaning of radiomics largely unexplained [2].

Habitat analysis has emerged as an attempt to reconcile imaging with the biological reality. Habitat analysis conceptualizes a tumor as an ecosystem composed of spatially distinct subregions, each representing a different microenvironment and biological behavior. While the idea is intuitively attractive, its implementation and interpretation have often been conflated, particularly in radiomics literature. In this article, we aim to clarify what habitat analysis truly is, what it is not, and where its real clinical value may lie.

WHAT IS HABITAT ANALYSIS IN A NUTSHELL?

Habitat analysis is a framework for mapping intratumoral heterogeneity. The central premise is simple: different regions within a tumor exhibit different combinations of tumor characteristics such as cellularity, vascularity, hypoxia, and metabolism [3]. These differences are not random noise but rather structured biological states that may be captured through multiparametric imaging. In practical terms, habitat analysis divides a tumor volume into multiple sub-regions, referred to as habitats, based on voxel-wise similarity across multiple imaging sequences. Therefore, a habitat is not defined anatomically but computationally, representing a cluster of voxels that share similar mathematical imaging signatures and, by extension, presumed biological characteristics [2].

The shift from summarizing a tumor to regionally mapping it according to different biological characteristics is important. This mirrors how expert radiologists already interpret images, mentally distinguishing areas of tumor aggressiveness prone to future recurrence or areas showing treatment-related changes by combining multiparametric imaging information. Habitat analysis can be viewed as a computational and quantitative extension of this long-standing cognitive process.

HOW ARE HABITATS COMPUTED?

Several methods to divide and quantify tumor subregions are known, including threshold-based, geographic, and data-driven multispectral methods [3]. Among these, most typical habitat analyses rely on a data-driven voxel-wise unsupervised clustering method. The creation of habitats begins with the registration of different image sequences, such as contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging, T2-weighted imaging, diffusion-weighted imaging, and perfusion imaging from a multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examination. Each voxel is then represented as a vector of imaging values derived from these multiple sequences. Unsupervised clustering machine learning algorithms (e.g., K-means or Gaussian mixture models) then group voxels with similar signal patterns into distinct clusters (Fig. 1).

Two conceptual issues are often underappreciated:

First, consistency among patients is a nontrivial issue. Subject-level clustering, in which unsupervised clustering analysis and habitat generation are performed independently for each patient, is appealingly simple but both technically and biologically fragile. The number of habitats, choice of input sequences, and preprocessing steps, such as normalization and standardization, all shape the resulting map. Without careful standardization, the same habitat labels in different patients may correspond to different tissue states. To address this, pooled approaches have been explored, in which all tumor voxels from a training cohort comprising multiple patients are clustered jointly to derive a set of cluster centroids and associate decision boundaries in the feature space. These centroids and decision boundaries are then applied to new patients to assign habitat labels, with the goal of enforcing consistency across patients (e.g., “habitat X” represents the same tissue state across individuals). Ultimately, the external validation of centroids and decision boundaries, together with standardized workflows, is necessary to improve the generalizability of habitat biomarkers.

Second, habitats are not entirely discovered; rather, they are imposed to a certain extent. Therefore, habitat analysis is not a neutral revelation of tumor biology, but a structured abstraction constrained by methodological decisions. Unsupervised clustering methods define habitats based on statistical similarities in imaging features without the explicit incorporation of biological knowledge. Consequently, the resulting centroids and decision boundaries are better understood not as deterministic rules but as probabilistic representations of the feature space, often allowing for the soft assignment of voxels to multiple habitats, where membership reflects the relative likelihood in feature space (e.g., “80% habitat X and 20% habitat Y”). Although conceptually appealing, this adds another layer of abstraction that distances the output from clinical intuition and highlights the biological fragility of habitats. To address this limitation, biological validation of habitat definitions against histopathology is essential.

HABITAT IMAGING VERSUS HABITAT RADIOMICS

One of the most persistent sources of confusion in the literature is the conflation of habitat imaging with habitat radiomics (Fig. 1). These are related but fundamentally different constructs.

Habitat imaging produces a spatial map, which is a visual partition of the tumor into biologically different regions. Its value lies in its localization. Surgeons, radiation oncologists, and interventional radiologists can directly act upon this information. The concept of “dose painting” in radiotherapy, where more aggressive habitats receive higher radiation doses, exemplifies this tangible clinical utility [4].

In contrast, habitat radiomics treats each habitat as a unit for feature extraction, rather than the entire tumor. Standard radiomic features (e.g., texture, intensity, and shape) are extracted within each habitat and used in predictive modeling, analogous to conventional whole-tumor radiomics. The result is not a map, but a prediction model consisting of a set of invisible parameters—often dozens or hundreds—that lack direct physiological meaning.

THE DOUBLE UNCERTAINTY PROBLEM: WHERE HABITAT RADIOMICS BECOMES FRAGILE

Despite its conceptual appeal, habitat radiomics suffers from what might be called double uncertainty. The first layer of uncertainty arises from unsupervised clustering. There is rarely a ground truth for habitat boundaries, and small variations in preprocessing or scanner characteristics can produce different partitions. The second layer of uncertainty emerges during radiomics modeling. The various methodological issues that limit the generalizability of radiomics modeling are well known [5]. These uncertainty components compound, rather than averaging out. A model must first assume that habitats are biologically meaningful and subsequently assume that features extracted from these habitats are stable, reproducible, and prognostically relevant. In practice, this often leads to overfitting, poor generalizability, and results that are statistically impressive, but clinically brittle.

Furthermore, habitat radiomics creates a nested-data structure; habitats are not independent observations, but correlated subregions within the same patient. Methods such as mixed-effects models and multiple-instance learning can address this dependency, although they do not fully eliminate concerns about robustness.

Finally, while habitat analysis was intended to enable the spatial interpretation of multiparametric imaging biomarkers, habitat radiomics reverses this rationale by applying high-dimensional computational features to spatially defined regions, rather than directly using voxel-level signals. By encoding relationships among voxels instead of intrinsic voxel values, habitat radiomics further distances analysis from underlying biology and complicates biological validation.

WHY THE BRAIN? THE NEURO-ONCOLOGY PREDOMINANCE IN HABITAT ANALYSIS

Habitat analysis has flourished disproportionately in brain tumor imaging, particularly in glioblastoma, although it has also been applied to tumors in other organs, such as the liver, lungs, and prostate. This is not accidental.

Brain MRI offers a uniquely rich multiparametric environment. Routine protocols already include multiple contrasts that are sensitive to complementary tissue properties, thereby creating ideal conditions for clustering algorithms. Biologically, glioblastoma is an archetype of intratumoral heterogeneity that is aggressive, adaptive, and spatially diverse compared with most other solid tumors [6]. Clinically, the brain presents a “no-biopsy” dilemma as tissue sampling is invasive, risky, and spatially limited. A single biopsy rarely reveals the most aggressive tumor component. In this context, habitat analysis functions as a form of virtual biopsy, offering whole-tumor characterization without physical sampling [278].

These converging factors explain why similar enthusiasm has not translated to other organs, where imaging is often less multiparametric and tissue sampling is more accessible.

CONCLUSION

Habitat analysis is a framework for mapping intratumoral heterogeneity, most commonly via unsupervised voxel-wise clustering analysis. It is neither a panacea nor a passing fad. At its best, it is a formalization of radiological intuition, an attempt to respect the spatial localization and complexity of disease. However, combined with radiomics, it has become an exercise in methodological excess, divorced from biological validation and clinical actionability.

To advance this field to move forward, clarity is required. Habitat imaging and habitat radiomic should not be conflated. Clinical value should be judged not by predictive performance alone, but by interpretability, robustness, and the ability to change patient management. In radiology, complexity is justified only when it enables us to make meaningful decisions. Habitat analysis will only endure to the extent that it remembers this principle.
