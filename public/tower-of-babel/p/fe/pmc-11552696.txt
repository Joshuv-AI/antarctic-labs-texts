Backroads into buried pasts: Irish border mobilities and narrative film
Authors: Lennart Soberon, Kevin Smets
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBecause of the Irish border’s troubled history and heightened symbolism, filmmakers have since long relied on the border as a setting to thematise issues of national identity, belonging, and trauma. Departing from the concept of border poetics this article takes films featuring the border between the Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland as its topic of inquiry. Beyond compiling a border film archive in which all cinematic representation of the Irish border is collected, a close reading is conducted of border mobilities in a sample of 43 narrative fiction films (1937–2021). The article’s textual analysis makes the distinction between three border mobilities (1) border crossing, (2) border inhabiting, and (3) border (re)visiting, to investigate how filmmakers utilise the Irish border for various narrative, formal, and ideological ends. As such, the border’s cinematic legacy can be better understood.KEYWORDS: Irish border, cinema, mobility, border crossing, border cinema

Introduction

With the advent of Brexit, the Irish border has been continuously revisited as a topic of concern and contestation. After being tended with the care of an old war wound for over a decade after the 1998 Good Friday Agreements, the line that demarcated Northern Ireland from the Republic of Ireland1 was set to separate the United Kingdom from the European Union (EU). Although tensions around the potential return to a “hard border” were eventually quelled through the solution of an Irish backstop, the 2022 post-election collapse of Stormont proved that this solution might be more fragile than anticipated. Considering these current political tensions, it is no surprise that arts and culture have responded to socio-political anxieties by intervening and interacting with the border and its troubled legacy. Whether it is in the context of Brexit, the Decades of Centenaries, or the EU Peace Programs, diverse institutions have commissioned work on and about the border, as well as generated platforms for its dissemination. From a political-economic point of view, border-set films have the additional advantage of being relevant to both countries. It is therefore no surprise that many recent border films are co-productions between the Republic and NI and their respective funding bodies: Screen Ireland and NI Screen.

As part of this growing interest in border art, the Irish screen border is similarly on the rise. Recent films such as Border Country (2018), Wildfire (2020), and Four Seasons in a Day (2021) show that the border can be considered a well-funded and timely subject matter for contemporary directors. The popularity of film as a medium is not surprising. As Fojas states, cinema constitutes an essential space for negotiating the legitimacy of borders.2 With its affective appeal to audiences and treatment of space, films can help reinforce hegemonic understandings of state sovereignty,3 question their legitimacy as “borderwork,”4 or defamiliarise how these borders stand in the collective imagination.5 Despite the evident socio-political importance of the Irish border, its depiction on screen has often been overlooked by film scholars and border theorists alike. Projects such as the recent Irish Film Institute (IFI) restoration of Blarney (1937) are a welcome reminder that cinematic border representation predates many canonical examples, going back as far as the Irish Free State. Such rediscoveries hint at a rich representational history that is near-forgotten, yet still has important things to say about current affairs.

To map out the film representation of the Irish border, as well as study its stylistic, narrative, and ideological properties, this article undertakes a more concise conceptualisation of what we refer to as Irish border cinema. Departing from cultural and critical theories around borders and the political potential of their artistic interpretation, particularly Schimanski and Wolfe’s notion of border poetics, a comprehensive reading will be given of how, through border representation, filmmakers employ the Irish borderland for various aims and ends.6 Complementarily, we are interested in how such depictions negotiate the position of the Irish border through various regimes of mobility. To achieve this, we constructed an archive of border cinema consisting of 44 films from an over 80-year period (1937–2021). By way of a close reading of these films, we offer a large-scale thematic analysis of border (im)mobilities and the poetics of the Irish border cinema. However, before delving into the analysis, we will first craft a theoretical framework that will help to understand Irish border cinema as a distinct category of films with their own representational specificities and artistic affordances.

Living on a thin line

Although borders are commonly associated and defined by their rigid materiality, it’s important to understand that their position is equally characterised by a degree of intangibility, liminality, and fluidity.7 As Bromley8 argues, borders are “symbolic, imaginary, and performative.” The Irish border is an excellent case in point. Having been a hard border alternating between various degrees of fixity since the Partition, including militarisation, during most of Operation Banner,9 the 1992 Single Market agreement and 1994 peace negotiations initiated a process in which the seventy-plus-year-old material infrastructure border was slowly ironed out of existence. While the Irish border is nowadays notably lacking in checkpoint infrastructure or other visual referents to state control, its presence is long-lasting through its affective entanglements with both past and future. The Brexit negotiations transformed the Irish border into a pending border. Its mundane and everyday effects aside, the Brexit border discussion highlighted how much the border’s legacy is internalised in the hearts and minds of many.

A primary challenge when studying narratives on the Irish border is to differentiate between the state border between NI and the Republic and the many cultural borders that run through the Northern borderlands dividing the territory between Catholic-Republican and Loyalist-Protestant factions. Considering NI a liminal state in which the British and Irish identities contest the national one, Waller understands the deepest border to be the ethno-cultural one cutting through both communities.10 The NI conflict has sharpened these divides through legacies of violence, intimidation, and displacement – especially in the northern borderlands. Moreover, because NI never transitioned through an institutionalised truth-and-reconciliation process both communities are involved in a matter of “combative commemoration” that seeks recognition of their victimisation.11 Within the context of this antagonistically fuelled memory work, the very symbol of the border takes up a polarising position. As Harris, one of the first Irish border anthropologists, wrote in the first years of the conflict: “The border, close physically and omnipresent psychologically, brought into sharp contrast not only those actually separated by it, but those separated because their opinion about it were opposed.”12 For Republican-Catholics,13 the mere option of returning to a hard border triggers traumatic memories of checkpoints under British rule. Loyalist-Protestants, on the other hand, consider the hard border the only doorpost to prevent the further erosion of their identity and a potential reunification of Ireland.14 As such, the border became the razor’s edge of a sectarian divide, cutting deep into the fabric of communities and leaving scars for future generations to heal.

However, as Nash and Reid state: “The border is not only a symbolically loaded political boundary but also a presence that impacts on everyday life as it is experienced on the ground.”15 Borderlanders who lived through the terror of the Troubles, as well as their descendants, still carry the traumatic burden of those violent times. Moreover, as Rosher shows, since Brexit would undermine the livelihood and everyday operations of many individuals, its advent has triggered a larger sense of “ontological insecurity” for those living at the border.16 This helps to understand how the border’s future is burdened by a volatile potentiality for both communities and is responsible for a great deal of anxiety for those who call the borderlands of the Republic and NI their home. Even though there’s a risk of essentialising something as “border cultures,” Donnan and Wilson consider there to be sufficient ground to look at borderlands as intimately entwined entities.17 As Anzaldua states, borderlanders in many ways embody the border.18 They’ve had to learn how to live with it by understanding and reacting to its affordances. Hence, to borderlanders the border is not only merely a line on a map, nor a symbolised abstraction, but rather a set of practices, rituals, and memories that define everyday life. Moreover, borderland communities often challenge traditional notions of nation-state identities through their heterogeneous compositions and frequent mobility.19 It is in this vein that Alvarez understands the borderland as a hybrid “third space” capable of opening indeterminate and liminal ways of belonging.20 Although many borderlands demonstrate solidarity and transnational cooperation, Martinez also warns about the magnification of identity in such areas, as well as their increased risk of ethno-cultural conflicts.21

The Irish borderland can similarly be read along those lines. Case in point are the everyday forms of bordering which take place at the borderland, and NI at large. The ethno-sectarian landscape at the Irish borderlands is rife with forms of visual demarcations that serve to assert territorial control or engage in acts of homemaking and placemaking. As Hastings and Wilson note, Irish borderlanders engage in forms of everyday bordering that signal if a zone is Catholic-Republican or Loyalist-Protestant.22 Murals, flags, colours, curbstones, parades, shrines, churches, signs, names, accents, and more have all been politicised in a manner that their presence indicates in which type of territory one ventures.23 Even the quality of the roads and the sight of the farmland is soaked in ethno-cultural associations. Moreover, as Santinomarks out, many of these visual signals are not fixed, but dependent on time and event.24 McCall refers to this process as “borderscaping,” adding that cultural expression and visual representation play an essential part in establishing and subverting power relations.25 Here especially, the border is not so much a line as a nodal point through which meaning is made and identities configured. As Donnan attests, such demarcations of space are especially present around contested border areas because they help retain a sense of control in a space of perpetual insecurity.26

As much as these material and affective borders have defined the borderland, borderscaping can also be understood from the angle of fluidity, hybridity, and mobility. Nail’skinopolitical approach, for example, defines borders as “regimes of motion” that try to regulate the mobilities of goods, individuals, and more.27 While nation states aim to control the flow of these objects and subjections through junction-like infrastructure, borderlanders can assert their agency over the terrain through practices of crossing, smuggling, and homemaking. Trandafoiu notes how these mobilities should also be understood in audiovisual terms.28 After all, through the exchange of images and narratives, “(w)e are perpetually in motion and incessantly mediated and mediatized.” How borders are depicted thus becomes part of a process of imaginative borderscaping which “mediates, represents, and enhances mobility.” To Schimanski and Nyman this process is tied to a system of poetics in which “form, medium, genre, and aesthetical strategies” help define the border’s discursive infrastructure.29 As such, borders become a text that is read both by mobilities enacted and mobilities represented.30 Over time these poetics can grow into powerful rhetorical figures that affect how these borders and their surrounding mobilities are perceived.31

Irish border cinema

While the poetics of Irish border mobilities have been studied from the angles of poetry,32 literature,33 oral storytelling,34 and photography35 there exists a notable absence of scholarly work on the Irish border’s cinematic representation. This is not necessarily because the borderlands are less prominent cinematically. Many canonical Irish film texts, helmed by prolific Irish directors such as Joe Comerford, Thaddeus O’Sullivan, and Neil Jordan, are set at the Irish borderlands or thematise acts of border mobility. Nevertheless, a tendency to downplay, if not wholly omit, the border within these respective readings seems to exist. Hopper’s study of Traveller (1981), for example, is more occupied with talking about the boundaries between reality and re-enactment rather than the actual land border that is repeatedly crossed in the film.36 More strikingly, the film adaptation of The Butcher Boy (1997) is seldomly related to its border town setting or the role of the border in Patrick McCabe’s oeuvre.

This lacuna becomes especially apparent when considering the prominent position of space and place within Irish film studies. According to Gibbons “landscape has tended to play a leading role in Irish cinema, often upstaging both the main characters and narrative themes in the construction of Ireland on screen.”37 In the last decades, the depiction of scenery such as the Donegal cliffs and Connemara Lakes, and topoi such as the Irish cottage or parochial village, have been utilised as important markers through which Irish cinema and its entanglements with culture and politics could be interpreted.38 However, despite these theoretical and thematic preoccupations, the Irish borderland is not attributed a similar degree of merit. Independently, such border myopia leads to a limited reading of a film. Yet, structurally, it is at risk of erasing the border and its ambivalences from a longstanding body of representation.

Fox’s study set on “reclaiming the Irish border in contemporary cinema” is the notable exception to the rule and actively tries to establish the Irish borderland as a crucial cinematic space that uses the border’s history and topography to engage with its contested nature.39 Intended as an introductory study on the theme, Fox’s analysis is limited in scope – restricting itself to the last 20 years – and mostly bent on mapping several key texts as part of a two-folded typology of how the Irish border appears on film. His analysis makes the distinction between films that utilise the border as a backdrop without cultural nor historic contextualisation, and those that delve deeper into Ireland’s troubled past and treat the border as a character. Because the borderland’s natural scenery is, for large parts, indistinguishable from other parts of the Irish countryside, Fox notes that the border is used interchangeably in many films. The street racing action film Accelerator (2001) is here, despite the characteristic Armagh-Louth border roads, used as an example of where the border is ultimately little more than a setting the characters pass through. This backdrop juxtaposes what Fox understands as the thematic border where “the narrative arises from the landscape and the actions and attitudes of the characters are shaped by the border terrain.”40 In the romantic drama Run of the Country (1995), the narrative is unmistakably defined by the border checkpoints cutting the community in half. Border security is a part of everyday life and regularly referenced or evoked in the film’s mise-en-scène. Even when not the focus of the narrative, it does complicate the desires of the protagonists and even influences the plot mechanics of the film’s cross-border romance.

The topic of border thematisation is an interesting argument to depart from since it allows us to acknowledge that borders are spaces that provide filmmakers with specific narratives and iconographies. Fojas,41 Schimanski, and others42 are convinced of the specificity of border-set films to the degree that they argue that “border cinema” can be regarded as a category of its own. After all, not only do borders provide filmmakers with a heterotopic arena wherein different rules apply and situations arise, but they also actively raise important questions of nationality, citizenship, and state power. In this light, border poetics become a strong conceptual tool that allows us to understand cinematic border representation as an aesthetic and discursive system. Through the interplay of form, style, and genre, the border is clothed in metonymic potential with a recursive relationship with life beyond the screen. Dynasties of road movies and westerns, for example, have umbilically bound USA-Mexico border crossing from the United States to Mexico to symbols of freedom and frontiermenship to the point that the way to the border is now a beaten path for any crime film in search of a third act. Similarly, in horror films, crossing national and regional borders is often attributed to transgressive power, signalling a departure into the uncanny.43 As such, borders become a toolkit for filmmakers constructing narrative progress or inciting drama. From a screenwriter’s perspective, the contrasts that borders create provide ample opportunity for conflict and complications. A border can be an obstacle, confrontation, transformation, or everyday banality, depending on who’s doing the crossing.

Border representation can also be a way of reading the world. As Rascaroli points out, a semiotic power stems from border images capable of doing “border work” that deconstructs these very borders and the power regimes holding them in place.44 In the Irish case, too, as Fox attests, the border is used to evoke the NI conflict and the hardships those regions endured. In other contexts, the Irish screen border is referenced chiefly as a metaphor for the backward and violent attitudes of border towns. In Neil Jordan’s Breakfast on Pluto (2005), for example, the transgender Kitty flees from her small border town in search of a more tolerant society, yet also towards an apolitical environment.45 The day-to-day violence and political struggle that defines life at the borderland is seen as regressive in contrast to the neoliberal stability of London. Rhyming her transgender identity with the “third space” disposition of the borderland, the border here becomes a way of gendering the nation – Ireland as violently masculine, England as delicately feminine. This connotation of the borderlands with atavist violence and underlying threat is also something that Fox spots in films such as Mapmaker (2001), where an atmosphere of suspicion and buried traumas define the borderland as a more mythical landscape.46 Like the narrow and twisting roads of the border roads, Irish border cinema constitutes an intricate network of references and properties capable of exploring different thematic spaces.

Building a border archive

Departing from border poetics and its relation to border mobilities, we offer a comprehensive inquiry into Irish border cinema as a distinct category of films. Although not an industry-utilised genre, it does benefit to conceptually understand Irish border cinema as part of a unifying system. Like other film theoretical categorisations, such as Naficy’s notion of accented cinema, border cinema is held together through the treatment of a specific subject matter and a shared commitment to understanding, re-reading, or de-familiarising a particular aspect of society.47 To arrive at a film sample that’s as extensive as possible, we first tried to construct an exhaustive border archive of all films set at the Irish border. This open definition allows us to take a broad approach to titles and fully grasp how the border is approached. During a three-month research stay, a list of every type of border representation that is not a newsreel or television programme was compiled. As such, we arrived at a list of 77 films spread over 87 years (1937–2024). Since our ultimate sample is focused on fiction films, we used 46 films for this study, consisting of 41 features and five shorts. In this study, we are particularly interested in how the poetics of border cinema can be brought in connection with questions of culture and power to understand their ideological connotations. Aligning with theories on border (im)mobilities, our textual analysis is tuned towards representation patterns that crystalise around different types of border mobilities.48

As Rennhak & Klein demonstrated, a kinopolitical approach to border fiction is particularly beneficial when in comes to the Irish border.49 With its estimated 200-plus crossing points, some stretching through farmland, rivers, and drumlins, the Irish border can be considered unusually porous and therefore prone to unsupervised or unhindered mobility. Even at the height of the British occupation, the border proved impossible to demarcate, let alone block. Furthermore, since the peace process helped to decommission almost all border infrastructure, it has become even more challenging to visually spot the border’s precise location apart from the odd road sign and the change in road quality. Because of this opacity, representing the actual borderline is a representational challenge for filmmakers. While signs, roadblocks, and other border infrastructure help denote the border, films must rely on hardhanded exposition scenes in which characters physically cross the border to make clear we’ve now entered borderland territory. In High Boot Benny, the film opens with the camera lingering on signs stating, “Border Control Ahead” and “Hoping You Make a Return Visit to Northern Ireland” as a prologue to the main narrative. In Heritage (1973), characters even refer to how they’re “literally living a mile from the border.” After having denoted the film’s setting, the border is continuously emphasised to help remind the viewer of the particularity of its place. Wildfire, for example, utilises a more poetic rendition in which the border cuts a local river in half where the main characters go swimming. Based on the border stretch that falls in the middle of the Cor River, this location helps bring the border back into the focus and emphasise its absurdity. This highlights the multifaceted ways, figurative or not, to bring the border into being cinematically.

Because of the material and symbolic complexity of the Irish border, it is more fruitful to let the border be defined by how film characters choose to interact with it rather than focusing on looking at the border as a thing itself. Specifically, we focus on three different regimes of border (im)mobilities: (1) crossing, (2) inhabiting, and (3) (re)visiting the border. With crossing, we refer to crossing from one side of the Irish border to the other. Here, we focus on how filmmakers utilise border crossing as a narrative and aesthetic tool. On the other hand, border inhabiting denotes those living in border areas and interacting with the border as part of their everyday experience. Lastly, border (re)visiting refers to themes of displacement and return to tackle the dubious relation between North and South. Although it’s difficult to delineate these three regimes of mobility strictly, we argue that each implies a distinct set of border poetical preoccupations.

Crossing the border

All borders are porous. The history of borders is, in some sense, also the story of people resisting its authority or profiting from the restrictions it imposes. As Leary remarks, the border’s porous nature has since its dawn been a source of stories detailing the exploits of smugglers, gunrunners, and other spectacular forms of crossing.50 The light-hearted whimsy of early border stories stands in stark contrast with the storytelling traditions that arose from the NI conflict,51 through which terrorist violence, everyday intimidation, and mass displacement are widely documented. Irish border cinema draws on the contents of both types of traditions and transforms the act of crossing into either film narrative event or a thematic lynchpin.

Broadly speaking, moments of border crossing representation feature narratives of crime and smuggling, terrorism and conflict, and confrontation and reconciliation. Each type of narrative bears its distinct attitude to the act of crossing and defines how it serves the working of the film’s plot. Thereby influencing who is crossing, how, and for what reasons. Because border films often represent different mobilities conjointly, we’ll revisit some of these crossing narratives in the following sections. However, isolating the act of crossing in these films is particularly interesting because it helps highlight how the border becomes a tool for filmmakers to structure the narrative and steer the audience’s emotions. The narratives of crime and smuggling, or terrorism and conflict, are particularly suitable for such ends. It’s perhaps for this reason that smuggling narratives are most prevalent in our sample, be it in many incarnations.

Despite the often-spectacular nature of smuggling histories, it’s notable that smuggling as a character action is often treated as a continuous and everyday activity in the lives of many borderlanders. While what is smuggled dramatically depends on the historical period of the film, smuggling as a form of day-to-day survival runs consistently through our film sample. The flow of goods and characters here occurs in a cyclical pattern, meaning that characters venture over the border only to return later in the story and repeat the process. In this sense, smuggling is hardly introduced as a one-off event but more as a routine that aligns with the progression of the film’s narrative. This going back and forth over the border, while eluding British military and border patrol, defines the smuggler by their ability to navigate the porous terrain. One that intimately knows and has mastered the borderlands. After all, the smuggler’s livelihood depends on their ability to read the border. Like actual smuggling accounts, the border crossing goods are often food, electronics, weapons, and – especially in post-ceasefire cinema – fuel. In some rare cases, filmmakers opt for more flamboyant smuggling actions to heighten the story’s stakes, supply comic relief, or add a symbolic dimension. In Blarney, for example, jewels are smuggled and lost over the border to raise the stakes to retrieve the missing material. Eat the Peach (1986) offers an increasingly elaborate set of smuggling disguises, including a milk truck, pigpen, and haystack to score some laughs. Similarly, in the partition-set farce Puckoon (2002), the cemetery is placed on the North while the congregation lies South. The smuggling of bodies over the border this way becomes an apt demonstration of the absurdity of how the border was drawn while also acknowledging the strain it puts on border communities.

Adding a layer of symbolism to the smuggling trade, High Boot Benny (1993) opens with the illegal trafficking of blood from a British military hospital over the border. Whether cases of such thefts occurred or are wholly fictional is beside the point; the transfusion of blood from NI to the Republic can be read as an effective metaphor for how the British occupation over the border provided the lifeblood for the Republican cause. Aside from early border representations such as Blarney and Oh, Mr. Porter (1937), in which smuggling is posed as a problem and smugglers are presented as the story’s villains, smuggling is mainly treated as the defining act of the romantic outcast. Characters who are left at the margins of traditional Irish society, such as unmarried women (The Playboys (1992)), travellers (Traveller), or those affected by the economic deprivation of the borderland (Eat the Peach), rely on smuggling as an everyday form of survival in an otherwise hostile atmosphere. In Mapmaker, a child with a canister of petrol is featured in the film’s opening minutes to show the ubiquity of laundering fuel across the border. This permissive approach to smuggling is often complemented by the continuous trials and provocations characters must face at the borderposts to go about their day. In Run of the Country, the romantic set-up of the film is being subverted by the border setting both live in. The boy-meets-girl formula gets complicated by the lovers residing in the North and South, leading to the film becoming a type of Romeo and Juliet at the borderlands. While there have always been cases of cross-border romance, and relationships remain one of the main reasons for crossing, Run of the Country relies on this cross-border love to heighten the brutality of the conflict. The scenic, pastoral beauty of the borderland is juxtaposed with the violent intrusion of border checkpoints and other military infrastructure.

Despite the military infrastructure having undergone considerable changes in the last centuries, the border has been framed as a place of risk and peril throughout its cinematic representation. This is in no way surprising. Since the existence of NI has resulted from a colonial project, British border representation has always had sentiments of abject othering. Therefore, even before the border hardened, we can see ideological scripts in place to read the space as a lawless and threatening moral void. Already in Oh, Mr. Porter, the borderlands were an abject place fraught with risk, threat, and an eerie aura of the supernatural. This attributes a fatalist edge to the act of crossing since the danger to the protagonist is heightened with every moment of crossing and often culminates tragically. Run of the Country’s romantic plot ends with the main character being tarred and feathered by borderland Protestants upon his final crossing. Similarly, aligning with the heist-film genre’s convention of “one last job,” or the film noir’s affection for impending doom, films such as The Gentle Gunman (1952) soak the final crossing of a character in the prophecies of their inevitable demise. What lies beyond the border is certain death. Moreover, it’s not uncommon for films to use border crossing as a cornerstone to usher in the film’s final and often tragic act. This trope takes many different forms and adheres to different generic code. In the Land of Saints and Sinners (2023) sees a group of IRA operatives make a run for the border after a job gone wrong. While hoping to lay low in Donegal, they end up in the crosshairs of a retired mercenary. A similar western-like showdown is offers in Trapped’s (2008). After hiding out in Paris for an extensive section of the film, the protagonist returns to this family home on the Irish borderlands. While the violence that ensues ends up defeating the corrupt policemen and IRA factions that plagued the borderlands, it ultimately leads to the film’s hero’s death as well. In The General (1998) the act of visiting NI is the final transgression that leads to Martin Cahill’s assassination. When he travels up North to sell a stash of stolen paintings to the UVF, the IRA jumps into action to retaliate. Even for lawbreakers and kingpins, some borders should not be crossed.

In crossing narratives centred around terrorism and conflict, filmmakers get a bit more creative regarding how the border is utilised for dramatic ends. From early on, films such as I See a Dark Stranger (1946) have displayed the potential of border crossings to generate dramatic scenarios that must be solved. In this WWII-set spy thriller, the main characters cross to the North by accident after a frantic getaway. While at first seemingly irrelevant, this slight deviation reveals itself to have massive ramifications for the plot. As one character is a suspect of selling war secrets to the Nazis, her apprehension in the North means she can be tried for treason and sentenced to be executed – in contrast to the neutral South. From that moment on, the story’s stakes are raised, and the entire final act of the film is occupied with finding a way to return over the border. This narrative complication of crossing the border as an escape is repeatedly revisited in IRA-themed films of the time, such as Night Fighters (1957). In many films detailing the IRA’s paramilitary campaign against the British state, acts of crossings are equally used as a source of suspense in which volatile subjects and objects are en route to destruction. Omagh (2004), for example, documents the journey of the Omagh bomb from South to North. During such scenes, an air of unease permeates the border representations, effectively communicating the tensions and terror that the border generated between communities. Most effectively, Heritage (1976) ends with a disillusioned and traumatised UDR volunteer driving into a border checkpoint, thus literally attacking – and ultimately getting killed by – the border’s material manifestation.

These wider socio-cultural anxieties are further articulated through a topophobic52 treatment of the borderlands as a cinematic space. Like other cinematic border representations,53 the border here functions as a symbolic threshold that changes the representational register of two different territories (one safe/one threatening). While the physical space on both sides of the border is indistinguishable, the travel from South to North occasionally signifies a shift from pastoral splendour to the more unstable surveillance aesthetic that Brown identifies as the standard way of portraying urban NI.54 The gaze of the spy, camera, or helicopter is integrated into the film’s cinematography DNA. One of the sharpest and most subversive expressions of this can be found in Eugene McCabe’s Cancer (1973). The film opens with aerial shots of what appears to be the Irish countryside. In line with the tourist infatuation with the splendour of the Irish land, the film seems to push Barton’s argument on Irish cinema’s “gaze of the tourist” to near parody.55 Only when the romantic music accompanying these images of natural beauty and folkloristic idyll fades out and is replaced by the droning noise of whirling helicopter rotors does this familiar tourist exposition make way for something far more insidious. The gaze through which we had been observing the landscape is not the eye of an enamoured tourist, but the POV from a British military helicopter set out to spy and surveil the terrain. We’re no longer in the Republic, the film makes abundantly clear. What you’re seeing is the North.

Films such as Accelerator, Trapped, and The Mapmaker dabble in a similar regime. Shots from windows, bushes, and security monitors showcase that somebody might be watching at any time. In Contact (1985) an inversion of this visual besiegement is given by following British soldiers on their tour of duty. For them, the IRA is seemingly everywhere and each bush, tree, or car becomes an encounter of threatening insecurity. However, these same films often poke self-referential fun at the stereotypes existing around NI and the symbolic weight that crossing the border carries. In Accelerator, for example, one Irish character muses it’s “Like another country,” while another quickly replies, “Another planet more like.” Hostile as much NI representation might be, it pales compared to how these same films affectively prefocus the borderland as a specific space. Akin to Aguirre’s symbolic reading of the border as “a liminal state at the edge of the world, bordering on to infinity” the Irish borderlands are represented in narratives of crossing as a transgressive space loaded with dread and destruction.56 In Crossmaglen, for example, the Armagh borderlands are a lawless place in which violence can be enacted with impunity. Black Ice considers this lawlessness to remain well into the present because of the complex legal jurisdictions that define the winding border roads. Racers and smugglers alike profit from the South and North crossing Concession Road, making it nearly impossible for the Garda and PSNI to apprehend criminals. The border road becomes, as one boy racer notes, “Where the law can’t touch you:” a liminal space existing within a legal vacuum. It’s therefore no wonder that films such as Trapped evoke tropes of the classic Western. Presenting the 1970s borderland as a space between wilderness and civilisation, IRA fighters, corrupt policemen, and British operatives are locked in a stand-off for the soul of the island.

This representation is not only related to the physical violence from which the borderland suffered. It becomes abstracted and symbolised as an almost spiritual sense of unease ingrained in the borderland. There’s a sense that unspeakable evil lurks in the dark corners of the unapproved roads. The narrative and aesthetic legacy of Eugene McCabe’s border gothic literature,57 in which border life is expressed by falling back on references of infection, pathology, and disintegration, finds its way to cinema through atavist renderings of the border. Although made in the post-ceasefire years, Accelerator shifts the gears of its crime-comedy story when its street racing characters drive into the borderland. Resembling the look and feel of a horror movie, the racers are picked off one by one through random acts of violence from the local community (“You picked the wrong place to stop”), gunfire from British soldiers, and car crashes due to the eerie border roads. The mobilities here venture beyond the borderland and offer a headfirst collision with the ghost of NI’s past. Ultimately, the journey even regresses beyond human history into a timeless void. The film ends on an almost symbolist shot of the boglands as a purgatory non-space in which the two surviving characters dwell while searching for a crossing. As the camera zooms out, the two figures are swallowed by a hostile and indifferent landscape to which there is seemingly no end.

Inhabiting the border

As much as the border is treated as a crossroads, Irish border cinema is equally defined by stories of the borderlands as a battleground. Herein, the landscape imposes an immobility that fixes borderlanders to a grim fate from which there is seemingly no escape. In line with NI’s sectarian divide, the border becomes part of a tug-of-war between the two ethno-cultural communities that renders them physically, affectively, and symbolically immobile.58 Despite Heslinga’s59 claim of the border running along a cultural-religious divide, the borderline has never been an interface area between a Catholic South and a Protestant North.60 While it’s true that the Northern borderlands offer a complex patchwork of overlapping borderscapes between both communities, most of the Northern borderlands are demographically occupied by Catholics – some of county Tyrone southern regions notwithstanding. While finding its historical roots in the borderland’s close affinity to the Irish Free State, the NI conflict further intensified this demographic composition because of the IRA border campaign in the late 1950s and terrorist actions during the 1970s and 80s, in some cases leading to an average population composition of between 90–100% in South-Armagh. However, an entirely different map is drawn when looking at Irish border cinema.

Whether for rhetorical purposes or based on the perceived differences between both communities, crossing the screen border from South to North immediately implies entering a rugged and inhospitable Protestant terrain. In Mapmaker, the main character gets lost in the labyrinthic network of border roads on his way to a fictional border town – described as “the most godforsaken place.” Upon driving into a July 12th-type parade full of Union Jacks, Orange Order emblems, and Lambeg drummers, he sneers, “we’re in the North alright.” In a post-peace agreement world, the border is no longer defined by military infrastructure but by the presence of loyalist Protestants. A similar sight is shown in Wildfire, where upon crossing over from the South a series of Union Jack flags declare the area as still partially under British rule. This is arguably because both films don’t reference one particular place, but rather an imaginative collection of tropes and themes associated with the border. The psycho-geographical landscape that is drawn is an affectively loaded assemblage of different attitudes and iconographies related to the Northern borderland. This visual demarcation also extends to characters. Since it’s impossible to distinguish Loyalists-Protestant and Republican-Catholics from one another physically, filmmakers often rely on a similar set of visual and cultural signifiers to declare the denomination of characters to the viewer. Lambeg drums resting in the background (Heritage (1976), Four Seasons in a Day), Big Houses and material wealth (Siege (1976), Run of the Country), and flag-riddled Orange Halls (Mapmaker) help communicate how to read places and people. Accents become an entire form of exposition, as characters’ performative vernacular aims to identify them as English, Irish, or neither. Such details within the film’s mise-en-scène help further elaborate on the pervasive nature of the Irish borderscapes. As much as the Irish border is a material presence, the cross-community borderscape, embodied by everyday objects, spaces, and practices, dominates those inhabiting the border region. Not surprisingly, narratives told from the perspective of border Protestants are minimal. Apart from several noteworthy examples such as Heritage, King of the Castle (1977), and December Bride (1990), everyday life accounts are in the majority presented from a Catholic-Nationalist, and often Republican, point-of-view.

From the Southern side of the border, this translates into a series of narratives that engage with anxiety about how the war affects their daily life. Films such as High-Boot Benny and Breakfast on Pluto deal with conflict escalation and the stains of violence that increasingly spread over the border. While the UVF bombing of an Irish border town signifies the definitive death of innocence in Breakfast on Pluto, High-Boot Benny builds its narrative catharsis out of the extralegal actions of the British military in collaboration with loyalist paramilitaries. As such, the North is here treated as a hazardous zone from which sectarian violence follows an ebb and flow movement between the UVF and the IRA’s struggle for control. However, although the Northern side of the border undoubtedly saw more violence, most films foreground the South’s anxiety about conflict escalation. Or as the debate between the farmer Dan and his wife Molly in Your Man of Six Counties (1976) aptly summarises:

Dan: Ah, would you look at the lot of us Molly, 15 miles on the edge of a bloody chaos and we be sitin’ here amongst the chickens, clucking, scratching away at their own little piles of muck. Heads under our wings if we catch a sniff of gunpowder on the breeze.

Molly: That border says it it’s none of our business.

Dan: Before long Molly it won’t be just sniffs of powder we be getting. It’ll be rolling barrels of it and no border’s going to stop it.

Even today, the NI borderlands are tainted for many by the half-life of the conflict. Through narrative, the border becomes a lieu de mémoire for the NI conflict at large that discursively claims the South’s involvement and co-responsibility to the war in the North.61 However, it’s often not the conflict between both communities that is placed centre stage of the narrative. The ideological borderline that cuts the deepest in Irish border cinema lies within the Catholic-Nationalist community and the division between those who seek to reach Irish unity through violence and those who reject such paramilitary practices. Trapped, for example, abandons the revolutionary quest of its protagonist for the threat of the IRA hunting down its own operatives. On the one hand, borderlanders are structurally represented as everyday civilians caught in the crossfires and suffering because of it. On the other hand, the IRA is represented as deeply embedded in the IRA borderlands. The Run of the Country foregrounds the apolitical romantic quest of the main character yet contains a secondary narrative about his best friend’s IRA activities, which are only revealed once the friend passes away. The IRA’s presence is rather a ubiquitous absence62 where they’re not seen, but affectively felt in every frame. “Whatever you say, say nothing” indeed remains applicable, even in the more banal or everyday interactions between characters.

Similarly, most borderland characters are defined through their relation to the NI conflict and are put on a continuum of indifference and violent revolution. This political dilemma is often fought out through interpersonal conflict between two or more characters representing different positions. At the crux of Your Man, for example, lays the struggle between the embittered revolutionary Pat and the pacifist Danny, whom Pat describes as “all fart and no fury.” When a young child is placed under Pat’s custody, the conflict between both men intensifies and leads to a violent conflict between both. The Gentle Gunman transports such narratives to a Cain and Abel-like tale of three brothers: one IRA, one informer, and the youngest caught in the middle. Similarly, Trapped frames its central conflict as that between loyalty to one’s family and allegiance to the revolutionary cause. In this men-centred universe, women either appear as femme fatales motivating their lover to die for the county, or as mother figures who function as moral compass for violence-hungry men. Dramatic tension often comes from these stories when characters who want to remain neutral are forced to take sides and take up arms. In Volkswagen, Joe, an Irish car mechanic, is forced to plant a bomb in the old timer of a UDR customer. The turn from civilian to victim, perpetrator, or both is easy and often forms the narrative line that a film follows. Even though Volkswagen Joe shows examples of British occupations, it is the activities of the IRA that are presented as the actual threat to the film’s protagonist. Characters such as Joe attempt to step out of the cycle of violence yet are coerced to be a part of the fight, knowing full well the state reprisals that his actions will trigger. High Boot Benny, Mapmaker, and Your Man all use a child as the symbolic axis of the conflict to further heighten the corruptive forces of the borderlands. While High Boot Benny, the young Benny ultimately joins the IRA after loyalist paramilitaries assassinate his headmaster, the post-GFA politics of Mapmaker allows for the redemption of the child to signal a sense of hope for the borderlands. In High Boot Benny the film doesn’t glorify this call to arms though but treats it as a spiritual loss. Just one more boy playing the patriot game.

It’s not uncommon for the border in these films to be embodied by specific archetypes. The interaction of Irish borderlanders with the border itself happens under the guise of border patrolmen or the British military. As Nail notes, border guards are an effective part of the border; a human-like piece of brick in a structure designed to impose restrictions and exert power.63 With a shortage of other visual cues at the border, the military security personnel becomes the border’s personification and the primary shorthand signifier to visualise the passage into the North. Moreover, in most cases, this interaction is an effective tool that helps to elicit negative emotions of anger and indignation in the audience. When in contact with the military, the characters must undergo all sorts of abuse and discrimination, further signalling that there’s no room for an Irishman in the North. In Breakfast when Kitty crosses the border, a British soldier references the events of Bloody Sunday and how they’re happy to have “thirteen less to deal with.” Similarly, Trapped lingers on British checkpoint soldiers abusing the elderly and the pregnant (“I’d blow you away and still get a medal”) while the protagonist is powerless to intervene. As such, the filmmakers maintain a mood of moral indignation, which is then released through violent retribution or other emotional crescendos.64 To further alienate and “other” this military presence, these soldiers are often racialised. In contrast with the dominantly white casts of these films, the British soldiers in films such as Traveller and Good Vibrations are notably black. While this could be interpreted as an aspect of verisimilitude, the lingering camera that emphasises the faces of the black border guards does hint at a spectacularisation of this Black-ness, possibly to further sharpen the difference between the British imperial invader and the myth of an ethnically homogenous Ireland of the 1970s.

While the representation of the Northern borderlands is almost exclusively tied to conflict and trauma, there’s a far greater diversity in stories when crossing over to the South. This is partially thanks to the work of authors such as William Trevor, Jennifer Johnston, and Patrick McCabe, who chronicled Irish border life with keen eyes and tender spirits. Although the border is literally absent from films such as Roma (1979), Ballroom of Romance (1982), and Korea (1995), it merits to understand them as slices of borderland life. The Southern side of the border has a range of stories that allow to shift the focus to economic precarity, rural isolation, and culturally acknowledged national traumas such as the Civil War and scandals of the Catholic Church. The Northern borderland, in contrast, is often entirely reduced to the border’s vicinity, thus rendering NI as an arena of heightened drama and relentless violence. These differences are further emphasised when looking at the different regimes of mobility. While the Southern borderlander is shown to live in a state of heightened mobility, able to cross at will and make a good living through smuggling, the borderlands of the North are represented by a more deterministic disposition. This translates either into expulsion or a fatalistic fixity in which characters refuse to let them be chased out of their homes despite the threat of certain doom. Films such as Wildfire, Mapmaker, and Run of the County all position their central conflict as that of leaving or staying. The struggle to engage in the act of homemaking lies at the thematic heart of many of these border narratives. As Patterson points out, McCabe’s Victims trilogy perhaps offers the bleakest of such accounts by tackling the darkest period of the conflict.65 In all three films characters are repeatedly presented with the possibility to leave yet hold on to their land regardless of the consequences. The middle instalment, Heritage, is told from the perspective of border Protestants threatened by the IRA, the moral dilemma stems from leaving and having to live as a coward or staying and ending up assassinated. When the IRA does in fact attack, it’s almost met with a sense of relief (“How lucky, the dead ones”) since it puts an end to a life in terror. This pat-position between death and dishonour eventually drives the family’s son to suicide; a symbolic act that insinuates that death is the only way to escape the plight of the borderland.

(Re)visiting the border

Looking at the acts of border crossing and border inhabiting has helped to understand how filmmakers use the Irish border to generate narrative tension or tackle intimate accounts of the NI conflict. The mobility of (re)visiting allows for another thematic preoccupation, namely the paradoxical nature of homemaking for the Catholic-Nationalist community in NI. Narratives of confrontation and reconciliation are prevalent categories in Irish border representations. These films are structurally about individuals leaving their homes because of the violence and intimidation that held a tight grip on their surroundings. As Connelly and McIntoch state, the enforced displacement in NI was “without parallel in Europe since the Second World War.”66 Many families moved over the border to be reunited with family or start anew entirely, often leaving everything behind. Such efforts of homemaking were often more complex since the Northern Catholic-Nationalist identity remains a liminal one. Stuck between a nation lost and a nation forsaken, Catholic-Nationalists occupied a third space of their own in which they struggled to find an imagined community they could belong to. Aligning with these families’ histories, most of the films detailing such events are set at the Donegal-Derry border and follow a narrative orbit in which characters leave from North to South, only to be caught up with their past or forced to return. The act of returning here becomes a way to draw a contrast between South and North, as well as between the character’s idyllic fantasies and harsh realities.

After 68 (1993), for example, tells the story of the brutal crackdown against the Derry civil rights movement through the fragments a young girl picks up throughout her everyday life. Forbidden to set foot outside during the political unrest, all she knows about the violence on the streets is told through television coverage. However, an alternative story is read on the mother’s body, as she returns home from the Battle of the Bogside wounded and in need of assistance. Through the gradual changes in her mother’s physical and emotional state, she is exposed to a larger, more volatile, world, that increasingly drowns out girlhood anxieties such as romantic infatuations and school performances. Eventually, the mother and daughter decide to leave for West Donegal to start a new life away from the violence. In contrast to the romantic idyll, which is commonly used as a filter for the Donegal coast, After 68 presents a refuge that is tainted by a double loss: one for a home in NI and a home in the Republic. Clashing with the romanticised expectation of a return to the motherland, the old cottage in which mother and daughter stay is rundown and ghostlike. The Donegal landscape offers equally little solace with harsh winds and isolated spaces matching the sentiments of the family. Such a corrupted incarnation of the Irish motherland subtly hints that the escape into a romantic past of Irish unity too is an illusion. The leavers’ guilt mother and daughter experience while their community is under siege adds another layer of melancholia. When they choose to forsake their Irish dream and return to Derry after the death of the grandmother, the situation has escalated further with the fortification of the Bogside. Upon driving through the neighbourhood one last time, the girl sees her childhood crush clad in Provo combat wear. This act of revisiting has ushered in her definitive loss of innocence.

A similar narrative set-up can be found in Hush-a-bye Baby (1990). While the first act is centred on the playful experiences of a group of girls trying to get through high school and dancing carelessly to Cyndi Lauper’s “Girls Just Want To Have Fun” on the weekends, things take a turn for the worst when one of the girl’s lover is arrested as part of the Supergrass trials. Although the conflict was already brooding in the background, the girl becomes increasingly confronted with the military occupation; a harsh coming-of-age which is amplified by an unwanted pregnancy. Because she writes to her incarcerated lover in Irish, her letters never reach him, and she is left to deal with the situation by herself. A second narrative keystone comes when she decides to cross over the Donegal borderline to visit her grandmother and figure out what to do with her child. Grandma’s cottage is a vision of paradise lost. Speaking freely in Irish and making cottage pie, Donegal becomes the home the girl has longed for. However, since her reprise in the Donegal will inevitably end, the return to Derry taints the pastoral happiness. The Republic thus becomes an almost dreamlike entity, simultaneously right there over the border and unattainable to possess. Not surprisingly, such border escapes are often met with an air of melancholia.

Leaving and returning over the border can thus be considered a practice that promises escape, but not without its ambivalence. In Shadows on our Skin (1980), Grianán of Aileach is the setting for a rainy-day picnic for a young boy and the young woman that has become the object of his affection. This 6th-century stone monument, anglicised as Greenan Fort, is a famous site that lies just over the border with Donegal. While Derry life is fraught with political and social tension, the wide-open space surrounding Grianán of Aileach here provides a rare refuge from such struggles. Crossing over the border is equated with crossing over to simpler circumstances, allowing both main characters to reach a new level of intimacy despite their age difference. Moreover, for those who know its history, Grianán of Aileach offers additional symbolic weight. Once the Kingdom of Ailech’s seat of power from which its territories could be controlled, the fort is now a reminder of how nature outlives all borders. With the edge of this kingdom now long gone, a faint promise fills this scene that even the current border will once equally expire. However, this happiness turns out to be short-lived since the problems return upon arriving in Derry. When the boy’s older brother begins pursuing the girl romantically, the boy insinuates she’s engaged with a British soldier. This triggers a reprisal from the brother, who uses his IRA connections to break into the girl’s home and physically assault and humiliate her. Whatever glimpse of freedom was experienced over the border is again vanished in the North’s fog of war.

This pendulum of hope and disillusion is swung throughout many such films. Even when characters don’t return over the border, the conflict finds a way to intrude on their lives. In The Railway Station Man (1993), a woman tries to start anew over the border with her son after the RUC has shot her husband for mistakenly being a Provo. Here too, the lush landscapes, vibrant colours, and Celtic musical motifs signal a new chapter away from the dark grey, rain-soaked surroundings of Derry. Underlining her new life is the romantic relationship with a British war veteran, now working in an old train station post. Although safely removed from the conflict, the violence she sought to escape finds a way back into her life when her son starts to become affiliated with the IRA. Looking for an outpost to store explosives in the old railway station, the son inadvertently triggers the death of his mother’s new lover in an explosion equally Oedipal and anti-colonial. As the film closes on imagery of a burned-out car and its blackened debris, we’re led to revisit the precise scenes the mother sought to leave behind. Here, the border becomes a mnemonic echo of a violent past that has caught up with her. The determinism of such (re)visitings suggests that the North lingers on the skin of the Northern in a way that no crossing can negate.

In a post-peace agreement setting, the action of (re)visiting is tied to defamiliarising the border and revaluating NI as a post-conflict society. Like Mapmaker, Wildfire offers an intimate investigation of the border’s historical burden through the return of one of the borderland’s lost children. Here, protagonist Kelly (played with a self-referential touch by borderland boxing legend Barry McGuigan’s daughter, Danika) returns to her nameless borderland town up North after having left years ago because of the death of her parents. The town itself, an imaginary version of Newry in South-Armagh, is dominated by a haunted feel. Trauma lingers unresolved, people talk in hushed tones, and former paramilitaries walk free since the GFA release of political prisoners. The spell of repressive silence hanging over the border town is only broken with Kelly’s arrival. Bent on reconnecting with her sister, Kelly confronts both her and the nation’s demons which have been left unaddressed for too long. After an extended dance sequence in a local bar, both call out the IRA men responsible for placing the bomb that (accidentally) killed their father. The arrival of the borderland’s prodigal daughter is a necessary disruptive element in an NI that has sought to forget the many wounds of the conflict, as if but a bad dream. Parallels between the national and the individual are further emphasised by the Brexit iconography (protest graffiti, signs, news broadcasting) incorporated in the film’s on and off-screen space. Like Kelly’s return, the Brexit referendum confronted the fragile border regions with their painful past and precarious future. Even though the wounds of the past are slowly mending, the issue of the border remains a sensitive issue brooding with violent potentiality. Wildfire’s commitment to confronting the past seems to insinuate that only by working through the sectarian divide and its history of violence a form of resolution can be achieved. Here the border becomes a reconciliatory nexus in which the rupture between past and present gets sewn together and the wounds between communities healed.

Conclusion

Back in 1937, Blarney expressed its desire for the border to “vanish one of these days before the power of this: a friendly shaking of Irish hands.” Decades later the border still rests uneasily on the Irish island. Contentious and troubled as it may be, its symbolic position is perhaps as diffuse as its porous geography. Through the tides of time and the weaving of stories, the border cannot merely be reduced to the scar of partition, but can instead be described in equal parts as a bridge connecting communities67 and a flashpoint that has the leverage to reignite the conflict.68 In cinema too, the border can be understood as a mercurial and multi-faceted entity capable of being utilised for various commercial, aesthetic, and political ends. This article attempted to establish a poetics of the Irish border by focusing on various regimes of border mobilities and their narrative, formal, and ideological underpinnings. Our border film archive helped to provide a comprehensive overview of the border as a thematic tool that filmmakers wield for different aims and ends. Border crossings such as smuggling are relied on for dramatic scenarios and narrative complications. The borderline hence becomes a symbolic demarcation that provides new situations or a shift in representational registers. Border inhabiting departs from borderlander narratives of displacement and fixity to outline the divides between the border’s combative communities, in the process marking out the borderlands as a battleground that extends the NI conflict to the Republic. Lastly, border (re)visiting, introduces and complicates the notion of an Irish homeland by dramatising NI borderlanders’ escape and subsequent return over the border. In a more contemporary setting, such (re)visiting can equally lead to a confrontation with NI’s violent past.

At the heart of this inquiry lies the complex position of landscape and location in cinema. Following Tuan, the border can be considered somewhere between abstract space mined for its metaphorical potential, and a particularised place that chronicles stories about its community.69 Because of the border’s heightened symbolism, every type of border representation can also be read as deeply political. By appealing to different forms of border mobilities, filmmakers not only lament partition, commemorate lives lost, and problematise the border’s burden on communities, but their films can also engage in critical forms of border work that defamiliarise the legitimacy of nation states at large. However, Irish border cinema also has its blind spots. Because of its more sensational episodes, the more everyday and banal aspects of border life are often omitted from border representation. As Nash and Reid mark out, for many people the border is used to shop for cheaper groceries and visit friends. In these seldomly screened acts of everyday life, the border is crossed by locals to the extent that it’s often forgotten.70 A Bump Along the Way (2019), for example, offers a break with existing Derry representation by omitting all aspects relating to the border and the afterlife of the conflict. Despectacularising the lives of borderlanders and their mundane interaction with the border can be seen as a representational step towards softening the sense of division and helping to cinematically turn the borderland into a lived space rather than a mythical entity stuck in a timeless void of threat and trauma.

Moreover, the preoccupation with Catholic and Protestant communities at the borderland has arguably led to the exclusion of other perspectives. As Cashman71 notes, the tight focus on inter-group antagonisms excludes examples of cross-community cooperation and matching acts of solidarity. Similarly, other religious denominations, ethnic groups, and sexual orientations have been structurally overlooked in the cinematic history of the border. While films such as Traveller, Mapmaker, Wildfire, and Turf (2021) do try to include perspectives of travellers, Quakers and migrant communities inhabiting the borderlands, there are many other stories to be told and perspectives to be offered. One thing stands certain: be it material or imagined, hard or soft, the border remains unfinished business.
