Silent burden of gynecological morbidities among rural women in Southern India: Evidence from a cancer screening center
Authors: Zari Anjum, Vibha Swaroop, Neha Singh, Suma Nair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Gynecological morbidities contribute significantly to the global burden of disease, yet remain underreported and undertreated, particularly among rural women in India. There is Limited evidence existing on gynecological morbidity as most studies rely on self-reported symptoms. Hence a cross-sectional study design has been used here to study the same.Objectives:To assess the prevalence and determinants of gynecological morbidities among women attending a community-based cancer screening center in rural Southern India.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted among 599 ever-married women attending a community cancer screening center in rural Karnataka. Data were collected using a pre-tested semi-structured questionnaire. Clinical examination, Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA), and Pap smear were performed. Data were analyzed using SPSS. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with reproductive morbidities.Results:Only 20.3% of women reported at least one gynecological symptom, whereas clinical and cytological assessment revealed a substantially higher burden of morbidity. White vaginal discharge was the most common symptom (13.4%), while cervical infections were detected in 53.9% of participants. Uterine prolapse was observed in 8.3% of women. Eighty percent of symptomatic women had not sought prior medical care. Increasing age was associated with lower odds of cervical infection (OR = 0.56, P = 0.007) but higher odds of prolapse (OR = 2.14, P = 0.012). Illiteracy and higher parity were significantly associated with uterine prolapse.Conclusion:A substantial proportion of gynecological morbidity remains unrecognized due to low symptom reporting and poor health-seeking behavior. Integrating routine screening and targeted awareness interventions into primary healthcare services is essential to address this hidden burden.

Introduction

India has made substantial progress in reducing maternal mortality in recent decades. Several measures have been taken to reduce the maternal mortality rate in response to the UN Millennium Development Goal 5, and the rate has decreased from 556 to 174.[1] Gynecological diseases contribute approximately 4.5% of the global disease burden, exceeding the burden posed by several major conditions, including malaria, tuberculosis, ischemic heart disease, and maternal disorders.[2] However, gynecologic morbidities and reproductive tract infections (RTIs) remain highly prevalent and are often underreported in India and other developing regions. Recent national data from NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 indicate that the prevalence of self-reported gynecological morbidities in India increased from 25.68% to 28.74%, with certain eastern states reporting even higher burden.[3]

In developing nations, the prevalence of specific illnesses and diseases are particularly difficult to assess due to poor health seeking behavior and inadequate screening methods. This issue is amplified in reproductive-age women of lower socioeconomic status. Previous studies have shown that although rural Indian women have a high prevalence of RTIs, majority do not seek medical help for these health issues.[4] Women tend to not approach health facilities due to a lack of social awareness or decision-making power and a hesitation towards getting gynecologic illnesses treated at health care facilities.[56] National-level evidence further suggests that only around 35–38% of women reporting gynecological morbidities seek treatment, with a greater preference for private facilities.[3]

In 1990, the burden of reproductive illness was highest in India at 12.5% according to “The Global Burden of Disease” report.[7] As per an updated study in 2004, South East Asia that includes India, contributed to 38.4% of the total disability adjusted life years (DALYs) for cervical cancer and to 42.7% of the total DALYs for chlamydia, having the highest percentage contribution of all regions in both cases.[8] Contemporary facility-based studies continue to report a high burden of gynecological morbidity, ranging from 61.2% in a Delhi outpatient setting to 81.7% in rural Kerala.[910] Commonly reported morbidities include dysmenorrhea, irregular menstruation, abnormal vaginal discharge, and lower abdominal pain.

Adolescents constitute another vulnerable group. A hospital-based study among 668 adolescent girls reported that 62.6% presented with menstrual disorders, with menorrhagia (30.38%), oligomenorrhoea (26.07%), and amenorrhea (21.77%) being the most common complaints.[11] Similarly, a study among adolescents in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh reported that nearly one-fourth experienced gynecological morbidities, yet only about one-third sought treatment.[12]

Studies have also shown illiteracy and low socioeconomic status to be important predictors of reproductive morbidities.[13] More recent NFHS-based analyses have demonstrated that education, wealth index, age at marriage, age at first birth, working status, menstrual hygiene practices, and awareness regarding sexually transmitted infections significantly influence both occurrence of gynecological morbidities and treatment-seeking behavior.[3]

However, most existing studies in India rely predominantly on self-reported symptoms that may underestimate true morbidity. Furthermore, while both community-based and facility-based studies report a high burden of gynecological morbidity, there is limited evidence integrating symptom reporting with clinical and cytological assessment, especially in rural settings of relatively developed regions like Karnataka. Therefore, this study attempts to map the burden of gynecological morbidities and their correlates among women attending a community cancer screening clinic in rural coastal Karnataka, one of the better-performing regions in India in terms of development indices. This population-based study provides insight into the unmet reproductive health needs of women in this setting.

Materials and Methods

This was a cross-sectional study carried out among women attending a community cancer screening facility covering rural and semi-urban population of Udupi taluk, a coastal region in the state of Karnataka, Southern India.

Cancer screening services for commonly occurring cancers of the breast, cervix, and oral cavity are provided through this community-based facility. No sampling technique was applied. This approach is like other facility-based cross-sectional studies assessing gynecological morbidity of the 630 ever married women who attended the screening facility over a period of 24 months, 599 women consented to participate in the study. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) and in accordance with the IEC protocol, a written informed consent was obtained prior to participation from the consenting women.

All women ever married belonging to the field practice area and consenting for the screening were involved in the study. Women not giving consent were excluded from the study.

A pre-tested semi-structured questionnaire was administered to participants to acquire socio-demographic information and reproductive history, including contraception use and personal hygiene practices. Socio-economic status was categorized using modified Udai-Pareek scale.[9]

As part of anthropometric measurements, weight was recorded using a calibrated standard electronic weighing scale, and height was measured to the nearest centimeter using a wall-mounted stadiometer. Body mass index (BMI) was categorized using the international WHO criteria.[10] Study participants were subjected to per speculum examination and Pap smears were obtained on sterile slides after removing mucus plug if present and were immediately fixed. This was followed by Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA) as a part of the screening protocol. VIA was performed by applying freshly prepared 3% acetic acid to the cervix and observing the cervix after one minute. Clinical examination and VIA screening were performed by trained healthcare professionals following standard protocols. Results of VIA were noted and reported in real time. Additionally, certain clinical data, such as self-reported gynecologic symptoms were documented. Pap smear slides were transported to the tertiary care facility and reported by an experienced pathologist. Identities of the participants remained confidential, and all corresponding data was linked to each participant by a unique code number.

Data was processed and analyzed using SPSS version 22.0. Categorical data was summarized as percentages and continuous data was summarized using means and standard deviations. Multiple logistic regression analysis was used to study the association between various reproductive morbidities and risk factors. The strength of association was presented as odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals. Association was considered significant at P < 0.05 level.

Of the 599 consenting participants, majority (68.0%) of participants were homemakers and more than half (54.4%) had a low socio-economic status. While 52% had completed at least ten years of schooling, 29% were either illiterate or school dropouts. More than half the women (52.7%) were malnourished of which almost 46.5% were observed to be either overweight or obese, while 6.2% were undernourished.

Table 3 illustrates the self-reported symptoms and clinical findings. Only 20.3% of women presented with at least one gynecological symptom, Common complaints were white vaginal discharge (13.4%) and lower backache (7.2%); post-coital bleeding (1.5%) and genital lesions (1.8%) were less frequent. Notably, 18% of symptomatic women had never sought prior medical care for their symptoms. On pelvic examination, the most common finding was white vaginal discharge (28.8%). Uterine prolapse of any degree was found in 8.3% of women, and cervical polyps in 3.5%; adnexal masses (ovarian cysts) were noted in 3.2%. VIA was positive in 11.2% of women. Cytological examination (Pap smear) showed that 54% of women had evidence of cervical infection (such as bacterial vaginosis, candidiasis, or Trichomonas) in the smear. Inflammatory changes without specific infection were present in 22.7%, and atrophic smears in 15.7%. Four women (0.7%) had high-grade cervical lesions (HSIL) on Pap smear.

Comparing symptomatic and asymptomatic women, 45.6% of those with self-reported symptoms had an “unhealthy” cervix on exam, versus 32% of asymptomatic women (P = 0.017). Similarly, 11.2% of symptomatic women were clinically diagnosed with suspected infection on examination, compared to 5.2% of asymptomatic women (P = 0.035). These differences suggest that large burden of reproductive tract infection remains undetected in women who do not report symptoms, possibly due to poor awareness about symptom or hesitant to disclose any gynecological issues.

Analysis of factors associated with morbidity revealed certain notable patterns. Higher educational and occupational status were paradoxically related to higher reported infection rates. Interestingly, 39.6% of the women who have had more than 10 years of schooling were reported to have white discharge, than those with lower education (21.2% in illiterate women, P = 0.004). This same trend was seen upon analyzing occupation as a factor for reproductive morbidity: Inflammatory Pap smears were found in 54.5% of white-collar workers but only 19.8% of housewives (P = 0.011), this possibly reflecting greater health awareness and reporting among the educated.

These findings suggest increased burden of reproductive infection in women with higher education and occupation levels, and that might be due to better awareness among the literate women. Another factor contributing here could be lack of proper toilet facilities at most of the workplaces of unorganized sectors in India.

An association was found between Oral Contraceptive Pill (OCP) use and abnormal Pap smear findings in this population: 51.3% of OCP users had infection on Pap smear versus 35.5% of non-users (P = 0.048). Women using OCPs also had higher rates of bacterial vaginosis (15.6% vs 4.7%, P = 0.004) and mixed cervical infections (5.1% vs 0.7%, P = 0.009). The observed associations indicates that women taking birth control pills in this population are more susceptible to cervical infections.

It was also found that 54.5% of women with cervical infections were in the age group of <30 years and the proportion decreases as age increased (P = 0.001). In contrast, uterine prolapse was more frequent in older women (P = 0.005). Logistic regression [Table 4] confirmed these trends. Women aged ≥50 years had significantly lower odds of cervical infection (OR = 0.56, 95% CI 0.38–0.86, P = 0.007) but had higher odds of prolapse (OR = 2.14, 95% CI 1.17–3.93, P = 0.012) than younger women. Education (Illiteracy) was strongly associated with prolapse (OR = 2.66, 95% CI 1.48–4.78, P = 0.001) but not with infection. High parity (≥3 children) had also increased odds of prolapse (OR = 1.91, 95% CI 1.05–3.47, P = 0.031). Early menarche (≤11 years) was also associated with a lower risk of uterine polyps (OR = 0.19, 95% CI 0.05–0.71, P = 0.003). Socioeconomic status and religion showed no significant associations with the concerned morbidities studied.

Table 4 summarizes the factors associated with various gynecologic morbidities in this study. Socio-economic status, in this sample of women, was skewed towards the low and middle categories, with several women falling a few points from the cut-off value for the “middle” status. Therefore, the socio-economic status was not significantly associated with any reproductive morbidity. Religion, in most cases, was also not correlated with any type of reproductive morbidity, most likely due to the small number of Muslims and Christians in the sample. However, weight did have effects on the type and prevalence of reproductive morbidities. Almost half (43%) of underweight women had white discharge, compared to 33.02% of normal-weight women, 23% of overweight women, and 26% of obese women (P = 0.022). This trend is likely due to the contribution of individual’s diet and nutrition on their immunity status and, in turn, reproductive infection rates.

VIA and Pap smear results for cervical lesions were compared. For the purposes of this study, a Pap smear result was considered positive if the result indicated high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL). As seen in the study, out of the 67 women positive for VIA, 3 women were found to have a positive Pap smear result, suggestive of high-grade precancerous lesions. Of those 497 women with negative VIA results one woman was diagnosed with HSIL on her Pap test (P < 0.006). Women with positive VIA results had 23 times higher chances of getting detected with pre-cancerous lesions of the cervix or cancer cervix.

The specificity and sensitivity of this test were determined, using this study’s results. It was observed that the ability of VIA test to detect pre-cancerous lesions of the cervix was 75.0% among the women with positive Pap smear finding, and its ability to identify women without lesions was 88.6% among those who were negative on Pap test.

The Pap smear had a positive likelihood ratio of 6.6 for detecting malignancy in affected women (i.e., 6.6 times more likely to be positive in those with HSIL) and a negative likelihood ratio of 0.28 (1/3.5) for ruling out disease in healthy women.

Discussion

This study demonstrated a substantial burden of previously unrecognized gynecological morbidity among women attending a rural cancer screening clinic in Karnataka. Although only 20.3% of women self-reported symptoms, clinical and cytological evaluation revealed much higher morbidity, highlighting the silent nature of reproductive health problems in rural women.

The proportion of women self-reporting symptoms (20.3%) in our study was lower compared to several community-based studies conducted in India. Studies from Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Hubli (Karnataka), Nanded, Delhi urban slums, Rajkot, Punjab, and Maharashtra have reported prevalence ranging from 33% to 88%.[1213141516171819] The Kerala study by Abraham et al.[12] reported high reproductive morbidity with significant menstrual and infection-related complaints. Similarly, Mani et al.[13] in Tamil Nadu documented a high prevalence of reproductive tract infections among rural married women. The Hubli study by Balamurugan et al.[14] reported substantial RTI burden among women in urban Karnataka. These differences may reflect variation in study settings, diagnostic criteria, and healthcare access, as our population included asymptomatic women attending a screening facility rather than symptom-driven clinic attendees.

Excessive white vaginal discharge (13.4%) was the most reported symptom in our study, followed by low backache (7.2%). Vaginal discharge has consistently been identified as the most common complaint in earlier studies conducted in Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Delhi slums, and Punjab.[12131618] However, the prevalence of discharge in our study was lower compared to the 23–58% reported in those studies. This discrepancy may be due to underreporting, sociocultural inhibition, or differences in awareness and perception of symptoms.

Clinically, 50% of women in our study were found to have reproductive morbidities, which is comparable to the 40–80% prevalence reported across various Indian community-based studies.[151617] The Delhi urban study described reproductive morbidity as a “silent epidemic,” emphasizing that many women suffer without seeking care.[20] This aligns closely with our finding that 80% of symptomatic women had never sought prior treatment.

Pap smear findings in our study revealed cervical infection in 5% of women, with candidiasis present in 11%. Previous Indian studies have reported varying prevalence of candidiasis, ranging from as low as 0.6–4.2% to as high as 16% or even 53%.[142122] Such variability may be attributable to differences in laboratory methods, rural–urban variations, hygiene practices, and age distribution of study populations. The relatively lower candidiasis rate in our study could also be explained by the higher proportion of peri- and post-menopausal women, as infection rates decline with age.

The prevalence of uterine prolapse (8.4%) in our study is like findings from North India, where self-reported prolapse was documented at 7–8%.[23] Structural morbidities such as prolapse continue to be an important yet neglected component of women’s reproductive health, especially in multiparous rural women.

An important observation in our study was the discrepancy between self-reported morbidity (20.3%) and clinically detected morbidity (50–59%). This gap highlights poor symptom recognition, normalization of discomfort, and sociocultural barriers to disclosure. Studies from Rajkot and urban slums have shown that untreated reproductive morbidities are strongly influenced by socioeconomic class, distance to health facilities, provider attitudes, and perceived quality of care.[17] The reluctance to seek care has also been attributed to stigma, shyness, financial constraints, and low decision-making autonomy.[121620]

The fact that 80% of symptomatic women had not sought care reinforces the findings from earlier studies across India that documented low treatment-seeking behavior despite significant morbidity.[121518] Women often consider symptoms such as discharge or dysmenorrhea as “normal,” opting for home remedies rather than professional consultation.[18] This underutilization of services perpetuates chronic infection, pelvic inflammatory disease, infertility, and long-term reproductive consequences.

Despite 70.7% literacy in our study population, healthcare-seeking behavior remained poor. This suggests that literacy alone may not translate into empowerment or health awareness. Cultural norms, gender roles, stigma associated with gynecological illness, and prioritization of family health over personal health continue to act as barriers.[1216]

This study highlights a substantial burden of unrecognized gynecological morbidity among women residing in rural area, with a marked gap between self-reported symptoms and clinically detected conditions. The use of clinical examination and cytological assessment strengthens the validity of findings. The findings reinforce existing evidence that reproductive morbidities are often underreported and undertreated, which indicates the need for integrating routine screening and awareness programs into primary healthcare.

Overall, our findings corroborate the consistent evidence across Indian states that gynecological morbidity remains highly prevalent, frequently underreported, and inadequately treated. Even in relatively better-performing states such as Karnataka, silent reproductive morbidity persists. Therefore, Future research should focus on longitudinal designs, behavioral determinants, and evaluation of integrated screening approaches.

Conclusion

Even though large proportion of women in the study group were literate, the study showed a high prevalence of unreported reproductive morbidities among the participants, indicating a societal barrier rather than a comprehension barrier to accessing reproductive health information. However, this finding may reflect greater awareness and reporting among educated women, rather than a true increase in morbidity amounting to reporting bias. Hence, Regular health education campaigns tailored to local cultural context are needed to raise awareness of reproductive health issues. However, solely understanding the factors contributing to specific morbidities in this population is not sufficient in instituting such an intervention. Education and outreach are necessary in both the female and male cohorts to decrease the stigma and increase the understanding regarding reproductive morbidities. Such efforts should empower women (and men) to recognize symptoms early and overcome stigma, enabling timely prevention and treatment of gynecological disease. Additionally, as this study is based on a cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be established, and the findings should be interpreted with this limitation in mind.
