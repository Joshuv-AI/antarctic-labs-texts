Gender gap in the timing of board certification and designated mental health physician qualification among intermediate psychiatrists in Japan
Authors: Yasuko Fuse‐Nagase, Shunsuke Takagi
Published: 2026

Career development and life planning often conflict, and this burden has historically fallen disproportionately on women. 1 In Japan, female physicians increased from 13.4% in 1994 to 20.4% in 2014, and among physicians younger than 45 years, from 13.8% in 1994 to 30.8% in 2016. 2 , 3 These trends may suggest that the gender gap in medicine is narrowing. Nevertheless, women remain underrepresented in senior academic positions, accounting for only 4.7% of professors in Japanese medical schools. 4 Thus, despite more women entering medicine, progression to leadership positions remains limited. Japanese policy has addressed this imbalance. The Fifth Basic Plan for Gender Equality promotes appointing women to leadership and senior academic positions, including professors, and universities may implement positive action measures, including women‐only recruitment, to correct substantial underrepresentation. 5 , 6 Similarly, in psychiatry, where women accounted for 28.4% of psychiatrists working in medical facilities in 2024. 7

If women are increasing in medicine and psychiatry and leadership policies are in place, barriers may remain between entry and eventual leadership. In psychiatry, board certification, qualification as a designated mental health physician (DMHP), a doctoral degree, and supervising psychiatrist certification may represent such milestones. Board certification is granted to psychiatrists who demonstrate the requisite knowledge and clinical experience. The DMHP qualification, a government‐certified legal credential, authorizes psychiatrists to make determinations regarding involuntary hospitalization and patient behavioral restrictions under the Mental Health and Welfare Act. Consequently, these qualifications are significant for Japanese psychiatrists.

Both board certification and qualification as a DMHP require substantial preparation, including clinical experience, case documentation, and other formal requirements, although the detailed criteria differ. Eligibility arises after at least 5 years of clinical experience, a period that often coincides with childbirth and childcare. Although some accommodation measures exist for board certification, qualification delay from childbirth and childcare may still accumulate disproportionately among women. These milestones may therefore represent a point where career development and life planning come into direct conflict.

We conducted a preliminary survey of psychiatrists affiliated with the Department of Psychiatry and Behavioral Sciences, Institute of Science Tokyo, who had 5–15 years of clinical experience after medical licensure, to examine whether application for these qualifications was delayed by childbirth or childcare and whether such delay differed between women and men (Ibaraki University Ethics Committee approval no. 25P3900). Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Participants were asked whether they had applied for board certification after 5 years of clinical experience. Those who answered “no” were asked whether the delay was related to childbirth (their own or their partner's) or childcare. Similar questions were asked regarding qualification as a DMHP.

Responses were obtained from 15 women and 7 men, out of 35 eligible women and 36 eligible men. Among the women, four reported delaying application for board certification because of both childbirth and childcare; three of them also reported delaying application for qualification as a DMHP for the same reasons. In contrast, none of the men reported delaying application because of their partner's childbirth or childcare responsibilities.

These results point to a gendered disparity at an intermediate career stage, although the small number of male respondents and potential non‐response bias warrant caution. Childbirth‐ and childcare‐related delay was reported only by women. This pattern may reflect both childbirth‐related constraints and gender disparities in childcare responsibilities.

Existing accommodations for childbirth and childcare in the psychiatry board‐certification system may be insufficient. Under the current system in Japan, interruption for pregnancy, childbirth, or childcare can be treated as a “specific reason,” and up to 6 months of interruption may be allowed without extension of the training period if required cases and competencies are subsequently fulfilled. However, if such interruption is borne predominantly by women, these provisions may function less to equalize career progression than to legitimize delayed qualification among women. A system designed to reduce disadvantage may therefore inadvertently allow timing disparities to persist unless broader measures reduce the gendered concentration of childcare‐related interruption.

Similar accommodation should also be considered for qualification as a DMHP. Support for childbirth and childcare should not be limited to permitting interruption. The UK experience may be informative: in the United Kingdom, postgraduate training allows less‐than‐full‐time training and structured return support after extended absence. 8 This suggests that more effective support may require not only allowance for time out of training but also measures that sustain continuity and facilitate re‐entry. Our findings therefore suggest that support in Japan should include flexible training pathways and structured return support, so that childbirth‐ and childcare‐related delays do not accumulate disproportionately among women. Such measures may help ensure that increasing numbers of women entering psychiatry can progress equitably toward leadership roles.

This was a preliminary study conducted at a single institution with a small sample size. However, these results may not represent nationwide trends. Additionally, the study did not investigate subsequent career outcomes. The relationship between delays in acquiring qualifications and career progression remains unclear. Future research should address whether delayed qualification acquisition results in measurable career disadvantages.
