Cholesterol accumulation in monocytes is associated with the severity of diabetic complications and the efficacy of glycemic control interventions in patients with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Hideyuki Okuma, Takahiro Tsutsumi, Asako Miyazaki, Kyoichiro Tsuchiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionMonocyte dysfunction is central to the pathogenesis of diabetes. This study investigated the association between cholesterol accumulation in monocytes and diabetic complications as well as post‐discharge glycemic control.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective two‐part study. Study 1, a cross‐sectional study, included 40 patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus to assess the associations between monocyte cholesterol levels and diabetic complication indices. Study 2, a longitudinal study, included 19 patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus hospitalized for an approximately 2‐week educational program. Changes in monocyte cholesterol levels from admission to discharge were compared between two groups: those whose hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) increased 3 months after discharge (HbA1c elevated group; n = 10) and those whose HbA1c decreased or remained stable (HbA1c non‐elevated group; n = 9).ResultsIn Study 1, monocyte cholesterol levels correlated with the severity of diabetic complications, including carotid intima‐media thickness, the fibrosis‐4 index, and retinopathy stage. In Study 2, during hospitalization, no significant change in monocyte cholesterol levels was observed in all patients, while a significant decrease was demonstrated in the HbA1c non‐elevated group.ConclusionsMonocytic cholesterol accumulation is associated with the severity of diverse diabetic complications and predicts the risk of glycemic deterioration after discharge. These findings suggest that monocytic cholesterol could serve as a novel translational biomarker for ‘metabolic memory’ and therapeutic resistance in type 2 diabetes mellitus.Keywords: Diabetic complications, Monocyte cholesterol, Type 2 diabetes mellitus

INTRODUCTION

The most crucial challenge in the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus is preventing the onset and progression of chronic complications, such as atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and diabetic microvascular complications [1, 2]. Currently, organ‐specific indicators like maximum intima‐media thickness (max IMT) and fundus findings are used to evaluate individual complications [3, 4]. However, a biomarker that can comprehensively and collectively assess the degree of chronic complication progression has not been sufficiently established [1].

Chronic inflammation is a common underlying basis for these diabetic complications, and the involvement of dysfunction in monocyte–macrophage systems has been strongly implicated [5, 6, 7, 8]. Multiple studies have shown that the hyperglycemic environment adversely affects both cholesterol uptake and efflux in monocyte macrophages [6, 9, 10]. Consequently, the excessive accumulation of cholesterol forms foam cells within macrophages, which then release potent inflammatory cytokines, inducing systemic chronic inflammation [6, 11]. This suggests that cholesterol accumulation in monocytes may be an important indicator reflecting the pathogenesis of diabetes.

Nevertheless, there have been no comprehensive reports investigating the association between cholesterol accumulation in monocytes and the full spectrum of systemic complications driven by hyperglycemia and inflammation, including macrovascular and microvascular complications, and the recently recognized metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH). Furthermore, the impact of the comprehensive glycemic control intervention such as educational admission on cholesterol accumulation in monocytes has been rarely investigated [1].

Therefore, this study aims to cross‐sectionally investigate the association between cholesterol accumulation in peripheral blood monocytes and various diabetic complications as a primary endpoint. Additionally, we aim to establish the clinical utility of cholesterol accumulation in monocytes by examining its changes in response to glycemic control achieved through educational admission.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This retrospective study, consisting of Study 1 and Study 2, was conducted using data and preserved samples collected in a previous study [12]. In Study 1, a cross‐sectional study, we included 40 patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. The inclusion criteria for patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus were age ≥ 20 years and a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus based on the criteria of the Japan Diabetes Society. Patients with severe acute illness or serious complications that markedly limit life expectancy were excluded. Among the patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus in Study 1, 19 individuals who consented to educational admission (approximately 2 weeks) were included in Study 2, which is a longitudinal study. Based on whether HbA1c levels increased 3 months after discharge, participants were classified into two groups: HbA1c elevated group (n = 10) whose HbA1c increased compared to admission and HbA1c non‐elevated group (n = 9) whose HbA1c decreased or remained stable. This study was conducted at the Faculty of Medicine, University of Yamanashi, and at Kua‐house Isawa, Isawa Hot Spring Hospital, in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committees of the University of Yamanashi (No. 3063). Informed consent was obtained through an opt‐out approach disclosed on the institutional website.

Clinical data collection

In Study 1, we analyzed demographics including age, sex, duration of diabetes, smoking status, and body mass index (BMI). Physiological measurements included systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP), ankle‐brachial index (ABI), brachial‐ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV), and max IMT measured by carotid ultrasonography. We also assessed current medications and laboratory parameters. As part of routine clinical care, parameters were measured: fasting plasma glucose (FPG), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), serum insulin, triglycerides (TG), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma‐glutamyl transferase (γ‐GTP), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR).

Blood samples were collected in the morning after an overnight fast of at least 12 h. Residual blood samples were processed to separate serum and monocytes, which were then stored at −80°C. Using these stored samples, we evaluated serum high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (hsCRP), advanced glycation end‐products (AGEs), and monocyte cholesterol levels. Serum hsCRP was quantified using an immunoturbidimetric assay (Kamiya Biomedical Co., Seattle, WA, USA), and serum AGEs were measured using an enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Cell Biolabs Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturers' protocols. The isolation of monocytes and measurement of monocyte cholesterol levels are described in the subsequent sections.

To assess diabetic complications, macrovascular involvement was evaluated using ABI, baPWV, and max IMT. As an indicator of liver fibrosis in metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH), the fibrosis‐4 (FIB‐4) index was calculated based on age, AST, ALT, and platelet count. The severity of diabetic retinopathy was evaluated by ophthalmologists and classified according to the International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy Severity Scale [13]. In Study 2, all laboratory parameters analyzed in Study 1, except for HbA1c, along with monocyte cholesterol levels, were measured at admission and discharge. HbA1c levels were measured at admission and at a 3‐month follow‐up visit after discharge.

Monocyte isolation

Approximately 25 mL of an early‐morning fasting anticoagulated blood sample (K‐EDTA) was diluted with an equal volume of lymphocyte separation solution (Nakalai Tesque, Japan) and carefully layered into a 50‐mL Falcon tube (Costar, Corning, NY, USA). Samples were centrifuged at 400 × g in a swing‐out rotor for 30 min at 22°C, and the PBMC layer was harvested using a pipette. PBMCs were repeatedly washed with Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) containing 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The PBMCs were cultured on collagen‐coated multi‐well plates in DMEM with 1% P/S containing 10% FBS for 2 h in an incubator. Next, the PBMCs were washed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) to remove lymphocytes [14, 15]. Monocytes were then replaced with DMEM containing 0.25% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and starved for 2 h in an incubator, leading to the downstream assays including the analyses of monocyte purity and monocyte cholesterol levels. Monocyte purity was verified using a FACSCelesta (Becton, Dickinson and Company, NJ, USA) and a phycoerythrin (PE)‐conjugated anti‐human CD14 Antibody (BioLegend, CA, USA) in our previous report [12].

Measurement of monocyte cholesterol levels

Total cholesterol levels in monocytes were measured using Cholesterol/Cholesterol Ester‐Glo™ Assay (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA). Specifically, preserved monocyte samples were seeded into 96‐well plates and cultured for 24–72 h or until reaching the desired confluence. After removing the culture medium, cells were washed twice with 100 μL of PBS. Subsequently, 50 μL of cholesterol lysis solution was added to each well. After briefly shaking, each well was incubated for 30 min at 37°C. Cholesterol level was determined by reading luminescence after 1 h incubation with 50 μL of cholesterol detection reagent with cholesterol esterase reagents at room temperature. Cholesterol concentration was extrapolated from a standard curve and normalized to protein concentration.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as median (25th–75th percentiles) or mean ± standard deviation. For comparisons between two groups, the Mann–Whitney U‐test or Student's t‐test was used, and for comparisons among three groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. For comparisons before and after hospitalization in the intervention study, the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was employed. Differences in the frequency of HbA1c increase were assessed using Fisher's exact test. Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed using log‐transformed variables for those with non‐normal distributions to meet the assumption of normality. Multicollinearity was assessed by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF), and we confirmed that all VIF values were less than 5. P‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0 and GraphPad Prism version 11.0.0 (93).

RESULTS

Correlation between monocyte cholesterol accumulation and severity of diabetic complications

In Study 1, the 40 participants had a median age of 64.5 (50.0–71.3) years, and 50% were female. The cohort was characterized by a median BMI of 25.5 (22.2–31.8) kg/m2, a diabetes duration of 8.0 (1.0–15.5) years, and an HbA1c level of 8.4% (7.7–11.5%) (Table 1). Monocyte cholesterol levels in the type 2 diabetes mellitus group (n = 40) showed a consistent and significant positive correlation with various indicators of diabetic complications (Figure 1). Specifically, it demonstrated a significant positive correlation with the macrovascular indicator max IMT, the hepatic fibrosis indicator FIB‐4 index (all P < 0.01; Figure 1a,b). Furthermore, multivariable regression analysis demonstrated that cholesterol accumulation in monocytes was significantly associated with both max IMT and the FIB‐4 index, even after adjustment for (1) age, sex, and BMI (with age excluded in the analysis of the FIB‐4 index); (2) systolic blood pressure (SBP) and HbA1c; and (3) LDL‐C, statin use, and smoking status as separate sets of covariates (Table 2). Regarding retinopathy, the subjects included 21 patients with nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy (NDR), 11 with simple diabetic retinopathy (SDR), and 8 with pre‐proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PPDR); no patients had proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR). Monocyte cholesterol levels increased progressively with the severity of diabetic retinopathy (Kruskal–Wallis: P < 0.01; Figure 1c). A positive trend was observed for hs‐CRP (Spearman's ρ = 0.301, P = 0.059), whereas no significant correlations were found between monocyte cholesterol and ABI, baPWV, LDL‐C, or HbA1c (Table S1).

Intervention effect of educational admission and prediction of glycemic control

For Study 2, a longitudinal study, the baseline characteristics of the 19 participants are summarized in Table 3. Briefly, the mean age was 66.6 ± 11.0 years, and 58% were female. The cohort had a mean BMI of 25.2 ± 6.0 kg/m2 and a mean diabetes duration of 12.5 ± 9.5 years, with a median HbA1c of 8.4% (7.5–12.6%) (Table 3). In this cohort receiving educational admission, monocyte cholesterol levels showed no significant change from admission to discharge (Figure 2a). However, the stratification analysis based on post‐discharge glycemic prognosis showed that the HbA1c non‐elevated group (n = 9) showed a significant decrease in monocyte cholesterol levels during hospitalization (Figure 2b), while this decrease was not observed in the HbA1c elevated group (n = 10; Figure 2c). Furthermore, monocyte cholesterol levels at discharge were significantly higher in the HbA1c elevated group than in the HbA1c non‐elevated group, similar to hsCRP (Figure 3a,b). The group in which monocyte cholesterol levels decreased during hospitalization (n = 11) had a significantly lower rate of post‐discharge HbA1c elevation than the non‐decreased group (n = 8; 27.3% vs 87.5%, P = 0.02; Table 4).

DISCUSSION

A critical finding of this study is that cholesterol accumulation in peripheral blood monocytes of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus significantly correlates with the severity of various diabetic complications, including macrovascular disease, hepatic fibrosis, and diabetic retinopathy. This not only introduces a new concept regarding the clinical significance of analyzing peripheral blood monocytes but also holds major clinical importance as the discovery of a novel biomarker capable of comprehensively assessing the progression of diabetic complications, which conventional organ‐specific markers often fail to capture.

Cholesterol accumulation in monocytes is likely induced by multiple factors, including the functional breakdown of cholesterol efflux mechanisms (such as the ABCA1 axis) mediated by hyperglycemia, enhanced uptake of glycated LDL, and lysosomal dysfunction due to insulin resistance [6, 9, 10]. This accumulation captures the groundwork for foam cell formation at the peripheral blood level, which is a fundamental process underlying atherosclerosis [11]. The finding in this study that cholesterol accumulation in monocytes was significantly and positively correlated with the markers of glycemic control, glycation stress, and systemic inflammation strongly supports this underlying mechanism.

Based on these results, we hypothesize that cholesterol accumulation in peripheral blood monocytes reflects two integrated aspects of the disease. First, it acts as a factor promoting macrovascular disease such as atherosclerosis by forming the substrate for foam cell formation, potentially via the ABCA1 axis breakdown. Second, it serves as an integrated reflection of systemic metabolic inflammation involved in the progression of complications such as retinopathy and hepatic fibrosis. Therefore, cholesterol accumulation in monocytes is proposed to be an extremely valuable, novel, and integrated biomarker for the early and comprehensive assessment of the progression and severity of various diabetic complications.

Notably, monocyte cholesterol showed no significant correlation with LDL‐C or HbA1c. This lack of association likely stems from the fact that these traditional markers are subject to short‐term fluctuations, whereas monocyte cholesterol serves as a stable indicator that more directly reflects the chronic progression of complications, independent of transient metabolic shifts. This is further supported by the significant correlations observed between monocyte cholesterol and max IMT, the FIB‐4 index, and the severity of diabetic retinopathy.

Interestingly, unlike max IMT, indices such as ABI and baPWV did not correlate significantly with monocyte cholesterol content. This discrepancy likely arises because max IMT reflects pathological processes, including foam cell formation [16], whereas ABI and baPWV primarily evaluate arterial stiffness and structural remodeling [17, 18]. Furthermore, the absence of correlation with renal function parameters suggests that the progression of diabetic nephropathy may not be driven solely by monocyte cholesterol or early inflammatory states, but rather by multifactorial processes, including hemodynamic factors such as blood pressure fluctuations [19]. These findings indicate that the underlying pathogenic mechanisms captured by each clinical index may differ. Therefore, monocyte cholesterol accumulation emerges as a unique marker of sustained hyperglycemia‐related damage and inflammation, distinct from structural changes. Further verification through large‐scale epidemiological studies is warranted to clarify these complex relationships.

In the longitudinal study, a significant reduction in monocytic cholesterol accumulation following intensive glycemic control was observed exclusively in the HbA1c non‐elevated group. Notably, despite a comparable reduction in serum LDL‐C levels across the entire cohort, the HbA1c elevated group failed to show a decrease in monocytic cholesterol. This dissociation suggests that monocytic cholesterol accumulation represents a ‘residual inflammatory risk’ that is not fully addressed by conventional lipid‐lowering or short‐term glycemic improvement. Persistent cholesterol accumulation in monocytes, even after systemic metabolic markers have improved, may reflect a state of ‘metabolic memory.’ In this state, prior exposure to chronic hyperglycemia and inflammation affects the monocytes through epigenetic or metabolic programming, leading to sustained intracellular dysfunction. Our results indicate that the resolution of systemic inflammation and hyperglycemia during hospitalization may induce the recovery of the cholesterol efflux mechanism only in certain patients. Therefore, intracellular monocytic cholesterol serves as a sensitive marker of therapeutic success, acting as a predictor of post‐discharge glycemic stability.

Conventionally, the efficacy of educational admission for diabetes has been attributed to the reversal of glucotoxicity and patients' adoption of improved lifestyle habits [20, 21, 22]. This study suggests that achieving good glycemic control after discharge may not only depend on adherence to lifestyle habits but also on the objective status of intracellular pathology, such as cholesterol accumulation in monocytes, at the time of discharge. Therefore, intracellular monocytic cholesterol is suggested to reflect treatment success, specifically the improvement of metabolism and inflammation at the cellular level and could potentially function as a novel predictor of post‐discharge glycemic control.

This study has several limitations that warrant cautious interpretation of the results. First, the small number of patients in this study prevented the simultaneous entry of all items that may affect the results, such as age, gender, BMI, SBP, and HbA1c, into the multivariate linear analysis, potentially weakening the statistical power of the findings. Second, we were unable to evaluate certain microvascular complications, such as diabetic neuropathy. Third, the method for measuring monocyte cholesterol levels is currently not standardized and is not readily applicable as a simple procedure in routine clinical practice. Finally, given the small sample size and the fact that the analysis was conducted at a single center, the generalizability of our findings requires careful consideration.

In conclusion, cholesterol accumulation in monocytes reflects systemic inflammation and correlates with the severity of diabetic complications. Its ability to predict the degree of inflammatory resolution achieved through educational admission and the risk of post‐discharge HbA1c elevation suggests its potential as a novel biomarker.
