Differentiation of non-hypervascular non-functional pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms from solid pseudopapillary neoplasms using dual-layer spectral detector CT
Authors: Jiaxin Yuan, Mingjie Chen, Jiawei Liu, Liqin Wang, Minghao Zhang, Ning Zhang, Shi-Ting Feng, Luyong Wei, Siya Shi, Yanji Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDual-layer spectral detector CT (DLCT) represents an advanced and emerging modality in CT imaging, offering multiparametric images that enhance the quantitative assessment of pancreatic diseases. Non-hypervascular non-functional pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (NF-pNENs) and solid pseudopapillary neoplasms (SPNs) frequently exhibit overlapping clinical and imaging features, complicating their differentiation. This study aimed to investigate the valuable quantitative parameters of DLCT in preoperative differentiation between non-hypervascular NF-pNEN and SPN, as well as to analyze their diagnostic performance.MethodsThis retrospective study included 52 patients with pathologically confirmed non-hypervascular NF-pNENs and SPNs who underwent DLCT examination before surgery between June 2019 and September 2025. To differentiate between non-hypervascular NF-pNENs and SPNs, independent relevant clinical-radiological features and quantitative parameters were identified using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression analysis and multivariate logistic regression analysis. The diagnostic performances of independent variables were assessed through receiver operating characteristic curves.ResultsThere were 34 patients with non-hypervascular NF-pNENs (46.7 ± 10.2 years, 19 females) and 18 patients with SPNs (32.8 ± 7.9 years, 14 females). Clinical, radiological features and parameters were evaluated with near-perfect agreements among two radiologists. Age and normalised iodine concentration of lesion in the arterial phase (nICa) were the independent factors for differentiating between non-hypervascular NF-pNENs and SPNs in multivariate logistic regression analysis. The areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUCs) for age, nCTa and nICa in tumour differentiation were 0.855 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.754–0.956; cutoff, 36 years), 0.797 (95% CI, 0.679–0.916; cutoff, 0.285) and 0.891 (95% CI, 0.806–0.975; cutoff, 0.147), respectively. The combined model (age + nICa) demonstrated the highest performance (AUC = 0.980; 95% CI, 0.952-1.000) when comparing to that of age (P = 0.008), nCTa (P = 0.002) and nICa (P = 0.03), with satisfactory accuracy (92.3%), sensitivity (88.2%) and specificity (100%).ConclusionsThe DLCT parameter nICa combined with age facilitates non-invasive quantitative differentiation between non-hypervascular NF-pNENs and SPNs with satisfactory diagnostic performance.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (pNENs) constitute a group of malignant heterogeneous tumours originating from pancreatic peptidergic neurons and neuroendocrine cells [1, 2]. Non-functional pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (NF-pNENs), which lack clinically evident hormone-related syndromes, account for approximately 60% to 90% of all pNENs [3]. Although typical pNENs commonly indicative of a hypervascular nature, a subset of cases demonstrates non-hyperenhancing patterns. These non-hypervascular lesions constitute approximately 42% to 49% of all pNENs [4, 5]. Solid pseudopapillary neoplasms (SPNs), a low-grade malignant tumour predominantly affecting young women [6], typically exhibit relatively hypovascular imaging features [7, 8], a characteristic that closely resembles non-hypervascular NF-pNENs. Nevertheless, these two tumour types differ significantly in terms of treatment approaches and prognostic outcomes. Surgical resection remains the treatment of choice for SPNs, with local pancreatic resection commonly performed to maximize preservation of pancreatic function, and patients with SPNs generally exhibit a more favorable prognosis compared to those with NF-pNENs [1, 9]. In contrast, treatment strategies for NF-pNENs are more heterogeneous. Surgical decision-making is individualized according to tumour size, location, and biological behavior: for small NF-pNENs (< 2 cm in diameter) without evidence of metastasis or regional lymph node involvement, active surveillance may be considered instead of immediate surgery [10]. In lesions measuring 2–3 cm, enucleation or central pancreatectomy should be carefully evaluated as viable parenchyma-sparing options [11]. For larger NF-pNENs (>3 cm) of grade 1 or 2, standard pancreatic resection is recommended, along with regional lymphadenectomy, reflecting a more extensive surgical approach than typically required for SPNs [12]. Given the differences in clinical management strategies and prognosis, accurate differentiation between non-hypervascular NF-pNENs and SPNs is crucial. However, these two lesions present similar early clinical features and imaging presentations, posing significant challenges to their identification [8, 13].

Endoscopic ultrasound combined with fine-needle aspiration biopsy is widely regarded as the optimal approach for identifying pancreatic tumours [14]. However, due to the deep retroperitoneal location of pancreas, this procedure carries a high risk of puncture-related complications [15]. Additionally, the diagnostician’s experience and the variability in tumour sampling sites significantly affect diagnostic accuracy [16]. Given that cystic components are common in NF-pNENs and some tumours displaying hypovascularity, their imaging manifestations often overlap with those of SPNs, complicating differentiation using conventional CT and MRI imaging [8]. While radiomics-based approaches have demonstrated promising classification performance, the inherent complexity of the developed machine learning models hinders their clinical translation for differential diagnosis [17].

Dual-layer spectral detector CT (DLCT) represents a cutting-edge advancement CT imaging, equipped with a robust post-processing platform capable of directly generating multi-parameter images, thereby providing more quantitative information for diagnosing pancreatic diseases [18]. Notably, virtual monoenergetic images (VMIs) enhance image quality and increase lesion detection rates by improving details when viewed at 40–200 keV [19]. The iodine concentration (IC) map, with its high iodine sensitivity, accurately reflects the blood supply to lesions [20]. The Z-effective atomic number (Zeff) map quantitatively describes changes in X-ray absorption across different substances, thereby highlighting compositional differences among various tumours [21]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that DLCT parameters aid in the identification of pancreatic tumours; for example, iodine density values and the slope of the attenuation curve have been useful in distinguishing pancreatic cancer from chronic mass-forming pancreatitis [22]. Additionally, prior studies have shown that DLCT quantitative parameters are effective in differentiating pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours from pancreatic neuroendocrine carcinomas [23], as well as in distinguishing non‑hypervascular pNENs and CA 19-9-negative pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas [24]. Collectively, these findings underscore the significant potential of DLCT in differentiating non‑hypervascular NF-pNENs from SPNs.

This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of DLCT in the preoperative differentiation of non‑hypervascular NF-pNENs from SPNs. Additionally, it sought to explore valuable quantitative parameters and to analyse their diagnostic performance.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study was approved by our institutional review board and waived the requirement for written informed consent. We consecutively enrolled 108 patients who underwent abdominal DLCT examination within 14 days prior to surgery at our institution between June 2019 and September 2025. All patients were definitively diagnosed with NF-pNENs (n = 77) or SPNs (n = 31) based on histopathological analysis. NF-pNENs were categorized as hyperenhancing or iso-/hypoenhancing based on lesion enhancement relative to normal pancreatic parenchyma on arterial phase images [4]. The enhancement patterns of the NF-pNENs were independently and blindly assessed by two radiologists with 8 and 14 years of experience in pancreatic imaging. Discrepancies in interpretation were resolved through consensus discussion with a senior abdominal radiologist with 20 years of diagnostic experience. A total of 34 patients were identified as non-hypervascular NF-pNENs and included in the study. Furthermore, according to the 2019 WHO classification criteria, the non-hypervascular NF-pNENs were subclassified into four categories [25]: neuroendocrine tumour-G1 (NET-G1), neuroendocrine tumour-G2 (NET-G2), neuroendocrine tumour-G3 (NET-G3), and neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) Hyperenhancing NF-pNENs lesions in the arterial phase relative to normal pancreatic parenchyma; (b) Presence of distant metastases exhibiting hyperenhancement; (c) Focal lesions exhibiting marked uptake on 68Ga-DOTA-PET/CT; (d) Poor image quality; (e) Incomplete clinical or pathological data; (f) Prior receipt of preoperative treatments such as radiotherapy or chemotherapy. CT acquisition and image reconstruction

All patients underwent contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scanning with a DLCT scanner (IQon Spectral CT, Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands) after at least 4 h of fasting. The scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage, 120 kVp; automatic modulation of tube current; tube rotation time, 0.5 s; helix pitch, 0.798; matrix, 512 × 512; collimator width, 64 × 0.625; slice spacing, 0.8 mm; slice thickness, 1 mm. The nonionic iodinated contrast agent iopromide (Ultravist 370, Schering, Berlin, Germany) was injected intravenously at 3 mL/s (1.5 mL/kg, maximal volume 100 mL), followed by flushing the tubes with 40 mL of physiological saline. The arterial and portal venous phase images were acquired approximately 35 and 65 s after the injection of contrast, respectively [23, 26].

After DLCT image acquisition, spectral-based images were generated using a spectral reconstruction algorithm (Spectral, level 3, Philips Healthcare) and transferred to a dedicated post-processing workstation (IntelliSpace Portal 10.0, Philips Healthcare) to reconstruct the spectral images, including VMIs at 40, 70, and 100 keV, IC maps, and Zeff maps of the arterial and portal venous phases. Conventional 120 kVp polyenergetic images were also reconstructed using a hybrid iterative reconstruction algorithm (iDose 4, level 3, Philips Healthcare). The reconstructed spectral images were fully overlapped with the conventional 120 kVp polyenergetic images.

Image evaluation

The image data of all patients were evaluated blindly and independently performed by two radiologists with 8 (reader 1) and 14 (reader 2) years of experience in diagnosing pancreatic diseases. Discrepancies in interpretation were resolved through consensus discussion with a senior abdominal radiologist with 20 years of diagnostic experience. Two months later reader 1 reassessed the data of all patients for subsequent inter-/intra-observer agreements analysis. Assessment of imaging signs included: tumour location (head-neck or body-tail), tumour size (maximum diameter of tumour) [13], tumour margin (well-defined or ill-defined) [4], cystic degeneration or necrosis, calcification (Hounsfield units [HU] >160) [27], dilatation of the main pancreatic duct (MPD), pancreatic atrophy, and peripancreatic infiltration (including peripheral vascular invasion). Intratumoural cystic degeneration or necrosis was defined as lesion with 10–30 HU in the plain CT and without increase in the arterial and portal venous phases (up to 5 HU) [28]. MPD dilation was defined as the MPD diameter exceeding 3 mm [29]. Pancreatic atrophy was defined as a pancreatic parenchymal width of less than 10 mm in at least half of the pancreas [30, 31]. Peripancreatic infiltration was defined as tumour involvement in the surrounding area of the pancreas [32]. Vascular invasion was defined as tumour-vessel contact of more than 180° or the presence of local vascular malformations or intravascular emboli [33]. Additionally, a clinician not involved in the assessment of the imaging features (reader 3) documented each patient’s clinical features, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), symptoms (abdominal pain), and pathology results.

Quantitative analyses were conducted on conventional 120 kVp polyenergetic images as well as on spectral images, including VMIs at 40, 70, and 100 keV, IC maps, and Zeff maps. Two radiologists (reader 1 and reader 2) delineated three circular regions of interest (ROIs) larger than 10 mm2 at the upper edge, axial maximum diameter, and lower edge of the tumour, respectively, while avoiding areas of calcification, blood vessels, cystic degeneration, or necrosis. ROIs were also placed at corresponding levels of the aorta, with careful avoidance of atheromatous plaque areas. The mean values of the ROIs were recorded for subsequent statistical analysis.

The quantitative parameters include those from conventional CT and DLCT. For conventional CT, parameters included CT attenuation of the lesion (CTlesion) and corresponding aorta (CTarota) during both arterial and portal venous phases. For DLCT, parameters encompassed measurements of the lesion and the corresponding aorta during the arterial and portal venous phases, including IC (IClesion and ICaorta), normalised IC (nIC), Zeff (Zefflesion and Zeffaorta), normalised Zeff (nZeff), and CT attenuation of VMIs at 40, 70, and 100 keV (VMI40lesion/arota, VMI70lesion/arota, VMI100lesion/arota), and normalised VMI (nVMI40, nVMI70, nVMI100). The letters “a” and “v” were appended to these parameters to denote values measured in the arterial and portal venous phases, respectively. The quantitative parameters detailed above were utilized for subsequent statistical analyses, based on the mean values recorded by the two readers. In instances where there was a disagreement between the two readers regarding the qualitative features, these features were jointly assessed by both readers before being included in the statistical analyses.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using the following software: SPSS 25 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), MedCalc (MedCalc Software, Ltd., version 20.218; Ostend, Belgium) and R software (R Development Core Team). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) and were compared between non-hypervascular NF-pNENs and SPNs using Student’s t-test or Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies or percentages and analysed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Inter- and intra-observer agreements were evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) and Kappa values (κ), classified as follows: 0–0.20, slight; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; and 0.81–1.00, near perfect [34, 35]. Univariate logistic regression was employed to identify relevant factors for distinguishing between non-hypervascular NF-pNENs and SPNs, followed by Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression analysis to determine independent factors. Multivariate models were built using forward stepwise logistic regression (likelihood ratio) with entry P < 0.05 and removal P ≥ 0.10. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the parameters. The area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity (Se), and specificity (Sp) were calculated, with cut-off values selected based on the Youden’s index. AUCs were compared using the Delong’s test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 52 patients were included in this study, comprising 34 patients with non-hypervascular NF-pNENs (46.7 ± 10.2 years, 19 female) and 18 patients with SPNs (32.8 ± 7.9 years, 14 female). The baseline characteristics of the two groups are detailed in Table 1. The mean age of patients with SPNs was significantly lower than that of patients with non-hypervascular NF-pNENs (32.8 years vs. 46.7 years, P < 0.001). However, non-hypervascular NF-pNENs were significantly larger in tumour size (P = 0.04) and more frequently associated with peripancreatic infiltration (PI) (P = 0.02) compared to SPNs. No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding sex, BMI, abdominal pain, tumour location, tumour margin, cystic degeneration/necrosis, calcification, MPD dilatation, and pancreatic atrophy (all P > 0.05). Interobserver agreement

The ICCs indicate that the two readers had near-perfect agreement on all quantitative parameter measurements in both arterial and portal venous phase (all ICCs > 0.900, as shown in Table 2). The following radiological features also demonstrated near-perfect agreement: tumour location (κ = 1.000, P < 0.001), tumour size (ICC = 0.995, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.993–0.997, P < 0.001); tumour margin (κ = 0.832, P < 0.001); cystic degeneration or necrosis (κ = 0.829, P < 0.001), calcification (κ = 0.847, P < 0.001); MPD dilation (κ = 0.811, P < 0.001), pancreatic atrophy (κ = 0.899, P < 0.001), and PI (κ = 0.907, P < 0.001).

Relevant features for differentiating NF-pNENs and SPNs

Table 3 indicates that both conventional CT parameters and DLCT parameters were statistically significant in distinguishing non-hypervascular NF-pNENs from SPNs. The parameters were classified into three categories: clinical and general radiological features, quantitative conventional CT parameters, and DLCT parameters. Univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted on these three categories of parameters (Table 4). Among the clinical and general radiological features, only age and peripancreatic infiltration were significant (P < 0.05); among the quantitative conventional CT parameters, only nCTa and nCTv were significant (P < 0.05); while among the DLCT parameters, only nZeffa and nZeffv were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Variables demonstrating statistical significance (P < 0.05) in univariate analysis of clinical/general radiological features and quantitative conventional CT parameters were entered into multivariate logistic regression. Final models identified age as the sole significant predictor in clinical/general radiological model and nCTa in quantitative conventional CT model (Table 5). To further identify the effective indicators among DLCT parameters for differentiating non-hypervascular NF-pNENs from SPNs, the DLCT parameters with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis (8 parameters in total) were firstly included in the LASSO regression analysis, and the results indicated that only nICa remained as a significant parameter (Fig. 3). Subsequently, the three identified candidate parameters (age, nCTa, and nICa) were jointly included in the LASSO regression analysis, and all three were retained (Fig. 3). The multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted on these three parameters to develop the combined model, and the results showed that only age and nICa were independent diagnostic factors for differentiating these two diseases (Table 5).

Performance of relevant features and models

The equation for the combined model (including age and nICa) was “predicted value = 0.202*age + 55.352*nICa-15.232” (cut off: 1.544). Delong’s test indicated that the combined model demonstrated the highest performance, with an AUC of 0.980 (95% CI: 0.952-1.000), surpassing the individual parameters: age (AUC = 0.855, 95% CI: 0.754–0.956, cut-off: 36 years, P = 0.008), nCTa (AUC = 0.797, 95% CI: 0.679–0.916, cut-off: 0.285, P = 0.002), and nICa (AUC = 0.891, 95% CI: 0.806–0.975, cut-off: 0.147, P = 0.03) (Table 6; Fig. 4). Additionally, the combined model exhibited satisfactory accuracy (92.3%), sensitivity (88.2%) and specificity (100%).

Discussion

Non-hypervascular NF-pNENs and SPNs have similar early clinical features, making them difficult to differentiate using conventional imaging techniques [8]. DLCT is a promising technology with a robust post-processing platform that offers various quantitative parameters to enhance diagnostic accuracy and differentiation of diseases. In our study, the combined model constituted by the DLCT parameter (nICa) and clinical feature (age) significantly improved the differential diagnostic performance between non-hypervascular NF-pNENs and SPNs, demonstrating excellent accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity.

In our study, conventional CT parameters (nCTa and nCTv) and DLCT parameters (nVMI40a/v, nVMI70a/v, nVMI100a/v, nICa/v) associated with lesion enhancement were significantly higher in non-hypervascular NF-pNENs than in SPNs, indicating distinct differences in enhancement characteristics between these two tumours. The enhancement characteristics of tumours correlate with tumour neovascularisation and microvessel density [36]. Generally, SPNs tend to demonstrate a heterogeneous iso- or hypo-enhancement pattern [37], while the enhancement pattern of NF-pNENs is linked to pathological grading and tumour differentiation [38, 39]. Well-differentiated NF-pNENs overexpress vascular endothelial growth factor receptor and platelet-derived growth factor receptor pathways, resulting increased neovascularisation and microvessel density [40]. Conversely, poorly differentiated NF-pNENs exhibit reduced microvessel density. Our study identifies nICa, measured during the arterial phase, as an independent predictor for differentiating non-hypervascular NF-pNENs from SPNs. This finding aligns with previous evidence demonstrating that arterial-phase CT measurements more accurately reflect tumor microvessel density than those obtained in the portal venous phase [41]. In conventional CT, although the CT attenuation can somewhat reflect the blood supply of a lesion, it is affected by X-ray energy and the material properties of the scanned object (atomic composition, density, and thicknesses) [42], making CT attenuation measurements unstable. Moreover, CT attenuation is vulnerable to imaging artifacts, which may introduce inaccuracies and potentially explain why nCTa was not retained in the final combined model. Sellerer T et al. found that DLCT was more accurate than others in detecting iodine concentration in abdominal imaging by comparing phantom studies across different platforms [43]. IC allows for indirect quantitative assessment of tumour neovascularisation and reflects tumour microvessel density by directly measuring iodine density in the lesion [20], with a relative error between IC values and actual iodine density shown to be less than 5% [44]. Therefore, IC is more responsive to changes in tumour blood supply than conventional CT attenuation measurements, and as demonstrated in our results, nICa exhibited higher sensitivity compared to nCTa (79.4% vs. 52.9%), further validating the superiority of IC-based metrics. The combined model achieved excellent specificity of 100% in distinguishing the two entities. Notably, although the included NF-pNENs were classified as non-hypervascular and lacked overt enhancement on conventional CT, IC values effectively amplified subtle perfusion differences, facilitating the identification of lesions that are difficult to differentiate. In conclusion, preoperative DLCT not only provides valuable imaging insights for pancreatic lesion characterization but also significantly improves diagnostic accuracy in differentiating non-hypervascular NF-pNENs from SPNs when integrated with clinical features and DLCT-derived parameters.

Our study confirms that age is also an independent factor for differentiating non-hypervascular NF-pNENs and SPNs, consistent with previous studies. Previous studies have identified a discernible age preference for SPNs, predominantly observed in young women aged 33–34 years [8, 17], which aligns with our findings of a mean age of 32.8 years. In contrast, the mean age of non-hypervascular NF-pNENs was 46.7 years, close to the previously reported age of approximately 50 years [8]. While non-hypervascular NF-pNENs and SPNs differ in features such as tumour size and peripancreatic infiltration, these are not independent factors. Although previous studies have reported larger tumour size in SPNs compared to NF-pNENs [45], our study excluded SPNs with preoperative imaging features allowing definitive diagnosis as well as hypervascular NF-pNENs. As a result, the ability of tumor size to discriminate between the two entities was diminished. Our findings also indicate that higher-grade NF-pNENs have a great incidence of peripancreatic invasion, whereas SPNs, despite being low-grade malignant tumours, can also invade peripancreatic structures [46, 47]. The inclusion of a higher number of G1 and G2 non-hypervascular NF-pNENs in our study may have an impact on the capacity of peripancreatic infiltration features to differentiate non-hypervascular NF-pNENs from SPNs. In addition, our results indicated that calcification did not show statistically significant differences between non-hypervascular NF-pNENs and SPNs, in line with previous studies [45, 48]. This may be attributed to the fact that, as these tumours increase in size, both entities might undergo cystic degeneration, potentially leading to dystrophic calcification [49]. Wang C et al. found that pancreatic or bile duct dilation did not reach statistical significance in differentiating between SPNs and hypovascular pNENs [13], while pancreatic duct dilation didn’t help to distinguish the non-hypervascular NF-pNENs and SPNs in our study. Additionally, our results showed a higher proportion of female patients among those with SPNs, but gender was not included in the ultimate discriminatory model, which was in line with previous studies [45, 50]. It seems that the gender was not so important as age in differentiation though SPNs are reported to be more likely to occur in younger women.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it was a single-centre study conducted in a large tertiary care hospital. Secondly, the sample sizes of the two groups were relatively small due to the exclusion of patients who had already received treatment or had incomplete pathological data, with a particularly low proportion of G3 and NEC patients among the non-hypervascular NF-pNENs. Consequently, the conclusions of this study need to be confirmed by multi-centre and multi-sample validation. Finally, the data in this study were obtained from a single DLCT machine manufacturer, necessitating further research to assess whether different machine manufacturers could influence the results.

In conclusion, this retrospective study demonstrated that the combined model, constructed using age and nICa, demonstrated superior diagnostic performance in differentiating non-hypervascular NF-pNENs from SPNs. As a promising non-invasive technique, DLCT enables accurate and quantitative identification of these two diseases, which can assist in guiding perioperative precision treatment and aid clinicians in formulating therapeutic strategies.
