Superior Impact‐Resistant Composite Hydrogels Through an Ionic Coupling Strategy
Authors: Hao Zhuo, Quyang Liu, Xinyu Dong, Hongzhi Zheng, Lingyi Hong, Wei Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImpact resistance emerges from the coupling of strong load‐bearing networks and dynamic interfacial interactions that enable effective stress transfer and energy dissipation. Although hydrogels are promising candidates for impact‐resistant soft materials, it remains challenging to reinforce both networks and interfaces simultaneously in hydrogels, which limits their performance under high strain‐rate loading. To overcome this limitation, we develop a composite hydrogel comprised of a poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) matrix reinforced with chitosan–sodium alginate nanofibers (CSNFs), using sodium citrate as a multifunctional ionic coupler that (i) strengthens the PVA matrix via the Hofmeister effect, (ii) reinforces the CSNF network through desolvation and electrostatic crosslinking, and (iii) improves their fiber–matrix interfaces, enabling efficient stress transfer and energy dissipation through the integrated composite network and layered microstructure. The composite hydrogel achieves superior impact resistance relative to high‐performance solid polymers, with an impact strength of 426.7 MPa and toughness of 106.4 MJ m− 3 at 7000 s− 1, while retaining excellent tensile properties (tensile strength: 54.2 MPa; fracture strain: 590%). By molecular‐level experimental and simulation analyses, this work establishes ionic coupling as a facile yet effective strategy for achieving composite hydrogels with extreme impact resistance, broadening the potential of soft materials in impact protection, damping, and energy absorption.

Introduction

Impact‐resistant materials are essential for safeguarding structures, devices, and humans against sudden mechanical shocks. They ensure safety, durability, and performance across various real‐life applications, ranging from automotive bumpers and safety helmets to protective coatings and electronics packaging. Common impact‐resistant materials, such as engineered foams [1, 2] and composites [3, 4, 5], absorb and dissipate impact energy through mechanisms including elastic and plastic deformation, controlled fracture, and interfacial debonding. Most of these processes are irreversible, and their energy dissipation via reversible processes is limited in magnitude. In addition, as these materials are typically hard and rigid, they are poorly suited to the growing demands for soft and flexible applications.

Among the most promising soft materials, hydrogels have rapidly evolved from simple water‐rich gels into mechanically robust and multifunctional systems that are increasingly explored in flexible electronics [6, 7], soft robotics [8, 9], and biomedical engineering scaffolds [10, 11]. However, most studies on strong and tough hydrogels primarily focus on their mechanical behaviors under quasi‐static conditions, rather than true impact performance, which is crucial for practical applications requiring mechanical reliability under dynamic loading and even high‐strain‐rate impacts. In such scenarios, the intrinsically soft and loosely connected networks of hydrogels struggle to support effective stress transfer and energy dissipation, thereby limiting their impact resistance. This limitation is further compounded by the solvation effect of water, which dominates and disrupts interactions between polymer chains [12, 13]. Therefore, achieving high impact resistance represents a key challenge in overcoming the intrinsic softness of hydrogels for mechanically robust applications.

In designing impact‐resistant hydrogels, an intuitive starting point is to take inspiration from natural impact‐resistant architectures. Inspired by nacre, Liang et al. constructed poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) hydrogels with lamellar microstructures that promoted crack resistance and progressive energy dissipation [14]. Such structural design can also be coupled with material engineering, as demonstrated by Zhang et al. through an integrative approach that combined in situ localized alignment with salting‐out to enhance the dynamic mechanical performance of ionogels [15]. Another prominent strategy is compositing, including organic–inorganic hybrids that incorporated elastic microspheres within a biomineralized hydrogel matrix [16], and fiber‐reinforced composites with enhanced interfacial bonding between the stiff reinforcement and the tough matrix [17]. Although these approaches can effectively translate design principles from biological materials and conventional composites into hydrogels, they also prompt a broader rethinking of the design problem from first principles: what constitutes high impact resistance, and how can those requirements be engineered into a hydrogel‐based system?

Impact resistance stems from the integration of two distinct yet complementary mechanisms: robust networks that provide structural strength and rapid stress transmission, and dynamic interfacial interactions that dissipate energy effectively. Yet it is difficult to achieve synergy between them, as enhancing one often compromises the other. We encountered this particular trade‐off in our earlier work when introducing tannic acid as an interfacial crosslinker between chitosan–sodium alginate nanofibers (CSNFs) and the PVA hydrogel matrix [18, 19]. While tannic acid strengthened the fiber–matrix interfaces effectively, it inadvertently weakened the intrinsic crystallinity of the PVA matrix. This finding exemplifies a common challenge in composite hydrogels: the difficulty of synergistically reinforcing the composite networks and interfaces, which has motivated us to explore a material design capable of strengthening both at once.

Herein, we propose a facile ionic coupling strategy using sodium citrate (SC) as a single multifunctional agent that reinforces both the CSNF and PVA networks as well as their interfaces. With a strong Hofmeister anion, SC reduces polymer hydration and promotes PVA chain associations into a highly crystalline hydrogel matrix. Importantly, the role of SC in this composite system extends beyond a conventional Hofmeister effect. It reinforces the CSNFs through a similar desolvation‐driven process, coupled with strong electrostatic interactions between citrate carboxylates and the amino groups of CSNFs. These matrix‐ and fiber‐level effects also converge at the interface, where SC promotes stronger molecular bonding at the fiber–matrix interfaces, integrating the fibrous composite structure. As a result, the composite hydrogel exhibits excellent quasi‐static mechanical performance with a tensile strength of 54.2 MPa and a fracture strain of 590%. More importantly, it achieves a remarkable impact strength of 426.7 MPa and impact toughness of 106.4 MJ m−3 at a strain rate of 7000 s−1. Hence, this work demonstrates that SC, as a single ionic coupler, can elevate the composite hydrogel's mechanical performance by synergistically reinforcing the fibers, the matrix, and their interface. It therefore offers a simple yet powerful strategy for developing mechanically robust, impact‐resistant hydrogels for applications in shock protection and vibration damping under extreme conditions.

Results

Ionic Coupling Strategy for Impact‐Resistant Composite Hydrogels

Figure 1 presents the design rationale of our ionic coupling strategy for constructing an impact‐resistant composite hydrogel. In this design, we integrate chitosan–sodium alginate nanofibers (CSNFs) as the reinforcement network, a poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) hydrogel as the matrix, and sodium citrate (SC) as a multifunctional ionic coupler. As illustrated in Figure 1a, we first prepared CSNFs using an ultrasonication‐induced assembly process developed in our previous work [19]. This unique bottom‐up assembly directly organized chitosan and sodium alginate macromolecules into nanofibers measuring 50–100 nm in diameter and several micrometers in length (Figure S1a,b), with abundant amino (─NH2) and hydroxyl (─OH) functional groups that allow for various intermolecular interactions. As such, we selected the PVA hydrogel matrix, as its characteristic ─OH groups can readily form interfacial hydrogen bonds with the CSNF surface groups [18]. Building on this fiber–matrix combination, the core of our ionic coupling strategy is to incorporate SC, a salt with citrate anions that carry three carboxylate groups (Figure 1b). SC is designed to enhance all three elements of the composite: (i) its citrate ions can exert a Hofmeister effect to strengthen the PVA matrix; (ii) a similar desolvation effect also promotes polymer association within the CSNF network, which is further crosslinked by electrostatic interactions with the citrate tricarboxylate groups; and (iii) it further enhances the fiber‐matrix interfacial interactions, enabling efficient mesoscale stress transfer and energy dissipation through the integrated composite network and layered microstructure.

Schematic illustration of the preparation strategy for impact‐resistant composite hydrogels. a) Ultrasonication‐induced assembly of chitosan–sodium alginate nanofibers (CSNFs). b) The ionic coupling strategy using sodium citrate to synergistically enhance the CSNF–PVA composite hydrogel. c, d) Hierarchical composite structures of the impact‐resistant hydrogel, including a layered microstructure formed by solvent evaporation, co‐strengthened fiber and matrix networks, enhanced fiber–matrix interfaces, and molecular‐level interactions.

Specifically, we first blended the ultrasonicated CSNF suspension with a PVA solution and dry‐cast the mixture for solvent evaporation, which induced the formation of a hydrogen‐bonded network with a layered microstructure under gravity (Figure 1c) [20]. This dehydration–rehydration process also contributes to mechanical reinforcement by densifying the network and strengthening interfacial interactions [18]. Subsequently, the dried composite was rehydrated in either pure water or a 1.5 m SC solution to produce CSNF–PVA (CP) and CSNF‐PVA‐SC (CPS) composite hydrogels, respectively. The preparation process is readily scalable, since the sample size is primarily determined by the mold dimensions (Figure S1c). Through the multifunctional effects of SC, the CPS composite hydrogel achieves high impact resistance by integrating (i) a highly crystalline PVA matrix strengthened by the Hofmeister effect; (ii) a reinforced CSNF network with reduced solvation and electrostatic crosslinking; and (iii) strong interfacial interactions that integrate the composite network with enhanced energy dissipation (Figure 1d). These mechanisms are examined in detail through the experimental and simulation results presented in the following sections. Collectively, these enhancements embody our ionic coupling strategy: a single ionic coupler synergistically enhances the matrix, the fiber network, and the interface, yielding a composite hydrogel with superior impact resistance.

Superior Impact Strength and Damping Capability

The superior impact resistance of the CPS hydrogel was validated using the Split‐Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB) tests (Figure 2a). As shown in Figure 2b, both the peak impact stress and strain increased with the loading rate, with noticeably faster growth at higher rates. This pronounced strain‐rate sensitivity suggested that the hydrogel could adapt to varying impact conditions through effective energy dissipation. At the highest strain rate of 7000 s−1, CPS achieved a peak impact stress of 426.7 MPa and toughness of 106.4 MJ m−3 (Figure S2a), outperforming most reported impact‐resistant hydrogels and elastomers (Figure 2c, Figure S2b and Table S1). Remarkably, its impact strength surpassed that of industry‐standard structural plastics, including reinforced polycarbonate (PC), poly(vinyl chloride) (PVC), and even Kevlar‐woven composites, while its impact toughness exceeded that of aramid‐reinforced polyethylene (PE). Moreover, under cyclic impacts at 3000 s−1, CPS still retained considerable impact resistance (Figure S2c), showing tolerance to repeated high‐strain‐rate loading. It therefore exhibited strong promise as a protective material, with a thin 1‐mm layer capable of shielding a fragile glass plate from shattering when struck by a steel ball dropped from 0.5 m (Figure 2d).

To further assess the hydrogel's impact resistance and the effectiveness of our ionic coupling strategy, we performed dropping‐ball tests to quantify damping capability. As shown in Figure 2e, the force‐time response exhibited a sharp peak upon impact, followed by a damping phase with gradually decreasing oscillations. Compared to direct impact, the PVA hydrogel exhibited a high peak force with pronounced oscillations, indicating limited damping. Introducing CSNFs only led to a minor reduction in both peak force and oscillation amplitude, whereas a markedly stronger attenuation was achieved upon further incorporation of SC in the CPS hydrogel. CPS effectively decreased the peak force from 3.22 N (blank) to 2.34 N, corresponding to a 27.4% reduction. In addition, the impulse—calculated as the time integral of the force response—was reduced by almost half to 0.12 N s. Together, these results demonstrate that the CPS hydrogel can effectively reduce both force and momentum transfer during impact through enhanced energy dissipation.

The energy‐dissipating dynamics of the hydrogel network was further examined by viscoelastic measurements. As shown in Figure 2f, introducing CSNFs into the PVA matrix increased both the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) in the CP hydrogel, indicating its enhanced elastic stiffness and energy dissipation from a more percolated fiber‐reinforced network structure. After SC treatment, the CPS hydrogel showed a similar G′ to CP but a substantially higher G″. This reveals that while the elastic network was mostly retained, the viscous component was enhanced to increase the hydrogel's energy dissipation capacity. Quantitatively, the damping capacity is described by the loss factor tan δ, the ratio of the viscous to elastic response (G″/G′). Despite increases in both G′ and G″, the CP hydrogel showed little improvement in loss factor relative to PVA, which had a loss factor of only 0.04 (Figure 2g). In contrast, CPS exhibited a substantial improvement, with a more than five‐fold increase to a loss factor of 0.23, confirming its markedly improved energy dissipation capability (Figure S3a).

In summary, the CPS hydrogel exhibits an exceptional combination of impact strength and toughness (426.7 MPa and 106.4 MJ m−3 at 7000 s−1), as well as damping capability (loss factor of 0.23). This superior impact resistance highlights the effectiveness of our ionic coupling strategy, motivating a deeper examination of the underlying mechanisms.

Simultaneous Enhancement in Tensile Strength and Toughness

To resolve the individual contributions within our ionic coupling strategy, we conducted quasi‐static tensile tests on hydrogels prepared with controlled processing variations. As shown in Figure 3a, the mechanical properties improved progressively from PVA to CP and ultimately to CPS (Figure S3b–d, Table S2). The pure PVA hydrogel exhibited a relatively low tensile strength of 4.7 MPa and a fracture strain of 330%. Incorporating CSNFs enhanced both strength and stretchability, with the CP hydrogel reaching 9.7 MPa in strength and 460% in strain. This enhancement indicates favorable CSNF–PVA interfaces, enabled by extensive hydrogen bonding between PVA chains and the surface functional groups of CSNFs (−OH and −NH2) [18]. With the further incorporation of SC, the CPS hydrogel showed the most substantial improvement, yielding an impressive tensile strength of 54.2 MPa and a strain of 590%. The simultaneous improvement in both strength and stretchability also led to the highest toughness of 150 MJ m−3 in CPS. In addition, the tensile strength of CPS can be further enhanced by stretch‐induced orientation. By air‐drying the CP hydrogel under tension before reswelling in the SC solution, an oriented CPS hydrogel (OCPS) was obtained, reaching 76.0 MPa in tensile strength and 280% in strain (Figure S3b,c).

Compared with the original PVA hydrogel, CPS increased strength and toughness by an order of magnitude (more than 11‐ and 23‐fold, respectively), highlighting a synergistic enhancement in load bearing and energy dissipation capacities. This synergy is also reflected in the coupled increases in stiffness and toughness (Figure 3b), a particularly challenging combination as high stiffness typically suppresses molecular mobility and reduces energy dissipation. Consequently, our CPS and OCPS hydrogels feature a combination of strength, stretchability, and toughness superior to most mechanically robust hydrogels reported in the literature (Figure 3c,d; Tables S3 and S4) [34, 36, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46]. Together, these results are consistent with the high impact resistance observed under dynamic loading, showing an effective combination of load bearing and energy dissipation in CPS across different loading conditions.

More specifically, cyclic tensile tests were performed to assess the toughening behavior in CPS (Figure 3e,f and Figure S4). CPS sustained markedly higher stresses and exhibited larger hysteresis loops (hysteresis coefficient: 71%) than PVA at comparable strain levels, showing substantially greater energy dissipation per loading–unloading cycle. This observation reveals the presence of distinct energy dissipation mechanisms in CPS beyond the intrinsic viscoelasticity of PVA. In addition, CPS showed excellent puncture resistance (Figure 3g,h), withstanding a maximum puncture force of 107.6 N while absorbing 1.57 J of energy at a loading rate of 2 mm s−1, both of which were over 9 times those of the PVA hydrogel (11.8 N and 0.17 J).

The toughening and energy dissipation mechanisms in CPS were experimentally evidenced by its fracture surface morphology. In contrast to the smooth and even fracture surface of the PVA hydrogel (Figure S5), CPS exhibited a highly irregular and rugged morphology with torn layers and evident fiber bridging and fiber pull‐out (Figure 3i,j). Formed by solvent evaporation, the layered microstructure can resist and deflect crack propagation while dissipating energy through interlayer dislocation and sliding. Within each layer, intrinsic energy dissipation arises from crack propagation through the PVA matrix and across the CSNF–PVA interfaces, while fiber‐matrix interfacial debonding leads to nanofiber pull‐out and bridging as additional extrinsic toughening mechanisms. As these mechanisms are primarily driven by molecular interactions within the matrix and fiber networks and across their interfaces, we next examined the specific effects of SC on each of these elements.

Strengthened PVA Matrix Through the Hofmeister effect

As previously reported [7, 35], citrate is among the most effective anions for inducing a strong Hofmeister effect on PVA hydrogels (Figure S6). Significant increases in both strength (33.6 MPa) and strain (570%) were observed in the PVA‐SC (PS) hydrogel (Figure S7). By reducing polymer hydration, SC can strengthen inter‐chain interactions and promote strong polymer–polymer associations in the PVA network. This effect directly shapes the nanocrystalline structure of PVA. According to differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, Figure 4a), the pure PVA hydrogel exhibited a typical melting temperature (T m) at 227°C [6], which decreased to 224°C in CP as the nanofibers disrupted the continuity of the PVA matrix. In contrast, CPS increased T m to 231°C, showing a higher PVA crystallinity due to the SC‐induced Hofmeister effect.

Co‐strengthening effects of SC on the matrix and fiber networks. a) DSC, b) XRD, c) SAXS spectra of the PVA, CP, and CPS hydrogels. d) N 1s XPS spectra of the CP and CPS hydrogels. e) Schematic illustration of the molecular interactions in the CPS hydrogel. f, g) DFT calculations of inter‐component binding energies, including PVA–PVA hydrogen bonds, CSNF–PVA hydrogen bonds, and CSNF–SC electrostatic interactions.

To further probe the nanocrystalline structure of the hydrogels, X‐ray diffraction (XRD) and small‐angle X‐ray scattering (SAXS) measurements were performed. As shown in the XRD spectra (Figure 4b), all samples exhibited a characteristic peak at 19.7°, corresponding to the (101¯) reflection plane of semicrystalline PVA [53]. The average size of PVA crystalline domains was estimated using Scherrer's equation, D = kλ/(β cos θ) [54], where k is the shape factor (set as 1), λ is the wavelength of the X‐ray, β is the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the diffraction peak, and θ is the Bragg angle (Table S5). Aligned with the DSC findings, the incorporation of CSNFs reduced the size from 3.75 to 2.93 nm, reflecting the disrupted continuity of the PVA phase in CP. In contrast, the crystalline domain size of CPS increased to 3.90 nm owing to the SC‐induced Hofmeister effect, which promoted chain aggregation to form larger PVA crystalline domains. Meanwhile, SAXS reveals the crystalline organization of the hydrogels. As shown in Figure 4c, broad peaks were observed in the 0.07–0.10 Å−1 range, centered at 0.074, 0.088, and 0.092 Å−1 for PVA, CP, and CPS, respectively. Using the Bragg relation L = 2π/q max, where q max denotes the scattering vector at the peak intensity, the average spacing between PVA crystalline domains was calculated (Table S5). The spacing increased slightly from 7.2 nm in the PVA hydrogel to 7.4 nm in CP, as the CSNF network intervened between PVA chains. CPS showed the largest spacing of 7.8 nm, with dissolved SC molecules further widening the distances between nanocrystalline domains.

In summary, SC enhances the crystallinity of the PVA matrix through the Hofmeister effect, enlarging the crystalline domains while slightly increasing their spacing by embedding within the network. Our findings validate that SC can retain its pronounced Hofmeister effect on PVA even within a composite system.

Reinforced Nanofiber Network Through Desolvation and Electrostatic Crosslinking

In parallel with its Hofmeister effect on PVA, SC is also able to reinforce the CSNFs. This can be inferred from previous mechanical results: when SC was present, the fiber‐induced reinforcement from PS to CPS was greater than that from PVA to CP without SC (Figure S7). To confirm the effect more directly, we cast‐dried CSNF suspensions into films and rehydrated them in SC solution, which produced higher mechanical properties than films rehydrated in water (Figure S8). This enhancement first arises from a desolvation process analogous to the Hofmeister effect, where SC reduces the solvation of the nanofibers and promote their associations. In addition, the citrate tricarboxylate groups are able to form electrostatic interactions with the amino groups of CSNFs. In N 1s X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), CP exhibited a single peak at 399.6 eV corresponding to ─NH2 groups from the chitosan component of CSNFs, whereas CPS displayed an additional peak at 401.8 eV, indicating the formation of protonated amino (─NH3 +) groups (Figure 4d and Figure S9a) [19]. This peak confirms the electrostatic interactions between ─NH3 + on CSNF and ─COO− on SC, which serve as additional crosslinks that further reinforce the CSNF network.

To understand the formation of the electrostatic interactions, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the M062X functional with the 6‐311++G(d, p) basis set. A chitosan molecule was used as a representative unit of the CSNF surface, while PVA and SC units were modelled in their full forms. The optimized molecular structures and calculated electrostatic potential (ESP) distributions are provided in Figure S10. A single SC molecule possesses three ─COO− groups that generate a highly negative electrostatic potential, enabling strong interactions with the positive‐potential hydrogen atoms in CSNF (Figure S10a,b). As shown in Figure 4e,f, the interfacial interaction between CSNF and SC was computed and compared with the intrinsic hydrogen bond strength between PVA chains (PVA–PVA) and at the CSNF–PVA interface. The CSNF–SC pair showed the highest binding energy of ‐18.0 kcal mol−1 (Figure 4g), indicating strong electrostatic interactions. They can also form strong hydrogen bonds, with a binding energy of ‐14.4 kcal mol−1 (Figure S10d). In comparison, the CSNF–PVA and PVA–PVA binding energies were weaker, at ‐12.1 and ‐8.0 kcal mol−1, respectively. These results demonstrate that SC introduces particularly strong interactions, including both electrostatic and hydrogen bonding, into the CSNF network. Together with its desolvation effect, the additional crosslinks synergistically strengthen the associations within the CSNF network.

Overall, Figure 4e illustrates the molecular interactions within the CPS hydrogel. The CSNF–PVA combination forms a primary composite network through hydrogen bonds within the PVA matrix and at the fiber–matrix interface. Building on this foundation, SC enhances PVA chain association and promotes the formation of crystalline domains through the Hofmeister effect, while reinforcing the CSNF network through a similar desolvation mechanism and additional electrostatic crosslinks.

Enhanced Interfacial Interaction and Energy Dissipation

By co‐strengthening both fiber and matrix networks, the SC‐induced interactions thereby reinforce the inherent fiber–matrix interfaces, producing a synergistically integrated composite. In the Fourier‐transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectrum, CPS showed characteristic peaks of SC, including the citrate carboxylate C═O stretch at 1605 cm−1 [55], along with the C─O/symmetric ─COO− stretches at 1297 cm−1 and 1263 cm−1 (Figure S9b) [56]. Compared with the asymmetrical C─O stretch (1082 cm−1 and 1140 cm−1) [57] in PVA and CP, CPS exhibited a red shift to 1076 cm−1, indicating stronger bonding among CSNF, PVA, and SC. In addition, the reduced intensity of the O−H stretch at 3258 cm−1 and the −CH2− asymmetric stretches at 2910 cm−1 and 2940 cm−1 suggested stronger interchain interactions (Figure S9c) [58]. Together, these spectral shifts and intensity changes confirm SC‐induced strengthening of molecular and interfacial interactions within the CPS hydrogel.

The effects of these interfacial interactions on the hydrogel's dynamic mechanics were studied by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, using a nonlinear shear‐lag model to analyze the SC‐mediated interface. During deformation, relative sliding between the nanofibers and the PVA matrix occurs, leading to interfacial friction and shear at the SC‐mediated fiber–matrix interface (Figure 5a). Therefore, we modeled the CPS hydrogel as a layered structure consisting of CSNF, solvent, and PVA. The CSNF layer was represented by 10‐chain chitosan and 10‐chain sodium alginate arranged in alternating parallel configurations, while the PVA layer consisted of 30 parallel chains (Figure 5b, c and Figure S11). To compare the CP and CPS hydrogels, the interfacial solvent was set as either water or SC solution.

Upon shearing the model, the simulated displacement and stress curves are presented in Figure 5d,e, with representative snapshots of the process shown in Figure 5f. Both CP and CPS hydrogels exhibited typical stick‐slip interfacial dynamics, with a slow sliding response in the stick phase followed by an abrupt displacement increase in the slip phase. As presented in Figure 5e, CPS exhibited a much higher maximum shear stress than CP, showing that the SC‐mediated interface can sustain greater shear before the PVA layer slips. This suggests that SC can enhance the interfacial stress transfer through stronger molecular bonding. Moreover, CPS also showed smaller stress drop after each slip event, reflecting a faster and more effective debond–rebond process within the interfacial region. Together, these behaviors capture how SC enhances the fiber‐matrix interfaces, which further extends to the mesoscale, where the integrated fibrous network, fiber pull‐out, and interfacial sliding collectively contribute to stress redistribution and energy dissipation. This multiscale dissipation is pivotal in achieving the hydrogel's high toughness—intrinsically through stronger composite crosslinks and extrinsically through more effective fiber pull‐out energy dissipation—and ultimately superior impact resistance under high‐strain‐rate loading.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed an ionic coupling strategy using sodium citrate that synergistically integrated composite network reinforcement and interfacial energy dissipation to achieve high impact resistance. In the composite hydrogel, the PVA matrix, CSNF network, and fiber–matrix interfaces were synergistically enhanced through coupled Hofmeister effect, desolvation‐driven association, and electrostatic crosslinking. As a result, it exhibited superior impact resistance, with impact strength of 426.7 MPa and impact toughness of 106.4 MJ m− 3 at a high loading rate of 7,000 s− 1, exceeding those of many high‐performance solid polymer materials. The hydrogel also showed excellent quasi‐static tensile performance, including tensile strength of 54.2 MPa and toughness of 150 MJ m− 3, while stretch‐induced alignment further increased the strength up to 76.0 MPa. Notably, the energy dissipation behavior was investigated through combined experimental and simulation analyses, revealing hierarchical energy dissipation across micro, nano, and molecular scales. Molecular dynamics simulations captured pronounced interfacial stick‐slip behavior in the composite hydrogel, enabling effective debond–rebond dynamics for energy dissipation. Together, these results demonstrated ionic coupling as a simple yet powerful material design for impact‐resistant hydrogels, where a single multifunctional sodium citrate brought multiple component‐specific effects into one step. By integrating strength, toughness, and damping capacity, the resulting hydrogel also opened new opportunities for soft materials in impact protection, damping, and energy‐absorbing applications.

Experimental Section

Materials

Chitosan (95% degree of deacetylation, 100–200 mPa s) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Sodium citrate (SC, dihydrate, ≥99%), acetic acid (≥99%), sodium alginate (SA, viscosity = 200±20 mPa s), and poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA, 99+% hydrolyzed, M w = 146 000—186 000) were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich. All reagents involved in this research were used as received without purification.

Preparation of Chitosan–Sodium Alginate Nanofiber (CSNF) Suspension

Chitosan (CS) and sodium alginate (SA) solutions (2 wt% each) were prepared by dissolving CS powder in 1 wt% acetic acid and SA in deionized water, respectively. The two solutions were then mixed at a 1:1 mass ratio and ultrasonicated for 2 h using an ultrasonic homogenizer (JY92‐IIDN, 900 W, 25 kHz, 15‐m probe; Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), yielding a chitosan–sodium alginate nanofiber (CSNF) suspension.

Preparation of Composite Hydrogels

A 10 wt.% poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) solution was prepared by dissolving PVA powder in deionized water under vigorous stirring at 95°C. The CSNF‐PVA mixture was obtained by blending the CSNF suspension with the PVA solution, followed by degassing and casting into custom molds for air drying. The dried composite was rehydrated in deionized water for 2 d to obtain the CSNF–PVA (CP) hydrogel, while the CSNF‐PVA‐SC (CPS) hydrogel was prepared by immersing the dried composite in a 1.5 m sodium citrate (SC) solution for 2 d. The oriented CPS (OCPS) hydrogel was fabricated by stretching CP to 100% strain during air drying, followed by reswelling in the SC solution. For comparison, the PVA‐SC (PS) hydrogel was prepared by reswelling dried PVA films in the SC solution.

Material Characterizations

Micromorphology was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi Regulus 8230 field‐emission SEM). X‐ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained using a Bruker D8 diffractometer. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra were recorded in attenuated total reflection (ATR) mode using an Agilent Cary 660 spectrometer. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was employed to characterize melting and crystallization behavior. Small‐angle X‐ray scattering (SAXS) measurements were conducted using a Xeuss 3.0 instrument (λ = 1.54 Å, sample‐to‐detector distance = 500 mm, exposure time = 600 s). X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed on a Thermo Scientific Nexsa system with an Al·Kα excitation source (1486.68 eV).

Rheology Measurements

Rheology measurements were performed on an Anton Paar MCR302e stress‐controlled rheometer using a parallel plate geometry with a radius of 25 mm. The temperature was held at 25°C with a Peltier‐controlled stage, and a solvent trap was fitted to suppress water loss. Frequency‐sweep tests were conducted at a strain amplitude of 0.1%–1%, a range verified by prior strain‐sweep measurements that fell within the linear viscoelastic regime.

Mechanical Tests

Mechanical tests were performed using a universal testing machine (Instron 34SC‐5, 1000 N load cell). Toughness was defined as the energy absorbed before fracture, calculated from the area under the tensile stress–strain curve. For uniaxial tensile and cyclic loading–unloading tests, hydrogels were cut into dog‐bone specimens (width of 2 mm, gauge length of 15 mm, and thickness of 0.30 mm) and tested at a constant crosshead speed of 2.0 mm s− 1. Puncture tests were conducted on the same instrument using a hemispherical steel probe (diameter of 35 mm) at 2.0 mm s− 1 on circular samples (diameter of 40 mm, thickness of 0.4 mm), and force–displacement data were recorded. High‐strain‐rate impact resistance was evaluated using Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) experiments with cylindrical specimens (6 mm in diameter and 3 mm in thickness) with a striker, incident, transmission, and absorption bar, integrated with synchronized data acquisition. Impact protection performance was further assessed using a custom‐built falling‐ball setup, where a steel ball was dropped from a fixed height of 63.5 cm onto the center of hydrogel samples (thickness 1.0 mm) supported by a force sensor.

Simulations

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Gaussian 16 (Revision A.03) computational package and GaussView 6 [59]. Classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out using the large‐scale atomic/molecular massively parallel simulator (LAMMPS) [60] to investigate the interfacial interactions within the composite hydrogel system.

Further details of the DFT calculations and MD simulations, together with the SAXS experiments and SHPB tests, are provided in the Supporting Information.
