The IQGAP1‐Claudin4‐JNK Signaling Axis as a Differential Biomarker in Renal Cell Carcinoma
Authors: Nahshon Puente, Xiuzhen Fan, Nagalakshmi Nadiminty, Obi Ekwenna, Firas G Petros, Puneet Sindhwani, Amira Gohara, Evgeny Yakirevich, Mahasin A Osman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRenal cell carcinoma (RCC) is a highly heterogeneous malignant neoplasm with multiple morphological and molecular subtypes; however, the basis of this heterogeneity remains incompletely defined, hindering effective diagnosis and treatment. The signaling scaffold IQGAP1 and claudins are tight junction protein partners involved in epithelial cell polarity and polarized secretion whose overexpression has been associated with RCC, though the molecular mechanism is obscure. RNA‐seq data on the TCGA database shows that the RNA expression levels of IQGAP1 and claudin‐2, ‐4, and ‐8 differentially change significantly in normal versus RCC tumors, suggesting a regulatory loop between IQGAP1 and each of these claudins. Previously, we reported that IQGAP1 differentially regulates claudin ‐2 and ‐4 localization and expression at the tight junctions and imparts on kidney epithelial structure and function. Here, we show that IQGAP1 localization, and not mere expression level, accompanies RCC phenotypes and differential expression of several junctional proteins where claudin‐4 localization remains intact or stabilized. Notably, IQGAP1 expression appears inversely correlated with that of claudin‐4, supporting a dynamic interaction at the tight junctions. These findings support the notion that IQGAP1 localization influences claudin dynamics, and its dysfunction imparts on RCC. Altogether, these findings pave the way for exploring the differential expression and localization of the IQGAP1/claudin complex as personalized diagnostics or therapeutics to address RCC heterogeneity.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is a heterogeneous cancer arising from kidney epithelia and encompassing many histological and molecular subtypes [1]. While the somatic von Hippel Lindau (VHL) tumor suppressor gene mutations have been widely implicated, genomic studies have identified alterations in epigenetic regulatory genes and revealed remarkable intratumor variations [1]. Early stage RCC tumors are amenable to resection or ablation; however, metastatic RCC is refractive to chemotherapy and is treated with several targeted agents, including immune checkpoint inhibitors and tyrosine kinase inhibitors, but the cancer‐specific survival rate remains low [2]. Therefore, identification of more molecular targets and precision therapies is crucial to understanding RCC biology and to addressing its heterogeneity clinically.

Claudins are a large family of tight junction transmembrane proteins that maintain the magnitude and selectivity of paracellular permeability of polarized epithelial cells by acting both as barriers and pores [3, 4, 5]. In renal tubules, they determine permeability and selectivity of the various nephrons [5]. At least 24 claudins have been identified in mammals, and their expression patterns appear to be tissue‐specific [6]. Correlation between the expression levels of claudin‐3 and ‐4 with overall survival in clear cell RCC presented the potential of claudins as diagnostic and prognostic markers in RCC [7]. Furthermore, several studies demonstrated the utility of claudin‐4 monoclonal antibodies in treating cancer or enhancing drug absorption by the paracellular permeation route [8, 9, 10]. However, the mechanism of action of claudins in the kidney and their contribution to RCC heterogeneity remains incompletely understood, thus limiting their utility as biomarkers or targeted therapeutics.

Claudins, like other adhesion proteins, are organized at the tight junctions by scaffold proteins that may or may not participate in the adhesion process [11, 12]. The signaling scaffold IQGAP1 is a key regulator of epithelial polarity and adhesion both at the adherens and tight junctions [13, 14, 15] that controls renal cell polarity and adhesion to maintain the integrity and function of glomerular filtration in vitro [15, 16]. In mouse and human nephrons, IQGAP1 exhibits a diverse localization pattern that includes the collecting duct and the proximal and distal convoluted tubules [17, 18]. In podocytes, it localizes and interacts with the components of the slit diaphragm [16]. Although the precise role of IQGAP1 in kidney function and pathology remains undefined, we showed that IQGAP1 phosphorylation is elevated in five RCC cell lines [19]. We surmised that these localization and phosphorylation patterns imply a function in modulating secretion. This notion is supported by our discovery that IQGAP1 influences tight junction integrity by interplaying claudin‐2 and ‐4 expression and localization in the model Madin‐Darby Canine kidney (MDCK) cells [15] and in mouse kidneys [20]. As the ramification of this modulation in the human kidney is unknown, we tested it by interrogating The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and RCC tumors resected from 10 different patients. Overall, it appears that certain claudin expression and localization patterns appear to be inversely correlated with those of IQGAP1 in many of the tumors, presenting new potential biomarkers for clinical diagnostics and treatment.

Methods

RNA‐Seq

Pair‐wise gene expression correlation analysis between IQGAP1 and claudin‐2, ‐4, and ‐8 was performed using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis 2 (GEPIA2) tool, an interactive web server for analyzing RNA‐seq expression data from TCGA and Genotype‐Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases [21]. For each gene pairing, analyses were performed using non‐logarithmic transcripts per million kilobase (TPM) expression data for both Tumor (n = 523) and paired Normal (n = 72) samples from the kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) dataset in TCGA. Pearson coefficient was used by the GEPIA2 tool to calculate correlation (R), and the results were displayed using a log scale.

Human Kidney Samples

Sex and Race as Biological Variables

Male, female, and race were included in human samples.

Nonneoplastic kidney tissues were obtained from Rhode Island Hospital of Brown University. RCC tissue samples (Table 1) were collected as part of the University of Toledo Urology Biospecimen Bank (UT IRB #202366). Patient samples were collected with informed consent, and a total of 10 de‐identified samples were provided to the investigators of this study, including five female, four male, and one unknown sample from Black and White ethnicities (see below). The samples were fresh frozen in liquid nitrogen within 2 h after surgery and stored at −80°C until use.

Chromogen Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

RCC kidney samples were fixed in 10% formaldehyde in PBS, then paraffin‐embedded, and 5‐μm thick Coronal sections were used for IHC. After deparaffinization and hydration, the sections were incubated in 3% H2O2 solution in PBS at room temperature for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was performed in sodium citrate buffer (0.01 M, pH 6.0) in 95°C water bath for 20 min. Nonspecific antibody binding was blocked by incubation with 5% normal goat serum in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were stained overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: IQGAP1 (Santa Cruz, Cat. No. sc‐1079, 1:200 dilution) and claudin‐4 (1:200, Ab clonal). The slides were subsequently washed and incubated with biotin‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 30 min, and then with Horseradish Peroxidase Streptavidin (HRP Streptavidin) for 30 min. The sections were developed using the 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate for 5 min (SPlink HRP Rabbit Detection (DAB) kit, cat. no. D‐03‐18; OriGene Technologies Inc.) and counterstained with hematoxylin QS (H‐3404, Vector Laboratories). For IQGAP1‐Claudin double‐staining, IQGAP1 was stained red/magenta using ImmPACT Vector Red Substrate Kit, Alkaline Phosphatase (SK‐5105), and claudin was stained brown using Vectastain Elite ABC Universal kit, Peroxidase (PK‐7200). The images were captured using an Olympus microscope.

Immunoblotting

The RCC tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer (20 mmol/L Tris [pH 7.4], 137 mmol/L NaCl, 1% NP40, 10% glycerol supplemented with protease inhibitors, phosphatase inhibitors, 10 mM DTT and 5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid [EDTA]). Samples were washed with ice cold TRIS buffered saline (TBS) and homogenized in ice‐cold lysis buffer on ice. The lysate was centrifuged at 1000g for 20 min at 4°C to remove the cell debris. The supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube and protein concentration was measured using a BCA kit. A total of 25 mg of tissue extract was boiled for 5 min and loaded onto 10% or 15% SDS‐Tris‐glycine gel and resolved at room temperature. Proteins were then transferred to PVDF membrane blocked in TBST blocking buffer (TBS pH 7.4, 0.1% Tween 20) and 5% nonfat milk (or BSA for phospho‐proteins) for 1 h. The membranes were probed with target‐specific primary antibodies in TBST with 5% nonfat milk (or BSA for phospho‐protein) at room temperature for 2 h or 4°C overnight. The membranes were washed in TBST 3X for 15 min and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated appropriate secondary antibodies (1:7000, goat to mouse or rabbit) in TBST with 5% nonfat milk (or BSA for phospho‐protein) for 1 h followed by washing. The bands were visualized with ECL system and imaged by ChemiDoc XRS (Bio‐Rad) and analyzed by Image Lab software (version 6.0, Bio‐Rad). We used Coomassie Brilliant Blue G‐250 stained total protein loading on PVDF membrane as reference for quantifying the target protein [22] followed by quantification with Image Lab Software. Results

Correlation of Claudin‐2, ‐4, and ‐8 Expression With IQGAP1 Changes in RCC

To examine the potential relationships between IQGAP1 and claudin 2, claudin 4, and claudin 8, pair‐wise gene expression correlation analysis was performed using TPM data from kidney renal clear cell carcinoma samples in TCGA. As shown in Figure 1A, in the Normal samples, IQGAP1 expression was found to have significant negative correlation with the expression of claudin‐2 (R = −0.4, p = 0.00059) and significant positive correlation with expression of claudin‐4 (R = 0.31, p = 0.0081) and claudin‐8 (R = 0.36, p = 0.0019). Interestingly, in Tumor samples, expression correlation values between IQGAP1 and claudin‐2, ‐4, and ‐8 were dramatically different from those in Normal samples. Specifically, claudin‐2 was found to have significant positive correlation with IQGAP1 expression (R = 0.11, p = 0.0089), and claudin‐8 was found to have significant negative correlation with IQGAP1 expression (R = −0.11, p = 0.014). Finally, claudin‐4 expression showed no significant correlation with IQGAP1 expression (R = 0.032; p = 0.46) in Tumor samples (Figure 1A).

IQGAP1 Localizes to Human Kidney Tubules and Is Mis‐Localized in RCC

The relationship of IQGAP1 and claudin in non‐neoplastic or diseased human kidney tissues has not been shown. Thus, we first determined the distribution of IQGAP1 in non‐neoplastic human kidney samples and its relationship to claudin in RCC tumors. In non‐neoplastic human kidney tissue, IQGAP1 localized to the distal tubule brush border in the Medulla (Figure 1A, upper panel) and cortex (Figure 1A, lower panel). Low IQGAP1 expression, if any at all, was detected in the glomerulus. This localization was altered in RCC samples (Figure 1B). In atrophic tubules found in some carcinomas (e.g., RCC5) and in RCC tumors (e., RCC10), IQGAP1 localization (in magenta) was dispersed throughout the cytoplasm in the lumen, whereas claudin‐4 (in brown) remained largely at the junctions in the tubules (Figure 1B). A higher magnification of these events is provided in Figure 1C. Previously, we demonstrated that RNAi‐mediated silencing of IQGAP1 in MDCK cell lines or iqgap1 gene knockout enhanced claudin‐4 localization but reduced the localization and expression level of claudin‐2 [15, 20]. Accordingly, we quantified the expression levels of cell junction signaling proteins in the various RCC samples.

Differential Expression of Junctional Proteins in RCC

The protein expression levels of five different markers of cell junction proteins, including claudins, known to interact with IQGAP1 were compared by immunoblotting in the various RCC samples, using sample #1 as a randomly selected reference (Figure 2). These markers included IQGAP1, claudin‐4 and ‐8, E‐cadherin, and nephrin. The levels of the various markers varied significantly between the samples, indicating a personalized dysfunction of cell–cell contacts in these patients (Figure 2A–F). When compared to that of IQGAP1, an overall picture emerges showing that claudin‐4 and ‐8 expression appears to inversely correlate with that of IQGAP1, whereas expression of E‐cadherin nearly mirrors that of IQGAP1. As IQGAP1 is a kinase scaffold, and we previously found that silencing or knockout of IQGAP1 activates the stress signal C‐Jun N‐terminal kinase (JNK), we measured total and pJNK levels in the 10 tumors. These levels varied between the samples in such a way that total JNK 54 and 46 isoforms were similar to that of IQGAP1. However, the levels of the phosphoforms did not correlate with those of the respective total JNK isoforms, and the pJNK‐54 isoform size was shifted higher in tumor 3, whereas tumors 6, 7, and 10 had no JNK46 activity.

Discussion

Besides their structural role in epithelial cell polarity and barrier, claudins were proposed to play a role as a platform for signal transduction pathways, but the underlying molecular basis is unclear [23]. Here, we uncover a novel correlation between claudins and the signaling oncoprotein IQGAP1 in human RCC at the kidney epithelial tight junctions. Our interrogation of the TCGA database (Figure 1A) shows that the correlation between the expression levels of IQGAP1 and each of claudin‐2, ‐4, and ‐8 differentially changes in normal versus tumor samples, suggesting a delicate regulatory balance among these junctional proteins, at least at the RNA level. This notion is consistent with our previous finding that silencing IQGAP1 by RNAi in MDCK cell lines enhanced claudin‐4 localization but reduced the localization and expression level of claudin‐2 [15]. This also is consistent in wild type and iqgap1 −/− Murine models untreated or treated with cisplatin [20]. To test the clinical significance of these findings, we first verified that IQGAP1 localized to renal tubules in normal human samples (Figure 1), the sites of claudin action [5]. IQGAP1 also localized to the renal medulla, whose function is to maintain the body's fluid and electrolyte balance. While the precise role of IQGAP1 in the medulla is unknown, its fundamental function in secretion may be involved and will be the subject of future investigation. In the RCC samples with atrophic tubules, IQGAP1 localization was compromised, while that of claudin‐4 remained intact. This finding is consistent with our earlier report that silencing IQGAP1 in MDCK cells or in knockout mouse kidneys increases claudin‐4 recruitment to the cell contacts [15, 20] and further supports the idea that it is the mislocalization of IQGAP1, rather than mere expression level, that underlies claudin‐4 stabilization. Given that the major role of renal claudins is to determine permeability and selectivity of the various nephrons [5], their aberrant stabilization may imbalance renal epithelial function, leading to RCC. It is also plausible that claudin‐4 plays an additional role(s) in signal transduction pathways emanating from the junctions through IQGAP1 and that dysfunction of this process contributes to RCC inception and/or progression. Indeed, we have established that IQGAP1 mislocalization, rather than mere overexpression, plays a crucial role in inducing tumorigenesis in normal cells [24].

Protein expression levels of signaling molecules often influence their localization patterns and protein–protein interactions at specific subcellular locations. Claudin‐4 expression was lower in sample 5 (black male) and higher in sample 10 (white male), yet its localization was still intact (or enhanced) in the tight junctions in both tumors. In contrast, IQGAP1 expression was high in tumor 5 and low in tumor 10 but mislocalized in both. These data suggest that IQGAP1 mislocalization is primary to claudin dysfunction in these tumors, a notion that must be verified mechanistically in the future. The differential expression levels of several junctional markers on TCGA and in the 10 different RCC patient tumors (Figures 1 and 2) present the utility of these junctional proteins as personalized biomarkers and likely clinical targets in personalized medicine. Although samples from both sexes and from Caucasians and Blacks were investigated, a correlation between biomarker expression and localization and race or gender cannot be made at this point and will require a population‐wide study. Regardless, these findings suggest clinical significance of the IQGAP1‐junctional complex as a diagnostic tool and likely as a therapeutic target alone or in combination therapy in RCC. Interestingly, JNK, which is an IQGAP1‐binding kinase, is a known stress signaling hub linked to apoptosis [25], perhaps explaining why both JNK46 and 54 were activated in tumor 5 and in no other tumors (Figure 2) where atrophic tubules were prominent (Figure 1). Three other tumors exhibited patterns similar to that of tumor 5, whereas others had one or the other isoform activated. Thus, overall, this study sets the stage for future studies to explore the suitability of the IQGAP1‐claudin‐JNK signaling axis as a potential biomarker in RCC and a plausible therapeutic target.
