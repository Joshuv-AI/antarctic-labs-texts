Calyceal diverticulum masquerading as gross hydronephrosis: A diagnostic challenge
Authors: Komal Kumari Navik, Gaurav Raj, Roopal Malik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Calyceal diverticula are infrequent congenital anomalies of the renal collecting system that often serve as “radiological traps” due to their resemblance to hydronephrosis. This report details the case of a 38-year-old male whose. Initial ultrasonography demonstrated a large cystic lesion within the left renal sinus, raising suspicion for severe hydronephrosis. CT urography was subsequently performed to evaluate for obstructive uropathy. Delayed excretory phase imaging demonstrated contrast opacification within the lesion through a narrow communicating infundibulum, confirming the diagnosis of calyceal diverticulum. This case emphasizes that in the absence of an identifiable obstructive cause, delayed-phase imaging is mandatory to differentiate diverticula from obstructive uropathy, thereby preventing unnecessary surgical intervention.

Introduction

A calyceal diverticulum is a rare developmental malformation where a portion of the renal collecting system fails to mature normally. Their prevalence is estimated between 0.21% and 0.6% in the general population [1,2]. Pathologically, these are urothelium-lined, nonsecretory cavities that communicate with the renal pelvis or calyx via a narrow infundibulum [3].

They are usually discovered incidentally but may be present with flank pain, infection, hematuria, or nephrolithiasis secondary to urinary stasis.

Because these lesions appear as simple fluid collections on ultrasound, they are frequently mischaracterized as hydronephrosis or parapelvic cysts [4]. Definitive diagnosis requires contrast-enhanced imaging to visualize the slow transit of contrast through the narrow neck of the diverticulum.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old man presented with a 3-month history of vague intermittent left flank pain. There was no history of fever, dysuria, hematuria, urinary tract infection, or nephrolithiasis. Laboratory investigations, including renal function tests and urinalysis, were within normal limits.

Initial transabdominal ultrasonography demonstrated a large well-defined cystic lesion within the left renal sinus, suggestive of gross hydronephrosis (however the narrow infundibulum visible on the Fig. 1A was missed on initial USG). No renal calculi or obvious obstructive lesion was identified. Based on the large size and communication with the renal pelvis and upper pole major calyx, the lesion was consistent with a type II calyceal diverticulum.

There was no associated calyceal blunting, proximal ureteric dilatation, significant cortical thinning, or significant mass effect to suggest obstructive hydronephrosis. The patient was managed conservatively because symptoms were minimal and renal function was preserved.

Discussion

Calyceal diverticula are uncommon benign outpouchings of the renal collecting system that communicate with a calyx or renal pelvis through a narrow infundibulum [5]. These lesions are classified into 2 types. Type I diverticula communicate with a minor calyx and are more common, whereas type II diverticula communicate directly with the renal pelvis or a major calyx and are usually larger and more symptomatic.

The clinical presentation is often nonspecific. Most patients are asymptomatic, although some may present flank pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, hematuria, or renal stone disease due to urinary stasis within the diverticulum [6]. Milk-of-calcium calculi may occasionally develop and can serve as an important diagnostic clue.

Radiologically, calyceal diverticula can closely mimic hydronephrosis or parapelvic cysts, particularly on ultrasonography. Hydronephrosis typically demonstrates dilatation of the collecting system associated with calyceal blunting and varying degrees of ureteric dilatation. In contrast, calyceal diverticula usually appear as isolated cystic lesions without diffuse collecting system dilatation or ureteric enlargement. CT urography with delayed excretory phase imaging remains the imaging modality of choice for diagnosis [7]. Delayed filling of contrast material within the lesion through a narrow communicating neck is considered the hallmark imaging feature. In the present case, delayed imaging was essential in differentiating the lesion from obstructive hydronephrosis and confirming the diagnosis.

Management depends on symptom severity and associated complications. Asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic patients are usually managed conservatively. Intervention may be considered in cases complicated by recurrent infection, persistent pain, or stone formation and may include endoscopic, percutaneous, or laparoscopic approaches [8].

This case highlights the importance of considering calyceal diverticulum in the differential diagnosis of cystic renal sinus lesions and emphasizes the value of delayed excretory phase imaging in avoiding unnecessary surgical intervention.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the potential for calyceal diverticula to simulate severe hydronephrosis on screening exams. When a patient presents with apparent renal collecting system dilatation but no identifiable obstruction, clinicians should prioritize delayed-phase CT imaging. This approach ensures a definitive diagnosis based on the passive retrograde flow of contrast into the diverticulum, confirming its connection to the renal pelvis [8]. Recognition of this entity is essential to establish the correct diagnosis and avoid unnecessary intervention.
