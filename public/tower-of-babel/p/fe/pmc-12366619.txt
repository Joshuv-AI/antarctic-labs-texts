Exploring sex differences in mortality among acute myocardial infarction
Authors: Lorenzo Cangiano, Alice Bonomi, Nicola Cosentino, Olivia Leoni, Filippo Trombara, Veronika A Myasoedova, Paolo Poggio, Daniela Trabattoni, Piergiuseppe Agostoni, Giancarlo Marenzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractABSTRACTBackgroundWomen with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) experience higher mortality rates than men. This disparity is influenced by factors such as older age, greater comorbidity burden, atypical symptom presentation and delays in treatment. This study analysed patients with AMI (2003–2018) from the Lombardy Health Database (Italy) to examine sex differences in in-hospital and 1-year mortality and the role of age, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and postdischarge therapy.Methods and resultsAmong 263 564 patients with AMI (93 363 women, 170 201 men), primary and secondary endpoints were in-hospital and 1-year mortality, respectively. Path analysis evaluated the direct and indirect effects of sex on outcomes, incorporating age, PCI and postdischarge therapy as mediators. Women had higher in-hospital (10% vs 5%; p<0.0001) and 1-year mortality (24% vs 14%; p<0.0001) but were less likely to receive PCI (40% vs 61%; p<0.0001) and postdischarge therapy (dual antiplatelet therapy 53% vs 63%; ACE inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers 60% vs 64%; beta blockers 53% vs 61%; lipid-lowering drugs 45% vs 58%; p<0.0001 for all differences). After adjusting for age, major comorbidities, PCI and postdischarge treatment, mortality differences were no longer significant (adjusted OR 1.04; 95% CI 0.99 to 1.07 for in-hospital mortality) or even reversed (adjusted HR 0.94; 95% CI 0.92 to 0.96 for 1-year mortality). Path analysis showed that female sex directly contributed 12% to in-hospital mortality and 4% to 1-year mortality, while age and undertreatment accounted for most of the disparity (88% and 96%, respectively).ConclusionWomen with AMI face higher mortality largely due to older age and undertreatment during hospitalisation and after discharge. Addressing these gaps could improve outcomes.Keywords: Myocardial Infarction, Percutaneous Coronary Intervention, Epidemiology

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death for both men and women in middle- and high-income countries. Among the key contributors to these deaths are coronary artery disease and acute myocardial infarction (AMI), which, despite traditionally being perceived as male-dominant conditions, significantly affect both sexes.1 Emerging research highlights sex-related differences in patients with AMI, with consistent evidence showing higher in-hospital and long-term mortality rates, as well as worse outcomes, in women.2,10

Female patients with AMI tend to be older, with more comorbidities compared with their male counterparts.3,711 However, these factors alone do not fully explain the observed disparity in outcomes. Women are more likely to present with atypical symptoms and experience delays in diagnosis and treatment, both of which contribute to adverse outcomes.312,17 Additionally, studies point to the undertreatment of AMI in female patients, with women being less likely to receive evidence-based interventions, such as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI)2,6 or guideline-recommended pharmacological therapies.2,616 This undertreatment may be partially attributed to their older age and higher comorbidity burden, which can reduce eligibility for aggressive treatment.

Nevertheless, findings from a large prospective Swedish registry suggest that undertreatment itself may represent an independent and modifiable risk factor for worse outcomes in women with AMI, as women with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) were less likely to undergo coronary angiography or PCI regardless of age.21 The benefits of evidence-based treatments—such as PCI22 23 and pharmacological therapies (eg, dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), ACE inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers (ACEi/ARB), beta blockers and lipid-lowering drugs)—are well established both in men and women.24,28 Hence, current guidelines recommend no differences in treatment approaches based on sex.29

In this study, we used population-based administrative data from Lombardy, Italy’s most populated region, to assess in-hospital and 1-year mortality among women hospitalised with AMI. We compared these outcomes to those of men with AMI, with a particular focus on the impact of age, PCI use and postdischarge guideline-recommended pharmacological therapies in influencing these endpoints.

Methods

Data source

The present study leveraged linkable administrative health databases from the Lombardy region of Italy, encompassing a population of approximately 10 million individuals between 2000 and 2019. These databases include a comprehensive population registry with demographic data on all residents, in addition to detailed records on hospital admissions and pharmaceutical prescriptions.30 Data access was granted through an official agreement between the Centro Cardiologico Monzino, IRCCS, Milan, Italy, and the Regional Health Ministry of Lombardy.30

Italy’s healthcare system is publicly funded and universally accessible to all residents, irrespective of socioeconomic status or employment. Each individual is assigned a unique personal identification number within the National Civil Registration System, facilitating consistent and accurate data integration.30 All registered residents are managed under the National Health System (NHS), which provides complete coverage and systematically collects healthcare utilisation data, including prescription records, diagnostic codes and follow-up duration.30

The pharmaceutical prescription database captures the drug name, the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification code and the dispensing date for medications reimbursed by the NHS.30 Hospitalisation data include admission and discharge dates, in-hospital mortality, primary diagnoses and up to five coexisting clinical conditions and procedures. Diagnoses are standardised across all Italian hospitals using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) codes, and are compiled by hospital specialists responsible for patient care. These records are further validated by healthcare facilities for reimbursement purposes, ensuring high data reliability.30

Postdischarge pharmacotherapy was identified using the prescription database. All datasets are interconnected through a unique identification code, which is irreversibly anonymised before data delivery to researchers.30

Study population

Patients with a hospitalisation due to AMI (either STEMI or non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) (ICD-9-CM code 410.x)) from 1 January 2003 through 31 December 2018 were included in the present analyses. Only hospitalisations in which AMI-associated ICD-9 code was listed as a primary diagnosis were abstracted.30 When patients were transferred between hospitals, we evaluated the complete episode of care. Since medical data were recorded in the Lombardy registry from January 2000, medical history was available for all patients for at least 3 years before admission. Data collection was achieved by two trained reviewers.

Study outcomes

The primary endpoint of the study was in-hospital mortality. One-year all-cause mortality was considered as a secondary endpoint. Patients were followed up from the index admission date until death, migration or up to the end of 1-year follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were evaluated using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the χ2 test. Continuous variables were described as mean±SD and compared using Student’s t-test.

Sex-based differences in in-hospital mortality were analysed using logistic regression models, with results expressed as ORs and 95% CIs. The association with 1-year mortality was investigated using Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier curves, with results presented as HRs and 95% CIs. The models were adjusted for age, major comorbidities, PCI treatment and guideline-recommended therapy prescription after hospital discharge (for 1-year mortality analysis only). All analyses were conducted for the overall population and separately for patients with STEMI and NSTEMI.

The direct and indirect effects of sex on in-hospital and 1-year mortality were assessed using path analysis.31 Age, PCI treatment and guideline-recommended therapy prescription (postdischarge) were included as mediators in the models. Path analysis was conducted using the SAS Proc CALIS procedure (SAS Institute) based on structural equation modelling, and the strength of relationships was quantified using standardised β coefficients.

Temporal trends in in-hospital and 1-year mortality over the study period, stratified by sex, were analysed using the Mantel-Haenszel χ2 test. To assess changes in the mortality gap between women and men over time, β coefficients and 95% CIs were calculated.

A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute).

As expected, women had significantly higher in-hospital and 1-year mortality rates compared with men, both in the overall population and within the subgroups of patients with STEMI and NSTEMI (figure 2). However, after adjustment for age, major comorbidities, PCI treatment and the prescription of guideline-recommended therapies after hospital discharge (for 1-year mortality only), sex-related differences in both in-hospital and 1-year mortality were no longer evident (adjusted OR 1.04; 95% CI 0.99 to 1.07) or were even reversed (adjusted HR 0.94; 95% CI 0.92 to 0.96), respectively. This pattern was particularly pronounced among patients with NSTEMI, whereas women with STEMI continued to exhibit higher in-hospital mortality and a 1-year mortality risk comparable to that of men (table 2).

Discussion

In this study, based on population-level administrative data from Lombardy, we observed significant differences in in-hospital and 1-year mortality rates between men and women admitted with AMI. Women experienced higher mortality rates, had more complicated hospital stays and showed increased incidences of cardiogenic shock, atrial fibrillation and acute heart failure. While women were generally older and had a higher burden of comorbidities, they were less likely to receive acute invasive treatments, such as PCI, or be prescribed guideline-recommended therapies at discharge, including DAPT, lipid-lowering agents, beta blockers and ACEi/ARBs. These findings align with previous literature and confirm, within a large Italian cohort, the higher mortality rates among female patients with AMI during hospitalisation and the 1-year follow-up.2,10 Furthermore, our analysis highlights a consistent pattern of undertreatment in women, particularly regarding PCI and evidence-based pharmacological therapies, as widely reported in prior research.2,1016

Although prior studies have shown worse outcomes for women with AMI, both in hospital and at 1 year, the specific relationship between undertreatment and mortality has been less extensively investigated and quantified. For instance, Hao et al6 demonstrated that the sex disparity in hospital mortality within a large Chinese cohort lost statistical significance after adjusting for clinical characteristics and acute treatment. Conversely, in their analysis of 1-year outcomes after AMI in young patients (18–55 years) in the USA, Sawano et al32 found that the poorer outcomes among young women were partially linked to psychosocial factors, with a minimal impact from AMI-related or predischarge treatment variables.

While previous studies have examined undertreatment and its effects on mortality, our study extends this work by quantifying these associations using a large contemporary dataset and path analysis. By leveraging the large size of our cohort, we conducted a path analysis that showed the excess mortality observed in women is only partially explained by their older age, with a substantial proportion of this excess mortality being directly linked to undertreatment. Specifically, over 30% of the excess in-hospital mortality was associated with the lack of PCI, while the same percentage was linked to a combination of lack of PCI and underprescription of guideline-recommended pharmacological therapies for 1-year mortality. Importantly, after adjusting for age, major comorbidities and treatment disparities, sex-related differences in in-hospital and 1-year mortality were no longer evident or were even reversed, respectively. This effect was particularly notable among patients with NSTEMI, whereas women with STEMI continued to experience higher in-hospital mortality and a 1-year mortality risk comparable to that of men. Our findings may seem in contrast with those of Sawano et al32; however, differences in the study population are critical. While Sawano et al focused on younger patients with AMI (under 55 years), our study analysed a broader real-world population that included older individuals. Younger women may experience AMI due to causes other than the most frequent type 1 MI of atherosclerotic origin, such as coronary artery dissection or spasm.33 For these conditions, PCI and the guideline-recommended therapies evaluated in this study are less applicable and less likely to improve outcomes. Additionally, prior research has consistently reported worse prognosis for younger women with AMI compared with their male counterparts.18 21 32

The reasons for undertreatment in women remain speculative and challenging to ascertain from the available data. Nonetheless, our findings underscore that undertreatment is an independent negative prognostic factor, irrespective of age. Prior literature has repeatedly documented sex disparities in the use of PCI and evidence-based pharmacological therapies, often attributed to concerns about side effects and bleeding risk in women. However, lipid-lowering agents, one of the drug classes most strongly associated with improved 1-year survival after AMI, have a favourable safety profile.28 Studies such as Redfors et al’s analysis of the SWEDEHEART registry have highlighted a lower likelihood of P2Y12 antagonist prescription for women, even after adjusting for in-hospital bleeding complications.21 This persistent underprescription of antiplatelet agents appears to stem more from concerns about postdischarge bleeding risks than actual complications. Nevertheless, given the significant mortality burden associated with undertreatment, it is crucial to emphasise that current clinical guidelines do not support sex-based differences in the use of PCI or medical therapies. Finally, temporal trend analysis from 2003 to 2018 demonstrated a twofold rise in the use of PCI, accompanied by a substantial increase in the prescription of guideline-directed medical therapies among both women and men. Despite this progress, the persistent treatment gap between men and women underscores the need for targeted efforts to address these disparities effectively. Interestingly, between 2003 and 2018, the proportion of patients presenting with NSTEMI significantly increased in the overall population—from 34% to 56%—as well as within both sexes. This trend is likely attributable to the incorporation of high-sensitivity troponin assays into the diagnostic workflow.

Our findings have important clinical implications, highlighting that undertreatment among female patients with AMI represents a modifiable risk factor. These results support adopting a more proactive and equitable approach to invasive procedures and pharmacological treatments for women, with PCI and guideline-recommended therapies withheld only in cases with clear and well-documented contraindications. Tackling these disparities could substantially reduce sex-based differences in mortality and lead to better outcomes for female patients with AMI.

Administrative databases are a valuable tool for describing outcomes in large cohorts and reflecting the real-world clinical settings, as they collect data over time in a standardised and cost-effective manner. However, certain limitations inherent to retrospective studies based on administrative datasets should be acknowledged. First, administrative data can be subject to systematic biases, as the quality of the data depends on the accuracy of coding. Our analysis relied on precise coding of AMI and other conditions, and biases may arise from under-reporting or changes in diagnosis and coding practices over time. However, the endpoints evaluated in our study—particularly in-hospital and 1-year mortality—are less likely to be affected by coding error. Nevertheless, we were unable to determine whether the in-hospital complications, specifically cardiogenic shock and cardiac arrest, occurred at the time of hospital presentation or during the hospital stay. Second, specific clinical variables and laboratory data closely linked to AMI prognosis—such as key cardiovascular risk factors (eg, smoking status and dyslipidaemia), left ventricular ejection fraction, renal function, extent and severity of coronary artery disease, completeness of myocardial revascularisation and in-hospital pharmacological treatments—were not available in our dataset. We also lacked information on the reasons for not performing PCI in patients managed conservatively, such as late presentation, severe comorbidities, lack of significant obstructive coronary stenosis or differing hospitalisation settings. Furthermore, because this study relied on administrative data without clinical event adjudication, we were unable to distinguish between cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular causes of death. Lastly, the generalisability of our findings to other regions of Italy or other countries may be limited, as healthcare systems, treatment practices and population characteristics vary across settings.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that women hospitalised with AMI experience significantly higher in-hospital and 1-year mortality rates compared with men. The primary drivers of this increased risk are older age and undertreatment, both during hospitalisation (PCI) and after discharge (guideline-recommended therapies). These findings underscore the urgent need to address sex-based disparities in AMI treatment, which could lead to a meaningful reduction in mortality rates and significantly improve outcomes for female patients with AMI.
