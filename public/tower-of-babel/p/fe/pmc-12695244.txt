Safety and efficacy of bariatric or metabolic surgery in septuagenarians: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Patricia Ortega, Ruifeng Yao, Chioma Izzi-Engbeaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Increasing life expectancy has expanded the older population of adults living with obesity. Effective and sustainable treatment strategies are essential, due to the significant impact of obesity in the elderly on health care systems. We aimed to determine the safety of metabolic/bariatric surgery in septuagenarians.Methods:A comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, Scopus, and Web of Science for studies published up to 31 December 2024, using Boolean logic and MeSH terms. Eligible studies involved patients aged ≥70 years undergoing primary bariatric or metabolic surgery that reported on 30-day morbidity and mortality, length of hospital stay, weight loss outcomes (BMI, %TWL, %EWL), and improvements in comorbidities. Two reviewers independently conducted study selection, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment using the ROBINS-I tool, with discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer. Publication bias was assessed via funnel plots and Egger’s test. Data were pooled using a random-effects model.Results:Out of 5000 articles, 12 studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising 197,194 patients, including 2528 septuagenarians. Septuagenarians had a mean age of 73.9 ± 2.6 years, and a lower preoperative BMI (42.4 ± 8.0 kg/m2) compared to younger patients (46.0 ± 7.8 kg/m2, P < 0.0001). In patients ≥70 years, the pooled 30-day morbidity was 9.0% (95% CI: 6.0–13.0%) and mortality 0.7% (95% CI: 0.0–1.2%). Compared to younger patients, those aged ≥70 years had higher odds of 30-day complications (OR 1.55, 95% CI: 1.26–1.91, P < 0.0001) and mortality (OR 4.31, 95% CI: 1.53–12.11, P = 0.006). No significant differences were found in hospital stay or late complications. Although postoperative BMI was similar, septuagenarians had lower %TWL and %EWL, with comparable improvements in type 2 diabetes and hypertension.Conclusions:Our findings support the feasibility of metabolic and bariatric surgery in septuagenarians, albeit with a higher short-term risk profile, necessitating careful patient selection and management.

Introduction

Obesity is a persistent global health challenge given its prevalence has been steadily rising over recent decades. In 2022, 890 million citizens worldwide were living with obesity[1] and this is predicted to rise to 1.53 billion individuals by 2035[2]. Concurrently, the global population is aging. Throughout the 20th century, life expectancy at birth in high-income countries increased by around 30 years, primarily due to significant progress in public health measures and medical advancements[3], leading to a proportional increase in older people living with obesity. As both aging and obesity impose significant burdens on health care systems, identifying effective and sustainable treatments for this population is increasingly essential.

Metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS) has emerged as the most effective long-term intervention for obesity, offering sustained weight loss, resolution of comorbidities, improved quality of life, and extended life expectancy[4,5]. While there has been a considerable increase in MBS procedures performed in patients aged 60 years and above over the past decade[6,7], patients older than 70 years old are often considered at higher risk compared to younger individuals. Aging naturally leads to a reduction in physiological reserve and functional ability, making older adults more susceptible to frailty. Similarly, frailty is linked to unfavorable outcomes, prolonged hospital stays, loss of independence, and an elevated risk of complications and mortality following surgery[8]. However, while frailty often correlates with older age, chronological age alone is not a reliable predictor of surgical outcomes[9,10].

Considering these factors, MBS in septuagenarians remains a topic of debate. The aim of the present study is to synthesize current evidence in this important patient group, providing a more reliable analysis of the impact of MBS on perioperative morbidity, weight loss and metabolic outcomes compared to younger individuals.

Material and methods

Search strategy, study selection, and data extraction

A systematic review was conducted following Cochrane recommendations[11] and according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)[12] guidelines and reported in line with Assessing the Methodological Quality of Systematic Review (AMSTAR) Guidelines[13].

A comprehensive search strategy was conducted with the assistance of a professional research librarian using Pubmed/MEDLINE, Embase (both via Ovid), Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, Scopus and Web of Science databases, using Boolean logic and Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms. We searched for publications between database inception and 31 December 2024. Eligible studies were required to contain terms related to “bariatric surgery,” “metabolic surgery,” “septuagenarians,” “elderly.” The detailed search strategy is provided in the Supplementary material (Supplementary Digital Content material 1, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/F71). To ensure a comprehensive search, the reference lists of articles and reviews were manually screened. All eligible studies published in peer-reviewed journals were included in the study analysis. Covidence software (www.covidence.org) was used to manage uploaded studies. Two authors independently conducted the article selection process, initially screening titles and abstracts followed by a full-text assessment for eligibility and inclusion. Any articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria were appropriately identified and excluded. In cases where discrepancies arose during the selection process, a third author was consulted, and consensus was reached. Relevant information, including study characteristics and patient demographics were extracted from each included article by two authors independently using a customized form specifically created for this study.

Eligibility criteria, objectives, and outcomes

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prospective controlled studies, matched cohort studies, retrospective cohort and case-control studies were considered for inclusion; whereas reviews, editorials, case reports, conference abstracts, and meta-analyses were excluded.

The inclusion criteria encompassed patients aged ≥70 years undergoing primary MBS [Roux en Y gastric bypass (RYGB), sleeve gastrectomy (SG), laparoscopic adjustable gastric band (LAGB), one anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB), single anastomosis with duodenal switch (SADS), equivalent to single anastomosis duodeno-ileal bypass with sleeve gastrectomy (SADI-S)]. The exclusion criteria included (1) studies that presented data only for patients aged <70 years; (2) revisional MBS. The primary objective was to assess postoperative outcomes, including overall morbidity and mortality within 30 days of surgery, as well as length of stay (LoS) if available. Additionally, weight loss outcomes, such as postoperative body mass index (BMI), percentage of total weight loss (%TWL), and percentage of excess weight loss (%EWL), were evaluated. The secondary objective focused on the improvement or resolution of obesity-related comorbidities, specifically hypertension (HTN) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). For clarity, the terms septuagenarians and patients aged ≥70 years are used interchangeably throughout this manuscript to refer to the study population.

This review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO, the international register of systematic reviews.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias (RoB) for the included studies was assessed using the Risk of Bias In Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I). Each study received a low, moderate, serious, or critical risk of bias in seven domains: confounding; selection of participants; classification of interventions; deviation from intended interventions; missing data; measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Each study was independently reviewed by two authors, and discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion to ensure consensus. The ROBINS-I ratings were presented using the robvis tool as “traffic light” plots[14]. Funnel plots were visually inspected for asymmetry, and Egger’s test was applied to quantify bias in outcomes from more than 10 studies, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using STATA version 18.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochrane Q test and I2 statistic, which quantifies the proportion of total variability across studies due to heterogeneity. I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were considered to indicate low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively[15]. Regardless of heterogeneity, the random effects restricted maximum likelihood model was used to pool results, as it provides more robust-effect estimates than fixed-effect models. Forest plots were generated to visualize the results, presenting individual study estimates, and the overall pooled effects. Outcomes reported on different scales were standardized to ensure comparability across studies. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed, systematically removing individual studies to assess their influence on the pooled effect estimates. To address between-study heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed when feasible, based on available study-level characteristics such as type of MBS.

Proportion meta-analysis

A single-arm meta-analysis was performed to aggregate data from all eligible studies reporting outcomes of interest in septuagenarians undergoing MBS. Proportion rates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used for binary endpoints while pooled means with 95% CIs were calculated for continuous outcomes.

Comparative meta-analysis

A subgroup of studies with a comparator cohort (patients younger than 70 years) was included in a comparative meta-analysis. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were used for dichotomous variables and mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs were calculated for continuous variables.

Artificial intelligence (AI) was not used in the research or manuscript development and this work has been reported in line with TITAN criteria[16]. The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

The most frequently reported early complications included surgical site infections (SSIs), pneumonia, pulmonary embolism (PE), and atrial fibrillation (AF), with notable variations across procedures and study populations. Late complications reported in patients aged ≥70 years included anastomotic strictures, gastroesophageal reflux, internal and port-site hernias, band-related issues (such as slippage, erosion, and removal), as well as abdominal pain requiring reoperation, poor oral intake, chronic diarrhoea, and strictures. While several studies did not report late complications separately by age group, those that did generally described a low but heterogeneous rate of late complications among septuagenarians, with some studies reporting no late complications in this subgroup. For patients aged <70 years, late complications were more frequently reported and included epigastric pain, reflux, strictures, chronic diarrhoea, and multiple reoperations. Patients aged ≥70 years had a significant risk of 30-day morbidity following MBS. The meta-analysis of all studies reported an overall 30-day morbidity rate of 9.2% (95% CI: 5.8%–12.6%), with individual study proportions ranging from 0.9%[23] to 26.0%[26]. Heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 80.56%), indicating high variability among studies (Supplementary Digital Content material Sp1, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/F71). The Egger’s P-value was 0.044, suggesting a statistically significant small-study effect. This indicates a potential risk of publication bias, meaning smaller studies might be reporting more extreme effects. The funnel plot (Supplementary Digital Content material Sp1, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/F71) also visually suggests asymmetry, further supporting this interpretation.

A subgroup analysis of seven studies[19,21,24–28] reporting 30-day morbidity specifically for RYGB showed a pooled complication rate of 10.8% (95% CI: 7.7%–13.9%). Heterogeneity was low (I2 = 25.5%), suggesting consistency across studies (Supplementary Digital Content material Sp1, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/F71). A subgroup meta-analysis of seven studies[17,19,20,24,26,27] reporting 30-day morbidity following SG showed a pooled complication rate of 6.6% (95% CI: 5.2%–7.9%). Heterogeneity was negligible (I2 = 0.07%), indicating high consistency across studies. Given the disproportionate influence of one study (Pechman et al[25].), which contributed nearly 59% of the total weight, a sensitivity analysis was performed excluding it. The resulting pooled morbidity rate was 6.7% (95% CI: 3.2%–10.1%), with low heterogeneity (I2 = 26.1%) (Supplementary Digital Content material Sp1, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/F71).

Patients aged ≥70 years had a 0.7% risk of 30-day mortality (95% CI: 0.0%–1.2%), as estimated from the 12 studies. Mortality proportions ranged from 0.0% to 1.0%, with the highest number of deaths reported in Pechman et al[25] (1%, 95% CI: 0.5%–1.5%). The overall mortality estimate was statistically significant (Z = 2.97, P = 0.0030). Furthermore, heterogeneity was low (I2 = 15.99%), indicating minimal variability across studies (Supplementary Digital Content material Sp2, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/F71). The Egger’s P-value was 0.23, indicating that there was no small-study effect. The funnel plot appears symmetrical, suggesting the absence of publication bias.

Patients aged ≥70 years had a 3.5% risk of 30-day readmission (95% CI: 1.5%–5.5%), based on eight studies[17,20–24,27,28]. Individual study proportions varied, with the highest readmission rate reported by Al-Kurd et al[17] at 10% (95% CI: 0.0%–20.7%), while several studies reported 0% readmission. Heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 33.11%), suggesting some variability among studies but no major inconsistencies (Supplementary Digital Content material Sp3, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/F71).

Patients aged ≥70 years had a significantly higher risk of experiencing 30-day complications compared to those <70 years, as reported by six studies[17,18,20,24,25,27]. The pooled OR was 1.55 (95% CI: 1.26–1.91; P < 0.0001), indicating 55% increased odds of complications in the older cohort. However, heterogeneity was low (I2 = 15.21%), suggesting consistency in reported outcomes across studies (Supplementary Digital Content material S1, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/F71).

Patients aged ≥70 years had a significantly higher risk of 30-day mortality compared to their younger counterparts[17,18,20,24,25,27]. The meta-analysis yielded an OR of 4.31 (95% CI: 1.53–12.11; P = 0.006), indicating a more than fourfold increased risk. Heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 35.40%), reflecting some variability among studies (Supplementary Digital Content material S2, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/F71). A sensitivity analysis excluding the study by Pechman et al[25], which accounted for almost half of the total weight in the primary analysis, was performed to assess the robustness of the findings. The updated pooled OR decreased to 2.17 (95% CI: 0.70–6.70); however, the association was no longer statistically significant (P = 0.178). Heterogeneity was absent (I2 = 0%), suggesting consistency among the remaining studies. Based on data from three studies[17,20,27] with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), there was no statistically significant difference in the risk of readmission within 30 days in patients aged ≥70 years compared with younger patients (OR 1.33 [0.93-1.89], P = 0.1193) (Supplementary Digital Content material S3, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/F71).

The meta-analysis of four studies[17,18,20,25], including 1582 patients aged ≥70 years and 162 979 patients <70 years, found no statistically significant difference in LoS. The MD was 0.37 days (95% CI: − 0.01 to 0.76; P = 0.0579), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 80.90%) (Supplementary Digital Content material S4, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/F71). To explore potential differences by procedure, a subgroup analysis was conducted for studies reporting LoS following SG only. Based on two studies[17,20], the pooled mean difference in LoS between patients aged ≥70 years and <70 years was 0.04 days (95% CI:−0.23 to 0.30), with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), indicating no significant difference between age groups. In contrast, a separate subgroup analysis of studies reporting LoS following SG and RYGB combined[18,25] showed a significantly longer hospital stay in patients ≥70 years, with a pooled mean difference of 0.70 days (95% CI: 0.58 to 0.82) and no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) (Supplementary Digital Content material S4, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/F71).

Only three studies reported late complications both in patients aged ≥70 years and patients aged <70 years[18,20,24]. No significant differences were found between groups, OR 0.82 (95% CI: 0.22-3.02; P = 0.76) with high heterogeneity (I2 = 66.3%) (Supplementary Digital Content material S5, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/F71).

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed based on procedure type (SG and RYGB) where data were available. Discussion

This meta-analysis provides the most comprehensive synthesis of evidence regarding the safety and outcomes of MBS in patients aged ≥70 years. Our findings indicate that MBS in septuagenarians is associated with a significantly higher risk of perioperative morbidity and mortality compared to younger individuals. While there is evidence of meaningful weight loss and improvement in obesity-related comorbidities, these benefits must be carefully weighed against the elevated surgical risks in this age group.

Even though the single-arm 30-day mortality was 0.7% (95% CI: 0.0%—1.2%), the comparative analysis revealed that septuagenarians had a more than fourfold increased risk of perioperative mortality compared to younger individuals (OR = 4.31; 95% CI: 1.53–12.11; P = 0.006). However, the sensitivity analysis excluding Pechman et al[25], which contributed over 47% of the weight in the primary meta-analysis, revealed that this association was no longer statistically significant (OR 2.17; 95% CI: 0.70-6.70; P = 0.178).

Procedure-specific subgroup analyses within the single-arm analysis offer important context for interpreting 30-day morbidity in septuagenarians undergoing MBS. The observed difference between RYGB and SG (10.8% vs. 6.6%, respectively) suggests that SG may be associated with a more favorable short-term safety profile in this age group. Importantly, the low heterogeneity observed in both subgroup analyses enhances confidence in the consistency of these estimates across studies. These findings are consistent with prior research indicating that, in this age group, SG tends to carry a lower risk of early postoperative complications than RYGB[29,30].

n contrast, long-term complication analysis did not demonstrate differences between the younger and older groups. Although revisional surgery was reported in some studies, its frequency did not significantly differ between age groups, suggesting that older patients tolerate primary MBS relatively well when appropriately selected. These findings align with previous studies[19,31] suggesting that age alone should not be an exclusion criterion for MBS but must be considered and on a case-by-case basis, with thorough preoperative evaluation and optimization.

With the growing use of minimally invasive surgical techniques and the implementation of enhanced recovery after surgery protocols, postoperative outcomes following MBS have significantly improved, leading to a steady reduction in hospital stay.[32–34] Nevertheless, septuagenarians are a population who need special attention. Indications for MBS in patients in their seventies are generally consistent with those for younger adults, with careful consideration of physiologic age, frailty, and functional status. The American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery (ASMBS) and the International Federation for the Surgery of Obesity (IFSO) endorse MBS in patients aged 70 years and older, stating that chronological age alone should not be a contraindication if the patient is otherwise an appropriate surgical candidate[35]. Potential advantages for septuagenarians include marked and sustained improvements in metabolic diseases, reduction in medication burden, and improved quality of life. In fact, studies consistently show that while the absolute magnitude of weight loss and comorbidity resolution may be somewhat lower than in younger cohorts, the clinical benefits remain substantial and durable in this age group[36,37].

Frailty is a key determinant of surgical risk in older adults and is increasingly recognized as a more relevant predictor of postoperative outcomes than chronological age alone. It encompasses a range of physiological impairments, including decreased muscle strength, reduced functional reserve, and an increased inflammatory state, all of which can predispose patients to poorer surgical outcomes[38,39]. Preoperative frailty assessment tools, such as the Clinical Frailty Scale and Edmonton Frail Scale[40], have been proposed as essential components in the preoperative evaluation of elderly patients undergoing surgery. Studies have shown that frail individuals have a significantly higher risk of postoperative complications, longer hospital stays, and increased mortality[41]. The American Society of Anaesthesiologists, the American College of Surgeons and the American Geriatrics Society recommend a comprehensive preoperative assessment among older adults that includes not only evaluation of frailty but also cognitive function, functional status, nutritional status, polypharmacy, and psychosocial factors[42,43]. Unfortunately, none of the studies in this meta-analysis reported formal frailty metrics or preoperative optimization, and only two (Belluzzi et al[19] and Pechman et al[25]) described functional status in broad terms such as independence or dependence. The absence of standardized frailty data limits the ability to stratify risk more precisely in our analysis. Nevertheless, we strongly believe that incorporating a thorough preoperative geriatric assessment into clinical decision-making alongside the bariatric multidisciplinary team (MDT) may improve patient selection and optimize perioperative care strategies to mitigate surgical risks in this population.

Our study demonstrates significant weight loss following MBS. However, the extent of weight reduction was slightly lower compared to younger individuals. Several mechanisms may explain the attenuated weight loss response among this patient group. Elderly and frail patients often exhibit dysregulation of key metabolic pathways, including chronic inflammation and altered gut hormone responses[44,45], all of which are potential causes of attenuated postoperative weight loss. Furthermore, reduced physical activity and sarcopenia, both common among older cohorts, can lead to lower energy expenditure and reduced capacity for postoperative lifestyle modifications, negatively contributing to this issue[46–48].

With regards to comorbidity improvement or resolution, MBS in patients ≥70 years old did not show differences in comparison to younger individuals in our meta-analysis. MBS improves insulin sensitivity, pancreatic β-cell function and induces gut hormone changes in people living with T2DM[49–51]. However, it has been demonstrated that patients with longstanding T2DM may experience lower remissions rates and later disease relapse[52,53]. Since advanced T2DM at the time of surgery is one of the strongest predictors of diabetes relapse, it is likely that patients ≥70 years old undergoing MBS had a longer disease duration, making them less likely to achieve remission. However, due to limitations within the included studies, we were unable to stratify patients based on diabetes duration, as this information was not consistently reported. Similarly, reductions in antihypertensive medication use were observed. This fact supports the cardiovascular benefits of MBS among this patient group despite the expected vascular aging and reduced autonomic adaptability[53,54], which might impact improvement of hypertension.

This study has several limitations, the most important being the quality and design of the included studies. All were retrospective observational studies, which are inherently prone to selection bias, confounding, and other limitations in internal validity. Using the ROBINS-I tool, 11 studies were assessed as having a moderate risk of bias and one as having serious risk of bias. Accordingly, the meta-analysis is based on evidence of generally low methodological quality. This restricts the strength and certainty of the conclusions, and the results should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution. While the findings suggest that MBS may be feasible and effective in selected septuagenarian patients, more robust prospective data are needed to support these outcomes. Another key limitation of our analysis is the high heterogeneity observed in long-term complications (I2 = 66.3%) and BMI changes (I2 = 73.23%), probably due to differences in study design, operative technique, comorbidity burden and/or duration, and definitions of adverse events. Furthermore, while perioperative outcomes, weight loss, and comorbidity resolution were assessed, functional outcomes and quality-of-life improvements were not reported.

While our findings suggest that MBS may offer meaningful benefits in terms of weight loss and comorbidity improvement for selected patients aged ≥70 years, these results must be interpreted with caution. Therefore, MBS in septuagenarians should be considered on an individual basis, following a thorough MDT assessment. Given the rising prevalence of obesity in older populations, future high-quality prospective studies are needed to evaluate safety, functional outcomes, and quality of life to better guide decision-making in this specific demographic.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis suggests that MBS may be a feasible and beneficial option for well-selected patients aged ≥70 years, offering significant weight loss and metabolic benefits despite higher perioperative risks relative to younger patients. Age alone should not be a contraindication, but decisions should be individualized. Future high-quality prospective studies are needed to better define optimal patient selection criteria and assess outcomes in this growing population.
