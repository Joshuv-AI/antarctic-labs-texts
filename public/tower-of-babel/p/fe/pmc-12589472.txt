Incremental prognostic value of ischemic score quantified by stress dynamic CT myocardial perfusion in patients with and without diabetes mellitus
Authors: Xiaoling Tao, Xueyan Hou, Qingshuang Liu, Shuang Pan, Xueqin Jiang, Mengnan Zhang, Ruili Qin, Hao Wang, Zhaoqian Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to assess the incremental prognostic value of ischemic score quantified by stress dynamic CT myocardial perfusion imaging (CT-MPI) in patients with and without diabetes mellitus (DM). 331 patients (92 patients with DM) with suspected coronary artery disease (CAD) who underwent coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) and CT-MPI were included. Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) included cardiac death, acute coronary syndrome (ACS), late revascularization, and hospitalizations attributable to heart failure. Ischemic score was assessed based on reduced myocardial blood flow across 16 segments. An ischemic perfusion defect (IPD) was identified as ischemic score ≥ 4. During a median follow-up of 15 (9, 40) months, 49 patients (14.8%) experienced MACE. IPD was an independent predictor for the development of MACE in both DM (HR: 11.64, p = 0.019) and non-DM patient groups (HR: 8.66, p = 0.004). Adding IPD to clinical risk factors and CCTA characteristics increased the C-index for predicting MACE from 0.72 to 0.80 in DM patients (p = 0.005) and from 0.76 to 0.83 in non-DM patients (p = 0.006). Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a complex metabolic disease, often combined with multiple comorbidities, associated with a significant increase in cardiovascular morbidity and mortality1. Patients with DM are 2–4 times more likely to experience major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) compared to those without DM patients2,3. Therefore, it is crucial to provide risk assessment for DM patients with suspected coronary artery disease (CAD).

Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) is an anatomical imaging technique that noninvasively assesses the presence and extent of coronary artery disease and provides prognostic information by demonstrating stenosis and plaque characteristics of coronary arteries. However, CCTA is limited in its ability to assess the hemodynamic significance of coronary artery stenosis, and further functional assessment of myocardial ischemia is often required4,5. Furthermore, diabetic patients are frequently comorbid with coronary microvascular disease (CMD)6, and these abnormalities may be missed by CCTA, limiting its usefulness for prognostic assessment in diabetic patients.

Positron emission tomography (PET) myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) and stress perfusion cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) have the advantage of quantifying absolute myocardial blood flow (MBF) and calculating myocardial flow reserve, which can be used to diagnose myocardial ischemia and identify CMD, and their prognostic value in patients with DM and without DM has been demonstrated7–10. However, because to their unavailability and high cost, PET and CMR have been used limited. In contrast, stress dynamic computed tomography (CT) myocardial perfusion imaging (CT-MPI) can identify myocardial ischemia in a noninvasive, quick, and accurate manner, with little radiation dosage and contrast agent concerns. According to studies, CT-MPI has diagnostic accuracy equivalent to CMR and PET, as well as superior than single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)11. In recent years, the value of stress dynamic CT-MPI in the prognosis of suspected or known CAD has been demonstrated in many studies12–16.

However, the value of stress dynamic CT-MPI in patients with DM and without DM has been rarely reported. As a result, the purpose of this study was to look into the incremental prognostic significance of ischemic score measured by stress dynamic CT-MPI in both DM and non-DM patients.

Methods

Study population

This was a retrospective observational study enrolling 682 consecutive patients who underwent CCTA and stress dynamic CT-MPI to evaluate CAD from April 2019 to January 2024 in our hospital. Exclusion criteria were: (1) history of coronary revascularization, including history of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG); (2) previous myocardial infarction (MI); (3) severe valvular or cardiomyopathies; (4) early revascularization (revascularization within 3 months of baseline stress dynamic CT-MPI); (5) CCTA or CT-MPI images missing or poor image quality; and (6) lost to follow-up. Patient demographic characteristics and baseline data were obtained from clinical electronic medical records or follow-up visits. According to the American Diabetes Association criteria17, DM was defined as patients with fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL), or 2-h blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) in an oral glucose tolerance test, or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level ≥ 6.5%, or treated with glucose-lowering medications; hypertension was defined as a diagnosis of clinically hypertension or use of antihypertensive drugs; dyslipidemia was defined as prior diagnosis of any major dyslipidemia or use of lipid-lowering drugs; history of smoking was defined as having smoked within the previous 90 days or being a current smoker; and family history of coronary artery disease was defined as coronary artery disease in the immediate family. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University (PJ-KS-KY-2024-345) and conducted following the principles contained within the Declaration of Helsinki. Each participating patient in this study recruited written informed consent.

CCTA image acquisition and analysis

The heart rate of every patient is routinely checked, and if the heart rate is > 80 beats/min, metoprolol 50–100 mg is given orally approximately 1 h before the examination. Nitroglycerin was given 0.25 mg sublingually in all participants 3–5 min before the acquisition of CCTA.

A second-generation dual-source CT scanner (SOMATOM Definition Flash, Siemens Healthineers, Germany) or a third-generation dual-source CT scanner (SOMATOM Force, Siemens Healthineers, Germany) was used for CCTA acquisition. It was performed by using a bolus tracking technique, with regions of interest placed in the ascending aorta. Using triphasic injection protocols: firstly, injecting iodine (iohexol, 300 mg I/mg, Yangtze River Pharmaceutical Group Co., Ltd., China) 40–50 ml at a flow rate of 5.5–6.0 ml/s via anterior elbow vein followed by Iodine 20–30 ml at 4 ml/s flow rate, then 30 ml of physiological saline was injected at a flow rate of 4.0 ml/s. Using a prospective ECG triggered sequence acquisition mode, data are acquired during the 25%−80% time window of the ECG R-R interval and the optimal ventricular diastolic and systolic phases are automatically selected. The reference tube voltage was set as 100 kV and used automatic tube current modulation (CARE Dose 4D, Siemens Healthineers, Germany). For the reconstruction parameters of CCTA images: the second-generation dual-source CT has a rack speed of 0.28 s/r, detector collimation of 64 × 0.6 mm, reconstructed slice thickness of 0.75 mm, and reconstructed slice interval of 0.7 mm; the third-generation dual-source CT has a rack speed of 0.25 s/r, detector collimation of 96 × 0.6 mm, reconstructed slice thickness of 0.75 mm, and reconstructed slice interval of 0.5 mm.

CCTA data was transferred to an offline workstation (Syngo Via, version VB40B, Siemens Healthineers, Germany) for further analysis. Coronary segments with a reference diameter of ≥ 1.5 mm were assessed for the detection of stenosis, and CCTA images were visually assessed. Patients were assessed for stenosis according to the Coronary Artery Disease Reporting and Data System (CAD-RADS)18, patients with CAD-RADS grade 3 or higher were considered to have obstructive CAD. CAD-RADS classifications 4 A and 4B were combined into one group due to the small number of patients with CAD-RADS class 4B. Patients with CAD-RADS class 4 and above were considered to have severe stenosis. Multivessel disease was defined as ≥ 50% stenosis in ≥ 2 vessels. The extent and severity of the CAD burden were measured using CCTA scores, which include segment involvement score (SIS) and segment stenosis score (SSS)19,20. SIS ≥ 4 and SSS ≥ 6 were associated with an increased risk of MACE21. The characteristics of high-risk plaque (HRP) in CCTA images were as follows: positive remodeling indicated by a remodeling index ≥ 1.1; low-attenuation plaque with CT values below 30 HU and an area greater than 1 mm2; spotty calcification identified within non-calcified plaques with a long diameter under 3 mm and density above 130 HU; and the napkin-ring sign, denoting a slightly elevated density rimming low-density plaques. Any lesion with 2 or more HRP features was considered HRP18,22. Two cardiovascular radiologists (with 13 and 5 years of experience in cardiac imaging) were unaware of the clinical history and findings and analyzed the lesions independently. Any disagreements between the two observers were resolved by consensus.

CT-MPI image acquisition and analysis

A third-generation dual-source CT (SOMATOM Force, Siemens Healthineers, Germany) was employed for Dynamic CT-MPI scanning (performed at least 2 h after the end of the CCTA examination). CT acquisition of coronary artery calcification was first performed in prospective electrocardiographically triggered scanning mode (slice thickness of 3 mm). Based on the calcium scoring images, CT-MPI acquisition covered the entire left ventricle. After Adenosine (Hangzhou Zhongmei Huadong Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) was intravenously infused at a flow rate of 140 g/kg/min for 3 min, 56 mL iodine (iohexol, 300 mg I/ml, Yangtze River Pharmaceutical Group Co., Ltd., China) was injected at a rate of 7 ml/s followed by a 20 ml saline flush. Dynamic CT-MPI acquisition was started 4 s after the beginning of contrast injection. Prospective ECG-triggered “shuttle” acquisition mode was used, the tube voltage was set as 70 kV and CARE dose 4D was used, dynamic data acquisition 250 ms after ECG R-wave, reconstructed slice thickness = 3 mm, and reconstructed slice interval = 2 mm. Dynamic data acquisition time 32 s, and the patient’s ECG was continuously monitored during adenosine injection.

The Agatston method was used for calculation, coronary artery calcification score (CACS) was categorized as follows: 0, 1–99, 100–399, and ≥ 400, representing no, mild, moderate, and severe calcification, respectively. CT-MPI image were post-processed using a CT-MPI software package (Syngo Via, version VB40B, VPCT, Siemens Healthineers, Germany) and manually corrected by the physician if the automatic identification of the left ventricle chambers were inaccurate, resulting in left ventricle long-axis and short-axis myocardial perfusion images. To quantify MBF, a hybrid deconvolution model was employed to integrate the myocardial time-attenuation curves with the arterial input function (AIF). Using the left ventricle myocardial segmentation method recommended by the American Heart Association (AHA)23, ROIs were placed on each of the 16 myocardial segments (excluding an apical segment) in the short-axis view of the MBF maps to measure MBF values on each segment. Cover the entire myocardial segment as much as possible when setting ROIs and avoid endocardial and epicardial borders. The lowest MBF value of each myocardial segment was measured by two physicians (with 5 and 3 years of experience in cardiac imaging), narrowly without knowledge of the clinical history and results, and the average of the two measured MBFs was taken as the MBF of that myocardial segment. The normalized MBF was determined by dividing the MBF of each individual segment by the maximum MBF observed across the 16 segments on the MBF map. Based on normalized MBF values, ischemic score was calculated by adding the scores of all segments using a 5-point scale12: 0 = normal (> 0.75), 1 = mildly abnormal (≤ 0.75, > 0.675), 2 = moderately abnormal (≤ 0.675, > 0.60), 3 = severely abnormal (≤ 0.60), and 4 = absent. Ischemic score ≥ 4 in each patient was defined as an ischemia perfusion defect (IPD)14. The global MBF was computed by averaging the MBF measurements across all segments, while the minimum MBF among the 16 segments was designated as the lowest MBF value.

Follow up

Follow-up data was gathered through clinician visits, phone calls, or a review of hospital records that was blind to CT results. MACE included cardiac death, acute coronary syndrome (ACS), late revascularization procedures, and hospital admissions due to heart failure. Cardiac death was defined as death caused by acute MI, ventricular arrhythmia, or congestive heart failure. ACS was categorized as ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, unstable angina, and non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. Late revascularization included PCI and/or CABG performed greater than 90 days following CT-MPI.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using the SPSS software package (version 26.0, IBM, Armonk, New York), Medcalc (version 20.217.0, Medcalc Software, Mariakerke, Belgium) and the R statistical package (version 4.5.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables are presented as the mean SD and were evaluated with the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as percentages and compared with the chi-squared or Fisher exact test. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW)24 was used to balance baseline characteristics between the DM and non-DM cohorts. Prior to multivariate modeling, collinearity diagnostics were performed using variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance statistics. Cox proportional risk regression models were used to evaluate risk variables for MACE, such as clinical factors, CCTA features, and IPD, and the results are presented as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). For variables with p < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariable analyses to determine independent predictors of MACE. The Harrell concordance (C)-index was used to compare the prediction performance of three different models (model 1: clinical risk factors, model 2: clinical risk factors plus CCTA characteristics, and model 3: clinical risk factors plus CCTA characteristics plus IPD). The cumulative event rates of MACE were calculated using Kaplan-Meier curves and compared using the log-rank test. A two-sided level of p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Outcomes

During a median follow-up of 15 (9, 40) months, 49 patients (14.8%) experienced MACE, including 26 cases of ACS, 2 instances of cardiac death and 21 cases of late revascularization. No patients were hospitalized due to heart failure. One patient died due to noncardiac causes, which were censored at the time of death. MACE was substantially more common in diabetic individuals than in non-diabetics (23.9% (n = 22) vs. 11.3% (n = 27), p = 0.004). Significant between-group differences persisted after IPTW adjustment (log-rank p < 0.001; Supplemental Fig. 1).

Univariable and multivariable analysis

Post-collinearity assessment, all retained covariates exhibited VIF < 5.0, confirming absence of significant multicollinearity. Univariable Cox regression analysis revealed that IPD (HR, 12.56; 95% CI, 1.69–93.47; p = 0.013) was the strongest predictor among DM patients, whereas obstructive CAD (HR, 20.30; 95% CI, 2.75–149.69.75.69; p = 0.003) was the strongest predictor among non-DM patients. In addition, hypertension, severe stenosis, and SSS ≥ 6 in CCTA were substantially linked with MACE in DM patients. In non-DM patients, dyslipidemia, severe stenosis, Multivessel disease, CACS, SIS ≥ 4, SSS ≥ 6 in CCTA, and IPD in CT-MPI were significant predictors (Table 3).

Risk stratification of patients with DM or non-DM according to IPD

Kaplan-Meier curves by IPD (Fig. 2) showed that annualized event rates for MACE were significantly different between patients with and without IPD among DM (11.5% vs. 0.6%; p = 0.001) (Fig. 2A) and non-DM patients (5.5% vs. 0.2%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). Moreover, annualized event rates for MACE in patients with ischemic score of ≤ 3, 4–7, and ≥ 8 were 0.6%, 6.1%, and 21.0% respectively, in DM patients (p < 0.001); and 0.2%, 3.3% and 8.2% in non-DM patients, respectively (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Incremental prognostic value of IPD in DM and non-DM patients

To determine the incremental prognostic value of IPD in DM and non-DM patients, the C-index was calculated independently for each. By adding CCTA features (severe stenosis) to model 1 (hypertension) the C-index increased from 0.61 (95% CI 0.56–0.66) to 0.72 (95% CI 0.64–0.80) in DM patients (p = 0.025) and increased the C-index from 0.60 (95%CI 0.516–0.691) to 0.76 (95% CI 0.67–0.85) in non-DM patients (p = 0.002). Adding IPD to Model 2 increased the C-index from 0.72 (95% CI 0.64–0.80) to 0.80 (95% CI 0.71–0.88) in DM patients (p = 0.005) and from 0.76 (95% CI 0.67–0.85) to 0.83 (95%CI 0.74–0.91) in non-DM patients (p = 0.006).

Risk stratification according to IPD in a population of patients with nonobstructive CAD and moderate-to-severe calcification

Figure 4A shows that in non-obstructive CAD patients, there was a significant difference in the annualized event rate of MACE between patients with and without IPD (2.3% vs. 0%; p = 0.002). In patients with moderate-to-severe calcification, the annualized event rate of MACE was significantly higher in patients with IPD than in patients without IPD (6.4% vs. 0.8%; p = 0.003) (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

The main findings of this study are as follows: (1) IPD quantified by stress dynamic CT-MPI was an independent predictor of the occurrence of MACE in the group of patients with DM and without DM. In addition, adding IPD into risk stratification models provided incremental value over traditional clinical risk factors and anatomical parameters for predicting MACE; (2) IPD was associated with poor prognosis in patients with suspected ischemia with non-obstructive CAD (INOCA) or moderate-to-severe coronary calcification.

As in previous studies, the extent and severity of lesions assessed by CCTA were significantly higher in DM patients than in non-DM patients25,26. In our study, the prevalence of obstructive CAD did not differ between diabetic and non-diabetic individuals, although the CCTA score showed differences between the two groups. Furthermore, DM patients had more severe myocardial ischemia as measured by stress dynamic CT-MPI.

DM is usually combined with hypertension, and studies have shown that having both diabetes and hypertension raises the risk of cardiovascular disease27,28. A recent study found that hypertension had an additive effect on coronary plaques in diabetic patients, making them more likely to develop multi-vessel calcification29, which is similar with our results. Diabetic vascular calcification is associated with oxidative stress, vascular endothelial dysfunction, altered mineral metabolism, and elevated inflammatory cytokine levels30. However, in the current study’s DM cohort, more than 60% of patients had obstructive CAD and were primarily hypertensive (81.5%). In this high-risk study group, adding CCTA features to the clinical risk variables showed no additional predictive value, whereas adding IPD was able to improve their risk stratification.

Diabetes and hyperglycemia are known to drive endothelial impairment and coronary microvascular dysfunction31, making patients with INOCA more common in patients with DM. Invasive coronary physiological evaluation (index of microvascular resistance, IMR) is currently regarded as the gold standard for diagnosing INOCA. However, in a prospective research using both the fractional flow reserve (FFR) and the IMR as reference standards, stress dynamic CT-MPI outperformed CCTA in terms of diagnostic accuracy32. Previous research has shown that CT-derived fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR) has an incremental value for predicting MACE in patients with and without DM33–35. While CT-FFR provides important hemodynamic assessment of epicardial stenosis, technical considerations merit attention in cases with severe calcification, chronic total occlusions, or stented segments. In such scenarios, CT-MPI may offer complementary advantages by simultaneously evaluating both macrovascular and microvascular dysfunction—particularly relevant for diabetic patients with microcirculatory impairment36,37. The benefits of CT-MPI are especially noticeable in diabetic patients who are prone to microcirculatory abnormalities (Supplementary Fig.2). Furthermore, CT-MPI is less complex than other stress tests, is less impacted by image quality, and may evaluate patients with severe calcification. Our study confirms that IPD has a better risk stratification in the subgroup of non-obstructive CAD and moderate-to-severe calcification.

CT-MPI-based MBF values have been initially used for risk stratification in diabetic and non-diabetic patients. A study, used the absolute MBF values (abnormally perfused myocardium was defined as MBF absolute value ≤ 142 mL/100 mL/min) for prognostic assessment in diabetic and nondiabetic cohorts, and showed that CT-MPI had incremental prognostic value over clinical risk factors alone or combined with CCTA characteristics in predicting MACE38. However, absolute MBF values that vary between individuals and there is no established threshold for detecting ischemia. The ischemic score used in this study was based on standardized MBF values (known as relative MBF values) for each myocardial segment. Ischemic score ≥ 4 has also been shown to have high prognostic value in cohorts of patients with suspected or known coronary artery disease14. Furthermore, an ischemia score can quantify the severity of ischemic burden; the poorer prognosis associated with a higher ischemic burden reported in our study emphasizes the relevance of assessing the severity of myocardial perfusion deficit for risk stratification.

Despite the promising results of this study with positive clinical applications, there are still some limitations. Firstly, as a single-center retrospective investigation, this study is susceptible to selection bias and is further limited by the modest size of the diabetes cohort and the relatively short follow-up duration. Therefore, prospective studies employing larger samples and extended follow-up periods are needed to obtain more robust validation. Secondly, because many of the patients in our group were outpatients, their subsequent pharmaceutical therapy was unclear, which may have influenced their results. Third, although the total radiation dose in this study was relatively small (Average ED: 8.9 ± 1.6 mSv), the addition of CT-MPI to CCTA increased the radiation dose compared with CCTA alone. Finally, all images in this study were obtained with dual-source CT. Absolute and relative MBF thresholds for CT-MPI varied across studies, which may be affected by the type of CT scanner and post-processing software, and further studies are needed to determine whether the same results as in our study can be obtained by using other types of CT scanners.

In conclusion, our study shows that IPD quantified by stress dynamic CT-MPI had incremental prognostic value over traditional clinical risk factors and anatomic parameters in each group with DM or non-DM patients and was prognostically useful in subgroups of patients with non-obstructive CAD or moderate-to-severe calcification. This finding may make it possible to identify high-risk patients and thus improve their management.
