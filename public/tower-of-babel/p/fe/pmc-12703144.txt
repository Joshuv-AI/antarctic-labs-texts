Development of an AAV-delivered microRNA gene therapy for myotonic dystrophy type 1
Authors: Giulio S Tomassy, Wei Fan, Shuwen Cao, Zhengyu Luo, Alessandro Magli, Tracy Zhang, Kristen Boyle, Robert Jackson, Brenda Richards, Dongyu Liu, Franck Rapaport, Shameer Khader, Edith L Pfister, Catherine O’Riordan, Amy Frederick, Jennifer Sullivan, James Cao, Kollu Nageswara Rao, Basel T Assaf, Martin Goulet, Christian Mueller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1), characterized by life-threatening muscle weakness, compromised respiration, and, often, cardiac conduction abnormalities, is the most common form of adult muscular dystrophy. DM1 is caused by a CTG repeat expansion in the 3ʹ untranslated region of the dystrophia myotonica protein kinase (DMPK) gene, resulting in aggregation of DMPK mRNA into insoluble ribonuclear foci that sequester RNA-binding proteins. Redistribution of essential splicing factors causes mis-splicing of factors responsible for muscle differentiation. Targeting the disease at its root by reducing DMPK RNA promises to reduce RNA foci and pathogenesis. Here, we present an adeno-associated virus (AAV)-RNAi-based strategy for DMPK reduction based on a muscle-targeted platform comprising an AAV capsid with high muscle transduction efficiency, a promoter with strong activity in muscle, and a DMPK-targeting artificial miRNA. In cellular and animal models of DM1, we show that AAV delivery of an artificial miRNA targeting DMPK reduces DMPK RNA levels and improves molecular, pathological, and clinically relevant disease hallmarks. In non-human primates, we show AAV-amiRDMPK/SAR446268 treatment is well tolerated and results in a dose-dependent downregulation of DMPK mRNA (up to 90%) in all major muscle groups. Our data provide evidence of the efficacy and safety of SAR446268.Keywords: DM1, AAV, myotonic dystrophy, gene therapy, DMSXL, RNAi, muscular dystrophies, spliceopathies

Introduction

Myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) is a rare, progressive genetic disease causing a range of muscle-related and systemic manifestations, with an estimated prevalence of 9.27 cases (95% confidence interval 4.73–15.21) per 100,0001; however, this is thought to be an underestimate, with 48/100,000 individuals having expansion of the CTG repeat in the dystrophia myotonica protein kinase (DMPK) gene.2 DM1 is one of the most common forms of muscular dystrophy.3 It is caused by a CTG repeat expansion in the 3ʹ untranslated region (UTR) of DMPK (chromosome 19ql3.3), which results in DMPK mRNA hairpin structures that aggregate as insoluble ribonuclear foci and sequester various RNA-binding proteins,4,5,6,7,8 including splicing factors. The resulting redistribution of essential splicing factors, such as muscleblind-like splicing regulator 1 (MBNL1) causes mis-splicing of many genes, resulting in the disruption of various functions.9,10,11,12 In healthy individuals, CTG triplets in DMPK range from 5 to 35, whereas DM1 patients can have between 50 and over 1,000 repeats.4,5,8,13

DM1 is a multi-systemic disorder with a broad clinical spectrum; it commonly presents as adult onset, although in individuals who carry large expansions, it can also affect fetal and postnatal development. Clinical onset spans a continuum from birth to late adulthood, with the disease classified into four phenotypes with a strong correlation between CTG repeat length and disease severity and onset: congenital onset, the most severe form of the disease (4.6%, CTG >1,000), infantile/childhood (14.8%, CTG >800), juvenile/adult onset (64.7%, CTG = 50–1,000), and late adult (15.9%, CTG = 50–150).14,15,16 The most typical appearance of DM1 is the adult-onset or classic phenotype (75% of all patients), which includes initial symptom of myotonia, preferentially involving muscles of the trunk, cranium, and distal limbs. However, diaphragmatic weakness may occur before the muscles of the limbs and trunk.17

There is currently no disease-modifying treatment for DM1; therefore, symptoms are treated according to a management plan that includes symptomatic treatments for cardiac arrythmia, anti-myotonic medications, management of hypertension, cataract surgery, respiratory aid, and treatment of sleeping disorders.3

Here, we describe the development of a muscle-targeting delivery platform that includes (1) a novel AAV.SAN011 capsid18,19 with enhanced muscle delivery and (2) an engineered human S6 promoter with high activity in all muscle cells tested, including skeletal and cardiac muscles (published patent applications WO2023196862A1 and WO2019178412A1). We further describe the preclinical validation of the platform and demonstrate its ability to efficiently and safely deliver a DMPK-targeting artificial microRNA (amiRNA or amiRDMPK) and to reduce DMPK mRNA in both in vitro and in vivo models of DM1 and in healthy cynomolgus monkeys.

Results

Identification of a promoter with high expression in muscle

DM1 is a pleiotropic disease that predominantly affects muscle relaxation and results in progressive muscle weakness. Here, we aimed to develop an adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based gene therapy targeting DMPK. Due to high levels of liver transduction, AAVs have the potential to cause liver toxicity.20 Therefore, our first aim was to develop a delivery platform that would allow for efficient and safe targeting of muscles with minimal liver expression. To identify promoters with high activity in muscle and low activity in liver, we screened a series of nine new, engineered promoters (Figure 1A) in primary human myoblasts and Huh7 cells (representing muscle and liver, respectively). Promoters were rationally designed by combining regulatory elements from known human or rodent muscle-specific genes: S1–S5 include sequences from either desmin,21 α-myosin heavy chain,22 or the creatine kinase23 promoters, while S7–S9 combine an engineered human desmin promoter and skeletal muscle cis-regulatory module 4.24,25 These promoters were cloned into a plasmid driving expression of nano-luciferase and electroporated or transfected into primary human myoblasts and Huh7 cells, respectively. Forty-eight hours later, we compared the luciferase activity of these promoters to the ubiquitous CAG promoter and to the MH,26 MHCK7,27 and sk-CRM4/Des24 promoters (Figure 1), which have previously been shown to have high activity in muscle cells.28 The reference MHCK7 promoter is included in vectors undergoing preclinical studies for the treatment of Pompe disease (NCT04174105),29 dysferlinopathy (NCT02710500),30 and Duchenne muscular dystrophy (NCT03769116).31 We found that of all the muscle promoters, the S6 promoter drove the highest expression in primary human myoblasts, albeit lower than the CAG promoter (Figure 1B). In contrast, expression from the S6 promoter was relatively low in liver cells (Figure 1C). Based on these results, we selected the S6 promoter as having the preferred profile.

An S6 promoter-driven DMPK-targeting amiRNA corrects DM1 phenotypes in vitro

We then tested the ability of the S6 promoter to drive expression of a DMPK-targeting amiRNA18 (amiRDMPK, published patent application WO2023196862A1; Figure 2A) and reduce total DMPK expression in two in vitro models of DM1: DM1 immortalized myoblasts and iPSC-derived DM1 cardiomyocytes. DM1-immortalized human myotubes were transduced with an AAV2 vector containing an amiRNA targeting the region upstream of the CUG repeat sequence in the DMPK 3ʹ UTR (Figure 2B). This amiRNA was embedded in an miR-155 scaffold32,33 and placed under the control of the S6 promoter (AAV2-S6-amiRDMPK; Figure 2B). We then measured the expression levels of amiRDMPK (Figure 2C) and DMPK mRNA (Figure 2D) by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The amiRNA was only detectable in treated cells, where DMPK levels were reduced by 65% compared to untreated cells (untreated = 1 ± 0.04; treated = 0.34 ± 0.018; mean ± SD; p < 0.0001, two-tailed unpaired t test).

Accumulation of nuclear RNA foci is one of the hallmarks of DM1.10,34,35 To determine whether amiRDMPK could reduce these nuclear DMPK mRNA-containing foci, we used fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) to quantify both the percentage of cells presenting nuclear foci and the number of foci/nuclei in treated and untreated DM1 human myotubes (Figures 2E–2G). Following AAV2-S6-amiRDMPK treatment, we observed a 60% reduction in the percentage of cells containing foci (untreated = 74.36% ± 9.3%, treated = 15.36% ± 4.2%, mean ± SEM, p = 0.0044, two-tailed unpaired t test) (Figure 2F). In addition, when compared to untreated cells, treated cells had significantly fewer foci per nuclei (untreated = 1.55 ± 0.08, treated = 0.15 ± 0.03, mean ± SEM, p < 0.0001, two-tailed unpaired t test) (Figure 2G).

Splicing defects are thought to be a key driver of DM1 disease pathogenesis.36,37 Sequestration of key splicing regulators such as MBNL1 drive aberrant splicing of hundreds of transcripts.38 Modest splicing improvements (16% and 27% in a 22-gene panel for AOC 1001 and Dyne-101, respectively) have been associated with clinical improvement in myotonia.39,40 To test whether amiRDMPK can correct DM1-associated splicing defects, we performed NanoString analysis in AAV2-S6-amiRDMPK-treated immortalized DM1 myotubes to examine the splicing profile of 36 genes previously shown to be affected in DM1.41 For several genes, MBNL1, GOLGA4, SOS1, CLASP1, and zTTN, we show significant improvement of their splicing profiles upon treatment (Figures 2H–2L). Other transcripts were either not affected in DM1 myotubes or not corrected by AAV2-S6-amiRDMPK treatment (Figure S1).

MBNL1 is a master regulator of developmentally associated alternative splicing. Sequestration of MBNL1 in nuclear RNA foci is responsible for many of the splicing defects seen in DM1. Like many of its targets, MBNL1 is also alternatively spliced with transcript variants, including exons 5 and 7 being expressed primarily during early differentiation stages. Expression of these transcripts in adult DM1 increases nuclear accumulation of MBNL1.42 We found an increase in the expression of MBNL1 fetal isoforms in immortalized DM1 myotubes, which was reduced by treatment with AAV2-S6-amiRDMPK (Figure 2H).

In addition to MBNL1, we saw changes in alternative splicing of proteins involved in muscle maintenance such as an increase in the inclusion of exon 23 of GOLGA4 (Golgin A4), and exon 5a of zTTN (Z band Titin).38,43 These increases were similarly reduced by AAV2-S6-amiRDMPK treatment (Figures 2I and 2L). Decreases in the inclusion of exon 25 of SOS1 (SOS Ras/Rac guanine nucleotide exchange factor 1) (Figure 2J) and the inclusion of exon 20 of CLASP1 (cytoplasmic linker-associated protein 1)43 (Figure 2K) were also partially corrected by AAV2-S6-amiRDMPK treatment.

To demonstrate the ability of S6-amiRDMPK to function in both skeletal and cardiac muscle cells, we transduced human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived DM1 cardiomyocytes with S6-amiRDMPK carried by another capsid, AAV.SAN011 (Figure S2). AAV.SAN011-S6- amiRDMPK treatment reduced DMPK mRNA by over 70% relative to untreated cells (treated = 1 ± 0.009, untreated = 0.25 ± 0.006, mean ± SEM, adjusted p = 0.0001, one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test) (Figure S2B). Importantly, DMPK silencing resulted in the reduction in the number of foci per nucleus (untreated = 4.286 ± 0.6, treated = 1.222 ± 0.2, mean ± SEM, p = 0.0001, one-way ANOVA) (Figure S2C).

To determine whether treatment also resulted in improved splicing profile in the treated cardiomyocytes, we quantified the ratio between the splicing isoform of the gene Ldb3,44 including or excluding exon 11 (Ex11). The +Ex11/−Ex11 ratio was significantly different in DM1-untreated cells compared to wild-type (WT), healthy cells (healthy cells: 0.73 ± 0.014, DM1-untreated cells: 7.32 ± 0.102, mean ± SEM, adjusted p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA). However, AAV.SAN011-S6- amiRDMPK treatment significantly reverted the ratio toward healthy values (DM1-treated cells = 3.078 ± 0.205, mean ± SEM, untreated DM1 vs. treated DM1, adjusted p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA) (Figure S2D).

Our data show that the S6 promoter drives the expression of a DMPK-targeting amiRNA in both skeletal and cardiac human muscle cells, key cellular targets for DM1 patients. AAV transduction of both cell types resulted in significant DMPK mRNA downregulation and associated improvements in hallmarks of the disease, namely nuclear RNA foci accumulation and mis-splicing.

A novel capsid, AAV.SAN011, achieves superior delivery to muscle in non-human primates

Our goal was to develop a muscle delivery platform comprising a muscle-tropic AAV with a muscle promoter. AAV.SAN011 is derived from AAV.rh74, a capsid that broadly transduces muscle.45 The AAV.rh74 serotype is recognized for lower seropositivity rate45 and high muscle transduction in humans.46 The effect of amino acid mutations on AAV transduction has been well established. Mutation of selected residues in VR-V, GH-loop, VR-VIII, and VR-IX have been shown to either increase muscle or reduce liver transduction and expression for several AAV serotypes.47,48,49,50 AAV.SAN011 was rationally designed based on existing knowledge about AAV variants. To demonstrate the superiority of the AAV.SAN011 mutant, we performed a non-human primate (NHP) study comparing AAV.SAN011 to the clinical capsid AAV.rh74,51 which is used in clinical trials for Duchenne muscular dystrophy46,51 (clinicaltrials.gov identifiers: NCT03375164 and NCT03769116) and limb-girdle muscular dystrophy types 2E/R4, 2D/R3, and 2B/R2 (NCT02376816, NCT03769116, NCT03652259, NCT01976091, and NCT02710500). We systemically injected AAV.SAN011 and AAV.rh74 carrying an identical green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter transgene under the control of the chicken β-actin (CBA) promoter at a dose of 1 × 1013 VG/kg (n = 5 per group). Three weeks after dosing, we collected the tibialis anterior, quadriceps, gastrocnemius, biceps, triceps, diaphragm, heart, and liver of the animals for quantification of vector genomes by PCR and GFP by ELISA. Compared to AAV.Rh74, AAV.SAN011 showed higher distribution in all muscle groups analyzed and a comparable quantity in the heart and liver (Figure 3A). Additionally, GFP expression was comparable or higher in the AAV.SAN011-treated muscles as compared to the AAV.rh74 group (AAV.Rh74 = 0.35 ± 0.1 pg/μg; AAV.SAN011 = 63.23 ± 17.7 pg/μg; mean ± SEM).

Our data show that compared to the clinical AAV.Rh74 vector, AAV.SAN011 has overall stronger transduction and expression in muscle tissue. Therefore, we selected AAV.SAN011 for further development.

SAR446268 corrects DM1 phenotypes in DMSXL mice

We evaluated the combination of AAV.SAN011 and the S6 promoter for amiRNA delivery in the widely utilized DMSXL mouse model of DM1. DMSXL mice carry a human DMPK transgene containing a CTG expansion of more than 1,000 repeats. This transgene is expressed in multiple tissues, including skeletal and cardiac muscles.52 Here, we show that a single intravenous dose of research grade SAR446268 (at a dose of 9×1013 vector genomes [VG]/kg) results in expression of the amiRNA in skeletal and cardiac muscles and significant silencing of DMPK mRNA in the heart, tibialis anterior, diaphragm, and gastrocnemius, but not in liver, confirming the muscle preference of the platform (Figure 4A).

As in our previous in vitro studies, we wanted to determine whether downregulation of DMPK mRNA would ameliorate disease hallmarks in treated mice. We first measured nuclear RNA foci in the hearts of the treated animals by ISH. Foci were often present as large formations that are potentially multiple coalescent foci (Figure 4B); therefore, rather than quantifying the number of individual foci, we measured the linear size of each formation in positive cells. Compared to the control group, these formations were significantly smaller in the SAR446268-treated group (untreated = 7 ± 0.32 μm, treated = 2.7 ± 0.08 μm, mean ± SEM, p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA) (Figure 4C). To assess whether the treatment could ameliorate the splicing abnormalities, we conducted a comprehensive transcriptomic analysis on RNA samples extracted from both control and treated animals. The results revealed that the administration of SAR446268 led to a widespread normalization of the splicing profile in the heart of the treated animals (Figure 4D). Similar findings were also observed at the single gene level in heart (Figure S3A) and gastrocnemius (Figure S3B), respectively.

In addition to splicing defects, DM1 patients exhibit myotonia, mildly recapitulated in DMSXL mice.52,53 Cardiac dysfunction is present in patients as well,54 with conduction defects being present in up to 75% of adult DM1 patients. Cardiac arrhythmias are a leading cause of death.55 We used electromyography (EMG) and echocardiographic analysis to examine these phenotypes in DMSXL mice. Myotonic discharges in DMSXL mice were small, but EMG revealed a marked reduction or absence of myotonia in quadriceps, gastrocnemius, and tibialis anterior following SAR446268 treatment (Figures 5A and S4). Echocardiographic analysis showed that DMSXL mice exhibited a narrowed aorta compared to their WT littermates (Figure 5B; p < 0.001, one-way ANOVA). but treatment with SAR446268 significantly increased aortic diameter (p < 0.05, one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test), making it indistinguishable from WT (Figure 5C). Moreover, we found that DMSXL mice had decreased ascending aortic blood velocity (cardiac output deficiency) compared to WT (p < 0.0001), a defect that was also corrected with SAR446268 treatment (p < 0.001).

SAR446268 achieves widespread reduction of DMPK in NHP muscle

The DMSXL mouse model provides a fitting platform to test the phenotypic consequences of DMPK knockdown. However, the evolutionary distance between mice and humans makes them a less than ideal model in which to examine transduction efficiency, biodistribution, and immune responses to AAV. NHPs exhibit high sequence homology and gene expression patterns similar to those of humans.56 Therefore, we turned to an NHP model to demonstrate the translational potential of our approach. We injected cynomolgus monkeys (Macaca fascicularis) with SAR446268 manufactured by a producer cell line (PCL) platform process at 5 × 1013, 1 × 1014, and 2 × 1014 VG/kg. Twelve weeks post-dosing we quantified VG (Figure 6A), amiRDMPK (Figure 6B), and DMPK mRNA (Figure 6C) expression in various skeletal muscles, cardiac muscle, dorsal root ganglia (DRGs), and liver. We found a dose-dependent increase in VG across all tissues examined, with the liver exhibiting the highest number of VG per cell (Figure 6A). Biodistribution of the vector was relatively even across all muscles examined. In contrast, amiRDMPK expression levels were lower in liver and DRGs than in muscle (Figure 6B), as expected given the relative preference of the S6 promoter activity in muscle cells. Consequently, DMPK mRNA levels were not reduced significantly in liver and DRGs (Figure 6C). In the muscles analyzed, we observed between 50% and 75% DMPK reduction in the low-dose (5 × 1013 VG/kg) group and between 75% and 90% in the middle- and high-dose groups (1 × 1014 and 2 × 1014 VG/kg, respectively) (Figure 6C). SAR446268 at this level of target engagement46 is comparable to levels measured in the DMSXL mouse model efficacy study, which demonstrated reduction of DMPK mRNA, reduction in RNA foci, functional splicing recovery, and phenotypic improvement.

SAR446268 up to 2 × 1014 VG/kg is well tolerated in NHPs

The tolerability of SAR446268 was evaluated by histology on tissue sections. All animals in the study were monitored during the in-life phase with detailed home cage observation and measurement of body weight, food consumption, and vital signs.

A comprehensive survey of tissues revealed no test article-related macroscopic observations except for minimal test article-related individual hepatocyte necrosis in the liver of one male administered 1 × 1014 VG/kg and one female administered 2 × 1014 VG/kg (Table 1). Minor test article-related clinical chemistry effects characterized by minimally to mildly increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) activities were identified in two animals administered a single dose ≥1 × 1014 VG/kg (Table 2). Enzyme increases were observed only at early time points and returned to baseline values by day 43. We observed no correlated microscopic liver findings in affected animals, except for the finding of minimal hepatocyte necrosis in one female administered 2 × 1014 VG/kg, as previously described. Liver histology appeared normal in animals administered 5 × 1013 VG/kg. No test article-related effects on hematology or coagulation parameters were detected at any dose level. Based on the generally small magnitude of change, minimal severity and incidence and lack of dose responsiveness, these changes were not considered adverse. It is noteworthy that at 2 × 1014 VG/kg, SAR446268 produced 90% DMPK mRNA knockdown (0.90 ± 0.05, mean ± SD, n = 4) in cardiac ventricle tissues of cynomolgus monkeys, without histological findings 12 weeks post-dosing. Overall, our vector demonstrates superior expression and preference for transduction of both skeletal muscle and cardiac tissue when compared to AAV.Rh74, has a favorable safety profile, and fulfills the challenges of delivery to the muscle.

Discussion

DM1 is the most common form of adult muscular dystrophy, characterized by muscle weakness, compromised respiration, and, often, cardiac conduction abnormalities. DM1 is challenging to treat via traditional therapeutic approaches due to the systemic nature of the disease and the inherent difficulty of targeting muscle tissue; however, since DM1 is a genetic disease caused by aberrant expansion of a genetic sequence within the DMPK locus (i.e., CTG repeats), reduction of DMPK transcripts harboring expanded CTG repeats has the potential to target the disease at its root cause.

Indeed, DMPK mRNA downregulation (e.g., via small interfering RNA [siRNA] and/or antisense oligonucleotides [ASOs]) has shown efficacy in animal models of DM1, and partial DMPK suppression was shown to be well tolerated both in mice and NHPs.57,58,59,60 Despite these positive pre-clinical results, patients receiving IONIS-DMPKRx, an ASO targeting DMPK, showed no clinical benefit; this has been attributed to insufficient drug exposure in muscle.57,58,59,60 Nonetheless, recent data have demonstrated that delivery can be enhanced by conjugating the DMPK-targeting nucleic acids to antibodies or antibody fragments. Avidity and Dyne Therapeutics are exploiting this strategy by employing an antibody targeting the transferrin receptor to deliver DMPK- targeting siRNA or ASO. The resulting clinical candidates, AOC-1001 (Avidity) and Dyne-101 (Dyne Therapeutics), have demonstrated target engagement in preclinical models, significantly reducing both DMPK expression and RNA foci40,61 and demonstrating clinical improvements in ongoing phase 1/2 clinical trials (NCT05027269 and NCT05481879).40,61

While both AOC-1001 and Dyne-101 mediate knockdown of DMPK, they achieve this through different mechanisms. Dyne-101 consists of a conjugated ASO that is targeted to muscles via a TfR1-binding antigen binding fragment (Fab) and works via an RNAse H cleavage mechanism in the nucleus.62 AOC1001, in contrast, employs an siRNA conjugated with similar TfR-targeting antibody. amiRNAs, which are modeled after endogenous miRNAs, use the same mechanism of siRNAs. Canonically, miRNAs and siRNAs are loaded into the RNA silencing complex (RISC) in the cytoplasm where they bind to their mRNA targets and, if there is complete sequence homology between the pair, then target cleavage occurs. Previous work has demonstrated that short hairpin RNAs delivered via lentiviral vectors can simultaneously target and reduce both nuclear-retained mutant and cytoplasmic normal DMPK mRNAs in differentiated primary human myotonic dystrophy cells.63 In addition, several lines of evidence suggest that miRNAs function in the nucleus. Most cellular miRNAs are present in both the nucleus and the cytoplasm, and specific miRNAs show specific nuclear enrichment.64,65,66,67 Although our current understanding is that RISC loading occurs exclusively in the cytoplasm, there is experimental evidence suggesting that miRNAs are re-imported into the nucleus by karyopherins (e.g., XPO1, XPO5) via the nuclear pore complex.68 The precise functions and mechanisms of miRNA in the nucleus are not currently understood, but silencing of nuclear targets such as RN7SK, U6, and MALAT1 has been reported.64,65,66,67,69 While these data support that miRNAs can silence nuclear mRNAs, the observed reduction in nuclear RNA foci could also be due to the nature of the foci. RNA foci in DM1 are thought to be labile, subject to a stochastic process of assembly and disassembly.70 Therefore, lower levels of DMPK mRNA might be expected to reduce foci by promoting disassembly.

Sustained, steady-state, muscle-targeted DMPK suppression facilitated by AAV therapy promises long-term benefits from a single injection, obviating the need for repeated administrations and avoiding the peak-to-trough fluctuations to potentially provide long-lasting benefits to patients. Long-term durability and safety of AAV gene therapy has been demonstrated in both preclinical models of disease and human patients. Recently, data from long-term follow-up studies have demonstrated continued efficacy and safety of one-time treatment with Zolgensma (onasemnogene abeparvovec) for spinal muscular atrophy up to 5 years post-dosing.71 Similarly, a long-term follow-up of a phase 2a clinical trial for an intramuscular injection of AAV1 α-1 antitrypsin revealed persistent vector expression up to 5 years after dosing.72 A single intravenous administration of scAAV2/8-LP1-hFIXco gene therapy for hemophilia B resulted in durable factor IX expression, sustained clinical benefit, and no late-onset safety concerns over a period of 13 years.73

DMPK mRNA expression is highest in muscle tissues, and muscle phenotypes are the most prominent feature of the disease. However, DMPK is also expressed in the brain, particularly during childhood. Expression peaks around birth and then declines, reaching its lowest levels around age 10 and subsequently remaining low.74 In keeping with this pattern, congenital and juvenile onset are often associated with intellectual disability and neuropsychiatric manifestations that may be neurodevelopmental in origin.75,76 Adult-onset patients often experience hypersomnolence, apathy, and more variable cognitive symptoms.77,78 Depression and anxiety are also common and are exacerbated by disease-associated stress.78 Cognitive deficits are highly heterogeneous and often include apathy, executive function dysregulation, and visuospatial deficits.77 Imaging studies indicate that DM1 patients exhibit white matter abnormalities, gray matter atrophy,79,80,81,82,83,84 and hypometabolism, particularly in the frontal and temporal lobes.85,86,87,88,89 Cognitive symptoms are progressive,90,91 suggesting a neurodegenerative process; however, a relatively small percentage of patients meet the criteria for neurocognitive disorder, with late-onset patients being the most affected.92 Patient-reported outcomes suggest that the symptoms associated with the greatest decline in quality of life are related to fatigue, daytime sleepiness, and loss of mobility.93 While daytime sleepiness is likely driven primarily by changes in the CNS, fatigue likely results from a combination of muscle and CNS pathology and loss of mobility from changes in muscle. Thus, given the inherent difficulties of targeting both tissues with current gene therapy modalities, at present, targeting muscle seemingly represents the best opportunity to improve patient quality of life and could be an important step forward in the treatment of DM1.

Here, we describe a novel delivery platform comprising a novel capsid, AAV.SAN011, and a new transcriptional promoter (S6) that efficiently transduces and strongly expresses in both skeletal and cardiac muscle. Targeting the CUG repeats directly poses significant challenges due to their high GC content and would potentially affect several transcripts that normally contain CUG repeats. A total of 73 transcripts have been reported to contain six or more CUG repeats.94 This would have a broader impact and could lead to unintended consequences. Therefore, our strategy relies on a non-allele specific amiRNA targeting the 3ʹ UTR of the DMPK transcript. Partial reduction of DMPK is a leading therapeutic strategy for DM1. Support for this strategy is provided by studies that showed that constitutive silencing with ASO or DMPK knockout had no effect on functional measures of cardiac or skeletal muscle in mice.95 Further evidence for the safety of this strategy comes from our NHP study, where we saw that up to 90% silencing of DMPK mRNA at 12 weeks post-treatment was well tolerated. Nonetheless, close follow-up will be required in patients to establish the safety of any DM1 gene therapy.

Our results show a significant reduction of DMPK expression in DM1 patient immortalized myotubes and iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes as well as clearance of RNA nuclear foci and correction of aberrant splicing. In the DMSXL mouse, a rodent model of the disease, we show significant knockdown of DMPK mRNA, reduction of RNA nuclei foci, correction of splicing, and improvements in cardiac output and myotonia. The DMSXL mouse model of DM1 effectively recapitulates several molecular hallmarks of the human disease, including nuclear RNA foci formation, MBNL protein sequestration, and alternative splicing defects.52 However, this model exhibits important limitations. The severity of splicing abnormalities and myotonia is milder than in human patients, and the expression of mutant DMPK transcripts is relatively low in mouse tissues. Specifically, the DMPK transgene in DMSXL mice is driven by the human DMPK promoter, and in homozygous DMSXL hearts, the endogenous mouse DMPK RNA is expressed at approximately one-third of the human transgene level. This reduced expression may underrepresent the toxic RNA burden necessary to drive severe cardiac phenotypes, resulting in a relatively mild cardiac phenotype compared to human DM1 patients.

In contrast, DM200 and EpA960 mouse models, which use inducible systems to express supraphysiological levels of expanded CUG repeats, exhibit more pronounced cardiac manifestations, including conduction defects, arrhythmias, and in some cases, sudden cardiac death, closely mimicking the cardiac pathology observed in human DM1.96,97 While these models are highly valuable for mechanistic studies and therapeutic screening, their non-physiological expression levels raise concerns about the translational relevance of drug efficacy data and complicates the extrapolation of preclinical findings to clinical settings. Despite its milder phenotype, the DMSXL model’s chronic and systemic expression of the human DMPK gene make it well suited for studying long-term disease progression and systemic effects of expanded human mutant DMPK.

We validated our approach in NHPs showing a dose-dependent decrease in DMPK mRNA levels in several muscle groups, including the heart and diaphragm, following treatment with SAR446268. Importantly, we did not see a statistically significant reduction in DMPK mRNA in the liver or in DRGs, underscoring the muscle activity of our promoter. Finally, the treatment of the animals with SAR446268 was well tolerated at high doses, with limited test article-related findings. These data provide critical preclinical evidence on the efficacy and safety of a novel AAV-based gene therapy for DM1 patients and support the initiation of a phase 1/2 clinical trial evaluating SAR446268 in patients with DM1. The trial has been registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT06844214) and is currently enrolling participants.

Materials and methods

amiRNA

amiRNA targeting DMPK was cloned into the miR-155 scaffold98 and transferred into expression plasmid. The amiRNA (DMPKamiR) was packaged into either AAV2 (for some in vitro studies) or into a proprietary capsid, AAV.SAN011, for both in vitro and in vivo studies.

AAV production

All recombinant AAVs used in this manuscript were generated either as research grade by using HEK293 (human embryonic kidney) cells by triple transfection method,99 or by a PCL platform process.100 AAVs were then purified and titer measured via quantitative PCR.

Cell cultures

Myotubes

Control immortalized myoblasts (AB1167C20FL) and DM1 immortalized myoblasts (ASA308DM1-11G) were obtained from Institut de Myologie (Paris, France). The DM1 immortalized myoblasts were derived from the gastrocnemius muscle of an 11-year-old female DM1 patient (ASA308DM1-11G; Institut de Myologie) and harbor 2,600 CTG repeats within the DMPK gene. Cells were plated at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well on chamber slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 154526) and 2.5 × 105 cells per well on 6-well plates in growth media (PromoCell, skeletal muscle cell growth medium C-23060 with SupplementMix C-39365). Cells were treated with or without AAV. S6-amiRDMPK (MOI = 1 × 106) with 2 IU Ad5ts149 for 2 days at 39°C (non-permissive temperature to limit replication). After removing the viral media, cells were changed to differentiation media (PromoCell, skeletal muscle differentiation medium C-23061 with SupplementMix C-39366) for 4 days at 37°C. Myotubes on chamber slides were fixed with ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Electron Microscopy Sciences, catalog no. 15714) for 15 min, followed by FISH. RNA was extracted from the cells using miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, catalog no. 217004), followed by digital PCR (dPCR) to quantify DMPK and amiRNA expression.

iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes

Control (GM 25256G) and DM1 (GM 24559B) iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes were obtained from Coriell Institute. The DM1 iPSC were generated by reprogramming fibroblasts isolated from a skin biopsy collected from a 2-year-old female DM1 patient (ASA308DM1-11G; Institut de Myologie). The donor DMPK gene has 1 allele with approximately 1,600 CTG repeats with minor species at 2,400 CTG repeats (GM24559; Coriell Institute). iPSCs were differentiated into cardiomyocytes using STEMdiff Cardiomyocyte Differentiation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, catalog no. 05010). Beating cardiomyocytes were obtained after 10 days of differentiation. Differentiated cardiomyocytes were seeded in 12-well plates in duplicate or triplicate at a density of 6 × 105 in a 4-well chamber slide in support medium (STEMCELL Technologies, catalog no. 05027); the next day, the medium was changed to maintenance media (STEMCELL Technologies, catalog no. 05020). Cardiomyocytes were treated with 1 × 106 MOI of either AAVSAN.011-S6-amiRControl or SAR446268 with 2 IU Ad5ts149 at 39°C for 4 days. Cells were then fixed in ice-cold 4% PFA (Electron Microscopy Sciences, catalog no. 15714) for 15 min, followed by FISH with a custom Cy3-(CAG)10 probe (Integrated DNA Technologies). RNA was extracted from the cells using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, catalog no. 217004), followed by real-time PCR to quantify DMPK and amiRNA expression.

Promoter screening

Electroporation of primary human myoblasts

The Primary Cell Optimization 4D-Nucleofector X Kit (Lonza, catalog no. V4XP9096) was used for the electroporation of primary human myoblasts (Lonza, catalog no. CC-2580). Growth media (PromoCell, skeletal muscle cell growth medium C-23060 with SupplementMix C-39365) were added at 0.5 mL/well into a 24-well plate, and pre-warmed at 37°C. We resuspended 1 × 105 primary human myoblasts in 20 μL Nucleofector solution master mix, followed by mixing with 2 μL of each plasmid (200 ng/μL, 400 ng per reaction). The mixtures were added into a nucleocuvette strip for electroporation. All culture media were removed from each well 48 h after transfection, and luciferase was measured using the Nano-Glo Luciferase assay system (Promega).

Transfection of Huh7 cells

Huh7 hepatocytes were plated at 5 × 104 cells per well with 100 μL culture media (DMEM media [Gibco, catalog no. 11965-092] + 10% fetal bovine serum [Gibco, catalog no. A38400-01]) in a 96-well plate. The CAG promoter in CAG-NanoLuciferase-FLAG-tag-T2A-NLS-mCherry-bGH plasmid was replaced by different muscle-tropic promoters. Plasmids (100 ng) were transfected to each well using Lipofectamine 2000 Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, catalog no. 11668019), three replicates per plasmid. At 48 h after transfection, all culture media were removed from each well, and luciferase was measured using the Nano-Glo Luciferase assay system (Promega).

Detection of DMPK RNA foci by FISH

Into each cell chamber, 500 μL 70% ethanol was added, and cells were incubated at 4°C overnight. On the second day, cells were rehydrated for 10 min in wash buffer 2× SSC (1× SSC = 0.15 M NaCl and 0.015 M sodium citrate, made from 20× SSC [Sigma, catalog no. S6639]) with 40% formamide (Sigma, catalog no. 47671) at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with pre-hybridization buffer (10% dextran sulfate [Sigma, catalog no. D8906], 2 mM ribonucleoside vanadyl complex [Sigma, catalog no. R3380], 2× SSC [Sigma, catalog no. S6639], 50% formamide [Sigma, catalog no. 47671], 200 μg/mL BSA [Sigma, catalog no. A3803], 1 mg/mL yeast tRNA [Roche, catalog no. TRNABAK-RO], and diethyl pyrocarbonate-treated water [Ambion, catalog no. AM9922] to attain final target volume) for 15 min at 37°C. We denatured 5 μL of a 500-ng/μL Cy3-(CAG)10 Probe (Integrated DNA Technologies) at 100°C for 10 min and 1 μL probe was added to 1 mL ice-cold pre-hybridization buffer (500 pg/μL final concentration of the probe), which was then added (250 μL) to each well. Cells were hybridized for 2 h at 37°C in a hybridization oven. Following hybridization, cells were rinsed 3 × 20 min in wash buffer 2× SSC+40% formamide at room temperature. Cells were then rinsed 3 × 5 min with PBS. DNA was stained with 2 μg/mL DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific Pierce Biotech, catalog no. 62247) in PBS for 10 min. Coverslips were mounted using the antifading mounting media (Vector Laboratories, catalog no. H100010). The slides were sealed and kept at 4°C in the dark. Confocal images of DMPK RNA foci and nuclei were taken using a 3i confocal microscope. DMPK RNA foci number and integrated intensities were quantified using CellProfiler (www.cellprofiler.org).

NanoString analysis

From each of 12 samples 100 ng of RNA were run by NanoString using a panel of custom-designed probes (36 healthy transcripts, 36 DM1-specific transcripts, and 6 housekeeping transcripts in the panel) targeting healthy and DM1-specific transcripts.101 The genes of interest were selected based on previous publications showing that alternative splicing of their transcripts were associated with DM1.38,41,102 The data were analyzed with nSolver software from NanoString. The count of each transcript was normalized to housekeeping transcripts. Percent spliced-in (PSI) was calculated for each event as the fraction of inclusion-isoform reads relative to all inclusion- and exclusion-isoform reads.

Animal studies

Animal welfare

All animals were handled in accordance with the rules and regulations of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, in compliance with the Animal Welfare Act, and we adhered to principles stated in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.103 The work was performed in an Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care, International-accredited institution. All efforts to minimize pain and distress in these purpose-bred animals were ensured.

NHPs

Selection of muscle-tropic capsid in cynomolgus macaques

Asian cynomolgus monkeys aged 24–50 months were obtained from Envigo Global Services. Animals were housed at Labcorp Early Development Laboratories in stainless-steel cages. Prior to the study, monkeys were screened for the presence of neutralizing antibodies against the test articles in blood, and only seronegative animals were used for the study. A total of 10 male monkeys were used in the study. Animals were dosed intravenously with 1 × 1013 VG/kg of either AAV.rh74 or AAV.SAN011 carrying GFP as a reporter transgene under the control of a CBA promoter. Tissues were harvested 3 weeks after injection for analysis.

Target engagement in cynomolgus macaques

Asian cynomolgus monkeys aged 2–4 years were obtained from Envigo Global Services. Animals were housed at Labcorp Early Development Laboratories in stainless-steel cages. Prior to the study, monkeys were screened for the presence of neutralizing antibodies against the test article in blood, and only seronegative animals were used for the study. A total of 16 animals (8 males and 8 females) were utilized in the study. Animals were dosed intravenously with three different doses of SAR446268 at 5 × 1013, 1 × 1014, and 2 × 1014 VG/kg. Tissues were harvested 12 weeks after injection for analysis.

ISH and imaging

Tissue necropsies from formulation buffer- and SAR446268-treated (2 × 1014 VG/kg dose) NHPs were formalin-fixed and then embedded in paraffin. Blocks were then sectioned (4 μm), and the resulting slides were maintained at −20°C until processing. RNA staining was performed on a LeicaBond Rx using the RNAscope Plus smRNA-RNA LS Reagents Kit (Advanced Cell Diagnostics [ACD], catalog no. 322786) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The following probes were used in this study: custom-designed amiRDMPK-S1 (ACD), Mfa-DMPK-C2 (ACD, catalog no. 856738-C2), and Mfa-UBC-C3 (ACD, catalog no. 461331-C3). The following reagents were used for detection: TSA Vivid fluorophore 650 (ACD, catalog no. 323273), TSA Vivid fluorophore 570 (ACD, catalog no. 323272), and TSA Vivid fluorophore 520 (ACD, catalog no. 323271). Nuclei were stained with DAPI (part of the RNAscope Plus kit). Slides were then mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher, catalog no. P10144). Fluorescent images were captured with the Axioscan Z1 microscope (Zeiss) using a 20× objective.

DMSXL mice

Eight-week-old DMSXL mice (males and females) were injected intravenously with 9 × 1013 VG/kg research grade SAR446268. Phenotypic analysis was conducted at baseline and 8 weeks post-injection. Tissues were collected 8 weeks after injection for molecular analysis.

Echocardiography

A high-frequency micro-ultrasound imaging system (Vevo 2100, FUJIFILM VisualSonics) was used for echocardiographic imaging. Heart rates were maintained between 350 and 450 beats per minute, and the rectal temperature was maintained between 35.0°C and 37.0°C. After applying ultrasound gel to the thoracic skin, an MS550D (22–50 MHz) linear array transducer (FUJIFILM VisualSonics) was used to acquire ultrasound images. The total time for image acquisition was 5–15 min per mouse, during which the electrocardiogram (ECG), body temperature, and respiration rate were continuously monitored. Two-dimensional (2D) B-mode images from the short-axis view, long-axis view, and aortic view of the left ventricle were acquired and stored as digital video loops. An ECG-gated kilohertz visualization mode was also used. The aortic diameter was measured at peak diastole in 2D aortic view at the aortic orifice just before the branching point of the internal carotid artery. M-mode recordings were obtained with the ultrasound beam perpendicular to the vessel. Pulsed wave Doppler velocity was measured with a sample volume that adjusted to cover the vascular lumen and the small intercept angle nearly parallel to the blood flow direction. The left ventricular and aortic imaging data were then analyzed with the Vevo Lab software version 5.1 (FUJIFILM VisualSonics).

EMG

Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, and two 29G needle electrodes were inserted into each of the muscles analyzed (tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius, and quadriceps). Myotonia was measured using Powerlab 4/35 and Bio-amp (AD Instruments). For each mouse, 10 recordings were taken per muscle group. After insertion of both needles, the muscle was gently manipulated with forceps close to the site of needle entry, to trigger a muscle contraction. Myotonia scoring was performed by an experimenter blinded to the identity of the genotype and treatments. Myotonia was graded via the following criteria: 0 indicates no myotonic discharges detected, 1 indicates less than 50% of muscle manipulations resulted in a myotonic discharge, 2 indicates 50%–80% of muscle manipulations resulted in a myotonic discharge, and 3 indicates that 80%–100% of muscle manipulations resulted in a myotonic discharge.104

RNA and DNA isolation

Tissues were homogenized and processed for total RNA isolation using the miRNAeasy 96 Kit (Qiagen). DNA extraction was performed using the QIAmp 96 Kit (Qiagen). The Qiacube HT (Qiagen) instrument was used to automate DNA extraction.

Real-time PCR

For some of the studies, real-time PCR assay was performed to measure DMPK RNA, amiRNA, and vector genome copies using the QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR machine. For DMPK expression, RNA samples underwent reverse transcription to cDNA on a thermocycler. Real-time PCR was performed with cDNA using a human DMPK TaqMan probe to detect DMPK and a mouse Hprt probe as a housekeeping gene (assay details below). For amiRNA expression, RNA samples underwent reverse transcription on a thermocycler for miRNA and the non-coding small nuclear RNA U6 reference gene separately, and then cDNA was diluted 1:10 for amiRNA and 1:100 for U6. amiRNA and U6 TaqMan probes were used for the real-time PCR assay. To analyze amiRNA expression, we adjusted the dilution factor used for cDNA dilution, and U6 was used as normalization gene. VG levels were determined using primers/probes specific for the polyadenylation signal.

RNA sequencing library construction and data pre-processing

RNA sequencing libraries were prepared and sequenced at Azenta. Illumina’s BCL2FASQ pipeline was used to generate the raw FASTQ files, which were then uploaded to the Qiagen Omicsoft Studio version 12.8 platform. Reads were mapped to the reference mouse genome GRCm38.p5 (with Ensembl annotation release version 89) using the OSA4 aligner with default parameters.105 OSA4 alignment was able to place 80%–85% of the reads from each sample on the mouse genome (GRCm38.p5). Exon-level counts were then obtained using the Summarize Exon/Exon-Junction Count function with default parameters.

Analysis of differential exon usage

The analysis of the differential exon usage was performed by the widely used R/Bioconductor edgeR package version 4.42.106 The lowly expressed exons were filtered out prior to the downstream analysis, and exon counts were subjected to trimmed mean of M-values normalization to eliminate composition biases between libraries. Differentially expressed exons were calculated using the QL F-test for the following comparisons: (1) disease sample (DMSXL−) vs. control (WT) and (2) treated samples (DMSXL+) vs. disease samples (DMSXL−). The diffSpliceDGE function was then employed to detect alternative splicing (both exons and junctions) by testing for differential exon usage between the comparisons. Resulting p values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Significant alternative splicing events were selected based on the corrected p value (false discovery rate [FDR]) and exon log fold change (calculated from the normalized data). The feature-level test results were then summarized at the gene level using either the Simes method,107 which combines feature-level p values within each gene, or an F-test, which combines feature-level quasi-likelihood F-test statistics for that gene.106 Under both the Simes method and the F-test, genes were considered to exhibit differential exon usage if the gene-level FDR was below 0.05. A heatmap was generated using the R package pheatmap.108
