Adaptation of the basic resuscitation skills self-efficacy scale in nursing students to Turkish : a validity and reliability study
Authors: Halil Ibrahim Gules, Gulten Sucu Dag
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNurses’ resuscitation self-efficacy levels are a key component in the acquisition of basic resuscitation skills. However, there is no measurement tool to determine nursing students’ resuscitation self-efficacy in our country. This study was conducted to adapt in the nursing students the Basic Resuscitation Skills Self-Efficacy scale to Turkish and determine its validity and reliability.MethodsA descriptive, cross-sectional, and methodological design was used. The study was conducted with nursing students from two universities in Northern Cyprus between August 2023 and November 2023. The study included 605, second, third, and fourth-year nursing students. The data were collected using descriptive characteristics form and the basic resuscitation skills self-efficacy scale.ResultsThe scale showed a 2-factor structure, namely CPR and OED use, Casualty Recognition and Safety and consisted of 18 items. In confirmatory analysis, all factor loads were found to be between 0.50 and 0.87. The fit indexes of the inventory were χ2/df = 3.647, GFI = 0.906, NFI = 0.908, CFI = 0.921, RMSEA = 0.079. Cronbach’s alpha of the total scale was 0.887.ConclusionsThe Turkish version of the Basic Resuscitation Skills Self-Efficacy Scale was valid and reliable and could be used to determine the students resuscitation self-efficacy.Keywords: Basic resuscitation skills, Self-Efficacy, Nursing students, Validity, Reliability

Introduction

According to the World Health Organisation (2021), cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are among the leading causes of death in the world, with 17.9 million people dying from CVD in 2019, representing 32% of all deaths worldwide [1]. Cardiac arrest is a sudden loss of cardiac function in a person with or without a diagnosis of heart disease. Healthcare professionals or rescuers from the public should start cardiopulmonary resuscitation without delay, assuming that the victim with agonal breathing is in cardiac arrest [2].

The average frequency of in-hospital cardiac arrest is 200,000 per year and the cardiac arrest survival rate is between 11% and 35 [3, 4]. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) refers to all life-saving actions that require rapid decision-making to ensure airway patency and circulation [5]. In cases of cardiac arrest, it has been reported that correct and effective basic life support practices of the people witnessing the event contribute significantly to the survival of the victim [6].

Nurses are expected to have basic resuscitation skills in recognising cardiac arrest, activating the emergency system, initiating effective cardiopulmonary resuscitation and safe use of an automated external defibrillator (OED) [7]. Despite this, studies in the literature emphasise that the skill levels of nurses and nursing students related to CPR are below optimal [8, 9]. Since nurses are the first healthcare professionals to diagnose and intervene in cardiac arrest in the hospital, performing high-quality CPR may be related to effective training, organising and conducting the care process during resuscitation, and perceived resuscitation self-efficacy level [10]. Nurses’ resuscitation self-efficacy levels are a key component in the acquisition of basic resuscitation skills [7].

CPR application is an important psychomotor skill of undergraduate nursing education to ensure professional competence and to increase casualty safety, to improve resuscitation self-efficacy [10, 11]. Nursing students should receive effective CPR training before starting their profession [12] and be able to initiate and perform effective CPR when they start their profession [13]. In the recent literature, underscoring the emotional and practical challenges faced by nursing students during CPR practice. For instance, Purwadi, et al. identified themes including fear, pride, difficulty distinguishing between mannequins and real patients, and the need for instructional feedback among nursing students performing CPR in a hospital setting [14]. Moreover, a qualitative study by Gutiérrez-Puertas et al. explored students’ experiences after applying advanced life support techniques and similarly reported increased self-confidence alongside heightened stress and anxiety during execution [15]. Nursing education institutions have a responsibility to make nursing students competent in CPR through well-developed basic life support programmes [13]. Nursing students should be educated with the most up-to-date and appropriate information about resuscitation and should be able to put this theoretical knowledge into practice [16].

Resuscitation-specific self-efficacy is defined as a judgement of perceived capacity to organize and carry out the care process during resuscitation [17]. In the studies conducted, the importance of increasing self-efficacy in the fields of resuscitation training for health professionals has been emphasized [18, 19]A strong sense of self-efficacy in resuscitation is a task-specific determinant of behaviour that can influence patient outcomes [10]. Self-efficacy may therefore be useful as a predictor of the implementation of learning [20].

Despite regular training and practice, many nursing students may lack self-confidence in initiating and performing cardiopulmonary resuscitation [15]. The lack of self-confidence experienced by the students may be associated with the fear of harming the patient, the fear of making mistakes and the fear of being sued [21]. If this situation is not managed correctly, it may cause students to tend to avoid clinical practice [22]. Therefore, in addition to developing competence in basic life support, it is important to support students to develop their self-confidence and self-efficacy through education [21]. In a study in which CPR knowledge level, chest compression performance and self-efficacy levels of nursing students at a university were evaluated over time, it was found that self-efficacy level tended to decrease over time [23]. In the study of Roh et al., it was determined that nursing students who applied correct chest compression had higher self-efficacy levels [24].

Standard measurement tools have been developed to assess self-efficacy in basic resuscitation skills [7, 18]. The Basic Resuscitation Skills Self-Efficacy Scale (BRS-SES) was developed by Padilla et al. to assess students’ perceived competence in performing CPR. The BRS-SES consists of 18 items and is scored on a scale of 0-100. The scale items assess students’ perceived self-efficacy regarding recognition and alerting, use of an Automated External Defibrillator (AED), and cardiopulmonary resuscitation procedures in CPR practice [7].

Assessment of nursing students’ resuscitation skills self-efficacy levels provides educators with the opportunity to evaluate the effectiveness of their teaching [7]. In Turkey, the medium of instruction in undergraduate nursing programs is Turkish, and nursing students complete both their theoretical and clinical training in Turkish. Although some students may have basic English proficiency, their levels of fluency are variable and cannot be assumed to be sufficient for reliably completing an English-language scale. Therefore, a culturally and linguistically appropriate Turkish version of the BRS-SES is needed to ensure accurate and standardized evaluation of nursing students’ resuscitation self-efficacy. It has been determined that there are standard measurement tools in the evaluation of resuscitation self-efficacy level at the international level, but there are no standard measurement tools in Turkish in our country.

This study was to provide a standardised measurement tool for the evaluation of basic resuscitation skills in nursing education. The aim of this study was to evaluate the Turkish validity and reliability of the BRS-SES in nursing students.

Method

Study design

A descriptive, cross-sectional, and methodological design was used.

Setting and participants

The population of the study consisted of 1000 s, third and fourth year students studying in the nursing departments of two universities in Northern Cyprus. First year students were not included in the study as there were no nursing courses in the curriculum. In scale development and validity and reliability studies, it is recommended that the sample should be 10–20 times the number of items and this number should not be less than 200 [25, 26]. The sample of the study consisted of 605 students. In this study, 45 students were included in the test retest analyses. It is stated to work with at least 30 people for test-retest [27].

Inclusion criteria

Being a second, third and fourth year nursing student to be enrolled in a Nursing degree programme.

Being a second, third and fourth year nursing student to be enrolled in a Nursing degree programme.

To know Turkish.

To know Turkish.

Volunteering to participate in the study.

Volunteering to participate in the study.

Data collection tools

In the study, “Descriptive Characteristics Form” and “Basic Resuscitation Skills Self-Efficacy Scale - (BRS-SES) -Turkish version(Tr)” were used to collect data.

Descriptive characteristics form

This form was prepared by the researchers based on the literature [7, 18, 19, 28]. The Descriptive Characteristics Form consisted of a total of 9 questions including the age, gender, the university attended, the class of study, the status of receiving CPR training, the status of witnessing cardiac arrest, the status of performing cardio pulmonary resuscitation (CPR), the status of observing CPR application, and the status of using an automatic extrernal defibrillator (OED).

Basic resuscitation skills self-efficacy scale

The Basic Resuscitation Skills Self-Efficacy Scale was developed by Padilla et al. on the basis of Bandura self-efficacy theory, European Resuscitation Council (2010) and Resuscitation Council (2014) recommendations [7]. The scale consists of 18 items and 3 sub-dimensions. These are (1) Recognition and alertness, (2) Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, (3) Safe use of an AED (Automatic External Defibrillator. The scale is evaluated on a scale of 0-100, where 0 means I absolutely cannot do it, 50 means I can do it moderately, and 100 means I am completely sure that I can do it. As the score increases, the level of basic resuscitation skills self-efficacy increases. The cronbach alpha level of the Basic Resuscitation Skills Self-Efficacy Scale was 0.96.

Data collection procedures

The data were collected online between 8 August − 29 November 2023. The data collection forms used in data collection were converted into online data collection forms using “Google Form”. The online data collection forms were shared with the “WhatsApp” groups of the students by contacting the nursing department lecturers of the universities constituting the sample of the study. Reminder messages were sent to the students every week. The time spent by the students for data collection is approximately 15 min. To assess the stability of the scale, test–retest reliability was evaluated. The BRS-SES was re-administered to a subsample of participants three weeks after the initial administration. A three-week interval was chosen in line with recommendations in the scale development literature, which suggests that a 2–4 week period is appropriate to minimize recall bias while avoiding true changes in the construct being measured [29].

Data analyses

SPSS 26.0 and AMOS 21 software were used for the analyses of the research data. The distribution of the socio-demographic characteristics of the students in the study was analyzed by frequency analyses. The validity and reliability of the Basic Resuscitation Skills Self-Efficacy Scale was analyzed by exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis, Cronbach alpha, and item total correlations. Descriptive statistics were given for the Basic Resuscitation Skills Self-Efficacy Scale scores of the students, and the normal distribution of the scale scores was examined with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and skewness-skewness coefficients, and it was determined that they were normally distributed. Accordingly, parametric tests were used to test the research hypotheses. In hypothesis tests, independent sample t test was applied if the independent variable was in two groups and ANOVA was applied if it was in more than two groups. If there was a difference between the groups according to ANOVA results, Tukey test was applied as a post-hoc test. Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) coefficient and Barlett’s Sphericity Test results were calculated to evaluate the suitability of the data for exploratory factor analysis. In order to reveal the factor pattern of the scale, maximum likelihood estimation method and covariance matrix were used to determine factors in Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). Acceptable fit indices for a good CFA model should be 𝛘2/df ≤ 3, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) 0.05–0.08, Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Comparative Fit Index(CFI), Normed Fit Inde(NFI) >0.90 [30–32]. In addition, internal consistency was assessed by Cronbach’s alpha, test-retest and Pearson correlation analyses. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients are considered unreliable between 0.00 and 0.40, low-reliability between 0.40 and 0.60, quite reliable between 0.60 and 0.80, and highly reliable between 0.80 and 1.00. The minimum value required for the item-total score correlation is 0.30, and it is recommended to remove items with a correlation value of less than 0.30 from a scale [31, 33]Statistical results were considered significant at 95% confidence and p < 0.05 levels.

Ethical dimension

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the the Eastern Mediterranean University Institutional Review Board and Human Research Ethics Committee on under decision number (ETK2023-0078). Participants were informed about the study’s purpose, duration, and their right to voluntary participation and withdrawal at any time. They provided digital informed consent before beginning the questionnaire. No personally identifiable data were collected; responses were anonymized and used solely for research purposes. Google Forms employs secure login, SSL/HTTPS encryption, and protection against unauthorized access to ensure data privacy and safety. In accordance with data protection principles, access to the form results was granted only once and exclusively to the researcher. No data were shared with third parties, and confidentiality was fully maintained.

Validity analysis

Language validity, content validity and construct validity were examined for the validity analysis of the BRS-SES.

Language validity

Language validity is the first stage of an adaptation process. In the adaptation process, the principles of the World Health Organization regarding the intercultural adaptation process of self-report scales were taken as a basis [34]. In the study, first, the English version of the BRS-SES was translated into Turkish by following a translation-back translation method. The original forms of the scales were translated from English to Turkish. Two independent linguists who did not know the content of the study translated them. Afterward, the researchers evaluated these translations, and the Turkish form of the scales was rearranged. A bilingual independent translator (native English fluent in Turkish) performed a ‘blind back-translation’. The English form of the BRS-SES was compared with the original wording of the scale and submitted to expert opinion for linguistic and content validity.

Content validity

Content validity of the BRS-SES was determined by following Lawshe’s method to calculate the Content Validity Index (CVI) for the 18 items compounding the scale [35]. An averaging calculation method was used to determine the content validity index for the overall Scale-Content Validity Index (S-CVI) [36]. When obtaining the content validity, an expert opinion form was prepared and each of the scale items was asked to be evaluated between 1 and 4 (not appropriate, very appropriate) in terms of content and meaning. Seven experts were academics in the field of nursing and trained in emergency care and resuscitation and working in emergency department nursing were consulted. According to the literature, for panels of six to eight experts, an I-CVI value ≥ 0.83 and an S-CVI value ≥ 0.80 are considered acceptable [36–38]. In this study, CVI analysis demonstrated excellent agreement among the experts. Both I-CVI and S-CVI values were 1.00, indicating complete consensus and confirming excellent content validity of the Turkish version of the BRS-SES.

After the language equivalence of the scales and expert evaluations, a pilot application was conducted with 14 nursing students who were not included in the main sample. Students were asked to evaluate each item on the scales regarding intelligibility, clarity, and relevance. In the pilot application, the students stated that the explanations of the scale were sufficient, the items were clear and understandable, and the scale was easy to follow, and the language validity phase of the scale was completed.

Construct validity

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

In order to determine the factor structure of the Basic Resuscitation Skills Self-Efficacy Scale, firstly, EFA was applied. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin coefficient of the BRS-SES was determined as 0.958. According to Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity results, the chi-square value obtained was 9191.44 and this value was statistically significant (p < 0.05).

When the factor structure and factor loads of the components obtained as a result of the EFA of the BRS-SES were examined, it was determined that the factor structure of the scale consisted of two different factors. The eigenvalue of the first factor was λ = 10,414 and the eigenvalue of the second factor was λ = 1,507. In total, the two factors of the scale explained 66.23% of the total variance. The factor loadings of the components of the BRS-SES ranged between 0.629 and 0.88. In this context, the factor loadings determined for 18 items in the scale are significant.

Confirmatory factor analysis

Confirmatory Factor Analysis was performed to confirm the factor structure of the BRS-SES determined because of the EFA and to determine the relationships between the factors.

When the confirmatory factor analysis model of the BRS-SES was analysed, it was determined that the factor loadings of the scale ranged between 0.50 and 0.87 (Fig. 1). These values are factor loadings that show how well each factor represents the measured variables.

Discussion

Self-efficacy is the confidence and belief in one’s own knowledge and skills. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation is affected by knowledge, experience and self-efficacy (Bandura 1982). Nursing students may witness cardiac arrest during their clinical experiences. Therefore, students should have the necessary knowledge and skills about CPR [28].

Validity analysis

Adaptation of a scale to different cultures and languages is important to ensure validity in the translated culture. This process involves harmonising the scale with the target culture in terms of language, expression and content. In addition, it is aimed to increase the validity and reliability of the measurement tool by taking into account the different understandings between cultures. At this stage, the researcher should get expert opinion from at least three and at most 10 people [37]. In the content validity analyses of the BRS-SES, the opinions of seven experts were obtained and the CVI was used in the expert opinion analyses. In line with the suggestions received from the experts regarding the scale items, some items were edited in terms of language and meaning. Among these, the 13th item “I can place the OED pads in appropriate locations considering possible contraindications” was revised as “I can place the OED pads in appropriate positions considering possible contraindications”, the 15th item “I can give a quick and safe shock by visually checking the victim and giving verbal commands” was revised as " ‘After providing visual control, I can shock the victim/patient quickly and safely by giving verbal commands’. In 16th item, the phrase “Existing guidelines.” was changed to “Current guidelines.“. Content validity index was used in the evaluation of expert opinions [37]. In the interpretation of the content validity index, it is recommended that the content validity index should be above 0.80 if the opinions of six or more experts are obtained [37]. In the present study, the scale’s I-CVI and S-CVI values were found to be “1” and a consensus was reached among the experts.

One of the important steps in scale adaptation is to test the construct validity of the scale. It is recommended to perform the first exploratory factor analysis to test the construct validity in order to determine intercultural differences in scale adaptation studies [39]. It was determined that the factor structure of the BRS-SES consisted of two different factors unlike the original scale. In this study, the items related to the subdimensions of CPR and OED use were grouped into a single factor. Considering the items in the scale and its content, this two-factor structure was close to the original scale, so the sub-dimensions were named as " CPR and OED use " (14 items) and “Casualty Recognition and Safety” (4 items). The first 3 items in the " Recognition and alertness " sub-dimension in the original scale were also included in the " Casualty Recognition and Safety” sub-dimension in this study. At the same time, the item “I make sure that no one touches the victim while the rhythm is being analysed” in the “Safe use of an AED” subscale was included in the “Casualty Recognition and Safety” subscale.

Several theoretical explanations may account for this variation. First, cultural and linguistic differences may have influenced the way in which students perceived the items. In the Turkish context, nursing students may tend to view CPR and AED use as inseparable skills, since they are often taught together in both theoretical and practical courses. Second, the sample characteristics should be considered. Students in this study had limited clinical exposure and therefore may not have distinguished between different technical dimensions of resuscitation self-efficacy but rather perceived them as a single psychomotor skill. Finally, the unequal distribution of items across factors (14 vs. 4) may indicate that students attach greater importance and confidence to the psychomotor aspects of CPR and AED application compared with recognition and safety tasks. Similar changes in factor structures have been reported in cross-cultural adaptation studies and are generally attributed to contextual and educational differences rather than methodological weaknesses [29, 38].

It was determined that Turkish form of the BRS-SES consisted of two different factors and the factor loadings were between 0.641 and 0.88. In factor analysis in scale studies, the factor load of the scale items should be above 0.30 [40]. The factor loadings of the original scale were found between 0.53 and 0.88, and the three-factor eigenvalue of the scale was found to be 74.3% [7]. In this study, no item was removed from the scale due to the high factor loadings. These values are factor loadings that indicate how well each factor represents the measured variables. Higher factor loadings indicate a stronger relationship with the relevant factor [31, 32]. In the study, the eigenvalue of the two-factor scale was greater than 1 and the two factors of the scale explained 66.23% of the total variance. In the literature, it is reported that the total variance ratio explained by the factors should not be below 60% [33]. In this study, the explained variance of the scale is above the recommended limits. These results confirm that the Turkish version of the BRS-SES maintains adequate construct validity and reliability despite the difference in the factor structure.

The goodness of fit indices of the confirmatory factor analysis model of the BRS-SES were used to evaluate the suitability of the scaling model to the data. These indices evaluate the fit between the observed data and the predicted model and show how well the model fits. The fit index of the scale was 0.906 and the RMSEA value was 0.079. When the fit index approaches 1, the model provides a good fit, and it is recommended that the RMSEA value is less than 0.08 [31, 32]. It was found that all goodness-of-fit indices of the Turkish BRS-SES were at an acceptable level and the construct validity of the scale was ensured.

The construct validity of the BRS-SES was evaluated by known group comparison. It was found that the mean scores of the BRS-SES and its sub-dimension scores of those who received cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) training, witnessed cardiac arrest, performed CPR, observed CPR application, used OED and gender were high and statistically significant (p < 0.05). These findings indicate that the BRS-SES is appropriately sensitive and specific in discriminating between groups with different characteristics.

Reliability analysis

In the reliability analyses of the BRS-SES, the internal consistency coefficient was evaluated, the overall internal consistency coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.956, and the internal consistency coefficients of the sub-dimensions were 0.963 and 0.737. Internal consistency coefficient is the most frequently used method to assess reliability in scales. An internal consistency coefficient above 0.70 is desirable [38]. According to the results of the internal consistency coefficients tests applied within the scope of internal consistency tests of the scale, the scale was found to be a reliable measurement tool. When the internal consistency coefficients of the original scale are compared with the findings of the research, it is seen that the total scale internal consistency coefficient is 0.96 and the sub-dimensions are between 0.85 and 0.96. It can be said that the scale has similar internal consistency coefficients with the original scale [7].

Test-retest method is one of the methods recommended for the evaluation of reliability in the adaptation or development of scales. The time invariance of the BRS-SES was evaluated by test-retest method with data collected 2 weeks later with 45 students who were suitable for the sample characteristics. Test-retest reliability is assessed by the correlation between the two applications and the comparison of the scale score averages obtained from the two assessments [38]. In the test-retest analysis in this study, it was found that there was a moderate positive correlation between the scale scores of the two assessments in the test-retest analysis, and there was no statistically significant difference in the mean scale scores obtained from the two assessments. These results can be accepted as an indicator of the scale’s invariance against time.

The item-total correlation coefficient shows the correlation between the scale items and the total scale score. The fact that this correlation is high and positive indicates that the items in the scale sample similar behaviour and the internal consistency coefficient of the scale is high. A correlation coefficient above 0.30 is recommended [40]. In this study, it was observed that all items of the scale were correlated with the total scale and the correlation coefficients were in the range of 0.325–0.835.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. the BRS-SES was specifically developed according to the European Resuscitation Council guidelines. It is recommended that relevant revisions be made in institutions that provide education according to different guidelines. The study was conducted with nursing students from two universities in Northern Cyprus therefore, the results are not generalizable. It is recommended that the BRS-SES be tested in nurses, doctors and other healthcare professionals working in hospitals.

Conclusions

The Turkish version of the BRS-SES was found to be a valid and reliable tool for assessing nursing students’ self-efficacy in resuscitation. As well as having good psychometric properties, the scale enables educators to assess the effectiveness of CPR education, identify areas where students feel less confident and design targeted interventions to strengthen these competencies. For example, it can be used to evaluate different teaching strategies, such as game-based learning, role-play scenarios, high-fidelity simulation, escape room activities, team-based learning and structured debriefing sessions, by measuring students’ self-efficacy before and after each one. This allows educators to compare the impact of different methods, identify which strategies best enhance confidence and competence, and adapt their curricula accordingly. Thus, the adapted scale supports evidence-based improvements in resuscitation education, ultimately contributing to higher-quality training and safer patient care. Although the structure was similar to the original scale, no adaptations in other cultural contexts were available for comparison.
