POTS: A neuroimmune disorder with cardiovascular manifestations?
Authors: Svetlana Blitshteyn
Published: 2026

In the study by Rao et al,1 23 participants with hyperadrenergic postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome were found to have a higher septal ST segment and lower skin sympathetic nerve activity (SKNA) when asleep than awake. Because of a significant negative correlation between the ST-segment height and the SKNA, the authors concluded that their findings support the hypothesis that low sympathetic tone is associated with the nocturnal ST-segment elevation in hyperadrenergic postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome.1 They further asserted that elevated vagal tone and reduced sympathetic tone were thought to be responsible for ST-segment elevation in postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS).2 Complementary to their assertion is a prior study demonstrating ST-segment depression as a physiological response to heightened sympathetic tone via the elevated SKNA in 19 healthy women.2 Although the study by Rao et al1 lacked a healthy control group, their study suggests significant differences in ST-segment elevation and SKNA in patients with POTS and healthy individuals.

Relevant to both studies are the following 3 mechanistic aspects: (1) small fiber neuropathy, which manifests with reduced epidermal nerve fiber density, reduced sweat gland density, and decreased sweat output, is a major comorbidity affecting at least 50% of patients with POTS3; (2) brain and brainstem abnormalities, involving neuroinflammation and cerebral hypoperfusion, resulting in cardiovascular and autonomic dysregulation4,5; and (3) immunologic and autoimmune perturbations affecting the autonomic nervous system via the inflammatory reflex mediated by the vagus nerve and other possible mechanisms.6

Recently, Malik et al7 reported findings of 3 T structural and functional magnetic resonance imaging from 18 patients with POTS and 20 controls, which demonstrated brain tissue structural changes, impaired neural responses, and cerebral hypoperfusion in autonomic regulatory sites during cardiovascular reflex testing in patients compared with controls. They concluded that central autonomic control deficits may help explain cardiovascular dysreflexia in POTS.7 Their findings provide further evidence for a prior hypothesis that POTS may be a central nervous system disorder with significant cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, immunologic, and metabolic manifestations.8 Importantly, we recently concluded that POTS is best viewed as a neuroimmune disorder, given the evidence of neuroinflammation, cerebral hypoperfusion (likely caused by or associated with neuroinflammation), glymphatic system alteration, autoimmune and immunologic factors, and other mechanisms.9 As such, a neuroimmune etiology, with the downstream effects on skin conductance, cardiovascular control of the heart rate and electrocardiographic measurements, is the likely pathophysiologic mechanism of POTS and its numerous cardiovascular, neurologic, and other multisystemic manifestations.
