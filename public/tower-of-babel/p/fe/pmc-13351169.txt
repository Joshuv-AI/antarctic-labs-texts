Predictors of Cerebral Small-Vessel Disease in Middle-Aged, Virologically Controlled People Living With HIV
Authors: Antoine Moulignier, Cédric Lamirel, Arbnor Zenuni, Patricia Koskas, Corinne Amiel, Roland Tubiana, Christine Katlama, Soraya Fellahi, Jean-Philippe Bastard, Jacqueline Capeau, Dominique Costagliola, Amélie Yavchitz, Augustin Lecler, Lambert Assoumou, for the Microvascular Brain REtina And Kidney (MicroBREAK) Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite durable viral suppression with antiretroviral therapy (ART), non-AIDS comorbidities remain frequent in aging people with HIV (PWH). Cerebral small-vessel disease (CSVD), a major contributor to stroke and cognitive decline, is up to twice as prevalent in middle-aged ART-treated PWH. Mechanisms underlying this excess risk remain unclear.ObjectiveThis study aims to identify inflammatory, viral, renal, and retinal vascular factors independently associated with CSVD in virologically controlled PWH.MethodsThe case–control MicroBREAK-2 study (NCT02210130), nested in the PWH group of the MicroBREAK-1 study, included 80 ART-treated (≥5 years) PWH aged ≥50 years with MRI-defined CSVD and 80 matched CSVD-free controls (age ± 5 years, sex, year of HIV diagnosis ± 5 years). Participants underwent brain MRI, renal, carotid, and orbital color Doppler ultrasonography, and comprehensive ophthalmologic assessment. Circulating inflammatory and coagulation biomarkers, Epstein–Barr virus plasma load, and antiretroviral exposure were measured. Independent associations were identified using conditional logistic regression with LASSO selection and bootstrap resampling.ResultsHypertension was the strongest independent correlate of CSVD (OR 4.92, 95% CI 1.23–19.62; P = .02). Markers of persistent inflammatory and coagulative activity were also independently linked to CSVD, including higher white blood cell count (OR 1.58; 1.17–2.14; P = .003) and elevated D-dimer levels (OR 1.52; 1.09–2.12; P = .01). Increased intraocular pressure was independently related to CSVD (OR 6.37; 1.38–29.4; P = .02). In contrast, HIV-specific parameters showed no independent relationship with CSVD.ConclusionsIn virologically suppressed PWH, CSVD is primarily associated with hypertension and persistent low-grade inflammation rather than HIV-specific factors, underscoring the need for aggressive cardiovascular risk management.Keywords: cardiovascular risk factors, cerebral small-vessel disease, HIV infection, hypertension, white matter hyperintensities

Cerebral small-vessel disease (CSVD) is a chronic microvascular disorder affecting intracranial arterioles, venules, and capillaries. Its main MRI features include white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), silent brain infarcts (SBIs), and cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) [1]. Highly prevalent in older adults, CSVD is associated with an increased risk of stroke, cognitive impairment, dementia, depression, and mortality, representing a major public health burden [2]. Aging is the strongest risk factor for CSVD [3]. In addition, cardiovascular risk factors (CVRFs), immunosenescence, and systemic inflammation contribute to its pathogenesis, and growing evidence suggests that chronic infection-driven inflammation may further increase risk [4–7].

With the widespread use of antiretroviral therapy (ART), people with HIV-1 (PWH) are living longer and increasingly exposed to age-related chronic diseases. People with HIV currently reaching middle age often have long-standing HIV infection acquired in earlier treatment eras, during which suboptimal antiretroviral regimens and prolonged immunodeficiency were common. As a result, they may accumulate risk factors for CSVD, including a higher burden of CVRFs and a history of severe immunosuppression, reflected by low nadir CD4 counts, potentially fostering persistent immune activation and chronic inflammation [8, 9]. Several studies report that the prevalence of CSVD is up to twice higher in middle-aged PWH compared with HIV-uninfected matched controls, even among individuals with sustained HIV suppression and after adjustment for traditional CSVD risk factors [10, 11]. The rising burden of CSVD and its clinical consequences in aging PWH has therefore become a growing concern in routine care. However, the mechanisms underlying the excess CSVD risk in well-controlled, ART-treated PWH remain incompletely understood.

Cerebral small-vessel disease is increasingly viewed as part of a systemic microvascular disorder affecting other end-organs with similar hemodynamic characteristics, notably the kidney and the retina, high-flow, low-resistance vascular beds particularly vulnerable to microvascular injury [12, 13]. These arterial territories provide accessible and relatively inexpensive surrogates for investigating systemic microvascular dysfunction in clinical practice and research settings [14, 15].

We conducted a case–control study in middle-aged PWH with sustained virological suppression, with and without CSVD, to determine whether inflammatory and viral variables, ART exposure, and markers of systemic vascular dysfunction, spanning carotid atherosclerosis and renal and retinal microcirculation, were independently linked to CSVD.

METHODS

Patient Consent Statement

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of CPP Île-de-France VI (Groupe Hospitalier Pitié–Salpêtrière) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and STROBE guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent. Data are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Population

The cross-sectional MicroBREAK-1 study (NCT02082574) was designed to compare the prevalence of silent CSVD detected by brain MRI in PWH and matched HIV-uninfected controls [10]. Recruitment procedures and eligibility criteria have been described previously [10]. Among 456 PWH included, 235 (51.5%) had MRI-defined CSVD.

The case–control study (MicroBREAK-2; NCT02210130) was nested within the PWH group of MicroBREAK-1. All PWH with CSVD were eligible as cases and were invited to participate. For each case, one control without CSVD was selected from the same cohort and matched 1:1 on age (±5 years), sex, and year of HIV diagnosis (±5 years). Nonparticipation was mainly due to logistical constraints, and no exclusion was based on clinical characteristics.

At inclusion, demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, sex, geographic origin (Caucasian, sub-Saharan African, and other), educational level, body mass index, and CVRFs. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or ongoing antihypertensive treatment. Diabetes mellitus was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 1.26 g/L, prior diagnosis, or antidiabetic treatment. History of cardiovascular disease (coronary artery disease, heart failure, peripheral artery disease, or stroke), tobacco smoking, alcohol and recreational drug exposure (including cannabis, cocaine, amphetamine, and heroine), and Epworth scale score were recorded. HIV-related variables included transmission group, CDC stage, current and past ART exposure, nadir and current CD4 counts, plasma HIV RNA level, and hepatitis B serology.

Cognitive performance was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) by trained raters blinded to case–control status [16]. All participants underwent a standardized neurological and ophthalmological examination, including retinal fundus photography. Retinal images were analyzed by an experienced ophthalmologist blinded to clinical and imaging data.

Neuroimaging

An experienced neuroradiologist, blinded to clinical data, reviewed all brain MRI scans. Severe CSVD was defined as Fazekas grade 2/3 WMHs, and/or ≥1 SBI, and/or ≥1 CMB, excluding patients with only Fazekas grade 1 WMHs, classified as mild CSVD [10].

Color Doppler ultrasonography of the neck, orbital, and kidney arteries was performed according to standardized protocols [17–19], with operators blinded to case–control status. Renal and orbital Doppler assessments included measurement of peak systolic (PSV) and end-diastolic velocities (EDV). These were obtained in the main renal, interlobar, and arcuate arteries, as well as in the ophthalmic and central retinal arteries. Carotid intima–media thickness (IMT) and plaque assessment followed Mannheim consensus criteria [20]. Atherosclerosis was defined as the presence of carotid plaque and/or IMT ≥ 0.9 mm.

Biological Parameters

Fasting morning blood samples were collected and processed according to standardized procedures. Serum and plasma aliquots were stored at −80°C until analysis. Biological measurements included blood cell count, blood and urinary creatinine, albumin, and microalbumin, fasting glucose, lipid profile, insulin, adiponectin, CMV, and EBV serology.

We also analyzed 12 circulating biomarkers reflecting key pathways of immune activation and endothelial dysfunction. Markers of systemic inflammation included high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, interleukin (IL)-6, IL-10, soluble tumor necrosis factor receptor-1 and -2, total homocysteine, soluble CD163, soluble CD14, and CCL2/MCP1. Markers of vascular inflammation and endothelial activation included D-dimer, intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1). Measurements were performed using commercially available assays as previously described [21].

Statistical Analyses

Baseline characteristics are presented as numbers (percentages) for categorical variables and medians [interquartile range, IQR] for continuous variables. Comparisons between cases and controls were performed using conditional logistic regression to account for matching.

To address multicollinearity and limit overfitting given the large number of candidate predictors, we implemented a robust resampling-based variable selection strategy. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation, generating 500 imputed datasets. For each imputed dataset, 100 bootstrap samples were drawn (total 50 000 resampled datasets). Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was applied within each resample to identify relevant predictors among demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables.

Variables selected in more than 75% of models were considered stable predictors and entered into the final multivariable conditional logistic regression models to identify independent associations with CSVD (Supplementary Table 1). This approach improves model stability and reproducibility while reducing the risk of spurious associations due to multiple testing.

Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was set at a 2-tailed P < .05. Analyses were conducted in R (v4.0.5).

The cohort was predominantly male. Cases were slightly older than controls (median 57.5 vs 55.5 years). Among PWH with CSVD, 45 (56%) met criteria for severe CSVD. People with HIV with Fazekas grades 1, 2, and 3 accounted for 52%, 34%, and 13% of cases, respectively. Silent brain infarcts were detected in 11%, and 17% had ≥1 cerebral MB.

Clinical and HIV-Related Characteristics

Compared with controls, CSVD cases had a higher burden of CVRFs, including hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, and heavy smoking (≥20 pack-years). Exposure to integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs) was significantly less common and of shorter duration among cases than controls.

Systemic Vascular Assessment

Color Doppler ultrasonography revealed a higher prevalence of atherosclerotic plaques in both carotid arteries among CSVD cases. Carotid IMT did not differ between groups. Renal and orbital Doppler analyses showed more pronounced hemodynamic alterations in CSVD cases. Reduced EDV were observed in central retinal arteries. In univariable analysis, abnormal fundus findings were associated with CSVD (OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.17–0.99, P = .048), but this variable did not meet the predefined stability threshold for inclusion in multivariable models.

Factors Independently Associated With CSVD

Results of univariable and multivariable analyses are presented in Table 4. After resampling-based variable selection and multivariable conditional logistic regression, hypertension (OR 4.92, 95% CI 1.23–19.62, P = .02), higher white blood cell count (OR 1.58, 95% CI 1.17–2.14, P = .003), and elevated D-dimer levels (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.09–2.12, P = .01) were independently associated with CSVD. Exposure to the INSTI class was independently associated with lower odds of CSVD (OR 0.26, 95% CI 0.09–0.75, P = .01). Traditional HIV-related variables, including nadir and current CD4 count, duration of ART, and exposure to other ART classes, were not independently associated with CSVD.

DISCUSSION

In this nested case–control study of virologically suppressed, long-term ART-treated PWH aged ≥50 years, CSVD was independently associated with hypertension and markers of systemic inflammation and coagulation activation, whereas HIV-specific immunologic and treatment history parameters were not. These results suggest that, in well-controlled HIV infection, cerebral microvascular lesions appear predominantly related to conventional and systemic vascular mechanisms rather than by ongoing viral replication or historical immunodeficiency.

This study benefits from a carefully matched case–control design within a well-characterized cohort of virologically suppressed PWH, limiting confounding by age, sex, and duration since HIV diagnosis. The comprehensive vascular assessment, integrating carotid atherosclerosis, renal and orbital Doppler hemodynamics, retinal evaluation, and circulating biomarkers, enabled CSVD to be examined within a systemic vascular framework rather than as an isolated cerebral condition. The cross-sectional design, however, precludes causal inference, and the sample size constrains statistical precision for some exploratory parameters. To mitigate the risk of false-positive findings, we applied LASSO-based variable selection combined with extensive bootstrap resampling to identify stable predictors. Replication in larger longitudinal cohorts will be essential to confirm these associations and clarify temporal relationships.

Hypertension was the strongest independent predictor of CSVD, conferring an almost fivefold increase in odds. This association persisted after extensive resampling-based variable selection, underscoring its robustness. Although traditional CVRFs were more frequent among cases in univariable analyses, only hypertension remained independently associated, suggesting that it may represent the principal hemodynamic driver of small-vessel injury in PWH [2, 10, 11].

WBC count and D-dimer were independently associated with CSVD despite sustained virological suppression and independently of nadir or current CD4 counts. Persistent systemic immune activation has been documented in PWH receiving effective ART. D-dimer, a marker of coagulation activation and systemic inflammation, has consistently been linked to cardiovascular events and mortality in PWH, including those with sustained viral suppression, as well as to ischemic stroke and CSVD in the general population [11, 22–26]. Higher D-dimer levels have also been linked to greater WMHs burden in PWH [11]. Elevated WBC count, a nonspecific marker of inflammation, has similarly been linked to cerebrovascular risk, including stroke and CSVD [27]. Chronic immune activation in treated HIV infection may represent a mechanistic pathway linking persistent immune activation and microvascular injury [28].

Higher intraocular pressure was independently linked to CSVD. In the general population, glaucoma has been associated with MRI markers of CSVD [29], supporting a connection between ocular and cerebral microcirculation [13]. Although confidence intervals were wide, this finding is biologically plausible and consistent with systemic microvascular vulnerability affecting high-flow, low-resistance end-organs. Elevated intraocular pressure may therefore represent a clinically accessible marker of systemic microvascular vulnerability, potentially identifying individuals in whom silent CSVD should be considered. Prospective validation will be required before clinical implications can be defined.

An unexpected inverse association was observed between cumulative INSTI exposure and CSVD risk. This finding is unlikely to reflect drug-specific protective effects given the relatively recent introduction of INSTIs and limited exposure duration in this cohort. During the study period, early INSTI use largely corresponded to raltegravir, preferentially prescribed to individuals with long-standing infection and greater comorbidity burden because of its favorable interaction profile [30]. These associations likely reflect treatment era effects, prescribing patterns, and survivor bias rather than causal relationships. In this context, antiretroviral exposure should be interpreted as a marker of patient trajectory rather than a determinant of CSVD risk.

In conclusion, among older PWH receiving effective ART, CSVD appears largely associated with hypertension and persistent systemic inflammation rather than HIV-specific disease or treatment parameters. These findings align with observations from large treated HIV cohorts such as CHARTER, in which neurocognitive decline was predominantly linked to the burden of comorbid conditions rather than to HIV itself [31]. Together, this converging evidence suggests that, in the era of sustained viral suppression, modifiable comorbidities may outweigh direct viral effects in shaping long-term neurological vulnerability. Viral control alone may therefore be insufficient to prevent vascular brain injury [9, 31–34]. Strengthening systematic identification and optimal management of cardiovascular and inflammatory risk factors may represent a critical opportunity to reduce future cerebrovascular complications in aging PWH [10, 35–37].
