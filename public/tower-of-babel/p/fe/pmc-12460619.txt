Fatty acid degradation-related gene signatures as biomarkers for ocular sarcoidosis
Authors: Zixuan Wu, Yuan Gao, Kang Tan, Xiaolei Yao, Qinghua Peng, Wenjuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSarcoidosis is characterized by the proliferation of noncaseating granulomas and presents as a complex chronic inflammatory disease. It extensively affects multiple organ systems, with 30–60% of patients experiencing ocular complications, predominantly bilateral granulomatous uveitis. Fatty acid degradation, a fundamental metabolic process, is crucial for cellular energy homeostasis, involving the breakdown of fatty acids to produce acetyl-CoA, NADH, and FADH2, which then enter the citric acid cycle and electron transport chain to generate ATP. Despite its importance, the role of fatty acid degradation genes (FADGs) in the pathophysiology of Ocular sarcoidosis (OS) remains unclear. To identify candidates potentially involved in OS, we intersected differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with a curated list of 177 FADGs. Advanced methodologies, including Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA), were employed to explore biological functions. Further refinement using Lasso regression and Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE) allowed for the identification of key hub genes and assessment of their diagnostic potential for OS. Our investigation identified two FADGs, ADH1B and ECI1, closely associated with OS. Functional analyses revealed their involvement in processes such as fatty acid metabolic processes, small molecule catabolic processes, and fatty acid oxidation. Importantly, the diagnostic capabilities of these FADGs demonstrated significant efficacy in distinguishing OS from unaffected states. Through rigorous bioinformatics analyses, this study identifies ADH1B and ECI1 as novel biomarker candidates for OS, elucidating their potential roles in the disease’s pathogenesis. Introduction

Sarcoidosis is a complex systemic inflammatory disorder characterized by non-caseating epithelioid giant cell granulomas in various organs1. The etiology of this condition, driven by interactions between inflammatory processes and environmental stimuli, notably infectious agents, remains poorly understood1. This disease predominantly affects the lungs, skin, eyes, and lymph nodes, with manifestations varying significantly among individuals and across populations2. Ocular sarcoidosis (OS), in particular, presents a broad spectrum of symptoms—ranging from significant ocular signs without systemic involvement to concurrent severe systemic disease, and may even precede systemic manifestations2. Diagnosing sarcoidosis is challenging due to the variable sensitivity of tests and the impossibility of definitive diagnosis without tissue biopsy in some cases3. Ocular involvement in sarcoidosis varies widely, reported between 7 and 60%, with posterior eye disease often correlating with increased central nervous system involvement4. OS can manifest with a range of symptoms, complicating diagnosis further due to its clinical overlap with other conditions5. Histologically, the presence of nonnecrotizing granulomas and negative stains for pathogens and foreign materials remains the diagnostic gold standard6. Currently, there is no alternative diagnostic test that can decisively confirm sarcoidosis or exclude other mimicking conditions, highlighting the complexity of its clinical presentation. Given these challenges, understanding the molecular pathways underlying OS is crucial6. Elucidating these pathways is essential for developing targeted therapies that may reduce disease recurrence and improve prognostic outcomes, thereby advancing treatment paradigms in sarcoidosis.

Fatty acid degradation, a pivotal metabolic process, is essential for cellular energy homeostasis. This pathway involves the breakdown of fatty acids into acetyl-CoA, NADH, and FADH2, which subsequently enter the citric acid cycle and electron transport chain to generate ATP. Predominantly occurring in the mitochondria of eukaryotic cells, this catabolic sequence is known as β-oxidation and consists of tightly regulated enzymatic steps7. The process begins with the activation of fatty acids, converting them into acyl-CoA through an ATP-dependent reaction catalyzed by acyl-CoA synthetase. The resulting acyl-CoA is transported into the mitochondrial matrix via the carnitine shuttle, a critical mechanism involving carnitine acyltransferases and the carnitine carrier protein[8]. Within the mitochondrial matrix, β-oxidation proceeds through a cyclic sequence of four reactions: dehydrogenation by acyl-CoA dehydrogenase, hydration by enoyl-CoA hydratase, a second dehydrogenation by 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase, and thiolytic cleavage by β-ketothiolase9. These reactions progressively shorten the fatty acid chain by two-carbon units, yielding acetyl-CoA and a shortened acyl-CoA that re-enters the cycle. Each round of β-oxidation generates one molecule each of FADH2 and NADH, which are crucial electron donors for the mitochondrial respiratory chain10. The acetyl-CoA produced is further oxidized in the citric acid cycle, generating additional NADH and FADH2. These molecules feed into oxidative phosphorylation, ultimately driving the synthesis of ATP. This highly efficient energy production mechanism underscores the significance of fatty acid degradation in maintaining metabolic balance, particularly during periods of fasting or extended physical activity when glucose availability is limited11. Moreover, the regulation of fatty acid degradation is intricately linked to the nutritional and hormonal status of the organism. Key regulatory points include the inhibition of carnitine palmitoyltransferase I by malonyl-CoA, which prevents the simultaneous occurrence of fatty acid synthesis and degradation, and the activation of key enzymes by AMP-activated protein kinase in response to low energy states12. In summary, fatty acid degradation via β-oxidation is a central metabolic pathway critical for energy production and metabolic homeostasis. Its regulation ensures metabolic efficiency and balance, adapting to the organism’s energy needs and nutritional status.

The advent of high-throughput transcriptome sequencing and the extensive clinical annotations provided by the OS Initiative have ushered in new opportunities for investigating the transcriptional dynamics and molecular pathways implicated in OS13–15. Bioinformatics analyses of these comprehensive datasets have yielded compelling insights, shedding light on the intricate pathophysiology and mechanisms underpinning OS from multiple perspectives. Despite these advancements, there remains a notable gap in the literature concerning the specific exploration of FADGs in OS using bioinformatics tools. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to leverage OS-related GEO datasets to elucidate the role and significance of FADGs within the OS framework, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Framework.

Materials and methods

We adopted the methodologies delineated by Zi-Xuan Wu and colleagues in 202316.

Raw data and differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

We leveraged two publicly available transcriptomic datasets from the GEO repository—GSE58331 and GSE105149—to systematically investigate the molecular underpinnings of ocular sarcoidosis (Table 1). GSE58331 served as the discovery cohort, while GSE105149 was utilized for independent validation. A curated list of 65 FADGs was obtained from the MSigDB (Table S1). Using custom Perl scripts, transcriptomic profiles were extracted and harmonized to ensure consistent gene annotation across datasets. DEGs among the FADGs were identified from GSE58331 based on rigorous thresholds (FDR < 0.05 and |log₂FC|≥ 1), followed by correlation analysis using Pearson’s method via the corrplot package in R. Beyond elucidating disease-specific molecular signatures, the integration of these transcriptomic datasets holds potential to enhance diagnostic precision in ocular sarcoidosis. Traditional clinical diagnosis of OS remains challenging due to its nonspecific presentation and overlap with other inflammatory orbital disorders. By contrast, transcriptomic profiling enables unbiased identification of gene expression patterns that may serve as molecular fingerprints of the disease. In particular, the reproducibility of DEG signals across both training and validation cohorts underscores their potential utility as candidate biomarkers. These insights lay the foundation for future development of transcriptome-based diagnostic assays—offering the possibility of earlier detection, more accurate differentiation from mimicking conditions, and ultimately, improved clinical decision-making in OS.

Development of a predictive model, immune cell infiltration, and functional enrichment analysis

To explore the biological relevance and pathway engagement of DEGs, we performed GO and KEGG analyses. We utilized the R programming environment to investigate the influence of differentially expressed FADGs on BP, MF, and CC. For model optimization, Lasso regression analysis was implemented using the glmnet package, enhanced through rigorous cross-validation procedures. Further validation was achieved through the deployment of the SVM-RFE algorithm, facilitated by the e1071 package, meticulously crafting a robust machine learning model. The reliability and accuracy of our model were stringently assessed via cross-validation, focusing on minimizing error rates and ensuring predictive consistency. Through the utilization of the ggplot2 package, our analysis prioritized differentially expressed genes, pinpointing pivotal genes crucial for disease classification. Moreover, the employment of the CIBERSORT algorithm enabled detailed analysis of immune cell composition, providing profound insights into the immunological milieu pertinent to the pathology under investigation.

Gene set enrichment and variation analyses and drug-gene interaction insights

To decipher functional dynamics and pathway deviations in diverse biological samples, we applied GSEA and GSVA. Using the R platform, we analyzed the impact of differentially expressed FADGs on BP, MF, and CC, and related pathways. This provided a comprehensive view of their involvement in disease pathophysiology. Recognizing the critical role of validated biomarkers in therapeutic design, precise drug-gene interaction predictions are paramount. For this purpose, we leveraged the DGIdb to forecast potential interactions for pivotal hub genes, thereby informing potential therapeutic targets.

Assembly of an mRNA-miRNA-lncRNA regulatory network

Non-coding RNA transcripts are crucial determinants in the genetic regulatory framework. miRNAs orchestrate gene expression by modulating mRNA stability and translation, whereas lncRNAs, which exceed 200 nucleotides in length, influence cellular mechanisms through chromosomal modifications, transcriptional activation, and molecular interference. Recent evidence underscores a sophisticated interaction between miRNAs and lncRNAs, manifesting in competitive binding dynamics with other regulatory molecules. This interaction model, referred to as the ceRNA hypothesis, elucidates how lncRNAs modulate gene expression by miRNA sequestration. To construct this regulatory network, we extracted validated miRNA-lncRNA-target interactions from databases such as miRTarBase and PrognoScan, providing a rich resource for understanding these complex regulatory relationships.

Model construction

We employed a comprehensive methodology involving LASSO regression analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression analysis (Figs. 3a,b). Subsequently, we constructed a machine learning model using SVM-RFE to validate the predictive accuracy and reliability of the model. This model demonstrated impressive accuracy (0.96) with a minimal error rate of 0.04 (Figs. 3c,d). A cross-reference of five FADGs identified through both LASSO and SVM methodologies was performed (Fig. 3e). Comparative analysis with these 2 hub genes revealed significantly high area under the curve (AUC) values, indicating robust predictive power: ECI1 (AUC = 0.699), ADH1B (AUC = 0.997) (Fig. 3f). Notably, an AUC of 0.997 (95% Confidence Interval: 0.986 − 1.000) was achieved in the GSE58331 dataset (Fig. 3g; Table 2 and S4).

Identification of Common RNAs and Construction of miRNA-LncRNA Shared Genes Network

We conducted searches in three databases to identify 25 miRNAs and 17 lncRNAs linked with OS (Table S7a-b). Table S7 demonstrates the matching of these genes against the corresponding miRNA database. The databases used for this search were miRanda17, miRDB18, and TargetScan19. Each match with the relevant miRNA in these databases was assigned a score of 1. Notably, a match in all three databases received a score of 3. The corresponding lncRNA data was obtained by matching the miRNAs with the spongeScan database. The miRNA-lncRNA-gene interaction network was established by intersecting datasets with shared genes identified through Lasso regression and SVM-RFE. This comprehensive network encompasses 16 lncRNAs, 25 miRNAs, and several common genes, prominently featuring hub genes: ADH1B (Fig. 8).

Discussions

Ophthalmic disturbances are prevalent in a significant proportion of sarcoidosis patients, affecting 30% to 60% of cases. OS may occur independently, without systemic manifestations, or as the primary site of affliction, accompanied by limited symptoms in other regions20. Although OS can affect any part of the eye, uveitis is the most common form, impacting 20% to 30% of those diagnosed with sarcoidosis21. The burden of these symptoms is considerable, affecting patients’ physical and psychological well-being and imposing substantial economic costs. Ocular complications can lead to reduced visual function, pain, discomfort, and diminished quality of life22. Addressing the challenges of OS necessitates a multidisciplinary approach involving ophthalmologists, pulmonologists, rheumatologists, and other relevant specialists to ensure comprehensive care. Accurate and timely diagnosis of OS is critical to prevent irreversible damage and optimize treatment outcomes23. Fatty acid degradation, particularly via mitochondrial β-oxidation, is a central metabolic process essential for cellular energy homeostasis across various ocular tissues. While the retina is well-known for its exceptional metabolic demands, it is important to emphasize that the current analysis is based on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples predominantly derived from orbital tissue, with limited or no direct representation of retinal architecture. Therefore, extrapolations regarding retinal-specific metabolism must be interpreted with caution. In orbital tissues, especially within inflammatory or neoplastic microenvironments, fatty acid catabolism may serve functions distinct from those in photoreceptors. Mitochondrial β-oxidation in these extra-retinal ocular compartments contributes not only to energy generation but also to immunometabolic reprogramming, redox regulation, and biosynthesis of lipid-derived signaling molecules. Our findings reveal that genes associated with FADGs interact significantly with transcriptional regulators implicated in angiogenesis and immune homeostasis24. Notably, ADH1B and ECI1 emerged as key nodes within this regulatory framework, demonstrating functional relevance to epithelial migration, tissue remodeling, and broader immune signaling cascades. These observations suggest that FADGs may play an underappreciated yet critical role in modulating the local immunometabolic environment of the orbit. Beyond canonical energy metabolism, their involvement in lipid signaling and immune modulation may contribute to pathological processes such as tissue fibrosis, neovascularization, and chronic inflammation observed in orbital disorders25. This paradigm shifts the focus of fatty acid metabolism research from photoreceptor energetics to a broader, tissue-specific context, underscoring the need for spatially resolved transcriptomic and proteomic approaches in future studies.

ADH1B and ECI1 are enzymes implicated in metabolic processes that significantly influence ocular health. ADH1B, a member of the alcohol dehydrogenase family, is primarily involved in the oxidation of alcohols to aldehydes, contributing to cellular metabolic homeostasis. In the context of the eye, ADH1B has been linked to the metabolism of retinoids, which are crucial for maintaining visual function. Retinoids, including retinol and retinal, play essential roles in the visual cycle, particularly in phototransduction within the retina26. Dysregulation of ADH1B activity can lead to alterations in retinoid metabolism, potentially impairing visual processes and contributing to retinal pathologies27. ECI1, on the other hand, is a key enzyme in the β-oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids, facilitating the conversion of cis-double bonds to trans-double bonds, which is a critical step in fatty acid catabolism. The retina, a highly metabolic tissue, relies on efficient fatty acid β-oxidation for energy production28. ECI1’s role in this pathway ensures the proper degradation of unsaturated fatty acids, contributing to the ATP supply necessary for photoreceptor function and overall retinal health29. Deficiencies or mutations in ECI1 can disrupt fatty acid metabolism, leading to energy deficits in retinal cells and potentially contributing to retinal degeneration30. Both ADH1B and ECI1 are thus integral to maintaining the metabolic environment of ocular tissues. ADH1B’s involvement in retinoid metabolism underscores its importance in the visual cycle and retinal health, while ECI1’s role in fatty acid β-oxidation highlights its contribution to the energetic demands of the retina31. Understanding the precise mechanisms by which these enzymes influence ocular health can provide insights into the pathogenesis of retinal diseases and inform the development of targeted therapeutic strategies. This body of evidence reveals a nuanced interplay between ADH1B and ECI1 in regulating retinal function and intraocular pressure, offering fresh insights into the pathophysiology of ocular diseases. Our study, supported by dataset GSE105149, delves into the roles of these genes in OS, suggesting that Angiogenesis-related traits could serve as potential prognostic indicators. This comprehension paves the way for novel approaches in managing and treating ocular diseases, underscoring the importance of ADH1B and ECI1 in preserving ocular health.

In the intricate landscape of OS, an expanding body of evidence is challenging the traditional view that links an enhanced immune response solely to CD4 cell activity. Instead, a more complex picture is emerging, one that includes pre-existing T-regulatory cells and a blend of both proinflammatory and regulatory elements, such as variations in cytokine levels32. This complexity may underlie a disordered immune reconstitution, rendering individuals more vulnerable to opportunistic infections—whether existing, latent, or previously managed[33]. As a result, diseases such as Tuberculosis, Cytomegalovirus infections, Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy, Kaposi’s Sarcoma, and various autoimmune disorders may become exacerbated or escape detection. Notably, Cytomegalovirus retinitis emerges as a leading opportunistic infection associated with Immunological Recovery Uveitis34,35. Emerging therapeutic strategies that aim to increase intracellular cAMP levels offer a promising avenue for mitigating chronic inflammation. Small-molecule phosphodiesterase 4 (PDE4) inhibitors, which prevent cAMP degradation, have shown efficacy in a range of inflammatory conditions, including Inflammatory Bowel Disease, Atopic Dermatitis, and Rheumatoid Arthritis36,37. Building on our prior research, we investigated the expression patterns of FADGs in the immune environment. Strikingly, our observations revealed significant expression in various cell types within the treated group, including naive B cells, memory B cells, follicular helper T cells, and resting NK cells. Through bioinformatics validation, we identified abnormal gene expression signatures linked to dysregulated immune responses in OS patients, offering crucial insights into the disease mechanisms. This study not only underscores the complex interplay of immune factors in OS but also highlights potential therapeutic avenues through the modulation of intracellular signaling pathways.

The exploration of biomarkers in the context of OS has been markedly limited, with the nexus between metabolic processes and ocular diseases only recently beginning to unfurl through bioinformatics analyse[41,42,43]s. In the rapidly evolving field of OS research, Liu et al. recently identified hub genes associated with the condition using weighted gene co-expression network analysis. Similarly, Hu et al. developed a bioinformatics model for thyroid eye disease, pinpointing 11 key genes. Huang et al. made significant strides by identifying six crucial genes central to diabetic retinopathy through sophisticated bioinformatics analyses, complemented by in vivo validation. Despite these advancements, the intersection between angiogenesis and OS remains largely unexplored. Our study aims to address this gap by investigating cell metabolism and examining FADGs extracted from GEO datasets. This innovative approach distinguishes our work, offering novel theoretical perspectives and methodological innovations. Several limitations of this study warrant critical consideration. First, our analyses were conducted exclusively on publicly available transcriptomic datasets, which—despite rigorous normalization procedures—remain susceptible to batch effects and inter-sample heterogeneity. Such technical and biological variability may obscure subtle yet biologically meaningful alterations in the expression of FADGs. Second, while our integrative bioinformatic pipeline suggests plausible mechanistic links between FADGs and immunometabolic regulation in OS, these associations remain correlative in nature and lack direct experimental substantiation. The absence of orthogonal validation techniques, such as quantitative PCR, Western blotting, or immunohistochemistry, limits our ability to definitively confirm FADG expression patterns at the protein level and to elucidate their downstream functional consequences. Moreover, our focus on protein-coding transcripts may have inadvertently excluded the contribution of non-coding RNAs—including lncRNAs and miRNAs—which are increasingly recognized as critical modulators of immune signaling, metabolic homeostasis, and disease progression. The exclusion of these regulatory elements may constrain a comprehensive understanding of the FADG-centered transcriptional network and its role in OS pathogenesis. To overcome these limitations, future investigations should adopt integrated multi-omics strategies, combining bulk and single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomic technologies, and proteomic profiling of patient-derived orbital tissues. Such approaches will be instrumental in resolving cell type–specific expression patterns and uncovering the spatial dynamics of FADG activity within the immune microenvironment. Ultimately, bridging computational inferences with functional validation will be essential for delineating the biological significance of FADGs and evaluating their translational potential as diagnostic biomarkers or therapeutic targets in precision ophthalmology for OS.

Conclusions

The pathogenesis and progression of OS are driven by intricate, multifactorial interactions involving a wide array of molecular targets, signaling pathways, and regulatory networks. Within this complex framework, ADH1B and ECI1 emerge as central regulators, exerting substantial influence over the fatty acid degradation program. Their functional relevance extends beyond metabolic turnover, potentially linking lipid catabolism to immunometabolic remodeling and tissue homeostasis in the orbital microenvironment.
