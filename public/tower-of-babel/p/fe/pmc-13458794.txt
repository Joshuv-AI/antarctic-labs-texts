A successful case of revascularization with a hybrid intervention for Leriche syndrome combined with bilateral femoropopliteal occlusive disease
Authors: Nobuo Ishiguro, Akimitsu Tanaka, Kazuo Kato, Takeki Ohashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Leriche syndrome (LS) is an aortoiliac occlusive disease (AIOD) characterized by claudication, decreased femoral pulses, and erectile dysfunction. We present an LS case with bilateral femoropopliteal occlusive disease (FPOD) considered untreatable with surgery or endovascular therapy alone but successfully revascularized via hybrid intervention (HI). An 84-year-old woman with a history of LS was admitted to our hospital with rest pain in the right leg, and coldness and numbness in the left leg. The ankle–brachial index (ABI) indicated severe bilateral amplitude reduction and pulse elongation. Computed tomography angiography revealed occlusion of the terminal aorta, bilateral common iliac artery to external iliac artery, common femoral artery to superficial femoral artery, and right popliteal artery with poor run-off. We initially decided to perform HI only on the right lower extremity. Easy guidewire passage through the occluded lesion suggested a primarily thrombotic nature. HI using Fogarty arterial thrombectomy was eventually performed for the bilateral lower extremities. Complete revascularization was achieved using covered and self-expanding stents for aortoiliac lesions, and drug-eluting stent deployment and drug-coated balloon angioplasty for FPOD. The post-operative ABI was elevated, and symptoms improved bilaterally. This HI is highly effective for treating AIOD combined with bilateral FPOD involving thrombotic lesions.Learning objectiveAortoiliac occlusive disease (AIOD) with bilateral femoropopliteal occlusive disease was considered difficult to treat with surgery or endovascular therapy (EVT) alone. Hybrid interventions are ideal for patients with AIOD with extension to the common femoral artery or superficial femoral artery, especially involving thrombotic lesions. This procedure achieves good results that cannot be obtained by surgery or EVT alone.

Introduction

Leriche syndrome (LS) is an aortoiliac occlusive disease (AIOD) characterized by claudication, decreased femoral pulses, and erectile dysfunction. An aging population with increasingly complex comorbidities has changed the clinical spectrum; thus, contemporary LS often presents with more complex anatomy (calcification, tortuosity, multilevel disease), making simple endovascular therapy (EVT) challenging. Moreover, standard aortobifemoral bypass surgery (BS) poses surgical risks. Technological developments have improved EVT outcomes, increasing demand for complex EVT or hybrid intervention (HI) [1]. We present a case of LS combined with bilateral femoropopliteal occlusive disease that was successfully revascularized using an HI.

Case report

An 84-year-old woman presented to our hospital with a 1-month history of right leg rest pain (Rutherford category IV) that did not improve with tramadol (150 mg/day) and acetaminophen (2000 mg/day). Bilateral leg coldness and numbness persisted for 2 years, with LS confirmed by magnetic resonance angiography 1 year prior. Despite refusing invasive therapy, unbearable rest pain prompted revascularization. The patient had chronic kidney disease, permanent atrial fibrillation, spinal epidural abscess, and iliopsoas abscess; permanently used a wheelchair; and took anticoagulants (edoxaban 30 mg/day) daily. Her legs were pale, cold, and pulseless. The ankle–brachial index (ABI) indicated severe bilateral amplitude reduction and pulse elongation. Computed tomography (CT) angiography revealed occlusion of the abdominal aorta (AA), bilateral common iliac arteries (CIAs) to external iliac artery (EIA), common femoral artery (CFA) to superficial femoral artery (SFA), and right popliteal artery (Pop.A) with poor run-off; the right distal EIA was partially patent (Fig. 1A–D). Revascularization was challenging due to absent appropriate EVT access, unsuitable femoral arteries for BS, and small (2.3 mm diameter) bilateral great saphenous veins. CT imaging revealed calcification along the vessel walls from the CFA to the SFA, suggesting an unexpectedly hard occluded lesion. After consulting a vascular surgeon, HI was performed only in the right lower extremity under total intravenous anesthesia. A longitudinal incision was made from the right inguinal ligament to groin. CFA, SFA, and deep femoral artery were identified and controlled via vessel loops. After 5000 U IV unfractionated heparin, a 6-Fr Destination guiding sheath (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) was advanced to the AA via the brachial artery. A guidewire was inserted through the introducer needle during the ultrasound-guided CFA puncture and advanced easily into the occluded EIA. After retrograde insertion of a 6-Fr sheath in the CFA, a knuckle guidewire was inserted using a 0.025-in. hydrophilic guidewire (Radifocus; Terumo) and a 4-Fr Fogarty catheter (FC; Edwards Lifesciences Corporation, Irvine, CA, USA) and advanced easily into the CIA. The guidewire penetrated the proximal cap of the AA occlusion site (Fig. 2A). The Radifocus was replaced with a cruise guidewire (Asahi Intec, Aichi, Japan) using a 4-Fr FC. Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS; Eagle-Eye Platinum ST; Philips, Amsterdam, Netherlands) confirmed intraluminal position of the guidewire. Simple and rapid penetration through the occluded lesion indicated a thrombotic lesion. After puncturing and antegrade insertion of a 6-Fr sheath into the CFA, a 6-Fr guiding catheter (GC) (Mach1, ST 90 cm; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) was inserted via the antegrade approach. A Jupiter FC3 guidewire (Boston Scientific) was passed through the occluded SFA to the Pop.A using IVUS (Fig. 2C). To minimize the risk of distal embolization (DE), the guidewire was exchanged with a 5.0-mm Filtrap wire (Nipro, Osaka, Japan) via a Mach1 GC. AT was performed using a 4-Fr FC, and massive red thrombi and tissues were retrieved (Fig. 2D).

The procedure lasted 65 min. As occluded lesions were considered thrombotic and bilateral symptoms were originally reported, an HI was similarly performed on the left lower extremity via surgical cutdown. Following retrograde 6-Fr sheath insertion in the left CFA, a 6-Fr Mach1 GC was inserted into the CFA. The Jupiter SFC guidewire (Boston Scientific) was advanced into the occluded EIA to the CIA under IVUS guidance, penetrating the proximal cap of the occluded AA (Fig. 2E). AT was performed using a 4-Fr FC, and massive red thrombi were retrieved, with weak back-bleeding (Fig. 2F).

IVUS revealed residual thrombi and plaque from the aorta to the bilateral iliac arteries, and eccentric fibrous plaque in the proximal segment of the left SFA. However, no thrombi or plaques were observed from the right SFA to Pop.A. AT using a 5-Fr FC was not performed considering vessel rupture. To prevent plaque protrusions (PPs) and perform aggressive balloon dilation after stent implantation, we used covered stents (CSs). This case involved long-segment AIOD; the high trackability and flexibility of GORE® VIABAHN® endoprostheses (VSGs; W.L. Gore & Associates, Flagstaff, AZ, USA) helped accommodate the tortuous EIA anatomy, potentially lowering the risk of thrombus migration owing to gradual expansion. Sequential proximal post-dilatation of the endoprosthesis was performed, followed by final post-dilatation with kissing 7 × 60-mm Sterling balloons (Boston Scientific) (Fig. 3C). Self-expanding stents (Epic, 8 × 100-mm; Boston Scientific) were deployed in the bilateral CIA to EIA overlapping the endoprostheses, and post-dilatation was performed (7 × 60-mm Sterling balloon). Angioplasty was performed for the right SFA to the Pop.A (6 × 80-mm Ranger drug-coated balloon; Boston Scientific) and for the left SFA (7 × 80-mm Eluvia drug-eluting stent; Boston Scientific). Final angiography demonstrated full patency without residual stenosis or DE (Fig. 3D–F).

Total operation time was 4 h 38 min (3 h 20 min for EVT), involving 49 mL contrast medium. Vital signs after surgery were stable (blood pressure 115/68 mmHg, heart rate 94 bpm). The post-operative ABI was elevated (right: 0.93, left: 1.09), with decreased rest pain in the right leg and coldness and numbness in the left leg. Apixaban (5 mg/day) and clopidogrel (75 mg/day) were prescribed postoperatively. Despite subjective symptom improvement, oral intake significantly decreased. Oral nutritional supplements were provided, with continued rehabilitation. The patient presented with right upper abdominal pain on post-operative day (POD) 15. Plain CT revealed gallbladder distention, wall thickening, and pericholecystic fat stranding without stones. The patient was diagnosed with acalculous cholecystitis and started on ceftriaxone after blood culturing. Percutaneous transhepatic gallbladder drainage was performed on POD 16. The patient became hemodynamically unstable (body temperature 35.8 °C, blood pressure 78/51 mmHg, heart rate 116 bpm), and hypotension persisted despite adequate volume resuscitation. Norepinephrine infusion was initiated, and antibiotic treatment was escalated to piperacillin/tazobactam on POD 17. Blood and bile cultures were positive for Klebsiella aerogenes. The patient's condition deteriorated and she died on POD 26.

Discussion

Total occlusion of the infrarenal aorta has been reported in 3–8.5% of patients with AIOD [2], [3]. AIOD typically begins at the distal aorta or CIA and slowly progresses proximally and distally. Patients with underlying peripheral artery disease may present with acute limb ischemia due to in-situ thrombosis from plaque rupture or an embolus. This patient had atrial fibrillation, and the embolic event may have occurred in the aortoiliac occlusive lesions in addition to thrombus formation from atherosclerosis, abnormalities of hemorheology, and turbulence at bifurcations and stenotic regions. Primary patency rates at 5 and 10 years following aorto-bifemoral BS for LS were reported to be 87.5% and 81.8%, respectively; however, high mortality (3.3–4.6%) and complication rates (8.3–13.1%), long hospitalization periods, and delayed recovery have also been reported [4], [5]. The procedural success rates and long-term patency of EVT for AIOD were reported to be similar to those of BSs [6], [7]. Additionally, the effectiveness of CS was also recently demonstrated [8].

HIs are ideal for patients with AIOD with extension to the CFA or SFA, which allows real-time adaptation of the treatment strategy based on intraoperative findings. Thrombotic lesions are associated with 1-year restenosis after EVT for AIOD due to the high incidence of PPs after stent implantation and avoidance of aggressive balloon dilation before and after stenting to minimize the risk of DE [9]. HIs and thrombus aspiration devices (TADs) may reduce the incidence of thrombus protrusions and risk of DE by reducing thrombus burden; EVT using TADs may have been considered if CFA lesions were not present and TADs were available in Japan. Although covered endovascular reconstruction of the aortic bifurcation may have been feasible, deploying two iliac balloon-expandable CSs after the initial CS in the distal aorta may result in embolization to the first CS.

This study is limited in that we could not evaluate the long-term outcomes of HI because the patient died from septic shock within 1 month postsurgery. Further studies are required to confirm the long-term outcomes of HI, as it may be effective for selected patients with AIOD involving thrombotic lesions.
