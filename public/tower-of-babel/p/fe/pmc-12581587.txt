Activity of daily living impairment mediates the relationship between pain and falls in Chinese older adults: a cohort study
Authors: Xuping Li, RuiLin Liu, Kai Kang, Gang Tian, Yibin Hao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFalls impose a substantial disease burden on older adults, yet whether ADL impairment mediates the pain-falls pathway remains unclear. This study investigates whether ADL impairment acts as a mediator in the relationship between pain and incident falls among Chinese community-dwelling older adults.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) on respondents aged ≥ 60 years. Baseline and follow-up information was obtained through structured interviews. Logistic regression first quantified the pairwise associations among pain, ADL impairment, and falls. Subsequently, bootstrap-based mediation analysis was applied to test whether ADL impairment mediated the pain-falls relationship, restricting the model to variates that were significant in all three logistic regressions.ResultsA total of 6,280 participants was included in the analysis. Pain frequency (PF) (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.10 to 1.22) and number of pain sites (NPS) (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.13 to 1.31) were significantly associated with falls. PF (OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.49 to 1.63) and NPS (OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.83 to 2.11) were significantly associated with ADL impairment. ADL impairment was significantly associated with falls. Mediation analysis revealed that ADL impairment mediated the association between PF (mediation effect = 6.06e-03, proportion = 22.47%) and falls, and between NPS (mediation effect = 1.10e-02, proportion = 25.01%) and falls. Specifically, the mediating effect of impairments in dressing, bathing and toileting were significant.ConclusionOur study illuminates the mechanistic cascade from pain to falls via ADL impairment and offer concrete, modifiable targets for fall-prevention programs tailored to Chinese community-dwelling older adults. Coupling effective pain management with targeted support for dressing, bathing, and toileting is likely to curtail fall risk and enhance quality of life in older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06505-4.

Introduction

Definitions of “older adults” differ across countries, though most adopt either 60 or 65 years as the threshold [1, 2]. Falls are a leading cause of injury in older adults and impose significant economic costs worldwide [3–5]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines a fall as “inadvertently coming to rest on the ground, floor or other lower level, excluding intentional change in position to rest in furniture, wall or other objects” [6]. A meta-analysis shows that one in three adults aged 65 and older experiences a fall each year, with 10% resulting in serious injury or hospitalization [7]. A cross-sectional study reported the two-year fall incidence of 22.02% among adults aged 60 years and older in China [8]. In 2021, the global disability adjusted life years (DALYs) lost due to falls was 555.09 per 100,000 individuals. In China, the aging population has increased the burden of fall-related diseases, with DALYs reaching 583.47 per 100,000 in 2021 [9].

Pain is highly prevalent among older adults, with up to 50% of community-dwelling older adults experiencing pain [10]. Globally, the disease burden attributable to pain has been escalating annually [11, 12]. In 2021, the years lived with disability (YLDs) caused by musculoskeletal pain and headache were 2,051.33 and 607.95 per 100,000 individuals, respectively [9]. Despite its prevalence and impact, pain is often overlooked by older adults, who may regard it as an inevitable aspect of aging [13]. Pain has been identified as a potential risk factor for falls in older adults [14–16]. Previous studies have demonstrated that several factors, including physical performance (such as strength and poor balance) [14, 17, 18], pain medication use [17], depression [14], and inflammatory cytokines mediate the association between pain and falls [19]. However, previous studies on the mediating effects between pain and falls in older adults have often focused on abstract constructs like general physical or psychological aspects, or relied on laboratory-based indicators. These approaches are difficult to directly apply to community-based interventions.

Falls among the older adults mostly occur at home [20], indicating home-based fall prevention measures are of great significance. The activity of daily living (ADL) refers to activities oriented toward taking care of one’s own body, such as bathing, toileting, dressing, and eating. These activities are fundamental to living in a social world and enable basic survival and well-being [21]. ADL is particularly important for older adults and play a crucial role in “successful” aging. Previous studies have shown that ADL impairment is significantly associated with falls [22, 23]. Compared to more abstract concepts such as grip strength and balance, ADL impairment is easier to understand and detect in daily life. However, whether ADL impairment plays a mediating role in the relationship between pain and falls remains underexplored.

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is a nationally representative longitudinal survey covering 28 provinces, 150 districts, and 450 communities in mainland China [24]. It includes data from approximately 10,000 households of middle-aged and older adults. CHARLS collects detailed information on demographics, family structure, and health conditions through face-to-face interviews. Law of the People’s Republic of China on the Protection of the Rights and Interests of Older Persons defines the older adults as those aged 60 and above [2]. This study used CHARLS data to examine the associations between pain and falls and whether ADL impairment mediates these relationships among community-dwelling older adults in China.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a prospective cohort study using the 2018 survey as baseline and the 2020 survey as follow-up. Participants were included if they completed the 2018 survey. We excluded individuals who (1) lacked 2020 follow-up data; (2) were younger than 60 years at baseline; (3) resided in institutions (e.g., nursing homes or hospitals) at baseline; (4) had missing ADL data; (5) had missing fall data at baseline or follow-up; and (6) lacked information on physician-diagnosed conditions. After applying these criteria, 6,280 participants remained for analysis. The participant flow chart is presented in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of sample selection and the exclusion criteria

Falls

As the outcome variable, falls were ascertained from the 2020 follow-up survey. Participants were asked: “Since the last interview, have you experienced a fall?” Responses were recorded as either “yes” or “no.” For this study, participants were classified as having experienced a fall if they answered “yes” to the question.

Pain

Pain data was derived from the 2018 survey. Participants were initially asked: “How often do you suffer from physical pain?” with response options of “none,” “a little,” “somewhat,” “quite a bit,” or “very.” If participants reported experiencing pain (i.e., responded with “a little,” “somewhat,” “quite a bit,” or “very”), they were further asked to identify the specific body parts where they experienced pain. The options included: head, shoulders, arms, wrists, fingers, chest, stomach, upper back, lower back, buttocks, legs, knees, ankles, toes, neck, and other. Two pain variables were included in the analysis: pain frequency (PF), the number of pain sites (NPS). PF was defined based on participants’ initial responses regarding the frequency of pain. NPS was determined by the number of specific pain sites identified by participants.

ADL impairment

The data of ADL impairment was from the survey conducted in 2018. ADL impairment is measured according to Activity of Daily Living Scale, which involves six aspects: dressing, bathing, eating, rising, toileting, and housework. (19) During the interview, the respondents were asked, “Have you found it difficult to dress yourself?”, “Have you found it difficult to take a bath?”, “Have you encountered difficulties in eating by yourself, such as being unable to pick up the food yourself?”, “Did you have any difficulty getting out of bed?”, “Is there difficulty in using the toilet? This includes squatting and standing up.”, and “Is there difficulty in doing household chores due?”. The participants can response: Don’t have any difficulty; Have difficulty but can still do it; Have difficulty and need help; Can not do it. In order to achieve multivariate analysis as much as possible, we follow the previous researches, using dichotomous variables [25]. For each item, if the respondent selects any option other than “Don’t have any difficulty”, it is considered that the activity has impairment. If there is any difficulty with any of the six items, then it is considered that ADL impairment exists.

Covariates

Covariates were from the survey conducted in 2018. The selection of covariates is based on previous studies [13, 15, 19]. The list of covariates can be found in Supplementary material Table S1.

Demographic characteristics

We collected data on sex, age, residence, cohabitation, education, and work status at the 2018 interview. Age was calculated as 2018 minus year of birth, taken from the national ID card; if the respondent reported a different actual birth year, the latter was used. Residence was originally coded as urban, urban-rural fringe, rural, or other; for analytic power we combined the first three categories into “non-rural” versus “rural.” Marital status was first recorded as married, separated, divorced, widowed, or never married. Participants who were non-married were then asked whether they currently lived with a partner. We classified respondents as “with partner” (married or cohabiting) or “without partner” of cohabitation. Education was grouped into four levels: primary incomplete, primary, secondary, tertiary “Work” was defined as any activity (agricultural or non-agricultural, paid or unpaid) that produced goods or services contributing substantially to the respondent’s livelihood. Individuals engaged in any such activity were coded as “with work”; all others were coded as “without work.”

Fall history

Two questions were used to assess the history of falls. First of all, the question is “Have you fallen down?” Then came the question: “Have you fallen since our last interview?” Responses were recorded as either “yes” or “no.” If the answer to any of these questions is “yes”, the participant is considered to have a history of falls.

Behavioral information

Behavioral information included sleep duration, physical activity, and alcohol use. The sleep duration was evaluated based on the question “During the past month, how many hours of actual sleep did you get at night?”. Physical activity was assessed using the question “How many days a week do you engage in physical activity for at least 10 minutes?”. Physical activity was assessed using the question “Did you drink any alcoholic beverages, such as beer, wine, or liquor in the past year? How often?”. Participants could choose “Drink more than once a month”, “Drink but less than once a month”, or “None of these”.

Environmental information

Environmental information included handicapped facility and toilet type in house. Handicapped facility was assessed using the question “Are there any handicapped facilities (e.g., non-stair ramp)”. Participants could choose “Yes”, “No” or “No steps on level ground, no need for handicapped facilities”. In our analysis, we classified “No steps on level ground, no need for handicapped facilities” as “yes”. Toilet type was assessed using the question “What is the type of toilet? Is it with or without a seat? If the respondent has both, select ‘toilet with a seat’”.

Medical conditions

Medical conditions included poor vision, poor hearing and 14 self-reported physician-diagnosed diseases (hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), liver disease, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, digestive disease, mental illness, memory-related disease, arthritis, asthma). Vision and hearing impairment were ascertained with the single questions “Do you have a vision problem?” and “Do you have a hearing problem?”, respectively. Physician-diagnosed diseases were self-reported via the prompt “Has a doctor ever told you that you have [disease, e.g., hypertension]?”. Participants responded “yes” or “no.”

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed using mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are presented as percentages. According to the suggestions of the mediation analysis, we first adopt a three-step analysis to examine the relationships among the exposure, the mediator, and the outcome [26]. The steps prior to mediation analysis are shown in Fig. 2. The associations between pain and falls were initially analyzed. To account for potential confounding effects, a series of models were employed to adjust for covariates. Model 1 adjusted for demographic variables, fall history and ADL impairment. Model 2 added adjustments for environmental and behavioral variables. Model 3 further incorporated adjustments for medical conditions. Next, the associations of pain with ADL impairment were analyzed, adjusting for all covariates. Then, the associations of ADL impairment with falls were examined, adjusting for all covariates. Logistic regression was used throughout these analyses. To examine the mediating role of ADL impairment, the Bootstrap method was employed. Only those results that corresponded to a positive outcome from the aforementioned three-step analysis were included in the mediation analysis. To further investigate which specific ADL impairments mediated the association between pain and falls, we repeated the aforementioned analyses using six specified ADL impairment as the potential mediating variables. To assess robustness of our findings, we conducted two sensitivity analyses. First, adding the 1,216 participants who lacked data on medical conditions, we assigned these missing values a value of 0 (without diagnosed diseases) using extreme-case imputation. Second, we restricted the analytic sample to the 4,400 participants aged ≥ 65 years by excluding the 1,880 individuals aged 60–64. In sensitivity analyses, we adjusted for the same set of covariates as in Model 3 of the primary analysis. A two-tailed P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed in RStudio (version 2024.04.2). Figures were created using WPS Office (version 12.1.0.20784).

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

Among 6,280 participants (mean age 69.21 ± 6.94 years), the majority were aged 60–69 years (n = 3,757, 59.82%). Men accounted for 43.57% (n = 2,736), and 74.44% (n = 4,675) lived in rural areas. Cohabitation with a partner was reported by 75.57% (n = 4,764). Over half had not completed primary education (n = 3,505, 55.81%), and 49.70% (n = 3,121) were with work. Regarding pain, 31.64% reported none, whereas 42.55% (n = 2,672) experienced pain in 1–5 body sites. At least one impairment of activity of daily living was present in 33.60% (n = 2,110), with household chores most commonly affected (n = 1,288, 20.51%). Full details are provided in Table 1.

Characteristics of participants

Falls by characteristic groups

Of the 6,280 participants, 21.72% (n = 1,364) had experienced at least one fall since the interview in 2018. Fall percentages by PF category were 15.80% (“none”), 18.25% (“a little”), 26.67% (“somewhat”), and 30.69% (“quite a bit”). Across NPS ranges, falls were reported by 20.17% (1–5 sites), 30.21% (6–10 sites), and 34.56% (11–16 sites). Among participants with any ADL impairment, the fall percentage was 31.75%. Specific proportions were 39.36% for dressing, 37.81% for bathing, 41.53% for eating, 39.46% for rising, 36.68% for toileting, and 32.45% for housework. Detailed results of falls by characteristic groups are presented in Table 1.

Association of PF and NPS with falls

Including ADL impairment as a covariate, both PF (OR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.11 to 1.22) and NPS (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.15 to 1.32) were significantly associated with higher odds of falls in Model 1. These associations remained significant after adjusting for other covariates in the fully adjusted Model 3. Specifically, each additional unit in PF was associated with 16% higher odds of falls (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.10 to 1.22). Similarly, each additional unit in NPS was associated with 22% higher odds of falls (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.13 to 1.31). When six specific ADL impairments were adjusted, the association of PF and NPS with falls are also significant. Detailed results of the correlation analysis of PF and NPS with falls are shown in Fig. 3. Across both sensitivity analyses, the associations of PF and NPS with falls were consistent with those in the primary analysis. Detailed results of the correlation analysis of PF and NPS with falls in sensitivity analysis are shown in Supplementary materials Table S2.

Association of PF and NPS with ADL impairment

After adjusting for all covariates, both PF and the NPS showed significant positive correlations with ADL impairment. Each additional unit in PF (OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.49 to 1.63) and NPS (OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.83 to 2.11) were associated with 55.70% and 96.24% higher odds of ADL impairment, respectively. When the six specific ADL impairments were included in the analysis, both PF and the NPS showed significant positive correlations with all six specific ADL impairments, with ORs ranging from 1.31 to 1.52 for PF and from 1.62 to 1.91 for NPS. Across both sensitivity analyses, the associations of PF and NPS with ADL impairment were consistent with those in the primary analysis. Association of ADL impairment with falls

After full covariate adjustment, including either PF or NPS, ADL impairment independently associated with falls. Specifically, the odds of falls were 61.57% higher (OR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.40 to 1.86) when PF was controlled as the pain variable, and 63.29% higher (OR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.42 to 1.88) when NPS was controlled as the pain variable. When using six specific ADL impairments as exposures, the impairment in dressing (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.02 to 1.64), bathing (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.00 to 1.58) and toileting (OR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.22 to 1.75) was significantly associated with falls after adjustment for PF and covariates. The same three activities, dressing (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.02 to 1.58), bathing (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.00 to 1.58) and toileting (OR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.22 to 1.75) also remained significant after adjustment for NPS and covariates. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary material Table S5. In sensitivity analyses one, the associations between ADL impairment and falls were consistent with those in primary analysis. In sensitivity analysis two, the associations between ADL impairment and falls remained significant. In item-level analyses, however, only the impairment in toileting retained significance after adjustment for PF and covariates (OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.08 to 1.64). When NPS was substituted for PF, the impairment in both dressing (OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.01 to 1.72) and toileting (OR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.09 to 1.65) were significantly associated with falls. Full sensitivity-analysis results are presented in Supplementary material Table S6.

For the association of PF and NPS with falls, we included ADL impairment and the specific impairments in dressing, bathing and toileting in the mediation analysis. Using PF as exposure, IAD impairment explained 22.47% of the total effect (mediation effect = 6.06e-03); the mediation proportions for the impairment in dressing, bathing and toileting were 9.56%, 9.95% and 13.55%, respectively. For NPS, ADL impairment accounted for 25.01% (mediation effect = 1.10e-02); the proportions of the mediating effect for the impairment in dressing, bathing and toileting were 11.71%, 11.45% and 15.93%. Discussion

The main aim of the present study was to examine whether ADL impairment mediates the associations of pian with falls. Our findings showed that PF and NPS were significantly associated with falls. Additionally, PF and NPS were significantly associated with ADL impairment, which in turn was significantly associated with falls. Mediation analysis revealed that ADL impairment significantly mediated the associations of PF and NPS with falls. The specific impairments in dressing and toileting mediated the association of PF and NPS with falls.

Previous studies have established a link between pain and falls in older adults [15, 27–30]. For example, the National Social Life, Health, and Aging Project data showed that pain at two sites and at ≥ 3 sites raised fall risk by 72% and 104%, respectively [15]. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported multisite pain predicts future falls risk (OR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.57 to 1.93) [26]. Our study has revealed the significant associations of PF and NPS with the occurrence of falls. This is consistent with the previous findings. These evidences underscore the necessity of systematic pain prevention and management as an important component of fall-prevention strategies in later life.

Pain can lead to decreased muscle strength, and poor balance [14, 31, 32]. These changes in physical function directly impact the ability of older adults to perform ADL. A recent meta-analysis demonstrated that community-dwelling older adults with pain exhibited significantly poorer balance compared to those without pain (SMD= −0.35, 95% CI: −0.43 to −0.27) [32]. Our study has revealed that both PF and NPS are significantly correlated with ADL impairment and the six specific ADL impairments. These findings to some extent confirm the association between pain and the decline of physical functions. This indicates the significant importance of pain prevention and management in enhancing the ability to carry out daily activities.

ADL impairment is a risk factor of falls in older adults. A cohort study using CHARLS and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing found that, among adults ≥ 50 years, ADL impairment increased fall risk to 1.66 (95% CI: 1.49 to 1.85) and 2.32 (95% CI: 2.01 to 2.68), respectively, relative to those without ADL impairment [23]. In a cross-sectional study of adults aged ≥ 70 years, severe ADL impairment was associated with a markedly elevated fall risk (OR = 4.30, 95% CI: 3.29 to 6.51) relative to those without ADL impairment [33]. Despite differences in age range and assessment methods, our results align with these findings, showing a robust association between ADL impairment and falls. Moreover, item-level analyses revealed that the impairment in dressing, bathing, and toileting were significantly associated with falls. Impairment in these activities can diminish autonomy and well-being. Among older adults, impairment in dressing, bathing, or toileting necessitates assistance to reduce fall risk.

Previous research on the mediating role of pain and falls in older adults has largely concentrated on physical indicators such as grip strength and balance, or on psychological markers like depression. For example, a Singaporean community study found that grip strength and depression explained 1.96% and 5.67%, respectively, of the association between pain and recurrent falls [14]. Falls risk follows a dynamic, downward spiral: pain curtails activity, and this reduction accelerates functional decline and increases the likelihood of falling [34]. Among the factors in this cycle, ADL impairment is readily identified and amenable to targeted intervention. Identifying ADL impairments and providing the necessary assistance can be part of a comprehensive strategy for preventing falls among the older adults.

Our study has several limitations. First, the measurement of the variables was conducted through retrospective self-reporting, introducing possible recall bias. Second, the CHARLS questionnaire lacks information on the cause of pain, pain duration (acute vs. chronic) and intensity. Therefore, we did not differentiate the cause of pain, pain subtypes or intensity in our analyses. Third, we did not collect information on pain medications or ADL assistance, and this omission may bias our findings. Fourth, we assessed only basic ADL, while using the instrumental ADL scale might have detected subtler functional changes linked to falls [35]. Additionally, the sample is representative of the Chinese population, and the analyzed participants were predominantly rural residents with relatively low educational attainment; therefore, generalizability to other contexts must be approached cautiously. Notwithstanding these constraints, our findings clarify the mediating role of ADL impairment in the pain on falls pathway and provide actionable, detailed guidance for future research and targeted fall-prevention interventions. Future studies could employ more comprehensive pain assessments, document the use of pain medications and ADL assistance, and use the instrumental ADL scale to conduct a more detailed exploration of the effect of ADL impairment in the association between pain and falls.

Conclusion

We systematically mapped the interrelations among pain, ADL impairment, and falls, then tested them within a unified mediation framework. ADL impairment emerged as a robust mediator of the effects that both PF and NPS exert on falls. Specifically, impairments in dressing, bathing, and toileting as the functional pathways underpinning this mediation. These results illuminate the mechanistic cascade from pain to falls via ADL limitation and offer concrete, modifiable targets for fall-prevention programs tailored to Chinese community-dwelling older adults. Coupling effective pain management with targeted support for dressing, bathing, and toileting is likely to curtail fall risk and enhance quality of life.
