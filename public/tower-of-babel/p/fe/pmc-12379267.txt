Sparse regularization inversion method for transient electromagnetic data and high-resolution prospection of subsurface targets
Authors: ZhiYong Zhou, Ke He, ShiHai Kang, JiaLiu Zhang, FuFeng Chi, Jie Ren, Wei Zhang, Chen Ning
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransient Electromagnetic Method (TEM) is a geophysical technique with great potential for high-resolution subsurface imaging. However, conventional inversion algorithms for TEM typically employ the L2-norm regularization in the objective function, which assumes a smoothly varying resistivity distribution. This assumption limits the resolution of sharp electrical interfaces. To address this issue, this paper implements an inversion algorithm that incorporates an L1-norm regularization term, promoting sparsity in the spatial gradient of the resistivity model and thereby enabling more accurate reconstruction of abrupt electrical boundaries under sufficient iteration. To overcome the numerical difficulties caused by the non-differentiability of the L1 norm at zero points, we adopt the Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) method within a Gauss-Newton (GN) inversion framework. In this approach, the L1 regularization problem is transformed into a sequence of weighted L2 regularization subproblems, each of which is solved using the GN method. This strategy retains the sparsity-promoting property of the L1 norm while effectively circumventing its computational challenges. Inversion results from both 1D and 2D synthetic models demonstrate that the proposed algorithm significantly improves the resolution of sharp resistivity transitions compared to traditional L2-norm regularization approaches. Furthermore, the method has been successfully applied to field TEM data from a real survey site, where it effectively identified underground coal mine voids and fault structures.Keywords: L1-norm, Iteratively reweighted least squares, Sparse regularization inversion, Transient electromagnetic methodSubject terms: Solid Earth sciences, Geophysics

Introduction

TEM is a geophysical exploration technique that is sensitive to good conductors1. Since the 1930s, TEM has made significant advancements in both methodological theory and application techniques, achieving notable success in exploration practices2,3. TEM is widely applied in various fields such as mine safety surveys4, mineral resource exploration5,6, groundwater exploration7,8, and hydrocarbon resource exploration9. The TEM observation systems can be categorized into two main types: loop sources and grounded wire sources. These systems are applicable to multiple exploration scenarios, including surface, marine, airborne, semi-airborne, borehole, and tunnel settings. Among these, the surface loop source method is relatively mature in terms of data processing and forward/inversion modeling techniques. However, 3D inversion is affected by several challenges, including the complexity of 3D forward modeling theory, computational efficiency, numerical accuracy, and the large volume of field measurement data10–12, leading to high computational costs that are not conducive to rapid qualitative interpretation in field surveys13,14. Therefore, 1D inversion remains the primary method for processing and interpreting real field data15–18. In conventional transient electromagnetic inversion, the regularization term in the objective function is typically the L2 norm. The assumption of global smoothness and flatness inherent in the L2 norm contradicts the need to highlight local electrical interfaces. As a result, the constraint imposed by the regularization term often distorts and blurs the final electrical structure19. By adopting the L1 norm as the regularization term, a sparse solution is more easily obtained in the model space gradient vector. The iterative process allows for abrupt changes in electrical interfaces. This approach has been applied by researchers to seismic, gravity, and magnetotelluric data to enhance the inversion’s ability to identify sharp electrical discontinuities20–22. In 1961, Lawason introduced the Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) method while studying nonlinear problems, aiming to transform nonlinear problems into linear form23,24. Later, Ba et al. discussed the convergence and stability of IRLS in electromagnetic signal processing25.However, the non-differentiability of the L1 norm at zero introduces numerical challenges in solving the optimization problem. Rewriting the L1 regularization using the iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) method effectively overcomes this difficulty23. Ruan et al. pointed out that the core advantage of IRLS lies in its ability to transform the complex L1 regularization problem into a sequence of solvable L2-norm subproblems through an iterative weighting mechanism19. This approach maintains computational efficiency while significantly improving solution sparsity and boundary resolution, making it particularly suitable for resolving sharp interface structures in geophysical inversion. The adaptive weighting adjustment mechanism not only enhances the algorithm’s robustness to noisy data but also achieves selective enhancement of true signal features by progressively focusing on key parameters. Compared to traditional inversion methods, IRLS demonstrates superior layer resolution and boundary delineation accuracy for anomalies in TEM data used for detecting mined-out areas. Additionally, it avoids the common convergence difficulties associated with direct L1 optimization, providing an effective solution for high-precision inversion under complex geological conditions.

Based on the above analysis, this study employs L1 regularization to construct the objective function for loop-source transient electromagnetic inversion and introduces an IRLS strategy within a Gauss-Newton iterative framework for approximate solution. The iterative stability of this approach is superior to directly minimizing approximations of the L1 norm or using subgradient search methods for optimization. Comparative inversion experiments using 1D and 2D models demonstrate that the proposed algorithm significantly outperforms traditional inversion methods in terms of layer interface identification accuracy. Furthermore, applying the method to field transient electromagnetic data from a specific site successfully identified underground coal mine goafs and fault zones. The reliability of the inversion results has been confirmed by borehole data, fully demonstrating the practical value of the method in engineering applications.

A sparse solution typically refers to a type of solution sought in fields such as mathematics, engineering, and computer science, especially in solving optimization problems. The characteristic of such a solution is that the majority of its elements are zero, with only a few being non-zero. In other words, if most of the elements in a vector or matrix are zero, the vector or matrix is considered to be sparse26.

The core reason why L1 regularization can produce sparse solutions lies in its unique mathematical properties. The diamond-shaped geometry of the L1 norm makes the optimization process more likely to generate solutions along the coordinate axes (i.e., some parameters are exactly zero), and the constant gradient of the L1 term for small values continuously drives these parameters toward zero.Lin and Herrmann’s research further demonstrates that L1-regularized sparse inversion is robust27. In contrast, the circular contours of L2 regularization tend to produce dense, non-zero solutions. From a probabilistic perspective, L1 regularization is equivalent to imposing a Laplace prior on the parameters, which has a peak at zero and inherently favors sparsity. This characteristic makes L2 regularization perform well in feature selection and overfitting prevention, especially suitable for model optimization under high-dimensional data. Examining the descent efficiency of the objective function, L2 regularization tends to decrease very rapidly in early iterations but slows down as it approaches the minimum. In contrast, L1 regularization maintains relatively consistent descent efficiency outside the vicinity of zero, but it cannot determine a descent direction within the neighborhood of the zero point. This is precisely the main difficulty in solving L1 regularization inversion problems.

IRLS theory

The sparsity characteristic of the L1 regularization term helps preserve underground electrical interfaces during inversion, which is beneficial for characterizing geological features such as faults and structural boundaries from the perspective of the expected inversion model. However, the non-differentiability at zero poses a limitation on the application of this technique in geophysical inversion. To address this, transformations of the L1 regularization problem are necessary to facilitate its solution.Wipf and Nagarajan’s research shows that sparse solutions can still be obtained by reformulating the L1 regularization using the IRLS method28. Gholami and Aghamiry conducted studies on the robustness of IRLS-based inversion algorithms using seismic data29.

Simulation experiment

1D model

This paper conducts inversion calculations using the proposed algorithm and the conventional algorithm under different resistivity configurations and layer thickness conditions, for three types of horizontally layered geological models, and comparative analysis of the results is carried out. The three-layer model has layer resistivities from the surface downward of 200 Ω·m, 10 Ω·m and 200 Ω·m, with the first two layers having thicknesses of 100 m and 50 m, respectively, and the last layer being infinitely thick. The four-layer model has resistivities of 50 Ω·m, 100 Ω·m, 10 Ω·m and 200 Ω·m, with thicknesses of 50 m, 50 m and 50 m, respectively. The five-layer model is a low-resistivity thin-layer model, with resistivities of 200 Ω·m, 10 Ω·m, 200 Ω·m, 10 Ω·m and 200 Ω·m, and thicknesses of 50 m, 10 m, 80 m and 50 m, respectively.

The radius of the transmitting coil is 200 m, the current is 1 A, and the receiving point is located at the center of the coil. Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 5% of the data value at each time channel was added to the forward response data of the models. The initial inversion model is a 40-layer uniform half-space model, with a resistivity of 50 Ω·m per layer and a thickness of 10 m. The initial regularization factor is set to 1000, and updated using a cooling strategy based on the RMS change rate. When the RMS reduction in continuous iterations is less than 5%, the regularization factor is halved, but it must not fall below the minimum value of 10. The inversion termination criteria are a maximum of 50 iterations or an RMS error less than 1.

Figure 2 shows a comparison between the inversion results and the true models. By comparing the inversion results of the three-layer and four-layer models, it can be seen that the L1 regularization inversion highlights the shape of the preset anomalies, with electrical boundaries closely approximating the true model. In contrast, the L2 regularization inversion results in blurred electrical boundaries due to the global smoothness constraint imposed by the regularization term in the objective function.From the inversion results of the five-layer model, it is evident that both algorithms can identify the low-resistivity thin layer structure. However, the L1 regularization inversion provides better resolution of electrical boundaries. This indicates that when sharp electrical discontinuities exist underground, the use of L1 regularization inversion techniques may yield more accurate and high-resolution results.

2D model

To evaluate the performance of the proposed 1D inversion algorithm on high-dimensional models, the forward response of a 2D model is computed using the 3D forward modeling technique proposed by Zhou (2025) et al.30,31. This paper employs a loop source with a side length of 400 m for background field calculation. The loop source moves along the survey line, with the observation point located at its geometric center. For numerical computation, the model is discretized using a structured hexahedral grid of 60 × 60 × 60 cells. The central computational domain consists of a 30 × 30 × 30 grid, with cell sizes of 30 m in the x and y directions and 20 m in the z direction. Outside the core region, 15 layers of grid cells are extended in both the lateral and longitudinal directions. The cell sizes in these extended regions increase progressively by a factor of 1.5 in the direction away from the center, ensuring a smooth transition from the finely resolved core to the outer boundaries. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the model exhibits geometric variations in one direction and is considered infinitely extended in the other directions. Therefore, only a representative cross-sectional plane is selected for analysis. The synthetic data were contaminated with Gaussian noise at three different levels 1%, 5%, and 10% added to each time channel.

Administratively, it falls under the jurisdiction of Zhangcun Town and Chencun Township in Mianchi County, Sanmenxia City, Henan Province.

After coal seams are mined out, a certain scale of air-filled space is formed in the short term, causing the electrical properties of the strata in the mined-out area to differ from those of the surrounding rock. Over time, the overlying rock layers above the mined-out area collapse and deform under gravity, leading to fracturing and cracking. This allows groundwater to flow along the fractured rock layers and cracks into the mined-out area, dissolving large amounts of electrolytes. Under hydrolysis, calcium, iron ions, and other elements in the rock layers exist in a free state. Therefore, water-filled mined-out areas exhibit low-resistivity characteristics. Due to the presence of collapse, fractures, and separation zones, the surrounding rock exhibits high resistivity features; the greater the deformation, the higher the resistivity. When the fracture zone is not filled with water, it shows high-resistivity characteristics; when filled with water, it presents low-resistivity characteristics. Based on this, the boundary and extent of the water-filled mined-out area can be determined.

In summary, the existence of a mined-out coal seam creates voids between the overlying and underlying rock layers, disrupting the integrity and continuity of the rock. As a result, the resistivity in these areas is significantly higher than that of the surrounding intact rock, exhibiting clear localized high-resistivity characteristics. When the voids in the mined-out area are filled with water, the resistivity shows low-resistivity characteristics. These properties provide a good geophysical basis for using transient electromagnetic methods to detect underground mined-out areas.

Conclusion

This paper validates the effectiveness and advantages of the designed inversion algorithm in terms of accuracy and stability through theoretical analysis, model tests, and comparison of inversion results with open-source programs on field data. The method is based on the Gauss-Newton framework and incorporates the Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) strategy, enabling high-quality imaging of complex subsurface electrical structures. The core design concept and advantages are mainly reflected in the following aspects:

During the inversion process, the objective function is composed of the approximate Hessian matrix of the data fitting term and the exact Hessian matrix of the regularization term. This allows for automatic adjustment of weights, facilitating the development of an inversion strategy that is both robust and focused. Compared to traditional L2 regularization, L1 regularization can obtain sparse solutions in the model gradient space. During the minimization of the objective function, it tends to extract the “essential features” of the model, allowing for abrupt changes in resistivity. This enables focused imaging of the boundaries of anomalous bodies.The IRLS method transforms the originally challenging L1 norm problem into a series of weighted L2 subproblems for iterative solving. This approach avoids the instability issues caused by non-differentiable points in traditional methods, significantly improving the stability and convergence of the inversion process.Whether inverting one-dimensional or two-dimensional theoretical models, or processing field-measured data with complex noise components, this method can clearly recover the boundary positions and morphological features of anomalous bodies, accurately characterizing the subsurface electrical structure. This demonstrates strong practicality and generalization ability.

In summary, the inversion method proposed in this paper demonstrates satisfactory performance in both theory and practice, providing a reliable technical basis for the detailed interpretation of transient electromagnetic data.
