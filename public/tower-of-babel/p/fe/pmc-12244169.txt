Perceptions of psychedelic-assisted therapy among Black Americans☆
Authors: Sierra Carter, Grace Packard, Callan Coghlan, Jamilah R George, Ashanti J Brown, Terence HW Ching, Jacob Julian, Jessica L Maples-Keller
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study investigated differences in perceptions of psychedelic-assisted therapy between Black and White Americans, as well as factors that may influence these perceptions. A final sample of 294 adults (42% female, 44% Black/African American or Mixed Race (of Black/African ancestry), 56% White American; Mage = 36.3 years) completed an online survey which assessed baseline knowledge and views of psychedelic-assisted therapy. Participants were then provided brief psychoeducation related to MDMA and psilocybin-assisted therapy. After psychoeducation, participants were queried on their perceptions of psychedelic-assisted therapy and factors potentially influencing these perceptions, including trauma history, current depressive and PTSD symptoms, perceived stress, and perceived barriers to psychological treatments. Psychoeducation had a positive impact on both level of interest and positivity of views of psychedelic-assisted therapy across groups. Black American participants reported more positive views of psychedelic-assisted therapy than White participants following psychoeducation. Greater depression and PTSD symptom severity was associated with greater baseline interest in Black and White Americans and there was significant interactions in predicting baseline view and interest, such that Black participants who reported greater depression symptom severity were more interested and had more positive views of PAT. Despite historical exclusion from psychedelic clinical trials and experiences of unethical treatment in research, Black Americans demonstrate more positive views of psychedelic therapy, and Black Americans more in need of novel mental health care demonstrate more interest and more positive views. Our findings demonstrate that the onus for diversification of psychedelic research samples is on research groups. These findings also provide an impetus for the psychedelic research community to rebuild trust in psychedelic research among Black Americans, conduct outreach, and provide culturally attuned psychedelic-assisted interventions that are accessible to Black Americans.Keywords: Psychedelic therapy, Black Americans, Perceived barriers, PTSD

The field of psychiatry is currently undergoing a “psychedelic renaissance” in which research suggests the safety and efficacy of psychedelic-assisted therapy (PAT; e.g., [29]). MDMA is a phenethylamine derivative associated with subjective effects including experiences of euphoria, increased feelings of closeness and love for others, and better access to emotionally intense material [5]. Psilocybin is a psychoactive alkaloid associated with alterations in consciousness which can include enhanced introspection, perceptual changes, and alterations in feelings of self and time [26]. MDMA assisted therapy is associated with reduction in PTSD symptoms and disability [23] as well as alcohol consumption and hazardous use [25]. Psilocybin assisted therapy is associated with reductions in depressive and anxiety symptoms and increases in mental wellbeing and flourishing in those with depression [8]. Notably, treatment gains appear to be maintained across long-term follow up in both MDMA and psilocybin interventions ([9], [19], respectively). For the present study, we focus on these two psychedelic substances as they both have significant bodies of research; there are currently 74 trials registered on clinicaltrials.gov that involve psilocybin and 97 that involve MDMA. It is paramount that these promising interventions are provided equitably to diverse communities and through models informed by cultural humility.

Racial and ethnically minoritized groups have been significantly underrepresented in psychedelic clinical trials, despite experiencing disproportionate risk for PTSD and depression in part due to experiences of racial discrimination and racial trauma [10], [16], [30]. Across 18 studies (N = 282), 82.3% of participants in psychedelic trials have been non-Hispanic White, with African Americans, Latinos, and Asians representing only 2.5%, 2.1%, and 1.8% of participants, respectively [22]. This is not specific to psychedelic research; one analysis found clinical trial samples continue to be disproportionately comprised of non-Hispanic White participants [20]. Additionally, Black Americans report the lowest recreational use of psychedelics compared to other racial/ethnic groups [40]. Research suggests Black Americans may also demonstrate a later age for initiation of MDMA use [28]. A recent study of ethnoracial differences with a pooled sample found overall equivalence in efficacy of MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD, with large effect size in PTSD symptom reduction across non-Hispanic Whites and participants of color [11]. These are potentially promising interventions with initial evidence indicating comparable efficacy in racial and ethnic minoritized populations. Research is needed to investigate perceptions of psychedelic therapy in diverse groups to inform outreach, reduce barriers, and inform culturally humble psychedelic treatment models.

A limited number of studies have explored perceptions of psychedelics, and there are no published studies exploring Black American’s perceptions of this treatment. A study on college student perceptions of hallucinogens found that most were reluctant to use them therapeutically; however, 83% of the sample believed that research regarding medicinal benefits should be conducted and 63% either agreed or strongly agreed that hallucinogenic research should receive federal funding [38]. While Black Americans were included in this sample, results did not report on racial differences in views. In a survey of resident fellows and attending psychiatrists, participants largely perceived classic hallucinogens to be hazardous for recreational use, however, almost 80% of the sample reported optimism regarding use for psychiatric treatment [3].

Subjective experience of psychedelics such as psilocybin and MDMA can involve significant alterations to consciousness (e.g., [35]) so trust is crucial to effective PAT. Historical and contemporary systemic racism against Black Americans, including medical injusices committed against communities of color (e.g., the Tuskegee Study of untreated Syphilis in Black men), racial disparities in psychiatric treatment, and ongoing inequities in the U.S. criminal justice system, particularly with regard to drug-related offenses [34], [37], may result in specific and valid mistrust or barriers related to psychedelic treatment. Notably, people of color (e.g., almost entirely Black prison samples) and other vulnerable populations (e.g., hospital patients with psychotic disorders) were exploited in early phases of psychedelic research between 1950 and 1980; 77% of these early studies would not have passed ethical review by current standards [32]. It is critical that psychedelic researchers consider how to improve treatment access to Black populations, including identifying and addressing unique barriers and determining effective culturally-informed outreach and treatment techniques. Indeed, perceptions of mental health treatment can be an important factor in help-seeking. For example, individuals with depression who believe in the helpfulness of antidepressants are significantly more likely to report engaging in this treatment over the next six months (Jorm et al., 2000). It is imperative to investigate perceptions of PAT in the Black community.

For the present study, we conducted an online survey (N = 294, average age = 35.98) to investigate differences in perceptions of PAT across Black and White Americans. This included assessments of initial knowledge and views of psychedelic therapy, participants’ views regarding psychedelic therapy after reading an overview describing the research and methods used within psychedelic therapy, as well as queries regarding participants’ views of barriers or benefits to psychedelic therapy. We assessed several variables of interest to also investigate predictors of views and interest in PAT. MDMA-assisted therapy has been most widely researched for PTSD treatment (e.g., Mitchell et al., 2021) and psilocybin-assisted therapy has been most widely researched for depression treatment (e.g., [8]). Thus, we included measures of perceived stress, depression, and PTSD in all participants in order to see how clinical presentations most likely to eventually be eligible for these treatments would impact perceptions. Given that Black Americans have experienced less exposure to psychedelics via clinical trials [22] and recreational use [18], we hypothesized that Black Americans would report less interest and less positive views of PAT than White Americans.

Methods

Participants and procedure

314 adults were initially recruited through Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (MTurk) website. From the initial sample of 314, 24 were removed after failing validity checks (e.g. “This is a quality control measure, please select strongly disagree”). The final sample included 294 adults (42% female; 44% Black American, 56% White American; Mage = 36.3, SD = 9.6). Racial identity was assessed via categorical response options. For example, participants could select “Mixed race” and select from multiple response options. Participants were included in the Black American group if they selected “Black or African American” or “Mixed Race” in addition to “Black or African American.” Approval from the institutional review board was obtained. Participants provided informed consent and were compensated $10 upon completion. Demographic information is presented in Table 1.

Materials

Beck depression inventory – 2nd edition (BDI-II; [4])

The BDI-II is a 21-item self-report instrument used to assess presence and severity of symptoms of depression (DSM-IV; [1]). The measure has exhibited reliability, convergent validity [2] and criterion validity (Groethe et al., 2005). Participants rated their feelings over the past two weeks on a number of topics which were then scored from 0 (e.g., “I do not feel sad”) to 3 (e.g., “I am so sad or unhappy that I cannot stand it”). In our sample, the scale demonstrated excellent reliability (ɑ =.94).

Perceived stress scale (PSS; Cohen, 1983)

The PSS is a 10-item self-report scale used to assess perception of stress over the past month. Participants are asked to rate how often they’ve had certain feelings (e.g. “how often have you felt nervous and ‘stressed’”?) over the past month from 0 (e.g. “Never”) to 4 (e.g. “Very often”). This measure demonstrates appropriate reliability and expected associations with life-events, depression symptomatology, and utilization of health services (Cohen et al., 1983). In our sample, the scale demonstrated good reliability (ɑ =.80).

Posttraumatic stress disorder checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; [7])

The PCL-5 is a 20-item self-report screening instrument that screens for PTSD symptoms. Participants are asked to respond to how much they have been bothered by listed symptoms (e.g. repeated, disturbing, and unwanted memories of the stressful experience) from 0 (e.g. not at all) to 4 (e.g. “Extremely”) in the past month. This measure demonstrates good internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and convergent validity with clinician-administered PTSD measures [7]. In our sample, the scale demonstrated excellent reliability (ɑ =.98).

Perceived barriers to psychological treatment scale (PBPT; [24])

The PBPT is a self-report measure of perceived barriers to engaging in psychological treatment which involves 25-items used to assess perceived difficulty from 1 (“Not difficult at all”) to 5 (“Impossible”) of overcoming potential obstacles (e.g. “Problems with transportation, cost of therapy”) in order to receive psychological treatment. This measure has demonstrated good internal consistency and concurrent and predictive validity [24]. In our sample, the scale demonstrated excellent reliability (ɑ =.97).

Psychedelic perceptions survey (PPS)

The Psychedelic Perceptions Survey is a self-report measure used to assess participant perception of PAT; it has been previously used in a published study of perceptions of PAT [15]. Previous research suggests that brief psychoeducation materials on psychotherapy can be used to assess perceptions and barriers to treatment engagement [6]. Previous research has also found that single item questions assessing attitudes towards mental health seeking broadly, with 5-item Likert scale responses, were significantly associated with actual mental health care use [17]. Measure items include questions related to perceptions of PAT using 1–5 Likert scale responses; response options for all items are provided below. First, participants were queried regarding their views of PAT prior to being provided any information (i.e., psychoeducation). Categories included Familiarity (Likert scale responses included “Extremely familiar”, “Very familiar”, “Moderately familiar”, “Slightly familiar”, and “Not familiar at all”), View (Likert scale responses included “Very positive”, “Positive”, “Neutral”, “Negative”, and “Very negative”), and Level of interest (Likert scale responses included “Very interested”, “Moderately interested”, “Somewhat interested”, “A little interested”, and “Not at all interested”). Subsequently, participants were provided with brief, four paragraph psychoeducation regarding MDMA and psilocybin-assisted therapy. The content included an overview of the research that has been conducted, history of use and legality of use in clinical trials context, length of subjective effects and range of subjective effects experienced with both medications, and potential side effects. After reading this information, participants were again queried regarding their view and level of interest in PAT.

Data analytical plan

Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic characteristics. Correlations and descriptive statistics for all study measures in both groups were calculated. Analyses comparing descriptives across groups were conducted. MANOVA analyses were used to compare perceived barriers to mental health treatment, PTSD symptoms, depression symptoms, perceived stress, and familiarity with PAT before psychoeducation across groups. Two mixed ANOVAs were used to examine differences in the effect of psychoeducation on view of PAT and interest in psychedelic therapy between Black and White participants including PTSD and depression as covariates, given the differences across groups in these symptom measures.

We also examined salient predictors of interest in and views of PAT using simple linear regressions, where race and mean-centered variables significantly correlated with pre-psychoeducation interest (i.e., age, LGBT identity, income, barriers, stress, PTSD symptoms, and depressive symptoms), and interaction variables were entered into the regression. Finally, a hierarchical linear regressions was used to gain insight on factors that predicted the efficacy of psychoeducation in increasing interest in PAT. View was not examined because no variables were significantly correlated with post-psychoeducation view. Variables that were significantly correlated with post-psychoeducation interest scores were included as predictors in the analysis. Pre-psychoeducation interest was entered as the only predictor for post-psychoeducation interest in the initial model, and significant variables identified in the correlation tables were then added in the second model.

Effect of psychoeducation on interest and views of PAT across groups

Participants were more interested in PAT after psychoeducation compared to before psychoeducation, F(1290)= 25.373, p < .001 (See Fig. 1). There was no interaction effect as the main effect of psychoeducation on interest did not vary by racial group, F(1290)= .176, p = .675. However, there was no main effect of race (F(1290)= 2.065, p = .152), nor was there an interaction between the effect of psychoeducation and PTSD (F(1290)= 3.379, p = .067) or depression (F(1290)= .418, p = .518).

Interest in PAT before and after psychoeducation.

Participants had more positive views of PAT after psychoeducation than compared to before psychoeducation, F= 28.209, p < .001 (Fig. 2). However, this main effect of psychoeducation varied by racial group (i.e., interaction effect), F= 4.568, p = .033. Black participants had more positive views of psychedelic assisted therapy than White participants after psychoeducation (mean difference =.294, p = .013), but not before psychoeducation (mean difference =.030, p = .811). Black participant’s views of PAT significantly increased from psychoeducation (mean difference =.396, p < .001), while White participant’s views of PAT did not (mean difference =.132, p = .103). There was no main effect of race such that Black participants in general did not report more positive views of PAT than White participants, F= 2.373, p = .125. Further, depression did positively predict the effect of psychoeducation on view, where participants endorsing more depression had greater increases in the level of positivity in their view of PAT than those who endorsed less depression symptoms, F= 6.061, p < .014.

Factors predicting baseline (i.e., pre-psychoeducation) interest in PAT

Variables that were significantly correlated with pre-psychoeducation interest for participants (i.e., age, LGBT identity, income, barriers, stress, PTSD symptoms, and depressive symptoms) were included in the regression model along with race and interaction variables. PTSD was a significant positive predictor of interest (ß=.36, p < .001). No other variables significantly predicted baseline interest in PAT. However, there was an interaction between race and depression (ß=.04, p = .037) such that depression predicted interest in PAT only for Black participants. See Table 4 for full summary.

Factors predicting baseline (i.e., pre-psychoeducation) view of PAT

Variables that were significantly correlated with pre-psychoeducation view for participants (i.e., LGBT identity, income, barriers, PTSD symptoms, and depressive symptoms) were included in the regression model along with race and interaction variables. Depression was a significant negative predictor of view (ß= −.038, p < .001). Race also significantly predicted view such that Black participants had more positive views than White participants (ß=.311, p = .015). No other variables significantly predicted baseline interest in PAT. However, there was an interaction between race and depression (ß=.057, p < .001) such that depression positively predicted view of PAT for Black participants, and negatively predicted view of PAT for White participants. See Table 5 for full summary.

Factors predicting post-psychoeducation interest in PAT

Variables that were significantly correlated with post-psychoeducation interest for participants (i.e., LGBT identity, income, barriers, PTSD, stress, and depressive symptoms) were included in the second hierarchical regression model along with race and interaction variables. These variables did not predict a significant amount of variance in interest in psychedelic therapy after psychoeducation beyond the variance explained by pre-psychoeducation interest (R2change =.019, Fchange(13,278)= .868, p = .588). Of the measured variables in the second model, pre-psychoeducation interest significantly (positively) predicted post-psychoeducation variance (ß =.623, p < .001), and depression significantly negatively predicted post-psychoeducation variance (ß = −.026, p = .007). No interaction variables were significant. See Table 6 for full summary.

Discussion

A key aim of the present study was to investigate perceptions of PAT and response to psychoeducation, examine potential differences in perceptions of PAT between Black and White Americans, and to identify factors associated with perceptions of PAT. Both Black and White Americans reported increased interest and increased positivity of views of PAT following brief psychoeducation. This indicates promise for a wide reach of PAT-related education efforts across Black and White communities. This finding is consistent with another study using the same perceptions of PAT measure from this study in a veteran and service member sample, which also found a positive increase in interest and view following this psychoeducation [15]. It is notable that this brief psychoeducation on PAT increases interest and positivity of views in two different samples, particularly as FDA approval for MDMA and psilocybin may occur in the coming years allowing for more widespread access of these novel interventions and the need to educate and provide access to groups at high need for novel interventions. This finding is also consistent with previous literature indicating that psychoeducational materials can enhance attitudes about mental health treatments more broadly (Jorm et al., 2003). For example, Beshai et al. [6] found that brief psychoeducation can be effective in increasing perceptions of mental health interventions (e.g., cognitive-behavioral therapy for depression). Overall, our findings extend the benefits of psychoeducation on shifting perceptions to novel treatment modalities such as MDMA- and psilocybin-assisted therapy. This is promising especially given previous research indicating that positive beliefs related to psychiatric treatments increase the likelihood that an individual will engage in these treatments (e.g., Jorm et al., 2000). Given the significant differences across groups such that Black participants reported significantly higher PTSD and depression symptoms, we investigated differences in the effect of psychoeducation on view and interest in PAT including depression and PTSD as covariates. There was not a significant effect of race on level of interest in PAT after psychoeducation, and the effect of psychoeducation on positivity of views after psychoeducation compared to before psychoeducation did vary by racial group- such that Black participants reported more positive views of PAT than White participants following psychoeducation. Notably, level of depression symptoms did positively predict the effect of psychoeducation on view, with participants with greater depression symptoms reporting greater increases in level of positivity of view of PAT than those with less depression symptoms, which suggests that those who could likely benefit the most from these treatments view them more positively after psychoeducation. Contrary to study hypotheses, Black Americans reported significantly more positive views of psychedelic therapy than White American participants following brief psychoeducation despite being underrepresented in psychedelic therapy research [22]. It is possible that novel and innovative treatments outside of mainstream psychiatric care may appear more appealing to this group. This finding is consistent with a recent study which investigated psychoeducation on PAT in BIPOC individuals which found that BIPOC participants were more likely to seek PAT than non-Hispanic White participants following psychoeducation [12]. As such there is accumulating evidence that psychoeducation is an effective tool in increasing interest and views of PAT broadly, and that racially minoritized groups respond significantly more to these psychoeducation approaches compared to non-minoritized racial groups, which is at odds with a narrative of stigma being the main barrier to racially minoritized individuals engaging in PAT clinical trials.

Another aim of this study was to examine potential factors that may be associated with perceptions of PAT, in light of historically oppressive psychedelic research that has been conducted with marginalized populations in the United States (Strauss et al., 2022). At the bivariate level, in both Black and White participants, higher level of perceived barriers to psychological treatment and higher levels of depressive and PTSD symptom severity was positively associated with interest in PAT. It remains encouraging, and perhaps expected, that individuals with greater PTSD and depression symptom severity on average reported greater interest in PAT, given that these are currently the two main indications most frequently researched indications for MDMA- and psilocybin-assisted therapy, respectively. When predicting baseline, or pre-psychoeducation, interest in PAT in the combined sample, PTSD was a significant positive predictor of interest and no other variables significantly predicted baseline interest. Notably, there was a significant interaction such that depression symptom severity predicted level of interest only in Black participants. When predicting baseline view of PAT in the combined sample, race was a significant predictor such that Black participants reported more positive views of PAT, and there was a significant interaction such that depression positively predicted view of PAT in Black participants, and negatively predicted view of PAT in White participants. This suggests at baseline, so before psychoeducation on PAT occurred, minoritized racial group identification was associated with more positive views of PAT, and that greater report of depression symptoms was associated with more positive views and interest specifically in the Black participant group, indicating that racially diverse individuals more likely to benefit from these novel interventions are more likely to report interest and positive views of them. When predicting for post-psychoeducation views and interest, race was not a significant predictor and there was not a significant interaction between race and the variables of interest. Studies often stereotypically frame the lack of mental health service utilization by minoritized populations as being fundamentally due to individual attitudes and stigmas [13], [33], despite evidence that small difference exist in willingness to enroll in clinical research (e.g., [36]). Our findings, with Black American racial identification being a predictor of more positive baseline views, and significant interactions for both interest and views such that Black participants reporting more depression symptoms reporting greater levels of interest and positive views of PAT, spotlight the need to look beyond individual attributions and focus more attention on the ways treatment structures and systems limit access to mental health treatments among minoritized populations. Despite historical experiences of unethical treatment in research and ongoing contemporary factors leading to barriers to treatment engagement, Black Americans demonstrate significant interest engaging in psychedelic clinical research and are particularly responsive to PAT psychoeducation, suggesting the need for more active outreach and efforts to minimize barriers to accessing PAT trials for minoritized groups [39]. In righting the wrong of historical exclusion of Black communities who might otherwise benefit from innovative clinical trials, the onus of change ostensibly falls on the shoulders of institutions that host these studies. While we acknowledge the tremendous labor inherent in conducting clinical trials particularly with a schedule 1 substance, we wish to encourage psychedelic research groups to endeavor to assume the additional effort required to diversify their samples, using strategies such as diversifying study staff, using culturally responsive recruitment materials, connecting with community gatekeepers, and diversifying study spaces in culturally responsive manner, as recommended by Williams and colleagues (2020). There have also been explicit calls for involvement of BIPOC medical professionals, Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs), and other BIPOC organizations within psychedelic research in order to ensure better therapeutic experiences for diverse individuals (e.g., [31]). Limitations & Future Directions.

Although MTurk is shown to provide high quality data and a more diverse participant pool than an undergraduate university [21], MTurk survey findings may impact findings generalizability [14]. The Black participant sample had significantly more participants who self-identified as bisexual or pansexual compared to the White participant sample, which may impact generalizability of study findings. Some prior research has suggested drug use patterns may differ across sexual orientations (e.g., Kerr et al., 2015) which could impact perceptions of PAT, as such future research can investigate if these findings replicate across different samples and an intersectional lens is warranted. The standard demographics form used in the current study did not include additional specific income options other than > $2000/a month as such the highest income group is likely heterogeneous; future research should investigate greater variety in income level and impact on perceptions. Future research could also use additional measures related to racial and ethnic identity, including factors such as salience of ethnic group membership (e.g., the Multigroup Ethnic Identity Measure; [27]), and should assess history of psychedelic use. The current study only utilized quantitative assessments to assess interest and barriers to psychedelic therapy; future qualitative and mixed-methods research is needed. The promotion of healing and treatment accessibility within minoritized communities also comes with the responsibility of addressing the systemic injustices within its realm. It could be helpful for future research to examine psychedelic therapy provider’s engagement in practicing cultural humility when working with Black clients to further examine potential institutional and structural barriers negatively influencing Black community engagement in this treatment. Additionally, future research would benefit from investigating perceptions of psychedelic therapy in additional minoritized groups.Conclusion.

While underrepresented in psychedelic therapy research [22] and demonstrating lower rates of recreational use of psychedelics [18], these results indicate that Black Americans report more positive views of psychedelic therapy compared to White Americans, and Black Americans with greater symptoms of depression, who may be more likely to benefit from PAT interventions, are more interested and report more positive views of PAT. This is at odds with the individual stigma view of understanding the racial disparity in engagement in psychedelic therapy research, and highlights the imperative of identifying and addressing systemic factors that may decrease access to these promising interventions. This highlights the onus on research groups to conduct appropriate outreach and culturally responsive interventions within diverse communities.
