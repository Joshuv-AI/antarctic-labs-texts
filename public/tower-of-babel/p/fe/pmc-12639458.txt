Development and validation of the distress inventory for altered bowel functioning in patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer
Authors: Ling-Chun Lu, Ying-Ru Chen, Chieh-Yu Liu, Ji-Shjang Hung, Been-Ren Lin, Peng-Sheng Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDistress from altered bowel functioning was though as a tragic price of survival in patients underwent sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer. Current questionnaire for assessing quality of life does not comprehensively match the patients’ real concern related to the bowel dysfunction. This study aims to develop and validate an instrument for assessing distress from altered bowel functioning in post-operative patients with rectal cancer.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in five phases: (1) definition of the measured concept and creation of the inventory; (2) content validity of the inventory was assessed by experts; (3) face validity was established by patients with altered bowel function after receiving sphincter-preserving surgery; (4) construct validity was validated by exploratory factor analysis and criterion-related validity was test by receiver operating characteristic curve; (5) reliability was assessed.ResultsFinal version of the inventory comprises 16 items, with a higher score indicating more severe distress from altered bowel functioning. Through exploratory factor analysis, it is across three domains of daily life, emotional regulation and social functioning. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis for identifying patients with distress of altered bowel functioning revealed an area under the curve value of 0.797. The cutoff score is 9.5. The Cronbach's α coefficients for reliability and test-retest reliability were 0.880 and 0.933, respectively.ConclusionsDistress inventory of altered bowel functioning is a reliable and valid instrument, which can help health care professionals proactively identify patients with distress from altered bowel functioning following sphincter-preserving surgery and provide timely interventions and individualized care.

Introduction

According to global statistics, more than 72% of patients who undergo sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer experience a range of symptoms associated with altered bowel functioning.1, 2, 3 The bowel problem can substantially affect patients’ quality of life for whole life, and represents a major concern for health care professionals.4, 5, 6 The patients often perceive a lack of support because perioperative education centers on urgent care and does not sufficiently cover the management of bowel-related problems at home.7 Tracing the possible reason is the perspectives of both health care professionals and patients on impactions from the altered bowel functioning may differ.8 Thus, to comprehensively evaluate the impactions can help health care professionals better understand care demands and plan tailored management strategies for their patients.2,9,10

Several qualitative studies have described the lived experiences of patients with altered bowel functioning.11, 12, 13 Because altered bowel functioning typically associated with unpredictable bowel urgency and episodes of rectal tenesmus, patients with this condition frequently visit the restroom and spend prolonged period on the toilet.7,11 The sense of urgency affects or even interrupts sleep patterns.7,10 Therefore, to control the frequency of their bowel movement, the patients tend to eat less or avoid certain types of food.10,12 Some of these patients may become reluctant to leave the house, and they may even became unable to engage in leisure activities or take public transport for prolonged peroids.10,13 In addition, some patients change how they work or seek workplace accommodations to better manage their condition.7 Many of them also perceive feelings of annoyance, irritation, anxiety, and helplessness because of their bowel-related problems.7,8,12 When the patients experience fecal incontinence, they tend to feel embarrassed, and start worrying about others perceiving unpleasant odor from them.7,8,10 Although these bowel-related problems represent a major source of distress and a tragic price of survival,8,13 the prevalence and impact of the distress remain unclear, necessitating further quantitative studies.7,11 In the context of cancer care, distress is defined as a subjective experience of discomfort or interference, which can be caused either by the disease itself and by its treatment consequences and can considerably affect quality of life.9,14, 15, 16 According to several qualitative studies, distress is characterized by emotional strain and disruptions to daily life, which negatively impact physical capacity, psychological well-being, social engagement and personal concerns.11, 12, 13,17

Although the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) QLQ-C30, QLQ-CR38, and QLQ-CR29, and the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–Colorectal (FACT-C) are designed to assess various aspects of quality of life in patients with colorectal cancer, these instrument primarily focus on evaluating the impact of the disease itself, and the influence of chemotherapy, and radical proctectomy with a stoma on patients' quality of life.18,19 Among these instruments, the EORTC QLQ-CR38, QLQ-CR29, and FACT-C are specifically designed to address the impact of stoma formation on patients' quality of life.19 By contrast, the EORTC QLQ-C30 is primarily designed to evaluate the effects of the disease itself and the influence of nonsurgical treatment across various dimensions of quality of life, with its assessment of bowel-related problems limited to the symptoms of constipation and diarrhea.18 Despite its usefulness, the EORTC QLQ-C30 does not comprehensively reflect the actual concerns of patients regarding their altered bowel functioning; for example, it does not reflect the obstacles faced by the patients in, for example, leaving their house, engaging in leisure activities, socializing with others, getting on public transportation, or working all of which considerably impact their ability to function in society.4,18,20 Health care professionals across a range of disciplines have highlighted the importance of comprehensively assessing the distress caused by altered bowel functioning, particularly because this approach can enhance our understanding of patients’ lived experiences and supports the development of more effective self-care strategies.8 In this study, an instrument, referred to as the Distress Inventory of Altered Bowel Functioning (DI-ABF), was developed and validated to assess distress resulting from altered bowel functioning in patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer.

Methods

Study design

In this scale development study used convenience sampling. It was implemented five phases (Fig. 1). In phase 1, the target concept was defined and an inventory was established. In phase 2, content validity was assessed by experts. In phase 3, face validity was established by patients with altered bowel function after they underwent sphincter-preserving surgery. In phase 4, construct validity was established through an exploratory factor analysis, and criterion-related validity was tested through a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. In phase 5, reliability was assessed.Fig. 1Phases of the development of the distress inventory of altered bowel functioning.Fig. 1

Phases of the development of the distress inventory of altered bowel functioning.

Study setting and sampling

This study was conducted using a self-report questionnaire administered at the colorectal cancer clinics of a 2200-bed medical center in northern Taiwan and a 940-bed hospital in central Taiwan between March 1, 2021, and December 31, 2022.

Participants

Patients meeting the following criteria were included in the study: (1) being ≥ 20 years of age; (2) having an educational level of at least primary school to be able to complete the self-administered questionnaire; (3) having alert consciousness and ability to complete the questionnaire; (4) having a diagnosis of adenocarcinoma in the rectosigmoid junction or rectum; (5) having undergone sphincter-preserving surgery, either alone or combination with a temporary stoma reversal; and (6) having at least one symptom of altered bowel functioning. The types of sphincter-preserving surgery considered were anterior resection, low anterior resection, ultra-low anterior resection, and total mesorectal excision performed using traditional, laparoscopic-assisted, or robotic-assisted methods. The symptoms of altered bowel functioning included increased stool frequency, fecal urgency, fecal or flatus incontinence, clustered defecation, incomplete evacuation, and variable stool consistency. Patients experiencing local recurrence or having any other underlying bowel diseases, such as gastroenteritis, irritable bowel syndrome, inflammatory ulcerative colitis, Crohn's disease, bowel obstruction, or ileus were excluded from the analysis.

Data collection

After written informed consent was obtained from all patients, data were collected at the aforementioned colorectal cancer clinics. An anonymous self-administered questionnaire was then distributed, comprising a participant characteristics sheet, Low Anterior Resection Syndrome (LARS) score, and DI-ABF.

Instrument reliability and validity

The DI-ABF was developed in five phases as follows:

Phase 1: target concept definition and inventory establishment

The target concept must be defined before an instrument is developed.21 In patients who undergo surgery for rectal cancer, altered bowel functioning is also referred to LARS.22 This syndrome is characterized by a series of bowel dysfunction symptoms that occur in patients with rectal cancer after undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery.2,21 These symptoms include increase stool frequency, fecal urgency, fecal or flatus incontinence, clustered defecation, incomplete evacuation and variable stool consistency, etc.3,22 These symptoms are typically associated with distress, which involves subjective experiences of discomfort and interference.23 In the context, distress from altered bowel functioning is defined as a subjective experience of discomfort or interference from postoperative bowel-related symptoms in patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer.21,23 Attributes of this distress are associated with emotional strain and interferences, which affects patients’ bowel habits, sleep patterns, eating behaviors, mood, outdoor mobility, social and leisure activity engagement patterns, job performance, and transportation use.7,24,25

The majority of items in the DI-ABF were developed on the basis of transcripts obtained from a qualitative study published by the first author,17 whereas the remaining items were derived from a comprehensive literature review. All referenced studies focused on the lived experiences of patients with altered bowel functioning. An item pool was generated by extracting transcript segments reflecting emotional distress and disruption related to bowel problems. For example, the statement “It so bothered, I must go to the bathroom for endless times a day” was transformed into the item “You feel bothered because of bowel problems”. Bollinger logic was used to conduct a comprehensive search in PubMed, MEDLINE, CINAHL and the JBI Library for relevant studies published between 1960 and 2020 (Table 1). Studies that qualitatively investigated the distress resulting from altered bowel functioning in patients with rectal cancer who had undergone sphincter-preserving surgery were included for analysis, whereas studies lacking transcript data were excluded. Phase 2: content validity assessment

The content validity of the DI-ABF was assessed by a panel of experts, who assigned scores for content validity. Two experts were separately interviewed to evaluate the integrity and structure of the item pool. These experts were university professors at the School of Nursing Education each with more than 10 years of experience in instrument development. Five health care professionals were also invited to rate the content validity of the questionnaire. These professionals comprised two surgeons, two nurse practitioners, and one ward nurse, each with more than 3 years of experience in colorectal surgery. All health care professional independently evaluated and rated the items of the DI-ABF in terms of appropriateness and comprehensibility. Appropriateness was assessed on the basis of whether an item can be used to measure distress resulting from altered bowel functioning. Comprehensibility was assessed on the basis of whether an item was clearly phrased and easy to understand. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale with the following endpoints. An endpoint of 1 indicated that the item was irrelevant and should be deleted. An endpoint of 2 indicated that the item was seemingly relevant but required considerable revision. An endpoint of 3 indicated that the item was relevant but required a few adjustments. An endpoint of 4 indicated that the item was relevant but required rewording. Lastly, an endpoint of 5 indicated that the item was relevant, clear, and precise. The numbers of items with ≥ 4 points were used to calculate the content validity index.27

Phase 3: psychometric evaluation of face validity

Eligible patients were asked to rate each item in the questionnaire on a dichotomous scale (clear or unclear). They also participated in face-to-face interviews to discuss the psychometric meaning and wording of unclear items.

Phase 4: establishment of constructive validity and criterion-related validity

To establish the constructive validity and criterion-related validity of the DI-ABF, discriminative items in an item analysis were retained. Factor structure was then examined in an exploratory factor analysis. Criterion-related validity in terms of the LARS score was established as a cutoff for identifying patients with rectal cancer who experienced distress from altered bowel functioning.

LARS score was used to establish the criterion-related validity of the DI-ABF. This score is used to measure the frequency of the following symptoms associated with LARS: flatus incontinence, fecal incontinence, increased stool frequency, clustered defecation, and fecal urgency. The total score ranges from 0 to 42 points, with a higher score indicating greater syndrome severity.28,29 This score is also used to classify syndrome severity into three categories with scores of 0–20 points indicating no LARS, 21 to 29 points indicating minor syndrome severity, and 30 to 42 points indicating major syndrome severity.29

Phase 5: reliability

Reliability, internal consistency reliability and test–retest reliability were examined using the data of eligible patients who were identified as eligible for this study on the basis of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Test–retest reliability was examined in 30 eligible patients, with the test and retest separated by at least 1-month.

Data analysis

Researchers disagree on the minimum sample size required for the development of a new instrument.30,31 Studies have recommended recruiting 5 to 10 participants per item.30 Therefore, because the DI-ABF consists of 18 items, nearly 180 participants would be required for an exploratory factor analysis. Another study suggests a sample size of 200–300 participants in case the target population comprises patients.31 Therefore, the estimated sample size was set to approximately 200 in this study.

Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentage distributions, means, and standard deviations (SDs), were used to summarize the patients' sociodemographic characteristics. Reliability was indicated using Cronbach's α coefficients, and internal reliability and test-retest reliability were indicated using correlation coefficients. To establish the construct validity of the questionnaire, an exploratory factor analysis was initially conducted through a principal component analysis. Factor extraction was then conducted using principal axis factoring, followed by Promax oblique rotation to account for correlations among factors. Items with factor loadings below 0.40 were excluded from the final model.30,32 Finally, a ROC curve analysis with Youden's index method was conducted to test the criterion-related validity of the questionnaire with the LARS score. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Reliability, test-retest reliability and internal consistency

Instrument reliability was assessed using Cronbach's α coefficients. The coefficients for the total scale and for the three domains of daily activity, emotional regulation, and social functioning were 0.880, 0.801, 0.870, and 0.851, respectively.

Test–retest reliability was evaluated in a subsample of 30 participants. The Cronbach's α coefficients for the total scale and for the three domains of daily activity, emotional regulation, and social functioning were 0.933, 0.807, 0.881, and 0.908, respectively.

Internal consistency was examined through correlation analyses between each domain and the total scale. Content validity

After a literature review and multiple interviews with experts in instrument development, a draft of an 18-item inventory was developed. Responses were rated on a 3-point Likert scale with endpoints of 0 (never), 1 (sometimes), and 2 (often) with a higher score indicating more severe distress. Five colorectal health care professionals gave scores on several content validity indices. The scores given ranged from 0.83 to 1, the scores given on the scale content validity index ranged from 0.86 to 1, the average score on the scale content validity index was 0.88, and the score on scale content validity with universal agreement was 0.89. The average score on the scale content validity index was greater than that on scale content validity with universal agreement.

Face validity

All patients agreed that the psychometric meaning of all but two major items in the questionnaire were clear. They recommended that certain items omitted because of semantic overlap. One such item (“You have worse quality of sleep because of bowel problems”) was found to overlap with already existing items such as “You have interrupted sleep because of bowel problems” and “You cannot go to sleep because of bowel problems.” Another item (“You felt irritated because of bowel problems”) was considered to be redundant with “You feel bothered because of bowel problems”. After the wording of the cover letter and unclear items was revised. Finally, 16 items were included in the questionnaire.

Criterion-related validity

Criterion-related validity was examined by determining whether LARS severity was associated with higher levels of distress. A Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.141 was identified between LARS total scores and DI-ABF total scores (P ​< ​0.05). It depicts ROC curve illustrating the relationship between DI-ABF score and LARS severity (Fig. 4). The results for the DI-ABF were an area under the curve of 0.797 (P ​< ​0.0001, 95% CI ​= ​0.726–0.867), a sensitivity of 71.8%, a specificity of 77.6%, and an optimal cutoff score of 9.5 (Youden's index). The results for daily activity were an area under the curve of 0.765 (P ​< ​0.0001, 95% CI ​= ​0.689–0.840), a sensitivity of 76.1%, a specificity of 74.1%, and an optimal cutoff score of 3.5 (Youden's index). The results for emotional regulation were an area under the curve of 0.734 in the area under the curve analysis (P ​< ​0.0001, 95% CI ​= ​0.661–0.807), a sensitivity of 73.9%, a specificity of 60.2%, and an optimal cutoff score of 3.5 (Youden's index). The results for social functioning were an area under the curve of 0.761 (P ​< ​0.0001, 95% CI ​= ​0.688–0.834), a sensitivity of 73.2%, a specificity of 70.0%, and an optimal cutoff score of 3.5 (Youden's index).Fig. 4Analysis for criteria-related validity. Discussion

This study developed the DI-ABF to identify distress resulting from altered bowel functioning in patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer. This instrument is valid and reliable and can be used to comprehensively assess the subjective experiences of discomfort and interference associated with LARS, as previously indicated in qualitative studies.7,11,12 Consistent with the findings of the present study, global statistics indicate that the incidence of rectal cancer increases with age; this disease predominantly affects men and is typically treated with surgery, either alone or in combination with chemoradiotherapy.33, 34, 35 This consistency indicates the similarity between the demographic characteristics of the study population and those of the global target population, supporting the proposed instrument's generalizability to patients with rectal cancer.

Overall, the instrument developed in this study has four key main advantages. First, health care professionals working in colorectal surgical departments and with altered bowel functioning may have different understandings of the patients' actual symptoms or distress associated with altered bowel functioning.7,36 In this study, to identify severe distress resulting from altered bowel functioning and measure the ability of such distress to reflect clinical reality, the content validity of the proposed instrument was verified by experts, and the face validity of the proposed instrument was assessed by patients with altered bowel functioning. This approach involving expert and patient opinions in the validation process may increase the instrument's accuracy and lead to uniform understanding between health care professionals and patients.

Second, some patients may feel embarrassed while discussing their bowel problems with health care professionals.7,17 In the present study, the participants recommended omitting the two items of “You have worse quality of sleep because of bowel problems” and “You felt irritated because of the bowel problems” because sleep interruption and sleep difficulty already indicate low sleep quality and because the terms “irritable” and “bothered” are semantically overlapping. A ROC curve analysis was conducted to identify patients with distress resulting from altered bowel functioning, LARS score was used to evaluate the criterion-related validity of the DI-ABF. Because LARS was a major effect on quality of life, LARS score was used as a criterion to identify the patients with distress resulting from bowel problems.4,5 Overall, these results indicated that the proposed instrument can be used to identify patients who require aggressive interventions.

Third, the DI-ABF enables patient to express their needs and concerns during interviews with health care professionals in clinical settings. Its domains can be used to conduct comprehensive assessments and identify patients with post-operative distress resulting from altered bowel functioning, which can in turn enable the development of more effective care strategies aimed at alleviating severe distress. ROC curve analysis can also be used to ensure an optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity in establishing cutoff scores. Because patients may have different care needs depending on the severity of their distress,24,25 items with a score of ≥ 2 points may enhance specificity to severe distress. Identifying patients with distress resulting from altered bowel functioning within a specific domain through cutoff points and at least one item with a score of ≥ 2 points may enhance the specificity for emotional regulation.

Fourth, the DI-ABF is effective in identifying distress.23 In clinical practice, this tool can not only be used to identify patients experiencing distress resulting from altered bowel functioning but also determine which specific domain of distress requires immediate intervention. Consequently, this tool can enable physicians, surgeons, and other health care professionals to promptly recognize their patients' care needs and arrange appropriate consultations, provide support, and develop educational strategies.8,23 Compared with the EORTC QLQ-C30 questionnaire,18 the DI-ABF offers a more set of distinct advantages. Its primary strength lies in its ability to identify patients experiencing distress from altered bowel functioning and to guide health care professionals in addressing specific care needs related to daily life disruptions, particularly during the disease-free period. A major weakness of the DI-ABF, however, is its focus on distress resulting only from surgical consequences. Despite this weakness, the DI-ABF focuses on patients’ long-term concerns and lived experiences, indicating its relevance and clinical value.

In this study, Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.141 was identified between LARS total score and DI-ABF total score (P ​< ​0.05), this weak correlation should not be viewed as a limitation. Instead, it should be viewed as evidence for the discriminant validity of the DI-ABF, indicating that it captures a distinct construct—subjective distress—rather than the objective symptom severity of LARS.2,28,29

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, all participants were patients with rectal cancer who had undergone sphincter-preserving surgery and had at least one symptom of altered bowel functioning. The proposed instrument is not applicable to patients with rectal cancer with a stoma or patients with no bowel problems. Second, all participants were required to be literate to complete the self-administered questionnaire. The proposed instrument is not applicable to patients with illiteracy. In this study, the rate of illiteracy was 1.5%. To address this limitation, a verbally or visually guided intelligent system may be required, particularly with current shortages in clinical workforce capacity. Third, because recruiting a sufficient number of patients during the COVID-19 pandemic was difficult, this study did not involve sample splitting or cross-validation, and it did not include a confirmatory factor analysis or known-group validation—two essential procedures for establishing construct validity. Nevertheless, the validity of the proposed instrument was supported through multiple complementary measures, including content validity analysis, face validity analysis, exploratory factor analysis, and criterion-related validity analysis. These measures provide meaningful evidence of psychometric robustness. Future studies should involve more rigorous validation techniques with larger and more diverse samples. Although the proposed instrument can be used to identify patients who frequently experience bowel-related problems, such as irritable bowel syndrome during the diarrhea phase and inflammatory ulcerative colitis during flare-ups, further validation is required to confirm its utility in these populations.

Clinical implications and future research

Overall, the DI-ABF can be used to identify patients with distress resulting from postoperative bowel-related problems. This tool can be used by health care professionals to proactively provide timely and personalized care for their patients and to develop individualized care strategies. Addressing the limitation of respondent literacy should be prioritized to ensure the generalizability of this tool. This can be achieved by developing a verbally or visually guided intelligent system.

Conclusions

The DI-ABF (Supplement File 1) is a reliable and valid instrument that can be used to identify distress resulting from altered bowel functioning in patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer. Identifying patients with distress resulting from altered bowel functioning can help health care professionals offer timely support to improve their patients’ quality of care and help them to cope with their condition.
