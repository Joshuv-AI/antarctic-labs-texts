Squamous Cell Carcinoma of Finger Invading Middle Phalanx: Surgical Considerations
Authors: Anna Podber, Shrestha Samuel, Gangadasu Reddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the nail unit is a rare but highly treatable malignancy. A tissue biopsy is recommended to establish the diagnosis and guide treatment. Mohs surgery is the standard of care to preserve tissue and function for early SCC. The authors report a 76-year-old patient with a very advanced and neglected SCC of the nail unit that grossly extended to the middle phalanx. No prior reports of such an extensive growth have been documented. Due to extensive skin and bone involvement, we proceeded with amputation at the level of the proximal phalanx to obtain negative margins. We achieved a tumor-free status with a well-healed stump at 1-year follow-up. The diagnostic workup should include a multidisciplinary approach with assessment of locoregional and distant spread and exclusion of atypical infections or metastatic disease. Amputation should be performed when there is extensive tissue and bone involvement.

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the nail unit is a common malignancy involving the hand, but it is overall a rare form of cancer.1 Due to its rarity and tendency for early lesions to mimic benign nail conditions, clinical suspicion is often delayed.2,3 Bony involvement occurs in approximately half of cases.4 Metastatic spread and death are rare.4 Metastasis from lung, renal, or breast cancer can cause secondary SCC of the nail unit.5 Mohs surgery is the standard of care for early-stage tumors to preserve tissue and finger function.6–8 However, for very advanced tumors, a multidisciplinary approach with appropriate workup and wide local excision and adjunct therapies is recommended per the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines.9

The tumor is usually isolated to the nail unit, distal to the distal interphalangeal joint.4 We present a neglected case of primary SCC of the nail unit extending to the middle phalanx requiring amputation through the proximal phalanx to obtain tumor-free margins. No previous case reports or studies identified in our literature search have documented such a finding.

CASE

A 76-year-old man presented to the trauma bay with multiple traumatic injuries after a fall down steps. He was noted to have a 4-cm fungating growth of the left ring finger involving the distal and middle phalanges (Fig. 1).

Clinical photographs of SCC of the ring finger with gross involvement of the distal and middle phalanges. A, The palmar side of the hand. B, The tumor extending proximally in the lateral view.

The patient reported localized pain and swelling of the finger starting 10 years prior. His primary care physician prescribed topical and oral antibiotics; however, no tissue biopsy was performed.

Radiographic imaging of this finger showed almost complete resorption of the distal phalanx, radial cortical irregularity of the middle phalanx, and soft tissue swelling of the ring finger (Fig. 2). As part of his trauma workup, computed tomography imaging of the head, chest, abdomen, and pelvis showed no evidence or source of metastatic disease. He did not have lymphadenopathy at the elbow, axilla, or mediastinum on physical examination and imaging.

Radiograph demonstrating bony involvement, with almost complete resorption of the distal phalanx and radial cortical deformity of the middle phalanx. Generalized arthritic changes are also present.

A tissue biopsy of the mass confirmed invasive SCC. Surgical amputation was recommended. Because skin involvement was more proximal than bone involvement, amputation was performed through the distal part of the proximal phalanx to provide skin coverage over the stump site. Traction neurectomies of the radial and ulnar digital nerves were performed. The stump site was closed.

Pathology demonstrated moderately differentiated invasive SCC with involvement of the periosteum and cortical bone without lymphovascular invasion. Perineural invasion was identified. The skin and bone margins were negative for carcinoma. At the patient’s 11-month postoperative follow-up, the amputation stump site was healing well (Fig. 3), he was at his baseline function, and was scheduled to see medical oncology shortly thereafter.

Clinical photograph showing the stump site healing well 11 months postoperatively.

DISCUSSION

Our 76-year-old male patient presented with a finger lesion of greater than 10 years’ duration without prior biopsy and definitive diagnosis. This case is noteworthy due to the lesion’s chronicity and extent. Histopathologic analysis revealed moderately differentiated SCC with bony invasion and negative skin and bone margins. Amputation through the proximal phalanx was performed due to more proximal skin involvement. Systemic imaging studies confirmed a primary SCC of the nail unit and were negative for axillary and mediastinal lymphadenopathy.

Typically, SCC of the nail unit is confined to the distal phalanx. However, invasion extending proximally into the middle phalanx is exceptionally rare and, to our knowledge, has not been previously documented. This underscores the importance of early recognition and biopsy of suspicious nail lesions to prevent progression to advanced disease requiring more aggressive surgical intervention. Due to gross bone involvement and the large tumor size, Mohs surgery was not used. The NCCN guidelines recommend a 1-cm margin for tumors greater than 2 cm.9 Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SNLB) is required for palpable lymphadenopathy or abnormal lymph nodes on imaging, per the NCCN guidelines. Patients, like ours, with very high-risk SCC and without clinical lymphadenopathy require either SLNB or imaging. Our patient had imaging assessment of the lymph nodes as part of his trauma workup; therefore, he did not require SLNB.

Although preserving length of the digit was considered, we proceeded with an amputation to a level that would achieve negative skin and bone margins and minimize reoperation. A ray amputation may be beneficial in a high-functioning patient. Because our patient had low baseline function, a ray amputation would have posed an increased morbidity risk. Cutaneous involvement of the tumor was more proximal than bony involvement, necessitating amputation through the proximal phalanx to have adequate stump site coverage. We advocate obtaining radiographs to identify the degree of osseous involvement for surgical planning.6 A preoperative computed tomography of the digit was not performed in our patient, as bone involvement of a digit dictates amputation through the joint immediately proximal to the affected phalanx. Long-term follow-up is essential to monitor for local recurrence or metastatic spread. For very high-risk patients with negative margins, the NCCN recommends follow-up every 3–6 months for 2 years, then every 6–12 months for 3 years, then annually for life.9 We recommended a multidisciplinary approach with consideration of adjuvant and neoadjuvant treatment.

This case highlights the aggressive potential of neglected SCC of the nail unit and serves as a reminder to maintain a high index of suspicion for malignancy in chronic or atypical nail lesions. Long-standing lesions would benefit from tissue sampling to establish a diagnosis at the initial encounter.

CONCLUSIONS

SCC of the nail unit is a rare but potentially aggressive malignancy that can invade underlying structures if untreated. This case illustrates a presentation of SCC of the nail unit extending grossly into the middle phalanx, requiring amputation through the proximal phalanx to achieve tumor-free margins. It emphasizes the critical importance of early biopsy and diagnosis of chronic and/or treatment-resistant nail lesions to avoid extensive surgical management. Clinicians should be aware of this potential for proximal extension in long-standing cases. Once diagnosed, a treatment plan should be formulated using a multidisciplinary approach.
