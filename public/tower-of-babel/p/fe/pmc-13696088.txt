Early assessment of compliance with California’s flavoured tobacco sales prohibition: evidence from vape shops
Authors: Arzoo Alam, Lisa Henriksen, Trent O Johnson, Judith J Prochaska, Nina Schleicher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn December 2022, California became the second US state to prohibit the sale of most flavoured tobacco/nicotine products. To our knowledge, this is the first observational study to assess violations of California’s law. It reports on the availability of flavoured vape products in vape shops and tests whether flavoured vape products were more common in vape shops nearer to college campuses compared with those farther from college campuses, adjusting for neighbourhood demographics.MethodsBetween April and July 2023, observations were recorded at vape shops (n=400) nearer to (≤3 miles) or farther from (>3 miles) a college campus. Vape shops were the focus of this study because a large proportion (48.2%) of US young adults purchase vapes from these venues. Availability of any disposable nicotine vapes, JUUL pods and flavoured varieties of these products were recorded. Multivariable logistic regressions tested relationships between flavoured product availability, proximity to a college campus and store neighbourhood demographics.ResultsIn violation of California’s law, flavoured disposable vapes and/or JUUL pods were available in 50.3% of all vape shops studied. Flavoured disposable vapes were in 53.2% of vape shops with any disposable vapes, and flavoured JUUL pods were in 30.9% of vape shops with any JUUL pods. Contrary to expectation, no significant relationships were identified between the availability of flavoured vapes and proximity to a college campus or store neighbourhood demographics.ConclusionsThis study revealed high rates of violations among vape shops within 4 to 7 months after the implementation of California’s law. Efforts to improve retailer education and enforcement are warranted.

INTRODUCTION

Young adults (aged 18 to 24) had the highest rates of nicotine vape use (15.2%) of any age group in the USA in 2022 (high school students=14.1%, adults ≥25 years=5.3% and middle school students=3.3%).1 2 Among college students, past-month nicotine vape use was 18.0% in 2023.3 Flavours are a major driver of current use and experimentation, often perceived as more appealing and less harmful than tobacco flavours.4 Over 80% of US young adults’ first tobacco product was flavoured (including menthol), and 96.9% of young adults who vaped in the past month used flavoured products.5 6 Given the widespread use of flavoured tobacco products, tobacco control experts have identified sales restrictions as a promising policy approach to reducing tobacco use.7

In August 2020, the California legislature passed Senate Bill (SB) 793—a statewide law prohibiting the sale of most flavoured tobacco/nicotine products, including menthol cigarettes, flavoured little cigars and cigarillos and flavoured vapes. Exemptions were made for premium cigars and loose-leaf tobacco in all retailers, and shisha tobacco sold in retailers that limit entry to adults aged 21 and over.8 SB 793 defines flavoured tobacco as having a distinguishing taste and/or aroma including, but not limited to, any fruit, chocolate, vanilla, honey, candy, cocoa, dessert, alcoholic beverage, menthol, mint, wintergreen, herb or spice.8 These flavours will henceforth be referred to as ‘explicit’ flavours. Growing in popularity, many tobacco products are also available in ‘concept’ flavours, with vague descriptors (eg, winter, tropic, gold), used as a loophole by tobacco companies to circumvent restrictions and maintain marketing and sales of flavoured tobacco products.9

Although SB 793 was passed in August 2020, the tobacco industry forced a ballot referendum, delaying its implementation by 2 years. Despite a US$23 million campaign, primarily funded by R.J. Reynolds and Philip Morris USA, 63.4% of voters upheld the law in November 2022.10 SB 793 went into effect on 21 December 2022, making California the second US state to prohibit sales of flavoured tobacco, after Massachusetts.10 Tobacco retailers were required to remove all prohibited flavoured products by the effective date.11 The penalty for violating SB 793 is a $250 fine for each infraction.8 In December 2022, the California Department of Tax and Fee Administration notified licensed retailers, distributors and wholesalers via email/mail and posted a notice on its website.12 In January 2023, the California Department of Public Health sent postcards and letters notifying retailers of the law and its website displayed fact sheets and frequently asked questions.12 13

Over 60 countries have adopted similar policies.14 For example, the European Union prohibits the sale of explicit flavours (effective May 2016), including menthol (effective May 2020), in cigarettes and roll-your-own tobacco.15 The Netherlands extended these restrictions to include flavoured vape products in January 2024.16 Similarly, Canada banned flavoured cigarettes, little cigars and blunt wraps (excluding menthol) in 2009, with many provinces implementing menthol bans in May 2015, followed by a federal menthol ban in October 2017.17 Sales data showed zero sales postban in all provinces.18

Observational studies in the US report varying levels of early-stage compliance with local flavoured tobacco restrictions. After Boston, Massachusetts prohibited the sale of flavoured tobacco to adult-only shops (February 2016), 14% of youth-accessible tobacco retailers continued to sell flavoured tobacco 8 months later (84% of these products were explicit-flavoured and 16% concept-flavoured).19 Similarly, Minneapolis (January 2016) and Saint Paul (April 2016), Minnesota also restricted flavoured tobacco to adult-only shops. Among randomly sampled grocery and convenience stores, 39% of stores in Minneapolis and 8% in Saint Paul still sold flavoured tobacco, 5 and 2 months post-policy, respectively.20 Chicago, Illinois prohibited flavoured tobacco sales at stores within 500 feet of schools (April 2016), but 58% of randomly sampled stores near schools remained non-compliant after 8 months.21 San Francisco, California was the first US city to implement a comprehensive prohibition of all flavoured tobacco sales (April 2018), however, only 17% of all retailers complied, 8 months postimplementation.22

To our knowledge, this is the first observational study to evaluate retail compliance with California’s law, SB 793. An early-stage assessment allows regulators and policymakers to understand implementation of the law and provide timely support or propose changes. With concern for the continued use of flavoured vape products among young adults, this study tests whether flavoured vapes are more common in vape shops nearer to (≤3 miles) a college campus compared with those farther from (>3 miles) a college campus. Vape shops are the focus of this study because 48.2% of US young adults (aged 18–24) obtain nicotine vapes from these venues.23 With concern for geographic disparities in retail violations, this study also assesses relationships between availability of explicit-flavoured vape products and neighbourhood demographics.

METHODS

Sample

We conducted retail marketing surveillance in California vape shops nearer to and farther from colleges, at two time points: 201924 and 2023. This study uses 2023 data that were collected 4 to 7 months after SB 793 went into effect. The sample consists of all vape shops from the 2019 surveillance that were phone-verified as eligible in 2023 (ie, sold vape products or other tobacco products).

In 2019 (June–August), a random sample of 614 vape shops were observed from two strata: shops nearer to (≤3 miles) and farther from (>3 miles) from California community colleges and 4-year universities. Community colleges are publicly funded institutions that typically offer 2-year associate degrees and certificates, whereas 4-year universities are higher education institutions that offer undergraduate and graduate degree programmes. Vape shops were identified through their Yelp classification (store type=‘vape shops’) and Google web scraping (eg, ‘vape store’, ‘vape shop’ or ‘vaporiser store’).24 The sample included both vape shops that sold only vape products (‘vape only’) and vape shops that sold both vape and other tobacco products (‘vape-and-smoke’). The cut-point for proximity to a college campus was 3 miles because two-thirds of US colleges have at least one vape shop within this radius.25 Vape shops were geocoded to latitude–longitude based on street address and linked to census tract data.24 Euclidean distance between vape shops and nearest college campus boundary was computed using campus shapefiles that we created.26

To determine which of the 614 vape shops visited in 2019 were eligible in 2023, we conducted an online web search in July 2022 and identified 84 stores that were permanently closed. In March 2023, we conducted telephone verifications for the remaining 530 vape shops. Reasons for ineligibility were permanently closed (n=52), no longer selling vape or other tobacco products (n=6), and restricted sales to online/pickup orders only (n=6). This resulted in a total of 466 eligible vape shops.

Nine professional data collectors attempted observations in 466 vape shops, with secondary attempts made at 83 stores to assess inter-rater reliability (IRR). Using the offline Qualtrics app on iPads, data collection occurred from April to July 2023, 4 to 7 months (mean=153 days, SD=17.2) after SB 793 took effect. The observation completion rate was 94.0% (438/466). Reasons for incomplete observations were store temporarily closed (n=8), permanently closed (n=10), data collector asked to leave (n=8), store not selling any tobacco (n=1) or store not found (n=1). The final analytic sample (n=400) also excluded 37 stores not selling any nicotine vape products, and one store where explicit-flavoured product availability could not be determined. For stores with two completed observations, data from the first visit were included in the sample and data from the second visit were used for IRR analysis.

Measures

Product availability

Data collectors recorded availability of any disposable nicotine vapes (eg, FLUM, HYPPE, Elf Bar), explicit-flavoured disposable nicotine vapes (eg, mint, fruit, sweet, alcohol, coffee, banana ice), any JUUL pods and explicit-flavoured JUUL pods (eg, menthol, other non-tobacco flavour). The Cohen’s kappa IRR statistics for these products were 0.51 for disposable vapes (n=78), 0.53 for explicit-flavoured disposable vapes (n=78), 0.74 for JUUL pods (n=77) and 0.61 for explicit-flavoured JUUL pods (n=77). JUUL was selected as a vape brand of interest as it was the best-selling vape and most prevalent among adolescents and young adults, in 2019.27 Data collectors also recorded the availability of other tobacco products, such as cigarettes, cigarillos, tobacco leaf wraps and shisha tobacco/hookah. Products were only classified as ‘available’ if they were in stock. Data collectors were not required to obtain permission from the store clerk but were provided with a sample script in case they needed to introduce themselves and gain store cooperation. They were advised not to interact with store clerks, unless it was a clarifying question (eg, cannot read product label, no price written, etc) to reduce reliability issues owing to different responses from the same, or different clerks.

Neighbourhood demographics

Store neighbourhood was defined as a census tract. Demographic measures were computed using the following variables from the 2017–2021 American Community Survey 5-year tract estimates: total population, population under 21 years, household income, population of non-Hispanic/Latino (NHL) White residents, population of NHL Black residents and population of Hispanic/Latino residents. Population density and percentage of population under 21 years were computed and quartiled for the models, based on state-wide distributions, with the first quartile (ie, lowest values) as the reference group. Model predictors also included Index of Concentrations at the Extremes (ICE) for household income and race/ethnicity measures (NHL White, NHL Black and Hispanic/Latino). ICE was designed to demonstrate the extent to which an area’s residents are concentrated at the extremes of deprivation and privilege.28 It is calculated by dividing the difference between the number of individuals from the privileged group and the number of individuals from the deprived group by the total population for whom group membership is known in a geographic area. The index ranges from −1 to 1, where −1 indicates that the entire population is concentrated in the least privileged group, 1 indicates that the entire population is concentrated in the most privileged group and 0 indicates an even distribution of both groups.28 While ICE was originally developed for use with economic measures, some studies have also used it for race/ethnicity.28 For ICE race/ethnicity, NHL White was the privileged group and NHL Black and Hispanic/Latino were the deprived groups. For ICE household income, the privileged group was households with an income greater than 80th percentile (US$133 000, rounded to $150 000 as per census tract income categories), and the deprived group was households with an income less than 20th percentile (US$61 000, rounded to $60 000 as per census tract income categories), using state-wide distributions.28 The ICE is preferable to other measures of economic and social inequality, such as the Gini coefficient and the Index of Dissimilarity, because it identifies which group is concentrated at the extreme and can be meaningfully used with smaller geographic units, such as census tracts.28

Analysis

With concern for assessing violations of SB 793, we computed the proportion of stores that sold flavoured products for three categories within two groups of stores. Product categories were: (1) any explicit-flavoured disposable vapes and/or JUUL pods, (2) explicit-flavoured disposable vapes and (3) explicit-flavoured JUUL pods. Availability of these products was described in two ways: among all vape shops, and among the subset of vape shops selling the respective product (disposable vapes or JUUL pods). Neighbourhood demographics were described for three groups of stores: (1) all vape shops, (2) vape shops with any disposable vapes and (3) vape shops with any JUUL pods.

Vape shops in the analytic sample (n=400) were located within 377 census tracts, of which 93.9% had only one store. Therefore, data were not treated as stores nested within tracts. Initial bivariate logistic regression models tested relationships between explicit-flavoured product availability (any explicit-flavoured disposable vapes and/or JUUL pods, explicit-flavoured disposable vapes and explicit-flavoured JUUL pods) and proximity to a college campus (nearer to=1, farther from=0). As sensitivity analyses, additional bivariate logistic regression models were fit with Euclidean distance to college campuses measured continuously and dichotomised at 1 mile, 0.5 mile and 1000 feet.

Multivariable logistic regression models tested whether systematic variation in explicit-flavoured product availability could be explained by proximity to a college campus and store neighbourhood demographics. Covariates included proximity to a college campus, ICE NHL White versus NHL Black, ICE NHL White versus Hispanic/Latino, ICE household income, population density (quartiled), percentage of population under 21 years (quartiled) and number of days since SB 793 went into effect. Since nearly all stores studied were vape-and-smoke shops (n=375, 93.5%), the model did not control for store type. All analyses were performed using R V.4.2.2.

DISCUSSION

This is the first known observational study to evaluate SB 793, revealing relatively high violation rates in vape shops just 4–7 months after the law went into effect. Overall, 50.3% of all vape shops sold explicit-flavoured disposable vapes and/or JUUL pods. Among shops selling disposable vapes, 53.2% offered explicit flavours, while 30.9% of shops selling JUUL pods offered explicit flavours. Violations of explicit-flavoured JUUL were lower, possibly due to the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) requiring JUUL Labs Inc to stop selling and distributing products, and removing those on the market, in June 2022.29 Findings from this study also complement evidence of violations from proprietary sales data of explicit-flavoured vapes in California during the same time period.30 Notably, proprietary sale data exclude vape shops, which were the focus of this study.

Similar violation rates have been observed in other countries. For example, India prohibited the sale of all vape products in December 2019, but a compliance study 1 month later found that 18.6% (n=37) of retailers still sold vapes.31 Of the 37 retailers that were non-compliant, 94.6% were vape-and-smoke shops.

There are at least three potential reasons for the high violation rates. First, retailers could have used the study period to sell off existing flavoured tobacco products, even though SB 793 went into effect immediately. Second, despite notices sent by the state, retailers may have had inadequate knowledge of the law. Other studies show improved compliance with retailer education. In San Francisco, compliance with a local law prohibiting sales of flavoured tobacco increased from 17% to 80% within a year, largely due to in-person retailer education, compliance checks and active enforcement.22 Similarly, violations of Minneapolis’ local law decreased from 39% (5 months postimplementation) to 15% (14 months after), likely due to increased retailer outreach by city staff.20 Such results highlight the importance of incorporating robust retailer education efforts when implementing any type of sales restriction. Finally, limited enforcement and relatively low penalties ($250) may encourage retailers to continue selling flavoured products, especially those who make significant profit from flavoured tobacco products. To strengthen enforcement of SB 793, California’s Assembly Bill (AB) 935 (effective 1 January 2024) allows for the suspension or revocation of a retailer’s state licence after three violations of SB 793. AB 935 also empowers the state health department to enforce the law, in collaboration with other state agencies and local authorities.32 With nearly 80% of disposable vape sales being explicit-flavoured,33 clear incentives and consequences are needed for retail compliance.

By focusing on vape shops, the results may not generalise to other tobacco specialty shops or state-licensed tobacco retailers in California. In addition, the study did not assess availability of other tobacco products covered by SB 793 (eg, menthol cigarettes) or reliably assess availability of other flavoured vape products (eg, concept-flavoured disposable nicotine vapes, prefilled cartridge-based vapes, nicotine e-liquids). This highlights the challenge of identifying and enforcing restrictions on concept-flavoured vapes, as it can be difficult to determine whether a concept-flavoured product meets the legal definition of a flavoured tobacco product from the name alone.34 Notably, a chemical analysis found that nearly all concept-flavoured tobacco products contained high concentrations of the same flavour chemicals found in explicit-flavoured products.35 Since many concept-flavoured vapes are thought to contain explicit flavours,36 this study likely underestimates violation rates in vape shops. Therefore, ongoing monitoring of these products is essential. Additionally, due to some moderately low Cohen’s kappa statistics (eg, 0.51 and 0.53), our study likely underestimates violations. This is because some coders may have occasionally overlooked a flavoured product and mistakenly reported it as absent. It is unlikely that a coder would have falsely recorded the presence of a flavoured vape that was not actually present. As such, any potential error is more likely to have led to under-reporting of violations. Finally, the lack of purchase attempts in the protocol may further underestimate violation rates, especially among retailers who sell flavoured tobacco products that are not visible to customers.

Despite these limitations, strengths of the study are providing early evidence of retail violations of SB 793 from a large sample of vape shops in California. The study focused on a store type that cannot be studied using proprietary sales data. In addition, assessing product availability in brick-and-mortar stores affords the opportunity to investigate geographic disparities in retail violations, even though none was observed.

Monitoring violations of SB 793, especially after AB 935, is crucial. Future research should explore reasons for violations, including the role of manufacturers and vape shop owner perspectives. A study on FDA vape regulations revealed vape shop owner concerns about the intention of restrictions, financial implications and complexity.37 Future research should include underage purchase attempts to better capture sales violations, as 43% of US youth (aged 15–20) who vape obtain products from retail sources, with 22% from vape shops.38 Moreover, since tobacco specialty shops (both vape only and vape-and-smoke shops) account for a small proportion (13.5%) of all tobacco retailers in California,39 future studies should include a representative sample. Future research should also assess all tobacco product categories, record brand names of available flavoured tobacco and assess compliance with local laws, such as restricting retailers near colleges.

Overall, this study highlights violations of California’s flavoured tobacco sales prohibition, with no improvement over time. To reduce violations, stronger retailer education and enforcement efforts are needed. Follow-up studies are essential to reassess violations and monitor changes in the tobacco retail environment.
