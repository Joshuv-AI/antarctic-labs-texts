Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio as a practical alternative to mHLA-DR measurement in delayed sepsis-induced immunosuppression
Authors: Thomas Lafon, Fabienne Venet, Anne-Claire Lukaszewicz, Guillaume Monneret
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Over the past decade, immuno-inflammatory alterations have emerged as a central determinant of outcomes in critically ill patients. The field has decisively shifted toward personalized immunomodulation, requiring both therapeutic strategies and intervention timing to be tailored to each patient’s immune trajectory [1]. These trajectories are inherently complex, frequently encompassing both hyperinflammatory and immunosuppressive responses. Despite these advances, the recently introduced SOFA-2 score still fails to incorporate immuno-inflammatory parameters, primarily due to the heterogeneous availability of immunomonitoring tools across institutions and the supposed lack of validated clinical decision thresholds [2].

Monocyte HLA-DR (mHLA-DR) has emerged as a reference biomarker for the identification of patients presenting with sepsis-induced immunosuppression, with strong clinical relevance and analytical standardization [3]. A validated threshold of 8000 antibodies bound per cell (AB/C) reliably identifies patients at increased risk of ICU-acquired infections and mortality [4] and has, therefore, been used to select patients for clinical trials evaluating the immunostimulatory efficacy of interferon-γ (IFN-γ) in the most immunosuppressed populations. However, mHLA-DR measurement is not routinely available in all centers, prompting the need for practical surrogate markers based on widely accessible parameters, such as leukocyte counts (i.e., neutrophils and/or lymphocytes) from a standard complete blood count.

To address this gap, we leveraged a large, well-characterized cohort previously reported in Intensive Care Medicine, in which serial mHLA-DR measurements were performed in more than 1000 patients with septic shock during the first week of ICU admission. Given that IFN-γ therapy in ongoing trials is reserved for patients with delayed decreased mHLA-DR < 8000 AB/C several days after admission, as a proof-of-concept study, our analyses focused on available results from leukocyte counts at 1 week (D7) after ICU admission.

We first examined associations between mHLA-DR, neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and clinical outcomes, including 28-day mortality and ICU-acquired infections (Table S1). In addition to mHLA-DR, both neutrophil counts and NLR were significantly different between patients with and without deleterious outcomes. Since NLR incorporates lymphocyte counts (lymphopenia being a hallmark of immunosuppression), we focused on the NLR relationship with mHLA-DR. ROC analysis showed that NLR discriminates between patients with mHLA-DR above or below 8000 AB/C, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.68 (p < 0.001, details in Table S2) and an optimal threshold of 10 (Youden index). mHLA-DR values differed very significantly between patients with NLR ≤ 10 versus > 10 (Fig. 1A). There was 64% concordance between the two biomarkers (i.e., NLR > 10 with mHLA-DR < 8000 or NLR < 10 with mHLA-DR > 8000 AB/C, alluvial plot, Fig. 1B). Among discordant patients, the 75 individuals with high NLR but mHLA-DR > 8000 AB/C displayed lymphopenia (median 0.84 G/L, Fig. S1), supporting the notion that NLR > 10 mostly identifies patients with immunosuppressive features, reflected either by low mHLA-DR or lymphopenia. Most importantly, patients classified as immunocompetent (i.e., NLR < 10 or mHLA-DR > 8000 AB/C) or not by either marker exhibited similar baseline clinical characteristics (Fig. 1C). That said, some patients with low mHLA-DR are not captured by NLR, and the underlying immuno-inflammatory mechanisms behind this discordance warrant further investigation. We next evaluated NLR across the three mHLA-DR trajectory clusters previously identified [4]. The “non-improvers” cluster, characterized by persistently low mHLA-DR values, exhibited significantly higher NLR values, with median D7-NLR of 12 compared with 8 and 6 in the other clusters (Fig. S2).

These findings were further validated in an independent cohort of septic shock patients (n = 54; recruited following patients from the 2025 study, Table S3). In this cohort, D7 ROC analysis associating NLR and mHLA-DR at D7 yielded an AUC of 0.96; patients with NLR > 10 consistently exhibited very low mHLA-DR values and concordance between markers was 75% (Fig. S3). Additionally, at this time point, dedicated flow cytometry monitoring of circulating neutrophils showed that immature neutrophils (CD10⁻CD16⁻, known to have immunosuppressive properties), accounted for 44% [22–87] of the total neutrophil population and increased up to 73% [43–87] in patients with NLR > 10 (data not shown).

Collectively, these results show that at D7, an NLR > 10 integrates multiple immuno-inflammatory features, including neutrophilia, a high proportion of immature neutrophils, and lymphopenia, thereby identifying a patient population that overlaps with that defined by mHLA-DR < 8000 AB/C. Although elevated NLR is not proposed here as a definitive biomarker of immunosuppression or as a strict surrogate for mHLA-DR, its universal availability from routine complete blood counts makes it a practical provisional marker for identifying patients who may benefit from immunotherapy, even though it does not fully overlap with mHLA-DR. At the very least, it could serve as an alarm parameter that triggers further assessment of immunosuppression. To our knowledge, this is the first study to propose such a readily accessible alternative to mHLA-DR. In addition, given the persistence of inflammatory responses in patients who remain immunosuppressed after sepsis, NLR—which reflects both neutrophil release from the bone marrow and lymphopenia—may, once validated, serve as an integrated biomarker capturing the coexistence of inflammation and immunosuppression.

Further studies are needed to validate these findings and to establish clinically relevant thresholds in septic patients, as well as in other ICU populations [5]. It is important to note that underlying leukocyte abnormalities in patients, such as profound neutropenia or lymphopenia, cannot be accurately assessed using NLR. Nevertheless, despite these limitations, and given its widespread accessibility, we strongly advocate for the inclusion of NLR in future studies, whether observational or interventional. Although deceptively simple, NLR appears to be a practical, complementary, and readily implementable marker for assessing immune status, with the potential—pending validation—to inform clinical decision-making in critically ill populations.
