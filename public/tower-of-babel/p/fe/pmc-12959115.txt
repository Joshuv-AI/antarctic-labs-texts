Thyrotoxicosis Triggering Takotsubo Cardiomyopathy and Acute Coronary Syndrome: A Case of Severe Cardiovascular Manifestations in Hyperthyroidism
Authors: Raiyan Ali, Nathaniel Lin, Taha Siddiqui, Mohit Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hyperthyroidism can lead to serious cardiovascular complications, including arrhythmias, high-output heart failure, and myocardial ischemia. Thyrotoxicosis, a hypermetabolic emergency, may mimic or exacerbate acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and, though rare, has been associated with takotsubo cardiomyopathy. We present a case of a 54-year-old thyrotoxic female without significant cardiovascular history who presented with atypical chest pain complicated by both takotsubo cardiomyopathy and myocardial infarction. This case not only emphasizes the diagnostic challenge posed by thyroid-induced cardiac dysfunctions but also highlights the importance of multidisciplinary management in these patients.

Introduction

Hyperthyroidism can lead to a range of systemic complications.1,2 Thyrotoxicosis is an acute, life-threatening complication of hyperthyroidism that can significantly impact the cardiovascular system.3 Such cardiac manifestations include tachyarrhythmias, takotsubo cardiomyopathy, and even atypical chest pain which raises concerns for acute coronary syndrome especially in females.4,5 We report a case of a 54-year-old thyrotoxic female who presented with atypical chest pain and dyspnea. Interestingly, she was found to have evidence for both takotsubo cardiomyopathy and ACS of non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). We suspect that both the takotsubo cardiomyopathy and ACS were possibly associated or triggered by the thyrotoxicosis.

Case presentation

54-year-old female with medical history of hypertension, recent diagnosis of hyperthyroidism, and extensive family history of coronary artery disease and hyperlipidemia presented to the hospital with complaints of heart palpitations, nausea, and right-sided chest pain radiating to right arm. She woke up in the morning feeling a burning sensation in the center of her chest radiating to the right arm lasting 20 min, neck and back associated with nausea. Her recent diagnosis of hyperthyroidism one-month prior prompted initiation of methimazole 5 mg daily, however, she had discontinued the medication after 2 weeks as she was experiencing vaginal bleeding likely secondary to uterine fibroids and incorrectly attributed this temporally to her methimazole.

On physical examination, her heart rate was 120 beats/min and regular, blood pressure was 152/85, temperature was 97.7 Fahrenheit, and respiratory rate was 18 respirations/minute on room air. Thyroid examination revealed 2 non-tender palpable nodules on the right upper and middle lobe, with normal movement upon swallowing. She appeared in mild distress and had multiple ice packs on her skin due to complaints of heat intolerance. She was alert and oriented without ophthalmopathy but demonstrated lid lag upon lowering her eyes from upward gaze to lower gaze. Her lungs were clear to auscultation. Cardiac exam revealed tachycardia with regular rhythm without rubs, murmurs or gallops. Abdomen was non-tender to palpation and non-distended in all four quadrants with normal bowel sounds and no organomegaly. There was no pitting edema.

Her electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed normal sinus rhythm, normal axis, evidence of left atrial enlargement and T-wave inversions in leads aVL, I, V5, and V6 (Fig. 1). Serial troponin levels were noted to be 193 ng/L, 290 ng/L (3 h later), and 351 ng/L (normal ≤22 ng/L). Further tests revealed elevated free thyroxine (fT4) of 6.57 ng/dL (normal <1.7 ng/dL) and increased total triiodothyronine (T3) of >600 ng/d (normal <200 ng/dL), and undetectable thyroid stimulating hormone (normal >0.27 uIU/mL). Complete blood count, liver function tests, blood urea nitrogen, and serum creatinine were within normal limits. Transthoracic echocardiogram revealed reduced ejection fraction (35–40 %), mid to apical anterior/septal/inferior/lateral wall hypokinesis and moderate apical aneurysm without pericardial effusion, and basilar hyperkinesis in the left ventricle. Chest X-ray findings did not reveal evidence of pulmonary edema nor cardiomegaly. A thyroid ultrasound performed 1 month prior had shown an enlarged and inflamed gland with several sub-centimeter nodules consistent with Grave’s disease.

Due to concern of NSTEMI she was given a loading dose of aspirin and clopidogrel. She was started on a heparin drip, atorvastatin, and low dose metoprolol. Goal directed medical therapy (GDMT) was initiated with angiotensin receptor/neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI) and sodium glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT-2i). She was also immediately restarted on methimazole 20 mg every 8 h, and potassium iodide drops were initiated. By the third day, lab results revealed elevated thyroid stimulating immunoglobulin (TSI) and thyrotropin receptor antibodies (TraB) confirming Grave’s disease. The patient’s clinical symptoms improved and total T3 and fT4 also improved. By day six, she had complete resolution of her symptoms and her fT4 was noted to be 3.01 ng/dL (normal 0.93–1.7 ng/dL). Potassium iodide drops were discontinued and methimazole was also decreased from 20 mg three times a day to 20 mg twice a day. The patient was planned for elective cardiac catheterization after her clinical symptoms subsided to prevent iodine-induced thyrotoxicosis from contrast. The patient underwent elective left heart cardiac catheterization about 2 weeks later, which revealed left ventricular ejection fraction of 60 % and non-obstructive coronary artery disease.

Discussion

Our 54-year-old female with medical history of hypertension, uncontrolled hyperthyroidism, and extensive family history of coronary artery disease and hyperlipidemia presented with atypical chest pain found and demonstrated to have multiple comorbidities including thyrotoxicosis, takotsubo cardiomyopathy, 6 and NSTEMI. Diagnostic criteria for takotsubo cardiomyopathy is controversial in such a clinical setting. However, researchers at the Mayo Clinic proposed diagnostic criteria in 2004, which have been modified recently (Table 1); our patient meets these updated criteria. Our patient’s Burch-Wartofsky point scale score for thyrotoxicosis was 25–35, suggestive of impending thyroid storm7 (Table 2). Additionally, her pre-test probability for coronary artery disease, based on age, gender, and presentation, placed her at intermediate risk. Her Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) and Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) scores for ACS were both low, and this supported our decision to defer emergent cardiac catheterization and instead pursue an elective approach after stabilizing her life-threatening cardiac complications from severe thyrotoxicosis.

Notably, her presentation included new T-wave inversions in anterolateral leads along with a modest troponin elevation from 193 to 290 ng/L (normal ≤22 ng/L) and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). These findings were consistent with underlying acute coronary syndrome, possibly a Type 2 NSTEMI, due to an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand. Studies have shown that there’s increased mortality and morbidity associated with Type 2 MI when compared to Type 1MI8,9 (Table 3). Due to our patient’s non-significant cardiovascular risk factors, MI with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) may also be a suitable diagnosis, which is associated with female sex, younger age (<55), genetics and physiological stress.10 Both MINOCA and type 2 MI are not benign,8–10 which poses the question of whether hyperthyroidism should be a significant risk factor for ACS with MINOCA.

Although thyrotoxicosis-induced myocardial infarction is rare, cases have been reported and have mostly been associated with Grave’s disease.11–13 Coronary angiography in these cases often reveals nonobstructive coronary artery disease, with coronary artery spasm occupying the majority of the existing data.13 Coexisting severely obstructive atherosclerosis, however, have also been seen.13

The mechanism of vasospastic angina may involve enhanced vasoconstriction in response to catecholamines and sympathetic a-adrenergic receptor stimulation. Calcium channel blockers (CCBs) are the first line treatment for vasospastic angina for its smooth muscle cell vasodilatory effect, whereas nonselective beta blockers are avoided due to beta 2 blockade induced vasoconstriction. On the contrary, esmolol (beta 1 blocker) or propranolol (beta 1 and 2 blocker) are one of the first line treatments for thyrotoxicosis to inhibit T4 to T3 conversion by inhibiting type 1 deiodinases through beta 1 blockade.14,15 Further research should investigate whether adjunctive dihydro-CCBs may be useful in thyrotoxicosis-induced chest pain for its antichronotropic and anti-vasospastic effects.

Our patient also exhibited evidence of takotsubo cardiomyopathy, which is clinically indistinguishable from ACS.16 Treatment is the same as heart failure until resolution.17 Although thyrotoxicosis-induced Takotsubo cardiomyopathy is also rare, cases have been reported in literature.18,19 This poses an interesting question since thyrotoxicosis has also been associated with high output failure.20 This may warrant further studies to investigate why some patients with thyrotoxicosis develop takotsubo cardiomyopathy while others develop high output failure.

Conclusion

This case emphasizes the importance of early recognition and multidisciplinary management in patients with severe hyperthyroidism and anginal symptoms. The development of takotsubo cardiomyopathy and ACS in the setting of thyrotoxicosis illustrates the profound impact that thyroid hormone dysregulation can have on the heart.21 Although thyroid-induced cardiac chest pain can be a diagnostic challenge, especially in female patients with atypical symptoms, our patient’s presentation met criteria for probable takotsubo syndrome, type 2 myocardial infarction NSTEMI, and MINOCA. Comprehensive management, including the use of antithyroid drugs, beta blockers, and close monitoring of cardiac and thyroid function, is crucial in these high-risk patients. This case also highlights the need for careful consideration of the risks of invasive cardiac procedures in the context of hyperthyroidism and the importance of a team-based approach involving both cardiology and endocrinology.
