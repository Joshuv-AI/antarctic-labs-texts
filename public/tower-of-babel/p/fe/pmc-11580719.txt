The role of polo‐like kinases 2 in the proteasomal and lysosomal degradation of alpha‐synuclein in neurons
Authors: Chun Chau Sung, Wai Yun Lam, Kenny K K Chung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson's disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder caused by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the brain stem. PD is mostly sporadic, but familial PD (FPD) cases are recorded in different studies. The first gene mutation that is linked to FPD is α‐synuclein (α‐syn). It was then found that α‐syn is also accumulated in Lewy body (LB), a classical pathological hallmark in PD patients. Different studies have shown that α‐syn accumulation and aggregation can be a crucial factor contributing to the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in PD. α‐syn has been found to be degraded by the ubiquitin proteasomal system (UPS) and autophagy–lysosomal pathway (ALP). In this study, we initially explored how α‐syn phosphorylation by GRK6, PLK2 and CK2α would facilitate its degradation in relation to the UPS or ALP. Unexpectedly, we found that the degradation of α‐syn through PLK2 phosphorylation could be modulated by UPS and ALP in a novel mechanism. Specially, attenuation of UPS could increase the amount of PLK2 and then could facilitate the phosphorylation and degradation of α‐syn through ALP. To test this further in vivo, we attenuate the proteasomal activity in a well‐established A53T α‐syn transgenic PD mouse model. We found that attenuation of proteasomal activity in the A53T α‐syn transgenic mice could reduce the accumulation of α‐syn in the striatum and midbrain. Based on our results, this study provides a new insight into how α‐syn is degraded through the UPS and ALP.Keywords: autophagy–lysosomal pathway, phosphorylation, protein degradation, ubiquitin‐proteasomal system, α‐synuclein

INTRODUCTION

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder that is caused by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the midbrain specifically in the substantia nigra par compacta (SNc). 1 , 2 , 3 This degeneration can then lead to movement impairments in PD patients. 1 , 2 , 3 PD is mostly sporadic, but familial PD (FPD) cases are recorded in different genetic linkage studies and the first gene mutation that is linked to FPD is α‐synuclein (α‐syn). 4 α‐syn is a pre‐synaptic protein first identified in electric eel 5 and it was later found that α‐syn is highly expressed in young bird during the process of learning to sing from adult. 6 After the initial study that showed α‐syn's linkage to FPD, it was later reported that α‐syn is also accumulated in Lewy body (LB), a classical pathological hallmark in PD patients. 7 LB is characterized as intraneuronal ubiquitin positive protein aggregate that is commonly observed in PD patients. 1 , 2 , 7 It has long been suggested that understanding how LB is formed will provide important insights into the pathogenesis of PD.

The fact that α‐syn is the major component of LB suggested that studying the pathways involving in α‐syn physiological function or metabolism will unravel the pathogenic mechanism behind PD. This notion is further consolidated by the findings that additional α‐syn point mutations or gene triplication are linked to other cohorts of FPD patients. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 The study that gene triplication of α‐syn can cause FPD indicates that the expression levels or metabolism of α‐syn might be important contributors to the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in PD. 8 , 10 Different studies have suggested that α‐syn protein aggregation might be a crucial factor in causing the dopaminergic neuronal degeneration in PD. 12 , 13 Several factors have shown to modulate the metabolism of α‐syn and affect its tendency to aggregate. For instance, increase expression of α‐syn as shown by gene linkage study can cause FPD. 8 Other studies have suggested that the degradation of α‐syn by the proteolytic systems would also be an important component in the pathogenesis of PD. 13 , 14 α‐syn has been reported to be degraded by the ubiquitin‐proteasomal system (UPS) and the autophagy–lysosomal pathway (ALP). 15 , 16 However, how these two systems could regulate and coordinate the degradation of α‐syn have not been conclusive with various studies. 12 , 14

Another component regarding metabolism of α‐syn is post‐translational modification (PTM). α‐syn has been reported to be glycosylated, nitrated, and phosphorylated and these PTM have been shown to enrich in LB, supporting their importance in the neurodegeneration in PD. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 One of the α‐syn PTM that have extensively studies is the phosphorylation of α‐syn at ser129. 17 , 20 Phosphorylation of α‐syn at ser129 (pSer129 α‐syn) has been shown to be enriched in the LB and suggested that pS129 α‐syn could contribute to the LB formation and hence pathogenesis in PD. 20 , 21 , 22 Several kinases family such as G‐protein‐coupled receptor kinases (GRKs), polo‐like kinases (PLKs) and casein kinases (CKs) have been found to phosphorylated α‐syn. 14 , 20 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 For instance, GRK5 and GRK6 mediated pSer129 phosphorylation of α‐syn has been found to enhance its membrane association and uptake of dopamine. 27 CK2 has been reported to be the major kinase in the brain that can phosphorylate α‐syn at pSer129 and also facilitate its aggregation in vitro. 28 , 29 Interestingly, PLK2 has been shown to promote pSer129 α‐syn phosphorylation and degradation through ALP, and it can suppress α‐syn induced neurotoxicity. 30 , 31

In this study, we initially explored how GRK6, PLK2 and CK2α, the most studied kinases that target α‐syn, would phosphorylate and facilitated its degradation in related to the UPS or ALP. Interestingly, from our results, we found that the metabolism of α‐syn through PLK2 phosphorylation could be modulated by UPS and ALP in a novel mechanism. Specifically, attenuation of UPS could increase the amount of PLK2. This could then increase the phosphorylation of α‐syn and facilitate α‐syn degradation by the ALP. We test this in vivo in a PD transgenic mouse model and found that moderate attenuation of the proteasomal system could reduce the accumulation of α‐syn in neuronal cultures and in the midbrain of a well‐established A53T α‐syn transgenic mouse model. Our study provides a new insight into how α‐syn phosphorylation is related to its degradation by the UPS and ALP.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals, antibodies, and plasmids

All chemicals were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) unless otherwise specified. Antibodies used for Western blotting: mouse anti‐β‐actin (Sigma‐Aldrich Cat# A5441, RRID:AB_476744, 1:10 000), rabbit anti‐α‐syn (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Cat# sc‐7011‐R, RRID:AB_2192953, 1:2000), rabbit anti‐pSer129‐α‐syn (Abcam Cat# ab168381, RRID:AB_2728613, 1:2000), rabbit anti‐PLK2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# PA5‐79832, RRID:AB_2746947, MA, 1:500), mouse anti‐FLAG (Sigma‐Aldrich Cat# F3165, RRID:AB_259529, 1:1000), goat anti‐Mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody, HRP (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# 31430, RRID:AB_228307, 1:10000), and goat anti‐Rabbit IgG (H+L) secondary antibody, HRP (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# 31460, RRID:AB_228341, 1:10 000). pRK5‐α‐syn, pRK5‐A30P α‐syn, pRK5‐E46K α‐syn, pRK5‐A53T α‐syn, pCMV‐Tag2B‐CK2α, pCMV‐Tag2B‐GRK6 were generated previously. pCMV‐Tag2B‐PLK2, pRK5‐α‐syn‐S129A, pRK5‐α‐syn‐A30P‐S129A, pRK5‐α‐syn‐E46K‐S129A, pRK5‐α‐syn‐A53T‐S129A, pRK5‐HA‐PLK2, pRK5‐HA‐PLK2‐K111R‐N210A (PLK2 KDM) were constructed by amplifying existing plasmid template by PCR and cloned into the pRK5 vector. All clones were verified by Sanger sequencing (BGI, Shenzhen, China).

Cell culture, transfection, and treatments

HEK293T and SH‐SY5Y cells (ATCC) were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2 atmosphere in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) supplied with 10% v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% v/v penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA). Transient transfection of plasmids was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. 48 h after transfection, the cells were washed with ice cold PBS 3 times before lysed in lysis buffer (PBS supplied with 1% v/v Triton‐X100, 1 mM leupeptin, 1 mM aprotinin, 1 mM benzamidine, 10 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 10 mM sodium fluoride, and 2 mM sodium orthovanadate). For drug treatments, 24 h after transfection, the cells were treated with 5 μM MG132, 10 μg/mL PSI, or 25 mM NH4Cl or control for another 24 h before cell lysis. The cell lysate was then centrifuged at 14 800 g for 30 min at 4°C and the supernatant was collected as triton‐soluble fraction. The protein concentration of supernatant was measured with Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) before subjecting to Western blot analysis. For immunoblotting with α‐syn and/or pSer129‐α‐syn, the membranes were fixed with 4% w/v paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 30 min at room temperature with rotation, then washed with TBST 3 times before blocking with 5% w/v milk in TBST. 32

Mass spectrometry

SH‐SY5Y cells were transfected with pRK5‐α‐syn, with pCMV‐Tag2B, pCMV‐Tag2B‐CK2α, pCMV‐Tag2B‐GRK6, or pCMV‐Tag2B‐PLK2. Two days post‐transfection, cells were lysed with 8M urea in 0.1M Tris pH 8.5 supplied with 10 mM sodium fluoride and 2 mM sodium orthovanadate. After 30 min, the cells were sonicated and centrifuged at 14 800 g for 30 min at 4°C. Protein concentrations were measured with Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit. One milligram of cell lysates was reduced in 20 mM dithionthreitol (DTT) for 10 min at 95°C, and alkylated with 40 mM iodoacetamide for 30 min at room temperature protected from light. The alkylation reaction was quenched by adding DTT solution to a final concentration of 10 mM. NH4HCO3 (50 mM) was added to the cell lysates to dilute the urea to a final concentration of 0.8 M. A quantity of 10 μg of Pierce Trypsin Protease, MS Grade (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were added and allowed to digest for overnight at 37°C. After digestion, the peptides were dried with vacuum centrifugation and resuspended in 0.1% v/v trifluoroacetic acid before desalted with Pierce Peptide Desalting Spin Columns (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to manufacturer's instructions. Phosphopeptides were then enriched with High‐Select Fe‐NTA Phosphopeptide Enrichment Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to manufacturer's instruction. A quantity of 200 ng of the enriched phosphopeptides were then analyzed with Bruker timsTOF Pro Mass‐spectrometer.

Autophagy reporter cell line

HEK293T cells stably expressing the autophagy reporter GFP‐LC3‐RFP‐LC3ΔG was generated as previously described. 33 Briefly, HEK293T cells were transfected with pMRX‐IP‐GFP‐LC3‐RFP‐LC3ΔG plasmid (RRID:Addgene_84572). Successfully transfected cells were selected by 25 μg/mL puromycin for 14 days. Two hundred cells were then plated on a 10 cm tissue culture dish and allowed to grow into single colonies. Single clones with proper integration of the GFP‐LC3‐RFP‐LC3ΔG fragment into the genome were confirmed by the genomic PCR using primers 5′‐CGCCGCCGGGATCACTCTCG‐3′ and 5′‐CCACCACACTGGGATCCTTA‐3′. A 1533 bp GFP‐LC3‐RFP‐LC3ΔG fragment was produced and the expressions of GFP and RFP were observed under fluorescence microscope. After transfection of α‐syn and/or PLK2 or PLK2 KDM, the cells were harvested with TrypLE express and washed twice with DMEM supplied with 10% v/v FBS and 1% v/v P/S. Exactly 10 000 cells were then analyzed with Aria IIIu (BD Life Sciences, Ashland, OR) equipped with 488 and 561 nm lasers. Fluorescence intensities of the GFP and RFP channels were measured to evaluate autophagic flux. After expression, the GFP‐LC3 and RFP‐LC3ΔG will be split. Upon autophagy, GFP‐LC3 will be degraded while RFP‐LC3ΔG will be retained in the cytosol serving as an internal control. Hence, a decrease in GFP/RFP fluorescence signal indicates an increase in autophagy events. 33 The flow cytometry results were analyzed using FlowJo™ v10.8 Software (BD Life Sciences, Ashland, OR).

Neuronal cultures and treatments

All animal experiments were conducted according to relevant national and international guidelines. The animal protocols used have been reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (approval number A19035). C57BL/6 wild type (WT), A53T α‐syn transgenic line M83 (JAX strain #004479, RRID:IMSR_JAX:004479) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). Homozygous A53T α‐syn transgenic mice were mated with WT mice to produce hemizygous embryos. Primary cortical neuron from mice were prepared from embryonic day 16.5 (E16.5) embryos. Briefly, E16.5 embryos were decapitated, and the cortexes were collected and digested with TrypLE express (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) for 15 min in 37°C with gentle agitations. 0.1 mg/mL DNase I, 2.5 mM MgCl2, and 0.5 mM CaCl2 were then added and incubated for 5 min. The digested cortexes were allowed to settle and washed with DMEM twice before trituration with fire polished fine glass Pasteur pipette. The dispersed neurons were then centrifuged at 300 g for 7 min before washing with DMEM +10% v/v FBS twice. Cortical neurons were resuspended in Neurobasal medium (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplied with 2% v/v B27 supplement and 1% v/v GlutaMAX then plated in poly‐L‐lysine coated tissue culture plates at 5 × 106 cells/mL. Medium was changed at day in vitro (DIV) 7, and on daily basis from DIV 8. At DIV14, neurons were treated with 1 μM MG132, 0.075 μg/mL PSI, or vehicle control. 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 h post treatment, the neurons were washed and lysed as previously described and subjected to Western Blot analysis. Cell viabilities of the neurons were assayed after 8 h of control PBS, 1 μM MG132, or 0.075 μg/mL PSI treatment by double staining of 5 μM Hoechst 33342 and 5 μg/mL propidium iodide (PI) for 10 min. Fluorescent images were analyzed by Fiji (Version 2.9.0/1.53 t). Cell viabilities were determined by the percentage of Hoeschst 33342‐positive‐PI‐negative cells.

Generation of mice for aging and transgenic study

Hemizygous A53T α‐syn transgenic mice were crossed to obtain hemizygous A53T α‐syn transgenic mice. The offspring were then genotyped by quantitative PCR according to the protocol provided by The Jackson Laboratory. For PSI injection, littermates were aged to 3 months and randomly selected to receive either 100 μL of 30 μg/kg PSI or PBS subcutaneous injection every other day for a total of 4 weeks. This dosage was determined from the in vitro neuronal culture data, and an approximate blood volume of 80 μL/g mouse. After injections, the mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation and the brain were harvested. Olfactory bulb, hippocampus, striatum, cerebral cortex, mid brain, and cerebellum were dissected for Western Blot analysis. The tissues were weighed and lysed at a concentration of 10 mg/μL lysis buffer by sonication. The tissue lysate was then centrifuged at 14 800 g for 30 min at 4°C and the supernatant was collected as triton‐soluble fraction. The pellet was washed with ice cold PBS twice and sonicated with the same volume of SDS solubilization buffer (lysis buffer supplied with 2% w/v SDS, 1% w/v sodium deoxycholate and 1% v/v NP‐40). The lysate was then centrifuged at 14 800 g for 30 min at 4°C and the supernatant was collected as SDS‐soluble fraction. Both fractions were subjected to Western blot analysis. For immunoblotting with α‐syn and/or pSer129‐α‐syn, the membranes were fixed with 0.4% PFA in PBS for 30 min at room temperature with rotation.

Statistical analyses

Data were expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical significances were determined by Student's t‐test or one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post‐hoc test with Graphpad Prism 9.5.

RESULTS

Phosphorylation and degradation of α‐syn mediated by PLK2, GRK6, and CK2α

To assess the role of PLK2, GRK6 and CK2α in the phosphorylation and degradation of α‐syn, we transfected fixed amount of α‐syn with the incremental level of PLK2, GRK6, and CK2α in HEK293T cells to determine how these kinases would affect the degradation of α‐syn. We used the same flag‐tagged expression construct in these kinases and therefore they should have same effect on the phosphorylation and degradation of α‐syn. Interestingly, even with the same construct, the expression levels of PLK2, GRK6, and CK2α were different, suggesting that they might have different rate of degradation in the cells (Figure 1A). As expected, expression of PLK2, GRK6, or CK2α could promote the degradation of α‐syn in a dose dependent manner (Figure 1B). We had also used mass spectrometry (MS) to confirm that co‐transfection of PLK2, GRK6, or CK2α enhanced the phosphorylation of α‐syn at pSer129 (Table 1). To further determine how the UPS and ALP could affect the degradation of α‐syn by these kinases, we expressed PLK2, GRK6, or CK2α with or without proteasomal inhibitor (MG132) or ALP inhibitor (3MA) and determine how these treatments would affect the degradation of α‐syn (Figure 1C,D). With the treatment of ALP inhibitor 3MA in cells expressing PLK2, GRK6, or CK2α, degradation of α‐syn were significantly inhibited when compared to control (Figure 1C,D). Interestingly, treatment of proteasomal inhibitor did not affect the GRK6‐ or CK2α‐mediated degradation of α‐syn as compared to control (Figure 1C,D). In contrast, in cells expressing PLK2, the degradation of α‐syn was significantly enhanced in the treatment of proteasomal inhibitor MG132 (Figure 1C,D). We therefore aimed to further elucidated how the degradation of α‐syn through PLK2 could be modulated by both the UPS and ALP.

Attenuation of proteasomal activity facilitates the PLK2‐mediated degradation of α‐syn through the autophagy pathway

To determine how the degradation of α‐syn mediated by PLK2 might be affected by proteasomal inhibitors (MG132 and PSI) or autophagy inhibitor NH4Cl, we co‐transfected SH‐SY5Y cells with PLK2 and α‐syn and treated them with either proteasomal or autophagy inhibitors. We switched to SH‐SY5Y cells to confirm our observation was not cell‐line dependent. Like our previous result section (Figure 1C,D), treatment of proteasomal inhibitors could significantly reduce the accumulation of α‐syn in the cells (Figure 2A,B). Treatment of autophagy inhibitor resulted in the increase accumulation of α‐syn, but it was not significant (Figure 2A,B). We also observed an increase in pSer129 α‐syn in cells treated with proteasomal inhibitors and decreased in pSer129 α‐syn in cells treated with autophagy inhibitor, although these results were not significant (Figure 2A,C). For PLK2, we found that there was a significant increase in PLK2 treated with proteasomal inhibitors (Figure 2A,D). Treatment of autophagy inhibitor did not affect the level of PLK2 (Figure 2A,D). These results suggested that attenuation of proteasomal activity could lead to the accumulation of PLK2 and facilitate the degradation of α‐syn through autophagy. To test this hypothesis, we employed an autophagy flux reporter 33 to determine if the expression of α‐syn alone or co‐expression of PLK2 could affect the autophagy flux in cells. In agreement with our hypothesis, the expression of α‐syn could increase the autophagy flux and it could be further enhanced by the co‐expression of PLK2 (Figure 2E–G). The effect of co‐expression of PLK2 was reversed if PLK2 kinase dead mutant (KDM) was used in the experiment suggesting that the enhanced autophagy flux was dependent on the kinase activity of PLK2 (Figure 2E–G).

PLK2 facilitated the degradation of α‐syn and is dependent on the phosphorylation of pSer129 α‐syn in different α‐syn mutants

As we were interested in the role of PLK2 in the degradation of α‐syn, we tested if PLK2 could phosphorylate and facilitate WT or α‐syn mutants degradation in the same extend. We therefore transfected WT, A30P, E46K or A53T α‐syn with or without PLK2 in SH‐SY5Y cells and determined the levels of α‐syn (Figure 3A,B). As expected, co‐expression of PLK2 and WT or different mutants of α‐syn could facilitate the phosphorylation and the degradation of α‐syn (Figure 3A,B). We further confirmed that this was dependent on the phosphorylation of S129 by performing similar experiments with S129A WT or different mutants of α‐syn (Figure 3C,D). To test this further, we performed similar experiments with WT or A53T α‐syn with WT or kinase dead domain (KDM) of PLK2 and confirmed that the PKL2‐facilitated degradation of α‐syn was dependent on PLK2's kinase activity (Figure 3E,F).

Attenuation of proteasomal activity enhances the accumulation of PLK2 and facilitates the degradation of α‐syn in cortical neurons of an A53T transgenic mouse model of PD

To test if attenuation of proteasomal activity could facilitate the degradation of α‐syn in the PLK2 mediated pathway, we treated cortical neurons derived from an A53T transgenic mouse model of PD with attenuation of proteasomal activity by MG132 or PSI (Figure 4A–G). We chased the levels of α‐syn, pSer129 α‐syn and PLK2 in a period of 8 h. We found that moderate treatment of MG132 or PSI in cortical neurons reduced the levels of α‐syn and increased the amount of pSer129 α‐syn (Figure 4A–C,E–F). This was also accompanied with an increased in the levels of PLK2 in both treatments of MG132 and PSI (Figure 4A,D,G). These results suggested that attenuation of proteasomal activity by low dose of MG132 or PSI could facilitate the degradation of α‐syn in a PLK2 dependent manner. We also had determined if the attenuation of proteasomal activity in these cortical neuronal cultures could affect their cell viability and found that their viability was not affected in our treatment (Figure 4H,I).

Attenuation of proteasomal activity reduces the accumulation of α‐syn in the mid‐brain and striatum of an A53T transgenic mouse model of PD

To test our hypothesis that attenuation of proteasomal activity could reduce the accumulation of α‐syn in vivo, we treated a well‐established A53T α‐syn transgenic mouse model of PD with low dosage of PSI for 4 weeks and then analyzed the accumulation of α‐syn in different areas of the brain in the triton soluble and the SDS insoluble fractions of the brain tissues. In the triton soluble fractions, we found that treatment of low dose of PSI significantly reduced the accumulation of α‐syn in striatum and mid brain (Figure 5A,B). For pSer129 α‐syn, we only observed a significant increase in olfactory bulb after the treatment (Figure 5A,C). For PLK2, we observed a significant increase in olfactory bulb, striatum, and mid brain after the PSI treatment (Figure 5A,D). For the SDS insoluble fractions, we were able to observe a significant reduction of α‐syn in cortex, striatum, and mid brain (Figure 6A,B). We were not able to detect the levels of pSer129 α‐syn and PLK2 in the SDS insoluble fractions by Western blot, possibly because of the young age in these mice (3‐month‐old) as accumulation of pSer129 α‐syn was shown to be more prominent in aged mammals. 34 , 35 Taken together, these results supported that the moderate attenuation of proteasomal activity could reduce the accumulation of α‐syn in the A53T α‐syn transgenic mouse model of PD.

DISCUSSION

Since the initial discovery that mutation in α‐syn can cause FPD and its accumulation in LB, the classical pathological hallmarks of PD, intensive studies have been focusing on how it is being degraded. 12 , 14 Different studies have suggested that α‐syn could be degraded by both UPS and ALP, and abnormal accumulation of α‐syn through the dysfunction of these pathways could contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of PD. 12 , 13 , 14 Initial finding has suggested that α‐syn can be targeted by the UPS for degradation. 14 , 15 Later reports have shown that α‐syn can also be degraded by the chaperone‐mediated autophagy (CMA). 14 , 16 , 36 Additional studies have suggested that α‐syn could be degraded through the autophagy pathway in association with its phosphorylation by different kinases. 12 , 14

In this study, we explored the role of α‐syn phosphorylation in related to its degradation in association with the UPS and ALP. α‐syn is known to be phosphorylated by different kinases and phosphorylation of α‐syn at S129 has been linked to its degradation and LB formation. 12 , 13 , 14 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 We first explored how different kinases that have been shown to phosphorylate α‐syn at pSer129 could affect its degradation as in other studies. 12 , 14 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 31 As expected, PLK2, GRK6 and CK2α all could enhance the phosphorylation and degradation of α‐syn, however, we found that co‐expression of PLK2 could significantly enhanced the phosphorylation of α‐syn and its degradation in comparison to GRK6 and CK2α. One important observation we found in our study was that attenuation of proteasomal activity could enhance the degradation of α‐syn in the co‐expression of PLK2. These results prompted us to study the relationship between UPS and ALP in the degradation of α‐syn through PLK2. From our cellular and neuronal experiments, we found that the attenuation of proteasomal activity could increase PLK2 levels and facilitate the degradation of α‐syn through the ALP. To test this further in vivo, we treated a well‐established A53T α‐syn transgenic mouse model of PD to attenuate the proteasomal activity by low dose of proteasomal inhibitor PSI. We found that this treatment could significantly increase the PLK2 levels and reduced the levels of α‐syn in the soluble fractions in the striatum and mid‐brain. Our in vitro and in vivo results suggest that UPS and ALP could modulate the degradation of α‐syn in different levels. For instance, we found that PLK2 through the phosphorylation of α‐syn could promote the degradation of α‐syn through the ALP which has been shown in other studies. 12 , 14 , 30 , 31 From our study, PLK2 is prone to the degradation by the UPS, suggesting that increased PLK2 levels through proteasomal activity attenuation could facilitate the degradation of α‐syn through the ALP.

Our results in this study have shown that moderate attenuation of proteasomal activity could facilitate the degradation of α‐syn. This finding might pose a contradictory notion that dysfunction of UPS is one the major culprits behind the pathogenesis of PD. However, we will need to factor in other contributors such as genetic and aging that can affect UPS and ALP which can tip the balance in these systems in the degradation of α‐syn in association with PD. Our results show that the UPS and ALP are working in a more intricate way in the degradation of aggregation prone protein like α‐syn. Hence targeting one of these system in the development of therapies in neurodegenerative disorder like PD might require a more careful consideration of how attenuation of UPS might also affect ALP. This is particular important as increasing evidence has suggested that the ALP dysfunction that led to protein aggregates accumulation, such as α‐syn, can contribute significantly in the pathogenesis of PD. 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40

Aging has been established to be a major contributing factor in the neurodegeneration of PD and it is well‐known that the dysfunction of UPS and ALP is also associated with aging. 41 , 42 Therefore, how the age‐dependent disruption of UPS and ALP function in related to the degradation of α‐syn will be of particular interest in the pathogenesis PD. According to our results in this study, if aging could lead to the dysfunction of both UPS and ALP, that could increase the accumulation of PLK2 and phosphorylation of α‐syn at pSer129. However, because of impaired ALP function in association with aging, that could result into the accumulation of pSer129 α‐syn which could subsequently lead to the formation of LB and dopaminergic neuronal degeneration in PD. Therefore, our study could provide a novel mechanism of how the UPS and ALP dysfunction in the degradation of α‐syn could contribute to PD along with aging. As we have not tested this hypothesis in this study, it will be interesting to determine if this notion is true in the future aging study.
