Large expansion of plasma commensal viruses is associated with SIV pathogenesis in Macaca leonina
Authors: Yanpeng Li, Tian-Zhang Song, Le Cao, Han-Dan Zhang, Yingying Ma, Ren-Rong Tian, Yong-Tang Zheng, Chiyu Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Human immunodeficiency virus-1 (HIV-1) infection disrupts the homeostatic equilibrium between the host and commensal microbes. However, the dynamic changes of plasma commensal viruses and their role in HIV/simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) pathogenesis are rarely reported. Here, we investigated the longitudinal changes of plasma virome, inflammation levels, and disease markers using an SIV-infected Macaca leonina model. Large expansions of plasma Anelloviridae, Parvoviridae, Circoviridae and other commensal viruses, and elevated levels of inflammation and D-dimer were observed since the chronic phase of SIV infection. Anelloviridae abundance appears to correlate positively with the CD4+ T cell count but negatively with SIV load especially at the acute phase, whereas other commensal viruses’ abundances show opposite correlations with the two disease markers. Antiretroviral therapy slightly reduces but does not substantially reverse the expansion of commensal viruses. Furthermore, 1387 primate anellovirus open reading frame 1 sequences of more than 1500 nucleotides were annotated. The data reveal different roles of commensal viruses in SIV pathogenesis.

INTRODUCTION

Human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) infection in humans and pathogenic simian immunodeficiency virus (pSIV) infection in macaques are characterized by chronic immune activation, systemic inflammation, and microbial translocation (1, 2). The mechanism is involved in massive CD4+ T cell depletion caused by active HIV-1/SIV replication, which results in the disruption of the integrity of the mucosal surface and subsequent microbial translocation. Translocation of bacteria and their products facilitates immune activation and inflammation, impairing the immune system (3). Furthermore, HIV-1/SIV invasion disrupts the homeostatic equilibrium between the host and coinfecting or commensal viruses, which is characterized by reactivation of persistent viruses and/or invasion by other viruses (4). The reason is that impaired immune system by HIV-1/SIV is unable to control the replication of coinfecting pathogenic viruses and prevent invasion by other viruses. Translocated bacterial products, as well as reactivated and newly infecting viruses, further activate the CD4+ T cells to provide more targets for HIV-1/SIV infection, leading to a vicious cycle to accelerate progression toward AIDS.

Interaction between HIV-1/SIV and coinfecting viruses affects and contributes to the pathogenesis of HIV-1/SIV infection. The expansions of adenovirus, picornavirus, and anellovirus in the gut were previously observed in HIV-infected individuals and pSIV-infected macaques, despite the fact that the longitudinal dynamic changes of the gut virome are much less detailed (5–8). Blood circulation system is another very important battlefield between HIV-1/SIV and the host; however, the interactions, especially the early interactions among HIV-1/SIV, commensal, and/or coinfecting eukaryotic viruses in blood and the host are less investigated (9, 10).

Previous cross-sectional studies have shown that HIV-1 infection results in an altered plasma virome, which was mainly characterized by the expansion of eukaryotic viruses (11–14). Although HIV/SIV-associated virome expansion was observed in both the blood and the gut, the involved eukaryotic viruses are markedly different, which is ascribed to various virome profiles in different tissues/organs (15). Different from the main contribution of adenovirus to the gut virome expansion, plasma virome expansion caused by HIV-1 infection is mainly driven by anelloviruses (11, 13, 14), which dominate the plasma virome (16). Furthermore, although most enrolled HIV-1–infected individuals were at the chronic infection phase or had developed AIDS, the cross-sectional studies revealed notable correlations of anellovirus abundance positively with HIV-1 viral load (VL), but negatively with the CD4+ T cell count (14, 17, 18), suggesting specific interactions of anelloviruses with HIV-1 and the host. However, the cross-sectional study design does not allow to reveal the dynamics of interactions among HIV-1, commensal viruses, and the host throughout infection, especially at the early stage when HIV-1 invades and actively replicates.

Under current 90-90-90 and even 95-95-95 policies, almost all newly diagnosed HIV-1–infected individuals are required to promptly receive antiretroviral therapy (ART). In China, about 92.8% people living with HIV-1 are receiving ART (19). Therefore, it is less likely to investigate the dynamics of virus-virus and virus-host interactions during natural HIV-1 infection without ART. The SIV-infected macaque model provides an ideal opportunity to simulate the HIV-1 infection process and further answer how HIV-1/SIV interacts with and affects the commensal/coinfecting viruses, and then how the latter influence disease progression and treatment outcome (20). We previously demonstrated that Macaca leonina is a unique Old World monkey susceptible to HIV-1 infection, and the SIV-infected M. leonina model can well reflect the hallmarks of HIV-infected individuals (21–23). Here, we performed a longitudinal study to investigate the dynamics of plasma virome, inflammation levels, and disease markers using the SIV-infected M. leonina model. We report a large expansion of plasma Anelloviridae as well as other commensal viruses such as Parvoviridae and Circoviridae caused by SIV infection and show different roles of these commensal viruses in SIV pathogenesis. The data reveal the complex interactions among SIV, plasma commensal viruses, and the host throughout SIV infection and subsequent ART.

RESULTS

SIVmac239-infected northern pig-tailed macaque model

A SIVmac239-infected nonhuman primate model was established with 11 northern pig-tailed macaques (NPMs; M. leonina) (Fig. 1A). ART was initiated at 125 days after the SIV challenge and maintained for 357 days. Various parameters, including SIV VL, the CD4+ and CD8+ T cell counts, main inflammatory cytokines, and T cell functions, were measured during SIV infection and subsequent ART. SIV VL rose rapidly and peaked on the 21st day after challenge (average 105.5 copies/ml, range 104.1 to 106.5), then rapidly declined to 103.1 copies/ml (range 101.0 to 104.7) on the 35th day, and remained relatively stable (102.9 to 102.6 copies/ml) until ART initiation (Fig. 1B). Accordingly, the CD4+ T cell count rapidly decreased from an average of 1344 cells/μl (range 719 to 1688 cells/μl) to 842 cells/μl (range 403 to 1755 cells/μl) on the 21st day after challenge, and then remained relatively stable (922 to 826 cells/μl) until ART initiation. According to the dynamics of both SIV VL and the CD4+ T cell count, the acute and chronic infection phases were defined as the first 35 days and 36 to 124 days of SIV infection, respectively. SIV VL rapidly decreased to below the detection limit of our assay after 78 days of ART and remained undetectable, whereas the CD4+ T cell count did not obviously increase during ART (Fig. 1B). As expected, plasma SIV VL significantly negatively correlated with the CD4+ T cell count regardless of which phase was involved (Fig. 1C), and the negative correlation was statistically significant during different phases of SIV infection (fig. S1A). The CD8+ T cell count shared a similar dynamic to the CD4+ T cell count during SIV infection and subsequent ART (fig. S1B).

Physical and biochemical parameters of these monkeys were measured (fig. S2). The body mass index (BMI) of these monkeys significantly declined after the SIV challenge, and ART did not change the decreasing trend in BMI. Total cholesterol and triglyceride in plasma and subcutaneous fat thickness in abdomen showed consistent decreasing trends in BMI. Accompanied by the decrease in BMI, the level of leptin progressively increased after the SIV challenge and even initiation of ART. Different from BMI and other parameters, the levels of glucose and insulin slightly increased after the SIV challenge, and ART initiation significantly reduced their levels.

The plasma virome of SIV-infected monkeys

To define the effect of SIV infection on commensal and/or coinfecting viruses in blood, we determined the global plasma virome from each monkey before and after the SIV challenge and during subsequent ART using next-generation sequencing (NGS). In total, 7133 million reads (range 2.0 to 51.5 million, median 25.6 million per sample) were obtained, and 341.4 million reads (range 4.4 to 63.5, median 28.9) were classified as viral sequences, accounting for 0.47% (range 0.0015 to 71.9%) of total reads. Approximately 94.7% of them were from eukaryotic virus families, including Anelloviridae, Parvoviridae, Retroviridae, Circoviridae, Flaviridae, Adenoviridae, etc. (Fig. 2A). Prokaryotic virus families including Autographiviridae, Siphoviridae, Myoviridae, Podoviridae, and Microviridae accounted for 5.1% and unclassified viral reads (mainly unclassified Caudovirales) accounted for 0.2% (Fig. 2A). Before the SIV challenge, Anelloviridae was the most abundant eukaryotic virus family (87.9%), followed by Parvoviridae (12.0%); during the acute phase of SIV infection, Retroviridae became the most abundant virus family (67.9%), followed by Anelloviridae (30.9%); Anelloviridae returned to being the most abundant virus during the chronic phase (77.5%) and subsequent ART (99.1%) (fig. S3A). For the prokaryotic virus families, we observed a gradual increase in the abundance of unclassified Caudovirales (from 10.9 to 41.4%) and a decrease in Autographiviridae (from 72.1 to 35.9%) following the course of SIV infection and ART (fig. S3B).

A large expansion of plasma virome at the chronic phase of SIV infection

We analyzed the dynamic changes in the plasma virome throughout SIV infection and subsequent ART. SIV infection obviously changed the plasma virome. The global viral abundance increased gradually in the first 28 days (4 weeks) of SIV infection, followed by a temporary decrease (days 35 to 49), and then sharply increased during the chronic phase with a peak on the 104th day (fig. S3C), indicating that SIV infection results in the expansion of plasma virome. ART initiation appeared to slightly decrease plasma viral abundance. The plasma virome richness showed a similar dynamic trend to viral abundance except for a further increase during ART (fig. S3D). Relative abundance analysis showed that about half of the plasma virome consisted of phages in these monkeys before the SIV challenge (fig. S3E). The SIV challenge substantially decreased the relative abundance of phages to about 0.9% at the chronic phase. These indicate that the expansion of plasma virome caused by SIV infection was mainly driven by eukaryotic viruses. We therefore focused on the eukaryotic virome in subsequent analyses.

Because eukaryotic viruses dominate the plasma virome, eukaryotic viral abundance exhibits an almost completely same dynamic trend as that of global virome throughout SIV infection and subsequent ART (Fig. 2, B and C). During the acute infection phase, eukaryotic viral abundance increased to a small peak on the 21st day after a slight decrease in the first 7 days of infection, and then decreased to near preinfection level. During the chronic phase, viral abundance sharply increased and peaked on the 104th day and slightly decreased since ART initiation (Fig. 2B). The richness and Shannon diversity of the eukaryotic virome shared similar dynamics to the viral abundance except that the diversity did not increase at the acute phase and the peak of richness appeared earlier than that of the abundance (at the chronic phase) (Fig. 2C).

The peak of the eukaryotic viral abundance at the acute phase completely overlaps with the peak of SIV VL. Relative abundance analysis showed that Anelloviridae is the most abundant virus family and dominates the plasma eukaryotic virome of the monkeys throughout SIV infection and subsequent ART, except at the acute infection phase when the virome was taken over by a transient expansion of SIV (Fig. 2, D and E). Therefore, the observed expansion of plasma eukaryotic virome at the acute infection phase was a result of massive SIV replication. Two stages of intense expansion (about four to five orders of magnitude) of the plasma eukaryotic virome were observed at the early (days 42 to 85) and late (days 91 to 104) chronic phase with a peak [about 5.2 × 105 reads per million (RPM)] on the 104th day of SIV infection (Fig. 2B). The large expansion of plasma virome was mainly driven by Anelloviridae. Apart from Anelloviridae, Parvoviridae and Circoviridae also contributed to the plasma virome expansion (Fig. 2E and table S2). The initiation of ART substantially decreased the viral abundance, but the expanded eukaryotic virome still remained at a very high level. On the other hand, the monkeys had a significant higher number of the eukaryotic virus families at the chronic phase and during ART than at the acute phase and before the SIV challenge (fig. S3F). In particular, more monkeys were found to be positive for Parvoviridae and Circoviridae, as well as Genomoviridae at the chronic phase compared to the acute phase and before the SIV challenge (table S2), indicating that SIV infection facilitates the reactivation and expansion of these eukaryotic viruses in the monkeys. The initiation of ART seemed not to prevent the expansion of these viruses (table S2).

The β-diversity analysis showed that the monkeys before the SIV challenge and at the acute infection phase shared similar eukaryotic virome community structures (fig. S3G), indicating that acute SIV infection did not significantly alter the plasma virome except SIV viremia. The eukaryotic virome community structure was significantly separated between the acute (and before infection) and chronic (and on ART) phases [Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), P = 0.001] (fig. S3G), implying that the change in plasma virome mainly occurred at the chronic phase. Furthermore, the virome community structure during ART was similar to that at the chronic phase, indicating that ART did not substantially reverse the altered virome caused by SIV infection.

The association of plasma anellome expansion with SIV infection

Because Anelloviridae was the most abundant plasma virus family dominating the virome, we further investigated monkey’s plasma anellome and its dynamics throughout SIV infection and subsequent ART. Most (98.3%) of the anellovirus sequences were annotated as members of the Alphatorquevirus genus (Fig. 3A). The Betatorquevirus genus accounted for only 0.5% and all other genera and unclassified sequences accounted for 1.2%. To further analyze the dynamics and evolution of anelloviruses, only the open reading frame 1 (ORF1) sequences longer than 1500 nucleotides (nt) were selected. In total, 1387 primate anellovirus ORF1 sequences were identified. More than half (744, 53.9%) of them shared an identity of <69% to the currently known anelloviruses, probably representing previously unreported anellovirus species. Phylogenetic analysis showed that these anellovirus ORF1 sequences formed four genetically distinct clades (Fig. 3B), and several previously identified Alphatorquevirus sequences (in purple) from nonhuman primates are distributed within the four clades, suggesting that all the primate anellovirus sequences in the four clades belong to the Alphatorquevirus genus. Although these four clades are annotated as Alphatorquevirus genus, they showed a relatively greater genetic heterogeneity and are evolutionarily distinct from the clades of human and other animals. The within-clade and between-clade genetic distances are 0.434 to 0.548 and 0.639 to 0.677 for the four primate anellovirus clades, respectively, slightly lower than those of three human anellovirus genera (0.572 to 0.646 and 0.71 to 0.737) (table S3). The four Alphatorquevirus clades have genetic distances of 0.674 to 0.765 to human and other animal anellovirus genera/clades.

We analyzed the dynamics of plasma anellome throughout SIV infection and subsequent ART. Compared to those before the SIV challenge, the abundance, richness, and Shannon diversity of the anellome obviously dropped and hit the bottom at the acute phase (days 14 to 28) and then sharply increased to and remained at a very high level at the chronic phase (Fig. 3, C and D). These results clearly indicate an anellome expansion at the chronic phase of SIV infection. Generally, plasma anellome abundance and richness at the chronic phase were significantly higher than those before the SIV challenge and the acute phase (P < 0.001), both of which did not significantly differ from each other (Fig. 3, E and F). ART initiation did not significantly reverse the expanded anellome; however, a transient decrease in anellovirus abundance was observed in almost all monkeys on the 8th day (Tre_8) of ART despite the fact that this drop was not statistically significant compared with those on the 0th and 20th day of ART (fig. S4A). The reason remains a mystery.

We further evaluated whether the abundance and/or richness of the plasma anellome can be used to discriminate the acute and chronic infections of SIV. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis showed that plasma anellome abundance and richness had area under the curve values of 0.975 and 0.999, respectively (P < 0.0005, fig. S4B). In view of a good correlation between anellovirus abundance by NGS and its VL by a universal quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay (r = 0.793, P < 0.0001, fig. S4C), a rapid qualification of plasma anellovirus by the qPCR assay might be helpful for determining the acute and chronic infection of HIV-1 if similar results were also observed in HIV-1–infected individuals.

The evolution of plasma anellome along its rapid expansion during the SIV pathogenesis

SIV infection results in the increase of plasma anellome abundance and diversity. To determine how the anellome expands and evolves throughout SIV infection, we further analyzed the anellome community structure. On the basis of a genetic similarity threshold of 95%, anelloviruses were classified into 464 lineages. The chronic phase and subsequent ART duration witnessed the enormous expansion of anellovirus lineages (Fig. 4A and fig. S4D). Of them, 97, 70, 427, and 439 lineages were identified before the SIV challenge, the acute and chronic infection phases, and during ART, respectively. Furthermore, the “unique lineages” that present in only one animal had a very low frequency at all stages, whereas more “shared lineages” by two or more animals appeared at the chronic phase and during ART (fig. S4E). Most lineages first appeared at the chronic phase (64.7%, 300/464), persisted during ART (93%, 279/300), and were widely distributed in almost all monkeys (fig. S4F). Furthermore, 58 lineages preexisting in healthy monkeys (before the SIV challenge) disappeared at the acute phase and reappeared at the chronic phase. Only 31 lineages newly appeared at the acute phase, and 30 of them persisted into the chronic phase. Most lineages detected during ART were derived from the chronic phase, and only 36 lineages newly emerged at this period (fig. S4F). Then, we further defined 169 core lineages that were shared by more than 70% of monkeys. Half (85, 50.3%) of them persisted throughout the chronic phase and subsequent ART, and 72 (42.6%) newly appeared during ART (fig. S4G). No core lineages were found in the monkeys before the SIV challenge and at the acute phase.

To reveal the population dynamics of aneloviruses, the presence and abundance of all lineages at each sampling time point from each monkey were analyzed (Fig. 4A and fig. S5). The longest persistence time of the lineages could reach up to 482 days during our sampling period. Only a small proportion of the lineages were detected before the SIV challenge, and most of them disappeared or had a decreased abundance until the acute phase of SIV infection. At the chronic phase, an enormous number of different lineages emerged and persisted in most if not all monkeys during the chronic and ART phases (Fig. 4A and fig. S5). Furthermore, most lineages experienced a tremendous increase in abundance during the chronic and ART phases (Fig. 4A, bottom). We further assess the dynamic changes of the top 50 lineages in absolute abundance over time (Fig. 4B). Except for a few resident lineages (lineages that existed before the SIV challenge) that could persist throughout SIV infection, most lineages newly appeared after SIV infection. An enormous increase in absolute abundances of these lineages was observed in the monkeys at the chronic phase. The combined abundance of the resident lineages averagely increased 1.2 orders of magnitude from preinfection (average 302 RPM, range 12.7 to 1910) to the chronic phase (4904 RPM, range 0.13 to 20,008), and the newly emerging lineages averagely increased 3.1 orders of magnitude from the acute phase (9.3 RPM, range 0 to 59.8) to the chronic phase (10,718 RPM, range 76.4 to 32,643). Together, these results indicate that SIV-associated plasma anellome expansion is driven by many newly emerged anellovirus lineages and the largely increased abundance.

Dynamics of plasma Parvoviridae and Circoviridae throughout SIV infection and subsequent ART

Because of the absolute predominance of Anelloviridae in plasma virome, the alteration of other virus families caused by SIV infection may be very small and be easily neglected. To see whether SIV infection also affects other eukaryotic viruses, we ruled out Anelloviridae and analyzed the dynamics of other main eukaryotic viruses. Similar to the trend of anellome, the chronic phase and subsequent ART duration witnessed an expansion of these virus families (Fig. 5A). Next, we zoomed in on Parvoviridae and Circoviridae, the second and third most abundant commensal virus families after Anelloviridae. The two virus families exhibited similar but slightly different dynamic changes throughout SIV infection and subsequent ART (Fig. 5B). Compared to that before the SIV challenge, no significant increase in absolute abundances of the two virus families was observed at the acute phase, whereas a significant increase was observed in Parvoviridae abundance at the chronic phase (Fig. 5C and fig. S6A), and in Circoviridae abundance during ART (Fig. 5D and fig. S6B). Accordingly, a higher positive rate of Parvoviridae was observed at the chronic phase and ART than before the SIV challenge and at the acute phase (Fig. 5E). Six genera were identified in Parvoviridae, and the number of the parvovirus genera obviously increased at the chronic and ART phases compared with those before the SIV challenge and at the acute phase (fig. S6A). An obviously increased abundance of these genera (mainly Dependoparvovirus) was also observed at the chronic phase and during ART (Fig. 6, A and C), and the increase in adeno-associated virus (AAV) in the Dependoparvovirus genus was accompanied by the increase in the abundance of a help adenovirus (fig. S6C). These results revealed the expansion of parvoviruses at the chronic phase, which is well consistent with the observation in anelloviruses (Fig. 4 and fig. S5). For Circoviridae, higher positive rate and abundance appeared in the samples and individuals during ART (Fig. 5F). We were not sure whether the expansion of Circoviridae was directly associated with ART or was due to the relatively short duration of the chronic phase.

Furthermore, we observed a short transient increase in pegivirus abundance at the acute phase (fig. S6, D and E). Human pegivirus belongs to the genus Pegivirus of the Flaviviridae family and has been well demonstrated to play a beneficial clinical role when coinfected with HIV-1, hepatitis C virus, and other viruses (24, 25). The origin and disappearance of the pegivirus remain largely mysterious.

Interaction of the plasma commensal viruses with SIV and the host

As mentioned above, SIV infection results in the expansion of various commensal viruses, implying that SIV disrupts the homeostatic equilibrium between the host and these commensal viruses. To further understand the interaction among SIV, commensal viruses, and the host, we plotted the longitudinal dynamics of several major commensal viruses with SIV load and the CD4+ T cell count. At the acute phase, a gradual decrease in Anelloviridae abundance was accompanied by an obvious increase in SIV load and a decrease in the CD4+ T cell count, and the decreasing trend of Anelloviridae abundance was completely reversed at the chronic phase where Anelloviridae abundance sharply increased and both SIV load and the CD4+ T cell count remained relatively stable (Fig. 6A and fig. S7). ART appeared to suppress SIV replication but not substantially change Anelloviridae abundance and the CD4+ T cell count. In general, the abundance of Anelloviridae appeared to be negatively associated with SIV load, but positively associated with the CD4+ T cell count, and the correlations were particularly obvious at the acute phase (P < 0.0001 and 0.036) (Fig. 6B). Distinct from Anelloviridae, the abundances of Parvoviridae were positively associated with SIV load (P = 0.046), but negatively associated with the CD4+ T cell count (P > 0.05). No obvious correlations were observed between the abundances of Circoviridae and both SIV load and the CD4+ T cell count (Fig. 6B).

We further investigated the correlations of Anelloviridae abundance with CD4+ T cell activation and exhaustion. Anelloviridae abundance showed a well-consistent dynamic trend to the proportion of the CD4+HLA-DR+CD38+ T cells at the acute phase, but an opposite trend at the chronic phase and during ART (Fig. 6C). Therefore, Anelloviridae abundance had a positive correlation to the proportion of the CD4+CD38+HLA-DR T cells before the SIV challenge and at the acute phase, but a significantly negative correlation at the chronic phase and during ART (Fig. 6, C and G). It is not surprising that the correlation of Anelloviridae abundance to the CD4+HLA-DR+CD38+ T cells is completely opposite to that of SIV load. There is no obvious dynamic relationship for both Anelloviridae abundance and SIV load with the proportion of CD4+PD-1+ T cells (Fig. 6D). Except for a negative correlation between Parvoviridae abundance and the proportion of the CD4+HLA-DR+CD38+ T cells during ART (P < 0.01), no obvious correlations were observed for the abundances of Parvoviridae and Circoviridae with the proportions of the two CD4+ T cell subsets throughout SIV infection (Fig. 6, E to G).

Longitudinal dynamics of proinflammatory cytokines, as well as sCD14 and D-dimer throughout SIV infection

We measured 10 proinflammatory cytokines to assess the level of systematic inflammation during SIV infection and ART. Substantially elevated levels of interleukin-5 (IL-5), IL-6, tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α), and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) were observed in these monkeys after SIV infection compared to before infection (Fig. 7A and fig. S8). The increase of IL-5 and CM-CSF mainly occurred at the acute phase and remained at the chronic phase and during ART. The increases of IL-6 and TNF-α levels mainly occurred at the chronic phase, and the levels slightly decrease after about 1 year of ART. In contrast, a substantially declined level of IL-4 was observed after SIV infection compared to before infection. The level of IL-1β decreased after ART initiation. No obvious changes in the levels of interferon-γ (IFN-γ), IL-12p70, IL-15, and IL-16 were observed during SIV infection and ART.

We also measured the levels of sCD14 and D-dimer (Fig. 7A and fig. S8). The levels of both sCD14 and D-dimer significantly increased at the chronic phase of SIV infection and then slightly declined since ART initiation. Rebounds of both factors were observed at the end of 1-year duration of ART.

Correlations of plasma commensal viruses with inflammation level and disease progression

We further investigated whether the expansions of plasma commensal viruses were associated with the elevation of inflammatory and disease markers during different infection phases. Anellovirus abundance appeared to significantly positively correlate with the levels of GM-CSF and TNF-α, but significantly negatively correlate with the levels of IL-4 and IL12p70 at the acute phase (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, it significantly positively correlated with the levels of IL-4 and TNF-α at the chronic phase and during ART, respectively. No significant correlations were observed between anellovirus abundance and the levels of sCD14 and D-dimer throughout SIV infection and subsequent ART. However, a significant correlation among anellovirus, parvovirus, and circovirus were observed at the chronic phase (Fig. 7B), which should be ascribed to the fact that they all substantially expanded at this phase. Parvovirus abundance was found to significantly positively correlate with the levels of IFN-γ and IL-4 at the chronic phase and circovirus abundance only significantly positively correlated with the IL-15 level during ART (Fig. 7B).

The level of sCD14 significantly positively correlated with the IFN-γ and IL12p70 levels at the acute phase (Fig. 7A), the IL-15 level at the chronic phase and during ART, and the circovirus abundance at the chronic phase (Fig. 7B). Significantly negative correlations of the sCD4 level with the proportion of the CD4+ HLA-DR+CD38+ T cells and the IL-4 level were observed at the acute and chronic phases, respectively. It was not surprising that the D-dimer level was significantly positively associated with the sCD14 level at the chronic phase and during ART. Furthermore, the D-dimer level significantly positively correlated with the proportion of the CD4+HLA-DR+CD38+ T cells and the IL-15 level at the chronic phase and negatively correlated with the proportion of the CD4+PD-1+ T cells during ART. The vast majority of the 10 proinflammatory cytokines showed significantly positive correlations with each other throughout the infection including on ART.

DISCUSSION

It is often impossible to study the virus-virus and virus-host interactions at the early phase of HIV-1 infection. Here, we investigated the longitudinal dynamic changes in plasma virome, immune cells, and proinflammation cytokines in the SIV-infected NPM model to better understand how SIV infection disrupts the homeostatic equilibrium between the host and commensal viruses in blood and reversely how the commensal viruses respond to SIV infection. We found that SIV infection results in a large expansion of plasma Anelloviridae and other commensal virus families such as Parvoviridae and Circoviridae at the chronic phase, and the commensal viruses exhibit different roles in SIV pathogenesis. Anelloviridae abundance was positively associated with the CD4+ T cell count but negatively associated with SIV load especially at the acute infection phase, whereas Parvoviridae and Circoviridae abundances showed opposite associations with the two disease markers. Furthermore, ART was found not to significantly reverse the expansion of plasma commensal viruses.

Complex interactions of SIV, commensal eukaryotic viruses, and the host

There is a dynamic equilibrium between the host and commensal (and/or coinfecting) viruses in a healthy condition (26). HIV-1/SIV infection can disrupt this homeostatic equilibrium and result in reactivation of commensal viruses and/or new viral infections, which are mainly ascribed to the impaired immunity caused by HIV-1/SIV (4). HIV-1/SIV infection generally experiences a very short acute phase with intense interaction between the virus and the host, and a long chronic phase, which is considered as an establishment of transient equilibrium between HIV-1/SIV and the host. Untreated infection will develop AIDS following the chronic phase. Enteric adenovirus expansion had been previously observed in HIV-1–infected individuals and pSIV-infected monkeys and was believed to participate in HIV-1/SIV pathogenesis and contribute to chronic immune reactivation (9, 10). However, when the adenovirus expansion occurs and what is the origin of the expanded adenoviruses (reactivation of persistent adenoviruses, new infection, or both) remain to be determined.

Different from the previous observations of adenovirus expansion in the gut, a large expansion of anelloviruses was found to mainly occur at the chronic phase in the SIV-infected NPM model. Anelloviridae abundance was positively associated with the CD4+ T cell count but negatively associated with SIV load throughout SIV infection, and the statistically significant associations were observed at the acute phase. The data challenge the previous cross-sectional observations of the negative and positive associations of Anelloviridae abundance with the CD4+ T cell count and HIV-1 VL, respectively (14, 17, 18). In particular, Anelloviridae abundance showed a completely opposite dynamic trend to SIV load at the acute phase, at which its abundance sharply dropped to the bottom along with the rapid rising of SIV load to the peak and then reversed along with the rapid decline of SIV load. These results indicate a strong competitive relationship between anelloviruses and SIV, which is beyond the traditional concept that anellovirus abundance was negatively associated with the immune status and is recognized as a potential indicator of immunosuppression and immunodeficiency (27, 28). One possible reason for the competition between anelloviruses and SIV is that they share the same target cells because anelloviruses have a broadly cellular tropism toward lymphocytes, including the CD4+ T cells (29, 30). In addition to lymphocytes, anelloviruses may replicate and reside in other cell types, because they have been detected in various biological samples, including plasma, nasal secretions, saliva, bile, feces, tears, semen, breast milk, urine, and many tissues/organs (31, 32). The simultaneous drop in Anelloviridae abundance and the CD4+ T cell count at the early acute phase may be due to the decline of the CD4+ T cells as target cells of anelloviruses. Whether anelloviruses and SIV use different molecular mechanisms to antagonize each other remains to be determined.

Apart from Anelloviridae, Parvoviridae (e.g., AAV), Circoviridae (e.g., circovirus), and other commensal viruses were also observed to expand at the chronic phase or during ART. The expansions of these commensal viruses indicate that the immune system has been impaired by SIV at the chronic phase and is unable to efficiently control the replication of these commensal viruses and/or to prevent new infections by these viruses. In contrast to Anelloviridae, the abundances of Parvoviridae, Circoviridae, and other viruses were positively associated with SIV load, but negatively associated with the CD4+ T cell count, suggesting that anelloviruses and other commensal viruses play different roles in SIV pathogenesis. Expanded Parvoviridae, Circoviridae, and other non-Anelloviridae commensal viruses may activate the CD4+ T cells to provide more targets for SIV, facilitating each other’s infection and worsening the impaired immune system, which forms a vicious cycle (1, 2, 4). Therefore, the chronic phase, rather than the acute phase, may be a very crucial stage for immune impairment and/or immunosenescence caused by SIV infection.

Evolution and origin of newly emerging anelloviruses

Anelloviruses belong to the Anelloviridae family with a small circular single-stranded negative-sense DNA genome and are extremely genetically diverse (32, 33). The expansion of anelloviruses at the chronic phase was characterized by the marked increase in viral abundance (load) and the emergence of many different lineages that were not detected before the SIV challenge and at the acute phase, which well witnesses the notion that SIV infection can lead to the reactivation of persistent viruses and infection of newly emerged viruses (4). We identified 1387 primate anellovirus ORF1 sequences, which greatly expand the current number of primate anelloviruses. All these lineages belong to the Alphatorquevirus genus and have very high genetic diversity. The emergence of many different anellovirus species/lineages raises a fundamental question where these species/lineages come from.

Anellovirus can be transmitted by a variety of routes, including aerosol droplets, fecal-oral, blood-borne, and vertical routes, and are ubiquitous (32). They can be detected in environments and various biological samples (15). Anelloviruses have a long coevolutionary relationship with their hosts (32, 34). Relatively distant phylogenetic relationships of the primate anellovirus sequences to those of human and other animals suggest that the newly emerging anellovirus lineages in these monkeys are highly unlikely to come from other animals or the environments. The most probable explanation is that these newly emerging anellovirus lineages come from the monkeys themselves (reservoir). Long-time persistence (over 30 years) of some anellovirus lineages in blood implies that anelloviruses can be controlled but not completely eliminated by the immune system (35–37). Anelloviruses have a wide distribution in various tissues/organs (31, 38), and most of them may be contained below the detection limit or persist as a reservoir in some tissues/organs (e.g., bone marrow) in normal physiological conditions and are ready for later reactivation when the immune system is impaired (35, 39, 40). This can explain why a higher abundance (expansion) of anelloviruses was often found in immunosuppressive and/or disease conditions. Housing these monkeys together before the SIV challenge might contribute to the establishment of the reservoir of anelloviruses via physical contact and fecal-oral transmissions, which can explain the very high proportion of shared lineages among these monkeys.

Association of plasma commensal viruses with immune activation and inflammation

Chronic immune activation and inflammation play a crucial role in HIV-1 pathogenesis and are associated with disease progression and non-AIDS comorbidities, which can be predicted by the serum levels of two crucial markers IL-6 and D-dimer (1, 2). Several factors (including HIV-1 proteins, microbial translocation, and coinfecting viruses such as CMV, etc.) are believed to cause chronic immune activation and inflammation but are unable to completely explain why they persist during ART (2). Proinflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-5, and TNF-α can enhance HIV-1 replication (41). Significantly elevated levels of IL-6, IL-5, and TNF-α, as well as D-dimer at the chronic phase of SIV infection witnessed the status of chronic immune activation and inflammation. An elevated level of sCD14 indicated that microbial translocation occurred at the chronic phase and contributed to immune activation and inflammation despite the superior intestinal integrity of M. leonina to humans (22, 42). Furthermore, no common DNA viruses such as CMV, EBV, and HSV-2 were detected in these monkeys. Therefore, we ask another question of general interest whether the expansions of anelloviruses and other commensal viruses also contribute to the chronic immune activation and inflammation.

Previous in vitro studies have demonstrated that certain human anellovirus isolates can induce the secretion of some proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IL-10, and IFN-γ) via the TLR-9 pathway (43, 44). The findings of positive correlations between Anelloviridae abundance and the levels of most proinflammatory cytokines at the chronic phase and during ART at least suggest that anelloviruses contribute to immune activation and inflammation. Similar positive correlations were also observed between Parvoviridae and Circoviridae abundances and some proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-4, IL-15, IL-6, and TNF-α) at the chronic phase and during ART, suggesting that they may also contribute to inflammation. On the other hand, the plasma GM-CSF level was found to increase and the IL-4 level was found to decrease after the SIV challenge. IL-4 has a dual function of up-regulating HIV-1 replication in monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) and down-regulating the surface expression of CCR5, the coreceptor of HIV-1 R5 strains, in monocytes to prevent HIV-1 entry and replication (41). GM-CSF may inhibit HIV-1 replication in MDM (45). Anelloviridae abundance had a significantly positive correlation with the level of GM-CSF, but a negative correlation with those of IL-4 and IL12p70 at the acute phase, completely opposite to the correlations between SIV load and these cytokines. The positive correlation between Anelloviridae abundance and the GM-CSF level may further support the competitive and antagonistic relationship of anelloviruses with SIV at least at the acute phase.

Implications for HIV-1 pathogenesis

The dynamic characteristics of plasma virome, proinflammatory cytokines, sCD14, and D-dimer clearly reveal that the chronic phase is the most critical stage for SIV disease progression. Initiating ART at the late chronic phase appeared to slightly reduce but not substantially reverse the progression, implying two possibilities. First, 1-year duration of ART might be not sufficient to reverse the effects of SIV (including the expansion of commensal viruses). Second, the impairment of immune system at the chronic phase might be difficult to reverse or irreversible, thereby suggesting an early ART initiation. The clinical benefits of early ART initiation in humans seem to support the second possibility. In addition, the findings of the expansions of plasma anelloviruses and other commensal viruses and their associations with inflammation, together with the previous observations of higher abundance of anelloviruses in HIV-infected individuals on and not on ART (13, 14, 46), suggest that these commensal viruses might be used as potential intervention targets in ART treatment especially for patients at the chronic phase.

Limitations of this study

First, this study was based on the SIV-infected M. leonina model that might not completely reflect the situation in HIV-infected individuals. Similar longitudinal studies to investigate the dynamic change of plasma virome and its association with the host immune and inflammatory responses are encouraged to be conducted in ART-naïve and treated HIV-infected individuals in the future. Second, these monkeys were treated for only 1 year. The short duration of ART limits our ability to determine whether long-term ART can normalize the altered plasma virome caused by SIV infection. Third, the roles of several main commensal viruses such as anelloviruses, parvoviruses, and circovirus in inflammation and/or immune activation need to be determined by in vitro experiments.

Conclusions

This is a longitudinal study that systematically reveals complex interactions among SIV, plasma commensal viruses, and the host throughout SIV infection and subsequent ART. SIV infection causes a large expansion of Anelloviridae and other commensal viruses such as Parvoviridae and Circoviridae since the chronic phase. The expanded commensal viruses might be associated with chronic inflammation at the chronic phase and during ART. Furthermore, anelloviruses might fight against SIV infection by scrambling for target cells at the acute phase. These findings not only provide different insights into the understanding of biological functions of anelloviruses that is beyond the previously well-known notion that Anelloviridae abundance might be a potential indicator of the host immune status, but also can stimulate further studies on the roles of plasma commensal viruses in HIV-1/SIV pathogenesis and even other infectious diseases.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study animals

Eleven NPMs (M. leonina) aged 5 to 8 years were obtained from the Kunming Primate Research Center, Kunming Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS), China (table S1). All enrolled animals are males and were tested seronegative for SIV, simian T-lymphotropic virus, simian betaretrovirus (serotypes 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 8), and Monkey B virus using dot-immunobinding assays (VRL Asia, Suzhou, China) before this study. All these monkeys were kept together for at least 75 days before the SIV challenge and were in separate cages with one monkey per cage after the SIV challenge. The SIV challenge was performed by intravenous inoculation with 4000 TCID50 (median tissue culture infectious dose) of SIVmac239 virus.

Physical (BMI) and biochemical parameters (total cholesterol, triglyceride, subcutaneous fat thickness in abdomen, leptin, glucose, and insulin levels) were measured at 2 months before the SIV challenge (preinfection), 3 months after the challenge (chronic phase), and 5 months after the initiation of ART. ART was initiated at 125 days after the SIV challenge. The ART regimens contain tenofovir (30 mg/kg), emtricitabine (50 mg/kg), and raltegravir (5 mg/ml). Blood samples were taken from each monkey at different time points as indicated in Fig. 1, including 3, 16, and 6 time points before the SIV challenge, after the SIV challenge, and during ART, respectively.

All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Kunming Institute of Zoology, CAS (approval number: SYDW-2015023).

SIV viral load

Plasma was isolated by the centrifugation of peripheral blood at 1000g for 10 min and was cryopreserved in small aliquots (500 μl) in −80°C until use. Viral RNA was extracted from plasma using the High Pure Viral RNA Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Germany). SIV VL was determined by an RT (reverse transcription)–qPCR method as previously described (47). The assay was performed using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit and Premix Ex Taq (Takara) on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Briefly, a 25-μl reaction contains 10 μl of Premix Ex Taq, 2 μl of the standard or samples, 0.4 μl of ROX reference Dye II, 200 nM of each primer, and 100 nM of the TaqMan probe. The primers and probe (targeting the conserved SIVmac gag region) are as follows: forward primer: 5′-TCGGTCTTAGCTCCATTAGTGCC-3′, reverse primer: 5′-GCTTCCTCAGTGTGTTTCACTTTC-3′, and TaqMan probe: 5′ FAM-CTTCTGCGTGAATGCACCAGATGACGC-TAMRA 3′. An RNA standard curve was generated from parallel wells with serial dilutions of in vitro transcripts of pGEM-4Z-SIVgag357 containing the SIVmac239 gag fragment.

Flow cytometry

Multiparameter flow cytometry was performed using fresh peripheral blood. The following anti-human flow cytometry antibodies that have cross-reactivity with macaques were used, including anti-CD3 APC-Cy7 (clone SP34–2), anti-CD4 PerCP-Cy5.5 (clone OKT4) (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), and anti-CD8 PECy7 (clone RPA-T8) (BD Pharmingen, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). T cell function including activation and exhaustion was measured by the HLA-DR and CD38 (clone AT-1) and PD1 (clone J105) (eBioscience, CA, USA) levels on CD4+ T cells, respectively. Flow cytometric procedures were followed as previously described (48, 49). Flow cytometry data acquisition was performed on a BD FACSVerseTM flow cytometer (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and the data analysis was performed using FlowJo vX.0.7 (TreeStar, Ashland, OR, USA). All procedures were followed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Proteomic chip-based cytokine assay and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

In total, 271 of 275 serum samples (one from the acute phase and three from the chronic phase were not tested due to limited sample amount) were subjected to inflammation cytokine profile measurement. A total of 10 cytokines were measured using the proteomic chip-based assay (Quantibody Non-Human Primates Cytokine Array, catalog no. QAN-CYT-1, Raybiotech, Norcross GA, USA). These cytokines include IL-1β, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-12 p70, IL-15, IL-16, GM-CSF, TNF-α, and IFN-γ. Assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Raybiotech). Each sample was measured in quadruplicate. Data were automatically processed by using the Raybiotech Q-Analyzer Tool with individual standard curve produced from each cytokine standard.

In total, 260 of 275 serum samples were subjected to measurements of sCD14 and D-dimer; the other 15 samples (1 from the acute phase and 14 from the chronic phase) were not tested due to limited sample amount. The RayBio Rhesus Macaque sCD14 and D-dimer ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) kits (catalog no. ELK-CD14 and ELK-D-dimer, Raybiotech, Norcross GA, USA) were used for the quantification of serum sCD14 and D-dimer levels. Assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, this assay employs an antibody specific for Rhesus Macaque sCD14 or D-dimer coated on a 96-well plate. Standards and samples are pipetted into the wells and sCD14 or D-dimer present in the sample is bound to the wells by the immobilized antibody. The wells are washed and biotinylated anti-Rhesus Macaque sCD14 or D-dimer antibody is added. After washing away unbound biotinylated antibody, HRP-conjugated streptavidin is pipetted to the wells. The wells are again washed, a 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine substrate solution is added to the wells, and color develops in proportion to the amount of sCD14 or D-dimer binding to immobilized antibodies. The intensity of the color is measured at 450 nm by the epoch (Biotek), and the levels were quantified through the standard curve.

Virus-like particle enrichment, library construction, and NGS

Approximately 500 μl of plasma sample was thawed on ice and homogenized for 5 min. Then, the plasma was centrifuged at 4°C for 15 min at 12,000g. To reduce cell debris, bacteria, and other background materials, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45-μm sterile filter (Costar Spin-X centrifuge tube filters, Corning, USA). To remove free nucleic acids, the filtered samples were incubated with a cocktail of nucleases, including 15 U Turbo DNase (Invitrogen, USA), 20 U Benzonase (Novagen, Germany), and 20 U RNase I (Promega, USA) for 90 min at 37°C, and the reaction was stopped by the incubation with 30 mM EDTA at 65°C for 10 min. Both DNA and RNA were extracted using the QIAamp MinElute virus kit (Qiagen, Germany) and were amplified using a random-amplification method (REPLI-g Single Cell WTA kit, Qiagen, Germany). The amplified products were purified by QIAquick PCR purification kit (Qiagen, Germany). Individual DNA library of each sample was constructed by the NEBNext UltraII FS DNA library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA). Libraries were quantified by a Qubit3.0 (Invitrogen, USA) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, USA) with 2 × 150-bp paired reads. Phosphate-buffered saline was used as the negative control in the same processing, nucleic acid extraction, and library construction, and elution buffer was used as the negative control in NGS. The negative control was used as an internal control to check and exclude potential laboratory contaminations.

Plasma virome analysis

Metagenomics data were analyzed as previously described (13). Briefly, adaptors and low-quality sequences were first removed by Trimmomatic v.0.38 (50). Reads from human genomes were removed by alignment to the hg38 database using Bowtie2 v.2.3.4.3 (51). The remaining high-quality reads were de novo assembled into contigs using Megahit v.1.1.3 (52). All contigs (longer than 300 nt) were subjected to searches against the viral nucleic acid and protein database (downloaded and updated from NCBI on October 2022.) using BLAST 2.11.0 (E value <10−10) and BLASTx (E value <10−5) (DIAMOND v.0.9.24), respectively (53). Potential viral hits from the above step were further subjected to searches against the NCBI nucleotide and nonredundant protein databases to further identify and remove false-positive sequences that have higher similarity to nonviral sequences. Each viral sequence was annotated as family, genus, and species. Viral abundance was assessed by the number of viral RPM.

The α-diversity was calculated by the Shannon diversity index (H = −Σ (pi log(pi)) and the Chao richness score (Richness) that computes the number of species in a community. The β-diversity of viral community was indicated by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances. Statistical significance for PCoA was determined by PERMANOVA testing.

Anellome analyses

All anellovirus contigs obtained from the abovementioned analysis were further curated using the ICTV anellovirus references (https://ictv.global/taxonomy). In total, 1387 anellovirus contigs longer than 1500 nt were obtained. All the 1387 contigs were classified as different viral lineages using an in-house pipeline. Briefly, after removing the identical sequences, an anellovirus lineage was determined if two or more contigs share at least 95% nucleotide sequence identity and have at least 70% coverage for the shorter sequence using CD-hit v4.8.1 (54). According to the above cutoffs, all anellovirus contigs were assigned into 464 lineages. To determine how many lineages exist, the anellovirus contigs from each sample were mapped to the 464 lineages. The presence of an anellovirus lineage in a sample is defined as the presence of at least one anellovirus contig that has a length of ≥300 nt with more than 10 mapping reads and the highest sequence identity (>95%) to the lineage. The abundance of a lineage in one sample was assessed by the number of reads mapping to the representative sequence of the lineage (i.e., the longest contig).

The lineages that exist in only one monkey, more than two monkeys, and more than 70% of monkeys (i.e., more than eight monkeys) are defined as unique lineages, shared lineages, and “core lineages,” respectively. The appearance of anellovirus lineages at different infection phases was analyzed and presented by a Venn diagram.

Phylogenetic and evolutionary analyses of anelloviruses

To infer the phylogenetic relationship of primate anelloviruses, all anellovirus ORF1 sequences longer than 1500 nt obtained from NPM in this study were subjected to the construction of a maximum likelihood (ML) tree. Anellovirus ORF1 sequences from human and other animals (e.g., raccoon, cat, seal, palm civets, dog, giant panda, lesser panda, pine marten, bat, chicken, tree shrew, horse, pig, rodent, mosquito, lemur, wild boar, sea lion, and hare) were extracted using NCBI’s ORF Finder tool using the “any sense codon” option and were curated by aligning against the in-house anellovirus reference sequence database (download from GenBank database). All ORF1 sequences were aligned using MAFFT (E-INS algorithm) (55). The best substitution model was determined by the Model test program. ML tree was constructed using IQ-Tree under a GTR + F + R6 model with 1000 bootstrap replications (56). Genetic distances within and between clades/groups were estimated using MEGA7.0.

The quantification of anelloviruses with qPCR

Previously reported primers (forward: 5′-ACWKMCGAATGGCT-GAGTTT-3′, reverse: 5′-CCCKWGCCCGARTTGCCCCT-3′) targeting the conserved UTR region of Anelloviridae was used (57). Sequence alignment demonstrated that these primers could well cover various anellovirus sequences obtained from NPM in this study. A 20-μl reaction mixture contains 10 μl of 2× SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix (AGBio, China), 1 μl of both primers (10 μM each), 3 μl of template, and 5 μl of H2O. The qPCR reaction program is as follows: 95°C for 1 min, 40 cycles at 95°C for 10 s, and 63°C for 1 min. The original DNA of each sample was used for the qPCR assay. A plasmid containing the full-length anellovirus genome (GenBank accession: AY666122) was used as the DNA standard for quantification. Anellovirus DNA copy number was calculated based on a standard curve generated by the parallel wells with serial dilutions of DNA standard.

Correlation analysis

The correlation between the virome (SIV load, Anelloviridae, Parvoviridae, and Circoviridae abundance), CD4+ T cell counts and function (activation and exhaustion), inflammation cytokine levels and markers of gut translocation (sCD14), and increased mortalities (D-dimer) was analyzed using Spearman correlation by the psych package in R4.2.3, and visualized by heatmap (corrplot package in R4.2.3).

Statistics

Continuous variables (viral abundance, richness, CD4+ T cell counts, cytokine levels, BMI, etc.) between groups were compared by the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction. Frequencies (positive rate of virus between different groups) were compared by Fisher’s exact test. The correlations between the qPCR and metagenomic sequencing abundance (RPM), VLs, and CD4+ and CD8+ T cell counts were calculated by Spearman’s correlation. ROC curve analysis was performed to determine the sensitivity and specificity of abundance or diversity of AVs for the prediction of the acute and chronic phases of SIV infection. All statistical analyses were performed using R studio v.3.8, GraphPad Prism 8, and/or SPSS 26.0. A difference with P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and P < 0.01, P < 0.001, and P < 0.0001 were considered highly statistically significant.
