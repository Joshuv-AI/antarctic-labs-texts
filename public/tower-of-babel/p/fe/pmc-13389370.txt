Directing intermediate phase crystallographic orientation promotes carbon-based CsPbI3 perovskite solar cells to beyond 20% efficiency
Authors: Gaofeng Li, Jieke Tan, Changqing Lin, Cheng Zhu, Yujiang Du, Zhou Li, Shulin Chen, Xiaozhen Wei, Zhe Xing, Yuzhe Wang, Chunyu Lv, Qixian Zhang, Huicong Liu, Weiping Li, Yang Bai, Shihe Yang, Zedong Lin, Zhike Liu, Haining Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inorganic CsPbI3 perovskite, known for its high chemical stability and near-ideal bandgap, offers a promising solution to the instability of organic-inorganic hybrid perovskites that limit perovskite solar cells (PSCs) longevity. However, the conventional intermediate phase (dimethylammonium lead iodide, DMAPbI3) templating method suffers from inefficient phase conversion, hindering high-performance PSC development. To overcome this limitation, we engineered the crystallographic orientation of DMAPbI3 to promote rapid volatilization of DMAI and accelerating the transformation. Through in situ anchoring of Pb2+-complexing groups (-F < -Cl < -SO4) on TiO2 substrates during chemical bath deposition, we direct the preferential in-plane growth of Pb-rich (100) planes of DMAPbI3, enhancing its [100] orientation. Crucially, stronger complexing groups yield higher orientation degrees, accelerating thermal conversion into highly oriented CsPbI3 perovskite with higher purity and better optoelectronic properties. This strategy enables carbon-based, hole-transport-layer-free CsPbI3 PSCs to achieve a record 20.72% efficiency (certified as 20.35%). Unencapsulated device retains > 85% of their initial efficiency after 1156 h of continuous maximum power point tracking under 1-sun illumination.

Introduction

The power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have reached over 27%, rivaling those of monocrystalline silicon solar cells1–3. Nevertheless, their commercialization are still impeded by inherent instability issues. This instability stems not only from the facile decomposition of the organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite but also from the degradation of organic hole transport layers (HTLs, e.g., Spiro-OMeTAD) and the corrosion of metal electrodes4–8. Replacing both the HTL and the metal electrode with a stable and low-cost carbon electrode to form carbon-based PSCs (C-PSCs) presents a promising solution, as it can significantly enhance device stability and reduce manufacturing costs9–16. However, a fundamental challenge persists: the volatile nature of organic cations (e.g., CH3NH3+) tends to induce the degradation of organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite (e.g., CH3NH3PbI3), which undermines long-term stability17–19. To address this critical stability issue, research focus has shifted toward all-inorganic perovskite absorbers. Among them, CsPbI3 stands out as a highly promising candidate due to its suitable bandgap (Eg ~ 1.7 eV) and superior chemical stability20–23.

The DMAPbI3-based intermediate phase templating strategy represents a significant milestone in the development of CsPbI3 PSCs, enabling devices with the PCEs exceeding 22%24–27. However, these PSCs are still underperforming relative to their organic-inorganic hybrid counterparts and suffer from inadequate operational stability28–32. The fundamental limitation lies in the incomplete thermal conversion of the DMAPbI3 intermediate phase into CsPbI3 perovskite, particularly at the film bottom33–35. Residual DMAPbI3 with its wide bandgap (Eg ~ 2.3 eV) and misaligned energy levels, acts as a charge transport barrier, thereby limiting photovoltaic performance36. While prolonged annealing or higher temperatures could enhance conversion, such approaches risk inducing a detrimental phase transition to photoinactive δ-CsPbI3 (non-perovskite phase) through thermal excitation, compromising both material and device stability34,37. This inherent trade-off between conversion completeness and phase stability poses a fundamental constraint on advancing high-performance CsPbI3 PSCs through heat treatment regulation.

To address the aforementioned challenges, various strategies have been developed to facilitate the conversion of the DMAPbI3 intermediate phase into CsPbI3 perovskite without obviously compromising stability. For instance, the vacuum-assisted annealing process has been adopted to enhance the extraction of DMAI molecules for accelerating the decomposition of the intermediate phase38. In addition, by introducing specific additives into the precursor solution for forming hydrogen bonds with DMA⁺ ions, the volatilization of the additives during the annealing process would help extract DMAI molecules33,39,40. Furthermore, suppressing excessive crystal growth for forming small DMAPbI3 grains through adding surfactants or structural modifiers could shorten ion diffusion paths and hence accelerate the conversion process41. Besides, some versatile molten salts have also been used to modulate the conversion from DMAPbI3 to CsPbI3 perovskite42–47. Although these strategies have accelerated the intermediate phase conversion to some extent, they often introduce process complexity or impurities that constrain crystal growth and charge transport. This limitation highlights the demand for strategies that address the problem at its root (e.g., the initial crystal growth) rather than trying to fix it later with additives.

Herein, to address these issues, we develop a substrate-engineering approach as an “impurity-free” and “bottom-up” method that enhances the crystallographic orientation of the DMAPbI3 intermediate phase, creates rapid volatilization channels for DMAI molecules, and promotes the formation of high-quality CsPbI3. Based on crystal structure analysis and theoretical calculations, inorganic acid groups (–F, –Cl, and –SO4) with strong Pb2+ complexation capabilities were introduced in situ into TiO2 substrates via chemical bath deposition (CBD) to regulate ion anchoring and crystal growth. The results indicate that the stronger complexation strength (–F < –Cl < –SO4) promotes higher [100] orientation of the DMAPbI3 crystals, significantly accelerating their thermal conversion to CsPbI3 perovskite with higher purity and better optoelectronic properties. Consequently, CsPbI3 C-PSCs without HTL achieved a record PCE of 20.72%. Moreover, under 1-sun illumination, the unencapsulated device maintained over 85% of its initial PCE after 1156 h of maximum power point (MPP) tracking.

Results

Theoretical calculation and CBD

DMAPbI3, typically crystallizing in a hexagonal structure (space group P63/mmc, a = b = 8.7087 Å, c = 8.1149 Å), consists of infinite linear chains of face-sharing [PbI6]4− octahedra extending along one crystallographic axis (Fig. 1a). These octahedral chains form the inorganic backbone of the structure. The organic DMA+ cations are located between the chains, isolating them from each other and creating a quantum-confined one-dimensional (1D) structure48. Figure. 1b illustrate the atomic composition of the (100) and (001) crystal planes. The Pb atom density on the (100) plane is approximately 1.86 times higher than that on the (001) plane (Fig. 1c). Our density-functional theory (DFT) calculations indicate that the Pb-terminated surface of DMAPbI3 exhibits a significantly higher adsorption energy (Eads) compared to I- or DMA-terminated surfaces, attributable to the stronger interaction between under-coordinated Pb2+ and surface O2− ions (Supplementary Fig. 1). Consequently, the (100) planes with higher Pb density favor in-plane growth, promoting a preferred [100] orientation of DMAPbI3 crystals on the TiO2 substrate (Supplementary Fig. 2). In this work, we developed a low-temperature and scalable CBD method to deposit TiO2 films. By leveraging in situ anchoring of reactive groups during deposition, we introduced strongly interacting functional groups to ensure robust binding with TiO2. Since the long carbon chains in organic titanium salts would hinder charge transport52,53, we primarily considered inorganic titanium salts as precursors. To screen suitable candidates, we performed DFT calculations to evaluate the Eads of various inorganic acid radical ions on both the (100) surfaces of DMAPbI3 and TiO2. As shown in Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 3, the sulfate ions (SO42−) exhibit the most negative adsorption energy, indicating the strongest interaction, among all tested ions on both surfaces. Based on experimental practicality and comparative relevance, we selected (NH4)2TiF6, TiCl4, and Ti(SO4)2 as precursors to deposit TiO2 films functionalized with –F, –Cl, and –SO4 groups, respectively (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 4).

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the various TiO2 films (Supplementary Fig. 5) display similar features, with all major diffraction peaks corresponding to anatase TiO2 and the FTO substrate. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 6 and 7) reveals distinct F 1s, Cl 2p, and S 2p signals for the TiO2 films derived from (NH4)2TiF6, TiCl4, and Ti(SO4)2 precursor solutions, respectively, confirming the successful incorporation of -F, -Cl, and -SO4 groups. These modified films are hereafter denoted as TiO2–F, TiO2–Cl, and TiO2–SO4. Furthermore, the Ti 2p peaks for the TiO2–F, TiO2–Cl, and TiO2–SO4 films are shifted by 0.05, 0.13, and 0.29 eV, respectively, relative to the unmodified TiO2 film. These shifts reflect varying interaction strengths between the functional groups and the TiO2 substrate, following the trend: -F < -Cl < -SO4, consistent with the earlier theoretical calculations. Additional confirmation of functional group incorporation was provided by energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) coupled with scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Supplementary Fig. 8). Finally, UV-Vis spectra (Fig. 1g) show that all TiO2 films possess similar optical transmittance within the 300–800 nm wavelength range55–57. Contact angle measurements (Supplementary Fig. 9) show that all TiO2 films exhibited very similar and high wettability. Furthermore, the electron mobility values (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Table 1) for all substrates fall within a narrow range. Together, these results demonstrate that the functionalization does not significantly alter the surface energy or electron transport properties of the TiO2 films.

The deposition of the DMAPbI3 intermediate phase

A conventional precursor solution containing PbI2, DMAI, and CsI was employed to deposit intermediate phase films on various TiO2 substrates via one-step spin coating. After deposition, highly crystalline DMAPbI3, accompanied by poorly crystalline Cs4PbI6, was formed through the reaction: 2PbI2 + DMAI + 4CsI → DMAPbI3 + Cs4PbI637. To investigate the influence of the TiO2 substrate on the crystal structure of the intermediate phase, grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) was performed. As shown in Fig. 2a–d, diffraction rings corresponding to the (100) plane of DMAPbI3 are clearly observed at qxy = 0.9 Å−1. The DMAPbI3 films deposited on TiO2–F, TiO2–Cl, and TiO2–SO4 substrates exhibit progressively sharper diffraction signals, indicating enhanced [100] orientation with increasing complexation strength of the substrate. For clearer comparison, normalized angular integration profiles at qxy = 0.9 Å−1 are presented in Fig. 2e. The control sample shows no distinct diffraction peak, suggesting weak orientation. The introduction of -F groups results in a broad peak near 90°, indicative of improved [100] alignment. Based on the GIWAXS results, the introduction of functional groups promotes a more pronounced [100] orientation in the DMAPbI3 crystal stacking, which subsequently leads to discernible morphology change in the intermediate-phase films (Fig. 2f). To further investigate this effect, SEM imaging was performed (Fig. 2g–j and Supplementary Fig. 13). The images reveal a gradual increase in grain size along with a distinct morphological evolution: the film transitions from initially isolated islands with random distribution to a well-organized, chain-like texture, accompanied by a crystal shape evolution from spherical/globular particulates to elongated, rod-like structures. These observations indicate that functionalization of the TiO2 substrates enhances both the [100] crystallographic orientation and guides the morphological development, effects primarily attributed to the coordinated interaction between the functional groups and lead ions58–61.

To exclude the potential influence of CsI on the crystal orientation of the DMAPbI3 intermediate phase, a precursor solution containing only PbI2 and DMAI (with a PbI2: DMAI molar ratio of 1:1) was used to deposit pure DMAPbI3 films (DMAI + PbI2 → DMAPbI3) on the different TiO2 substrates. The XRD patterns in Supplementary Fig. 14 confirm the formation of pure DMAPbI3 crystals and show a gradual enhancement of the (100) orientation with increasing complexation strength of the functional groups, consistent with the behavior observed in the PbI2/DMAI/CsI precursor system. SEM images (Fig. 2k–n and Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16) reveal that the crystal morphology evolves from upright prisms to recumbent ellipsoids and eventually to horizontally aligned regular cubes. This evolution is attributed to stronger Pb-coordination, which preferentially anchors the Pb-rich (100) plane to the substrate62–64. Meanwhile, the polar DMA⁺ interlayers confine the neutral Pb–I–Pb layers along the c-axis65, thereby restricting vertical stacking and promoting lateral expansion along the preferred (100) plane, ultimately transforming upright prisms into recumbent cuboids. Similar morphological transitions, widely documented in other hexagonal crystal systems (e.g. ZnO66,67, α-Fe2O368, MoS269, and AlN70), are understood to result from an “ordered stacking” mechanism driven by surface energy minimization in hexagonal crystals68,71.

The conversion of the intermediate phase to CsPbI3 perovskite

Following the deposition of the DMAPbI3 intermediate phases, all samples were thermally annealed at 220 °C to facilitate conversion into CsPbI3. The transformation kinetics were systematically tracked using in situ imaging during heating (Fig. 3a). The control sample began turning from pale yellow to brown after approximately 50 s, indicating the onset of the phase transition (3DMAPbI3 + Cs4PbI6 → 4CsPbI3 + 3DMAI↑)25,37. This color change corresponds to the sublimation of DMAI and insertion of cesium ions, resulting in the formation of γ-CsPbI3.The control sample turned fully dark brown after about 3 min. However, upon further annealing to approximately 9 min, scattered yellow regions reappeared and gradually expanded, suggesting a thermally induced transition from γ-CsPbI3 to the non-perovskite δ-CsPbI3. It is obvious that the introduction of functional groups significantly shortened the time required for the transition from pale yellow to dark brown, an effect that became more pronounced with increasing the complexation capability of the groups. Furthermore, the undesirable transition from γ-CsPbI3 to δ-CsPbI3 was progressively delayed with stronger complexation strength. These results indicate that the functionalized TiO2 substrates promote faster and more stable formation of γ-CsPbI3. Notably, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 17, accelerated conversion to CsPbI3 perovskite occurred not only in the mixed phase but also in pure DMAPbI3 films with enhanced orientation via a two-step method. To validate the above claims, XRD patterns were collected to monitor compositional evolution over annealing time (Fig. 3b–e). At the initial stage of 5 s, all four films exhibit similar diffraction patterns, with the prominent peaks corresponding to DMAPbI3. Notably, the intensity of the (100) peak of DMAPbI3 increases consistently with the complexation capability, indicating enhanced [100] orientation. After 50 s of annealing, all DMAPbI3 diffraction peaks weaken, while the peaks assignable to γ-CsPbI3 emerge, confirming the transformation of the intermediate phase into perovskite. Importantly, the incorporation of functional groups accelerates this conversion, with the higher complexation capability leading to the more complete conversion. Further extending annealing time to 3 min, the conversion degree increases further, and nearly no residual DMAPbI3 diffraction peaks are detected for the TiO2–SO4 sample. These findings are supported by UV-Vis spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 18), which corroborates the accelerated conversion kinetics facilitated by the functional groups. When the annealing time is extended to 15 min, the diffraction peaks associated with PbI2 and δ-CsPbI3 emerge in the control TiO2 sample, indicating degradation of γ-CsPbI3. This degradation is effectively suppressed in functionalized samples. Remarkably, the TiO2–SO4 sample retains nearly phase-pure γ-CsPbI3, demonstrating significantly improved stability.

GIWAXS patterns were also taken to evaluate the crystal orientation of the samples after 3 min annealing. As displayed in Supplementary Fig. 19, the diffraction rings (qxy = 1.0 Å−1) of the p-CsPbI3 (110) plane show increasingly concentrated signals with the complexation capability of the groups, indicating the gradually enhanced [110] orientation of the p-CsPbI3 crystals. The normalized angular integration at qxy = 1.0 Å−1 (Supplementary Fig. 20) further confirms this improvement in [110] orientation of the p-CsPbI3 crystals. In addition, the I–q plots extracted from the GIWAXS patterns (Supplementary Fig. 21) affirm the accelerated and complete conversion from DMAPbI3 to CsPbI3 for the TiO2–SO4 sample. SEM (Supplementary Figs. 22 and 23) and AFM images (Supplementary Fig. 24) show that the functional groups also improve film morphology, yielding a smoother surface and enlarged grains.

To examine the atomic-scale crystal structure of the TiO2 and TiO2–SO4 samples annealed for 3 min, we conducted transmission electron microscopy (TEM) combined with focused ion beam (FIB) cross-sectioning (Supplementary Fig. 25 and Supplementary Note 1). For the TiO2 sample, high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images taken from four distinct regions (yellow circles in Fig. 3f, g) across the film’s cross-section reveal variations in lattice fringe orientation and interplanar spacing. The measured spacings of 0.24, 0.28, and 0.62 nm correspond to the (122), (112), and (110) planes of CsPbI3, respectively. This provides strong evidence for the random orientation of perovskite crystals. In contrast, HRTEM images acquired from four regions along the cross-section of the TiO2–SO4 sample (Fig. 3h, i) show consistent interplanar spacing (0.62 nm, corresponding to the (110) plane of CsPbI3) and uniform crystal orientation, as further verified by fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns in Supplementary Figs. 26 and 27. Furthermore, while both samples underwent identical FIB preparation, PbI2 (likely from decomposed residual DMAPbI3) is present in the TiO2 sample but absent in the TiO2–SO4 sample72–74. This critical observation confirms the higher phase purity and more thorough conversion achieved in the TiO2–SO4 sample.

To elucidate the DMAPbI3-to-CsPbI3 transformation mechanism75–77, we established two DMAPbI3 model systems: (i) a randomly oriented Pb-I framework (control model, Fig. 3j) and (ii) a uniformly aligned framework (working model, Fig. 3k). To quantitatively assess component interactions during the transformation, we calculated the binding energy (Ebind = Esystem – ΣEconstituents) for each state. A negative Ebind signifies attractive interaction, with larger absolute values indicating greater stability. The endothermic DMAI sublimation process increases the Ebind from state 1 to state 2 (+0.28 eV for the control model vs +0.05 eV for the working model), which destabilizes the initial 1D architecture and generates metastable state 2–4. The system subsequently evolves through Cs⁺ incorporation during thermal annealing to ultimately form the final CsPbI3 lattice (state 5). We further calculated the escape energy of DMA+ (Eesc = (Esystem (DMA⁺ removed) + EDMAI (gas)) – Esystem (intact)) at the initial state for the random and oriented configuration of DMAPbI3 crystal. As indicated in Supplementary Fig. 28, the oriented configuration exhibits a lower Eesc (−1.47 eV) than the control one (−1.13 eV). This difference arises because a randomly oriented structure forces migrating ions to follow tortuous paths and encounter frequent dead-ends at misaligned grain boundaries, hindering efficient escape, which is consistent with the more arduous transition from state 1 to state 5 in the control model. In contrast, an aligned orientation creates continuous diffusion channels along the inter-chain gaps, enabling more efficient mass transport (DMAI out, Cs+ in) and thereby accelerating and ensuring a more complete phase transformation4,78.

Mechanical and optoelectronic properties of different perovskite films

Mechanical and optoelectronic properties of the perovskite films deposited on different functionalized TiO2 substrates were further investigated. Interfacial adhesion strength was evaluated by designing a three-point bending test (setup shown in Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 29a, and Supplementary Note 2). Stress-strain curves (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 29c) reveal that the functional groups enhance interfacial bonding strength and fracture energy (GC), with both parameters scaling proportionally to the group’s Pb²⁺ complexation capacity. This correlation suggests chemical interactions between functional groups and perovskite Pb²⁺ ions significantly reinforce the interface79. To verify this mechanism, XPS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 30) and SEM images were performed at the buried interface after mechanically delaminating perovskite films. The groups with higher complexation capability induce more pronounced shifts in Pb 4f and I 3d binding energies, confirming their direct chemical interaction with the perovskite lattice. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 31a, all substrates revealed clean TiO2 surfaces without perovskite residue, providing definitive evidence that the failure mode was purely adhesive at the perovskite/TiO2 interface. Moreover, the SEM images of the detached perovskite films (Supplementary Fig. 31b) show that the control sample exhibits voids and cracks at the interface, while the functionalized samples display denser and more coherent interfacial morphology. Grazing incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD) was used to assess residual tensile strain in various perovskite films. Measurements involved acquiring XRD patterns at a fixed 2θ angle while varying the instrument tilt angle (ψ) (Supplementary Fig. 32 and Supplementary Note 3). Among the prominent perovskite diffraction planes, the (012) plane (peak at 32.3°) was selected for detailed analysis due to its high diffraction angle and intensity, providing reliable structural symmetry information (Supplementary Fig. 33)80. As shown in Fig. 4c, the linear relationship between 2θ and sin2ψ reveals the strain state through its slope. The control film exhibits a pronounced negative slope, indicating tensile strain corresponding to a tensile stress of 5.2 ± 1 MPa. Introducing surface groups reduces this tensile stress, with stronger complexation capability leading to lower stress. Consequently, the perovskite film on the TiO2–SO4 substrate shows the lowest tensile stress (0.14 ± 0.02 MPa). This reduction in tensile strain (Supplementary Fig. 34) is attributed to the formation of the high-purity and oriented perovskite crystals facilitated by the surface groups.

The surface groups on the substrate influence not only the mechanical properties but also the optoelectronic performance of the perovskite. DFT calculations of the Eads for various ions on the perovskite, along with the corresponding charge density distributions (Fig. 4d), reveal that the groups with the stronger complexation capabilities exhibit the higher binding strength. The Eads values increase progressively from −1.14 eV for the TiO2 substrate to −2.56 eV for the TiO2–SO4 substrate. This enhanced binding is attributed to the formation of coordination bonds between the surface groups and undercoordinated Pb2+ in the perovskite, which helps suppress defect formation81. The reduction in defect density is further corroborated by the progressively stronger steady-state photoluminescence (PL) intensity (Fig. 4e) and slower PL quenching kinetics (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Table 2, and Supplementary Note 4) observed as the complexation capability of the surface groups increases82,83.

To further validate the above interpretation, the external PL quantum yields (PLQYs) of different FTO/TiO2/PVK stacks (Supplementary Fig. 35) were measured to determine the quasi-fermi level splitting (QFLS). As shown in Fig. 4g-j, the QFLS increases gradually with the complexation capability of the surface groups, consistent with more effective defect passivation84. Electroluminescence (EL) characterization of the corresponding light-emitting diodes (FTO/TiO2/PVK/C) also exhibits a progressive enhancement in EL intensity and external quantum efficiency (EQE) (EQEEL, Supplementary Fig. 36), implying a reduction in defect density and suppressed non-radiative recombination85,86. To quantitatively assess the defect density, space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurements were performed on electron-only devices (FTO/TiO2/Perovskite/PC61BM/C)87,88. The SCLC results (Supplementary Fig. 37) yield trap densities (nt) of 5.37 × 1016, 4.23 × 1016, 3.75 × 1016, and 3.04 × 1016 cm−3 for the perovskite films grown on the TiO2, TiO2–F, TiO2–Cl, and TiO2–SO4 substrates, respectively.

Based on the obtained defect densities, physical models were constructed (Supplementary Fig. 38 and Supplementary Table 3) using COMSOL Multiphysics (V6.1) software to evaluate the influence of defect density on the photoelectric properties of CsPbI3 films and the photovoltaic performance of C-PSCs89. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 39, a reduction in defect density leads to a decrease in surface potential and an increase in conductivity of the CsPbI3 films, accompanied by a systematic improvement in overall photovoltaic performance of the devices (Supplementary Fig. 40). To experimentally verify the simulated trend in conductivity, we performed conductive atomic force microscopy (C-AFM). The results (Fig. 4k–n) show a significant enhancement in film conductivity for group-anchored TiO2 substrates, most notably for TiO2–SO4. This progressive improvement in conductivity should be mainly attributed to the highly oriented CsPbI3 crystals with fewer in-plane grain boundaries (Fig. 3h, i), which usually act as barriers to charge transport90,91.

Photovoltaic performance and stability of PSCs

The photovoltaic performance of the various CsPbI3 perovskite films was further evaluated by fabricating the C-PSCs without HTL (FTO/TiO2/CsPbI3/Carbon, Fig. 5a). The cross‑sectional images reveal a device structure with a ~15 nm compact TiO2 layer, a ~700 nm CsPbI3 perovskite layer, and a ~ 40μm carbon electrode (Supplementary Fig. 41). Statistical photovoltaic parameters in Supplementary Fig. 42 reveal that the TiO2–SO4 devices achieve significantly improved photovoltaic performance compared to the TiO2 devices. This enhancement is particularly evident in open-circuit voltage (VOC) and fill factor (FF), leading to an increase in the average PCE from 17.42% to 20.50%. This performance improvement is attributed to the formation of oriented perovskite crystals with higher purity, lower defect density, and enhanced charge transport, which collectively suppress trap-induced recombination (Supplementary Fig. 43–45). The champion TiO2–SO4 device (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 4) achieves a PCE of 20.72% (certified as 20.35% in Supplementary Fig. 46), with a VOC of 1.202 V, a short-circuit current density (JSC) of 20.72 mA/cm2, and an FF of 83.2%. As summarized in Fig. 5c and Supplementary Table 5, this PCE represents the highest reported value for CsPbI3 C-PSCs to date. Additionally, we have also fabricated the large-scale C-PSCs with an active area of 1 cm2, which achieves a PCE of 17.64% (Supplementary Fig. 47). Moreover, the Au-electrode CsPbI3 PSCs, both with and without Spiro-OMeTAD HTL, fabricated on TiO2–SO4 substrates also show enhanced performance (Supplementary Fig. 48). The comparison of the forward and reverse scanned J–V curves reveals significantly reduced hysteresis in the TiO2–SO4 device compared to the TiO2 device (Fig. 5b). The champion TiO2–SO4 device achieves a steady-state power output of ~20.49% at the MPP (Fig. 5d), substantially higher than the TiO2 device. This further confirms the suppressed hysteresis in the TiO2–SO4 device. EQE spectra (Fig. 5e) exhibit a broad spectral response across the 300–730 nm range for both devices. The integrated JSC values are 20.11 and 20.40 mA/cm2 for the TiO2 and TiO2–SO4 devices, respectively, closely matching the JSC values obtained from the J–V curves.

Device stability was evaluated following ISOS protocols92,93. Operational stability was assessed under MPP tracking (ISOS-L-1) under continuous 1-sun illumination in air with 30–40% RH at 25 °C. As shown in Fig. 5f, the TiO2–SO4 device demonstrates significantly enhanced operational stability compared to the TiO2 device, with T85 lifetime increasing from ~310 h to over 1156 h. Thermal stability was evaluated by heating unencapsulated devices at 85 °C in a nitrogen atmosphere (ISOS-D-2). After 1000 h, the TiO2 device retained only ~68% of its initial PCE, whereas the TiO2–SO4 device maintains 93% (Fig. 5g), confirming its superior thermal stability. In addition, the humidity stability of the encapsulated devices was assessed under 25 °C and 85% RH (Supplementary Fig. 49). After 500 h, the TiO2–SO4 device retains over 91% of its initial PCE, compared to only about 77% for the TiO2 device. Moreover, the double-85 stability was tested on the encapsulated devices under 85 °C and 85% RH (Supplementary Fig. 50). After 300 h, the TiO2–SO4 device maintains >80% of its initial PCE, significantly outperforming the TiO2 device, which retains merely ~43%. Notably, as summarized in Supplementary Table 6, our CsPbI3 C-PSCs without HTL achieve unprecedented dual stability, exceeding 1000 h for both operational (T85) and thermal (85 °C) stress, marking the first report of such simultaneous long-term stability for this device architecture.

Discussion

We successfully deposited TiO2 films functionalized with -F, -Cl, and -SO4 groups via a one-step CBD method. The groups exhibiting higher Pb2+ complexation capacity (-F < -Cl < -SO4) promoted stronger [100] crystallographic orientation of the DMAPbI3 crystals. This enhanced [100] orientation facilitates more efficient mass transport (DMAI out, Cs+ in) and significantly accelerates the subsequent thermal conversion to CsPbI3. Consequently, we obtained a highly oriented, high-purity, and stable CsPbI3 crystal, enabling CsPbI3 C-PSCs to achieve a record PCE of 20.72%. Furthermore, the unencapsulated devices maintained over 85% of their initial PCE after 1156 h of MPP tracking under 1-sun illumination.

Methods

Materials

FTO glass (15 Ω/cm2), CsI (99.9 %), DMAI (99.5 %), PbI2 (99.99 %), Spiro-OMeTAD (99.9%), and [6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PC61BM, 99.5%) were purchased from Advanced Election Technology Co. Ltd. 4-tert-Butylpyridine (t-BP, 98.0%) and Lithium bis (trifluoromethylsulfonyl) imide (Li-TFSI, 99.0%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%, extra dry), Ethanol (99.5%), 1-butanol (99.7%), isopropanol (IPA, 99.5%), and Chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8%) were purchased from Acros Organics. TiCl4 (99.9%), Ti(SO4)2 (96%) and (NH4)2TiF6 (98%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co. Ltd. Titanium diisopropoxide (75 wt% in isopropanol) was purchased from TCI (Shanghai) Development Co. Ltd. Carbon paste was purchased from Guangzhou Seaside Technology Co. Ltd. All solvents and chemicals were obtained commercially and used as received without any purification.

Deposition of TiO2 films

FTO glass was cleaned with successive sonication for 30 min in deionized water, ethyl alcohol, and isopropyl alcohol, respectively, and then stored in an ethyl alcohol solution for further use.

TiO2: the cleaned FTO glass substrates were blown to dry and treated with UV-Ozone for 15 min, then the titanium diisopropoxide solution dissolved in 1-butanol (0.15 M) was spin-coated onto FTO glass at 2000 rpm for 20 s, and then annealed at 550 °C for 30 min.

TiO2–F: the cleaned FTO glass substrates were blown to dry and treated with UV-Ozone for 15 min, then were immersed in the mixed solution of 200 mL aqueous solution and 7.92 g (NH4)2TiF6 for 60 min at 70 °C to prepare the TiO2 layer. After that, the substrates were rinsed with ultrapure water and annealed at 180 °C for 40 min.

TiO2–Cl: the cleaned FTO glass substrates were blown to dry and treated with UV-Ozone for 15 min, then were immersed in the mixed solution of 200 mL aqueous solution and 4.5 mL TiCl4 for 60 min at 70 °C to prepare the TiO2 layer. After that, the substrates were rinsed with ultrapure water and annealed at 180 °C for 40 min.

TiO2–SO4: the cleaned FTO glass substrates were blown to dry and treated with UV-Ozone for 15 min, then were immersed in the mixed solution of 200 mL aqueous solution and 9.6 g Ti(SO4)2 for 60 min at 70 °C to prepare the TiO2 layer. After that, the substrates were rinsed with ultrapure water and annealed at 180 °C for 40 min.

Deposition of DMAPbI3 intermediate phase films

The precursor solution was prepared by dissolving CsI, PbI2, and DMAI in DMF with a molar ratio of 1:1.5:1.5 (CsI concentration is fixed at 1 M) and stirring for 12 h at room temperature.

Before the deposition, the cleaned FTO/TiO2 substrates were treated with UV-Ozone for 15 min. Then, the precursor solution was spin-coated on different TiO2/FTO substrates at 2000 rpm for 20 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 5 min. The whole procedure was conducted in an air atmosphere (RH < 20%).

Deposition of pure DMAPbI3 films

The precursor solution was prepared by dissolving PbI2 and DMAI in DMF with a molar ratio of 1:1 (CsI concentration is fixed at 1 M) and stirring for 12 h at room temperature.

The cleaned FTO/TiO2 substrates were treated with UV-Ozone for 15 min. Then, the precursor solution was spin-coated on different TiO2/FTO substrates at 2000 rpm for 20 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 5 min. The whole procedure was conducted in an air atmosphere (RH < 20%).

Deposition of CsPbI3 perovskites

One-step method

After obtaining the DMAPbI3 intermediate phase films, it was further annealed at 220 °C to obtain the CsPbI3 perovskite layer. The whole procedure was conducted in an air atmosphere (RH < 20%).

Two-step method

After obtaining the pure DMAPbI3 films, when cooling down to room temperature, it was immersed in a saturated DMAI ethanol solution for around 3 min to obtain the final CsPbI3 perovskite films.

Deposition of Spiro-OMeTAD

The Spiro-OMeTAD solution was prepared by dissolving Spiro-OMeTAD (90 mg) into chlorobenzene (1 mL), and then adding t-BP (36 µL) and Li-TFSI (22 µL, 520 mg in 1 mL acetonitrile) into the above mixture solution to stir over 12 h.

Then, the Spiro-OMeTAD solution was spin-coated onto the CsPbI3 perovskite films at 5000 rpm for 30 s. The whole procedure was conducted in an air atmosphere (RH < 20%).

Deposition of electrodes

Carbon

For fabricating C-PSCs, a carbon electrode was directly deposited on the perovskite films by using a commercial carbon paste that was purchased from Guangzhou Seaside Technology Co. ltd. The carbon paste was painted on the perovskite films at room temperature, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 30 min. The whole procedure was conducted in an air atmosphere (RH < 20%).

Au

For fabricating the Au-electrode CsPbI3 PSCs, a 70 nm Au electrode was deposited on the perovskite films both with and without Spiro-OMeTAD HTL by the physical vapor deposition method through a shadow mask to obtain the CsPbI3 devices with an active area of 0.04 cm2.

Fabrication of electron-only devices

To fabricate electron-only devices (FTO/TiO2/Perovskite/PC61BM/carbon) for measuring SCLC plots, a 10 mg/mL PC61BM solution dissolved in CB was spin-coated on the CsPbI3 films at 4000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, Carbon paste was painted on the PC61BM layer at room temperature and annealed at 100 °C for 30 min. The whole procedure was conducted in an air atmosphere (RH < 20%).

To fabricate electron-only devices (FTO/TiO2/Au) for measuring electron mobilities of TiO2 films, a 70 nm Au electrode was deposited on the TiO2 films by the physical vapor deposition method through a shadow mask.

Device testing

The photovoltaic performance was tested under a solar light simulator (Newport Oriel Sol 3A, model number 94063A, AM 1.5 global filter) in ambient air. The light intensity was calibrated to 1 Sun (100 mW/cm2) using an Oriel reference solar cell (monocrystalline silicon) and meter. Current density-voltage (J–V) curves, steady-state output, dependence of VOC on light intensity, Dark J–V measurement, and SCLC plots of the electron-only device were measured on ZENNIUM pro electrochemical workstation (ZAHNER-Elektrik GmbH & Co., KG, Germany). The active area of cells was masked at around 4 mm2. The scanning speed of J–V curves are is 100 mV/s for both forward and reverse scans.

Stability testing

The long-term operational stability was tested by exposing unencapsulated C-PSCs under 1-sun equivalent LED lamp in an air atmosphere (RH ~ 30%) at room temperature (~25 °C), and the MPP was automatically recalculated ever 30 min by tracking the J–V curves. For evaluating the long-term thermal stability, the unencapsulated devices were heated at 85 °C in N2 atmosphere, and the J–V curves were recorded periodically in ambient air atmosphere (RH ~ 30%).

Characterizations

XRD patterns were obtained by a Rigaku D/MAX-2500 X-ray diffractometer with an X-ray tube Cu Ka radiation. (λ = 1.5406 Å). Two-dimensional synchrotron radiation GIWAXS was performed at Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. SEM and EDS images were obtained on a SUPRA55 SEM at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. AFM and C-AFM images taken on a Bruker Dimension ICON. XPS spectra were obtained on an X-ray photoemission spectroscopy (ESCALab250Xi). PL spectra were recorded on a Nanolog FL3-2iHR (Horiba Jobin Yvon Ltd) with an excitation wavelength of 340 nm, while TRPL spectra were taken on an ultrafast lifetime Spectrofluorometer (Delta Flex), and a 475 nm ultrafast laser was used as the excitation light source. EL spectra, EQEEL were acquired through the Enlitech measurement system (LQ-100). The UV-vis spectra were acquired using a Shimadzu UV-3600 UV-vis spectrometer. The GIXRD was measured with a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (a wavelength of 1.5418 Å). EQE measurements were carried out using a QE-R EQE system (Enlitech, Taiwan). ToF-SIMS measurement was performed using a PHI NanoTOF II instrument (ULVAC-PHI, Inc.), where an Argon gas beam was used for erosion, and a 30 keV Bi+ pulsed primary ion beam was used for the analysis. Stress-strain measurement was obtained by a universal mechanical tester (Instron8871 W, China) at room temperature according to the GB/T 2567-2021 standard. HAADF-STEM images were acquired in a low-dose (<1500 e−/Å2) regime using a probe-corrected Thermo Fisher Titan Themis Z microscope operated at 300 kV with a probe semi-convergence angle of 21 mrad. The quasi-Fermi level splitting (QFLS) was measured using the LuminQY absolute PL quantum yield detection system (Fuxiang Optics). The static contact angles were measured using the sessile drop method via a goniometer (KRUSS DSA25S).

Computational methods

We performed first-principles pseudopotential calculations using the projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotentials in the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) code. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) approximation was used for the exchange-correlation functional. The cutoff energy was set to 450 eV, and a 2 × 2 × 1 Γ-centered Monkhorst–Pack K-point grid was adopted. The electronic energy convergence criterion was 1 × 10−5 eV, and the structures were relaxed until the forces on each atom were below 0.01 eV /Å. The van der Waals interactions were included in all of our calculations based on the DFT-D3 Grimme method. To simplify the calculations, we constructed five-layer slabs for both TiO2 and DMAPbI3 surfaces, fixing the bottom three layers in place to mimic a semi-infinite substrate. To avoid artificial interactions between periodic replicas of low-dimensional nanoclusters, a vacuum interval of 15 Å was introduced in all structures. Device model and simulation

The simplified physical models were established in COMSOL Multiphysics V6.2. The charge transport across the interfaces of CsPbI3 and TiO2 is computed using a continuous quasi-Fermi level model and drift-diffusion equations. The continuous quasi-Fermi level model was adopted as the theoretical framework and coupled with the drift-diffusion equations for numerical solution, exploring the charge transport behavior at the interface between CsPbI3 perovskite material and TiO2. The Schottky contact theory was employed to define appropriate boundary conditions and physical models, enabling the simulation of carrier injection and recombination processes at the perovskite/carbon interface.
