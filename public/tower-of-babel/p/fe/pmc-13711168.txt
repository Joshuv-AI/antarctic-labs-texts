Left atrial pressure continuous monitoring improves early postoperative outcomes after double-lung transplantation for pulmonary hypertension
Authors: Olaf Mercier, Luce Dedier, Justin Issard, Laurent Savale, Emeline Plubel, Dominique Fabre, Delphine Mitilian, Jean Baptiste Menager, Thibaut Genty, Iolanda Ion, Jérôme Le Pavec, Elie Fadel, Marie Lannelongue Transplantation Program
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDouble-lung transplantation (DLTx) has become the treatment of choice for patients with end-stage pulmonary hypertension (PH). However, DLTx remains a demanding procedure, associated with a greater incidence of severe primary graft dysfunction (PGD) compared with other indications. PH-induced cardiac remodeling includes diastolic left heart dysfunction, which may increase the risk of PGD. We hypothesized that continuous monitoring of left atrial pressure (LAP) could improve early outcomes.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the chart of patients who underwent DLTx for PH between 2015 and 2022. The use of postoperative LAP monitoring was left to the discretion of the attending transplant surgeon.ResultsA total of 135 patients underwent DLTx for PH. Among them, 72 were managed either with prophylactic postoperative extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) or with ECMO weaning guided by transesophageal echocardiography and Swan-Ganz catheter, whereas 63 underwent systematic ECMO weaning after the placement of a LAP catheter maintained for 3 to 5 days. Both groups were comparable regarding age, PH diagnoses, and disease severity. The LAP group showed significantly less postoperative ECMO use (25% vs 83%, P < .001), shorter duration of mechanical ventilation (5 days [2-13] vs 17.5 days [7-35], P < .001), shorter stay in the intensive care unit (10 days [6-25] vs 28 days [14-49], P < .001), and lower rates of bleeding, groin infection, and overall complications (P ≤ .05). Intraoperative red blood cell transfusion was identified as an independent predictor of LAP monitoring strategy failure. Ninety-day postoperative mortality was not significantly different between groups (1.5% vs 8.3%, P = .12).ConclusionsContinuous postoperative monitoring of LAP after DLTx for PH was associated with improved early outcomes. This strategy may help reduce the incidence of severe postoperative complications in this high-risk population.Key Words: critical care, pulmonary hypertension, lung transplantation

Shorter ICU stay and fewer Clavien-Dindo grade ≥3 complications in the LAP group.

Central Message

Left atrial pressure monitoring after lung transplantation for pulmonary hypertension was associated with shorter ventilation, fewer severe complications, less PGD grade 3, and shorter ICU stay.

Perspective

These findings, derived from a retrospective study in a rare disease population, warrant validation in multicenter registries and prospective trials. If confirmed, left atrial pressure monitoring could be extended to candidates for lung transplant with left heart diastolic dysfunction to optimize perioperative management and improve outcomes.

Despite major advances in the medical management of pulmonary hypertension (PH), lung and heart-lung transplantations remain the only solution for selected patients with end-stage refractory disease. Donor scarcity and the improved understanding of right ventricular reverse remodeling have led transplant teams worldwide to favor double-lung transplantation (DLTx) over heart-lung transplantation for patients with PH.1 However, DLTx remains a challenging procedure, associated with greater rates of primary graft dysfunction (PGD) and poorer long-term survival compared with other indications for lung transplantation.2,3 The mechanisms underlying the increased risk of PGD after DLTx for PH are not yet fully understood. The severity of PH remains the only well-recognized factor accounting for the susceptibility to PGD.4 Levels of pulmonary arterial pressure, right atrial pressure, and serum creatinine have been elucidated as PH-specific risk factors related to disease severity. Experimental evidence suggesting the presence of left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD) secondary to PH-induced myocardial fibrosis5 has prompted growing interest in LVDD as determinant of posttransplant outcomes in patients with PH.6, 7, 8 Interestingly, global left heart diastolic dysfunction, including left atrium involvement, has been identified as a risk factor for PGD and adverse outcomes regardless of the underlying indication for lung transplantation.8,9

Advances in cardiac imaging, including echocardiography10 and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging,11 now allow for improved screening, diagnosis, and grading of LVDD before transplantation, thereby helping clinicians assess the risk of PGD at the time of listing. Importantly, the presence or severity of LVDD does not preclude the benefit of transplantation, because cardiac function recovers after DLTx, with no significant differences in 1-year survival or cardiac adverse event between patients with or without diastolic dysfunction.12 Nevertheless, LVDD has an impact on early postoperative management, because left heart filling pressures may increase markedly in response to fluid shifts, increasing the risk of postcapillary elevated pressure and lung edema.

The use of postoperative prophylactic extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) in patients with PH was shown to improve outcomes by mitigating the consequences of severe PGD during the initial phase of cardiac reverse remodeling.13,14 However, prolonged ECMO exposes patients to substantial risks, including bleeding, vascular complications, limb ischemia, and infection.

We hypothesized that continuous monitoring of left atrial pressure (LAP) during the early postoperative period would allow optimized management of LVDD without the need for prophylactic ECMO by providing in real-time assessment of intravascular volume status and early detection of increasing postcapillary pressures before the development of lung edema. The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of the use of LAP monitoring on early postoperative outcomes.

Methods

The institutional review board of the French Society of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery approved this study (CERC-ctcv-42271) and waived the need for informed consent in compliance with French law on retrospective studies of anonymized data. We retrospectively collected data from patients with PH who underwent DLTx between January 2015 and December 2022 at Marie Lannelongue Hospital. The donor characteristics were obtained from the French National Transplant Database of the Agence de la Biomédecine. Patients were divided into 2 groups depending on whether postoperative management was guided by LAP monitoring (Figure 1). PH was defined by a mean pulmonary arterial pressure >20 mm Hg, a pulmonary arterial occlusion pressure ≤15 mm Hg, and a pulmonary vascular resistance >2 Wood units, in accordance with the 2022 European Society of Cardiology/European Respiratory Society guidelines.15

Flow diagram of patient selection. All consecutive lung transplant recipients during the study period were assessed for eligibility. Patients with pulmonary hypertension were identified and included in the analysis and subsequently stratified according to the use of left atrial pressure monitoring. ESC/ERS, European Society of Cardiology/European Respiratory Society; ECMO, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation.

Lung Procurement

A standard technique was used for double-lung harvesting.16 Donor assessment included routine arterial blood gas analyses, a radiograph of the chest, bronchoscopy, and visual inspection of the lungs. Donor organ preservation was achieved by injecting 500 mg of prostaglandin E1 into the pulmonary artery, then infusing 4 L anterograde and 1 L retrograde of PERFADEX Plus (XVIVO). The lungs were preserved using 4 °C cold static storage. In donors after circulatory death, ex vivo lung perfusion was systematically performed in accordance with the Agence de la Biomédecine protocol. In the present study, ex vivo lung perfusion was used exclusively in this setting.

DLTx and Left Atrial (LA) Catheter

We used the standard sequential lung transplantation procedure described by Kaiser and colleagues.17 Sternum-sparing anterolateral thoracotomies or a clamshell incision was performed depending on the surgeon's preference. Heart and lung support was provided by central or peripheral ECMO.18,19 The use of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was exceptional and restricted to rare cases of pulmonary artery aneurysm repair. Patients who were bridged were kept on peripheral ECMO during the procedure. Dissection of the intrapericardial pulmonary artery and veins was performed and lungs implanted on each side. At the end of the procedure, patients in the “standard of care” group (control group) were managed either with prophylactic postoperative ECMO or with ECMO weaning guided by transesophageal echocardiography and Swan-Ganz catheter monitoring.20,21 Conversely, patients in the LAP monitoring group underwent a systematic attempt at ECMO weaning after the placement of a LA catheter (Centracath 18G-L30 cm; Vygon) maintained for 3 to 5 days. The choice of posttransplant procedure management was left to surgeon preference.

The LA catheter was inserted through the left atrioventricular groove before ECMO weaning (Video 1). A PROLENE 5/0 suture purse (Ethicon) prevented displacement and bleeding at the time of removal. Care was taken to deair the pressure line and the catheter to avoid any air embolism. Preventive anticoagulation was administered throughout the hospitalization period. Removal occurred at the patient's bedside, in the intensive care unit, around postoperative day 3 to 5 after mechanical ventilation weaning.

Early postoperative care in intensive care unit (ICU) was guided by LAP monitoring. The goal was to maintain an LAP less than 15 mm Hg. LAP monitoring was not used as a therapeutic target per se but rather as a guide for postoperative hemodynamic management. When LAP was elevated, clinicians generally avoided further fluid administration, promoted diuresis when appropriate, and relied preferentially on vasopressor support to maintain adequate systemic perfusion pressure. Conversely, when LAP was low, cautious fluid administration could be considered to optimize preload. In cases in which adequate systolic blood pressure or oxygenation could not be maintained with optimal medical management, ECMO was considered. LAP monitoring strategy failure was defined as the need for ECMO or the occurrence of PGD grade 3.

Statistical Analyses

The impact of continuous LAP monitoring on post-DLTx outcomes was assessed by comparing 2 groups of patients according to the use of postoperative LAP monitoring. Continuous variables were described as mean ± SD or median [interquartile range] and compared using the Student t test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test depending on whether the distribution was gaussian or not. Categorical variables were described as number (%) and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Univariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with LAP monitoring strategy failure and PGD grade 3. Given the limited number of events, multivariable models were restricted to a maximum of 2 variables selected on the basis of clinical relevance and univariable results (P < .10). Results were expressed as odds ratios with 95% CIs. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Preoperative Characteristics

A total of 135 patients underwent DLTx for PH (Table 1). Among them, 63 patients received LAP monitoring at the end of the procedure before weaning from cardiopulmonary support. Baseline characteristics, including age and disease severity, were similar between groups. Donor characteristics were also comparable with respect to age, total lung capacity ratio, partial pressure of oxygen/inspired oxygen fraction ratio, and cytomegalovirus mismatch. Donors after circulatory death were used exclusively in the control group.

Intraoperative Characteristics

The surgical approach, ischemic times, lung volume reduction, and rate of delayed chest closure did not differ between groups (Table 2). The control group included a greater proportion of patients bridged with peripheral ECMO (25.3% vs 6.2%, P = .07). Peripheral ECMO was the predominant cardiopulmonary support strategy in the control group (73.6%), whereas central ECMO was more commonly used in the LAP monitoring group (63.5%). Patients in the LAP monitoring group were discharged from the operating room without ECMO in 95% of cases, compared with 36% in the control group (P < .001). Overall, although a similar proportion of patients received intraoperative transfusions, those in the LAP monitoring group required significantly fewer packed red blood cell (RBC), fresh-frozen plasma, and platelet units.

Postoperative Outcomes

Patients in the LAP monitoring group experienced significantly lower rates of PGD, postoperative complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥3), blood product transfusion, surgical-site infections, femoral vessel complications, and pulmonary embolism (Table 3). This was associated with a shorter ICU length of stay (10 days [6-25] vs 28 days [14-49], P < .01) and reduced duration of mechanical ventilation (5 days [2-13] vs 17.5 days [7-35.25], P < .01), whereas 90-day mortality was similar between groups (1.5% vs 8.3%, P = .12). Fluid balance was significantly more balanced in the LAP monitoring group, without a corresponding reduction in the need for dialysis. The incidence of PGD requiring ECMO and the use of ECMO for indications other than PGD were comparable between groups. No complications were directly attributable to the LA catheter. In logistic regression analyses (Figure 2), the number of intraoperative RBC units transfused was identified as an independent risk factor for LAP monitoring strategy failure. Although LAP monitoring was the only variable associated with a lower risk of PGD grade 3 in univariable analysis (Table 4), this association did not remain significant after multivariable adjustment.

Discussion

Our study suggests that LAP monitoring enables safe weaning from cardiopulmonary support at the end of DLTx for PH and is associated with lower rates of severe complications and PGD grade 3, reduced transfusion requirements, and shorter durations of mechanical ventilation and ICU stay. Increased intraoperative RBC transfusion was associated with failure of the LAP-guided strategy (Figure 3).

Overview of study design and key outcomes associated with LAP monitoring after lung transplantation for pulmonary hypertension. LAP, Left arterial pressure; ECMO, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; LV, left ventricle; ICU, intensive care unit; PGD, primary graft dysfunction; RBC, red blood cells.

Perioperative fluid management during DLTx is critical, particularly in patients with PH, in whom transplantation may unmask LVDD.7,8 Hypervolemia is a well-established contributor to posttransplant morbidity, including respiratory complications, prolonged ICU stay, and extended mechanical ventilation.22,23 Excessive intraoperative fluid administration is associated with an increased risk of PGD grade 3, with each additional liter increasing the risk by approximately 22%.7 International consensus recommendations support a restrictive fluid-management strategy after lung transplantation while emphasizing the need to mitigate renal morbidity.24 Continuous LAP monitoring provides a practical tool to guide volume status, titrate fluid administration and inotropic support, and potentially limit the adverse effects of restrictive strategies. In our cohort, dialysis rates were not increased in the LAP group, despite near-neutral fluid balance over the first 72 hours. LAP monitoring also facilitates the early identification of patients requiring ECMO when optimal fluid management fails to maintain adequate gas exchange or hemodynamic stability, as reflected by similar durations of postoperative ECMO between groups.

Although CPB remains necessary in rare situations such as repair of giant pulmonary artery aneurysms (>6 cm),25,26 venoarterial (VA)-ECMO is increasingly favored for intraoperative support during DLTx in PH.19 Compared with CPB, VA-ECMO requires less anticoagulation, induces less inflammation, is associated with a lower risk of PGD, and can be continued postoperatively in cases of hemodynamic or respiratory instability. Prolonged VA-ECMO has even been proposed as a prophylactic strategy,27 with potential survival benefits, particularly in PH, by supporting right ventricular reverse remodeling. However, extended ECMO exposure increases bleeding and infectious complications. In our cohort, LAP monitoring enabled successful weaning from ECMO at the end of the procedure in 95% of patients, with similar rates of secondary ECMO in the ICU compared with control patients, including those managed with prophylactic ECMO. These findings suggest that LAP monitoring is safe and may help identify patients who do not require prophylactic ECMO, thereby avoiding unnecessary ECMO-related complications. Notably, patients bridged to transplantation were more frequently maintained on ECMO, despite representing those with the longest exposure and greatest complication risk. The 3 patients who could not be weaned using LAP monitoring had postoperative ECMO durations comparable with those managed with a prophylactic strategy, supporting attempts at early ECMO discontinuation even in bridged patients. Finally, a high intraoperative RBC transfusion requirement was independently associated with failure of the LAP-guided strategy, suggesting that in the setting of significant bleeding and hemodynamic instability, prophylactic ECMO may be preferable.

Regarding safety, LA catheterization is routinely used in cardiac surgery. A large series reported a low complication rate of 0.24%,27 with bleeding and catheter rupture during removal being the most common events. In our cohort, no catheter-related complications were observed, supporting the safety of our protocol. Discontinuation of heparin 4 hours before removal and early removal by a thoracic surgeon may have contributed to minimizing the risks of bleeding and catheter rupture.

The retrospective design of the study introduces inherent biases, particularly as LA catheter placement was left to the surgeon's discretion without standardized criteria. Nevertheless, surgeons contributed to both groups, and no surgeon exclusively used or exclusively avoided LA pressure monitoring. The overall distribution of patients between groups remained balanced, resulting in comparable sample sizes. However, differences in ECMO management may have contributed to the observed outcomes. Hence, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and warrant confirmation in multicenter studies, randomized trials or international registries. Our cohort predominantly comprised group 1-PH. However, LVDD may also be relevant in more prevalent conditions, including group 3 PH and other lung diseases. The applicability of LAP monitoring in these populations requires further investigation. In addition, patients managed with prophylactic ECMO were not extubated early in our cohort, leading to longer mechanical ventilation duration. A multidisciplinary awake ECMO program might have reduced the duration of mechanical ventilation and postoperative complications, although the relatively short median duration of prophylactic ECMO (3 days) makes the existence of significant benefits uncertain.

Conclusions

Continuous postoperative LAP monitoring after DLTx for PH was associated with improved early outcomes and a lower incidence of severe postoperative complications including PGD grade 3. This strategy may be particularly valuable in this high-risk population and warrants evaluation in other conditions associated with LVDD. High intraoperative RBC transfusion requirements were associated with failure of the LAP-monitoring strategy, suggesting that prophylactic ECMO support may be preferable in such settings.
