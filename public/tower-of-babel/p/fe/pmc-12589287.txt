Consecutive non-Aspergillus Fungal Invasive Infections in Chronic Granulomatous Disease: Data from the French National Reference Center for Primary ImmunoDeficiencies and literature review
Authors: Leïla Lefevre, Olivier Paccoud, Bénédicte Neven, Marie-Elisabeth Bougnoux, Mickaël Alligon, Julie Bruneau, Alain Fischer, Despina Moshous, Jacinta Bustamante, Capucine Picard, Sylvain Poiree, Romain Guery, Martin Castelle, Felipe Suarez, Ambroise Marcais, Morgane Cheminant, Claire Rouzaud, Dea Garcia Hermoso, Hélène Salvator, Emilie Catherinot, Benoit Pilmis, Luminita Luca, Jean-Paul Brion, Matthieu Revest, Virginie Gandemer, Lucie Lelievre, Muriel Alvarez, Anne Conrad, Fanny Fouyssac, Catherine Gaud, Sophie Blumental, Stéphane Blanche, Olivier Lortholary, Nizar Mahlaoui, Fanny Lanternier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-Aspergillus invasive fungal infections (NAFI) are increasingly reported in patients with Chronic Granulomatous Disease (CGD), but precise clinical descriptions remain scarce.Objective and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of NAFI cases among CGD patients in the French National Registry of Primary Immunodeficiencies (CEREDIH) and in a comprehensive literature review.ResultsWe identified 16 proven NAFI (9 molds, 6 yeasts and 1 Pneumocystis) among 263 CGD patients from CEREDIH and included an additional 106 probable/proven NAFI from a literature review (75 molds, 29 yeasts, 1 Pneumocystis, 1 dimorphic). Mold NAFI occurred at a median age of 17 years [IQR 9–23], and were mostly located to the lungs (79%, 65/82). Mold NAFI were breakthrough in 59% of patients (35/59), and 24% were receiving immunosuppressive treatments (13/54, mostly high-dose corticosteroids, n = 11). Lung surgical biopsies yielded the highest diagnostic rate (39/39) compared to less invasive methods (BAL 8/18 and transthoracic punctures 8/12). Nine patients with mold NAFI, including 3 refractory cases, were cured after Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation (HSCT). Overall mortality for mold NAFI was 25% (20/81). Yeast infections occurred at a median age of 5 years [IQR 0–13], and 36% were receiving immunosuppressive treatments (5/14, mostly anti-TNF agents, n = 4). Infections were frequently located to lymph nodes or lungs, and 64% (21/33) were disseminated. Two yeast NAFI were cured after HSCT. Mortality was 26% (7/27).ConclusionNAFI in CGD patients are frequently severe, often occur despite prophylaxis and under additional immunosuppression, commonly require invasive procedures for diagnosis, and may be effectively managed with HSCT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10875-025-01903-0.

Introduction

Chronic Granulomatous Disease (CGD) is a primary immunodeficiency (PID), or inborn error of immunity. CGD is caused by genetic defects affecting the function or regulation of the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase complex, resulting in increased susceptibility to both infections and inflammatory complications. Among PIDs, CGD is associated with the highest lifetime incidence of invasive fungal infections (IFI). Indeed, in a French cohort, up to 50% of CGD patients experienced at least one IFI during their lifetime [1–3]. Despite the widespread use of antifungal prophylaxis, IFI remain the leading cause of death among CGD patients [4].

While Aspergillus species are the most frequently implicated pathogens—accounting for 55% of probable and proven IFI in a prior French study [3], non-Aspergillus fungal infections (NAFI) are increasingly reported in CGD patients [5, 6]. This trend may reflect both improved fungal identification techniques and the selective pressure of antifungal prophylaxis, which can favor the emergence of less common fungal pathogens, as previously observed in patients with hematologic malignancies [7].

These infections are rare, often difficult to diagnose and treat, and remain poorly characterized in the literature, with most data limited to isolated case reports or small series (reviewed in Dotis et al., 2013, and Henriet et al., 2013 [5, 8]). In a previous French cohort study, 26 NAFI cases were mentioned but not analyzed in detail [3]. Herein, we aimed to provide a thorough description of NAFI from the French CGD cohort and from a comprehensive literature review.

Methods

Patients

French Cohort

We performed a cross-sectional analysis of the medical records of all CGD patients diagnosed with a NAFI in France from 1986 to 2020. In France, patients diagnosed with CGD phenotypically and/or genetically are included in the French National Reference Center for PID (CEREDIH) registry [9]). Patient medical data, including the occurrence of infections, are prospectively recorded. We included patients with proven or probable IFI according to the 2019 European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer and Mycoses Study Group (EORTC/MSG) criteria [10]. We excluded non-invasive infections, cases of possible IFI, and cases occurring after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). All patient records were reviewed by two physicians (LL, FL). Written and informed consent was obtained from patients and/or their legal guardians by the attending clinician upon enrollment in the CEREDIH registry.

Literature Review

In addition, we performed a comprehensive review of the literature from 1976 to 2021 with PubMed, using a list of keywords (detailed in the Supplementary Material). References from relevant articles were screened for additional cases. We excluded duplicates, those with no genetic disease identified, and those lacking sufficient information to classify the infection as proven or probable according to the EORTC/MSG 2019 criteria [10].

Data Collection and Definitions

For each patient, we collected data on demographics, infectious and inflammatory complications of CGD [2], antifungal prophylaxis, immunosuppressive treatments, NAFI characteristics, and outcomes (case-fatality ratio and cause of death). Dissemination was defined as the involvement of at least two non-contiguous sites or fungemia. Cure was defined as complete or partial response 90 days after the start of treatment based on the 2019 EORTC criteria, with no evidence of relapse. Previously published definitions of inflammatory flares were used [11]. Mulch pneumonitis was defined as acute bilateral pneumonia occurring following massive inhalation of spores and hyphae [12].

Data Analysis

We performed a pooled analysis of NAFI cases from the French cohort and of those from the literature review. For each variable, missing data were excluded from the analysis. Tables were generated using Stata Software (StataCorp. 2019. Stata Statistical Software: Release 16. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and proportions, and continuous data are presented using medians and their associated interquartile range (IQR).

Results

Distribution of Fungal Species Among Cases of NAFI

We identified 28 cases of NAFI among a total of 263 CGD patients from the CEREDIH registry, as of April 2020. We excluded five possible IFI, two non-invasive infections, two cases with missing clinical data, and three cases occurring after HSCT. Sixteen cases of NAFI from the French cohort, diagnosed between 1986 and 2018, were therefore included in the final analysis. These NAFI included (Table 1): Acremonium (n = 1), Arthrographis (n = 1), Mucorales (n = 1), Paecilomyces (n = 1), Phaeoacremonium (n = 1), Pleurostomophora (n = 1), Rasamsonia (n = 1), Scedosporium (n = 2), Candida (n = 5), Trichosporon (n = 1) and Pneumocystis jirovecii (n = 1). Characteristics of Patients According to Type of Infection

In pooled analysis, the median age of patients at diagnosis of NAFI was 13 years [interquartile range, IQR: 5–21], 90% were male (104/111). Among patients from the French series experiencing multiple episodes of IFI during follow-up, NAFI was the first documented IFI in nearly two third of the patients (9/14, 64%). Furthermore, a yeast NAFI was the inaugural sign of CGD in two patients from the French series, leading to the diagnosis of CGD.

Characteristics of patients developing yeast or mold NAFI according to fungal species are detailed in Table 3. Mold NAFI tended to occur at older ages compared to yeast infections. Sex distribution did not differ between yeast and mold NAFI (respectively 4/32 and 7/76 female patients). In pooled data, 27% (18/68) of patients experiencing mold or yeast NAFI were receiving immunosuppressive treatments (with the same proportion in the French series: 3/9 molds and 2/6 yeasts). For mold NAFI, immunosuppression mostly involved high-dose corticosteroids (i.e. > 0.5 mg/kg/d) in 11 literature cases and TNF-inhibitors in 3 cases. The most common mold NAFI occurring in patients receiving immunosuppressive therapies was Mucorales (6/17, 35%). Candida NAFI occurred following treatment with broad spectrum antibiotics in 64% cases (7/11) and in patients receiving immunosuppressive treatments in 38% cases (5/13, TNF-inhibitors [n = 4] and/or high-dose corticosteroids [n = 3]). The two Pneumocystis NAFI occurred while under high dose corticosteroids. Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis (Table 3)

Mold Infections

The lungs were the main site involved in mold NAFI, however half of mold infections presented without respiratory signs (26/58, 45%). Fever was also reported in less than half of cases (23/58, 40%). Mulch pneumonitis was reported in nine cases, and involved frequent coinfections (Aspergillus sp., Mucorales, Penicilium and Rasamasonia). Of note, lung infections were often associated with contiguous spread to the thoracic wall (14/65, 22%), heart (4/65), and vertebrae (1/65). Cardiac involvement was either pericarditis (n = 2) and/or endocarditis (n = 3). Phaeohyphomycoses presented as lung infections in most cases (10/12), associated with concurrent central nervous system (CNS) involvement in four cases. Overall, CNS involvement was reported in 21% of mold NAFI cases mostly due to dissemination (17/81, excluding one case of Lomentospora prolificans isolated brain abscess). Neuro-ophtalmologic signs were found in less than half of the cases (6/14) in patients with a CNS involvement. Dissemination from lungs was reported in more than a quarter of cases (22/79, 28%) but was more frequent among the phaeohyphomycoses (5/10) with secondary locations in the CNS or bones). CNS extension from lungs was also reported in Inonotus tropicalis, Rasamsonia argillacea, Rhizopus sp. and Scedosporium apiospermum. A third of mucormycoses had secondary locations in the liver, the spleen, and/or the kidney (5/17). Notably, half of the Paecilomyces NAFI were extra-pulmonary (4/9), including isolated bones and surrounding tissues (n = 2), spleen and liver (n = 1) and combined liver and bone involvement (n = 1). Inonotus tropicalis and Phellinus sp. also frequently (respectively 4/7 and 3/6) presented with extra-pulmonary involvement, including bone, soft tissues or lymph node locations.

In the diagnostic workup of mold pulmonary NAFI, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was less informative than surgical biopsies both in the French cohort and in pooled cases (Table 4). Three of the 18 positive BAL were mulch pneumonitis. Among the remaining 15 positive BAL, eight had a positive direct examination without species identification in culture. Subcutaneous abscess punctures were highly specific in identifying mold NAFI. Diagnosis of CNS involvement was more challenging: lumbar puncture evaluations were never diagnostic (0/6), and initial cerebral biopsies were sometimes inconclusive, requiring several attempts at cerebral biopsies to obtain a microbiological diagnosis. In the French series, median delay between first symptoms and mold NAFI diagnosis was 8 months [IQR: 2–11]. Yeast Infections

The clinical presentation of Trichosporon and Candida infections differed (Table 3). Candida infections were mostly disseminated (20/25, 80%), involving lymph nodes in half of cases (12/25), with frequent fever (13/16) and/or hepatosplenomegaly (7/16). Two cases of CNS involvement (meningitis and cerebral abscess) were reported during disseminated candidiasis in children under five years of age. One case disseminated to the eyes [63]. In contrast, Trichosporon cases were often paucisymptomatic (4/5 were asymptomatic at diagnosis) involving a single site, mostly the lungs. There was one isolated CNS Trichosporon infection.

Diagnosis of Candida infections relied mostly on lymph nodes biopsy when available (9/9). Candida NAFI were also diagnosed with blood cultures in 9/12 and liver biopsies in 2/2 cases. Among the two cases of cerebral candidiasis, one had a positive lumbar puncture culture, the other had meningitis without positive culture but diagnosis was confirmed through a brain biopsy. Trichosporon infections were diagnosed through surgical biopsies (lung 4/4, bone 1/1 and brain 1/1) or transthoracic puncture (2/2). In the French series, median time from symptom onset to diagnosis of yeast-related NAFI was 1 month [IQR: 0–1.2].

Treatment & Outcome

Overall, the first line of antifungals after fungal identification included mostly azoles for molds and liposomal amphotericin B for yeasts (Table 3). Pneumocystis cases were treated with high dose trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (TMP/SMX). Focusing on the French series, patients received a median of 3 [IQR: 2–4] antifungal treatment lines for mold NAFI and 2 [IQR: 1–2] for yeast NAFI. Median treatment duration was 18 months [IQR: 7–39] for mold infections and 3 months [IQR: 2–6] for yeast infections. Across pooled data, an adjuvant treatment was frequently required, mainly surgery (Table 3). Interferon-gamma (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$IFN-\gamma$$\end{document}) or granulocyte transfusions (GTX) were performed in more than a quarter of cases (for mold NAFI: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$IFN-\gamma$$\end{document} was used in 54% cases, 36/67 and GTX in 21% cases, 12/57; for yeast NAFI: GTX was used in 43% cases, 6/14). Eleven patients with NAFI underwent HSCT within the year following the diagnosis of NAFI. Three of these patients were progressing by the time of the procedure (Scedosporium, n = 2, Mucorales, n = 1); seven were stabilized or already cured (Paecilomyces n = 1, Scedosporium, n = 1, Mucorales, n = 2 French series, Candida, n = 2 French series, Inonotus, n = 2); one lacked outcome details (a Rasamsonia). As of April 2020, all patients with NAFI were cured after the HSCT.

The overall case-fatality rate was 24% (27/111). Excluding mulch pneumonitis, case-fatality rates varied by fungal genus: Penicillium 100% (2/2), Phaeoacremonium 75% (3/4), Mucorales 38% (5/13), Rasamsonia 33% (4/12), Scedosporium 33% (2/6), Candida 32% (6/19), Trichosporon 14% (1/7); No fatalities were reported in infections caused by Acremonium (0/3), Arthrographis (0/2), Chladophialophora (0/3), Inonotus (0/6), Paecilomyces (0/9), Phellinus (0/7), or Pneumocystis. All fatal cases from the French cohort (n = 3) involved CNS extension of mold NAFI (Scedosporium, n = 2; Rasamsonia, n = 1). Phaeohyphomycoses altogether had a case-fatality rate of 33% (4/12). Mulch pneumonitis presentation had a fatality rate of 63% (5/8). When detailed, patients who died from Candida NAFI were either infant (n = 4), and/or presented as a hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (n = 3) and/or as a disseminated candidiasis (n = 6). Finally, the case-fatality rate decreased over the study period: before 2010 the case-fatality rate was 33% (14/43) and 29% (6/21) for mold and yeast NAFI respectively, while after 2010 they were respectively at 18% (6/33) and 17% (1/6) for mold and yeast NAFI.

Discussion

In this French national observational retrospective study, we collected 16 episodes of NAFI among 263 CGD patients over 31 years. These data were further supported by a thoroughly conducted literature review. Despite the evident publication bias and the extensive time span over which it was conducted, this study highlights notable observations regarding the features of NAFI in patients with CGD.

The first noteworthy feature of NAFI in patients with CGD is their frequent occurrence in the setting of additional immunosuppression. This risk factor seems to vary depending on fungal species. For instance, among molds, Paecilomyces, Scedosporium and Mucorales infections were mostly reported in patients receiving immunosuppressive treatments. With the exception of mulch pneumonitis, mucormycosis are rarely reported in CGD, and typically occurs in the context of additional immunosuppression, such as high-dose corticosteroids or during the neonatal period [8, 24]. However, almost half cases of mucormycoses (excluding mulch pneumonitis) occurred without any additional immunosuppressive treatments. Among yeasts, invasive Candida infections have been reported in CGD [5, 8]. The major risks identified were the neonatal period and immunosuppressors, including, recently TNF-inhibitor treatment [8, 28]. Whether TNF-inhibitors could be an emerging risk factor for Candida invasive infection in CGD needs to be properly addressed in future studies. Finally, Pneumocystis pneumonia is not a distinctive infection of CGD [5, 8]. This observation is consistent with all Pneumocystis cases reported here, as they occurred in patients with immunosuppression (high dose corticosteroids).

This study highlights the concept of breakthrough IFI deriving from observations in oncohematology patients under antifungal prophylaxis is applicable to CGD patients and NAFI [7]. Indeed, our study confirmed that mold NAFI could occur despite long-term prophylaxis. Mold NAFI were breakthrough infections in 100% of cases from the French cohort and more than half cases in the pooled analysis shared this feature. Interestingly, certain molds—including Arthrographis, Penicillium, Phaeoacremonium, Inonotus, Rasamsonia, Scedosporium and Phellinus —were more frequently reported in patients receiving mold-active prophylaxis than in those without prophylaxis. The reduced natural susceptibility of some of these fungi (Rasamsonia, Phaeoacremonium) to ITC, the current standard prophylactic antifungal agent used in CGD, could explain their emergence under prophylaxis [18, 19]. Posaconazole has been suggested as an alternative prophylaxis to ITC in the 2010 s [77]. Remarkably, we found four cases of mold NAFI occurring during posaconazole prophylaxis. Future studies are warranted to compare the respective risks of mold NAFI (as well as invasive aspergillosis, IA) under posaconazole versus ITC prophylaxis in CGD populations.

Diagnosis of mold NAFI in CGD patients was often delayed because of the paucity of symptoms and lack of specificity of the signs associated with NAFI [70]. As observed for IA, where exaggerated local inflammation with granuloma formation prevents angioinvasion leading to low sensitivity of indirect fungal markers and challenging microorganism documentation, similar findings were observed in this study for NAFI [10]. A significant proportion of NAFI cases were ultimately diagnosed after surgical biopsies. However, when accessible, sampling of superficial or localized sites such as subcutaneous abscesses or lymph nodes—particularly in cases of Candida infections—seemed to be diagnostically effective. In pulmonary or CNS NAFI, surgical lung biopsies and brain biopsies should be considered when non-invasive methods failed and should be repeated if needed. One exception though was mulch pneumonitis for which BAL was very informative. Given the often-silent progression and potentially fatal nature of secondary sites of infection, we advocate for a systematic extension work-up including at least brain MRI (or contrast-enhanced brain computed tomography) and thoraco-abdominopelvic computed tomography imaging.

Formulating universal treatment recommendations for NAFI remains difficult due to the heterogeneity of available reports. Nevertheless, long-term antifungal therapy is frequently required, and existing therapeutic guidelines for specific fungal pathogens generally apply to CGD patients as well [78–82]. Regarding new antifungals, olorofilm seems promising in vitro towards Scedosporium and Rasamsonia [80], and isavuconazole towards Scedosporium apiospermum [83]. However isavuconazole displayed variable Minimum Inhibitory Concentrations according to Mucorales species and high Minimum Inhibitory Concentrations for Rasamsonia [84]). These two drugs are awaiting approval in the therapeutic strategy of NAFI. Finally, antifungal strategies for other emerging fungi are poorly defined (Arthrographis, Inonotus…). Here we reported adjunctive surgery in half cases of mold NAFI. The same proportion of adjunctive surgery was found in IA at the National Institutes of Health (NIH, [4]). Other adjuvant therapies such as those contributing to restoring neutrophils activity can be part of NAFI treatment. The use of GTX is still debatable due to the risk of alloimmunization that could compromise engraftment in subsequent HSCT [71]. A retrospective NIH study reported an 82% rate of partial or complete response in patients with severe or refractory IFI treated with GTX [85]. However, GTX failure were more frequent in the context of NAFI, particularly in infections caused by Mucorales, Rasamsonia, and Scedosporium, with treatment failure observed in four of twelve mold-related cases.

Likewise, robust evidence are lacking to recommend the use of IFN-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} and anti-inflammatory drugs as adjuvant therapies in IFI in CGD [86]. HSCT appeared as a promising salvage therapy in CGD patients with hard-to-treat, and even under progression mold NAFI, but warrants further study.

Finally, our data underscore the significant case-fatality rate associated with NAFI in CGD. While mortality has decreased over time—reaching 18% after 2010 – it may still exceed that of IA, for which the case-fatality rate was reported at 9% in the NIH cohort as of 2012 [4]. Given NAFI’s persistent high mortality risk, their frequent occurrence despite prophylaxis, and the apparent role of additional immunosuppressive therapies as predisposing factor, HSCT should be systematically considered for every CGD patient requiring further immunosuppression.

Conclusions

This study provides a detailed description of the features of NAFI occurring in patients with CGD, highlighting several characteristics of interest in clinical practice. These findings could assist physicians in the management of these rare, difficult-to-treat and deadly infections. Future collaborative efforts should aim to compile all NAFI, especially detailing the prophylaxis, the diagnostic methods and the identification at fungal species level, to keep improving our understanding and optimize patient outcomes, including after HSCT.
