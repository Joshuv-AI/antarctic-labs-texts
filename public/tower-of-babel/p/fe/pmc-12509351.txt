Development of a non-contact screening approach for identifying oral function high-risk older adults using jaw movement and diadochokinetic performance
Authors: Chen-Fu Hung, Min Hsu, Hsiu-Yueh Liu, Mong-Fong Horng, Chia-Chi Yang, Lan-Yuen Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdentifying older adults at high risk of progressive oral functional decline is a critical public-health priority, as early decline often manifests initially as subtle swallowing difficulties. Standard diagnostic tools (e.g., VFSS, FEES) are invasive, whereas most existing screening methods assess only a single functional dimension. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate a fully non-invasive, multidimensional screening approach that integrates high-resolution video-based jaw-movement analysis with diadochokinetic (DDK) performance to enable the early detection of older adults at risk.MethodEighty-nine participants aged 65–95 years were recruited from community care stations in Taiwan. Participants were classified as high risk for oral functional decline if they performed fewer than three swallows within 30 s on the repetitive saliva swallowing test (RSST). The assessment included DDK performance and jaw movement analysis using video tracking. Logistic regression was used to identify significant predictors, followed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to evaluate diagnostic performance.ResultsAmong the 89 participants (mean age = 77.6 years; 22 men), 41 (46%) met the RSST high-risk criterion. Female participants exhibited significantly faster jaw-movement velocities compared to male participants (p < 0.05). Multivariate logistic regression confirmed age, DDK performance, and jaw-kinematic variables as independent predictors of high-risk status. A combined model of key DDK measures and average jaw-closing angular velocity achieved optimal discrimination (AUC = 0.83) and high sensitivity (0.95), outperforming individual predictors.ConclusionThis study presents a practical, cost-effective, and non-contact screening method for early identification of oral function high risk in older adults. Integrating jaw movement with DDK performance offers high sensitivity, supporting potential use in early clinical interventions and community-based screening programs.Trial registrationISRCTN11010971 (https//www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN11010971). Introduction

Oral function is fundamental to human life activities. It involves complex and coordinated processes such as chewing, salivation, bolus formation, articulation, and swallowing. These functions depend on the integrity and interaction of the nervous system, musculature, and oral structures (1). With global population aging, a decline in oral function has become a growing concern that threatens the health and quality of life of older adults.

According to the Japanese Society of Gerodontology, oral hypofunction is defined as a progressive decline of multiple oral functions resulting from aging or diseases. This condition encompasses seven key domains: oral dryness, poor oral hygiene, reduced occlusal force, decreased tongue pressure, masticatory dysfunction, articulation disorders, and swallowing difficulties [1].

Additionally, Tanaka et al. (2018) proposed the related concept of oral frailty, emphasizing its significance as an early indicator of physical frailty and mortality risk, which is defined through six assessment items [2].

However, both oral hypofunction and oral frailty have clearly established diagnostic criteria and scopes, which may limit their direct applicability to frontline or community-based screening. Therefore, this study adopts the term oral function high-risk group, emphasizing a multidimensional, non-invasive preliminary screening approach aimed at early identification of individuals at potential risk of oral function decline.

It is important to note that while oral hypofunction, oral frailty, and swallowing difficulty are closely related clinically, they differ fundamentally. The former two concepts represent broad frameworks evaluating multiple dimensions of oral function, whereas swallowing difficulty specifically refers to impairments in the swallowing process itself [2].

Thus, identifying the oral function high-risk group through precise screening methods could enable earlier preventive interventions to delay or reduce the progression toward significant oral hypofunction or swallowing disorders.

As both frameworks were originally designed for clinical diagnosis and research categorization, their criteria may not fully align with the practical needs of frontline community screening.

Previous studies have indicated a high prevalence of oral function decline among older adults, with swallowing difficulty reported as one of its key manifestations, affecting approximately 53.8–63.1% of community-dwelling elderly individuals [3, 4]. Declines in oral function not only impair nutritional intake but may also trigger a series of adverse health outcomes, such as malnutrition, weight loss, sarcopenia, and general physical deterioration [5]. Furthermore, existing literature has highlighted a complex triangular relationship involving swallowing disorders, aspiration risk, and poor oral hygiene, emphasizing the broader implications of oral function decline for overall health [6]. Therefore, early identification and timely intervention targeting individuals within the oral function high-risk group are essential strategies for maintaining health and preventing further deterioration in older adults.

Clinically, videofluoroscopic swallowing study (VFSS) and fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) are considered the gold standards for assessing severe swallowing disorders [7–9]. However, these instrumental assessments are invasive, costly, and involve radiation exposure. These characteristics make them less suitable for large-scale, frontline screening in community settings. To address this, more accessible screening methods such as the repetitive saliva swallowing test (RSST) have been developed [10, 11]. The RSST is a widely used clinical screening tool for detecting swallowing difficulties, particularly in older adults. It evaluates the ability to perform voluntary saliva swallows within a fixed time window, making it a simple, quick, and non-invasive method for assessing oropharyngeal function [10]. Due to its ease of administration and good sensitivity for detecting aspiration risk, RSST is often adopted in community and geriatric care settings as a preliminary indicator of potential dysphagia. Several studies have demonstrated a significant correlation between RSST and VFSS in evaluating aspiration risk, with reported correlation coefficients of 0.614 and 0.581, respectively [10]. When using three swallows within 30 s as the cutoff, the sensitivity of RSST ranges from 0.80 to 0.98, and its specificity from 0.54 to 0.66. Another study further showed that RSST correctly identified 93% of individuals without swallowing risk and 69% of those at high risk [11]. Nonetheless, RSST captures only a single dimension swallowing frequency and does not reflect the complex neuromuscular control or biomechanical dynamics involved in oral function.

To achieve a more comprehensive assessment of oral function, recent studies have explored new evaluative dimensions. The diadochokinetic (DDK), measured through the rapid repetition of syllables such as /pa/, /ta/, and /ka/, effectively reflects the neuromuscular coordination of the lips, tongue, and soft palate key components of oral motor function [12–14]. Empirical research has further quantified the relationship between DDK performance and swallowing risk. For instance, Takeuchi et al. found that a/pa/DDK rate below 6.2 syllables per second effectively distinguished older adults with declined oral function, as indicated by elevated Eating Assessment Tool-10 (EAT-10) scores [12]. Another study by Pu et al. reported that DDK performance was associated with signs of aspiration [15]. In parallel, jaw movement analysis provides a complementary biomechanical perspective. Parameters such as opening and closing velocity and angular changes are closely related to the activity of the suprahyoid muscles, which play a central role in initiating swallowing [16–18].

Given the multifaceted nature of oral function and the limitations of existing screening tools, this study proposes that integrating multiple assessment methods represents a promising direction for future research. Specifically, combining diadochokinetic (DDK), which reflects neuromuscular coordination, with jaw movement analysis, which captures biomechanical efficiency, may overcome the limitations of single-parameter tools. This integrated approach offers a more comprehensive, non-invasive, and community friendly evaluation of oral function.

In this study, individuals with fewer than three swallows in 30 s on the RSST were categorized as part of the oral function high-risk group.

To further enhance screening accuracy, this study compares DDK and jaw movement metrics between individuals with and without RSST-defined risk and applies receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to identify the optimal combination of predictors for estimating oral function risk.

We hypothesize that a non-contact assessment method that integrates both jaw movement analysis and diadochokinetic (DDK) performance will achieve higher sensitivity and accuracy in identifying individuals in the oral function high-risk group, compared to conventional single-parameter assessments. This multidimensional approach may enhance early detection and provide valuable insights for preventive strategies in older adults, especially in resource-limited or community-based healthcare settings.

Methods

Study setting and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at multiple community care stations in Taiwan between April 2022 and February 2023. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kaohsiung Medical University (KMUHIRB-E(II)−20210396) and carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Participants aged 65 to 95 years were recruited using convenience sampling (Fig. 1). Initial eligibility was determined based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) no apparent structural impairments in oral, labial, or mandibular movement; (2) age between 65 and 95 years; and (3) adequate cognitive comprehension. Individuals were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: (1) temporomandibular joint disorders; (2) communication difficulties; or (3) inability to complete the full experimental procedures.

Assessment methods

All assessments were conducted by trained personnel at designated community care stations using a standardized protocol. The evaluation process included three main components: swallowing function assessment, jaw movement analysis, and oral motor skill evaluation. The following measurement tools were employed:

Oral function High-Risk screening using RSST

In this study, oral function risk was assessed using the repetitive saliva swallowing test (RSST), a simple and non-invasive tool commonly employed to screen for early signs of oropharyngeal decline. Rather than serving as a clinical diagnostic tool for dysphagia, the RSST was adopted to preliminarily identify individuals at elevated risk of oral function decline in community settings.

Based on previous community-based research [10], participants who completed fewer than three swallows within 30 s were classified as belonging to the oral function high-risk group, a threshold shown to provide good sensitivity for early detection.

During assessment, the examiner palpated the participant’s laryngeal prominence using their index and middle fingers. Participants were then instructed to repeatedly swallow saliva within a 30 s window. Prior to the test, oral moisture was checked. Following Published recommendations, 1 mL of water could be used to moisten the mouth, and participants were asked to completely swallow or spit out the water to ensure no residual fluid remained in the oral cavity [19].

Participants who completed fewer than three swallows within 30 s on the repetitive saliva swallowing test (RSST) were classified as belonging to the oral function high-risk group.

Participants classified as high-risk were informed of the potential implications of their screening result and advised to consult a physician or qualified healthcare provider for further evaluation and management.

The RSST has demonstrated strong correlation with videofluoroscopic swallowing study (VFSS). Studies report that using the fewer than three swallows cutoff, RSST achieves a sensitivity of 0.80 to 0.98 and a specificity of 0.54 to 0.66 in detecting aspiration [10, 20], supporting its utility as a frontline community screening tool.

Jaw movement assessment and video-based analysis

A standardized protocol was developed to assess jaw movement, based on previous studies demonstrating correlations among mandibular movement, tongue strength, and hyoid bone displacement [16, 17, 21]. This protocol incorporates findings from high-speed jaw-opening exercises. These findings suggest that strengthening the suprahyoid muscles may reduce aspiration risk in older adults with low resting positions of the hyoid bone [16].

Video data were collected using an iPhone 13 camera positioned 50 cm from the participant’s face at a standardized distance (Fig. 2), precisely aligned horizontally with reference point A. Three anatomical markers were placed on standardized facial landmarks (Fig. 3) to enable accurate tracking of movement trajectories. Prior to recording, the distance between points B and C during maximum mouth opening and closing was measured to establish calibration parameters. Participants were instructed to perform ten consecutive cycles of maximal jaw opening and closing under consistent conditions.

Diadochokinetic (DDK)

Diadochokinetic (DDK) is assessed by the rapid repetition of syllables (/pa/,/ta/,/ka/) to evaluate oral motor skills, particularly the speed and coordination of the lips, tongue, and jaw complex [12]. Previous studies have shown that a reduced DDK rate is significantly associated with core swallowing function indicators, such as decreased hyoid and laryngeal elevation observed through videofluoroscopic swallowing study (VFSS) and reduced amplitude of suprahyoid muscle activation detected via surface electromyography (sEMG) [22, 23]. In addition, the natural age-related decline in hyoid movement velocity has been taken into account in this assessment method [24], making it a particularly suitable non-contact neuromuscular indicator to complement RSST and jaw movement analysis. The DDK evaluation protocol integrates both traditional and optimized measurement approaches to ensure comprehensive assessment.

Participants were instructed to repeat single syllables (/pa/,/ta/,/ka/) as quickly as possible within a single breath cycle. Audio recordings were analyzed using Audacity software to extract relevant parameters. This modified approach maintains assessment validity while accommodating age-related limitations in sustained phonation. The measured variables included the ten- and fifteen-syllable repetition rates, the total number of syllables produced per breath, the total phonation time, and the average syllable repetition rate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) to process and systematically interpret the study data. Chi-square tests were conducted to examine differences in demographic characteristics between participants in the oral function high-risk and normal groups. In addition, independent samples t-tests were used to compare jaw movement and DDK performance between the two groups, aiming to identify key motor behavior differences that distinguish the two functional states.

A multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify predictors of membership in the oral-function high-risk group. The model explored the association between RSST outcomes and candidate predictors, including jaw-movement parameters and DDK measures. The ultimate objective was to establish a statistically robust model capable of estimating an individual’s likelihood of belonging to the oral-function high-risk group.

Subsequently, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive performance of the developed model. Several metrics were calculated to determine the model’s precision and clinical applicability, including the area under the curve (AUC), optimal cut-off point, sensitivity, and specificity.

Given the limited predictive sensitivity observed when using individual variables alone, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to identify and integrate variables with significant correlation coefficients. These integrated variables enabled the construction of an optimized predictive model that achieved improved sensitivity and specificity. The cut-off point on the ROC curve was determined by maximizing Youden’s Index, ensuring a reliable balance between sensitivity and specificity.

The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. In addition, G*Power 3.1 software was used to calculate the required sample size to ensure adequate statistical power. With parameters set at α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and a medium effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.25), the calculated sample size was 102 participants, fulfilling the statistical reliability and validity requirements of this study.

DDK analysis

DDK recordings were analyzed using Audacity audio software (https://www.audacityteam.org/). The analysis began at the peak of the first syllable and continued until the appearance of the 10th, 15th, and final syllables (Fig. 5). Parameters analyzed included the repetition rate from the first to the 10th and 15th syllables, the total number of syllables produced, and the phonation duration within a single breath. If a participant was unable to complete ten continuous repetitions, the task was considered incomplete.

Discussion

This comprehensive experimental study demonstrated the feasibility and effectiveness of a non-contact, video-based approach for identifying older adults at high risk of oral functional decline.

Integrating multiple assessment parameters most notably average closing angular velocity (°/s) and the standardized/pa/one-breath assessment yielded highly sensitive screening outcomes. Specifically, this combination achieved a sensitivity of 0.95. These findings establish a solid foundation for advancing the clinical applicability of this screening approach.

The findings revealed notable demographic trends, with the mean age of participants reaching 79.54 years, underscoring a significant association between advancing age and the likelihood of being classified in the oral function high-risk group (p < 0.05).

Within this community-based elderly cohort, the shift into the oral function high-risk group appeared to occur later than general age-related decline [4]. This finding suggests that older adults may gradually develop adaptive compensatory mechanisms.

The relationship between DDK performance and oral function high-risk status emerged as a key finding of this study. Our novel assessment approach combining DDK measures with jaw-movement analysis showed superior sensitivity for identifying older adults in the high-risk group compared with conventional techniques. This sensitivity notably surpasses that reported in earlier studies [14, 22, 23].

The findings revealed a complex biomechanical relationship between jaw-movement characteristics and classification into the oral function high-risk group.

The physiological basis of this relationship centers on the suprahyoid muscles, which coordinate mandibular motion and swallowing. Prior studies showed that weakness in these muscles can cause incomplete opening of the upper esophageal sphincter [18].

Moreover, normal jaw movement is essential for facilitating coordinated hyoid bone elevation, a key biomechanical event required for safe swallowing [16]. This interdependence explains why jaw movement serves as an important indicator in the comprehensive assessment of oral function.

In recent years, studies using wearable devices have increasingly examined how jaw strength metrics relate to oral functional performance. While some studies have reported no statistically significant association between these variables [25], others suggest that jaw strength may serve as a predictor of pharyngeal residue [17]. However, the majority of these investigations have been limited by a reliance on single-parameter assessments. This multidimensional approach showed higher sensitivity and accuracy than single-metric assessments. Thus, it offers enhanced clinical value for identifying older adults at high risk for oral function decline. This study transcends conventional screening approaches by utilizing advanced image analysis techniques to generate detailed quantifications of jaw movement parameters, including average opening and closing distances, angles, and velocities. Compared to wearable devices [13, 17], our non-invasive method is more comfortable for patients and provides higher measurement accuracy. The comprehensive nature of this methodology capable of capturing multiple dimensions of oral motor performance represents a substantial innovation for evaluating oral function and identifying older adults at high risk. Further empirical evidence supports the relationship between specific oral movements and swallowing function. Studies have shown that the repetition of/pa/and/ka/is correlated with food crushing ability and tongue pressure [26]. This connection also extends to the broader context of oral sarcopenia [27], suggesting wider implications for age-related declines in oral function. There is a moderate association between/pa/repetition and masticatory ability [26], and between/ka/production and posterior tongue function [28, 29]. Together, these findings underscore the comprehensive scope of our assessment framework.

These findings establish a new paradigm for identifying high-risk older adults. They demonstrate that integrating multiple oral-motor measures significantly improves classification accuracy. Our non-invasive and highly sensitive approach is valuable for clinical practice. It also has potential for future research on monitoring oral function and early detection of swallowing-related issues.

Limitations

First, convenience sampling may introduce selection bias, potentially affecting internal validity.

Second, we used the RSST as the primary criterion for assigning participants to the oral function high-risk group. Nevertheless, the RSST assesses only swallowing capacity just one of the seven recognized components of overall oral function so basing group allocation on this single metric inevitably constrains the robustness of the classification [1].

Both the RSST and the proposed video-based method should be regarded as preliminary tools for identifying membership in the oral function high-risk group, rather than as definitive diagnostic instruments. While useful for identifying potential cases in community settings, they do not replace gold-standard diagnostic procedures for dysphagia, such as VFSS or FEES, nor do they substitute for assessments of other oral functions such as tongue pressure or occlusal force.

Future studies should aim to incorporate other validated screening tools, such as the Eating Assessment Tool (EAT-10) or the 3-oz water swallow test, alongside instrumental diagnostic assessments (e.g., VFSS or FEES), to comprehensively evaluate swallowing function and further validate our proposed screening approach.

The sample was predominantly female (75.2%), and only key demographic variables (age and gender) were analyzed. These factors may limit the external validity of the findings. Furthermore, excluding participants with comorbidities, neurodegenerative diseases, cardiovascular conditions, or medication effects may have led to an incomplete assessment of the factors influencing classification into the oral function high-risk group. To improve the reliability and generalizability of future research, we recommend incorporating multiple validated assessment tools and expanding the scope of variables to include medical history and specific health conditions.

Future validation of this screening tool should include broader populations, not limited to those diagnosed via VFSS or FEES. A more ideal approach would involve recruiting participants who have been evaluated through the full diagnostic criteria for oral hypofunction or oral frailty.

Conclusion

This study proposed a non-contact method for screening older adults at high risk for oral function decline. This approach integrates jaw-movement analysis and DDK metrics. Combining jaw-closing angular velocity with DDK parameters resulted in high sensitivity. This finding highlights the value of a multimodal strategy for early identification of oral function decline.

Although these results are promising, interpretation should consider the study’s methodological limitations. Further validation with comprehensive diagnostic tools and more diverse samples is recommended to confirm clinical applicability.
