Mechanical and impact behavior of lightweight geopolymer concrete produced by pumice and waste rubber
Authors: Ali İhsan Çelik, Memduh Karalar, Ufuk Tunç, Mehmet Fatih Şahan, Essam Althaqafi, Osman Ahmed Umiye, Yasin Onuralp Özkılıç
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study examined the impact of incorporating pumice and rubber obtained from waste tires as aggregate in specific ratios on geopolymer concrete (GPC). For this aim, various combinations and replacement percentages of pumice and waste tire rubber (WTR) were tested in GPC. Pumice was replaced with coarse aggregate in varying proportions of 0%, 5%, 10%, and 15% and WTR was added into mixture with same proportions. A total of 144 GPC specimens were cast and tested. Tests were performed to measure the compressive strength (CS), splitting tensile strength (STS), flexural strength (FS) and dynamic drop-weight tests. Evaluations of the setting time and workability were also conducted. When natural coarse aggregate was substituted with pumice in concrete, the strength of the resulting mixtures fell. The second research varied the percentage of WTR in the concrete mixture from 0%, 5%, 10%, and 15% while keeping the pumice percentage stable. The study showed that when the pumice ratio was set as constant, there was a steady loss of strength as the percentage of WTR increased. The experimental results demonstrated that partial replacement of coarse aggregate with pumice (up to 15%) led to a progressive reduction in both CS (up to 18.17%) and STS (up to 37.13%) of GPC. This decline in strength became more pronounced with the concomitant utilization of WTR. The findings demonstrated that as the WTR content elevated from 0 to 15%, there was a consistent decline in FS across all levels of pumice replacement, with reductions ranging approximately between 20 and 26%. Additionally, the study created an empirical formula related to STS and CS and compared with the formulas previously developed by various researchers. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Energy dispersive x-ray (EDX), and thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) are also conducted. According to the data gathered from the impact tests, adding pumice to concrete has a negative impact on its impact behavior; on the other hand, adding 10% WTR has a favorable impact.Keywords: Geopolymer concrete, Recycling, Pumice, Rubber, Workability, Setting time, Compressive, Flexural, Splitting, Dynamic drop testsSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Granulated volcanic pumice is a naturally occurring lightweight aggregate that resembles a sponge. It is created when molten lava cools quickly. Mediterranean nations, including Greece, Italy, Spain, and Turkey are rich in it1. In many countries, it can be employed as an aggregate in the creation of concrete that is lightweight2. As gases in the liquid lava particles are retained during cooling, lots of small air spaces or bubbles develop, forming the porous structure of pumice. Because of its lightweight, strong durability against fire, great noise and heat insulation, adequate CS, and acceptable elasticity, as well as a porous composition with low permeability and non-combustibility, pumice is a desirable choice for lightweight aggregate3. Over the last ten years, GPC-based structures have garnered a lot of attention despite being relatively new technology in the construction industry.

Geopolymers, which bond like cement and are cheaper and greener than cement, are favored for building applications. The combination of extensive manufacturing, rising landfill costs, limited landfill space, and stricter disposal rules, recycling fly ash (FA) has become a major issue. FA is best used to make GPC by dissolving Si and Al in an alkaline activator and polycondensing to form a firm solid. Therefore, GPC made of FA can be a great alternative to regular cement in meeting the rising demand for concrete as a building material while also reducing the negative impact on the environment caused by the production of regular cement. In terms of the early development of strength, it was discovered that the oven curing technique was the most beneficial and effective approach for curing GPC. Nevertheless, it is challenging to perform this type of curing for applications that are cast in situ.

In recent years, there have been several research studies conducted on the combination of geopolymer material with pumice as a lightweight aggregate due to its popularity and the desired properties of pumice. The characteristics of geopolymer mortars with different mix parameters, both fresh and hardened, were examined by Ekmen et al.4 Pumice aggregate was used in the production of mortars. Workability, rheology, and mechanical characteristics of the hardened mortars were among the engineering attributes that were being studied. A consistent aggregate mix was calculated to attain acceptable CS and generate lightweight mortar, considering the impact of pumice aggregate on the mechanical characteristics. Top and Vapur5 developed FA-based GPC by employing a combination of basaltic pumice aggregates and FA (Class F) for the construction of lightweight GPC. It was discovered that the addition of more basaltic pumice aggregate led to a reduction in the material’s strength. Kabay et al.6 evaluated the mechanical, physical, and microstructural properties of geopolymer pastes and mortars made from ground granulated blast furnace slag and pumice, using sodium hydroxide (NaOH), potassium hydroxide, and sodium silicate (Na2SiO3) solutions as activators. The test findings demonstrated that the CS of the geopolymer mixes was mostly influenced by the amount of the activator and the proportion of the pumice. Nevertheless, the inclusion of 60%, 70%, and 80% of pumice in the binder did not result in substantial alterations to the mechanical characteristics of the geopolymer mortars. Safari et al.7 demonstrated that the mixes achieved their highest FS and CS when subjected to oven curing at a temperature of 60 °C for 120 h, using an alkali solution with a concentration of 12 M.

Aly et al.8 investigated the use of WTR in GPC as a substitute for aggregates. The study presented a rubberized GPC combination that exhibits exceptional strength, ductility, and impact resistance. This mixture was specifically designed for use in structural components that are exposed to dynamic loads and impacts. Upon concluding the investigation, CS of slag-based GPC improved slightly when the crumb rubber fraction was increased to 10%. The possibility of using recycled tire crumb rubber in GPC was established by Park et al.9 To investigate the impact on the CS of rubberized GPC, various parameters were analyzed. These parameters included the concentration of NaOH solution, the sizes and quantities of aggregates, the curing temperatures and methods, and the duration of curing. The specific effects were observed based on the different types of FA used. The study revealed a negative correlation between the crumb rubber concentration and the CS of GPC. Dong et al.10 measured the mechanical and durability parameters of rubberized GPC using ten mixes with varying rubber components, binder compositions, and treatment techniques. The results demonstrated a significant relationship between bulk density, elastic modulus, ductility, STS, and water absorption and the CS of rubberized GPC. The study conducted by Pham et al.11 examined the impact of varying amounts of crumb rubber, which replaced a portion of both the coarse and fine particles in GPC. Investigators conducted tests on three distinct volume proportions, namely 0%, 15%, and 30%. The testing findings demonstrated that rubberized GPC consistently demonstrated superior impact resistance in comparison to conventional GPC, as indicated by the start and progression of cracks, collapse mechanism, and standardized energy absorption. Due to the large pace of production of waste tire rubber (WTR) across the world and the potential for adverse health and environmental effects if they accumulate in the environment and water, the presence of these tires in natural settings is a major cause for concern12,13. The environmental impact of employing this ecologically damaging waste material in concrete is lessened, and it is utilized as an innovative construction material to tackle scrap tire concerns.

Dheyaaldin et al.14 performed another investigation. The purpose of this research is to investigate the advantages of using waste tire aggregate as an alternative to pumice aggregate in alkali-activated slag mortars within the range of 0–60% (by volume) replacement ratios. Furthermore, mortar mixtures were supplemented with silica fume at a concentration of 10% by volume. Use of wasted tire aggregate significantly reduced mechanical strength and durability, according to test results. Significant improvements in mechanical strength and durability, particularly at an early age, have been achieved with the use of silica fume. Although it was noted that the compressive strength (CS) decreased steadily when waste tire aggregate was introduced to the mixture as an aggregate, the mixture that contained 10% silica fume and 0% waste tire aggregate exhibited the highest compressive strength of all the combinations that were examined. The effects of silica fume and WTR aggregates on the characteristics of a lightweight geopolymer composite were studied by Mabrouk et al.15 at 80 and 100 °C for curing. To achieve this, WTR aggregates replaced the pumice aggregate in the light geopolymer at varying rates (15%, 30%, and 45% by volume) and silica fume replaced 20% of the ground granulated blast furnace slag. When the amount of WTR aggregates and silica fume was increased, both the CS and flexural strengths (FS) declined, particularly at higher temperatures. When WTR particles and higher curing temperatures were added to lightweight geopolymer composites, their CS reduced. Addition of 20% silica fume resulted in additional reductions of 34.22–187.99%, while CS fell by up to 25.18% at 80 °C and 33.85% at 100 °C.

As previously mentioned, several studies have been conducted in the literature on the effects of concrete containing varying percentages of rubber and pumice particles on GPC16–19. The individual impacts of these two products on GPC have been studied independently. However, the lack of research investigating their combined consequences represents a significant gap in existing literature. Furthermore, there is a lack of information in the literature about the response of GPC, including these two materials, when subjected to impact loading. The purpose of this study is to fill in this information gap that has been identified in previous research on this Topic. Consequently, this research provides many significant contributions to existing literature. To accomplish this goal, coarse aggregate was substituted with pumice and waste rubber in different ratios. To do this, it was decided to create 12 mixtures, each with a different ratio of pumice to rubber replacement.

Material and methods

Experimental program

In this study, the coarse aggregate was replaced with 3 different ratios of pumice by weight and WTR was added into mixture by volume ratio. The experiments were carried out in the laboratory department of Construction at Kayseri University. Few research9,11 have addressed this issue, however those that have used crump rubber by volume percentages of 0%,15%, and 30% have ignored the impact effect. In these studies, it has been stated that there is a decrease in strength values after a certain rate. For this reason, in the mixture, pumice was replaced with coarse aggregate at the rates of 5%, 10% and 15% by weight. Then, tests were carried out with the addition of 5%, 10% and 15% WTR by volume of coarse aggregate.

This experimental study focuses on the mechanical properties and load-bearing capacity of lightweight GPC. It is known that the strength properties decrease as the proportion of pumice and rubber increases20. The optimum partial replacement ratio of coarse aggregate with pumice aggregate in concrete is determined to be 5%, but it can go up to 15%21. Based on this, the replacement ratios have been set at 5%, 10%, and 15% as shown in Table 2, to ensure that the CS of the GPC obtained from this experimental study does not decrease significantly. Additionally, for each fixed pumice, WTR was also added with ratio 5%, 10%, and 15% by volume of coarse aggregate.

The binder (FA) and aggregate (fine and coarse aggregate, and pumice) quantities are clearly presented in Table 2 and the binder/aggregate ratio was kept constant. Throughout the study, water was not added to the mix as any additional free water; the required fluidity was achieved through an alkaline activator solution (Na2SiO3 + NaOH). Therefore, the “activation solution/binder” ratio was considered instead of the “water/binder” ratio, and this ratio was kept constant for all mixes. Workability parameters were monitored during the pouring of the mix, and no workability problems were encountered while placing the mixes in the mold. However, the binder/aggregate ratio and the effectiveness of the solution used in the mix will be highlighted in the text for clarity. Binder/total aggregate = 410/(649.3 + 1205.7) ≈ 0.23, alkali activator/binder = 235/410 ≈ 0.57 and Na2SiO3/NaOH ratio = 164.7/70.3 ≈ 2.34. No free water was added, workability was provided entirely with the activator solution.

For the study, aggregate from WTR was obtained by shredding tires and resizing them to match the coarse aggregate specifications defined within the scope of the study. First of all, 40 mm × 40 mm × 160 mm prism-shaped samples were prepared in accordance with ASTM 34923 Standard for FS. After FS, cube samples with dimensions of 40 mm × 40 mm × 40 mm were used in CS24. The use of smaller specimens, such as 40 mm × 40 mm × 40 mm cubic blocks, for the CS test was chosen to ensure the economical use of resources. Additionally, cylindrical samples with dimensions of 100 mm in diameter and 200 mm in height were prepared in accordance with the ASTM C496/C496M25 for splitting tensile strength (STS). A laboratory concrete mixer was used to achieve a homogeneous mixture, and the mixture was poured into molds. The process of placing GPC into molds involved detailed compression and vibration procedures. In the reference mixture used in the study (P0–R0), slump values were observed to be in the range of approximately 70–100 mm, and workability lasted 25–35 min. As pumice replacement increased, slump values decreased significantly. At P15, slump decreased to 35–55 mm, while workability duration remained unchanged. WTR addition, on the other hand, had a more limited effect, decreasing slump by 5–15%, while workability time remained in the range of 25–40 min. In mixtures where pumice and WTR were used together, the effects were cumulative, with slumps decreasing to 30–50 mm and workability time remaining between 20–30 min. These values are within reasonable ranges depending on the material properties used and are consistent with the fresh mix behavior observed in the study. The samples underwent a curing process in an oven at 85 °C for 24 h. Curing at 85 °C for 24 h accelerates the chemical reaction rate of geopolymer concrete (GPC), leading to early strength gain. Additionally, this temperature enhances the microstructural strengthening of the concrete, contributing to the improvement of its mechanical properties26,27. The samples were kept in a laboratory environment with an average temperature of 21 °C and a humidity level of 35% for 28 days after oven curing. To evaluate the performance of GPC incorporating pumice and WTR, five different types of experiments were conducted. Additionally, dynamic drop-weight tests were carried out as an impact test. Each of these tests was carried out in triplicate for every combination and type of test. The preparation, compaction, and curing processes for the test samples are depicted in Fig. 3.

Dynamic drop-weight tests

A specific weight is dropped on the sample from a predetermined height to conduct a drop-weight impact test. Drop-weight tests come in two varieties: instrument-free and instrument-equipped. The dynamic properties of the material are ascertained using the instrumented drop-weight impact test method. In contrast to other approaches, the instrumented impact test system can execute the sample’s sticking, puncture, and repeated impact tests by modifying different heights and weights to produce the necessary energy22,28,29. In comparison to other tests, the dynamic drop-weight test stands out as a very advantageous option, especially with respect to fracture development and propagation. The ability to evaluate how the fracture spreads and how much energy the material being examined is able to absorb in the event of a rapid impact is made possible by this. In comparison to traditional mechanical testing, it replicates loading situations that are more rapid. The setup details for the drop-weight test are displayed in Fig. 5. A total impact load of 16.235 kg consisting of the loading frame, striking tip, and additional mass was dropped on the cylinder specimen, which was standing upright at the bottom of the chamber, from a height of 314 mm. The energy impact was 50 Joules. The impact load was transferred to the specimen using an impact strain gauge tube with a 63.5 mm diameter hemispherical striking tip. The data in the impact strain gauge tube was collected with the help of a data collector, and force–time and energy-time graphs were obtained.

Instrumented drop-weight test machine.

Experimental consequences and discussions

The primary objective of this research project is to conduct a comprehensive evaluation of various production-based sub-assemblies of GPC with additions of pumice and WTR. To accomplish this objective, the coarse aggregate was substituted with three varying proportions of pumice and WTR tires. In addition to the reference, the pumice in the mixture was switched out for coarse aggregate at the percentages of 5%, 10%, and 15% respectively. After that, experiments were performed with 5%, 10% and 15% of WTR added to the mixture. Following the completion of the analysis, the CS test, STS test, and FS test were carried out. Moreover, SEM, EDX, and thermal gravimetric examination were also accomplished. The results of the examination will be discussed in the sections that follow.

Comprehensive investigation of the compressive test (CS)

The impact of pumice on the CS

This section of the study aims to provide a thorough assessment of several production-based sub-assemblies of pumice-added GPC. An assessment was conducted to determine the impact of partially replacing the aggregate in concrete with pumice on the CS of cube specimens. To achieve this goal, the concrete’s PR was adjusted to 0%, 5%, 10%, and 15% in Set#1. Pumice was used to replace natural coarse aggregate in concrete, and the results showed that the CS of the concrete decreased by 2.5%, 5.64%, and 13.25%, respectively, with replacement rates of 5, 10, and 15%.

As observed in Set #2, a pumice ranging from 0 to 15%, along with 5% WTR, was added to the mixture. This resulted in a mean CS of 34.41 MPa for a replacement ratio of 5%. Additionally, the average CS values for replacement ratios of 10% and 15% were 33.12 MPa and 29.92 MPa, respectively, reflecting reductions in CS of 3.74% and 13.05%, respectively, compared to the control concrete. In Set #3, a pumice varying from 0 to 15%, along with 10% WTR, was incorporated into the mixture. For a replacement ratio of 5%, this resulted in a mean CS of 32.18 MPa. Additionally, the mean CS values for substitution rates of 10% and 15% were 31.11 MPa and 29.22 MPa, respectively.

Based on the data provided, the concretes’respective CS decreased by 3.32% and 9.19%. In the final trials, as shown in Set #4, a pumice ranging from 0 to 15%, along with 15% WTR, was added to the mixture. This resulted in an average CS of 28.46 MPa for a replacement ratio of 5%. The average CS values for 10% and 15% replacement ratios were 26.36 MPa and 23.34 MPa, respectively, reflecting decreases of 7.37% and 18.17%. The test results indicated that altering the pumice from 0 to 15%, in combination with the addition of WTR, has an inverse effect on the mixture’s CS, as shown in Fig. 6.

Compressive test results.

The outcomes are comparable to those reported in the literature. Oz et al.30 investigated the effect of common pumice on the workability of self-compacting concrete. The researchers concluded that as the pumice content increased, the CS of the concrete decreased. This reduction in CS was observed with the increased proportion of pumice in the mixture. The discrepancy arises because the specific gravity of pumice is significantly lower than that of crushed stone, leading to reduced strength when compared to mixtures containing crushed stone30. In Shafiq et al.’s study31, the CS of concretes containing pumice was compared to the CS of concretes that were traditionally made with natural aggregate at different curing times. According to the test findings, there was a strength reduction of around 1%, 26%, 37%, and 50% when natural aggregate was partially substituted with pumice. According to the findings of Parhizkar et al.’s study32, the CS reduced as the amount of pumice replacement increased. To build a structural lightweight concrete, a laboratory study using FA as a mineral additive and basaltic pumice as the aggregate was partially examined by Yasar et al.33 Two types of lightweight concrete mixtures were prepared: a control combination consisting of lightweight basaltic pumice and regular Portland cement, and a FA lightweight concrete mixture that replaces 20% of the cement with FA on a weight basis.

Kurt et al.34. reported that the CS decreases as the proportion of pumice increases while substituting natural aggregate with pumice in ratios ranging from 0 to 10% with 20% increments. The effects of substituting natural fine and coarse aggregate with pumice aggregate on the CS were examined by Bhavana and Rambabu35. The findings indicated a 9.60% drop in CS when 10% of the pumice and fine aggregate were substituted, a 15.13% reduction when 20% was substituted, and a 16.97% reduction with a 30% substitution. Similarly, reductions of 13.28%, 22.51%, and 33.65% in CS were observed when coarse aggregate was substituted. Widodo et al.36 demonstrated that an increasing volumetric proportion of pumice used as coarse aggregate in concrete causes a systematic decline in the concrete’s CS.

It was found that the volume percentage of pumice aggregate, used as coarse aggregate in the mixes, tends to lower the CS in an inversely proportional manner. Kılıç et al.37. observed a 72.76% loss in CS when pumice completely replaced natural aggregate. Similarly, Rajeswari and George38reported a 65.29% reduction in CS when 70% of the coarse aggregate was substituted with pumice. Desai et al.39 conducted an experimental study on concretes with pumice replacing coarse aggregate at varying levels, from 0 to 100%. The study revealed a systematic decline in CS as pumice content increased, with reductions of 16.80%, 45.76%, 60.76%, and 68.67% for pumice replacement levels of 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%, respectively, compared to reference concrete. In a similar investigation, Sultan et al.40. progressively replaced coarse aggregate with pumice in 25% increments, ranging from 0 to 100%. Their findings showed that the CS dropped from 27.46 MPa to 14.72 MPa with a 25% replacement, and further declined to 14.01 MPa, 11.74 MPa, and 10.64 MPa for replacement levels of 50%, 75%, and 100%, respectively.

The impact of the WTR ratio on the CS

This part of the research examined the impact of WTR on CS by varying the ratio of rubber while keeping the pumice constant. To achieve this, the proportion of WTR in the concrete mixture was adjusted to 0%, 5%, 10%, and 15%, while the pumice was fixed at 5%. The average CS values for replacement ratios of 0%, 5%, 10%, and 15% were 35.1 MPa, 34.41 MPa, 32.18 MPa, and 28.46 MPa, respectively. It was determined that with a pumice content of 5%, the CS decreased by 1.97%, 8.32%, and 18.92% as the WTR content increased. In a second observation, the pumice was set at 10%, and the percentage of WTR was varied between 0%, 5%, 10%, and 15%. The mean CS values for these replacement ratios were 33.97 MPa, 33.12 MPa, 31.11 MPa, and 26.36 MPa, respectively. The study demonstrated that increasing the WTR content by 5%, 10%, and 15% resulted in CS reductions of 2.50%, 8.42%, and 22.40%, respectively, compared to reference concrete with 10% pumice.

Lastly, the WTR proportion was adjusted from 0 to 15%, while the pumice was fixed at 15%. The average CS values for replacement ratios of 0%, 5%, 10%, and 15% were 31.23 MPa, 29.92 MPa, 29.22 MPa, and 23.34 MPa, respectively. The study showed that increasing the WTR content by 4.19%, 6.44%, and 25.26% led to a gradual decrease in CS compared to the reference concrete containing 15% pumice. The results indicate that the CS of the concrete is inversely impacted by the variation in WTR content (0–15%) when the pumice is fixed, as illustrated in Fig. 7. These findings align with the literature, where it has been observed that WTR deforms more than the binder under load due to its flexibility. This variation in deformation creates an incompatibility, resulting in microcracks at the rubber-binder interface41. Another explanation found in the literature is that the rubber-binder interface dries out as the mixing water used for cement hydration is consumed over time. Since WTR has a weaker bond with the binder compared to aggregate and a higher shrinkage potential, microcracks may form around the rubber particles, acting as points of stress concentration and crack propagation41.

Detailed examination of the STS

The impact of pumice on the STS

As a result of replacing natural coarse aggregate with pumice in concrete, the results demonstrated a decrease in STS of 3.30%, 16.06%, and 20.66% at 5, 10%, and 15% replacement rates, correspondingly. The research results indicate that an increase in the pumice led to a decrease in the STS of the concrete samples. As shown in Set #2, by adding 5% WTR and the pumice, which varies from 0 to 15%, to the mixture. With a replacement ratio of 5%, this produced a mean STS of 3.42 MPa. Also noteworthy are the average STS values of 3.02 MPa and 2.88 MPa, respectively, for replacement ratios of 10% and 15%. The data presented indicates that the concretes’STS decreased by 11.68% and 15.93%, respectively. The combination in Set #3 used the pumice, which ranged from 0 to 15%, together with 10% WTR to accomplish this objective. A mean STS of 3.19 MPa resulted from using a replacement ratio of 5%. It is also important to note that the mean STS values for 10% and 15% replacement rates are 2.55 MPa and 2.21 MPa, respectively.

The data supplied indicates that the respective STS of the concrete fell by 19.97% and 30.91%. In the last experiments, this was accomplished by adding 15% WTR and a pumice that ranges from 0 to 15% to the mixture, as indicated in Set #4. The replacement percentage was 5% and the average STS was 3.02 MPa as a result. Additionally, it is noteworthy to mention the mean STS of 1.90 MPa and 2.22 MPa for 10% and 15% replacement percentages, respectively. According to the findings, there was a 26.61% and 37.13% drop in the concretes’STS. Based on the test results, the addition of WTR to the mixture has an inverse effect on the STS when the pumice is changed from 0 to 15%, as demonstrated in Fig. 8. Similar findings to those reported in the literature were found. The impact of substituting pumice for coarse aggregate in concrete samples measuring 100 mm in diameter by 200 mm in length was examined by Manoj et al.42 It was observed that in comparison to control concrete, the STS dropped at all replacement levels, according to the research’s conclusions. To study the STS of concretes, Reddy et al.43 substituted Pumice in ratios of 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% for natural aggregate at 28 days. When the replacement level reached 25%, the strength decreased by around 33%. Sangeetha et al.44 conducted an experimental study to compare ordinary concrete with concrete that had varying percentages (10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, 60%, 80%, 100%) of pumice replacing the coarse particles. The test findings demonstrated a consistent decline in the STS performance as the pumice content increased.

STS tests.

The impact of the WTR ratio on the STS

This section of the study fixed pumice while adjusting the material proportion to investigate the effect of WTR on STS. To achieve this goal, the pumice was set at 5% and the amount of WTR was changed to 0%, 5%, 10%, and 15% of the concrete mixture, respectively. Remarkably, the average STS measurements for the proportions at 0%, 5%, 10%, and 15% are 3.72 MPa, 3.42 MPa, 3.19 MPa, and 3.02 MPa, respectively. After comparing the reference concrete with pumice at 5%, it was found that the expansion of the WTR ratio content by 8.06%, 14.25%, and 18.82% would lead to a progressive drop in STS. The amount of WTR in the concrete mixture was changed between 0%, 5%, 10%, and 15% in the second investigation, in addition to maintaining the pumice at 10%. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis

SEM analysis was conducted for microstructural examinations. SEM analysis was performed by selecting a piece from the 8th sample. Because in this sample, it was easier to observe when the pumice and rubber ratio was 10% and the strength was at medium levels. Images derived from the SEM analysis are depicted in Fig. 21a–d. Upon examination of these SEM images, it is evident that voids and unreacted FA are distinctly observable. Furthermore, the presence of microcracks, geopolymer gel formation, and notably substantial C–S–H (calcium silicate hydrate) formation is discernible. Specifically, the detailed observation of void formation resulting from the addition of Pumice to the geopolymer mixture is illustrated in Fig. 21a and b. Microstructural analysis in structural elements is an important issue to observe the distribution and pores of the elements forming the structure by examining the reactions taking place in the internal structure of an element. This analysis is used to understand material properties, determine structural properties, and evaluate material behavior. Additionally, microstructure analysis also plays a critical role in optimizing material processing processes and mechanical properties. In this way, its effects on the durability, performance, and longevity of structural elements can be examined in detail53–56. Figure 21a shows the pumice aggregate with large pores in the matrix, while Fig. 21b shows the WTR with a more irregular, rounded shape. Unreacted FA particles are generally spherical or amorphous in structure. Their surfaces are generally smooth and show no traces of dissolution or reactive products, as shown in Fig. 21c5. In SEM images, different materials appear with different contrasts. Figure 21d CSH gels are shown; in environments with high calcium content, it may contribute to the formation of CSH gel along with the geopolymerization process. In comparison to other substances that react to generate geopolymer gel, FA substances that do not completely react throughout the alkali activation reaction contribute less and stay weak areas that restrict strength. The concrete’s capacity for carrying loads cannot be directly increased by unprocessed FA substances because they do not completely integrate with the matrix; instead, they may cause voids that weaken the concrete57. SEM pictures show that the compressive capacity of lightweight concrete may be affected in positive as well as negative ways. The compact, interconnected structure created by geopolymer gel and C–S–H phases improves CS, ensuring persistence under pressure. These phases may contribute to increased levels of strength, since the structure is porous. To maximize strength, however, weak spots like the ITZ region and unreacted particles must be controlled in this kind of lightweight concrete58.CS may be reduced by several causes, including microcracks, porosity, weaknesses in the ITZ, and unreacted FA particles. It is possible for the load-bearing capacity of the concrete to be reduced due to the presence of voids and loose structures in these different places59.

Energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis

Energy Dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis is a technique used to determine the chemical composition of a material. This analysis was carried out to understand the components and properties of GPC. In EDX analysis, the electron beam strikes an inner shell of an atom, ejecting an electron and leaving behind a positively charged electron hole. When another electron from an outer shell fills this vacancy, an X-ray is emitted. This analysis works in integration with a SEM. In this way, the EDX analysis applied on the GPC sample shows the presence and density of alumina, silica, calcium, iron, magnesium and other elements in the focused area in the SEM image in Fig. 22. Thanks to the air bubbles formed in GPC concrete containing pumice, Oxygen (O) is seen at the highest rate with 43.15%. Silicon (Si) is an element found abundantly in natural rocks and is seen at a rate of 21.29%. The presence of Si and Al in GPC is a critical factor determining the performance and durability of the material. The silica (SiO₂) present in FA dissolves and becomes active during the alkali activation process. Si dissolves under the influence of alkali activators and transforms into silicate ions. These silicate ions undergo polycondensation reactions with aluminate ions, forming a three-dimensional network structure (geopolymer matrix) composed of Si–O–Si and Si–O–Al bonds. The structure composed of amorphous silica is shown in the SEM image in Fig. 22. This structure provides the mechanical strength and chemical resistance of the concrete. The results are consistent with previous studies62,63. In addition to providing information on the distribution of pumice and waste rubber additives throughout the matrix, the elements that were identified in the EDX study highlight the primary components that make up the geopolymer structure. While alkali components like sodium and potassium help the activation and stability of binding structures, the composition’s high quantities of oxygen, silicon, and aluminum offer the perfect matrix for the development and endurance of geopolymer gel. With the greatest concentration at 43.15%, oxygen (O) is an important component of other oxide compounds and the structure of the geopolymer gel. In GPC, sodium (Na) and potassium (K) are present at percentages of 15.66% and 2.11%, respectively. These two elements are related to the use of alkali activators. An essential part of the geopolymerization process is played by these alkali elements, which are responsible for activating the Si and Al structures64. The geopolymer structure is comprised of essential components such as aluminum (Al) and silicon (Si), with Al accounting for 9.35% and Si accounting for 21.79%, respectively65. The presence of these components helps to contribute to the production of geopolymer gel, which in turn helps to enhance the structural strength of anything. The presence of magnesium (Mg) and calcium (Ca) at 1.93% and 2.44%, respectively, often supports reactions within the matrix and contributes to the geopolymer structure. The discarded rubber or pumice may have provided 3.57% of the iron. The concrete has a homogeneous appearance due to a homogeneous matrix of Si–O–Si and Si–O–Al bonding.

TGA analysis

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) is an analysis method that examines the weight change of a material depending on the temperature increase. Thermogravimetric analysis of GPC was performed to determine the thermal behavior and thermal degradation properties of the material. This analysis can help determine how the material responds to temperature change and the separation temperatures of various components. The TGA analysis in Fig. 23 shows the response of GPC concrete with pumice in addition to thermal changes from 0 to 1000 °C. The blue line represents the change in mass. Between 50 and 200 °C, water that is physically bound disappears. The geopolymer matrix’s surface or pores evaporate water molecules, causing mass loss. The geopolymer structure’s chemically attached water molecules break down between 200 and 400 °C. The hydroxyl groups created through the geopolymerization procedure are the source of these water molecules. Geopolymer matrix portions thermally degrade at 400–800 °C. Thermal stress may disrupt Si–O–Si and Si–O–Al bonds, causing mass loss. The geopolymer matrix degrades progressively at 800–1000 °C and higher. Decomposing partially crystalline components and residues increases mass loss. Conclusions

The study explored the combined effects of pumice as a partial replacement of coarse aggregate and WTR as addition in GPC. The mechanical properties, such as CS, STS, and FS, are reduced with increasing replacement levels, but optimum performance can be attained at the lower replacement ratios, especially 5% pumice and 10% WTR. The use of pumice lowers the overall density and enhances sustainability but imparts low strength due to its light weight and porous nature. Likewise, rubberized concrete improves the impact and energy characteristics, thereby making these mixes suitable for shock absorbing and other non-load-bearing uses. Microstructural analysis revealed that the presence of voids and unreacted particles lead to strength reductions, while TGA demonstrated good thermal stability of the geopolymer matrix. The present outcomes emphasize the potential of using the GPC with pumice and WTR as a sustainable material for the aforementioned applications, highlighting the need for further optimization of mix designs for enhanced mechanical performance.
