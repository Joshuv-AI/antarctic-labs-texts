Targeting ROR1 with KAN571C disrupts resistance networks in mantle cell lymphoma
Authors: Wen Zhong, Mohammad Hojjat-Farsangi, Swapnali Barde, Parviz Kokhaei, Lotta Hansson, Marzia Palma, Wen Zhong, Anders Österborg, Ali Moshfegh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•MCL cells rely on ROR1-related signaling in overcoming resistance to Ibru and Ven.•ROR1 small-molecule inhibitor KAN571C kills both sensitive and resistant MCL cells by blocking ROR1 signaling and triggering apoptosis.

Introduction

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is an aggressive and often incurable subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, accounting for 3% to 10% of adult-onset non-Hodgkin lymphoma in Western countries.1,2 Traditionally, MCL is characterized by the chromosomal translocation t(11;14)(q13;q32), leading to the overexpression of cyclin D1, which is the genetic hallmark of MCL and drives unregulated cell cycle progression.1,2 However, because of its significant heterogeneity, MCL is now considered a group of subcategories with various molecular and clinical features generally divided into 2 types: aggressive classical/conventional MCL (SOX11+) and leukemic nonnodal MCL (SOX11−), which has a more indolent behavior.2,3

The first-line treatment of MCL has recently changed, consisting of intensive rituximab-based chemoimmunotherapy incorporating ibrutinib (Ibru), a Bruton tyrosine kinase (TK; BTK) inhibitor (BTKi), instead of offering high-dose chemotherapy and autologous stem cell transplant as consolidation.4,5 Meanwhile, the single-agent activity of the BCL2 antagonist venetoclax (Ven) has been observed and considered in patients with MCL who have relapsed after BTKi.5 Early studies (phase 1/2) reported high response rates for the combination of Ibru and Ven, which is recommended as one of the standard post–first-line therapies for BTKi-naive patients with relapsed disease.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10

Like with most lymphomas, most of the patients with MCL initially respond to treatment but frequently experience relapse because of the emergence of drug resistance.11, 12, 13 Around one-third of patients with MCL are resistant to Ibru or other BTKi.14, 15, 16 Alternative pathway activation, such as phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)–AKT or NF-κB signaling, usually contributes to the primary resistance to BTKi.17, 18, 19 BTK C481S mutations are common mechanisms for secondary resistance that prevent covalent BTKis (such as Ibru) from binding to their target.17,20 Although Ven also demonstrated notable activity, particularly for high-risk cases with aggressive disease, resistance has also been found in patients with MCL correlated with the increased expression of antiapoptotic proteins MCL-1 and/or BCL-XL.13,21 In general, resistance to current therapy remains a significant barrier for patients with MCL to long-term disease control, which brings up the need to develop novel therapeutic options.

Receptor TK-like orphan receptor 1 (ROR1) is a transmembrane protein that plays a crucial role in embryonic development.22,23 ROR1 is highly expressed in various malignancies but largely absent in normal adult tissues.24,25 Research on ROR1 functions is mostly conducted in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), in which the expression of ROR1 increases with disease progression.22,26 The binding of Wnt5a to ROR1 stimulates multiple signaling pathways, including AKT signaling, that trigger tumor cell proliferation, survival, migration, and metastasis.22,24,27 The high expression and prosurvival effect of ROR1 have also been found in MCL, breast cancer, ovarian cancer, melanoma, and Ewing sarcoma.28, 29, 30 ROR1+ stem cells were found to have higher capacities to form spheroids or tumor xenografts than ROR1− populations in ovarian cancer.31 Given its pivotal role in cancer progression and minimal expression in normal tissues, ROR1 has garnered significant attention as a therapeutic target. Previously, we analyzed ROR1 expression and the effects of its inhibition by a small molecule (KAN0441571C [KAN571C]) in freshly isolated patient-derived MCL cells and naive human MCL cell lines with well-defined genotype and phenotype characteristics.32 KAN571C has been previously shown to induce dephosphorylation of ROR1 and downstream inhibition of ROR1-associated signaling pathways in ROR1+ malignancies.32, 33, 34 However, because elevated ROR1 expression has been linked to resistance to therapies such as Ibru and Ven, its efficacy against Double-resistant MCL warrants further investigation.27,35 Therefore, this study aims to investigate the mechanisms of Ibru and Ven resistance in MCL as well as the role of ROR1 and its small-molecule inhibitor KAN571C in drug resistance.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and generation of MCL-resistant cell lines

The human MCL cell lines JEKO-1 (ACC 553) and Granta-519 (ACC 342) were purchased from Leibniz Institute DSMZ–German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH (Braunschweig, Germany). Z138 (ATCC CRL-3001) was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA). Cell lines were cultured with the Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium (Gibco, Life Technologies, Karlsruhe, Germany) supplied with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco), and 1% l-glutamine (Gibco) in a humidified incubator with 5% carbon dioxide at 37°C. Naive JEKO-1, Granta-519, and Z138 cells were cultured with continuous and gradually increased exposure of Ibru (up to 10μM; Novakand Pharma AB, Stockholm, Sweden), Ven (up to 5μM; Novakand Pharma AB), or their combination (up to 5μM for each drug). Cells were constantly cultured in the presence of Ibru and/or Ven even after the establishment of resistance. Fresh culture medium containing 10μM Ibru and/or 5μM Ven was replaced every 48 hours to ensure continuous drug exposure and maintain a consistent resistance phenotype. The strategy for resistance induction and dose changes is illustrated in supplemental Figure 1.

MTT assay

For the MTT assay, 100 μl of MCL cells (JEKO-1, Granta-519, and Z138) were seeded in triplicate in 96-well plates (Techno Plastic Products, Trasadingen, Switzerland). The seeding concentration of each naive and resistant cell line was determined using a titration protocol, ranging from 0.3 × 109/L to 1.2 × 109/L, depending on the cell lines. The addition of 1% Ibru (range, 0mM-1mM), Ven (range, 0mM-1mM), Ibru + Ven (range, 0mM-1mM), or KAN571C (range, 0μM-1μM; Novakand Pharma AB) was performed at time 0. Culture medium alone and cells treated with 1% DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO) were included as background and experimental controls. JEKO-1 and Z138 cells were treated for 24 hours, whereas Granta-519 cells went through 72 hours of treatment. After incubation, 10 μL MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) solution (5 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) was added to each well, followed by a 4-hour incubation at 37°C. The reaction was subsequently terminated by adding 100 μL stop solution (10% sodium dodecyl sulfate solution [Thermo Fisher Scientific] + 0.08% hydrochloric acid [37%; Sigma-Aldrich]) for overnight incubation at 37°C. The experimental outcome was measured using the VersaMax Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA) at λ = 570nM.

Western blot

Naive and resistant MCL cell lines were seeded (2 × 109/L) in 24-well plates (Techno Plastic Products) with fresh culture medium. The addition of 0.1% DMSO or 500nM KAN571C was performed at time 0. Cells were collected at different treatment periods based on cell cycle and sensitivity to treatment (5 hours, 12 hours, or 48 hours, respectively, for naive and drug-resistant JEKO-1, Z138, or Granta-519 cells).

The collected cells were then washed with 1× Tris-buffered saline (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA), lysed in Pierce IP Lysis Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) containing 1% Halt Protease & Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (100×; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 40 minutes on ice, and centrifuged at 14 000g for 20 minutes at 4°C. Supernatant was collected after centrifugation, and protein concentration was measured using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) based on the standard curve produced by Albumin Standard (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Loading samples for western blot were prepared by boiling a mixture of protein sample, 1× NuPAGE Sample Reducing Agent (10×; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1× NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer (4×; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 minutes at 95°C. When cooling down, loading samples containing 15 μg of protein, together with 5 uL PageRuler Plus Prestained Protein Ladder (Thermo Fisher Scientific), was loaded on NuPAGE 10% Bis-Tris 1.5-mm, 10-well Gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and performed as previously described.32 The primary and secondary antibodies used are shown in supplemental Table 1.

RNA isolation, integrity analysis, and real-time qPCR

RNA extraction was performed using the QIAzol Lysis Reagent, followed by the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen). The total concentration was checked using a Nanodrop (Saveen & Werner, Limhamn, Sweden); 1 μg total RNA was reverse transcribed to generate complementary DNA (cDNA) using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Life Technologies). A gradient quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed to estimate the efficient concentration of the sample required for the gene of interest. Based on this, 30 ng cDNA was added to the 96-well qPCR plate, and amplification was performed in duplicates for ROR1 and the internal reference gene, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), by using TaqMan assays (Life Technologies). Relative fold changes were calculated by using the comparative threshold cycle method (2−ΔΔCT).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software Inc, La Jolla, CA) or RStudio (Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA) with R version 4.4.2. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between 2 groups were conducted using the unpaired 2-tailed Student t test. For comparisons involving more than 2 groups, one way ANOVA was performed, followed by the Tukey multiple-comparison test. A paired t test was used for the before- and after-treatment comparison in western blot densitometry analysis. Correlation of protein expression was performed with Spearman correlation analysis and visualized in a heat map after hierarchical clustering. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

Results

Generation of MCL cell lines resistant to Ibru and Ven

To establish drug-resistant models in MCL cell lines, naive JEKO-1, Granta-519, and Z138 cells were continuously cultured with gradually increasing concentrations of Ibru and Ven, as described in “Materials and methods”. Stable resistance to Ibru (10μM), Ven (5μM), and Ibru + Ven (Double; 5μM each) was successfully developed in all 3 cell lines. These resistance models exhibited lower cytotoxicity when treated with each drug or the drug combination (Ibru, Ven, or Double resistant) compared to naive cells (Figure 1A). Effect of KAN571C on naive and drug-resistant MCL cell lines

Naive and drug-resistant MCL cell lines were treated with various concentrations of KAN571C (0nM-1000nM) for 24 hours (JEKO-1 and Z138 cells) or 72 hours (Granta-519 cells); 50% effective concentration (EC50) values of KAN571C treatment were calculated by MTT assay (Figure 1B). Among 3 naive MCL cell lines, Z138 exhibited the highest sensitivity to KAN571C treatment (24-hour EC50 = 200nM), followed by JEKO-1 (24-hour EC50 = 300nM). However, KAN571C achieved only a maximum of 40% killing in naive Granta-519 cells, with the highest concentration (1000nM) at 24 hours (supplemental Figure 8), making it the least sensitive cell type to KAN571C. Because the EC50 values of KAN571C were calculated based on the maximum killing, the 24-hour EC50 for naive Granta-519 cells was not comparable to those of other cell types. In this case, the cytotoxicity of KAN571C treatment on Granta-519 cells was collected and calculated after 72 hours (Figure 1B).

In general, KAN571C induces dose-dependent cytotoxicity on both naive and all drug-resistant MCL models (Figure 1B). To compare the effects of KAN571C on naive and resistant models, cytotoxicity was also calculated using the area under the curve (Figure 1C) from the aforementioned dose-response analysis. Ibru-resistant models demonstrated comparable sensitivity to KAN571C as naive cells for all 3 cell types, with no statistically significant differences (Figure 1C). However, KAN571C showed significantly higher cytotoxicity at 24 hours in Ven-resistant and Double-resistant JEKO-1 models compared to naive cells (P < .001 and P < .01, respectively; Figure 1C). No significant difference in KAN571C cytotoxicity was observed between Ven- and Double-resistant JEKO-1 models (Figure 1C).

A similar pattern was also observed in the Granta-519 resistance model tested after 72 hours of incubation. Both Ven- and Double-resistant models showed significantly higher cytotoxicity after KAN571C treatment than naive Granta-519 cells (P < .01), with no significant difference between each other (Figure 1C). As for the Z138 resistance model, all resistance models demonstrated similar high sensitivity to KAN571C as naive cells at 24 hours (Figure 1B-C). No statistically significant difference in KAN571C cytotoxicity was found between naive and resistant cells (Figure 1C).

Comparison of KAN571C and Ven on overcoming Ibru resistance

The established Ibru-resistant models of the 3 MCL cell lines were treated with either Ven or KAN571C for 24 hours (JEKO-1 and Z138) or 72 hours (Granta-519). Cytotoxicity and the EC50 values are summarized in supplemental Figure 2. KAN571C showed higher total cytotoxicity and lower EC50 values than Ven on both JEKO-1 and Z138 cells resistant to Ibru. However, Ven demonstrated a lower EC50 than KAN571C in the Ibru-resistant Granta-519 system within 72-hour treatment.

Mechanisms of Ibru and Ven resistance and the effect of KAN571C on signaling pathways

Signaling pathways in naive MCL cell lines

Under naive conditions, the 3 MCL cell lines JEKO-1, Granta-519, and Z138 use distinct signaling pathways for survival (supplemental Figure 3A,C). Although total ROR1 protein expression was consistent among the 3 cell lines, Z138 cells significantly displayed the highest levels of phosphorylated ROR1 (p-ROR1) at both TK and proline-rich domains (PRD). JEKO-1 cells showed moderate expression, whereas Granta-519 cells exhibited a relatively lower expression level. This expression pattern also correlated with phosphorylated PI3K (p-PI3K) in the 3 MCL cell lines, whereas no significant difference in phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT) was observed.

In contrast, Granta-519 showed significantly higher expression of BCL2 compared to the other 2 cell types. This distinguished expression of p-ROR1 and BCL2 correlated with the differential sensitivity of naive cells to the treatment of KAN571C and Ven, in which cells with the highest expression demonstrated the most cytotoxicity after incubation (supplemental Figure 3B). Although the expression of p-BTK also varied among the 3 MCL cell types, no correlated cytotoxicity was observed, as expected, as Ibru is well known not to have any direct killing effect on targeted cells. Besides, Granta-519 cells appeared to use alternative survival pathways, including c-Jun and β-catenin, which are involved in JNK and canonical WNT signaling, respectively. Z138 cells also showed the highest expression of p-MAPK among the 3 MCL cell types at the naive phase.

Changes in signaling pathways under drug-resistant conditions

In JEKO-1 drug-resistant systems, BCL2 expression was downregulated in all 3 resistance models (Figure 2A; supplemental Figure 4). Notably, Ven-resistant JEKO-1 cells exhibited significantly elevated levels of p-ROR1 at both TK and PRD (P < .05) as well as p-PI3K (P < .05) compared to naive JEKO-1 (Figure 2A; supplemental Figure 4). Drug-resistant Granta-519 cells showed no significant changes in p-ROR1, whereas a reduction in p-PI3K (P < .01) and an upregulation of β-catenin (P < .05) were observed in Ven-resistant Granta-519 cells compared to naive cells (Figure 3A; supplemental Figure 5). The increased expression of β-catenin was also observed in Z138 models when comparing Double resistant to naive cells (P < .05; Figure 4A; supplemental Figure 6). On the mRNA level, Ven- and Double-resistant Z138 cells exhibited a significantly increased expression of ROR1 compared to naive cells (P < .01; supplemental Figure 7). A similar upregulation of ROR1 was also observed in Double-resistant JEKO-1 cells (P < .05). In contrast, an opposing pattern was detected in the Granta-519 cell line, in which ROR1 mRNA expression was significantly reduced after the acquisition of resistance to either Ven or both Ven and Ibru (P < .01).

Effect of KAN571C on signaling pathways in naive and drug-resistant conditions

Lysates of naive and drug-resistant cells treated with 500nM KAN571C were collected at different time points, as described earlier. In response to drug exposure, a significant dephosphorylation of p-ROR1 TK and PRD was observed in both Ven- and Double-resistant JEKO-1 cells (Figure 2A; supplemental Figure 4) and Z138 cells (Figure 4A; supplemental Figure 6), whereas no substantial changes were noticed in naive cells. Correspondingly, KAN571C also dephosphorylated p-PI3K in a similar pattern in both Ven- and Double-resistant JEKO-1 and Z138 cells, in contrast to naive cells. An inhibition of p-PI3K was also found in Ibru-resistant JEKO-1 cells after KAN571C treatment. For all resistance models of JEKO-1 and Z138, KAN571C was able to block AKT signaling by reducing both total and p-AKT, whereas no such effect was found in naive cells.

A similar pattern was also observed for AXIN-1 and β-catenin in the JEKO-1 resistance system, in which the expression of both proteins was significantly reduced after KAN571C exposure in all resistance models but not in naive cells. In parallel, KAN571C reduced the expression of AXIN-1 and β-catenin in both naive and drug-resistant Z138 models and the dephosphorylation of MAPK. However, MAPK dephosphorylation was only found for Ibru-resistant JEKO-1 cells after incubation with KAN571C. Meanwhile, the inhibition of c-Jun was detected after KAN571C exposure for both naive and resistant cells in both the JEKO-1 and Z138 models. The apoptosis of cells was mediated through the cleavage of caspase 3 and PARP in all experimental groups.

In the Granta-519 system, both naive and drug-resistant cells demonstrated significant dephosphorylation of the p-ROR1 TK domain and p-PI3K after KAN571C treatment and the inhibition of AXIN-1, β-catenin, and c-Jun (Figure 3A; supplemental Figure 5). Dephosphorylation of the p-ROR1 PRD was only observed in Ven-resistant cells. A nonsignificant trend toward p-AKT dephosphorylation was also found in both naive and drug-resistant models. Notably, only Ven- and Double-resistant Granta-519 cells activated the cleavage of caspase 3 and PARP after KAN571C exposure, consistent with the high cytotoxicity levels observed in those 2 resistant groups, as shown in Figure 1C.

Overall, combining the features in resistance pattern and drug exposure effect, the phosphorylation levels of p-ROR1 at the TK and PRD domains and of p-PI3K were consistently correlated across all 3 MCL resistance models (Figures 2B, 3B, and 4B). At the same time, the expression of c-Jun was associated with AXIN-1 and the phosphorylation pattern of p-PI3K under all experimental conditions. The correlation between AXIN-1 and β-catenin was only found in the JEKO-1 and Z138 resistance models. p-MAPK clustered with c-Jun, AXIN-1, and β-catenin in Z138 drug-resistant models. On the contrary, p-BTK was negatively correlated with the p-ROR1 TK domain, p-PI3K, AXIN-1, β-catenin, and c-Jun across all resistance models after incubation with KAN571C. A positive correlation between the p-ROR1 TK domain and p-AKT was observed only in Granta-519 resistance and treatment groups. Discussion

In this study, we investigated the in vitro efficacy of the ROR1 small-molecule inhibitor KAN571C in overcoming resistance to Ibru and Ven in MCL models. Drug-resistant cell lines were established from 3 MCL lines (JEKO-1, Granta-519, and Z138) through continuous exposure to Ibru, Ven, or their combination, thereby generating double-refractory models. KAN571C demonstrated marked cytotoxic activity across both drug-naive and drug-resistant MCL cells, with particularly pronounced effects observed in Ven-resistant and Double-resistant models. Mechanistically, KAN571C treatment induced dephosphorylation of ROR1 at its TK and PRD domains, subsequently downregulating the PI3K/AKT and β-catenin signaling pathways and culminating in caspase-dependent apoptosis.

Notably, attempts to generate stable KAN571C-resistant cell lines were unsuccessful, underscoring the compound’s antitumor potential. Collectively, these findings highlight ROR1 as a tentative therapeutic target for overcoming treatment resistance in MCL and possibly in other B-cell malignancies.

Double refractoriness, that is, to both BTKi and BCL2i, remains a critical hurdle in diseases such as MCL and CLL. In our study, resistance models were generated in the presence of Ibru and/or Ven to maintain consistent resistance in MCL. Resistance to Ibru has been extensively characterized and involves a complex interplay of genetic and nongenetic mechanisms. Acquired resistance is frequently driven by mutations in BTK (often involving but not limited to C481S) and, to some extent, also PLCγ2, leading to the reactivation of B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling.14,17 However, accumulating evidence also implicates alternative survival pathways, particularly PI3K/AKT, MAPK, and NF-κB signaling, as contributors to resistance.18,20 Our in vitro findings are in line with this model because we observed the maintenance of cell viability despite Ibru exposure, alongside the activation of non-BCR survival pathways.

Ven resistance involves the upregulation of alternative antiapoptotic proteins such as MCL-1 and BCL-XL, genomic alterations, and metabolic reprogramming.21 Notably, Ven resistance has also been associated with increased ROR1 signaling, particularly in CLL.22,24 Our data extend this observation to MCL because Ven-resistant JEKO-1 and Granta-519 cells demonstrated enhanced ROR1 activation and downstream PI3K and β-catenin signaling. Our observations imply that ROR1-associated signaling may serve as an adaptive mechanism to sustain cell viability during drug treatment, underscoring the potential of ROR1 inhibition as a tentative strategy in overcoming resistance in MCL.

ROR1 functions as a developmental antigen and a pseudokinase involved in noncanonical Wnt5a signaling. Its restricted expression in adult tissues and overexpression in several cancers, including MCL, make it an attractive target.22,27

KAN571C treatment resulted in marked dephosphorylation of the TK and PRD of p-ROR1, accompanied by the inhibition of key downstream signaling pathways. This modulation was associated with a pronounced increase in apoptotic activity, particularly in Ven-resistant cells. These findings indicate that ROR1 may function as a central integrative node in therapeutic resistance, coordinating survival signals from multiple pathways to support tumor cell viability under treatment pressure. These results align with previous work in CLL showing that targeting ROR1 disrupts compensatory signaling networks.28,32 At the same time, we have observed that ROR1 inhibition through KAN571C increases BTK phosphorylation, suggesting a signaling interplay between the BTK and the ROR1 pathways. This compensatory relationship is also supported by our previous study, in which similar trends were reported in patient samples after ROR1 inhibition.33 Prior studies also proposed that ROR1 may function in opposition to pre-BCR signaling.36

A strength of this study is the careful use of multiple replicates in the western blot experiment for each of the 3 different MCL cell lines, which have well-documented differences in ROR1 expression and signaling. This strategy allowed us to capture the diversity of MCL and to see both common and unique patterns of resistance and drug sensitivity. At the same time, the development of robust MCL resistance models was accomplished through continuous exposure to Ibru and/or Ven, ensuring the establishment of stable and consistent resistance phenotypes. By creating Ibru-, Ven-, and Double-resistant models, we built a platform to study how resistance develops. The model has so far been restricted to cell lines, given the well-known technical limitation of long-term culturing of freshly obtained MCL cells from patients.

Limitations thus include the exclusive reliance on in vitro cell line models. Although these models recapitulate key features of clinical resistance, they lack the complexity of the tumor microenvironment, immune interactions, and pharmacokinetic considerations. The absence of in vivo validation represents an important gap; however, xenograft systems and patient-derived models are warranted, which could strengthen causal interpretations. Furthermore, detailed pharmacological characterization of KAN571C, including pharmacokinetics, target engagement, selectivity profiling, and in vivo pharmacodynamic evaluation, was outside the scope of this study and warrants further investigation.

In summary, our study demonstrates that ROR1 small-molecule inhibition via KAN571C effectively disrupts key survival pathways in various MCL cell lines, particularly in the context of Ven and Ibru resistance, or both, without signs of emerging KAN571C resistance. KAN571C’s capacity to eliminate Ven-resistant cells in vitro supports its further development toward clinical trials.
