Takotsubo syndrome mimicking left anterior descending coronary artery injury after left bundle branch area pacing: a case report

Introduction

Takotsubo syndrome (TTS) is a reversible cardiomyopathy often triggered by physical or emotional stress.1–5 Iatrogenic TTS associated with medical procedures, including surgery and catheter interventions, has also been reported, although cases following pacemaker implantation are rare.6

Left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) has emerged as a physiological pacing strategy that provides more synchronous ventricular activation than conventional right ventricular apical pacing.7 However, because the lead is advanced deep into the interventricular septum, its anatomical proximity to the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) is relevant.8 When chest discomfort or wall motion abnormalities occur after the procedure, differentiation from LAD injury is essential.8

We report a case of iatrogenic TTS following LBBAP requiring differentiation from LAD injury. This case highlights the diagnostic dilemma between coronary artery injury and TTS after LBBAP and the role of computed tomography (CT).

Case presentation

An 81-year-old woman was referred to our hospital after complete atrioventricular block was identified during evaluation of dyspnoea at a local clinic. On admission, her heart rate was 43 beats per minute (b.p.m.) with complete atrioventricular block (Figure 1A). Transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular systolic function without structural heart disease. Her medical history included hypertension, dyslipidemia, and hypothyroidism treated with levothyroxine. The clinical course is summarized in Figure 2.

(A) ECG obtained on admission demonstrating complete atrioventricular block with a ventricular rate of 43 b.p.m. (B) ECG obtained one day after pacemaker implantation showing a ventricular paced rhythm with a heart rate of 72 b.p.m. No significant ST-segment changes are present, while T-wave inversion is observed in leads V4–V6. (C) ECG obtained ∼6 months after pacemaker implantation showing a ventricular paced rhythm with a heart rate of 81 b.p.m. No significant ST-segment changes are present, and the previously observed T-wave inversion has resolved.

Timeline of the patient’s clinical course, including admission, pacemaker implantation, onset of chest symptoms, diagnostic evaluation, biomarker changes, treatment, and follow-up.

Permanent pacemaker implantation was indicated, and LBBAP was selected to achieve physiological ventricular activation. The lumenless pacing lead (SelectSecure™ 3830, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) delivered through a fixed-curve sheath (C315 His, Medtronic) was used. The lead was advanced into the interventricular septum with 15 rotations under fluoroscopic guidance. The operator targeted the LBBA. However, accepted electrophysiological criteria for definitive LBB capture were not fulfilled, pacing parameters were acceptable and the paced QRS was narrow; therefore, the lead was fixed at that position. Procedural parameters are summarized in Figure 3.

Fluoroscopic images during pacemaker implantation. (A) Left anterior oblique 35° projection. Advancement of the lumenless pacing lead into the interventricular septum for left bundle branch area pacing through a C315 His delivery sheath. The impedance change during lead insertion was within the normal range. (B) Final fluoroscopic image after successful lead placement (Anteroposterior projection). The V6 R-wave peak time measured ∼110 ms and the interpeak interval between V1 and V6 was ∼40 ms. At the end of the procedure, pacing threshold was 0.7 V at 0.4 ms, R-wave amplitude was 7 mV, and bipolar impedance was 817 Ω. No pericardial effusion was observed. Detailed unipolar measurements and current of injury data were unavailable.

On post-operative day 1, chest discomfort developed; however, she remained hemodynamically stable without heart failure or respiratory distress (blood pressure 158/78 mmHg, SpO2 97% on room air). Electrocardiogram (ECG) showed T-wave inversion in leads V4–V6 without ST-segment elevation (Figure 1B), and laboratory data revealed elevations in CK and high-sensitivity troponin I levels (Table 1). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed asynergy extending from the anterior wall to the apical segments, a distribution not consistent with isolated septal branch injury from the ventricular lead. Pacemaker interrogation was normal.

LAD injury caused by the ventricular lead was suspected. Chest discomfort was mild, and no ischaemic ST-segment changes were observed despite QT prolongation. Therefore, contrast-enhanced CT was selected as the initial diagnostic modality to evaluate possible coronary artery injury. The ventricular lead tip was located near the proximal LAD, but no obvious coronary artery injury or contrast extravasation was observed (Figure 4A,B). A lateral reconstructed coronary CT image showed that the LAD extended toward the apical region but did not demonstrate a definite wrap-around course (Figure 4C). ECG-gated CT demonstrated an apical ballooning pattern that did not correspond to a single coronary artery territory (Figure 4D). These findings suggested TTS.

Conservative management was initiated with olmesartan (20 mg/day) and bisoprolol (0.625 mg/day). Symptoms improved and CK levels peaked on post-operative day 2. During hospitalization, she remained hemodynamically stable. Echocardiography before discharge showed akinesis extending to the apical region with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 43%.

During outpatient pacemaker follow-up, device interrogation showed stable pacing parameters. Six months after pacemaker implantation, the ECG showed resolution of T-wave inversion (Figure 1C). Echocardiography showed recovery of left ventricular ejection fraction to 62% and resolution of wall motion abnormalities. Based on this reversible course, the patient was diagnosed with iatrogenic TTS following LBBAP.

Discussion

This case describes chest discomfort and apical-dominant wall motion abnormalities after LBBAP for complete atrioventricular block. Although LAD injury was initially suspected, the final diagnosis was TTS. This case illustrates overlap between LBBAP-related coronary injury and TTS.

LBBAP has increasingly been adopted as a physiological pacing strategy.7 However, because the screw-in lead must be advanced deep into the interventricular septum, LAD injury has been reported after LBBAP.8,9 In the present case, contrast-enhanced CT confirmed that the lead tip was located near the proximal LAD. In addition, the paced QRS morphology with negative complexes in leads I and aVL suggested a relatively high anteroseptal or right ventricular outflow tract-like pacing position rather than typical distal LBB capture. The relatively high anteroseptal lead position may have increased anatomical proximity to the proximal LAD. When chest discomfort and elevation of cardiac biomarkers were observed the day after implantation, coronary artery injury was initially suspected. However, coronary CT showed no contrast extravasation, vessel disruption, or occlusion. Furthermore, the distribution of wall motion abnormalities did not correspond to a single coronary territory. Nevertheless, CT alone could not exclude limited coronary dissection or intramural haematoma. The marked biomarker elevation may indicate concomitant myocardial injury. ECG-gated CT showed an apical ballooning, and follow-up echocardiography confirmed recovery of left ventricular systolic function. The InterTAK score was 56, corresponding to an intermediate probability of TTS. The presence of apical ballooning, reversible dysfunction, and recovery during follow-up supported the diagnosis, these findings were consistent with the 2018 International Expert Consensus criteria for TTS. Nevertheless the marked elevation in CK and high-sensitivity troponin I levels was atypical for isolated TTS and suggested substantial procedural myocardial injury. Procedural myocardial injury related to deep septal lead implantation, including mechanical or vasospastic injury near the proximal LAD, was considered plausible. However, the extensive apical ballooning pattern extending beyond a single coronary territory and the reversible course were difficult to explain solely by focal septal injury, suggesting secondary TTS triggered by procedural myocardial injury. However, because coronary angiography was not performed, significant procedural myocardial injury or transient ischaemic injury could not be definitively excluded.

TTS is known to occur after physical stress and medical procedures, including surgery, catheter interventions, and pacemaker implantation.1,3 Mechanical or procedural stress related to lead implantation may have contributed to TTS; however, this remains speculative and cannot be established based on a single case.

This case highlights that when chest discomfort and left ventricular dysfunction occur after LBBAP, coronary artery injury should first be considered. However, TTS should also be included in the differential diagnosis, particularly when wall motion abnormalities extend beyond a single coronary artery territory.1,2 In elderly women undergoing invasive procedures, iatrogenic TTS should be considered. Attention to the anatomical relationship between the lead and coronary arteries and post-operative imaging are important for procedural safety.

Several cases of TTS following LBBAP have been reported.10,11 Compared with these reports, the present case is distinguished by the use of ECG-gated CT to differentiate suspected LAD injury from TTS without performing invasive coronary angiography, highlighting a non-invasive diagnostic approach. Complications of LBBAP remain under investigation. Interpretation of imaging, biomarker trends, and clinical course is necessary to avoid unnecessary invasive treatment.

Conclusion

Iatrogenic TTS after LBBAP may mimic coronary artery injury. When chest discomfort, marked biomarker elevation, and left ventricular dysfunction occur after LBBAP, both TTS and coronary injury, including limited dissection or intramural haematoma, should be considered. Multimodality imaging and follow-up are essential for evaluating this diagnostic uncertainty.
