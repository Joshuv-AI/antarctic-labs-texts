Effects of ultrasound on bubble dynamic behavior of flow boiling in microchannel
Authors: Yong Guo, Qingqing Zhu, Shiliang Song, Yan Li, Zongbo Zhang, Liang Gong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBubble dynamics is paramount in comprehending the heat transfer mechanisms of flow boiling in the microchannel within ultrasonic field, which is regarded as a promising method to confront challenges of thermal management posed by microelectronic devices. Nevertheless, the impact of ultrasound on bubble behaviors and its underlying mechanisms remain largely unexplored. This study first delves into the effect of ultrasonic parameters on bubble dynamic behaviors and associated mechanisms, subsequently further analyzing the forces acting on bubbles through the constructed force model. The findings suggest that although growth force serves as the significant resistance, the primary Bjerknes force dominates the rapid detachment of bubbles. The secondary Bjerknes force results in the bubble only sliding along the bottom wall rather than lifting off. Furthermore, the elevated ultrasonic pressure amplitude resulting from augmenting ultrasonic power induces a substantial increase in the critical detachment diameter and growth rate by 55.49 % and 59.42 %, respectively. The enhanced primary Bjerknes force, attributed to the rise in ultrasonic frequency, leads to a 71.42 % increase in sliding velocity and a 46.45 % reduction in growth time. The positive impacts arising from ultrasonic power and frequency are anticipated to notably enhance the thermal performance of microchannels. Besides, surface tension acts as the resistance and diminishes slightly with an augmentation of the boiling number, resulting in a moderate variation in sliding velocity and growth time.Keywords: Bubble dynamic behavior, Flow boiling, Microchannel, Ultrasonic field, Force analysis

Introduction

Owing to the miniaturization and integration of microelectronic devices, its safe and efficient operation is seriously threatened by thermal failures. Hence, developing efficient cooling methods to dissipate the increasing heat is urgently demanded [1]. Flow boiling in microchannel has become one of the most promising methods for dealing with the heat dissipation problems of microelectronic devices due to its excellent thermal performance and availability of work medium [2]. However, the surge in heat flux arising from further advancements in the performance of microelectronic devices imposes a greater desire for augmenting heat extraction capability [3]. Previous explorations have indicated that ultrasound can significantly enhance the thermal performance of flow boiling in the microchannel to meet the requirements for thermal management under high heat flux [4], [5]. As a foundational step toward understanding the heat transfer mechanism of flow boiling under ultrasonic fields, it is crucial to delve into the corresponding dynamic behavior of bubbles [6].

For bubble behaviors of pool boiling under ultrasound, the stretch of ultrasonic negative pressure and heat absorption substantially accelerate the growth of vapor embryos, while subsequent positive-pressure compression brings about the contraction or even collapse of expanding bubbles. The oscillating bubbles expand rapidly within the interplay of ultrasonic and thermal fields [7]. Moreover, the acoustic radiation forces, including primary Bjerknes force and secondary Bjerknes force, facilitate the attached bubble’s rapid detachment [8]. Notably, the primary Bjerknes force, stemming from the ultrasonic pressure gradient, drives the bubble with the resonance frequency exceeding its driving frequency towards the nearest pressure antinode. It propels the bubble with resonance frequencies lower than the corresponding driving frequency towards the pressure node [9]. Additionally, radial oscillations of two adjacent bubbles in the ultrasonic field result in the secondary Bjerknes force, which exhibits either a repulsive or attractive force determined by the phase difference between them [10]. When the resonance frequencies of the two oscillating bubbles are larger or less than the driving frequency, the phase difference between the two bubbles is zero, and the secondary Bjerknes force manifests itself as an attractive force. Conversely, when the driving frequency lies between the resonance frequencies of the two oscillating bubbles, the phase difference between the two bubbles is 180°, and the secondary Bjerknes force manifests itself as a repulsive force [11]. Albeit the bubble dynamics of pool boiling under the ultrasonic field has been extensively studied, the influence of ultrasound on the bubble behavior of flow boiling in microchannel remains largely unexplored. Previous investigations have demonstrated that ultrasound can accelerate the bubble migration of flowing boiling in the minichannel [3], [4]. Yu et al. [3] speculated that the acoustic radiation force dominated the rapid motion of the bubbles. Additionally, Xiao et al. [12] found that the growth rate of bubbles of flow boiling in the minichannel remained unaffected by ultrasound, in contrast to the macroscopic experimental results [9]. Besides, Guo et al. [4] found that the bubble collapsed during accelerated migration in a microchannel with ultrasound by numerical simulation, which is also quietly different from experimental observations of the pool boiling. The above preliminary studies indicate that ultrasound can promote the rapid detachment and migration of bubbles in microchannels. However, the research into the effect of ultrasound on bubble dynamics and associated theoretical analysis has not yet been carried out. In addition, there are significant discrepancies between the macroscopic and microscopic observation of bubble dynamic behaviors, and the underlying mechanism for the distinctions needs to be further elucidated.

In this study, a visualization system equipped with a high-speed camera and a microscope, synchronizing with the characteristic time of ultrasound, is induced to document bubble behaviors. Given the complexity of flow boiling, the characteristic parameters of the bubble dynamics obey some kind of probability distribution, rendering it challenging to accurately estimate the ultrasound’s impact on bubble dynamics by the simple average. Therefore, based on observational data, the statistical analysis of the characteristic parameters of the bubble dynamic behaviors is conducted to quantify the effect of ultrasound. Experimental results demonstrate that ultrasound significantly promotes the growth, detachment, and migration of bubbles. Moreover, a force model is constructed, elucidating the predominant role of primary Bjerknes force on the promotion of bubble detachment. It is envisioned that this research into bubble dynamics within the ultrasonic field can serve as theoretical guidance for the further enhancement of the thermal performance of flow boiling in microchannels.

Experimental apparatus and method

Experimental setup

Comprehensive investigations are performed utilizing the experimental setup depicted in Fig. 1. The system comprises a heat sink, fluid flow loop, heating module, data acquisition system, ultrasound module, and visualization module. The heat sink is fabricated on a copper substrate (Ls = 98 mm, Ws = 98 mm, Hs = 5 mm) containing six parallel microchannels (Lch = 60 mm, Wch = 0.40 mm, Hch = 0.40 mm) and two buffer zones (Lb = 25.4 mm, Wb = 5 mm, Hb = 0.40 mm). The degassed deionized water is chosen as the working medium, with its mass flow rate controlled by the micro-injection pump (LONGER, LSP02-2A). Four cartridge heaters (maximum heating power Ph = 130 W) are inserted into side holes located in the extended copper plates and powered by direct current (MESTEI, DP3020-600W). The K-type thermocouple (Omega, TT-K-40) is employed for temperature measurement of the wall and fluid (refer to Fig. 2), while the pressure transducer (Hosswill, HCP100-H) is utilized to capture the pressure difference between the inlet and outlet. Voltage signals converted from temperature and pressure sensors are transmitted to the computer via data acquisition (Keithley, 2700). Furthermore, the ultrasound module consists of the ultrasonic generator (Mereal, MF-100) and the transducer. The visualization system comprises a high-speed camera (Photron, NOVA S6) and a microscope (Navitar, Ultra Zoom12X). Besides, the optical tensiometer (Biolin Scientific, Theta Lite) and infrared laser Doppler vibrometer (KVD-4525S, KathMatic) are used to measure the contact angle and microchannel displacement within acoustic field, respectively.

Experimental procedure

Experiments are conducted at operating conditions of mass flow rate of 34.7 kg·m−2·s−1, heat flux from 2.18 kW·m−2 to 5.77 kW·m−2, ultrasonic frequency of 26 kHz, 31 kHz, and 40 kHz, and ultrasonic power of 20 W, 40 W, and 60 W. The initial temperature of working medium in the inlet is 24.50 °C (error ± 0.52 °C). Before performing the experiments, the microchannel heat sink is heated up to 120 °C for 20 min, and the deionized water is degassed. Then, the liquid is injected into the test section, and operating parameters (heat flux and mass flow rate) are adjusted to the desired conditions. The ultrasound starts working as the temperature and pressure drop reach the steady state. Meanwhile, bubble behaviors are documented by the visualization module (80000 frames·s−1).

Force model

To elucidate the mechanism of ultrasound acting on the bubble behaviors, the force model is constructed.

Distinct from the scenario without ultrasonic field, the ultrasound results in the radiation forces acting on bubbles, known as the primary Bjerknes force and secondary Bjerknes force. Distinct from the pool boiling, the height and width of microchannels are much smaller than the ultrasonic wavelength λu (the ultrasonic wavelength with frequencies of 26 kHz, 31 kHz, and 40 kHz are 59.35 mm, 49.78 mm, and 38.58 mm, respectively). The ultrasonic pressure gradient in the direction of height (Y axis) and width (Z axis) are approximated to be zero. Moreover, the radius oscillations of adjacent bubbles under the ultrasonic field lead to the secondary Bjerknes force, whose force mode is determined by the phase difference between the two bubbles [9]. Once the driving frequency is either larger or smaller than the resonant frequencies of two bubbles, the phase difference is zero, and the force manifests as an attractive force. While the driving frequency falls between the respective resonance frequencies of two bubbles, the phase difference approaches 180°, and the force manifests as a repulsive force. Limited by the microscopic view, this paper exclusively researches the growth and detachment of the single bubble at its nucleation site. Consequently, the secondary Bjerknes force between two adjacent bubbles with radial oscillations is ignored. Nevertheless, when the heating surface is perpendicular to the wavefront [21], it exerts a significant attractive force on the attached bubble. This is attributed to the secondary Bjerknes force between the real bubble growing at nucleation site and corresponding virtual image bubble. Results and discussions

Effect of ultrasonic power on bubble behavior

Fig. 4(a) depicts the bubble behavior within the ultrasonic field (f = 31 kHz, P = 60 W). Red and blue represent the process of growth and detachment, respectively, while yellow denotes the critical detachment state. Initially, the vapor embryo experiences rapid growth (0–0.04 ms) due to the stretching caused by negative ultrasonic pressure and heat absorption. Subsequently, the bubble undergoes compression due to the positive pressure (0.04 ms-0.05 ms). The continuous heat absorption elevates the internal pressure of the bubble, significantly weakening the compressive effect of the positive pressure. Hence, the phenomenon of bubble collapse is hardly observed during experiments. The bubble continues to undergo rapid oscillation under the interaction between the ultrasonic and thermal fields (0.04 ms–0.09 ms). It initiates sliding along the wall with rapid oscillations (0.09 ms–0.34 ms) after reaching the critical detachment state. Moreover, with the bubble expansion, capillary waves emerge on its surface. As depicted in Fig. 4(a-c), decreasing the ultrasonic power from P = 60 W to P = 20 W results in a noteworthy reduction in both the growth time and critical detachment diameter of the bubble, alongside a substantial increase in sliding velocity. This phenomenon is quite different from the conventional flow boiling, indicating that the variation in ultrasonic power significantly affects the bubble behavior.

Bubble behavior under different ultrasonic powers.

To quantify the impact of ultrasonic power on bubble dynamic behavior, this study constructs the probability density function by Gaussian distribution [17], which effectively fits the characteristic parameters derived from 90 to 110 sets of experimental data for bubble dynamics under different ultrasonic parameters (refer to Fig. 5). As the ultrasonic power diminishes from P = 60 W to P = 40 W, the critical detachment diameter and growth rate decline by 16.86 μm and 0.29 m·s−1, respectively. A further reduction to 20 W decreases the critical detachment diameter and growth rate by 63.15 μm and 0.69 m·s−1, respectively. This characteristic demonstrates that the ultrasonic power directly determines the bubble diameter and the growth rate, which is distinct from the scenario without ultrasound. As shown in Fig. 6, the amplitude of microchannel displacement rises by 38.6 %, with ultrasonic power declining from 60 W to 20 W, contributing to a significant decrease in the ultrasonic pressure amplitude. Therefore, the stretching effect of negative ultrasonic pressure is substantially weakened. Moreover, the drop of ultrasonic power from 60 W to 20 W decreases the growth time from 0.09 ms to 0.05 ms and increases the sliding velocity from 1261.21 μm·s−1 to 1475.11 μm·s−1. Thus, ultrasound significantly reduces growth time and elevates sliding velocity compared to those dynamic parameters of conventional flow boiling [3]. This promotion on bubble departure suggests that ultrasound exerts a substantial driving force on bubbles, breaking the force equilibrium early. Notably, despite the large primary Bjerknes force induced by high power, the bubble dynamic behavior exhibits an opposing trend.

Based on the above experimental results, force analyses are conducted to further unveil the mechanism of ultrasonic power acting on bubble dynamics. As depicted in Table 2, surface tension and growth force significantly impede bubble detachment. Given the dominant impact of ultrasonic power on bubble diameter, an escalation of power from 20 W to 60 W augments the critical detachment diameter, thereby elevating surface tension and growth force. Meanwhile, the rise in ultrasonic power engenders rapid bubble growth and leads to a further surge in the growth force, which serves as the main resistance for bubble detachment. Additionally, critical detachment diameters under ultrasonic powers of 20 W, 40 W, and 60 W are 63.15 μm, 81.33 μm, and 98.19 μm, respectively. The corresponding resonant frequencies are 105.38 kHz, 81.58 kHz, and 67.46 kHz, respectively. All of them exceed the driving frequency of 31 kHz. Consequently, the primary Bjerknes force acting on the bubbles at the observation point (X-axis coordinate X = 45.00 mm) directs towards the nearest pressure antinode (X = 54.89 mm), i.e. pointing to the outlet (X = 60.00 mm). At the critical detachment state, the quasi-steady drag force Fqs is negligible owing to the little relative velocity between the bubble and near-wall liquid. Moreover, FPB under different ultrasonic powers significantly surpasses the associated sum of resistances, namely Fsx and Fdux. This demonstrates that FPB is predominant in promoting bubble detachment. The ultrasonic wavelength is kept constant under the same frequency, while increased power elevates the ultrasonic pressure amplitude. Consequently, the ultrasonic pressure gradient and the resulting FPB augment substantially with the rising power.

Effect of ultrasonic frequency on bubble behavior

Fig. 7 illustrates the bubble behaviors under different ultrasonic frequencies, including 26 kHz, 31 kHz, and 40 kHz, respectively. It is evident that with the augmentation of ultrasonic frequency, the growth time and sliding velocity diminish noticeably, while the diameter and growth rate at the critical detachment state remain relatively stable. As depicted in Fig. 8, the growth time decreases by 16.00 % as the ultrasonic frequency escalates from 26 kHz to 31 kHz. It continues to diminish by 36.24 %, with the frequency further rising to 40 kHz. In contrast, the sliding velocity escalates from 1033.74 μm·ms−1 to 1453.57 μm·ms−1 as the ultrasound frequency ascends from 26 kHz to 40 kHz. This signifies that, the rising frequency enhances the driving force induced by ultrasound and leads to an earlier disruption of force equilibrium, thereby diminishing growth time and elevating sliding velocity. In addition, according to Eq. (9), ultrasonic power and frequency collectively determine the ultrasound pressure amplitude. Under the same ultrasonic power, the oscillation amplitude declines as the frequency increases (refer to Fig. 9). The corresponding amplitudes for different frequencies of 26 kHz, 31 kHz, and 40 kHz are 0.76 μm, 0.68 μm, and 0.52 μm, respectively. The resultant acoustic pressure amplitudes are approximately equal, namely 0.183 MPa, 0.195 MPa, and 0.192 MPa. Hence, the growth rate dominated by acoustic pressure amplitude remains relatively constant. It is noteworthy that critical detachment diameter experiences a little decline of 8.93 % as the driving frequencies increase.

This is because constant ultrasonic power leads to the same amplitude of the ultrasound pressure under different frequencies, while the wavelength decreases with the increase in frequency. The resulting gradient of the ultrasonic pressure increases notably. Additionally, with the rise in ultrasonic frequency, the growth rate remains stable, and the critical detachment diameter decreases slightly. Thus, the growth force remains relatively constant, albeit with a little reduction in surface tension. Due to the minimal disparity in bubble diameters under different ultrasonic frequencies, the corresponding bubble mass varies insignificantly. As the frequency rises, the net force increases, and the force equilibrium of the bubbles is disrupted earlier, resulting in the shorter growth time and faster sliding velocity of bubbles.

Effect of boiling number on bubble behavior

Bubble behaviors subjected to the ultrasound (f = 26 kHz, P = 60 W) with different boiling numbers Bo are depicted in Fig. 10. With the increase of boiling number, the variations of growth time, critical detachment diameter, and sliding velocity are insignificant. To explore the slight distinctions in the bubble behaviors under different operating conditions, the corresponding characteristic parameters are statistically counted, as shown in Fig. 11. As the boiling number rises from 2.80·10−5 to 7.41·10−5, the growth rate remains stable, the increment in sliding velocity is 9.65 %, and the reduction in growth time and critical detachment radius is merely 10.0 % and 2.95 %. The ultrasound parameters remain stable while the net force rises slightly, implying that the variation of boiling number affects the resistance during bubble detachment.

Conclusion

This study investigates the effect of ultrasound on bubble behaviors and associated mechanisms. The main findings are as follows:

(1)The growth force, serving as the crucial resistance, is enhanced by the alternating positive and negative pressures of ultrasound. Nevertheless, the primary Bjerknes force still predominates in promoting the rapid bubble departure. Additionally, the secondary Bjerknes force between the real bubble attached to the wall and the image bubble manifests as an attraction, causing the bubble to depart from its nucleation site by sliding along the bottom wall rather than lifting off.

Additionally, the secondary Bjerknes force between the real bubble attached to the wall and the image bubble manifests as an attraction, causing the bubble to depart from its nucleation site by sliding along the bottom wall rather than lifting off.

(2)The ultrasonic power plays an important role in ultrasonic pressure amplitude, thereby dominating the bubble diameter and growth rate at the initial boiling stage. With the increase in ultrasonic power, the critical departure diameter and growth rate significantly elevate by 55.49 % and 59.42 %, respectively. Moreover, as ultrasonic power escalates, the rising tendency of primary Bjerkenes force remains slower compared to that of bubble mass. Therefore, corresponding bubble acceleration exhibits a pronounced downtrend.

(3)With the increase in ultrasonic frequency, the growth time decreases by 46.45 %, while sliding velocity increases by 65.83 %. This is attributed to the significant enhancement of ultrasonic frequency in primary Bjerknes force. Under the same power, the ultrasonic frequency rises while the amplitude of the microchannel displacement diminishes. Hence, the amplitude of ultrasonic pressure remains stable, leading to an unaltered growth force. Besides, the enhanced primary Bjerknes force under high frequency brings about the early disruption of force equilibrium, which results in a little decrease of associated critical departure diameter.
