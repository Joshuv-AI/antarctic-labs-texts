Diversification in ANME-1 archaea is associated with the presence of highly variable genomic hotspots
Authors: Ying-Li Zhou, Jing-Chun Feng, Rui Lu, Zhe Chen, Paraskevi Mara, Xuanyu Tao, Jinyi Liu, Yongji Huang, Junlin Hu, Jianyun Yao, Virginia P Edgcomb, Andreas Teske, Xiaoxue Wang, Si Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnaerobic methanotrophic (ANME) archaea have been primarily documented by metagenomic analysis of environmental samples. The mechanisms that drive their diversification and speciation are poorly understood. Here we analyse the phylogenomic diversity at the species and strain levels of clade ANME-1 from deep-sea cold seeps, as a model system with a well-studied phylogenetic framework. We reconstruct high-quality circular metagenomic-assembled genomes (cMAGs) and identify highly variable genomic hotspots that distinguish them. Genomic differentiation and diversification in ANME-1 is associated with genes involved in prokaryotic defense systems, transport mechanisms and methane metabolism. In addition, heterologous expression of ANME-1 hicAB operons supports their proposed role as toxin/antitoxin systems, possibly involved in mediating responses to environmental stresses.Subject terms: Evolutionary ecology, Marine biology, Speciation, Archaeal evolution, Metagenomics

Introduction

Cold seeps are geological formations along tectonic margins and subduction zones that channel hydrocarbon-rich and sulfide compounds, including methane, from subsurface reservoirs to seafloor sediments and overlying water columns1. These dynamic ecosystems sustain deep-sea chemosynthetic communities, host endemic fauna2 and diverse microbial consortia adapted to the extreme redox gradients of the seeps3,4. Within the cold seep sediments, anaerobic methane oxidation coupled to sulfate reduction dominates carbon cycling, and is mediated primarily by anaerobic methanotrophic (ANME) archaea and their syntrophic partners, sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB)5,6. This ANME-SRB partnership consumes >90% of seafloor methane emissions, forming a critical barrier against methane release from the seafloor to the atmosphere7. Despite the global distribution and significance on methane cycling, ANME physiology, diversification, and speciation remain enigmatic because of the unsuccessful axenic growth of ANME under laboratory conditions8. To understand the evolutionary strategies on how ANME diversify and live successfully in cold seeps, one must rely only on the available ANME genomic data that in many occasions appear to be incomplete or highly fragmented9–12.

The anaerobic methanotrophic archaeon ANME-1, formally classified within the order Candidatus Methanophagales, was initially identified through 16S rRNA gene surveys in methane seep sediments13. Candidatus Methanophagales 16S rRNA genes and genomes have since been recovered from a wide spectrum of anoxic environments, including marine cold seeps, hydrothermal vents and deep subsurface aquifers9. Within ANME-1, recent work has resolved several deeply branching subclades, including ANME-1a, ANME-1b and the thermophilic, basal ANME-1c lineage14,15, indicating substantial hidden diversity and functional differentiation within this order9,12. Across global methane seeps, ANME-1 communities, including ANME-1a and ANME-1b, tend to dominate deeper, more reduced sediment layers below the sulfate-methane transition zone (SMTZ), whereas ANME-2 typically prevails in shallower SMTZ horizons12. Within individual cold seeps, ANME-1a is often more abundant near the SMTZ and upper anoxic sediments, while ANME-1b becomes enriched in deeper, sulfate-depleted horizons, indicating subtle vertical niche partitioning within the ANME-1 clade16,17. Deep-branching ANME-1c is mainly reported from hydrothermally heated sediments such as those in the Guaymas Basin, where it forms syntrophic consortia with thermophilic Thermodesulfobacteria and performs sulfate-dependent anaerobic oxidation of methane (AOM) at temperatures reaching the upper thermal limit of anaerobic methane oxidation15. Despite this remarkable phylogenetic diversity, broad biogeographic range, and clear evidence for niche partitioning, the environmental and selective pressures that have shaped ANME‑1 diversification and enabled their adaptation and thriving in such dynamically changing habitats2,18 remain largely unresolved.

Recent genomic surveys of ANME-1 have expanded the clade’s known diversity and uncovered an expansive virome comprising novel viral lineages, including undescribed families whose metabolic genes (e.g., thymidylate synthase14) appear to undergo host gene replacement mediated by recombination. Complementary studies on methanogenesis evolution indicate that methane and short-chain alkane metabolisms originated early in archaeal history and were repeatedly reshaped by horizontal gene transfer, providing a broader evolutionary framework for ANME-1 diversification19,20. However, current ANME-1 genomic datasets remain dominated by fragmented short-read metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), which obscure fine-scale genomic differentiation and struggle to resolve structural variations or to identify gene-level selection pressures across complete microbial genomes, critical mechanisms driving microbial evolution4,21,22. Long-read sequencing circumvents many of these barriers by enabling reconstruction of complete, circular MAGs23,24; yet its broad adoption remains limited due to high sequencing costs and stringent requirements for DNA quantity, purity and integrity during library preparation.

Here, we utilize ultra-deep, short- and long-read metagenomics and single-cell genomics to reconstruct 692 non-redundant MAGs and 4069 single-amplified genomes (SAGs), including 130 complete circularized genomes for sediments from the Haima cold seeps in the South China Sea. Our results show strain-level diversity within ANME-1 species not previously described by short-read metagenomics surveys. Using different metrics, we show that the diversification and genomic differentiation of ANME-1 in cold seeps is largely driven by positive selection on highly variable genomic regions, in particular prokaryotic defense genes, including the widely distributed hicAB operon, which is present in all our ANME-1 strains. This operon encodes a toxin-antitoxin (TA) system that functions via ribosomal RNA binding and cleavage25,26, thought to control prokaryotic cell growth, survival, and response to environmental stressors27,28. We examined its role in regulating population size and inducing lethality after expression in E. coli. By complementing the comparative genomic analysis of closely related strains with expression studies of critical genes in diversification hotspots, we show how the function of specific genes and gene clusters contributes to the genomic differentiation of uncultured ANME-1 archaea.

Results

Microbial community assemblages in the Haima cold seep sediments, and domination of ANME in deeper sediments

To gain insight into the genomic diversity and plasticity of ANME in cold seeps, we combined ultra-deep, short- and long-read metagenomic data from four sediment layers (0–2, 4–6, 21–24, and 27–30 centimeters below sea floor (cmbsf)) (Fig. 1a, b, Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 1). Geochemical analysis of the sediment core demonstrates a marked increase in dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) concentration coupled with progressively lower δ13C values of DIC at greater depths (Supplementary Fig. 2). This trend indicates enhanced anaerobic oxidation of methane activity in the deeper sediment layer (Supplementary Notes 1). Our approach generated higher sequencing depth for ANME-dominated metagenomes (Supplementary Data 2, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Notes 2 and 3) than previously published cold seep sediment metagenomes (n = 153 samples) (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Data 3). We further recovered 568 long-read-derived and 313 short-read-derived MAGs by dereplicating at the strain level with average nucleotide identity (ANI) ≥ 99% (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Data 4, 5 and 6). Long-read MAGs exhibited high recovery of important functional elements, including full-length ribosomal RNA genes and intact insertion sequences (IS) that represent mobile genetic elements or transposons (Supplementary Fig. 4). Our MAG dataset contains the largest number of circular MAGs (cMAGs, see Methods, n = 130) recovered from deep sea cold seep sediments to date12,29,30 (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Figs. 5–7 and Supplementary Notes 4).

Short-read and long-read MAGs were combined and dereplicated at the species level (ANI ≥ 95%), enhancing the microbial recovery efficiency (Fig. 1e, f, Supplementary Figs. 8, 9 and Supplementary Notes 5), yielding 487 non-redundant, species-representative MAGs (Supplementary Fig. 10). Taxonomy assigned with Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB, release 214)31 indicates that 89.7% of the 487 MAGs (437 MAGs) represent novel lineages at species or higher levels (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Data 7). Long-read ANME MAGs accounted for 28% of sequenced reads and in our cold seep sediments below 20 cmbsf and demonstrated higher ANME recovery efficiency at both species and strain levels than short-read MAGs (Fig. 1e). In the MAG dataset assembled in this study, 88 MAGs (99% ANI dereplicated) represent 24 ANME species (Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Data 8); 22 out of the 24 represent previously undescribed taxa.

We additionally generated 4,069 SAGs from our deepest sediment sample (27–30 cmbsf), that exhibited maximal ANME enrichment (Fig. 1e). Within this sediment horizon, ANME-1 dominated methanotrophic communities, accounting for 20% of metagenome reads (Fig. 1g). SAG-derived relative abundances confirmed ANME-1 and the bacterial phylum Desulfobacterota as co-dominant lineages, complementing the MAG-based observations (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Data 9, 10).

High-quality MAGs and SAGs at strain level improve genomic functional analysis and show high diversity of ANME-1 in cold seeps

To characterize the undescribed ANME taxa in Haima cold seep sediments, we compared our data with the publicly available ANME metagenomic data and performed phylogenetic and genomic functional analyses. We recovered 52 ANME-1 MAGs (99% ANI dereplicated) exhibiting ≥90% completeness from our long-read metagenomes. These MAGs resolved 12 phylogenetically distinct species-level clades (intraclade ANI ≥ 95%), of which 11 have not been previously reported (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Data 8). Strain-level diversity was further supported by analysis of SAGs from the 27–30 cmbsf sediment layer, which revealed 726 SAGs affiliating with the HP11-cbin57-related ANME-1 MAGs at subspecies and strain levels (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Notes 6).

To perform genomic functional analysis in our ANME-1 genomes, we first compared our ANME-1 MAGs obtained through long-read sequencing to publicly available ANME-1 MAGs deposited in the GTDB and National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) databases. The MAGs obtained in our study displayed significant improvement in assembly continuity and completeness (average 96.35% vs. 86.92%) compared to existing and publicly available ANME-1 genomic data (Fig. 2b, p < 0.001, two-sided Wilcoxon test; Supplementary Data 11). Although our dataset showed a small numerical increase in average contamination (1.87% in our data vs. 1.73% publicly available data), this difference is not statistically significant (p > 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 14) and remains well within high-quality standards32. To minimize the potential impact of even minor contamination, we manually curated the long‑read ANME-1 MAGs (See “Methods”).

We compared our ANME-1 cMAG (HP11-cbin57) with the best quality publicly available genome for the same species, acknowledging that the publicly available genome contains 248 contigs and it is highly fragmented (GCA_013139985.1; estimated completeness 87%) (Fig. 2c). To identify whether the deposited ANME-1 genomes lacked genes that are not recovered due to short-read fragmentation artifacts, we compared the cMAG (HP11-cbin57, this study) against five short-read MAGs for the same species: two from the NCBI database and three assembled in our study. Ortholog clustering analysis (≥90% nucleotide identity) revealed 585 genes present in the long-read HP11-cbin57 cMAG that were missing from all five short-read MAGs (Supplementary Fig. 15a and Supplementary Data 12). These 585 missing genes accounted for 18% of the total genes identified in the long-read HP11-cbin57 cMAG. While most of the missing genes represented proteins with uncharacterized function, we find that genes with better annotations encoded housekeeping proteins, including proteins associated with DNA replication, recombination and repair (Supplementary Fig. 15b). Most genes missing from the five short-read MAGs were dispersed throughout the long-read HP11-cbin57 cMAG. A contiguous region found in the long-read HP11-cbin57 cMAG was absent from all five short-read assemblies and encodes viral marker proteins, including the baseplate-tail protein found at the distal end of the tail of bacteriophages (Supplementary Fig. 15c and Supplementary Data 12). It is possible that the short-read ANME-1 assemblies (both in our study and also in the public databases) miss essential mobile genes acquired via horizontal gene transfer or through viral infections due to fragmentation artifacts associated with short-read sequencing.

Structural variations shape the diversity of ANME-1 taxa in cold seeps

To identify the factors that contribute to ANME-1 diversity and speciation in cold seep sediments, we searched the identified ANME-1 species for genomic structural variations (SVs; deletions, insertions, inversions duplications), by comparing the consensus MAGs of different ANME-1 strains belonging to the same species against their best representative MAG found in our study (see “Methods” and Supplementary Notes 7).

Among the 12 ANME-1 species found, seven were represented by species clusters containing more than one strain; these seven clusters were used for the SV analyses (Fig. 2a). Overall, SV analyses revealed a total of 7119 SVs with average length of 7568 nucleotides (Supplementary Fig. 16), including 3201 deletions, 3210 insertions, 376 inversions and 332 duplications (Supplementary Data 13). DNA insertions (45%) and DNA deletions (45%) were the most common SVs among ANME-1 species (Fig. 3a). DNA inversions were the longest SVs with a median length of 21.7 kb (Fig. 3b). The number of SVs identified within a species is influenced by its strain diversity, as higher number of intra-species comparisons results in a greater raw SV count (Supplementary Fig. 17). Therefore, these values should be interpreted in the context of the number of reconstructed strains per species. After normalizing for the number of pairwise comparisons, we observed the species represented by HP9-3-cbin343 exhibited the highest accumulation of SVs per comparison (~ 226 SVs; Fig. 3a). The ANME-1 variant HP11-cbin57, which contains a DNA insertion in its genome, was also found to be infected by proviruses. We found viral protein domains in HP11-cbin57 (Supplementary Fig. 18) that were absent in other ANME-1 strains.

To examine whether the identified SVs occur in certain genetic loci or if they are present randomly in the ANME-1 genomes, we investigated the function of genes on SVs (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Data 14). We find that the SV-affected genes were predominantly associated with three functionally annotated KO groups: transporters (n = 102), prokaryotic defense systems (n = 60) and methane metabolism (n = 49) (Supplementary Fig. 17b). Genes encoding ABC-2-type system permease protein (ABC-2.P) represented the most prevalent SV-associated transporters (n = 26). These proteins form transmembrane channels in ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, mediating selective import of nutrients or export of toxins33,34. This functionality likely supports ANME-1’s cellular homeostasis, stress adaptation, and syntrophic partnerships in anaerobic methane-rich ecosystems. SVs were also found to influence methane metabolism genes encoding the formylmethanofuran dehydrogenase (FwdBDE) and heterodisulfide reductase (HdrA and HdrD) (Supplementary Data 14). These proteins are critical for methanogenesis35, and function in reverse for the anaerobic oxidation of methane, the key metabolism of ANME-1. Further global analysis of methane metabolism genes within SV revealed distinct distribution patterns in different lineages and habitats. Genes involved in the synthesis of coenzyme M (CoM), such as comA, and comB, were found to have a patchy distribution and were exclusively detected in marine-derived ANME-1a, ANME-1b, and ANME-2c lineages (Supplementary Fig. 19a). In contrast, the potential hydrogenase gene frhB and the subunits of heterodisulfide reductase (hdrA and hdrD) were widely distributed across different ANME lineages and were detected in all collected habitats (Supplementary Fig. 19b). Notably, although the isolated hdrD homolog was identified in ANME-1 lineages, it lacked the hdrE subunit required for formation of the canonical membrane-bound HdrDE complex, consistent with established models of electron cycling9.

The SVs related with prokaryotic defense systems contained genes for restriction-modification enzymes (R-M), including adenine-specific DNA-methyltransferase, type I restriction enzyme (hsdRSM), and type II toxin-antitoxin (TA) operons (hicAB, mazF, parE, doc and yafQ) (Supplementary Data 14). The TA operons showed SV-mediated insertions and partial deletions (Fig. 3d). Notably, 6 hicAB genes showed different types of SV-mediated arrangements within the ANME-1 genomes (Fig. 3d). The HicAB module is thought to function by binding RNA that is then cleaved by the putative RNase HicA26. Comparing the different hicAB operons to three different species reference ANME-1 genomes of this study (ANME-1 HPA-cbin912, HP11-cbin57 and MP9-3-cbin541; Fig. 3d), we find that the SVs in loci upstream and downstream of hicA exhibit multiple gene insertions associated with signal transduction, DNA repair and DNA replication (i.e., strain HP9-3-cbin210; Fig. 3e). We aligned our long-reads against the species-representative ANME-1 genomes to estimate the coverage of SVs in the hicA loci (Fig. 3d). The alignment demonstrated that the predicted SV regions in the hicA loci are widely distributed in different ANME-1 strains (Supplementary Figs. 20–25). We also identified homology between our SV-associated HicB and an MGE-encoded protein14 associated with ANME-1 (Supplementary Data 15), which suggests mobile genetic elements mediate horizontal transfer of hicB related SVs. Phylogenetic analysis indicates horizontal gene transfer (HGT) of the hicAB operon between ANME lineages and four diverse archaeal/bacterial phyla: Atribacterota, Thermoplasmata, Fermentibacterota, and Nanoarchaeota (Supplementary Fig. 26).

ANME-1 genomic differentiation occurs through positive selection on certain ANME-1 genes

To identify genetic loci that may be hot spots contributing to ANME-1 genomic differentiation, we calculated different metrics, including single nucleotide variation (SNV), the ratio of non-synonymous (pN) to synonymous (pS) polymorphisms (pN/pS), average linkage disequilibrium (D’) and nucleotide diversity in our samples (Supplementary Data 16 and see Methods). Based on the estimated major allele frequencies on bi-allelic SNVs in the dominant ANME-1 species HP11-cbin57 (a metric that indicates which allele appears most often in the genome of individuals within a given population), the deeper sample (27-30 cmbsf) exhibited more strain diversity of ANME-1 species when compared to the surficial sample (0–6 cmbsf) (Supplementary Fig. 27). At 27–30 cmbsf, we find 106,360 to 240,249 SNV-sites in 12 species level ANME-1 MAGs (average:179,848). MAG HPA-cbin238 had the most SNV sites with a density of 81.9 SNVs per Kbp (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Data 16). The average genome pN/pS value of the 12 species MAGs was 0.225 (Supplementary Fig. 28a), with MAG HPA-cbin912 having the highest pN/pS value (0.345).

To investigate the role of polymorphisms in shaping genome dynamics and the diversity of ANME-1, we also estimated average D’ value across the genome, the parameter which shows how often alleles at genetic loci are inherited together, within the 12 ANME-1 species. D’ can show selection pressures and recombination rates within prokaryotic populations and is influenced by population size and growth dynamics36. Gene shuffling during recombination can decrease D’ through creating new combinations of alleles at different loci37. We find a significant negative correlation (R = −0.84, p = 0.0012) between average D’ and species abundance (Fig. 4b). The SNV density, pN/pS, and nucleotide diversity all exhibited weak, negative and non-significant correlations with species relative abundance (p > 0.05). Two evolution metrics (Average D’ and SNV densities) appear structured at the higher genus level, which differed significantly between ANME-1a and ANME-1b lineages belonging to the same family. However, distinct genera within ANME-1a (QENJ01 and QEXZ01) exhibited similar average D’ and SNV densities (Supplementary Data 16). These results suggest that while deeper divergence between ANME-1a and ANME-1b reflects distinct genomic dynamics, genus-level diversification within ANME-1a does not produce detectable differences in these metrics.

Further examination of the effect of different selection pressures across functional gene categories using pathway-based analysis of pN/pS values in ANME-1 genomes and the KEGG ortholog classification (Supplementary Data 17) revealed that genes associated with bacterial motility proteins (ko02035, n = 15, average pN/pS = 0.303), prokaryotic defense systems (ko02048, n = 51, average pN/pS = 0.295) and secretion system (ko02044, n = 17, average pN/pS = 0.268) were the three categories (KEGG Brite or pathway classification) with the highest average pN/pS values (Supplementary Data 18). These values were significantly higher (two-sided Wilcoxon test, p < 0.01) compared to the pN/pS values of genes associated with core metabolism, including carbon fixation (ko00720, n = 14, average pN/pS = 0.141), methane (ko00680, n = 65, pN/pS average = 0.16) and nitrogen metabolism (ko00910, n = 6, pN/pS average = 0.188) (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 28b). Within defense systems we note that genes associated with the hicAB TA operon had the highest pN/pS value (1.95) suggesting the hicAB operon is under positive selection (Fig. 4d). Similarly, within the transport systems 59 out of 94 identified transporter genes showed elevated pN/pS ratios (>0.225) when compared to the genome-wide average pN/pS, with ABC-2-type permease (ABC-2.P) exhibiting strong positive selection (pN/pS = 2.74) (Fig. 4d). Our analysis revealed that genes associated with environmental adaptation are under greater positive selection pressure than those involved in core energy metabolism.

Heterologous expression of the ANME-1 hicAB operons suggest their bona fide functional and regulatory role in population size control

The hicAB operon was found in all cold seep-derived ANME-1, -2 and -3 clades analyzed in this study (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 29a), and in publicly available genomes from diverse habitats, including hydrothermal vents (ANME-1c) and terrestrial systems (e.g., ANME-1b), though occurrence remained minimal in ANME-1c (Supplementary Fig. 19a). The hicAB operon was also absent in analyzed genomes of the closest relative of ANME-1c (Alkanophaga and Syntrophoarchaeia) (Supplementary Data 19). hicA and hicB genes are widely distributed among our cold seep prokaryotes, detected in 45 of 57 phyla. The highest copy numbers occurred in Halobacteriota, primarily in the ANME-1 and -2 groups in our dataset (Supplementary Fig. 29b). The 12 ANME-1 species-representative MAGs had an average of 3.6 hicA and 6.6 hicB copies. Our phylogenetic analysis indicates that the HicA proteins in ANME-1 are highly diverse and can be divided into 16 classes (Fig. 5a), with 36.2–88.9% amino acid similarity (Supplementary Fig. 30a). The ANME-1 HicA proteins have low similarity with the HicA protein from E. coli (Supplementary Fig. 30a) due to structural differences in the C-domain. 15 out of the 16 ANME-1 HicA protein classes have α3 helices in the C-domain, whereas the E. coli HicA protein (P76106.2) has α1 and α2 configurations (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 30b).

Aside from its high diversity, the hicAB operon presented also different arrangements in ANME-1 genomes. We find three types of hic arrangements: (1) hicAB- where hicA is upstream of hicB, (2) hicBA- where hicB is upstream of hicA and (3) orphan hicA- lacking the proximal hicB gene (Fig. 5c). Collinearity analysis shows that the different hicAB operons colocalize within a genomic region and are linked to other genes (purB, ABC-2.P, hdsM, and frhB) under positive selection (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 31). This syntenic association suggests that they can be co-regulated or experience shared evolutionary pressures.

We examined whether different ANME-1 hicAB operons encode bona fide TA pairs by expressing different hicAB operons in E. coli. We selected four hicAB operons with different hic arrangements in ANME-1 genomes. The hicA and hicB genes were generated using our in silico data, and their sequences were cloned into the plasmid vector pBAD under the araBAD promoter (induced by L-arabinose). The plasmid pBAD was expressed in E. coli K-12 arabinose auxotrophs grown in liquid and solid media (see Methods).

As shown in Fig. 5d,E. coli cultures expressing only the ANME-1 hicA gene exhibited decreased growth using both optical density (OD600) and colony-forming units (CFUs). E. coli cells overexpressing the hicA gene from the ANME-1 HPA-sbin557 MAG (hicA-16-HPA-sbin557 gene) displayed a lethal phenotype confirmed via the CFU assay (Fig. 5d). We note the ANME-1 hicA-16-HPA-sbin557 gene had the highest pN/pS value (highest positive selection; pN/pS = 1.95) among all hicA genes identified in our ANME-1 genomes, and substitutions on conserved amino acid residues of HicA-16-HPA-sbin557 did not influence its toxicity (Supplementary Fig. 32 and Supplementary Data 20). The expression of hicB in E. coli did not arrest cellular growth or vitality (Fig. 5d). Co-expression of hicB with hicA reversed the lethal phenotype (Fig. 5d; hicAB) and allowed normal growth of E. coli that previously exhibited suppressed growth when expressing only the hicA gene (Fig. 5d). We performed heterologous co-expression experiments pairing the HicA toxin with various HicB antitoxin homologs. Our results show that although multiple HicB homologs can neutralize HicA toxicity, HicB homologs with higher sequence similarity to the native partner exhibit stronger cross-protective activity (Supplementary Fig. 33).

The heterologous expression of ANME-1 hicAB operons in E. coli also demonstrates their role in enhancing plasmid stability and inheritance (Supplementary Fig. 34). The plasmid stabilization observed suggests a broader potential role for hicAB systems in ANME-1. Since homologous toxin-antitoxin systems are known to stabilize diverse mobile genetic elements38, including prophages39 and genomic islands40, we hypothesize that the hicAB operons located within structure variants of ANME-1 may similarly contribute to the stable maintenance of these horizontally acquired genomic regions.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the diversity, genomic differentiation and adaptive strategies of ANME-1 in Haima cold seep sediments using ultra-deep, short- and long-read metagenomic sequencing, including > 2000 × coverage of long-read metagenomes for the dominant ANME-1 strain HP11-cbin57, together with high-throughput single-amplified genomes. Population genetic metrics were applied to identify genetic loci underlying ANME-1 diversity, and heterologous expression experiments targeting loci under positive selection were conducted to validate bona fide mechanisms involved in mediating ANME-1 archaeal responses to environmental stresses in cold seep habitats.

We find that genetic variability exists between the different ANME-1 strains, and it may provide advantages that enhance their survival in cold seeps. The recovered ANME-1 MAGs give information on the micro-diversity and evolutionary trajectory of different ANME-1 species that are exposed to dynamic selection pressures in their native cold seep habitat2,4. A negative correlation between linkage disequilibrium and species abundance suggests that dominant ANME-1 species undergo more frequent genomic recombination than their low-abundance counterparts, potentially accelerating their genetic diversification and niche adaptation.

The strain-specific differences seem to support speciation, niche partitioning, advantages in the face of viral attacks, and other phenotypic characteristics21. Structural variations (SVs) are known to promote environmental adaptations and genetic diversity in microbes41. Analysis of SVs and other population genetic metrics indicates that transporters and defense systems are likely key drivers behind ANME-1 diversification. Notably, from the 94 transporter-related genes, the ABC-2-type permease has the highest pN/pS ratio (2.74; Fig. 4d), exemplifying ongoing adaptive diversification in ANME-1 genomes. Given the roles of transporters in detoxification, signaling, and nutrient uptake, positive selection on ABC transporters supports ANME-1 adaptation and persistence in cold seep environments. The high number of SVs detected on certain genes of methane metabolism (FwdBDE, hdrA and hdrD) may influence the directionality of methane metabolism, highlighting genomic loci that act as diversification hot spots mediating transitions between methane oxidation and methanogenesis9,42.

Apart from average D’, the speciation features examined here may follow non-linear relationships with ANME-1 population dynamics and can be influenced by horizontal gene transfer (including viruses and exogenous DNA inputs) as well as interspecific interactions, such as competition or syntrophic partnerships with bacteria. Prokaryotes use defense systems to maintain genomic stability against integration of exogenous DNA and to limit the propagation of mobile elements. Restriction-modification (R-M) systems are the most common defense systems in prokaryotes, providing defense against mobile genetic elements, including viruses and plasmids43. The evolutionary role of R-M systems shows that they shape pathways of gene flow between populations, by influencing horizontal gene transfer, and by contributing to prokaryotic diversity44,45. Adenine-specific DNA-methyltransferase and type I restriction enzyme (hsdRSM) are among our identified R-Ms that have structural variations (SVs) and high pN/pS values in our ANME-1 genomes (Fig. 4d). The other identified defense systems with high presence of SVs and high pN/pS values are toxin-antitoxin systems (TA) commonly found in prokaryotes. TA systems are versatile agents of natural selection in prokaryotes46. This notion, together with the fact that structural variations and high pN/pS occur in genes associated with R-Ms and TAs, suggests R-Ms and TAs contribute to the observed ANME-1 diversity in Haima cold seeps.

The different TA operons found in our data exhibit SV-mediated genome reorganization, including partial DNA deletions and insertions. The multiple copies of hicAB operons exhibit high diversity in both gene and protein sequences, probably because of the positive selection exerted on hic genes via DNA insertions and deletions in the gene loci. Our expression experiments in E. coli confirm that ANME-1 HicA proteins act as toxins, whereas ANME-1 HicB proteins act as cognate antitoxins. The strong phenotypic resilience of hicA may indicate either functional redundancy in the catalytic core of the HicA proteins or structural compensation mechanisms.

Toxin-antitoxin modules in prokaryotes can arrest the growth of the host and induce dormancy to avoid environmental stress47, and hicA from Sinorhizobium meliloti were proved degrade ribosomal RNA to regulate cell growth25. The different possible cell fates (cellular arrest, normal growth, lethality) provide an advantage for ANME-1 populations that live in dynamic cold seep environments exposed to fluctuating environmental stressors. As an example, although ANME-1 archaea rely primarily on methane oxidation for energy, the spatial and temporal variability in methane availability in the cold seeps2,48 raises important questions about their adaptive strategies under methane-fluctuating conditions. Our observation that ANME-1 encodes a greater number of hicA gene copies than other cold seep microbe suggests that this toxin-antitoxin system may contribute to population-level stability during periods of methane fluctuation by modulating growth and survival under energy-limiting conditions. We propose that HicA-mediated growth restraint may serve as an energy-conserving adaptation, enhancing ANME-1 resilience in methane-limited conditions. In addition to other systems under positive selection (i.e., ABC transporters), diversification of defense systems may drive speciation of ANME-1 in the cold seeps.

Viruses and other mobile elements can control the abundance and community structure of deep-sea bacteria and archaea49–51. Competition between host defense systems and mobile elements creates selection pressures and can shape the genomes of hosts, a significant outcome of host-mobile DNA co-evolution52,53. Environmental parameters of cold seeps (e.g., redox potential, methane availability, presence/absence of syntrophic SRBs) may also act as pressures that shape the diversity of genes found under positive selection in ANME-1 genomes (transporters, defense and motility genes), a topic that will require further examination.

Methods

Sampling, DNA extraction, metagenomic library preparations and sequencing

We collected one sediment push core from the Haima cold seep in the South China Sea at 1435 m water depth with FaXian ROV (Fig. 1a) during the cold seep cruises in June-July 2022 (R/V KeXue). The intact sediment core was immediately sectioned into 2 or 3 cm sediment intervals to cover the total length of the core (~30 cm) (Supplementary Data 1). We collected four sediment layers: 0–2 cmbsf (P22B56A1), 4–6 cmbsf (P22B56A3), 21–24 cmbsf (P22B56A9) and 27–30 cmbsf (P22B56A11). The sediment samples were frozen at −80 °C until DNA extraction inshore. DNA from 20–50 g of sediment was extracted from each sediment layer using the PowerMax soil DNA isolation kit (MoBio, Carlsbad, CA, USA cat#12988-10), following the manufacturer’s instructions. We selected to extract up to 50 g of sediment per sediment depth, in order to recover >6 μg of DNA that ensures enough material for long-read PacBio sequencing and short-read metagenomics. DNA concentrations were measured using Qubit dsDNA HS assay kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and Qubit™ 4.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). We used 5 μg of genomic DNA to prepare PacBio sequencing libraries, and 200 ng for short-read sequencing libraries with an insertion size of 350 bp. Short-read sequencing libraries were generated using the VAHTS Universal DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V3 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China cat#ND607). Paired-End sequencing (PE 2 × 150) for short-read metagenomes was performed in NovaSeq 6000 Illumina platform. Long-read metagenomes were sequenced on PacBio Sequel IIe and PacBio Revio platforms. Both long-read and short-read metagenomes were sequenced by Biomarker Technologies company (Qingdao, China).

The distribution of ANME in cold seeps sediments increases with sediment depth4. To confirm that ANME in Haima cold seeps are more abundant below >20 cmbsf, and to ensure higher ANME genomic resolution, we decided a) to increase the sequencing effort in one of our two deeper samples (21–24 cmbsf) by using two PacBio sequencing platforms (see below), and b) to create both MAGs and single amplified genomes (SAGs) from other deep sample (27–30 cmbsf). The reason why we did not generate SAGs and MAGs from both deep samples or use two PacBio sequencing platforms as we did for sample (21–24 cmbsf) is cost- and time-related. Hence, for sediment sample (21–24 cmbsf) we used two cells on PacBio Sequel II platform and one cell on PacBio Revio, while the other three samples (0–2; 4–6; 27–30 cmbsf) were sequenced into three separate cells using the PacBio Sequel II platform. Long metagenomic reads from the total five cells used on the PacBio Sequel II platform displayed a mean length of 10.5 kb. Across the five cells, the sequencing yield ranged from 23.2 to 34.1 Gb high fidelity (HiFi) reads. The Revio HiFi reads produced a shorter mean length of 5.9 kb, but higher output with 61.8 Gb data (Supplementary Data 1).

Separation and sequencing single amplified genomes (SAGs)

SAGs were generated in our deepest cold seep sediment (27–30 cmbsf) by MobiDrop Co. Ltd. (China) using established protocols54 with appropriate modifications for recovering cells from sediment samples. In brief, to homogenize our sediment and release microorganisms bound in sediment particles, we washed ~5 grams of sediment with 20 mL of phosphate-buffered saline solution (0.01 M PBS, Sangon Biotech, China) followed by 10 min of vortex. After vortexing, the sediment sample was centrifuged at room temperature at low speed (500 g) for 5 min (Eppendorf Centrifuge 5910 Ri, Germany). The sample was filtered on ice manually through a 40 μm membrane filter (pluriSelect, Germany). The supernatant was transferred into a 0.22 μm ultrafiltration tube (MAPM02C67, Pall, USA) and received 20 mL of PBS. The ultrafiltration tube was centrifuged at room temperature at 500 × g for 5 min. These two steps (wash with 20 mL PBS and centrifuge) were performed twice. After the second wash, we collected the supernatant on 0.45 μm filter (MAPM45C67, Pall, USA), followed by centrifugation for 5 min at 7000 × g (Eppendorf Centrifuge 5910 Ri, Germany). We resuspended the recovered cells on the membrane with 2–3 mL of PBS and transferred them into 1.5 mL sterile tubes. The suspended cells were centrifuged at room temperature at low speed (500 × g) for 5 minutes (Eppendorf Centrifuge 5910 Ri, Germany). The supernatant was filtered with gravity through a 5 μm membrane filter (pluriSelect, Germany) to obtain the recovered cells in solution. The supernatant and cell suspension before each filtration step were thoroughly resuspended by gentle pipetting. Cells were stained using the LIVE/DEAD BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit (Thermo Fisher, USA) for cell counting, viability assessment, and evaluation of the homogenization process (Supplementary Fig. 35a–c). Individual cells were isolated from the suspended population via microfluidic sorting (MobiNova®-M1, MobiDrop, China) and subsequently processed through an automated workflow implementing: (1) oil-aqueous phase encapsulation for compartmentalization, (2) chemical lysis buffer treatment (prepGEM Bacteria, MicroGEM, UK), (3) whole-genome amplification using φ29 polymerase (30221-1, Lucigen, USA) that mediates multiple displacement amplification (Supplementary Fig. 35d, e), (4) genome tagmentation driven by Tn5 transposase (Vazyme, China), (5) dual-index barcoding using PCR and (6) microchamber-mediated pooling, all executed within the integrated microfluidic platform. The SAG libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 Illumina platform (PE 2 × 150 bp).

Assembly and binning of metagenome

Short-read metagenomic data underwent quality control using fastp55 (v.0.23.4, -q 20 -u 20 -l 50 -w 16 -5 -M 30 -g -D --dup_calc_accuracy 6 --detect_adapter_for_pe) to filter low-quality bases and trim adapters on raw reads. HiFi long-read data were processed through platform-specific workflows: PacBio Sequel IIe reads with CCS (v6.4.0), and Revio reads with Google DeepConsensus. Only reads that passed our cutoffs were kept for downstream analyses.

Short-reads metagenomes were assembled with Megahit56 (v.1.2.9, setting: --kmin-1pass --presets meta-large) and metaSPAdes57 (v.3.15.5, setting: -meta -k 21,33,55,77,99,127). Scaffolds < 2000 bp were removed before genome binning. Long-reads metagenomes were assembled with Canu58 (v.2.2, setting: -pacbio-hifi), metaFlye59 (v.2.9.3-b1797, setting: --meta --keep-haplotypes), hifiasm-meta60 (v.0.3-r063.2, default setting) and metaMDBG (v.0.3, default setting). Because the four samples in our study are from the same sediment push core, we co-assembled them to gain recovery of microbial species with low-abundance61. For both short- and long-read metagenomes, genome binning was performed on each assembly (four individual samples and one co-assembled sample) to improve binning performance. To distinguish whether assembled contigs are circular, we first check the result from the assembler. If the assembler did not detect circular contigs, we continued with manual curation to check whether the beginning and ending of the contig had overlaps (>20 nt). All long-read MAGs recovered in this study follow a consistent naming scheme that encodes their assembly software and depth provenance. Each MAG name consists of a three-character prefix: the first character indicates the assembler used (C for Canu, M for metaMDBG, F for Flye, H for hifiasm meta); the following two characters denote the push core depth layer (Fig. 1b) from which the MAG was recovered (P1:0-2 cmbsf, P3:4-6 cmbsf, P9:21-24 cmbsf, P11:27-30 cmbsf). For MAGs generated from a co-assembly of all four depth layers combined, the suffix PA is used.

To bin the genomes in our short-read metagenomes, we used MaxBin (v.2.2.6)62, MetaBAT (v.2.12.1)63, CONCOCT (v.1.0.0)64, which synergistically leveraged tetranucleotide frequency patterns and differential coverage profiles across samples to optimize contig clustering. We combined the raw genome bins resulting from the three binning approaches described above, and we selected the best binning result for each genome set using the bin_refinement module in MetaWRAP65. To bin the long-read MAGs, we used the HiFi-MAG-Pipeline (v.2.0.2)66. After the binning refinement process, we used CheckM2 (v.1.0.2)67 to evaluate the percentage of completeness and contamination for each bin. Good quality MAGs (≥70% completeness and ≤10% contamination) were retained for further analysis. We used dRep (v.3.4.2)68 to select the best representative genome for the species-level cluster and strain-level cluster. We dereplicated redundant MAGs at a species-level using dRep settings “-nc 0.3 -pa 0.90 -sa 0.95”, and at strain-level using dRep settings “-nc 0.60-pa 0.95 -sa 0.99”69. This resulted in 487 prokaryotic non-redundant MAGs at a species level and 692 non-redundant MAGs at a strain level from short- and long-read metagenomes. For the long-read MAGs, only those MAGs that were identified as circular by the assembler and met the following criteria were considered high-confidence circular MAGs as circular MAGs (cMAGs) in this study: single circularized contig with no gaps, completeness ≥90%, contamination ≤5%, the presence of essential genes (including 23S, 16S, and 5S rRNA genes, and more than 18 tRNA genes), and genome length >1 Mb (or >0.5 Mb for members of the Patescibacteria phylum).

Assembly and taxonomic assignment of SAGs

Raw sequencing reads from SAGs were processed using fastp55 (v.0.23.4) with default parameters to perform automated adapter trimming and removal of low-quality bases. Clean reads from each SAGs were assembled using SPAdes (v.3.15.5, setting: -sc)57. The completeness and contiguity (N50) of SAG dataset is significantly lower than MAG dataset (Supplementary Fig. 36), we exclude SAG as a reference genomes for subsequent population variant analysis.

To determine the taxonomy of the assembled SAGs, a reference dataset of MAGs in our study, previously annotated using the GTDB-Tk, was utilized. Taxonomic assignment was performed by linking SAGs to matching MAGs based on genomic similarity and read recruitment. Specifically, SAGs were assigned to a MAG-defined lineage if they satisfied two criteria: (1) an Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) greater than 95%, and (2) a read mapping rate greater than 50% to the reference MAG. ANI between the assembled SAG contigs and reference MAGs was computed using skani (v.0.3.0)70. Read mapping was performed using Minimap2 (-ax sr), and the resulting alignments were processed with Samtools to calculate read mapping rates. Only SAGs meeting the two thresholds were assigned the taxonomic classification of their corresponding MAGs. The “HP11-cbin57” species was the most abundant population within our SAG dataset, and the only taxon with sufficient SAG coverage for a meaningful intra-population diversity analysis.

Phylogenetic analyses

To assign taxonomy in our MAGs and SAGs, we utilized GTDB-Tk (v.2.3.0)71 based on the GTDB R214 that identifies universal and single-copy phylogenetic marker genes. To find universal and single-copy phylogenetic marker genes, we first concatenated the alignments of the conserved proteins (53 proteins for Archaea and 120 for Bacteria), and we treated missing proteins as alignment gaps. To align the protein sequences of the phylogenetic marker genes, we used Mafft72 (v.7.407) with two separate parameters: --maxiterate 1000, and --localpair. To refine the alignments, we applied trimAl73 (v.1.4); this eliminated poorly aligned regions. We constructed a maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenomic tree using the concatenated aligned protein sequences, using the IQ-TREE2 (v.2.2.6)74 with the following settings: -m MFP -asr -bb 1000 -nt AUTO. The analysis selected the best-fit substitution model (plant + F + R3) that was conducted with over 1000 replicates. For the phylogenetic analyses of the HicA proteins, we used the same method as described above. All phylogenetic trees were visualized with iTOL v775.

Assembly errors detection and manual curation of ANME-1 MAGs

We screened assembly artifacts for 66 ANME-1 long-read MAGs recovered in this study using two complementary approaches: the anvi-script-find-misassemblies pipeline76 within Anvi’o (v8-dev) and the GUNC (v.1.0.6)77 tool. These pipelines assessed assembly errors by detecting coverage clips (abrupt changes in coverage), zero-coverage regions, and higher taxonomic level chimerism via the clade separation score (CSS). For the detection of more nuanced inter-strain chimerism, we performed k-mer multiplicity analysis78 (https://myloasm-docs.github.io/qc/) of ANME-1 MAGs. Any contig longer than 1 Mb with an average k-mer multiplicity exceeding 1.05 was flagged as a potential intra-specific chimera resulting from the co-assembly of multiple strains. The k-mer multiplicity for all our ANME-1 long-read MAGs remained below 1.05 (Supplementary Data 21). To ensure the reliability of ANME-1 MAGs for downstream analyses, we applied a strict selection strategy for curation and MAGs filtration. First, we retained only those MAGs that met high-quality thresholds (≥90% completeness and ≤10% contamination) by CheckM2 (v.1.0.2)67. Qualified MAGs were selected for targeted manual MAG curation, while lower-quality genomes were excluded in downstream analysis.

To resolve the error-prone regions identified above, both Illumina paired-end reads and PacBio HiFi long-reads were mapped to the corresponding MAG contigs, and all coverage clips and zero-coverage regions were subjected to manual inspection in IGV (v.2.17.4)79. For each flagged locus, we evaluated read depth continuity and alignment consistency, regions lacking reads support or consisting of anomalous sequence patterns were considered likely mis-joined segments and were removed at the inferred breakpoints80. After corrections, Illumina paired-end reads, and PacBio HiFi long-reads were remapped stringently to confirm that curated regions no longer contained unsupported sequence blocks and that coverage and read alignments were coherent across the corrected junctions. For terminal zero-coverage regions located at contig ends, we tested whether the affected contig terminus could be resolved by re-pairing with another contig whose start region showed a sequence match to the terminal segment. Candidate contig pairs were identified based on end-to-end sequence similarity/overlap and further supported by the consistency of reads mapping at the proposed join. When a supported connection was evident, contigs were reconnected to eliminate the terminal zero-coverage segment, and the resulting sequence was validated by remapping reads to ensure continuous coverage.

Abundance of microbiome

To estimate the abundance of the microbial taxa present in the sediment microbiota, we first annotated both short- and long- metagenomic reads with Kaiju (v.1.10.1)81 using the NCBI-nr database, and then we calculated the relative abundance of each phylum using read ratios. The estimation of MAG relative abundance depends on the recruitment rates of quality-filtered short metagenomic reads. Clean reads were aligned to contigs using BWA-MEM (v.0.7.17)82 with default parameters. Sorting of the alignments was done using Samtools (v.1.19.2)83. Reads that exhibited an alignment identity ≥95% and a minimum alignment length of 50 nt to MAGs were classified as high-quality hits. The relative abundance of MAGs was estimated using CoverM (v.0.7.0) (https://github.com/wwood/CoverM) with the following parameters: --min-read-percent-identity 95 --min-read-aligned-percent 75 in genome mode. Stringent identity and alignment thresholds were applied to reduce the risk of spurious mappings and the overestimation of abundances.

Gene annotation

Proteins in each MAG were predicted using the prokaryotic genome annotation tool Prokka (v.1.14.6)84 and the annotation tool Prodigal (v.2.6.3)85 using single procedure mode. KEGG Orthologs R104 (KOs) were assigned to the predicted protein sequences using KofamScan (v.1.1.0)86 for pathway-based analysis. To identify novel proteins, we used eggNOG-mapper (v.2.0.0)87 against the eggNOG database (v.5.0.2) with default settings (-m diamond --seed_ortholog_evalue 1e-5). DIAMOND BLASTp88 (e-value < 1e-5, sequence identity >40%, and alignment length >40% of the shortest sequence) was also used against the NCBI non-redundant (nr) protein database. Provirus boundaries and genes in regions containing viral marker genes (e.g., phage tail protein genes) were identified using CheckV (v.1.0.3)89. Insertion sequence (IS) elements on genomes were detected using ISEScan (v1.7.2.3, -removeShortIS)90 and short IS elements filtration.

Structural variants detection

For the detection of SVs, including insertions, deletions, duplications and inversions, we used MUM&-Co software (v3.8)91. SVs with predicted region ≤50 bp were not retained. We counted DNA contractions (because DNA contractions can be tandem repeats) as DNA duplications following published pipelines92. Because the analyses of SVs require comparisons to a reference genome, we examined our MAGs for SVs using as reference genomes the MAGs that had in our study the highest completeness (MP9-3-cbin541, HPA-cbin593, HP11-cbin57, HP9-3-cbin343, HPA-cbin912, MP9-1-cbin22 and HPA-cbin293). Genes on the SV region were identified with MetaSVs92. This approach simplifies the analysis and ensures that all SVs from the same species can be accurately recovered during MAG alignment. To minimize the risk of false positives in SV detection, we excluded any SV events occurring within 10 base pairs at the beginning or the end of contigs in the MAGs. The normalized number of SV-associated genes for each species was calculated by dividing the total number of genes identified on SVs by the number of query strains. We also manually checked in our long-read metagenomes the sequencing depth of key SV regions to confirm the reliability of the results. Briefly, minimap2 (v. 2.26-r1175, -ax map-hifi)93 was used to align long reads to reference genomes to generate bam file. Samtools (v.1.19.2, setting: -q 30)83 were used to sort and filter low-quality mapping. The alignment result was visualized using IGV (v.2.17.4)79.

Microdiversity analysis of MAGs

Clean reads from each short-read metagenome were mapped to the best representative MAGs of each species using Bowtie2 (v2.5.3)94 with default settings. From these mappings, we calculated population statistics and nucleotide metrics such as linkage disequilibrium (D’), nucleotide diversity (single nucleotide variants per kilobase), the ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous mutations (pN/pS), and major allele frequency, using with default parameters the profile module in the inStrain program (v.1.8.0)95. Genetic annotation of the MAGs was conducted using Prodigal (v.2.6.3, -p single) using the gene module in the inStrain program. The criteria for identifying SNVs where: (a) SNVs to have at least 5× coverage in the positions, (b) to have a minimum of 5% frequency of variant base, and (c) the variant base reads to exceed the expected sequencing error rate of 1 × 10−6.

Collinearity analysis

For the genome-wide collinearity analysis of DNA sequences, we compared the alignments between genomes following the established pipeline23. Briefly, we used nucmer (v.4.0.0)96 to align entire genomes and mummerplot to visualize all alignments. To find collinear genes between genomes, we performed all-vs-all BLASTP (E < 1 × 10−5) that calculates similarity between genes. MCScanX (v.1.0.0)97 was used to find the collinear regions. Missing genes against the best reference genome recovered in our study were visualized with CGView98.

Expression experiments of hicA, hicB and hicAB genes in E. coli K-12 cells

The different plasmids vectors encoding ANME-1 hicA, hicB and hicAB genes expressed in E. coli K-12 Bw25113 cells are given in Supplementary Data 20. All experiments were performed at 37 °C in solid and liquid LB growth media (Luria-Bertani). The coding regions of hicA, hicB and hicAB were synthesized by Huayu Gene Company (Wuhan, China) using the recovered hicAB sequences from the ANME-1 MAGs (Supplementary Data 20). The sequences were cloned into pBAD plasmid vectors (V011896, NovoPro, China) at the XbaI/HindIII restriction sites using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (C112-01, Vazyme, Nanjing). pBAD plasmid vectors are induced by L-arabinose. The growth of the starting E. coli K-12 Bw25113 cultures carrying pBAD plasmids was determined by spectrophotometer (Agilent) at 600 nm (OD600) after the addition of 10 mM L-arabinose at OD600 0.1. To ensure reproducibility in our ANME-1 hic expression experiments, we performed four culturing experiments per expressed hic gene (n = 4), plus an experiment with an empty vector as a control (not encoding any hic genes). We note that the E. coli cells used for expression experiments do not encode in their genomes endogenous hicAB operon. For the colony forming unit (CFU) assays, E. coli cells were grown for 2 to 6 h under L-arabinose inductive conditions, and then were diluted in 0.85% NaCl (145 mM) from 10−1 to 10−7 via 10-fold sequential dilutions99. The dilutions were plated onto LB agar to determine cell viability99. The toxicity of the toxin (hicA), antitoxin (hicB) and their co-expressed genes (hicAB operon) were determined by streaking the bacterial broth from overnight cultures on an LB plate of ampicillin (100 ng/mL) with or without 10 mM L-arabinose as an inducer. We also used synthesized genes to assess whether hicA toxicity persists after introducing point mutations on amino acid residues, or truncations in the α3 helix in C-terminal. For strains with highly toxic toxin genes (lethal genes), 10 mM glucose can be added when necessary to inhibit the expression of toxin genes with the inducible arabad promoter.

Plasmid stabilization assay

To check the stability of the plasmid encoding the hicAB operon, we first performed expression experiments using the pUC19 plasmid vectors (6013, Takara, Japan) in LB media amended with ampicillin for selection. We utilized the pUC19 plasmid vector as it does not contain a strong/enhanced promoter, and therefore, does not overexpress the insert gene. The hicA and hicB genes were cloned in the pUC19 vector under their native promoters and were constitutively expressed. The E. coli K-12 BW25113 cultures carrying either empty vectors (pUC19; n = 2) or the cloned genes (pUC19-hicAB; n = 2) were grown overnight at 37 °C in LB medium amended with 100 ng/mL ampicillin. The overnight cultures were diluted in 1% of LB medium without antibiotics and then incubated at 37 °C. This process was repeated every 12 h for 5 days, and the plasmid maintenance rate was detected with CFU assays as described above.

Geochemical analyses

Porewater was collected from sediment samples by centrifuged at 6000 × g for 10 min and filtered using 0.22 µm membranes (Jin Teng, China). DIC concentrations and δ13C isotopic compositions were determined using a Gas Bench II system (CTC Analytics AG, Switzerland) connected to a Delta V Advantage isotope ratio mass spectrometer (IRMS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at the Institute of Deep-sea Science and Engineering (IDSSE). Porewater was collected from sediment samples by centrifuged at 6000 × g for 10 min and filtered using 0.22 µm membranes (Jin Teng, China). DIC concentrations and δ13C isotopic compositions were determined using a Gas Bench II system (CTC Analytics AG, Switzerland) connected to a Delta V Advantage isotope ratio mass spectrometer (IRMS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at the Institute of Deep-sea Science and Engineering (IDSSE). In brief, 12 mL headspace vials containing a predetermined volume of phosphoric acid were first purged with helium gas (flow rate of 80–100 mL/min) for approximately 10 min to eliminate atmospheric background. Subsequently, 0.2 mL of the porewater sample was injected into each purged vial. The vials were then heated to ensure complete, quantitative release of CO2 from the dissolved inorganic carbon. The released CO2 was transported via a helium carrier gas through an isothermal chromatographic column to isolate the CO2 prior to isotopic measurement. Analytical precision was ±0.15% for DIC concentration and ±0.2‰ for δ13C determinations.

For sulfate quantification, porewater samples were diluted 100-fold with ultrapure water to reduce high-salinity matrix effects on the chromatographic column and to ensure sulfate concentrations fell within the instrument’s linear calibration range. The porewater pH was adjusted to 7–9 to ensure optimal column performance and analytical accuracy. Diluted samples were analyzed using a Dionex Aquion AQ-1200 ion chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Chromatographic separation was performed on an IonPac AS19 anion-exchange column (4 × 250 mm, Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a matching guard column (IonPac AG19, 4 × 50 mm), with the column temperature maintained at 30 °C. The mobile phase was potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution, generated online by an eluent generator (EGC III KOH, Thermo Fisher Scientific), delivered at 1.0 mL/min. The injection volume was 25 μL. The system used a conductivity detector with the cell temperature set to 35 °C, and background suppression was achieved using an auto-suppressor (ASRS 300, 4 mm) operating at 100 mA.

The assembled MAGs and SAGs are publicly available via Figshare [10.6084/m9.figshare.32084286.v2].
