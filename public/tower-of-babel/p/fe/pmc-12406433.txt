Comparative analysis of dominant limb postural control in adults with and without fear of falling
Authors: Paul S. Sung, Dongchul Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFear of falling (FOF) is associated with an increased risk of functional decline; however, findings remain inconsistent regarding its effects on balance control mechanisms. The aim of our study was to compare dominant limb differences in postural control, as indicated by sway distances between the center of pressure (COP) and center of gravity (COG), in adults with and without FOF.MethodsTwenty-one subjects with FOF and 22 control subjects participated in three dominant and non-dominant limb standing tasks on force plates. The outcome measures included the separation sway distance (COP-COG) and the ranges of COP and COG sway during the tasks.ResultsThere was a significant interaction between groups, directions, and repeated trials for COP sway distance during non-dominant limb standing (F = 4.95, p = 0.03), particularly in the first trial in the mediolateral direction (t = -1.77, p = 0.04) and the third trial in the anteroposterior (AP) direction (t = -2.30, p = 0.02).ConclusionsThe FOF group exhibited increased sway distances and sway range asymmetries, particularly in the AP direction during the third trial of non-dominant limb standing. These findings emphasize the importance of neuromuscular trunk control, particularly in relation to increased COG and COP sway ranges observed for the non-dominant limb. Such understanding is crucial for assessing postural adaptations following repeated trials and mitigating fall risks among adults with FOF.

Background

Falls amongst older adults are common and are associated with significant consequences, including impairments in balance, gait, and functional mobility [1]. Fear of Falling (FOF) has been linked to imbalances that not only increase the risk of falls, but also contribute to a decline in overall functional ability that restricts participation in daily activities [2–5]. Assessing how FOF affects neuromuscular control during challenging balance tasks has crucial implications for clinical practice, particularly in designing interventions to alleviate FOF and improve balance.

A decline in postural stability has generated significant health and clinical problems, ranging from a loss of independence to premature mortality [6]. Several studies have linked the risk of falls in individuals with reduced fall efficacy and diminished self-perceived confidence [7–9]. The emergence of FOF is often correlated with a decreased willingness to engage in daily activities, including walking [10, 11]. Individuals with FOF often exhibit decreased trunk motion, muscular weakness, and an elevated risk of falls. While FOF is associated with balance deficits and altered postural control, there is a lack of understanding of sway ranges and postural control mechanisms for balance. The ability to maintain postural stability is essential for the prevention of fall-related injuries.

The importance of dynamic stabilization through the trunk has been reported when considering dominant lower limb control [12–14]. Since limb dominance determines balance strategies, there needs to be more in-depth research examining postural stability during repeated dominant and non-dominant limb standing trials. One-leg standing tests serve as a cost-effective measure for evaluating postural stability by observing the body’s ability to maintain its center of gravity (COG), which is a key part of staying balanced while standing and moving [7, 15]. Studies indicated that postural control during one-leg standing is clinically essential to reduce fall injuries and to measure postural stability. The test measures the body’s ability to maintain balance within the base of support, since balance deficits have been associated with performance in daily activities. Moreover, recent research has emphasized the importance of considering limb dominance, which can influence postural stability [14, 16]. Studies have further explored how limb dominance affects one-leg standing performance and may shape the balance control strategies employed during such tasks.

Research suggests that older adults have more difficulty maintaining balance when standing on their non-dominant leg compared to their dominant leg [13, 17]. The dominant leg is typically the one a person would use to kick a ball, while the non-dominant leg is used more for support. Previous results suggest that limb dominance affects biomechanical and neuromuscular control strategies during unilateral standing [13, 18–20]. The acceptability of self-control and the applicability of increasing sensitivity on dominant limb standing might be critical. Dynamic standing balance strategies must be considered to determine the effect of one-leg standing on asymmetry and its relationship to laterality for balance deficits [18, 21]. Given the importance of dominance limb control, further research on postural stability during dominant and non-dominant limb standing trials is needed to address clinical challenges related to falls.

Although reactive postural strategies are essential for fall prevention, protective control mechanisms, such as maintaining a stable COG relative to the center of pressure (COP), are equally critical during both standing and the initiation of gait [22]. While proactive strategies can effectively reduce fall risk, existing research suggests they may not be sufficient on their own, highlighting the influence of additional modifiable risk factors [21–24]. To better conceptualize these mechanisms, the fear-avoidance model has been proposed. Originally developed to describe the cycle of chronic pain and disuse, the model also provides a useful framework for understanding how FOF leads to protective behaviors and altered motor control, potentially compromising postural stability. According to this model, individuals with FOF may adopt stiffened or constrained movement patterns due to anxiety about falling, which impairs proactive postural adjustments and results in greater sway variability [25]. In addition, the cognitive-behavioral model has proven to be a valuable framework for understanding how FOF affects both movement planning and sensorimotor control during postural tasks [26, 27]. Their findings suggest that elevated fear can lead to maladaptive motor planning strategies that increase sway and instability. Incorporating such models helps contextualize how FOF leads to changes in central motor control mechanisms, particularly during challenging tasks like unilateral standing. Despite these theoretical frameworks, there remains a lack of empirical research specifically examining how FOF influences sway control during repeated unilateral standing tasks, especially in relation to limb dominance. Therefore, our study seeks to fill that gap by investigating differences in COP-COG separation and sway ranges between adults with and without FOF, with attention to both dominant and non-dominant limb performance.

Previous research has explored adaptation effects on trajectory and sway ranges of the COG in both anteroposterior (AP) and mediolateral (ML) directions that affect postural balance [28–30]. These results represented a combination of disturbance and postural control reactions when individuals were engaged in a balance task to measure postural sway. Despite growing evidence that FOF impairs balance, few studies have examined limb-specific postural adaptations across repeated one-leg standing trials. This study investigates whether the non-dominant limb provides a more sensitive condition to detect sway variability and instability among older adults with FOF. Therefore, our study was conducted to investigate the differences in the sway range of COP and COG in AP and ML directions and to analyze the distances of COP and COG when considering limb dominance among adults with and without FOF. It was hypothesized that the FOF group would demonstrate increased sway ranges in the AP and ML directions following repeated non-dominant limb standing trials compared to the control group.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from the community via both online and offline advertisements. Those who met the study’s inclusion criteria were informed about the study, and informed consent was obtained from all individuals. The study adhered to the guidelines of the Institutional Review Board (1653.21). Eligibility criteria included ages between 60 and 75 years, right limb dominant, absence of limb pain for at least three months prior to data collection, no serious pathology (such as nerve root compromise) at the time of data collection, and no conditions preventing standing. Anthropometric variations in age and body mass index (BMI) were considered during recruitment of the control group to ensure group comparability. The dominant side of the upper and lower limbs was identified as a confounding factor. A previous study showed that limb dominance can significantly influence neuromuscular control, postural alignment, and balance responses during unilateral tasks [13].

Experimental procedures

Upon arrival at the laboratory, subjects completed individual health status questionnaires, which included demographic data. They were also assessed for FOF using the modified Falls Efficacy Scale (FES). This scale, an extended version of the original, comprises a 14-activity questionnaire designed to measure perceived self-efficacy in avoiding falls during essential, non-risky daily living activities. The FES is a valid and reliable tool for assessing FOF, with strong psychometric properties demonstrated across diverse older adults [31, 32].

Subjects were instructed to remove their footwear and to stand barefoot on the platform with their medial malleolus aligned with the horizontal guideline and the calcaneus with the AP guideline to ensure a COG. The ankle joint was carefully aligned with the transverse rotational axis and the lateral aspect of the calcaneus. In this setup, AP movements were represented along the y-axis of the platform, while ML movements took place along the x-axis. The dual force plates aligned with the x-axis and served as the transverse axis for the ankle joint, establishing a reference point for calculating sway angles. Positioning on the balance platform is shown in Fig. 1. Subjects were then given standardized test protocol instructions to demonstrate balance performance during one-leg standing tasks. Subjects stood on the Bertec Balance Advantage® system for Computerized Dynamic Posturography with Immersion Virtual Reality (CDP-IVR) with the feet positioned comfortably apart (Fig. 1). The CDP-IVR allowed for the measurement of balance performance and the monitoring of postural stability improvements. A full-body safety harness was loosely fitted and suspended from an overhead support; it did not exert any pressure or restrict movement during testing and was only activated in the event of an actual loss of balance to prevent a fall. The harness was not weight-bearing during trials and did not apply pressure on participants unless triggered by a loss of balance.

The one-leg standing test assessed postural stability using the Bertec Balance System®. It consisted of two conditions: eyes open with the dominant limb and eyes open with the non-dominant limb, presented in random order. Subjects were instructed to maintain stability on the dominant foot for 10 seconds, as programmed into the balance plate device, followed by a 10-second rest interval between trials. This exact duration was set to ensure replication of the study. Subjects began each trial in a relaxed standing position with their eyes open. Upon receiving a signal, they then assumed an upright posture on the force plate, flexing the contralateral hip and knee to approximately 30 degrees. Subjects kept their arms at their sides during initial standing, but compensatory arm movements were permitted to control dynamic standing balance.

Ground reaction force (GRF: Fx, Fy, and Fz) was recorded using a calibrated Bertec force plate at a sampling frequency of 1,000 Hz. The manufacturer calibrated the force plate, and a sensitivity matrix was provided to convert the voltages to forces and torques. The data were collected from the unloaded platform to determine the zero offset, and the balance changes imposed during one-leg stance balance tasks were utilized. All kinetic data were filtered using a fourth-order low-pass Butterworth filter with a 20 Hz cutoff frequency, and normalization was performed based on individual body weight.

All subjects were able to stand the amount of time requested successfully, and the total standing time was determined until the flexed leg touched the force plate during the test protocol. During one-leg standing, the body’s orientation was such that it was vertically aligned with the mathematical COG situated over the weight-bearing surface of the supporting foot. Regarding the kinetic data, the COP and COG sway ranges for the ML and AP directions were compared. Each parameter on the repeated trials was separately analyzed for both lower limbs (dominant and non-dominant). Only measurements with three valid test trials per subject were included. The three repeated trials were analyzed for each X (ML direction) and Y (AP direction).

The COP position in this context was determined using vertical ground-reaction forces measured by the Bertec force plates. The COG position was then estimated based on these COP measures along with the subjects’ anthropometric characteristics [33]. To calculate the COG position, a moving average finite-duration impulse response filter was applied to the COP data, considering the movement around the ankle as an inverted pendulum. This approach helps in understanding the biomechanics of balance and postural control, especially in the context of assessing stability and fall risk. It is important to note that our study focused on analyzing postural sway, not the magnitude of the GRF. In this context, COG represents the point at which the body’s mass is evenly distributed, allowing for optimal balance. The force acting on the COG is mathematically defined as COG force (N) = body weight (kg) × acceleration due to gravity (g), as referenced in prior research [34].

Mean and standard deviation of sway ranges were analyzed using linear measures root mean square and ranges (max - min) for the AP and ML directions [35, 36]. The difference (COP-COG) represented the instantaneous distance between the position of the COP and the COG, independent of the effect of body weight. COP, indicating the point of application of the GRF vector, described the organization of posture. During dominant and non-dominant limb standing, the force on the platform was calculated based on the coefficient of elasticity of the spring and the vertical distance measured by a sensor. The analogue signals were AD-converted and sampled by a computer at a rate of 100 Hz. Statistical analysis

Preliminary power analyses were conducted based on the pilot data comparing groups, under the assumption of setting the type I error rate at 0.05. In our study, the sample size calculation determined that a minimum of 20 subjects would provide 80% power to detect an effect size of 0.4. The cutoff points of the effect size were 0.2, 0.4, and 0.8, which were considered small, medium, and large, respectively [37]. The effect sizes were analyzed by partial Eta-squared values (η2p) within repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) squared (small ≥ 0.01, medium ≥ 0.06, large ≥ 0.14), which was used to indicate the mean difference between groups. The independent variables included groups (with and without FOF).

To explore the differences in individual characteristics between groups, an independent t-test was employed. A mixed repeated measure ANOVA was used to scrutinize any main and/or interaction effects on the sway distance and COP and COG sway ranges, while considering limb dominance and sway directions. The general linear model was applied to assess all continuous dependent variables with the foundational design involving a by-group factorial experiment. For multiple comparisons, post-hoc analysis utilizing the Bonferroni test was conducted to compare the differences.

Demographic factors were used as covariates if a group difference was revealed. It is critical to consider these individual characteristics for dynamic standing balance strategies. Without controlling those confounding factors, the outcome measures could result in limited generalizability in older adults with FOF. All statistical analyses were executed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). The significance level for all statistical tests was maintained at 0.05.

Discussion

Clinicians often apply therapeutic interventions based on neuromuscular control related to COP-COG sway distance as well as COP and COG sway range changes during one-leg standing tests. Our study compared the differences in sway distances (COP-COG) and COP and COG sway ranges following repeated one-leg standing trials while considering limb dominance in adults with and without FOF. Our findings suggested no significant group interaction based on dominance, direction, and repetition. However, the results considering non-dominant limb standing indicated that the FOF group demonstrated significantly increased sway distance differences and COP and COG sway ranges in the first trial in the ML direction and the third trial in the AP direction.

Balance deficits may provide clinical insights to enhance rehabilitative strategies when considering limb dominance and direction of sway ranges following repeated trials. However, the adaptation effects following repeated trials have yet to be carefully investigated. It is assumed that the distance between the COP and the COG reflects the relationship of the balance control mechanism during one-leg standing [38]. By establishing these parameters, our study not only seeks to resolve conflicting reports in the existing literature, but also aims to inform clinical practice by providing a comprehensive understanding of the influence of FOF on postural stability during repeated dominant and non-dominant limb standing trials.

In a clinical context, functional stability, especially in older adults with sagittal imbalance, highlights the need for targeted interventions focusing on sagittal alignment to enhance compensatory mechanisms [39, 40]. Older adults with sagittal imbalance typically show reduced trunk proprioception and postural stability [41]. This imbalance is often associated with decreased proprioception and stability in the trunk, leading to impaired control over the COG. Moreover, anticipatory postural adjustments in older adults involving coordinated muscle activities during one-leg standing are crucial for minimizing postural sway distance between COP and COG on the supporting limb to prevent falls [42]. Such deficits can result in gait disturbances, and clinical strategies should emphasize anticipatory postural adjustments, including coordinated muscle activities in tasks like one-leg standing, to minimize sway and reduce the risk of falls [43, 44].

We hypothesized that the FOF group would demonstrate increased sway ranges in the AP and ML directions following repeated non-dominant limb standing trials compared to the control group. This hypothesis was partially validated, as significant interactions were observed regarding directions and repeated trials for sway distance and COP and COG sway ranges. These group interactions were particularly significant during non-dominant limb standing in the third trial in the AP direction. Despite no interactions on direction and trials between groups and for the dominant limb, the FOF group exhibited controlled sway distances and ranges, particularly on the non-dominant limb.

Our study found significant group interactions for non-dominant limb standing across different directions and trials. However, the first trial in the ML direction and the third trial in the AP direction showed significant differences. These results suggest that enhancing neuromuscular control, particularly in the AP direction during the third trial of non-dominant limb standing, is crucial for optimizing postural sway. The influence of limb dominance on functional stability and mobility presents challenges in postural tasks, as seen in the FOF group’s potential struggles with somatosensory and visual feedback. This aligns with previous research highlighting the impact of limb preference on motor development and performance asymmetries [45]. Moreover, dynamic balance sway, as indicated by shifts in the COG, provides valuable insights into an individual’s stability limits and the complex sensory, motor, and central adaptations involved [46, 47].

The COP mechanisms contributed to corrections of the COG acceleration after the perturbations, while the counter-rotation mechanism counteracted the COG acceleration during treadmill walking [48]. Although their study focused on treadmill walking, their findings align with our results in showing that anticipatory adjustments and COP-COG mechanisms play a key role in stabilizing forward motion after perturbation [49, 50]. However, their results were only indicated in the initial phases of the first step after the perturbation. Understanding the mechanisms used in repeated standing trials may have implications for the strategies used in clinical interventions to minimize fall incidence. Postural stability has been assessed using posturography systems based on a force plate measurement of the vertical-ground reaction force and computing the COP and the COG over the base of support [51, 52].

In clinical practice, limb dominance has been considered for the different movement components of motor learning. The compensatory movement pattern should be expected to maximize confidence in dynamic standing balance to minimize sway distance to avoid fall injuries. Lower limb laterality, for example, is associated with different activation characteristics in the primary sensorimotor cortex and the basal ganglia [53]. If differences in sensorimotor control are responsible for limb dominance, one would expect to observe differences in postural control from one-leg standing tasks between the preferred and nonpreferred limbs. Our results included significant differences in sway distances, particularly in non-dominant limb standing, indicating that limb dominance plays a crucial role in postural stability. This suggests that a greater understanding of neuromuscular control in the non-dominant limb is essential for addressing balance deficits in individuals with FOF.

Although the dominant limb is typically stronger, the non-dominant limb often demands greater cognitive and neuromuscular effort for stabilization. Research supports that dominant limbs typically demonstrate more stable motor control, while non-dominant limbs may be more sensitive indicators of balance deficits due to comparatively lower neuromuscular efficiency [13, 54]. This asymmetry is particularly valuable for identifying subtle impairments that might remain undetected during dominant limb assessments. Our findings demonstrated that repeated trials on the non-dominant limb reveal increased sway variability associated with FOF, which may inform targeted rehabilitation strategies.

Our study acknowledges several limitations that may have influenced the results, including fatigue accumulation, trial duration, anthropometric variations, and inconsistencies in one-leg standing tasks. Although there were no significant group differences in BMI or age, anthropometric matching was considered to ensure balance-related outcomes were not confounded by known influences such as body mass or limb length [35]. Despite subjects’ similar ages and BMI characteristics, factors such as COG shifts may impact standing behaviors, potentially resulting in diverse strategies such as hip strategy. The study indicated the importance of exploring motor learning to gain deeper insights into neuromuscular control. Clinicians should consider incorporating non-dominant limb stance into balance assessments to improve sensitivity in detecting fall risk. Targeted training on the non-dominant limb may enhance neuromuscular control and sensory integration, promote motor adaptation, and reduce reliance on compensatory strategies.

Conclusion

Our study indicated the importance of testing postural control on the non-dominant limb to improve functional stability. Neuromuscular control significantly influenced postural stability in individuals with FOF, particularly during non-dominant limb standing. The role of limb dominance enhances our understanding of postural control mechanisms in developing effective fall prevention strategies.
