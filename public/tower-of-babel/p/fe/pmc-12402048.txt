Identification of key candidate genes regulating hundred-grain weight in the maize inbred line Ye107 from Southern China
Authors: Xiaoli Hong, Ranjan K Shaw, Fuyan Jiang, Jiachen Sun, Yaqi Bi, Xiao Wu, Tao Dai, Fengli Ye, Jiaguo Zhu, Tingting Guan, Chunxia Xu, Xingming Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractKey messageA potential candidate gene regulating maize hundred-grain weight was identified in the Ye107 inbred line through a genome-wide association study and QTL mapping, providing insights for high-yield maize breeding.AbstractHundred-grain weight (HGW) is a key agronomic trait that significantly affects maize yield. Identifying HGW-related candidate genes is crucial for genetic improvement of maize. To overcome the limitations of traditional association panels in dissecting complex traits and to improve detection power and accuracy, this study constructed a multi-parent population (MPP). The MPP, consisting of 482 F7 recombinant inbred lines (RILs), was developed by crossing three elite maize inbred lines (D39, R-2-1-1 and YML1218) with a backbone inbred line, Ye107. The study integrated two complementary approaches: genome-wide association study (GWAS) for fine-mapping and quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping, which is effective at detecting major-effect loci. Using these methods, 42 SNPs and 17 QTLs significantly associated with HGW were identified. Through phenotypic variation analysis, functional annotation, and qRT-PCR validation, five candidate genes, including Zm00001eb225640, Zm00001eb225650, Zm00002eb228310, Zm00004eb228320, and Zm00001eb378820 were identified. Among them, Zm00001eb378820, which encodes the transcription factor GRF13 (GRFTF13), was identified as a key candidate gene regulating HGW in maize. Genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs) indicated that population pop3 (YML1218 × Ye107) exhibited higher values than other subpopulations, suggesting its potential for high-yield breeding. These findings offer critical genetic targets for increasing maize yield and highlight the advantage of integrating MPPs with multiple genetic analysis strategies to dissect complex quantitative traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00299-025-03595-7.Keywords: Maize, Hundred-grain weight, GWAS, QTL mapping, Candidate genes, GEBV

Introduction

As one of the most important food crops in the world, maize (Zea mays L.) plays a crucial role in maintaining food security, meeting the demand for industrial raw materials, and promoting the development of animal husbandry (Chen et al. 2017). According to the projections by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), global animal protein production must increase by more than 70% by 2050 to meet the demands of a growing population (Baldi and Gottardo 2017). The production of animal protein is heavily dependent on the supply of feed protein, with maize widely regarded as the "king of feed"—playing a pivotal role in the global feed supply system. However, factors such as population growth, environmental degradation, decreasing arable land, and climate change pose significant challenges. Hence, increasing maize yield on limited arable land has become an urgent priority (Huang et al. 2020). Maize yield is a complex trait regulated by multiple genes and is highly influenced by environmental factors, which contributes to its relatively low heritability (Ljubičić et al. 2023; Shi 2017). Maize yield comprises several components (Austin and Lee 1996), among which grain weight exhibits relatively high heritability and is considered one of the key determinants of yield potential (Li et al. 2014). Grain weight is influenced by both the rate and duration of dry matter accumulation during the grain filling period (Wang et al. 2021a, b). In recent years, molecular biology research has uncovered several key pathways involved in regulating grain size and weight in cereal crops. These pathways include the ubiquitin–proteasome system, G-protein signaling, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) proteins, post-transcriptional regulation of RNA, and the regulation of plant hormones (Yan et al. 2023). Grain weight is easy to measure and has stable phenotypic expression (Alvarez et al. 2014). Due to the fact that HGW is positively correlated with single kernel weight (Liu et al. 2017), HGW was selected in this study as a core trait for dissecting the genetic basis of maize yield. Commercially, HGW serves as a core component of yield and directly determines the economic return for farmers and seed companies (Sun et al. 2022). For stakeholders in the feed and animal husbandry industries, high HGW ensures a more stable and abundant supply, making high-yielding traits highly desirable (Baldi and Gottardo 2017). From a broader public perspective, increasing yield through traits like HGW plays a vital role in enhancing food security, especially with the global population growing, thus giving high-yielding varieties significant societal value (Shiferaw et al. 2011). Furthermore, grain weight is inherently correlated with grain size and shape (Sondarava et al. 2023). While this study focuses on the easily measurable HGW, it essentially investigates the complex genetic network that governs kernel size, shape, and density.

Quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping and genome-wide association analysis (GWAS) are two common methods used to identify genetic loci associated with grain size and weight (Li et al. 2019a, b). QTL mapping is used for analyzing complex networks between genes and traits, revealing gene interactions and environmental influences. According to the Maize GDB database (http://www.maizegdb.org/data-center/qtl-loci-sumary), more than 146 QTLs associated with grain weight have been identified, with the majority located on chromosomes 1, 2, 5, and 9. These QTLs are predominantly clustered in the bins 1.01–1.04, 1.06–1.11, 2.01–2.06, 2.08–2.09, 5.03–5.06, 9.01, 9.03, and 9.04 (Li et al. 2019a, b). Sharif et al. (2016) used maize recombinant inbred lines (Zheng 58 × SK) to identify 16 QTLs regulating maize grain traits across multiple environments, explaining over 10% of the phenotypic variation. Similarly, Pan et al. (2017) identified 62 QTLs associated with HGW per plant, ear weight, and grain weight across six environments. Sun et al. (2022) identified a QTL, qHGW3, spanning a region of approximately 40.66 kb. Another study identified a dominant QTL, qHKW1, which controls grain size and weight, encodes a CLV1/BAM-related receptor-like kinase protein and plays a crucial role in controlling maize yield (Yang et al. 2019). Compared with traditional QTL mapping, GWAS based on diverse populations offers a more efficient approach for dissecting the genetic architecture of complex agronomic traits (Xiao et al. 2021). In recent years, GWAS has been widely used in maize to explore the genetic basis of various traits, including disease resistance (Olukolu et al. 2016), leaf angle (Kuang et al. 2023), and grain-related traits (Liu et al. 2020). Conducting GWAS under different environmental conditions enables the identification of genes responsive to environmental changes, supporting the breeding of broadly adaptive cultivars (Wang et al. 2023). In studies of grain size and related traits, GWAS has identified numerous quantitative trait nucleotides (QTNs) (Dai 2021), and candidate genes have been functionally validated. For instance, Zeng et al. (2022) conducted GWAS on a panel of 291 maize inbred lines and identified 59 SNPs significantly associated with yield-related traits along with 66 candidate genes. Similarly, Yin et al. (2024) identified 244 candidate genes significantly associated with carotenoid-related traits using six recombinant inbred line populations. Additionally, Ndlovu et al. (2022) employed GWAS and genomic prediction in 410 tropical maize inbred lines under low-nitrogen stress, identifying 42 SNPs significantly associated with grain protein, starch, and oil content, and 51 putative candidate genes.

Although numerous genes and QTLs associated with HGW have been identified, providing important insights into the genetic basis of this trait, the proportion of phenotypic variation explained by these loci remains limited. Moreover, the increasing genetic homogenization of maize varieties has created a bottleneck for yield improvement, highlighting the urgent need to identify novel functional genes to broaden the genetic base for maize breeding. Previous studies on QTLs and candidate genes related to ear traits have largely focused on tropical germplasms, while research involving crosses between temperate and tropical or subtropical germplasm remains relatively scarce. This suggests that favorable alleles may still be hidden in these underexplored genetic backgrounds. To address this, a multi-parent population (MPP) with significant variation in HGW was developed using the elite inbred line Ye107 as the common female parent crossed with three other inbred lines. The four parents included one tropical and three temperate maize lines. The core strategy of this study was to leverage a MPP for genetic dissection, combining the advantages of traditional linkage analysis and association mapping. The MPP facilitates high statistical power for QTL mapping through abundant recombination events from controlled crosses among elite parents (Broman et al. 2019). On the other hand, the MPP introduces sufficient allelic variation to enable high-resolution fine-mapping by incorporating diverse parents from different heterotic groups and ecotypes. This design overcomes the challenges inherent in using standard diversity panels, where complex population structures can lead to spurious associations, and rare but beneficial alleles may have insufficient detection power (McMullen et al. 2009). The MPP approach addresses this limitation by "enriching" the frequency of valuable alleles from the selected parents. By simplifying population structure and combining the strengths of two mapping methodologies, the MPP provides a more powerful and precise platform for dissecting the genetic basis of HGW. The objectives of this study were to: (1) identify QTLs and SNP loci associated with HGW through QTL mapping and GWAS; (2) integrate QTL mapping and GWAS results to discover novel candidate genes for HGW; and (3) provide novel genetic resources and elucidate the molecular basis for improving maize yield.

Materials and methods

Parental lines and construction of multi-parent population

Based on extensive breeding practices, the research group conducting this study proposed a "three heterotic group" pattern consisting of Suwan1 × Reid, Suwan1 × Non-Reid, and Reid × Non-Reid crosses (Fan et al. 2013). Three elite inbred lines, D39 (Suwan1), R-2–1-1 (Reid) and YML1218 (Non-Reid) (Fig. 1) were selected as donor parents and crossed with the backbone inbred line Ye107 from the Reid heterotic group to generate three F1 hybrids. These F1 plants were self-pollinated for six consecutive generations through the single-seed descent method. Finally, a multi-parent population comprising 482 F7 recombinant inbred lines (RILs) was developed, comprising three RIL subpopulations: pop1 (D39 × Ye107), pop2 (R-2–1-1 × Ye107) and pop3 (YML1218 × Ye107). Pop1 contained 183 F7 RILs, while pop2 and pop3 consisted of 151 and 148 F7 RILs, respectively. The pedigree, heterotic group, and ecotypes of the four parental lines are provided in Table 1.

Field trials and phenotypic data collection

In this study, a completely randomized block design (RCBD) was used to evaluate HGW of 482 RILs of the multi-parent population, along with the four parental lines. Field trials were conducted in Yanshan County, Yunnan Province, China (104.27°E longitude, 23.61°N latitude) during 2021(YS21), 2022 (YS22) and 2023 (YS23), with three replicates to ensure the reliability and statistical robustness of the phenotypic data. Each F7RIL was cultivated in a single-row plot, with 14 plants per row. Each row was 4 m in length, with a row spacing of 0.70 m and an interplant spacing of 0.25 m. Standard agronomic practices were followed throughout the experiment. The HGW of each RIL was measured after the maize matured and was naturally air-dried. To obtain a representative sample of the plot and reduce sampling bias, one primary ear was harvested from five representative plants within each RIL plot to create a composite sample measurement. Since the performance of a single maize plant can be significantly influenced by micro-environmental variations within the plot, relying solely on a single-plant measurement would introduce substantial random error and may not accurately reflect the genetic potential of the line. Therefore, we selected five representative plants from each plot and collected their respective primary ears. By mixing the kernels from these five ears into a composite sample for measurement, the effects of these micro-environmental differences are effectively averaged out. This ensures that the measured hundred-grain weight value is a more stable and reliable representation of the RIL’s overall genetic performance within that replicate. The ears were threshed using a mechanical thresher, and the seeds from the five ears were thoroughly mixed. A sample of 100 mixed kernels was weighed using an electronic balance, and the average weight was recorded as the HGW (g) of the RIL (Liu et al. 2017).

After the preliminary processing of the phenotypic data collected over three years, the Anderson–Darling test was performed using Minitab 19 Statistical Software (USA, State College, Pennsylvania, Minitab, LLC) to assess the normality of the data. Descriptive statistics including the mean, standard deviation, variance, skewness, kurtosis, coefficient of variation were analyzed. The Pearson correlation was conducted in R software (V4.1.3) to evaluate the correlation of HGW across different populations and years. The “aov” function in R was used to perform one-way analysis of ANOVA and broad-sense heritability was estimated using the “lme4” package (Bates et al. 2014). DNA extraction and whole genome sequencing (WGS)

Genomic DNA was extracted from the leaves of maize seedlings of the RILs and parental lines using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method (Maroof 1994). The quality and quantity of the extracted DNA were measured using agarose gel electrophoresis and the Qubit DNA Assay Kit on a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA). After confirming that the DNA met the experimental requirements, the TruSeq Nano DNA HT library preparation kit was used to construct the genomic DNA library. The DNA was fragmented and ligated using T4 DNA ligase. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was then performed using primers complementary to the two adapters, and PCR products in the range of 300–350 bp were selected for library construction. Sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq platform using a 2 × 150 bp paired-end protocol. The sequencing reads were aligned to the maize reference genome B73 (RefGen_v5) using BWA software (Li and Durbin 2009), and the resulting alignment files were converted to BAM format using SAMtools (Li et al. 2009). To ensure data reliability, SNPs with a missing rate > 0.2 or a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.05 were filtered out (Bi et al. 2024). High-quality SNPs were then annotated using ANNOVAR (Wang et al. 2010) to determine their genomic distribution and mutation types.

Principal component analysis (PCA), phylogenetic tree, population structure and linkage disequilibrium analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using genome-wide SNPs in R v4.1.3. The analysis was conducted with the FactoMineR package, and the PCA results were visualized using the scatterplot3d package. To understand the genetic structure of the multiparent population, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using TreeBeST 1.9.2 software (https://github.com/Ensembl/treebest) based on the distance matrix calculated among the RILs.

Population structure was analyzed using the STRUCTURE software (Jonathan et al. 2000), and the results were visualized with the pophelper package in R. Different population numbers (K values) were tested, and cross-validated error was used to determine the optimal K value.

To analyze genome-wide linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay, PopLDdecay was used to calculate the pairwise LD among SNPs and determine the LD (r2) value corresponding to physical distance (Zhang et al. 2019). The relationship between physical distance and r2 was analyzed by fitting a curve to assess the rate of LD decay with increasing distance. The LD decay curve was generated using the Perl script Plot-OnePop.pl and visualized using the LD decay package in R. A critical r2 threshold of 0.1 was set to define the maximum physical distance for LD decay.

Genome-wide association analysis for HGW

Genotypic and phenotypic data of the RILs of the multi-parent population, evaluated across three environments, were used for GWAS. BLUP values were also used to identify SNPs significantly associated with the HGW. Prior to GWAS, low-quality SNPs were filtered out, and only high-quality SNPs were retained for analysis. GWAS was performed using the FarmCPU model implemented in GEMMA software (http://www.xzlab.org/software.html). The analysis followed the model described by Shim et al. (2015). In this approach, the first three principal components (PC1, PC2, and PC3) derived from PCA, along with kinship matrices, were included as covariates to control for population structure and reduce false positives. To effectively control false positives while accounting for LD among SNPs, we applied the -log₁₀(1/N) method (where N represents the total number of SNPs) to establish a significance threshold of -log₁₀(p) > 5.5. Although the Bonferroni correction represents the most stringent standard (Kaler et al. 2019), its conservative nature may lead to false negatives due to the non-independence of SNPs in LD. The adopted threshold is consistent with established maize GWAS studies (Wang et al. 2024), thereby achieving an optimal balance between statistical power and false positive control. The "ggplot2" package in R (V4.1.3) was used to generate Manhattan and Q-Q plots. The maize reference genome B73 RefGen_v5 was used to identify candidate genes regulating HGW that were located in proximity to the significant SNPs. As LD decayed at approximately 20 kb, candidate genes were identified by screening a 20 kb region upstream and downstream of significant SNPs. Subsequently, functional annotation databases were used to annotate and predict the functions of the candidate genes, and potential candidate genes regulating HGW were identified.

Construction of genetic linkage map and QTL mapping

Genome-wide high-quality SNPs were used to construct linkage maps for the three subpopulations. SNPs with high missing rates and showing segregation distortion were excluded from the analysis. Linkage analysis was performed using JoinMap 4.0 (Van et al. 2009), and SNPs were assigned to different linkage groups based on the recombination frequencies. The maximum likelihood method was used to sort the markers within each linkage group, and genetic distances (cM) were calculated using the Kosambi mapping function. Linkage groups were established using a LOD threshold of ≥ 2.5 and genetic maps for the three subpopulations were then constructed. QTL mapping for HGW was conducted using phenotypic data, following composite interval mapping (CIM) in MapQTL 6.0 followed (Van et al., 2009). QTLs with an LOD score ≥ 2.5 were considered significant. An appropriate LOD threshold was determined using 1000 random permutation tests at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05 (Delannoy 2008). The percentage of phenotypic variation explained by individual QTL (R2) was calculated as the square of the partial correlation coefficient, and the additive effects of each QTL were determined. Stable QTLs with significant effects were then selected.

Combined GWAS and QTL mapping and gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis

Based on the B73 reference genome, RefGen_v5, the physical locations of significant SNPs identified through GWAS were compared with QTL intervals to assess their potential overlap to confirm co-localization. Following the co-localization analysis and identification of candidate genes regulating HGW, gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed using the AgriGo tool (Du et al. 2010; https://www.agrigo.com/tools). Significantly enriched GO terms related to biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC) were identified using default parameters. The enrichment results were visualized using the bioinformatics online platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/login/).

Haplotype analysis and sequence variation in candidate genes

Candidate genes located in proximity to the significantly associated SNPs, consistently detected across multiple environments and in the BLUP analysis, were selected for haplotype analysis. Haplotype blocks in the target regions were constructed based on genotypic data, and the LD patterns were analyzed to assess their effects on HGW. The LD value was calculated using Haploview software (Barrett et al. 2005). By comparing the associations between different haplotypes and HGW phenotypes, superior haplotypes that significantly affect this trait were identified, revealing potential functional variants and their genetic effect. Based on the coding (CDS) sequence of the reference genome B73 RefGen_v5, nucleotide and amino acid sequence variations of key candidate genes across different parental lines were analyzed.

Analysis of candidate gene expression

To study the expression patterns of candidate genes during early maize seed development, this study combined the candidate genes identified through GWAS and QTL mapping. The FPKM values of these candidate genes in the B73 genotype (B73 RefGen_v5) were retrieved from the maize GDB database (https://qteller.maizegdb.org/results_B73v5.php). The expression data of candidate genes were obtained from dynamic transcriptome profiles of the B73 inbred line across early seed development (16–24 days after pollination, DAP), embryo development (16–24 DAP), and endosperm development (2–24 DAP). Data processing and visualization were performed by using Microsoft Excel 2016 and a bioinformatics online platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/login/). The expression patterns of the candidate genes were compared to explore their potential roles in seed development and their impact on HGW.

Comparative analysis of candidate gene expression in parental lines

Maize kernels from the four parental lines were collected at 15 days after pollination (DAP), 25 DAP, and at maturity. Total RNA was extracted using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China), and cDNA was synthesized with the FastKing RT Kit (with gDNase) (Tiangen, Beijing, China). qRT-PCR was performed on an Archimed R4 Real-Time Fluorescence Quantitative PCR System (AsnBio, Hangzhou, China) using the Tiangen SuperReal PreMix Plus (SYBR Green) reagent kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China), with maize actin as the internal reference gene. Gene-specific primers were designed using Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primerblast/), and primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2. All reactions were performed with three technical replicates. The qRT-PCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s and annealing/extension at 58 °C for 30 s. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001).

SNP effect and genomic estimated breeding value (GEBV) analysis

To assess the genetic effects of significant SNPs and predict the breeding potential of the recombinant inbred lines (RILs), this study employed the Genomic Prediction method based on genome-wide markers. This method used Genomic Estimated Breeding Values (GEBVs), which evaluated the genetic merit of individuals based on foundational work by Meuwissen et al. (2001). The analysis was executed as follows: first, the genomic relationship matrix (GRM) was constructed using PLINK software (Purcell et al. 2007). Next, GCTA software (Yang et al. 2011) was employed to estimate the additive and dominant effects of using the genomic restricted maximum likelihood (GREML) method. Dominance effects of SNPs were assessed by constructing a dominance GRM, while epistatic effects between significant SNP pairs were also analyzed within GCTA. Finally, based on the constructed GRM and corresponding phenotypic data, the GEBV of each RIL was predicted, allowing for a comparison of breeding values across the three subpopulations.

Results

Phenotypic analysis of HGW in the multi-parent population

The HGW phenotypes of the RILs were obtained by planting the multi-parent population in Yanshan County, Yunnan Province, China for three consecutive years (2021, 2022, and 2023). The descriptive statistical analysis of HGW for the three subpopulations is shown in Table 2 and Fig. 2. Across the three environments, pop2 exhibited the lowest average hundred-grain weight, while pop3 had the highest. Notably, pop3 showed the highest HGW in the YS23 environment. The coefficient of variation (CV) for the three subpopulations across the environments ranged from 0.19 to 0.24, indicating variability in hundred-grain weight among populations. The frequency distribution of HGW for the three subpopulations followed a normal distribution, suggesting that HGW in maize is a quantitative trait (Fig. 2b). The absolute values of skewness and kurtosis were both less than 1, indicating that the distribution of HGW was in accordance with normal distribution with minimal bias. The broad-sense heritability for pop1, pop2, and pop3 was 85.96%, 84.87%, and 91.59%, respectively, suggesting that HGW is primarily controlled by genetic factors and is less influenced by environmental variation.

SNP identification and distribution

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) identified a total of 194,963,454 genome-wide SNPs. To ensure quality, several filtering criteria were applied, and SNPs with a quality score (QUAL) ≤ 20, sequencing depth (DP) ≤ 3, missing rate > 20%, and minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.05 were removed. Finally, 6,360,332 high-quality SNPs were retained for further analysis. The SNPs were evenly distributed across the 10 chromosomes of maize (Fig. 4a). Chromosome 1 contained the highest number of SNPs (886,383), while chromosome 10 had the lowest (461,177). The filtered SNP dataset had a mean MAF of approximately 0.125(Fig. 4b) and a mean missing rate of about 0.175 (Fig. 4c), indicating high-quality SNP data suitable for subsequent GWAS.

Phylogenetic tree, PCA, population structure and LD decay analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that 482 RILs were grouped into three subpopulations (Fig. 5a), consistent with the experimental design of this study. Phylogenetic tree analysis showed that 482 RILs were clustered into three subgroups, with the majority of RILs from the same subpopulation grouping together (Fig. 5b). Population structure analysis also classified the 482 RILs into three subpopulations at K = 3 (Fig. 5c). An in-depth analysis of the background genetic composition reveals that the observed clustering aligns perfectly with the experimental design, wherein the three subpopulations correspond to the three founding cross combinations (pop1, pop2, and pop3). The "admixture" pattern observed across the subpopulations in the STRUCTURE plot arises primarily from the use of Ye107 as the common parent in all crosses. Since approximately 50% of the genetic background in each RIL originates from Ye107, the STRUCTURE algorithm detects this shared genetic component across all individuals, manifesting as genetic blending between the subpopulations. Despite this shared background, each subpopulation retains a dominant and distinct genetic component (represented by different colors in the plot), reflecting the unique genetic contributions from the other three donor parents (D39, R-2-1-1, and YML1218). This well-defined population structure, characterized by both clear clustering and a known shared background, validates the effectiveness of MPP design and establishes a robust foundation for effectively controlling for population stratification in the subsequent GWAS analyses. The observed admixture among the subpopulations may be attributed to gene flow, natural hybridization, or the use of a common male parent Ye107 during population development. Overall, the results of the phylogenetic analysis, PCA, and population structure analysis were consistent.

To assess LD decay in the multi-parent population, the chromosomal distribution of 6,360,332 SNPs was used to estimate LD (r2), which measures the degree of correlation between pairs of SNPs. As shown in Fig. 6, the r2 value gradually decreased with increased physical distance. Over short distances, the r2 value dropped rapidly, indicating a faster LD decay. GWAS for HGW and candidate gene identification

GWAS was performed using 329,755 SNPs in combination with the mean HGW values of the RILs across three environments to identify SNPs associated with HGW. Additionally, GWAS was performed using BLUP values of HGW. The FarmCPU model, implemented in the GEMMA software, was used to perform GWAS. The Q-Q plots indicated effective control of false positives, though a few false negatives were observed (Fig. 7a–d). At a significant threshold value of –log₁₀ (p) > 5.5, 42 SNPs were identified as significantly associated with HGW. These SNPs were distributed across chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, and 10, with the most significant association found on chromosomes 5 and 9 (Fig. 7a–d). The phenotypic variance explained (PVE) by these SNPs ranged from 0.37% to 8.91%. Construction of linkage map and QTL mapping

Genetic linkage maps were constructed for the three subpopulations (pop1, pop2 and pop3) using polymorphic SNPs identified between the respective parents of each subpopulation. The genetic map of pop1 was developed using 910 polymorphic SNPs, spanning a total genetic distance of 588.07 cM, with an average inter-marker distance of 0.65 cM. The genetic map of pop2 was constructed using 2,837 polymorphic SNPs, covering a total genetic distance of 2,487.83 cM, with an average inter-marker distance of 0.88 cM. The genetic map of pop3 was constructed using 2,400 SNPs and spanned 1,501.77 cM, with an average inter-marker genetic distance of 0.63 cM.

QTL mapping was performed for the three subpopulations across three environments and based on BLUP values to identify QTLs tightly linked to HGW. Using 1000 random permutation tests, the LOD threshold was set at 2.5 at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05. The QTL mapping revealed a total of 17 QTLs, three in pop1, eleven in pop2, and three in pop3, distributed across chromosomes 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 8, and 9 with LOD values ranging from 2.52 to 6.34 (Table 6). The phenotypic variation explained by these QTLs ranged from 5.73% to 13.93%. Notably, in pop2, three QTLs — qHGW1-1, qHGW1-2, and qHGW1-3 located— on chromosome 1 were consistently identified in the BLUP, YS22, and YS23 environments, respectively, indicating overlapping QTL intervals that could be considered stable. Similarly, in pop3, two QTLs, qHGW9-3 and qHGW9-4, located on chromosome 9 and identified for the BLUP values and YS21 environments, respectively, showed overlapping intervals (Table 6, Fig. 8).

Haplotype analysis of candidate genes and nucleotide and amino acid variation

The candidate gene Zm00001eb378820 was consistently identified through GWAS and QTL analyses across multiple environments and was found to be highly expressed in grain-related tissues. Consequently, Zm00001eb378820 was considered a key candidate gene regulating HGW in maize. To further explore its role, haplotype analysis was conducted to identify the superior haplotype associated with high HGW. The haplotype analysis of Zm00001eb378820 revealed four haplotypes: Hap1, Hap2, Hap3, and Hap4 (Table 7, Fig. 12b). Among the 482 recombinant inbred lines (RILs), the frequency distribution was 11.96% for Hap1, 0.21% for Hap2, 5.36% for Hap3, and 5.57% for Hap4. Hap2 exhibited exceptionally low frequency (0.21%), observed in only a single RIL. This prevents reliable statistical analysis of its effect on HGW, as a single observation lacks power for meaningful inference. Consequently, while Hap2 is included in our findings for completeness, we caution that any interpretation of its effect based on the data presented would be speculative. Validating the existence and functional role of this rare haplotype would necessitate future studies by screening larger, more diverse germplasm collections or developing specific populations to increase its frequency for rigorous statistical testing. Among the subpopulations, pop1 contained all four haplotypes, while pop2 contained Hap1 and Hap4, with Hap4 showing a frequency greater than 60% (Fig. 13a). In contrast, pop3 exhibited only the Hap1 haplotype (Fig. 13a). Notably, Hap1 was associated with significantly higher HGW than other haplotypes (Fig. 13b), suggesting that it may represent a favorable allele of Zm00001eb378820 with potential application in molecular breeding. For instance, when breeding other populations, introducing this novel haplotype may potentially increase the yield level of the new population. Conversely, Hap4 exhibited the lowest HGW. The haplotype distribution patterns in the subpopulations were consistent with their average HGW phenotypes, with pop3 showing the highest average HGW, while pop2 had the lowest. Therefore, Hap1 was considered the superior haplotype of Zm00001eb378820 for improving HGW in maize. Zm00001eb378820, spanning 3.017 Kb, was located 14.435 Kb downstream of the significant SNP 9–27,712,538 (Fig. 13c).

SNP effect analysis and genomic estimated breeding value (GEBV) estimation

In this study, additive effects, dominant effects, and epistatic interactions were analyzed for four SNPs (5–51,394,339; 5–64,069,455; 6–73,588,308; and 9–27,712,538) significantly associated with HGW. The results revealed that, compared to the T/G (5–51,394,339), A/G (5–64,069,455), and A/T (6–73,588,308) variants, the C/T variant at locus 9–27,712,538 exhibited stronger additive and dominant effects (Table 4, Fig. 14a), suggesting that the C/T allele is the favorable allele. Epistatic interactions among the four SNPs were analyzed, revealing that SNP 5–51,394,339 had significant interactions with SNP 6–73,588,308 and 5–64,069,445 (p < 0.05). The epistatic effect between SNPs 5–51,394,339 and 6–73,588,308 was 2.24, while the effect between SNPs 5–51,394,339 and 5–64,069,445 was − 2.05. These findings suggested the prevalence of gene–gene interactions among these SNPs, potentially leading to non-additive genetic effects, influencing HGW. Furthermore, estimation of the GEBVs for the three subpopulations revealed significant differences, with pop3 showing the highest mean GEBV, and pop2 the lowest (Fig. 14b). This indicated that pop3 has greater potential for genetic improvement. These results provided a valuable basis for further population and genotype selection.

Discussion

Comparative analysis of SNPs, QTLs and candidate genes related to HGW with previous studies

Comparative analysis of SNPs and QTLs related to HGW with previous studies

Through QTL mapping and GWAS analysis, this study identified 17 QTLs closely associated with HGW in maize and detected four significant SNPs that were consistently expressed across multiple environments. To enhance the reliability of these findings, an integrated analysis combining QTL mapping and GWAS was performed, revealing two SNPs co-located within three QTL regions. Further comparison of these significant SNPs and QTLs with previously reported loci uncovered several key genomic regions. On chromosome 2, QTL qHGW2-2 (176,416,946–193,070,938 bp) overlapped with the HGW-associated SNP2-186,957,636 reported by Wang et al. (2024). On chromosome 5, the significant SNPs identified in this study (5–51,394,339, 5–64,069,455, and 5–73,588,308) were found co-located within the 50–75 Mb region, previously described as a grain weight hotspot by Yan et al. (2023), suggesting that this region may harbor important genetic determinants of kernel weight. Additionally, on chromosome 9, SNP9-27,712,538 co-located within two QTLs regulating HGW reported by Liu et al. (2017), respectively the QTL (24.49–55.52 Mb) identified in the KUI × B77 RIL population and the QTL (24.96–76.77 Mb) identified in the DE3 × BY815 RIL population. Gong et al. (2023) reported that overexpression of ZmKL9 (105,145,216–105,147,364 bp) can increase HGW in maize hybrids. Fei et al. (2022) also identified a QTL for kernel row number, qKRN9.1 (97.6–111.8 Mb), which partially overlaps with qHGW9-1 (107,287,635–117,890,572 bp) detected in this study. These overlapping regions between our findings and previous studies suggest that the identified SNPs and QTLs may represent novel candidate loci for HGW, providing valuable insights for further investigation into the genetic mechanisms underlying grain weight in maize.

Comparative analysis of candidate genes with previous studies

The candidate gene Zm00001eb378820 is located on chromosome 9 in the B73 RefGen_v5 reference genome, spanning genomic coordinates 27,725,473 to 27,731,489 bp. It encodes a growth-regulating factor (GRF) family protein and has been designated as GRFTF13. Interestingly, Zhu et al. (2023) functionally characterized this gene using the B73 RefGen_v4 maize reference genome, where it was annotated as GRF6, likely due to differences or updates in genome annotation versions. Transcriptomic and translational analyses across various maize tissues and developmental stages confirmed that ZmGRF6 plays a role in growth regulation. ZmGRF6 contains conserved QLQ and WRC domains and can regulate genes associated with phytohormones such as auxin, gibberellins (GA), and abscisic acid (ABA). Previous studies have shown that nearly all known plant hormones are involved in regulating grain size and weight in cereal crops (Yan et al. 2023). Additionally, Qin et al. (2022) reported a gene encoding GRFTF13 (Zm00001eb052070) located in the 250–300 Mb region of chromosome 1, which was found to be highly expressed in seeds. Therefore, we speculated that Zm00001eb378820 may influence HGW in maize by regulating the expression of key genes in plant hormone signaling pathways. The genes Zm00001eb228310 and Zm00001eb228320 have not been directly reported in previous studies, but their corresponding gene numbers in the maize reference genome B73 RefGen_v4 have been mentioned (Wang et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2021a, b).

Functional annotation of candidate genes

Functional analysis of key candidate gene Zm00001eb378820

Zm00001eb378820 encodes GRFTF13, a class of plant-specific proteins. Liu et al. (2022) reported that some GRF protein family members promote leaf growth, accelerate grain filling, and increase grain size and weight. However, different members of the GRF protein family have distinct functions and mechanisms of action. For example, overexpression of ZmGRF10 in maize resulted in decreased leaf length and plant height, while also affecting cell proliferation, but it did not have a significant effect on yield-related traits (Wu et al. 2014). Li et al. (2022) found that the GRF action factor GIF1 could regulate the branching and size of the maize ear inflorescence meristem. Other studies have shown that miR396 negatively regulates ZmGRF4, which plays an important role in both leaf and grain development of maize. ZmGRF4, ZmGRF9, ZmGRF12, ZmGRF14, and ZmGIF2 are related to the ABA response of maize and can affect its growth and development (Qin et al. 2022). The GRF family has also been extensively studied in rice and Arabidopsis. AtGRF5 stimulates chloroplast division and increases the number of chloroplasts in cells, maintaining a higher rate of photosynthesis (Vercruyssen 2015). Yan et al. (2023) confirmed through CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing that OsGRF4 plays an important role in regulating grain size. Therefore, GRFTF13, encoded by Zm00001eb378820, may play a similar function in affecting maize kernel development. To further confirm this hypothesis, the qTeller tool was used to query the gene expression profile of Zm00001eb378820, revealing significantly higher expression of this gene in the meristems compared to other tissues. This suggested that GRFTF13, encoded by Zm00001eb378820, could affect the growth and development of maize grains, thereby affecting HGW.

Functional analysis of other candidate genes

Zm00001eb225640 encodes GTP dissociation inhibitors (GDI), an important class of proteins that regulate the activity of GTP-binding proteins. GTP-binding proteins are involved in various cellular processes, including growth, differentiation, and intracellular transport (Sano et al. 1995). In Arabidopsis thaliana, the RGS protein AtRGS1 regulates plant cell proliferation by accelerating GTP hydrolysis of the Gα subunit, suggesting that GDIs may influence plant growth and development by modulating this process (Chen et al. 2003). In rice, CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout of the OsGDI1 gene led to observable phenotypic changes. Compared to the wild type, the OsGDI1 mutant exhibited reduced plant height and produced longer, narrower grains (Shad et al. 2023). In maize, GTP-γS, a non-hydrolyzable GTP analog, was shown to inhibit ABA-induced GTPase activity, thereby supporting the role of GTP dissociation inhibitors (GDIs) in hormone signaling pathways (Irving et al. 1998). Based on these findings and functional annotations, it was hypothesized that the GDIs encoded by Zm00001eb225640 may influence maize grain growth and development, thereby affecting HGW.

Zm00001eb225650 encodes the M3A/M3B catalytic domain of peptidase and belongs to the peptidase M3 protein family. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that this gene is significantly enriched in metal ion binding and mitochondrial-related processes. Although the M3 protein family has been less studied in maize, its homologous gene AT5G51540 in Arabidopsis has been shown to function primarily as a mitochondrial ATP-independent protease (Peltier et al. 2004). Additionally, OOP and CyOP peptidases within the M3A family are involved in the degradation of oligopeptides in endosymbiotic organelles (such as mitochondria and chloroplasts) as well as in the cytoplasm. Oct1 peptidases, in particular, are involved in the maturation of mitochondrial preproteins, a function conserved across eukaryotes (Kmiec et al. 2016). Based on these findings, it was hypothesized that Zm00001eb225650 may have a similar function during maize growth and development, potentially influencing energy metabolism, which in turn could influence kernel development and HGW.

Zm00001eb228310 encodes Ataxia telangiectasia mutated protein (ATM) and RAD3-associated protein 1 (ATR1), both members of the PIKK protein family. ATM is primarily involved in responding to double-stranded DNA breaks (DSBs), while ATR responds to a broader range of DNA damage, including damage that interferes with DNA replication (Zou et al. 2007). The specific functions of ATR and ATM vary between Arabidopsis and maize. In Arabidopsis, ATR deficiency does not affect plants under controlled growth conditions, whereas in maize, ATM deficiency does not trigger meiosis defects. Additionally, ATR protein can protect genomic stability and prevent early endosperm cell cycle initiation and cell death during maize kernel development. Loss of ATR function results in kernel shrinkage, reduced protein and starch content, and random kernel abortion, though some of these effects are compensated by the function of ATM proteins (Pedroza et al. 2021). Relevant studies have shown that Zm00001eb228310 is involved in the growth and development of maize, particularly in the DNA damage response, especially in response to UV-B-induced DNA damage, which can sense and trigger cell cycle arrest (Wang et al. 2019). In maize growth and development, if the DNA damage response leads to cell cycle arrest, it may affect cell proliferation, indirectly impacting kernel size and development.

Zm00001eb228320 encodes the 127th transcription factor of the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) protein family. bHLH transcription factors recognize and bind specific DNA sequences to regulate downstream gene expression (Massari and Murre 2000). In Arabidopsis thaliana, the TT8-encoded BHLH transcription factor is involved in flavonoid biosynthesis in the seed coat and contributes to the post-zygotic barrier during seed development (Zumajo-Cardona et al. 2023). This suggests that bHLH transcription factors may influence seed structure and function by regulating specific metabolic pathways and cell differentiation processes. SPT, another member of the bHLH family, is a light-stable suppressor that regulates cell elongation and mediates seed germination (Penfield et al. 2005). Moreover, bHLH transcription factors participate in plant adaptive responses to abiotic stresses, such as drought, salinity, and cold (Qian et al. 2021; Zuo et al. 2023). Zm00001eb228320 is implicated in regulating seed germination lethality and is expressed in the apical meristem and the newly developed six leaf primordia in maize (Wang et al. 2021a, b), regions where active cell division drives new tissue and organ formation (Simon et al. 2001). This activity is crucial for organ development and growth. In rice, overexpression of the Rice Big Grain 1 (RBG1) gene enhances cell proliferation, auxin and stress protein accumulation, significantly increasing grain and organ size (Lo et al. 2020). Based on these functions, the bHLH transcription factor encoded by Zm00001eb228320 may play a key role in influencing HGW in maize by modulating metabolic pathways and stress response mechanisms.

Zm00001eb388530, located in proximity to SNP 9–108,046,020 identified through GWAS and overlapping the QTL interval qHGW9-1, encodes a pentapeptide repeating protein (PPR). Previous studies have demonstrated that PPR proteins play important roles in maize seed development. For instance, the PPR278 protein is involved in mitochondrial transcript splicing and RNA editing during maize seed development (Yang et al. 2022). Chen et al. (2018) systematically analyzed the relationship between the expression of PPR genes in grains and grain-related traits and found that four PPR genes were negatively correlated with HGW. Therefore, it can be inferred that Zm00001eb388530 may influence HGW in maize, although its effect might be relatively small.

Breeding value of Ye107 and genetic control of hundred-grain weight

HGW is a key indicator for evaluating maize yield and quality. The parental lines Ye107, D39, R-2-1-1, and YML1218 used in this study exhibited wide genetic variation and high breeding value. Ye107, a temperate elite maize inbred line developed in Southwest China, belongs to the Reid heterotic group and has demonstrated high combining ability in breeding programs (Yin et al. 2022). In addition to improving yield and disease resistance, Ye107 has been widely used in genetic analyses of various maize traits, contributing to the identification of novel candidate genes and serving as the male parent in multiple studies (Jiang et al. 2023). As the male parent, Ye107 has been used to develop elite hybrids in Southwest China, including Ludan 8, Yunrui 8, Yunrui 88, Yunrui 888, and Yunrui 519, which have been cultivated on over one million acres in the region. Additionally, D39, from the Suwan1 heterotic group, is a popular hybrid that was crossed with Ye107 to develop the superior hybrid Desan 5. Therefore, using Ye107 in maize breeding in Southwest China holds great significance for identifying candidate genes associated with hundred grain weight.

The MPP constructed in this study is notable for its exclusive use of elite germplasm rather than landraces or unadapted germplasm. While landraces are invaluable reservoirs for discovering novel and diverse alleles, this study prioritized an application-oriented approach aimed at identifying favorable alleles with immediate utility in modern maize breeding. The selected parental lines, including Ye107 and D39, are elite inbred lines that form the backbone of current commercial hybrid development. Consequently, favorable alleles and QTLs identified within such an 'elite-by-elite' population are already embedded in improved genetic backgrounds, minimizing the risk of "linkage drag" commonly associated with landraces-derived alleles. This enhances the efficiency of incorporating beneficial variations into new elite lines. The panel was designed to maximize genetic diversity by crossing parents from different heterotic groups (Reid, Suwan1, and Non-Reid) and ecotypes (temperate and tropical). Therefore, the significance of this panel lies in its breeding-centric design, enabling immediate use of its findings in high-yield breeding programs, offering a practical genetic roadmap to break through existing yield bottlenecks in elite germplasm.

In this study, pop3 was developed by crossing YML1218 with Ye107, which exhibited the highest GEBV. A candidate gene, Zm00001eb378820, was identified in this population, indicating that GEBV not only accurately reflects the performance differences in HGW across populations but also has practical value for guiding genetic improvement strategies. Furthermore, comparative analysis with other populations showed that pop3, with its high GEBV, exhibited more stable HGW performance under multi-environment conditions, further validating the predictive effectiveness of GEBV in breeding for complex traits. In future studies, hybridization experiments could be conducted to confirm the genetic potential of high-GEBV parents, providing a scientific basis for high-yield maize breeding. The heritability estimates for all three subpopulations were greater than 80%, indicating that HGW is strongly influenced by genetic factors. This further supports the value of this study in identifying candidate genes associated with HGW and provides a theoretical foundation for the future application of Ye107 in breeding superior maize varieties.

Significance of the combined GWAS and QTL mapping

This study is largely attributable to the strategy of synergistic use of GWAS and QTL mapping within a MPP. MPP was developed by crossing multiple parental lines with distinct genetic backgrounds, offers greater genetic diversity than traditional biparental populations. Such diversity enables a more comprehensive dissection of the genetic architecture underlying complex traits such as HGW (Broman et al. 2019). Moreover, the MPP design effectively bridges the gap between conventional QTL mapping and diversity-panel GWAS. It combines the high statistical power derived from abundant recombination events in controlled crosses while simultaneously introducing the allelic diversity necessary for fine-mapping. The integration of GWAS and QTL mapping provides deeper insights into the genetic architecture of complex traits. GWAS is effective at detecting genetic variants with small effects, whereas QTL mapping identifies loci with larger impacts on phenotypic variation. Combining these two approaches enables a more comprehensive understanding of the genetic basis underlying complex traits and offers critical support for elucidating the molecular mechanisms of crop phenotype formation (Huang et al. 2023; Gusev et al. 2016; Wu et al. 2019). Moreover, integrating significant SNPs identified by GWAS with QTL intervals improves the accuracy of candidate gene identification. In this study, the integration of GWAS and QTL mapping enables the identification of a co-localized candidate gene, Zm00001eb378820, associated with the regulation of HGW in maize. It is worth noting that although multiple HGW-related candidate genes were identified, their functional roles remain experimentally unvalidated. Future studies employing molecular biology approaches such as gene editing, transgenic analysis, and mutant characterization will be essential to fully elucidate the regulatory mechanisms through which these genes influence HGW in maize. Currently, the research group is conducting gene knockout verification work to further verify the impact of the candidate gene Zm00001eb378820 on HGW.

Haplotype-based breeding schema for improving HGW

The identification of the superior haplotype Hap1, strongly associated with high HGW, offers a direct target for improving maize yield through molecular breeding. Based on these findings, we propose a systematic, haplotype-based breeding strategy (Fig. 15) comprising the following steps:

A schematic breeding framework for improving HGW in maize, based on the superior haplotype Hap1 and integrating Marker-Assisted Selection (MAS) with Genomic Selection (GS)

Development of Functional Molecular Markers: Key SNPs defining the superior haplotype (Hap1) should be converted into breeder-friendly molecular markers, such as KASP (Kompetitive Allele-Specific PCR) markers. These markers must be robust, highly specific, and compatible with high-throughput screening to facilitate large-scale genotyping in breeding populations. Marker-Assisted Selection (MAS): These markers can enable rapid identification of Hap1 carriers in early-generation segregating populations (e.g., F2 or backcross populations). MAS accelerates breeding cycles and enhances selection precision by targeting seedlings carrying the favorable haplotype. Introgression and Pyramiding of Favorable Haplotypes: Hap1 can be introgressed into elite lines lacking it via marker-assisted backcrossing (MABC). Additionally, Hap1 may be pyramided with other favorable alleles controlling different important traits (e.g., disease resistance, stress tolerance) to develop an "ideotype" that combines multiple desired characteristics. Field Validation and Evaluation: Newly developed Hap1-containing lines should undergo extensive field trials across multiple locations and environments. This step is crucial to validate that the superior haplotype consistently confers a yield advantage in diverse genetic backgrounds and to confirm its commercial value before release. This haplotype-based breeding framework integrates genetic discoveries with advanced breeding technologies, providing a practical roadmap for the rapid and precise translation of valuable genetic resources into improved crop productivity.

Conclusions

In this study, the backbone inbred line Ye107 was crossed with D39, R-2–1-1, and YML1218 to construct a multi-parent population containing 482 F7 RILs. Through combined GWAS and QTL mapping studies, five candidate genes associated with HGW were identified, including Zm00001eb225640, Zm00001eb225650, Zm00001eb228310, Zm00001eb228320, and Zm00001eb378820. qRT-PCR analysis indicated that all five genes are involved, to varying degrees, in the regulation of HGW. Notably, Zm00001eb378820 is located 14.435 kb downstream of the significantly associated SNP 9–27,712,538, which overlaps with the QTL intervals qHGW9-3 and qHGW9-4 identified in population pop3, further supporting its potential role as a key regulatory gene. These findings provide valuable insights into the genetic regulation of HGW in maize and offer promising gene resources for molecular breeding aimed at improving grain weight.
