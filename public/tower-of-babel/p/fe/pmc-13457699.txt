Clinical Complications and Comorbidities Associated with Secondary Glaucoma: A Structured Literature Review
Authors: Anjali Deepak Patil, Adithi Vishwanath, Vijay Hari Karambelkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSecondary glaucoma is caused by underlying medical conditions, ocular injury, or drugs that increase intraocular pressure (IOP), causing damage to the optic nerve. It has a multifactorial etiology, making diagnosis and treatment complicated, and posing risks of irreversible loss of vision or blindness if left untreated.AimThe review assessed clinical complications and comorbidities of secondary glaucoma.Materials and methodsA literature review was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar, focusing on studies from the past decade. Included studies involved patients aged ≥40 years diagnosed with secondary glaucoma in tertiary care or ophthalmology centers. Data on clinical complications, surgical outcomes, visual impacts, and the influence of hypertension, diabetes, and treatment modalities were analyzed.ResultsPatients with secondary glaucoma were found to have a significantly higher incidence of comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes. Pseudoexfoliation syndrome was described as the most common secondary glaucoma. The visual acuity test results were variable for a given patients, and the mean IOP for neovascular glaucoma stood at 40.67 mm Hg. Cup-to-disk ratio varied for traumatic and uveitic glaucoma, indicating structural damage to the optic nerve. The male patients had higher prevalence rates.ConclusionEarly diagnosis and a multidisciplinary management strategy are essential for preventing complications of secondary glaucoma. Modern diagnostic modalities such as optical coherence tomography and visual field testing, along with targeted therapies, can improve clinical outcomes and minimize morbidity.ContributionThis review provides an overview of the complications associated with secondary glaucoma.

Introduction

Glaucoma is a chronic, progressive optic neuropathy that is characterized by degeneration of retinal ganglion cells and their axons. It leads to characteristic optic nerve head changes and progressive vision loss, with or without elevated intraocular pressure (IOP).1,2 Glaucoma affects approximately 76 million people and remains one of the leading causes of irreversible blindness worldwide. The burden is high in developing countries such as India, where nearly 23.5% of cases are glaucoma-related visual impairment.3,4 While primary glaucoma remains the key contributor, secondary glaucoma has been reported in as many as 0.3–21.84% of cases in India or the Indian subcontinent.5,6

The primary and secondary glaucomas are further subdivided into open-angle or angle-closure forms.7–9 While primary glaucoma involves idiopathic causes, secondary glaucoma is associated with identifiable preexisting or underlying ocular or systemic conditions that disrupt aqueous humor dynamics and elevates IOP.10 Common etiologies for secondary glaucoma include pseudoexfoliation and pigment dispersion syndromes, intraocular infection or inflammation, retinal vascular disease, blunt trauma, eye surgeries such as keratoplasty or congenital cataract surgery, neovascularization, uveitis, lens-induced issues (phacomorphic, phacolytic, lens particle, and phacoanaphylactic glaucoma), and corticosteroid use.10,11 Secondary glaucoma is a significant risk factor for acquired, irreversible blindness.12

Management of secondary glaucoma differs from that of primary glaucoma as the treatment must not only reduce IOP but also address the underlying condition. While medical treatment alone is insufficient, surgical management carries a higher risk and complications. Clinical complications of glaucoma treatment may include vision loss due to optic nerve fiber damage, postoperative inflammation and corneal edema, neovascular glaucoma, hyphema, and hypotony. Such complications are detrimental to daily activities and could lead to a reduced quality of life.13

Despite its clinical importance, there is variability in the presentation, and the associated comorbidities pose challenges in the diagnosis and management of secondary glaucoma. This review aims to dive into current evidence on the clinical complications and comorbidities associated with secondary glaucoma. The emphasis of this review is on presenting disease heterogeneity, outcomes, and clinical implications in secondary glaucoma.

Materials and Methods

Design

A narrative review was conducted using a structured literature search approach to summarize current evidence on the clinical complications and morbidity associated with secondary glaucoma. While this review did not perform a quantitative meta-analysis, it was designed to provide a comprehensive overview of disease etiology, clinical presentation, associated comorbidities, and outcomes. Efforts were made to ensure that the methods were transparent and reproducible.

Literature Search

A literature search was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar to identify studies published between August 2015 and July 2025 that addressed clinical complications and morbidity due to secondary glaucoma.

The search terms included a combination of clinical complications, secondary glaucoma, morbidity, vision loss, optic nerve damage, hyphema, surgical complications, corneal edema, and retinal detachment. A structured search strategy using Boolean operators (AND, OR) was applied. The search terms were expanded to improve sensitivity and included:

(“secondary glaucoma” OR “neovascular glaucoma” OR “uveitic glaucoma” OR “traumatic glaucoma” OR “steroid-induced glaucoma” OR “lens-induced glaucoma” OR “pseudoexfoliation glaucoma” OR “pigment dispersion glaucoma”) AND (“clinical complications” OR “morbidity” OR “vision loss” OR “optic nerve damage” OR “hyphema” OR “corneal edema” OR “surgical complications” OR “visual field defects”).

Reference lists of relevant articles were also manually screened to identify additional eligible studies.

Study Selection Process

Although this was not a systematic review, a structured approach was followed for study selection. All retrieved records were screened based on title and abstract, relevant full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and duplicate records were removed. Final inclusion was based on predefined eligibility criteria. Figure 1 presents the study selection process.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were manually selected based on their relevance to the topic of clinical complications and morbidity associated with secondary glaucoma. Prospective, retrospective, observational, and hospital-based cohort studies were included.

Exclusion criteria included case reports, review articles, and editorials. The search strategy was exhaustive in nature; however, non-English publications and article indexes other than the selected database were excluded, which could have implications. While the review articles were not included, they were used to contextualize findings where appropriate.

Data Extraction and Synthesis

A qualitative analysis summarized the results reported in the individual studies considered in the review. The primary themes focused on clinical complications and morbidity associated with secondary glaucoma. Where applicable, studies concerning numerical data on visual outcomes and complication rates were combined for descriptive analysis.

The study characteristics consisted of basic bibliographic details such as the authors' names, year of publication, and the study design. The review aimed to assess the prevalence of secondary glaucoma among patients from the inpatient as well as outpatient departments.

The demographics and clinical characteristics of the study group were examined to analyze the distribution of secondary glaucoma among various age groups, gender, and comorbidities. Various systemic comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and having both conditions, were observed among participants. Ocular comorbidities, such as the presence of glaucomatous optic nerve head changes and visual field defects in the patients, were documented.

Intervention approaches for secondary glaucoma included visual acuity recording, slit-lamp examination, fundoscopic examination (direct and indirect), gonioscopy, IOP measurement, visual field testing (perimetry), optical coherence tomography (OCT), and anterior segment OCT (AS-OCT).

The outcome measures evaluated IOP, cup-to-disk ratio (CDR), visual field defects (perimetry results), and vision status. This review investigated the prevalence of different types of secondary glaucoma depending on the age and gender of the participants.

Assessment of Study Quality

Due to significant variations observed across included studies, a quantitative analysis was not performed, and findings were synthesized qualitatively. Although this was not a systematic review and meta-analysis, methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed using appropriate tools. Statistical comparisons or correlations were not analyzed. Some included studies evaluated both primary and secondary glaucoma populations. In such cases, only the data specific to secondary glaucoma were extracted and interpreted.

Clinical Presentation and Visual Acuity Variation

Patients with secondary glaucoma exhibited a wide spectrum of visual impairment, ranging from light perception to moderate visual loss (Snellen visual acuity between 6/9 and 6/60). This variability reflects differences in disease severity at presentation and underscores the importance of early diagnosis and intervention.14,15,19,23

IOP and Disease Progression

The IOP levels varied widely across different subtypes of secondary glaucoma.23 Neovascular glaucoma had the highest mean IOP (40.67 mm Hg), while uveitis and secondary angle closure glaucoma showed the lowest average IOP values (approximately 28 mm Hg).14,15 The differences in IOP values across the various subtypes of secondary glaucoma reaffirm the need for etiology-based treatment decision-making.16

Optic Nerve Damage and CDR

There were variations in CDR among the studies, indicating different degrees of structural optic nerve damage.3 The CDR values for neovascular glaucoma and pigment dispersion syndrome were 0.65 and 0.71, respectively.15 Traumatic glaucoma had the highest CDR (0.75), and uveitic glaucoma showed a CDR of 0.74.14,23 Higher CDR values were reported in traumatic, uveitic, and pigment dispersion glaucoma, suggesting a strong association between underlying etiology and structural damage.

Impact of Comorbidities

Hypertension (31.11%) and diabetes mellitus (24.44%) were the most commonly reported comorbidities. The coexistence of both hypertension and diabetes (15.56%) was associated with worse clinical outcomes, highlighting the need for integrated ocular and systemic management approaches.17–21

Gender Differences in Secondary Glaucoma Prevalence

A higher prevalence was observed in males than females, with a male-to-female ratio of 2:1. This disparity might be due to systemic comorbidities, occupational causes such as ocular trauma, and lifestyle risk factors.14–16

Distribution and Types of Secondary Glaucoma

Pseudoexfoliation syndrome (PXF) was the most common type (31.11%), followed by phacomorphic glaucoma (17.78%) and steroid-induced glaucoma (13.33%). Although less prevalent (6.67%), neovascular glaucoma was associated with poor visual outcomes and more progressive disease.3,14,15,18–23

Visual Field Defects and Disease Subtypes

The visual field defects were highly variable among study participants and were predominantly seen in the forms of arcuate scotomas, central scotomas, and nasal steps. For example, neovascular glaucoma presented almost exclusively with nasal steps, while pigment dispersion syndrome was associated with both nasal steps and paracentral scotomas. Neovascular glaucoma mostly presents with vision loss and elevated IOP. A study identified impairment of vision and increased IOP as the most common clinical presentations in secondary glaucoma.15,23

Limitations

The majority of studies were from hospital or tertiary care centers with small sample sizes, potentially leading to selection bias and overrepresentation of severe or advanced cases. The exclusion of non-English publications may have introduced language and publication bias. Variability in study design and reporting limits the ability to perform quantitative synthesis. Furthermore, the absence of standardized risk-of-bias assessment tools across studies may affect the interpretability of findings. These limitations highlight the need for large, population-based studies to better define the risk stratification of secondary glaucoma. Despite these limitations, consistent patterns across studies strengthen the overall conclusions regarding the morbidity associated with secondary glaucoma.

Future Perspectives

Future management of secondary glaucoma involves advanced diagnostics, such as high-resolution optical coherence tomography and better visual field tests, enabling earlier detection and precise assessment of optic nerve damage. Integration of multidisciplinary care and development of therapies will greatly help manage secondary glaucoma-related morbidity.

Conclusion

Secondary glaucoma is a clinically heterogeneous condition associated with a significant risk of irreversible vision loss. The burden of complications varies according to underlying etiology, with neovascular, traumatic, and lens-induced mechanisms contributing to more severe disease. Systemic comorbidities, particularly hypertension and diabetes mellitus, play a critical role in disease progression and outcomes, highlighting the importance of a multidisciplinary approach to management. Early diagnosis, etiology-specific treatment, and careful monitoring are essential to minimize complications and preserve visual function. Future research should focus on large-scale, population-based studies and standardized reporting methods to better understand disease patterns and optimize management strategies.

Clinical Significance

This review underlines the clinical importance of secondary glaucoma as a major cause of irreversible blindness. The evidence from literature underscores the heterogeneity among secondary glaucoma subtypes, variations in IOP patterns, optic nerve damage, and visual field loss, all of which require individualized treatment strategies. The observations from this review highlight the need for a multidisciplinary approach that integrates both ocular and systemic care to improve patient outcomes and reduce the disease burden.
