The seventh annual meeting of the CLOTS expert group was held in Madrid, Spain, on December 9 and 10, 2022. The annual meeting brings together investigators from a clinical, engineering, and applied science background to discuss current challenges in acute ischemic stroke (AIS) endovascular therapy, with the common purpose of understanding and aiding resolution of these challenges through scientific research (Figure). The meeting format comprises a roundtable group discussion following sequential scientific contributions delivered as oral presentations; each attendee contributes a topic for discussion based on his/her area of expertise. The intent of the meeting is to have ample time within the group to debate and discuss the topics presented. Research topics are submitted on the basis of predetermined focus areas, which amounted to 5 different sessions at CLOTS 7.0, chosen by the meeting's steering committee:

Since the initial CLOTS meeting in Heidelberg, Germany, in 2015, the focus of each meeting has adapted in tandem with progress in the research field. Some general themes have persisted since inception, resulting in several collaborative research initiatives, with annual updates and future research directions being a tangible result of CLOTS. Many contributors have attended multiple meetings, ensuring continuation of collaborations. A list of attendees and location for each of the previous CLOTS meetings is provided as supplementary information to this article. Cerenovus (Irvine, CA; a member of the Johnson & Johnson family of companies) organizes and funds the meeting. Members of medical device Research and Development and Clinical Research from the company also contribute scientific presentations for discussion.

One persistent theme of CLOTS has been, as the name of the meeting implies, AIS clot research. This area of research has seen tremendous expansion since the large‐scale adoption of mechanical thrombectomy following the 5 pivotal trials reported in 2015. The collection and study of these clots has been a particular focus of CLOTS and has the EXCELLENT (Embotrap Extraction and Clot Evaluation and Lesion Evaluation for Neurothrombectomy, Cerenovus) and RESTORE (Registry of Clot Histology and Stroke Pathology) University of Galway, Ireland) registries as a direct output, as well as providing significant input to the STRIP (Stroke Thromboembolism Registry of Imaging and Pathology, Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN) and the registry of Prof Simon De Meyer (KU Leuven, Belgium). Another common theme of the meeting is the mechanistic research enabled by modeling mechanical thrombectomy on the bench. A preponderance of this research has been achieved using synthetic artery models, blood clot analogues, blood mimics, and pumps to simulate physiological conditions. However, other approaches involving preclinical and biological models are also commonly used. This year, a novel approach that uses a human placenta model through the venous side was introduced at the meeting. Another promising modeling approach, using theoretical models (such as computational fluid dynamics), is a less frequent, but growing area of research. In CLOTS 7.0, the use of theoretical models to understand pressure and flow characteristics during thrombectomy was presented, with this work being initially used in the context of aneurysm modeling; similarly, the use of in silico models to simulate stroke trials sparked debate on the feasibility of such an approach, where it could be practically applied, and whether this would be acceptable for regulatory bodies to complement or even replace larger clinical trials.

Presentation of procedural challenges relating to mechanical thrombectomy is always a fruitful source of debate at all CLOTS meetings. This topic, which is far ranging, from hard to retrieve clots, to underlying vascular disease, to difficulties in accessing the occlusion, can lead to many insights relating to new treatment approaches, best practices, and identification of unmet clinical needs in the field. Various preclinical studies aimed at understanding these challenges have been suggested and subsequently debated at CLOTS meetings. A review of the evidence for endothelial damage resulting in reocclusion at the site of the AIS occlusion, assessed through long‐term macroimaging at 1 center, was presented at CLOTS 7.0. The contributor concluded that irrespective of the approach used, contact aspiration or stent retriever, there was no evidence of procedural‐related reocclusion when cases with obvious intracranial atherosclerosis at the occlusion site were excluded. Another contribution conducted a review of algorithms dedicated to the evaluation of the cervical and intracranial vessels’ tortuosity and an automated algorithm of calcific burden of the intracranial vessels (like the calcium score in the coronary disease field). It was discussed that such an approach aimed at anticipating technical difficulties may help to improve access to occlusions and to tailor the access devices chosen.

The field of neurointervention has seen an expansion of treatment indications, additional eligible patient populations, and extension of vascular territories being treated. CLOTS has been to the fore in discussing these trends using scientific studies to understand possible new mechanistic and biological responses that may be uncovered in these new applications of the therapy. At CLOTS 7.0, there were robust discussions about medium‐vessel occlusion treatment and mechanichal thrombectomy in the context of underlying intracranial atherosclerosis disease, with 3 presentations on the former alone.

Introduction of medical devices with new features and technology to improve access and clot retrieval has been rapid and sustained in the past decade, with no indication that this pace of innovation will relent in the near to medium term. Evaluation of advancements in device design and the new trends established by their adoption is a common theme of discussion at CLOTS. At the meeting this year, the SPERO study (Post Marketing Study to Evaluate Neurothrombectomy Geometric Clot Extractor Effectiveness in Patients With Challenging Occlusions), describing early clinical experience with the Nimbus revascularization device (Cerenovus) for difficult clots was discussed, as well as the consequences of using aspiration catheters that match the size of the target vessel whether in the context of large‐vessel occlusion or medium‐vessel occlusion; an early assessment of a novel approach of coated stent retrievers that exploit neutrophil extracellular traps to improve clot retrieval and reduce emboli was also discussed.

Characterization of the occlusion via medical imaging was once again a common topic at CLOTS 7.0. Many attendees recognize the potential of establishing prognostic characteristics of the occlusion before intervention, although there is consensus that this information must be capable of rapid acquisition without adversely impacting time to treatment and procedural times. The use of histologic analysis of ex vivo AIS clots, to derive imaging markers for occlusion characteristics, remains an active area of research for many attendees of CLOTS. However, other markers, such as the shape of the proximal face of the occlusion on Digital Subtraction Angiography acquisition, were presented as another approach that would represent a quick method to characterize the occlusion and inform the best procedure. Apart from the occlusion itself, the vascular access route and methods of predicting challenging access were discussed as unmet needs that would enable selection of the best procedural approach if solved.

At the CLOTS 6.0 meeting in Miami, Florida, it was agreed to use the CLOTS 7.0 meeting to conduct a Delphi Consensus on the topic of challenging clots, with the meeting attendees suitably making up the expert panel. The goal is to establish whether and how challenging clots can be recognized and endovascular therapy approaches altered when challenging clots are encountered. An experienced organizer of Delphi Consensus methods led the organization and facilitation of a premeeting survey. Two live meeting sessions were held to share the initial round results and to conduct a second poll, followed by a discussion of its results on the first day of the meeting. Finally, during the second meeting day, participants were given the opportunity to reconsider their responses considering the previous day's discussions in a third, final round of questions. The analysis of the results and a manuscript draft were circulated shortly after the meeting to complete the exercise.
