Phase engineering of atomically thin magnetic chromium tellurides via molecular beam epitaxy
Authors: Guangyao Miao, Zongnan Zhang, Yichen Jin, Florian Kronast, Kai Hu, Pan Chen, Dongdong Xiao, José D Cojal González, Haojie Sun, Chenxi Meng, Wei Jiang, Yugui Yao, Qinlin Guo, Jiandi Zhang, Carlos-Andres Palma, Weihua Wang, Jürgen P Rabe, Jiandong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCrystal structure engineering is pivotal for tailoring material properties, particularly in atomically thin two-dimensional (2D) transition-metal chalcogenides that exhibit rich, crystal-phase-dependent electronic, optical, and magnetic properties. However, their crystal-phase-controlled synthesis remains a critical challenge that limits their practical applications. Here, we report the phase-controlled synthesis of atomically thin chromium telluride films, including CrTe3, CrTe2, Cr1+δTe2, and CrTe, on graphene/SiC substrates via precise tuning of molecular beam epitaxy parameters. The growth mechanism is further revealed. We have systematically characterized the different structures and electronic, chemical, and magnetic properties of these films using scanning tunneling microscopy/spectroscopy, scanning transmission electron microscopy, density functional theory calculations, X-ray photoelectron/absorption spectroscopy, and circular-polarized X-ray photoemission electron microscopy. Heterostructures of different chromium telluride phases are further fabricated, demonstrating band engineering enabled by phase control of chromium tellurides.Subject terms: Magnetic properties and materials; Surfaces, interfaces and thin films

Introduction

2D transition metal chalcogenides (TMCs) are among the most intensively studied 2D material families after graphene1. The atomically thin TMCs crystals exhibit unique properties distinct from their bulk counterparts, due to enhanced quantum confinement and reduced dimensionality effects2–8. The tunable physical properties under multiple external stimuli, including electrical and magnetic fields, strain, and light, endow atomically thin TMCs with significant potential for fundamental research and next-generation device explorations9–15. Atomically thin TMCs crystals have been prepared via exfoliation, chemical vapor deposition/transport, and molecular beam epitaxy (MBE). These methods demonstrate trade-offs in controllability, homogeneity, purity, and lateral size. MBE’s ultrahigh vacuum (UHV) environment minimizes surface contamination, enabling atom-level engineering of 2D materials. MBE can achieve large-area, phase-controlled growth of 2D atomically thin TMCs by accurately regulating composition, the strain introduced by substrate lattice mismatch, and growth thermodynamics and kinetics. This is ideal for the synthesis of atomically thin TMCs, as their properties are susceptible to stoichiometry deviations and crystal structure variations16–22.

As an emerging family of 2D binary magnetic materials, atomically thin chromium tellurides (CrxTey) have multiple bulk counterparts with different compositions23. Different phases with distinct chromium-to-tellurium ratios exhibit unique magnetic and electronic properties24–27. For instance, CrTe3 is a layered magnetic antiferromagnetic material28. The metastable CrTe2 features a 1 T structure, bulk/thick film ferromagnetism with a high Curie temperature, and tunable magnetic ordering at the monolayer limit29–37. Cr1+δTe2, the Cr-self-intercalated phases of CrTe2, exhibit robust ferromagnetism approaching the 2D limit, large perpendicular magnetic anisotropy, and serve as a platform for studying the giant anomalous Hall effect and magnetic skyrmions in bulk/thick films35,38–45. CrTe crystallizes in a non-van der Waals structure and exhibits high-Curie-temperature ferromagnetism in ultrathin films46–48. Their crystal structures are compared in Fig. 1a. Phase-controlled synthesis and characterization from the atomic-scale crystal structure to the micrometer-scale magnetic domains are necessary for further applications. Lasek et al. reported the successful MBE growth of monolayer (ML)-CrTe2 and its spontaneous evolution to intercalated Cr1+δTe2 from bilayers (BL) on MoS216. A similar structural transition from ML-CrTe2 to Cr1+δTe2 has been reported during the growth of CrTe2 on Si and on graphene/SiC by Yu et al., and Xian et al.29,49. The fabrication of the in-plane CrTe3/CrTe2 heterostructure was reported by Yao et al., and Li et al.28,50. The growth of bilayer (BL) CrTe2 without Cr intercalation has been reported to be limited in previous studies and was recently revealed in ref. 35. Despite these important advances, several critical issues remain unresolved. 1. The growth parameters, particularly the substrate temperature during growth, vary widely from 200 °C to 350 °C, even for the same CrTe2 phase across different reports. The growth of metastable non-VdW ML-CrTe with the NiAs crystal structure has yet to be realized. A conclusive phase map is still lacking. 2. The transition from pristine BL-CrTe2 to self-intercalated BL-Cr1+δTe2 remains poorly understood, and the role of substrate temperature in this transition remains unclear. 3. There are inconsistencies in the reported surface structure of different phases16,29,33,49. 4. The direct imaging of magnetic domains of atomically thin CrxTey induced by spontaneous magnetization remains elusive.

Here, we study the basic physical process in the MBE growth of atomically thin CrxTey films and report their phase engineering. We synthesize single-phase atomically thin CrTe3, CrTe2, Cr1+δTe2, and CrTe films and characterize their surface structures, electronic and chemical states for each phase using scanning tunneling microscopy/spectroscopy (STM/STS) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). We directly image the spontaneous magnetization of the ferromagnetic atomically thin Cr1+δTe2 and CrTe using synchrotron-based X-ray photoemission electron microscopy (XPEEM). The heterostructures of various phases are further constructed, by which the lateral/vertical heterojunction is constructed under control. Our research provides a reference for phase engineering of atomically thin transition-metal chalcogenides with diverse stoichiometries and facilitates foundational investigations and device innovations based on atomically thin chromium tellurides.

Results and discussion

The identification of different phases of chromium tellurides

Figure 1b–e show the STM images of ML-CrTe3, ML-CrTe2, BL-Cr1+δTe2, and ML-CrTe nanocrystals, respectively. The ML-CrTe3 and ML-CrTe2 islands show round shapes, while the BL-Cr1+δTe2 and ML-CrTe have hexagonal shapes and neat edges. The apparent heights of the CrxTey phases on graphene measured by STM (at a bias voltage of −2 V) are 1.01 nm for ML-CrTe3, 0.98 nm for ML-CrTe2, 1.60 nm for BL-Cr1+δTe2, and 1.28 nm for ML-CrTe, as displayed in Fig. 1f–i. The 0.98 nm-high phase is further confirmed as 1T ML-CrTe2 by cross-section scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) (Fig. S1a). Due to contributions from different electronic states between graphene and CrxTey films, the STM-measured apparent heights are larger than the structural heights and are sensitive to the bias voltage during scanning (Fig. S1b). Notably, ML-CrTe2, BL-Cr1+δTe2, and ML-CrTe exhibit distinctly different apparent heights across the entire range of experimental bias voltages. Although ML-CrTe3 exhibits an apparent height similar to ML-CrTe2, these two phases can be unambiguously distinguished by their fundamentally different surface lattice patterns (discussed below). The heights of the following steps can also be a characteristic for identifying different CrxTey phases, as shown in Fig. S2.

The atom-resolved STM images in Fig. 1j–q acquired at 78 K and 300 K show the surface structures of the different phases. Their DFT-simulated STM images are compared in Fig. S3. The STM images of ML-CrTe3 at 78 K and 300 K show a rectangular 2D unit cell with a = 1.11 nm and b = 1.13 nm, which is unique in these CrxTey phases and consistent with the previous report of CrTe328,50. The challenge for identifying the other three phases originates from their similar surface structures. Though both the ML-CrTe2 and BL-Cr1+δTe2 show a (2 × 1) surface structural modulation at 78 K, the ML-CrTe2 has a structural transition from rectangular at 78 K to triangular at 300 K, and thus the (2 × 1) structural modulation at 78 K disappeared at 300 K correspondingly. The structural transition of ML-CrTe2 is related to the zigzag antiferromagnetic ground state and the spin-lattice interaction, as discussed in several experimental and theoretical works35–37,51. In contrast, the structural modulation of BL-Cr1+δTe2 persists from 78 K to 300 K, which originates from the periodic Cr intercalation, as depicted in the structural model in Fig. 1a. This is supported by our density functional theory (DFT) simulation shown in Fig. S3 and is consistent with the previous reports on atomically thin Cr1+δTe2 films16,35,39,49. Unlike BL-Cr1+δTe2, the STM image of BL-CrTe2 shows a triangular (1 × 1) surface structure from 300 K to 5 K, indicating the absence of Cr-intercalation in the vdW gap as shown in Fig. S4. The ML-CrTe, with an apparent height of 1.28 nm, exhibits a triangular surface structure at 78 K and 300 K, consistent with the previous STM measurement on CrTe48. The different CrxTey phases can be identified by STM measurements of their characteristic heights on graphene, temperature-dependent surface atomic structure, and the subsequent step heights beyond the monolayer (summarized in Table S1).

For the phases with similar surface structure (CrTe2, Cr1+δTe2, and CrTe), we perform cross-section STEM experiments for cross-validation. As shown in Fig. 2, high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) STEM images and the intensity profiles along the black and red polylines clearly reveal the structural difference of these phases. In CrTe2, an obvious vdW gap is observed between the CrTe2 layers, as indicated in Fig. 2a, d. For Cr1+δTe2, there is no vdW gap, but a modulation of partially Cr-intercalation between two CrTe2 layers as shown in Fig. 2b, e. For CrTe, all the centers of the octahedron Te are occupied by Cr as shown in Fig. 2c, f. These observations are consistent with the side views of the crystal structures.

The MBE and phase engineering of chromium tellurides

In Fig. 3a, we sketch the speculated thermodynamic and kinetic processes involved in the MBE growth of chromium tellurides based on the experimental observations. In the deposition step, nucleation and migration compete with the decomposition and desorption of the chromium telluride, determining the chemical composition of the nucleated phases and the growth mode. The growth rate is determined by the evaporating flux of the high-temperature element, chromium. In the post-annealing step, the decomposition of chromium telluride, desorption of Te, and re-nucleation of chromium tellurides dominate. The phase-controlled MBE growth of CrxTey is achieved by finely tuning these epitaxy parameters. The MBE growth includes three steps: deposition of Te and Cr while holding the substrate at a specific temperature, post-annealing after deposition, and cooling the substrate. When the substrate temperature exceeds 150 °C, the deposited Te atoms will start to desorb from the surface as the sticking coefficient is nearly zero at this temperature52,53. Only Cr atoms and the nucleated CrxTey adsorb on the substrate. The higher the substrate temperature, the lower the surface Te concentration, which favors the formation of Cr-rich phases.

Figure 3b shows the growth parameters for the epitaxy of different phases of CrxTey, concluded from over 100 experiments. Here, we select an ultralow growth rate (several tens of minutes per layer, varying across different crystal phases) to finely tune the atomically thin chromium telluride phase. At a fixed flux ratio of Cr: Te around 1:30, the substrate temperature of 160–180 °C, 200–270 °C, 300–340 °C, and 400–450 °C favors the nucleation of CrTe3, CrTe2, Cr1+δTe2, and CrTe, respectively. The substrate temperature during deposition increases from the vdW to the non-vdW phase. It should be noted that the temperatures shown here are relative and should be adjusted based on the trend if a different Cr: Te flux ratio and growth rate are used, which may vary across different MBE conditions27. This is rationalized by the kinetic differences in the deposition step as the substrate temperature varies. The coexistence of the different phases is observed at temperatures between these critical points. For example, at 400 °C, which is in the crossover region for the nucleation of CrTe and Cr1+δTe2, both phases are observed in the STM image of the fabricated sample (Fig. 3c).

In addition to nucleation, the film growth mode is also influenced by the substrate temperature during growth. For example, the CrTe2 nucleates between 200 °C and 270 °C; in the first two layers, the relatively higher substrate temperature favors a layer-by-layer mode (Frank-van der Merwe mode), while a lower temperature favors a layer-plus-island mode (Stranski-Krastanov mode), as shown in Fig. 3d, e. It could be understood that with higher temperatures, the second-layer islands can overcome the Ehrlich–Schwoebel barrier (barrier hindering interlayer diffusion downward the steps, described by the effective additional energy required by an adatom to cross a descending step) and diffuse from the first-layer CrTe2 down to graphene and form 2D uniform layers54–56.

To synthesize large-scale, single-phase atomically thin CrxTey, the post-annealing process and the cooling rate should also be finely tuned. In Fig. 3f, we show a sample grown at 360 °C with a slow cooling rate (cooling down below 150 °C in 30 min, ~7 °C per minute) after deposition. In addition to the expected ML-CrTe and BL-Cr1+δTe2, small islands of ML-CrTe3 and ML-CrTe2 are also observed. The speculated mechanism is that during the deposition step of the Cr-rich phases (at a relatively higher substrate temperature), their nucleation rate exceeds their decomposition rate, whereas the Te-rich phases are in a balance between nucleation and decomposition. This balance is broken after closing the shutter of the Cr and Te sources, as the decomposition rate of Cr-rich phases is much higher than the nucleation rate immediately. A slow cooling rate generates a temperature gradient on the substrate, leading to the accumulation of Te and Cr atoms near the surface. Te-rich phases could also nucleate when the substrate cools to a specific temperature range. For the fabrication of single-phase CrxTey, we do not perform post-annealing in UHV at the growth temperature; instead, we cool the substrate immediately after deposition. Fast cooling could reduce the decomposition rate of CrxTey and lower the concentrations of Cr and Te atoms near the surface, thereby preventing the nucleation of Te-rich phases, which is especially important for the single-phase synthesis of Cr-rich phases. By fine-tuning the growth parameters as discussed previously, we synthesized single-phase atomically thin chromium telluride with different chemical compositions, as shown in the large-scale STM images in Fig. S5.

Furthermore, we reveal the phase transition of CrxTey by post-deposition annealing in UHV. The transition from CrTe3 to CrTe2 has been reported in previous works28,50. As shown in Fig. S6, the as-grown CrTe2 exhibits some cracks even after post-annealing in UHV, indicating a slow decomposition of the sample during the post-annealing. Here, we show the transition from the CrTe2 phase to the Cr1+δTe2 phase upon post-annealing in UHV, as shown in Fig. 3g. The coverage decreased from ~120% to ~59% while exposed step heights changed from 0.98 nm plus 0.62 nm to 1.60 nm after post-annealing, indicating decomposition of CrTe2 and re-nucleation of Cr1+δTe2. This post-annealed induced change in the metal-Te ratio has also been reported in other telluride films57,58.

The electronic, chemical, and magnetic states of different phases of chromium tellurides

The electronic states of different phases of CrxTey are investigated by our STS measurements, as shown in Fig. 4a. The ML-CrTe3 featured a semiconducting behavior with a peak-shaped electronic state at ~0.60 eV in the differential conductance (dI/dV) spectrum, similar to the previous report of ML-CrTe3 grown on highly oriented pyrolytic graphite, but with a different Fermi level due to the doping effect from the substrate49. The measured STS of ML-CrTe2 showed metallic behavior and a flat step from +0.15 eV to +1.04 eV, which agrees well with previous reports28,50. The dI/dV spectrum of BL-Cr1+δTe2 with periodic Cr intercalation in the vdW gap shows two prominent peaks at +0.31 eV and +0.68 eV related to the Cr-intercalation compared to the dI/dV spectra of ML-CrTe2 and BL-CrTe2 (Fig. S7). The dI/dV spectrum of ML-CrTe shows a noticeable two-shoulder feature at ~ −0.50 eV and −1.20 eV compared with other phases. These STS measurements provide another characteristic to identify these phases. To clarify the origin of the above spectral features, we performed DFT calculations to investigate the electronic structure of ML-CrTe3, ML-CrTe2, BL-Cr1+δTe2, and ML-CrTe, and provided atom-resolved projected density of states (PDOS) for Cr and Te elements (Fig. 4b). Generally, the calculated band structure and PDOS reproduce the experimental spectral features. Our calculation indicates that ML-CrTe3 has an AFM ground state and a band gap of ~0.44 eV. Notably, the peak at ~0.60 eV observed in Fig. 4a can be attributed to a partially flat band near the conduction band minimum (CBM) of CrTe3, due to its unique quasi-1D spin configuration. In ML-CrTe2, the PDOS of Cr and Te are generally the same in the energy window of 0 to 1 eV, evidencing the strong hybridization between Cr 3 d and Te 5p orbitals, and the enhanced electronic states above 1 eV are mostly contributed by Cr atoms. In comparison, for the BL-Cr1+δTe2, there are two distinct peaks at ~0.40 eV and ~0.70 eV in addition to that of ML-CrTe2, which are mainly contributed by Cr atoms, possibly from the periodic intercalated Cr atoms in the van der Waals gap. For ML-CrTe, the calculated PDOS shows two similar shoulder features in the experiments near −0.70 eV and −1.20 eV, which mainly originates from Cr atoms. By combining surface morphology, apparent height on graphene, surface atomic structure, and dI/dV spectra, we provide STM fingerprints of the different phases of chromium telluride.

To compare the chemical states of different phases of chromium telluride, we conduct XPS and XPEEM measurements. Figure 4c compares the XPS data for the Te 3d and Cr 2p binding energies across different phases. The Cr 2p peaks overlap with the Te 3d peaks in all phases and behave as a shoulder feature. The binding energies of the Te 3d peaks in the metallic phases ML-CrTe, BL-Cr1+δTe2, and ML-CrTe2 are pretty close. This arises from covalent bonding between Cr and Te atoms and the close Cr/Te ratios in these phases at the 2D limit. The Cr: Te stoichiometry is 1:2 in CrTe2, 1:1.6 in BL-Cr1+δTe2, and 1:1.5 in ML-CrTe. As shown in ref. 59, the Cr 2p/Te 3d overlapped peaks of the bulk Cr-Te system exhibit a blue shift with increasing Cr/Te ratio. Our XPS data for these phases show similar behavior (black dashed lines in Fig. 4c). The existence of part Te-Te self-bonding in few-layer CrTe3 (FL-CrTe3), together with the semiconducting nature, results in a higher binding energy of Te 3d and Cr 2p of CrTe3 than that of these metallic phases. To compare the evolution of the intensity (area) ratio of the Cr 2p/Te 3d peaks across the different phases, we fit the XPS spectra using the received data, including constrained full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the same peaks and the Cr 2p3/2 to the shoulder’s position (Tables S2 and S3 for the detailed fitting parameters). Note that XPS is not suitable for accurate absolute stoichiometry quantification here, mainly due to the severe spectral overlap between Cr 2p and Te 3d peaks. The area ratio of Cr 2p3/2/Te 3d5/2 increases as their chemical composition evolves from Te-rich to Cr-rich phases (Fig. S8), providing evidence for a trend in the relative Cr/Te ratio among these phases. Due to the same coordination environment of Cr in the different phases (at the center of Te octahedron, as shown in Fig. 3b) and electronic structure near the Fermi level as revealed by the STS, the measured X-ray absorption spectra (XAS) of the different phases have a similar shape, with slightly varied delicate structures near the Cr-L3 absorption edge, as shown in Fig. 4d. Given Cr’s valence state, the Cr atoms are in the center of Te octahedron in all phases. The strong covalent nature makes the valence states of Cr very close in these 2D structures. Thus, the multiple-peak feature is more pronounced than the peak shift. This variation in the multiple-peak intensity is observed in CrTe2, Cr1+δTe2, and CrTe and is related to the electronic states.

Although the chemical states are similar, different phases exhibit distinct magnetic properties. The magnetic states of the various phases are characterized by the X-ray magnetic circular polarized dichroism PEEM (XMCD-PEEM) as sketched in Fig. 5a. The XMCD-PEEM asymmetry is defined as (Iσ− − Iσ+)/(Iσ− + Iσ+), where Iσ− and Iσ+ are the XAS intensities at the maximum taken with left and right circularly polarized X-rays, respectively. The XMCD contrast originates from the ferromagnetic alignment of Cr spins. XMCD-PEEM measurements are conducted near the Cr L3 edge after cooling the sample from room temperature to 45 K in the absence of a magnetic field (Fig. S9a–d for the XPEEM image of different phases with Cr L3 edge absorption). No XMCD contrast is observed on the FL-CrTe3 and ML-CrTe2, as shown in Fig. 5b, c, which is consistent with the previously reported antiferromagnetic ordering in ML-CrTe3 and ML-CrTe227–29,35–37 (Fig. S9e for spin-polarized STM measurement on ML-CrTe2). In contrast, obvious XMCD signals from opposite ferromagnetic domains are directly imaged on the BL-Cr1+δTe2 and ML-CrTe as shown in Fig. 5d, e. Because the measurements were conducted with zero-field cooling, the random magnetization of the ferromagnetic domains shows two opposite contrasts (blue and red), corresponding to domains with opposite spin alignments along the out-of-plane magnetic easy axis.

Note that the films grown on graphene/SiC always have a non-negligible strain effect due to the thermal expansion mismatch60–63, which would influence the atomic structure and magnetism of the grown CrxTey. For CrTe2, the magnetic ground states are quite sensitive to the strain. It has been shown that the ML-CrTe2 can exhibit distinct magnetic states under different strains (DFT calculations in Fig. S10). This is consistent with previous reports30,31,35. In contrast, Cr1+δTe2 shows a quite robust ferromagnetic ground state but tunable Berry curvature under strain and doping as reported in refs. 35,64. Experimentally, the first layer CrTe2 grown on graphene/SiC shows a smaller in-plane lattice constant compared to the second layer, leading to a transition from a zigzag-antiferromagnetic ordering in the monolayer to ferromagnetic ordering in the second layer35. We found that BL-Cr1+δTe2 exhibits three 2 × 1 structural modulation directions along with domain features, and that the CrTe phase exhibits 1D domain boundaries induced by a half-unit-cell mismatch, as shown in Fig. S11. This may be related to the strain accumulation during growth.

Heterostructures of different phases of chromium tellurides

Based on the control of thermodynamic and kinetic processes during MBE growth of chromium telluride, we further fabricate heterostructures of different phases by varying the cooling rate and growing the sample with a gradient-substrate temperature. As shown in the STM images in Fig. 6a, b, the in-plane heterostructure of ML-CrTe3 and ML-CrTe2, and the out-of-plane heterostructure of ML-CrTe and ML-Cr1+δTe2 are synthesized by applying a gradient of the substrate temperature during growth. The zoomed-in STM images near the heterostructure indicate a continuous atomic structure spanning the boundaries of the different phases, as shown in Fig. 6c, d. The height profiles shown in Fig. 6e, f, and the dI/dV spectra acquired at different phases shown in Fig. 6g, confirm the phase assignment in the heterostructures based on the above discussion. The differential conductance mapping crossing the heterostructure suggests that the interfaces behave as a heterojunction. The Fermi level of CrTe2 is aligned only 40 meV below the valence-band edge of CrTe3. Downward band bending occurs on the CrTe3 side and extends from the interface. In contrast, Cr1+δTe2/CrTe shows a typical metal/metal contact and low-barrier behavior, as shown in Fig. 6h. There is no band bending, but only a sharp change in the electronic structure within 1 nm of the interface, as shown in Fig. 6i.

For tellurides, a dense set of closely competing structures is very common. When reduced to 2D, slight structural changes can lead to large differences in physical properties. Beyond providing a complete phase diagram for the growth of atomically thin CrxTey films via substrate temperature control, the law of the rule, using substrate temperature and post-annealing to control the phase transition and construct a heterostructure, is revealed. The detailed characterization of surface structure, apparent height, and electronic states, as revealed by the STM/STS, provides fingerprint features of these phases, which are cross-validated by DFT calculations and cross-section STEM measurements. We also reveal that for the growth of large-scale chromium telluride films for research using mesoscale or macroscale techniques, such as transport and magnetization measurements, the cooling rate after growth should also be finely tuned. Our XPEEM measurements directly image the spontaneous magnetization of the ferromagnetic states of BL-Cr1+δTe2 and ML-CrTe for the first time. The phase engineering mechanism of chromium telluride revealed here could serve as a reference for other transition-metal chalcogenides, paving the way for fundamental research and innovative device applications for chromium telluride and related transition-metal chalcogenides with varied chemical compositions and phases.

Methods

Sample preparation

The CrxTey films were grown by molecular beam epitaxy in two MBE-STM combined systems (Unisoku and Guo Cheng Inno) with base pressure better than 3 × 10−10 mbar. The ultrathin CrxTey films were grown on few-layer graphene/SiC(0001) substrates. The epitaxial few-layer graphene was prepared by high-temperature annealing of the 2 × 10 mm2 4H-SiC(0001), following the previous report65,66. High-quality Te (99.999 + %, Alfa Aesar) was evaporated from a Knudsen cell at 270 °C, and Cr (99.95% Trillion Metals Co. Ltd.) was evaporated using an electron-beam evaporator. The substrate was heated by direct current. The substrate temperature was controlled by modifying the direct current value to synthesize different phases of CrxTey. The pressure of the chamber is lower than 1.5 × 10−9 mbar during growth.

STM/STS measurements

In-situ STM investigated the as-grown samples at 5 K, 78 K (Unisoku), and 300 K (Unisoku and Guo Cheng Inno). The STM topographic images were taken in constant-current mode with the bias voltage applied to the sample. The STM tip materials are Pt/Ir or W and are modified by crashes and pulses on Au(111) films or graphene/SiC. The differential conductance spectra of graphene/SiC substrate are the references for calibration STS before and after the STS measurements. The dI/dV spectroscopy signals were acquired using a lock-in amplifier with a sinusoidal modulation of 1517 Hz at 10 mV to measure STS. We typically acquired over 6 spectra at the same site to verify reproducibility and then averaged them to increase the signal-to-noise ratio.

STEM measurements

STEM cross-sectional samples were made by focused-ion beam equipment (Thermofisher Scios 2) with a standard workflow. TEM characterization was performed in a double-aberration-corrected JEOL Grand ARM 300. The HAADF-STEM image was acquired with a collection semi-angle of [54, 220] mrad and a convergence semi-angle of 22 mrad.

XPS measurements

The XPS experiments were conducted on the Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250X at room temperature. The binding energy (BE) of the XPS is calibrated with respect to the pure bulk Au 4f7/2 (BE = 83.96 eV), Ag 3d5/2 (BE = 368.21 eV), and Cu 2p3/2 (BE = 932.62 eV) lines. The BE is referenced to the Fermi level (Ef), calibrated by using pure bulk Ni as Ef = 0 eV. The samples were capped with ~10 nm thick Te films and transferred into the XPS chamber in 30 minutes. Before conducting the XPS, post-annealing at ~150 °C in UHV was conducted to remove the capping layers.

XPEEM

XAS-PEEM, and XMCD-PEEM imaging experiments were carried out at the SPEEM-UE49-PGM beamline of the BESSYII synchrotron, Berlin, Germany67. The samples were capped with ~10 nm-thick Se and 5 nm-thick Te films and sealed in a rough vacuum to protect them from oxidation. Before conducting the XPEEM measurement, post-annealing at 150 °C was conducted in UHV to remove the Se/Te capping layers. Real-space XAS and XMCD measurements near the Cr L3 edge (~575.2 eV) are conducted after cooling the sample from room temperature to low temperature (~40 K) without a magnetic field. The fixed angle of incidence of the incoming X-rays with respect to the sample surface was 16°. The XMCD asymmetry is defined as (Iσ− − Iσ+)/(Iσ− + Iσ+), where Iσ− and Iσ+ are the XAS signals at the maximum taken with left and right circularly polarized X-rays, respectively. The magnetic contrast is proportional to the component of magnetization projected along the X-ray propagation direction. Thus, XMCD-PEEM measurements show magnetization parallel or antiparallel to the X-ray polarization, which appears blue or red in the images, respectively.

The DFT calculations

The complete geometrical optimization and electronic calculations of the CrxTey were carried out using the projected augmented wave method as implemented in the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package68, where the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) parametrization was adopted to treat the exchange-correlation function69,70. All self-consistent calculations were performed with a plane wave energy cutoff of 500 eV to ensure convergence. Geometric optimizations were carried out without any constraint until the force on each atom was less than 0.01 eV⋅Å−1 and the change of total energy per cell is smaller than 10−4 eV. The Brillouin zone K-mesh sampling was set as 15 × 15 × 1. To eliminate the interaction between layers along the z direction, we introduced a vacuum layer with a thickness of 15 Å. To better describe the localized 3 d electrons of transition metals, additional on-site Hubbard U terms were added in calculations with different U values tested.
