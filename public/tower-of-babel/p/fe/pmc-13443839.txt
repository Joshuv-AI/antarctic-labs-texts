Optimization of tunnel ventilation system by modeling changes in air flow rate and carbon monoxide concentration during a fire
Authors: Nan Zhang, Ziwei Luo, Zhiyong Ouyang, Yagebai Zhao, Ruofan Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the optimization of tunnel ventilation during fire scenarios using Fire Dynamics Simulator (FDS)-based numerical modeling for a Shanghai Metro tunnel section. The model evaluates temperature evolution, heat release rate, carbon monoxide concentration, and the airflow required to maintain structural safety and support sprinkler-assisted fire suppression. The results showed that the inlet airflow rate strongly influenced the thermal and gas-dynamic conditions in the tunnel: the gas temperature varied from 420 to 720 °C, the heat release rate ranged from 2 to 9 MW, and the peak CO concentration at the tunnel outlet reached approximately 1500 ppmv during the most intense fire stage. Based on the airflow-temperature relationship, two critical control thresholds were identified. Mechanical ventilation should be activated when the tunnel temperature reaches 138 °C, whereas intensified ventilation and sprinkler-assisted suppression become necessary near 525 °C. The calculations further showed that the required air supply increases by approximately 3.6 times only at the critical stage, which indicates that sequential activation of ventilation and sprinkler subsystems can avoid unnecessary full-capacity operation during earlier fire phases and thus improve the energy efficiency of tunnel emergency management. The proposed control logic provides a practical basis for coordinating ventilation and sprinkler operation while maintaining target air-quality and structural-protection conditions.Keywords: airflow rate, FDS modeling, temperature fields, tunnel durability, ventilation systemSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

The optimization of ventilation systems during tunnel fires is a critical area of research, as it directly relates to human safety and the effective mitigation of fire consequences. Fire simulations were performed using the Fire Dynamics Simulator (FDS, version 6.7.9), a computational fluid dynamics model developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)1. Fire and ventilation system modeling is an essential task that involves analyzing fire behavior, smoke propagation, and the efficiency of ventilation systems in buildings and various infrastructures. Such models enable predictions of fire spread, temperature levels, toxic gas concentrations, and the effectiveness of evacuation procedures and property protection measures2.

Modeling the optimization process for ventilation systems during fires requires determining a wide range of parameters. These parameters include tunnel dimensions, such as height and structural framework shape. For ventilation, key considerations involve identifying the type and capacity of the system, the placement of fans, and the location of evacuation exits. Proper organization of air exchange can significantly reduce toxic gas concentrations and improve visibility for evacuating individuals3. In the event of a tunnel fire, it is crucial to promptly identify the fire’s origin and its direction of spread, allowing for appropriate adjustments to the ventilation system.

The efficiency of ventilation systems is also influenced by the tunnel’s design, length, lighting, and the availability of emergency exits. Modern technologies enable automated ventilation management through smoke and temperature sensors, enhancing response speed in emergency situations. Therefore, meticulous planning and regular maintenance of ventilation systems are essential prerequisites for ensuring the safety of passengers and staff in underground transportation facilities.

The organization of ventilation systems plays a crucial role in effectively removing smoke, hot gases, and harmful substances while improving visibility and reducing temperatures in fire zones. The primary types of ventilation systems for tunnels include natural, mechanical, and hybrid ventilation.

In tunnel fire safety systems, ventilation is primarily provided by mechanical jet fans that control the longitudinal airflow and prevent smoke back-layering. In the present study, the term “natural ventilation” refers only to the buoyancy-driven air movement caused by temperature gradients and pressure differences during the initial fire stage. However, such airflow cannot ensure stable smoke control, and therefore mechanical ventilation is required to regulate the air velocity and maintain safe evacuation and fire suppression conditions4. In this context, back-layering refers to the upstream movement of smoke and hot gases against the direction of the ventilation airflow, which can significantly reduce visibility and compromise evacuation safety. The term “flame blow-off” describes the suppression or displacement of the flame front due to a sufficiently strong airflow, which disrupts combustion stability and reduces heat transfer to surrounding structures.

During the optimization of ventilation systems for fires, various fire scenarios are tested using developed models. This involves identifying the location and size of potential fire sources. Fire scenario modeling for ventilation systems considers factors such as temperature, air velocity, and airflow direction. Environmental influences, including external climate conditions such as wind speed and atmospheric pressure, must also be accounted for, as they can impact the efficiency of exhaust systems. Consequently, regular testing and system evaluations across different seasons are necessary to ensure reliability under all conditions.

Modern trends in ventilation technology emphasize the use of intelligent systems capable of real-time data analysis and automatic adjustments to ventilation in response to environmental changes. These innovations enhance the ability to manage emerging threats and optimize evacuation processes, which is critical in high-risk situations. A key area of focus is integrating smart technologies into ventilation management systems5. Air quality sensors and real-time monitoring systems enable rapid responses to operational changes. Contemporary installations also incorporate heat recovery and fan operation optimization, significantly reducing energy consumption. The adoption of such solutions not only lowers operational costs but also minimizes the carbon footprint, an essential consideration in the context of global climate change.

Another significant trend is the adoption of modular ventilation systems. These systems allow for quick and efficient adaptation to changing conditions, such as increased vehicle flow or the implementation of new safety standards6. The modular design facilitates maintenance and upgrades, making such systems more durable and cost-effective over time.

The integration of these innovations highlights that the future of tunnel ventilation lies in the synergy of advanced technologies, energy efficiency, and flexibility, meeting contemporary demands for safety and user comfort within transportation infrastructure.

Literature review

The creation of energy-efficient ventilation systems is based on improving the performance of electric drives and supplementing them with natural ventilation to reduce energy consumption for mechanical fans7,8. Enhancing the efficiency of mechanical ventilation systems is achieved through the use of variable frequency drives (VFDs), which optimize fan rotation speeds for tunnels. VFDs allow the fan speed to be varied depending on real conditions, such as air pollution levels or temperature. This allows for a significant reduction in energy consumption compared to traditional fixed speed systems9,10.

In the theory of tunnel ventilation system optimization, contradictions arise from the complex interaction of various factors, including physiological, environmental, and economic aspects. A primary challenge is that optimal solutions for one part of a tunnel system may prove inefficient for another. For instance, increasing airflow to enhance fire safety in a tunnel may lead to higher energy costs and adverse environmental impacts.

The airflow parameter is critical for fire safety systems11. The higher the fire flashpoint and ignition limits within a tunnel, the greater the airflow required to support fire protection systems. However, increased airflow necessitates greater fan capacity, which negatively impacts the environment by altering natural air distribution, disrupting the ambient temperature regime, and increasing electricity consumption, thus contributing to additional power generation and environmental strain12,13.

The integration of ventilation systems with other fire suppression methods is a topic of active research. In particular, optimal configurations of sprinkler systems combined with ventilation systems are being studied in China and several European countries14–17. Air-based sprinkler systems are emerging as replacements for traditional water and foam sprinklers. A promising area of development involves sprinkler systems equipped with digital accelerators and exhausters for rapid air discharge from duct systems12,14.

Digital accelerators and exhausters, allow for significantly faster and more precise system control. These technologies are increasingly being implemented in advanced ventilation and sprinkler systems15. Current efforts focus on augmenting combined systems of mechanical ventilation and sprinklers with fire-resistant doors to slow the spread of smoke and fire16. However, in tunnels, fire-resistant doors are generally applicable only to pedestrian evacuation exits, which limits the integration of a unified system combining mechanical ventilation, sprinkler systems, and fire-resistant doors.

For tunnels, a more effective solution would be the implementation of a combined ventilation and sprinkler system, offering a cohesive approach to fire suppression and smoke management17. The following parameters are defined and optimized for such systems:

This approach necessitates coordination between fire ventilation systems and sprinkler systems. Industrial controllers facilitate this integration by transmitting information through various communication protocols, including Ethernet, Modbus, Profibus, Profinet, EtherCAT, CAN, DeviceNet, AS-Interface, Foundation Fieldbus, HART, P-NET, IO-Link, CC-Link, DF-1, Interbus, HostLink, Data Highway (DH+), Actual Sensor Interface (ASI)17.

The primary task for such systems in tunnel applications is managing the upper-level controllers responsible for the lower-level systems of ventilation and sprinklers. The key control parameter is the airflow rate required to reduce temperatures or extinguish fires18,19.

Although previous studies have investigated smoke propagation, ventilation strategies, and fire dynamics in tunnel environments, most existing works focus either on general airflow behavior or on the performance of individual fire protection components. In particular, the interaction between ventilation control and sprinkler-assisted fire suppression is rarely analyzed in an integrated framework. Therefore, there remains a need for studies that evaluate how ventilation parameters influence fire development, temperature distribution, and gas concentration simultaneously. The present work addresses this gap by applying numerical modeling using the Fire Dynamics Simulator to analyze the coupled behavior of ventilation and fire suppression processes and to determine optimal control thresholds for tunnel ventilation during fire scenarios.

In existing tunnel ventilation practice, operational strategies are typically based on predefined control scenarios developed during the design stage. These approaches rely on fixed airflow regimes and predefined fan activation sequences to ensure smoke control and evacuation safety5,9,15. Such strategies are generally not adaptive to the evolving thermal and gas-dynamic conditions during fire development. As a result, fixed-control approaches may lead either to excessive energy consumption due to early full-capacity operation or to insufficient response under rapidly changing fire conditions17,20. Recent studies have focused on improving ventilation efficiency and smoke control; however, the integration of temperature-dependent airflow control with fire suppression mechanisms remains limited.

Therefore, the novelty of the present study lies in the development of a sequential activation strategy that links ventilation intensity directly to the evolving temperature field and fire dynamics. This approach enables adaptive regulation of airflow based on physically derived thresholds, improving both energy efficiency and fire safety performance.

Discussion

The temperature curve of the gas medium during a fire follows a logarithmic distribution, as shown in Fig. 2, a pattern observed by numerous researchers14,23,24. According to this curve, a temperature of 1000 °C is reached at approximately 90 min of fire duration. In the works of colleagues, the temperature of 1000 °C was reached at 67 min and 82 min, respectively24–26. This temperature is crucial for understanding the wear resistance of the tunnel structure. For structures consisting of only concrete frames and slabs, the critical temperatures will be higher than for metal frames22,27.

This difference in thermal resistance is primarily associated with the fundamental material properties of steel and concrete. Steel structures exhibit significantly higher thermal conductivity and experience a rapid reduction in mechanical strength when exposed to elevated temperatures. In particular, structural steel may lose up to 40–60% of its load-bearing capacity at temperatures around 500–600 °C, which corresponds closely to the second critical threshold identified in this study (525 °C). In contrast, concrete structures demonstrate lower thermal conductivity and higher thermal inertia, allowing them to maintain structural integrity for longer periods under similar fire conditions.

As a result, the thermal vulnerability of metal frames necessitates more aggressive ventilation control to limit temperature rise and prevent structural deformation. The observed 3.6-fold increase in required airflow at the critical stage is directly linked to the need for enhanced convective heat removal from the steel frame. This intensified airflow promotes faster heat dissipation and supports the “flame blow-off” mechanism, thereby reducing thermal loading on the structure. Consequently, the proposed airflow adjustment strategy is particularly important for tunnels with metal structural elements, where delayed or insufficient ventilation response may lead to rapid structural degradation.

Tunnel fire safety is regulated by several international standards and guidelines, including PIARC recommendations and NFPA codes, which define requirements for ventilation performance, smoke control, and evacuation safety. These frameworks primarily rely on fixed operational strategies, where ventilation and fire suppression systems are activated according to predefined scenarios and constant airflow regimes. While such approaches ensure a baseline level of safety, they do not account for the dynamic evolution of thermal and gas-dynamic conditions during fire development.

In contrast, the present study introduces a sequential activation strategy based on real-time temperature-dependent airflow adjustment derived from numerical modeling. Unlike conventional fixed-control approaches, the proposed method identifies critical transition points (138 °C and 525 °C) that correspond to distinct fire development stages and structural risk levels. This enables a gradual and adaptive increase in ventilation capacity, avoiding premature full-capacity operation while ensuring timely intervention under critical conditions. Therefore, the proposed model addresses a key limitation of existing standards by incorporating a dynamic control logic that links airflow regulation directly to the evolving thermophysical state of the tunnel environment. This approach enhances both energy efficiency and fire safety performance, providing a more flexible and responsive alternative to traditional fixed ventilation strategies.

The distribution shown in Fig. 3 indicates that the highest temperature values are concentrated in the internal part of the tunnel. This temperature distribution is typical for beam-frame constructions, as thermal loads caused by the fire affect the inner part of the frame22–28. The obtained temperature distribution is characteristic of vertical tunnel slabs, as confirmed by numerous studies22,29,30.

Due to the low wear resistance of tunnel frames, the task of ventilated fire suppression using directed air jets for “flame blow-off” becomes critical25,31–35. To optimize the operation of the ventilation system, air flow simulations for the tunnel frame section were conducted. The air flow curve for reducing the frame temperature to the initial 20 °C was obtained. Figure 4 illustrates the air flow curve, which shows that the air supply must be significantly increased to “blow off” the flame when the temperature exceeds 400 °C. This is necessary to preserve the integrity of the tunnel frame, as most tunnels have metal frames18,20,22,35.

A linear approximation of the obtained air flow curve was performed, which identified characteristic points of air flow increase. The two intersection points of the curve and the linear function determine the temperatures at which ventilation must be activated and the air supply power must be increased to initiate the “flame blow-off” effect. The activation of mechanical ventilation for the modeled system occurs at a temperature of 138 °C, and the air supply power needs to be increased significantly when the temperature reaches approximately 525 °C. Based on the obtained airflow rate, the mechanical ventilation airflow should be increased from 100.0 m³/s to 360.0 m³/s, representing an approximately 3.6-fold increase36. The automatic increase in air supply contributes to a significant reduction in the time for “flame blow-off” and the cooling of the structure. In modern systems, the air supply increment factor is adjusted depending on the tunnel geometry, material properties, and typically ranges from 2 to 9.6 times27,36.

In addition to the temperature thresholds identified in the simulations, the ventilation behavior can also be interpreted through flow and heat-transfer characteristics. The increase in airflow required during the fire development stage corresponds to a transition to a more intensive convective regime of the longitudinal air stream, which can be characterized by an increase in the Reynolds number of the flow. At the same time, the cooling efficiency of the tunnel structure is associated with the effective convective heat-transfer coefficient between the hot gases and the tunnel lining. Therefore, the identified control temperatures (138 °C for the activation of mechanical ventilation and approximately 525 °C for intensified ventilation and sprinkler operation) correspond to operating regimes where convective heat removal and smoke transport become sufficiently strong to stabilize the thermal conditions in the tunnel.

The required increase in airflow during fire conditions is directly related to the transition from standard ventilation regimes to emergency thermal and gas-dynamic conditions. At elevated temperatures, particularly near and above the critical threshold of 525 °C, significantly higher airflow rates are necessary to enhance convective heat removal, support smoke extraction, and suppress flame propagation.

The lower bound of the proposed range (approximately 2× the nominal airflow) corresponds to moderate fire development, where increased ventilation is sufficient to control smoke spread and reduce temperature growth. In contrast, higher values (up to 10×) are associated with severe fire scenarios characterized by high heat release rates, where intensified airflow is required to prevent back-layering and facilitate the “flame blow-off” effect. Such airflow amplification ranges are consistent with established tunnel fire ventilation studies and reflect the need to maintain stable longitudinal flow under critical thermal conditions. Therefore, the proposed 2–10× range represents a physically justified and practically applicable guideline for emergency ventilation control.

During the simulation, the maximum CO concentration reached approximately 1500 ppmv at the peak fire stage. This value significantly exceeds commonly accepted exposure limits. For example, according to NIOSH guidelines, exposure to CO concentrations above 1200 ppm may lead to life-threatening conditions within a short time, while even lower concentrations can cause severe physiological effects during prolonged exposure. Therefore, the obtained concentration levels indicate a critical hazard for evacuation safety under peak fire conditions. The results also demonstrate that increasing the airflow rate plays a key role in reducing CO accumulation by enhancing longitudinal smoke removal and dilution of toxic gases. The proposed ventilation control strategy contributes to maintaining more acceptable air quality conditions by limiting the duration and spatial extent of high CO concentration zones.

The elevated CO concentrations observed during peak fire conditions further justify the need for coordinated activation of ventilation and sprinkler systems. While increased airflow enhances dilution and removal of toxic gases, it may not be sufficient under high-intensity fire scenarios. The activation of the sprinkler system contributes to reducing combustion intensity, thereby limiting CO generation at the source. Thus, the combined control strategy not only regulates temperature but also mitigates hazardous gas exposure. This integrated approach is essential for maintaining tenable conditions for evacuation by reducing both thermal and toxic risks within the tunnel environment.

The sensitivity analysis demonstrates that variations in tunnel geometry and fire load significantly influence both the required airflow and the identified activation thresholds. An increase in tunnel length leads to higher aerodynamic resistance, reducing effective airflow velocity and requiring earlier ventilation activation. This effect may increase airflow demand by approximately 10–25% compared to the baseline configuration. Changes in cross-sectional area also affect ventilation efficiency. Larger tunnel sections reduce airflow velocity for a given volumetric flow rate, requiring higher fan capacity to maintain effective smoke removal. This may result in an additional airflow increase of approximately 15–30%. Fire load intensity has the strongest impact on system behavior. Higher heat release rates accelerate temperature rise and shift the critical thresholds (138 °C and 525 °C) to earlier fire stages, while increasing airflow demand by approximately 20–40%. These results indicate that the proposed thresholds should be considered configuration-dependent reference values rather than universal constants. For practical applications, the airflow-temperature relationship must be recalibrated based on specific tunnel geometry and expected fire load conditions.

Although the model was developed for a Shanghai Metro tunnel section, the proposed control strategy depends on key geometric and fire parameters. Tunnel length influences aerodynamic resistance, potentially reducing airflow efficiency and shifting activation thresholds to lower temperatures. Cross-sectional area affects flow velocity: larger sections require higher volumetric flow rates to achieve the same cooling effect, while smaller sections may lead to faster heat accumulation. Fire load characteristics, including fuel type and heat release rate, also affect the thermal response. Higher heat release rates accelerate temperature rise and may require earlier activation of ventilation and higher airflow intensity. Therefore, the identified thresholds (138 °C and 525 °C) should be considered as reference values and adjusted for specific tunnel configurations. Overall, the proposed sequential control strategy remains applicable, provided that the airflow-temperature relationship is recalibrated for the given geometry and fire scenario.

Conclusions

The study presents the results of modeling a section of a tunnel, illustrating the effect of temperature on the tunnel design, a typical gas curve of the environment during a fire, the concentration of carbon monoxide, and the airflow of ventilation systems to reduce temperature. The work also considers the calculation of airflow for ventilation systems equipped with sprinklers.

The primary focus of this research is on methods for determining the necessary air flow rate to ensure the effective operation of the system, in compliance with applicable standards and regulations. Recommendations are provided for optimizing the operating modes of ventilation equipment, including fan power calculations and the selection of control systems. Based on the analysis, optimal solutions for ventilation organization are proposed, enabling the maintenance of required air flow quality parameters. This approach will enhance the safety and comfort of tunnel users and ensure compliance with sanitary regulations and standards.

A promising direction is to carry out processes for optimizing the tunnel ventilation system based on a predictive analysis of possible temperature conditions during a fire. This field presents a significant opportunity for increasing energy efficiency and optimizing ventilation system operations during fires in tunnels. Potential research in this area includes air quality monitoring and management. Optimization of tunnel ventilation systems is an important task that includes not only ensuring a safe and comfortable environment for users, but also energy efficiency. Optimization of tunnel ventilation systems requires a comprehensive approach, including monitoring, analysis, automation and innovative technologies, which allows creating a safe and efficient environment for users.
