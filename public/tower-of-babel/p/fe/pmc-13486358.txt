Current advances in the development of Nipah virus vaccines
Authors: Xueting Zhang, Jiajia Zheng, Qile He, Yuhan Xu, Hua Ji, Shibo Jiang, Naru Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•NiV as a highly pathogenic zoonotic agent, poses a persistent and severe threat to global public health.•This review systematically reviews the viral structure and pathogenic mechanisms of NiV.•This review summarizes the progress and breakthroughs in vaccine development and addresses current research limitations.•The review provides theoretical support for the precise prevention and control of NiV.

Introduction

Nipah virus (NiV), a member of the Henipavirus genus in the Paramyxoviridae family, is a highly pathogenic zoonotic pathogen with a broad host range. Fruit bats serve as its natural reservoir, and the virus can be transmitted to humans via multiple routes, including respiratory droplets and aerosols (Sahay et al., 2025). NiV exhibits a specific tropism for neurons and microglial cells in the central nervous system (CNS) (Worwa et al., 2025), primarily causing respiratory and neurological disorders with such clinical manifestations as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), encephalitis, myocarditis, fever, and headache. Outbreaks of NiV infection have been reported in multiple Asian countries, including Malaysia, Singapore, Bangladesh, India, and the Philippines. In Malaysia where the case fatality rate of NiV infection reached 40%, the majority of patients presented with severe acute encephalitis syndrome (Spengler et al., 2025).

To date, no vaccines or therapeutic agents for NiV have been approved for commercial use, prompting this systematic review of advances in NiV research. Moreover, we comprehensively review the viral structure, pathogenic mechanisms, and current status of NiV vaccine development. We also summarize research achievements and limitations, aiming to provide a theoretical basis for subsequent basic research and effective prevention and control to combat NiV.

Genomic structure of NiV and its encoded proteins

NiV is a non-segmented, negative-sense single-stranded RNA virus with a genomic length of 18,246 nucleotides. Six functional genes, including N, P, M, F, G, and L, are arranged sequentially in the 3′ to 5′ direction, encoding six major structural proteins, respectively (Figure 1). The P gene can further encode the non-structural V, W and C proteins through an RNA editing mechanism (Jin et al., 2025;Faruque et al., 2025;Pelissier et al., 2019). The N protein specifically binds to viral RNA to form a helical nucleocapsid, which plays a core role in protecting the integrity of the viral genome and providing a stable scaffold for its replication. As a cofactor of viral polymerase, the P protein forms a complex with the L protein to regulate the transcription and replication of viral RNA. Meanwhile, the V, W, and C non-structural proteins encoded by the P gene antagonize the host interferon (IFN) signaling pathway, enabling the virus to effectively evade surveillance and elimination. The V protein is mainly localized in the cytoplasm and antagonizes host innate immunity. The W protein is enriched in the nucleus and interferes with the transcription of host immune-related genes, while the C protein is distributed in the cytoplasm and on the cell membrane, regulating viral replication and inhibiting IFN production (Halpin and Gomez Roman, 2026;Lawrence and Escudero-Perez, 2022). The M protein acts as a scaffold for viral particle assembly, mediating the interaction between the nucleocapsid and envelope glycoproteins and providing structural support for the maturation and release of viral particles (Watkinson and Lee, 2016). Following cleavage into F1 and F2 subunits by host proteases, the F protein undergoes conformational rearrangement upon binding of the attachment glycoprotein G to receptors on the host cell surface, thereby mediating fusion between viral envelope and host cell membrane (Broder et al., 2016). Pathogenic mechanisms of NiV

NiV displays a broad cellular tropism, primarily targeting vascular endothelial cells, respiratory mucosal epithelial cells, and neurons in the CNS (Al-Timimi et al., 2026). Glycoprotein G on the viral envelope acts as a key attachment protein that specifically recognizes and binds to the Ephrin-B2/B3 receptors on the host cell surface (Hawkins et al., 2026). This receptor binding induces a conformational rearrangement of the G protein, which, in turn, activates homologous fusion protein F. Activated F protein mediates rapid fusion between viral envelope and host cell membrane, completing viral entry and releasing the internal nucleocapsid into the cytoplasm (Kaza and Aguilar, 2023). A lipopeptide derived from the heptad repeat 2 (HR2) region in the F protein of NiV is extremely potent in inhibiting NiV F protein-mediated membrane fusion of NiV (Wang et al., 2026b;Wang et al., 2026a). The uncoated nucleocapsid remains stable in the cytoplasm, and the virus-encoded L-P polymerase complex (RNA-dependent RNA polymerase) catalyzes the sequential synthesis of mRNA transcripts and full-length antisense genomic RNA using the negative-sense RNA genome as a template, thereby achieving the replication of progeny negative-sense genomes. During this process, viral structural proteins and various auxiliary proteins are synthesized synchronously, providing the material basis for subsequent biological processes, such as viral particle assembly and immune evasion (Ricco et al., 2025;Parks and Alexander-Miller, 2013). Newly synthesized viral genomic RNA is rapidly and tightly encapsulated by the N protein to form a functional nucleocapsid, which is transported to the inner side of the cell membrane under the regulation and recruitment of the matrix protein M. The nucleocapsid then assembles in an orderly manner with envelope glycoproteins, such as G and F, embedded in the membrane. The high pathogenicity of NiV is closely associated with its cellular tropism and immune evasion mechanisms. Its cellular tropism is mainly reflected in the specific infection of cells related to the central nervous system and respiratory system. Efficient replication of NiV in susceptible cells directly causes cytoskeletal damage, abnormal syncytium formation, and lytic cell death, leading to extensive degeneration, hemorrhagic necrosis, and structural disintegration of the affected tissues (Gurajala and Gurajala, 2026;Ayres, 2020). In the vascular system, NiV infection of endothelial cells can induce diffuse vasculitis, destroy the integrity of the vascular barrier, and further trigger vascular leakage, thrombosis, and microcirculation disorders, exacerbating tissue ischemic and hypoxic damage. Employing IFN, which triggers IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) to limit viral replication and spread, the host's immune system mounts an antiviral response. Nonetheless, NiV can evade host innate immune surveillance through multiple means, significantly inhibiting the production and signal transduction of host type I IFN, weakening the host's antiviral defense capacity and creating conditions for persistent viral replication and systemic spread (Tripp et al., 2025;Debroy et al., 2023). Uncontrolled viral replication can drastically activate host inflammatory response, triggering a massive release of proinflammatory factors and chemokines, in turn leading to uncontrolled inflammatory infiltration and bystander tissue damage. In the respiratory system, the above pathological processes can cause alveolar structural damage, pulmonary edema, and inflammatory exudation, severely impairing gas exchange and ultimately progressing to ARDS and respiratory failure. In the central nervous system, direct viral damage to neurons and secondary inflammatory responses can induce neuronal apoptosis, excessive activation of glial cells, and meningoencephalitis, leading to extensive central nervous system lesions with clinical manifestations of severe encephalitis, disturbance of consciousness, and even death (Debroy et al., 2023;Sekimoto and Chiappelli, 2024;Liu et al., 2019).

Vaccine development

As a highly pathogenic and lethal zoonotic virus, the development of NiV vaccine platforms, such as subunit, viral vectors, or nucleic acid formulations, predominantly focuses on the viral G and F proteins as antigenic targets (Lu et al., 2023;Zhou et al., 2024;Lv et al., 2025). Most vaccines are still in the experimental stage, but some candidate vaccines have advanced to clinical trials and have shown good potential against NiV infection (Singh et al., 2019;Faus-Cotino et al., 2024;Madhukalya et al., 2025).

Recombinant subunit vaccines

Recombinant subunit vaccines, a mainstream direction for NiV vaccine development, express key viral structural proteins as antigens, thus having the advantages of high specificity and safety (Faus-Cotino et al., 2024). Current research focuses on the core epitopes of viral fusion protein F and attachment glycoprotein G, key molecules mediating viral entry into host cells and the main targets of neutralizing antibodies (Talukdar et al., 2023).

NiV and Hendra virus (HeV) are closely related zoonotic paramyxoviruses that have emerged and re-emerged in past years. A subunit vaccine formulation containing soluble G protein from HeV (sG(HeV)) and CpG adjuvant was evaluated as an NiV vaccine in a cat model. It elicited protective systemic NiV-specific Ig and antigen-specific IgA mucosal immunity in vaccinated cats (McEachern et al., 2008), providing significant progress towards the development of a human subunit vaccine against NiV. A human codon-optimized HeV soluble G protein (HeV-sG) expression vector was constructed by genetic engineering, and after cell culture and affinity chromatography purification, a combined preparation was prepared with aluminum adjuvant and CpG ODN 2007. This vaccine, effective against the NiV-Malaysia strain (NiV-M), NiV-B and HeV, could induce cross-protective immunity in multiple non-human primate models and significantly reduce morbidity and mortality after infection. Although this vaccine has no protective effect in pig models, it has entered Phase I clinical trial (NCT04199169), a single dose of 0.1mg HeV-sG, formulated for use in humans, was capable of inducing a completely protective immune response in African green monkeys from a 5✕105 pfu challenge dose of NiV-B delivered by i.t. and i.n. administration at just 7 days post-vaccination (Geisbert et al., 2021), indicating that the HeV-sG is suitable for use as either an emergency or prophylactic vaccine for the prevention of infection and disease caused by NiV and HeV.

In addition, subunit vaccines targeting the F and G proteins of NiV itself are in the research and development (R&D) stage. Stabilizing pre-fusion conformation of F protein or optimizing the expression form of G protein can effectively increase the antigen’s immunogenicity, thereby inducing the production of high-titer neutralizing antibodies (McLean et al., 2025). The researchers innovatively employed a cell-free protein synthesis system to co-translate and integrate the signal peptide-deleted full-length NiV-F with NiV-G transmembrane proteins into PC/PE/PS complex liposomes, achieving natural antigen conformation and correct membrane-oriented expression, while maintaining compatibility with MPLA lipid adjuvant co-delivery (Hu et al., 2025). Such vaccines have the advantages of strong targeting and precise action on key pathogenic sites of NiV, but they are still challenged by poor protein expression level, stability and immunogenicity (Loomis et al., 2020;Chen et al., 2023). The optimized cell-free synthetic protein liposomal vaccine can induce stronger specific neutralizing antibodies and T-cell responses in mice. It also elicits effective protective immunity without the need for glycosylation modification, providing a novel strategy to address the aforementioned bottlenecks (Hu et al., 2025).

Virus-like particle-based vaccines

Virus-like particle (VLP) vaccines without viral nucleic acid can express the F, G and M structural proteins of NiV through host cells and self-assemble to form particle structures similar to those of natural NiV. Prepared from animal cell cultures, this vaccine induces a strong specific neutralizing antibody response in the body through cleaved and activated F and G proteins. It has demonstrated many advantages, such as strong immunogenicity, high safety, and high biosafety. At present, this vaccine is still in the preclinical stage in anticipation that it will become an important supplement to NiV prevention and control vaccines (Walpita et al., 2017;Bezeljak et al., 2025) .

Viral vector vaccines

Viral vector vaccines use harmless viruses as vectors to introduce pathogen antigen-encoding genes into host cells and express antigens, thereby inducing an immune response in the body. They have the advantages of strong immunogenicity and effective protection induced by a single immunization (Wang et al., 2023). Current NiV vector vaccines in development mainly include recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus (rVSV) vector vaccines, adenovirus vector vaccines and recombinant measles virus vector vaccines (Ganguly et al., 2025).

A recombinant replication-competent VSV-based vaccine that encodes a NiV G as an antigen protects African green monkeys from disease one month after a single intramuscular administration of the vaccine. Vaccination resulted in a rapid and strong virus-specific immune response which inhibited virus shedding and replication. (Prescott et al., 2015). Researchers assessed replication-restricted single-injection rVSV vector-ed NiV vaccine expressing the NiV G against NiVB challenge in African green monkeys. All vaccinated animals survived to the study endpoint without signs of NiV disease and all showed development of NiV F Ig, NiV G IgG, or both, as well as neutralizing antibody titers (Mire et al., 2019). Preclinical studies in African green monkeys revealed excellent safety profiles with a 100% protection rate against lethal dose strain challenge 7 days after a single vaccination and a 67% protection rate 3 days after vaccination. These results indicate that the rVSV-ΔG-NiVBG vaccine can rapidly activate the body's immunity and induce specific neutralizing antibodies, making it an excellent candidate vaccine for emergency prevention and control of NiV outbreaks (Foster et al., 2022). Immunization of golden hamsters with replication-competent rVSV expressing NiV attachment G and F glycoproteins (rVSVΔG-eGFP-NiVBD F/G) induced potent neutralizing antibody responses (Guo et al., 2025), demonstrating its robust immunogenicity. The rVSV-NiV chimeric vaccine PHV02 sponsored by the Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations (CEPI) is homologous to the Ebola vaccine (Ervebo); it can express both Ebola envelope glycoprotein and NiV G protein. Using a replication-restricted rVSV as the vector, this vaccine targets the F and G glycoproteins of NiV-B, and a single intramuscular injection can induce the production of high-efficiency neutralizing antibodies. Studies have confirmed that neutralizing antibodies induced by PHV02 serve as the core protective factor. An antibody titer ≥1:5 predicts a 100% survival probability, while a titer ≥1:40 achieves aseptic immunity. Effective protective effects can still be observed when vaccination is administered 7 to 14 days prior to viral exposure (Monath et al., 2023). Another adenovirus vector vaccine, ChAdOx1 NiVB, is constructed by inserting the G glycoprotein-encoding gene of NiV-B into a modified adenovirus vector. Preclinical studies in Syrian golden hamsters and African green monkeys demonstrated complete survival against lethal NiV challenge after single or two-dose immunization with sustained high neutralizing antibody levels (Van Doremalen et al., 2019; van Doremalen et al., 2022). Its Phase 1 clinical trial in the UK was finished in early 2024, showing favorable safety profiles and robust humoral immunity in healthy adults (Madhukalya et al., 2025). Currently, the Phase 2a clinical trial enrolling 306 endemic participants in Bangladesh is ongoing to evaluate real-world safety and immunogenicity. Recombinant measles virus vector vaccines (rMV-NiV), including rMV-HL-G and rMV-Ed-G, are constructed by expressing the G protein of NiV. A single immunization in Syrian hamster models could stimulate strong humoral immunity and achieve complete protection against lethal dose NiV challenge (Yoneda et al., 2013). A single intranasal immunization with chimpanzee adenoviral-vectored NiV vaccine, AdC68-F, induced robust and sustained cellular and humoral responses in BALB/c mice and completely protected against NiV-M challenge in Syrian hamsters (Lu et al., 2025). This study highlights the potential of single-dose intranasal AdC68-based NiV vaccines for rapid protection during outbreaks. In addition, live-vector vaccines constructed based on attenuated rabies virus have been evaluated for effectiveness in wildlife models, providing the possibility of expanding application scenarios, as well as the prevention and control scope of vaccines (Keshwara et al., 2019).

mRNA vaccines

mRNA vaccines synthesize mRNA molecules encoding viral antigens through in vitro transcription, deliver them to host cells via lipid nanoparticles (LNP), and use the host's own translation system to express the corresponding antigens. These vaccines offer a short R&D cycle, flexible production and strong immunogenicity. Widely tested and validated during the COVID-19 pandemic, this technology has now been extended to the R&D of vaccines against zoonotic viruses, including NiV (Tan et al., 2025;Ploquin et al., 2026).

NiV mRNA vaccines have made strides in antigen optimization and protective mechanisms. For example, the mRNA vaccine based on 60-mer (60-polymer) G glycoprotein nanoparticles can enhance B-cell activation through multivalent high-density antigen presentation, thereby inducing potent neutralizing antibody responses (Kling et al., 2024). Additionally, three types of LNP-mRNA vaccines targeting F and G single antigens, as well as the co-expression of F and G antigens, have all demonstrated highly efficient specific immune responses, achieving 100% protection and effective viral load clearance in lethal challenge models using Syrian hamsters (Karthika et al., 2024). Among them, the mRNA-1215 vaccine represents a joint venture between the US National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) and Moderna, Inc. This vaccine encodes the pre-fusion F protein of NiV-M, which is covalently linked to the G protein monomer, and uses N1-methylpseudouridine modification to boost its stability, finally completing in vivo delivery after LNP encapsulation. The clinical trial enrolled forty healthy adults, who met eligibility criteria, into the 10-, 25-, 50- or 100-μg dose groups with ten participants per group. Each participant received two doses of mRNA-1215, intramuscularly, at a 4-week interval except for one participant in the 10-μg dose group who received only the first dose. The first-in-human, structure-based, chimeric mRNA-1215 elicited robust Pre-F and G binding antibody titers by 2 weeks after the prime in all dose groups. Responses increased after the boost and remained elevated for at least 1 year after vaccination. Overall, the vaccine was well tolerated and no serious adverse events occurred during the study. This clinical study demonstrates that mRNA-1215 is a promising vaccine candidate for continued clinical development for populations at risk for regional and potentially larger outbreaks caused by NiV (NCT05398796) (Ploquin et al., 2026). However, cross-protective efficacy against different NiV strains and long-term safety remain to be elucidated (Rodrigue et al., 2024;Wilson et al., 2024).

The core advantage of mRNA vaccines is their ability to quickly adapt to mutant viruses, noting that specific responses to new NiV variants can be achieved by adjusting the antigen-encoding sequence. However, this technology still faces key challenges, such as the optimization of delivery systems, high requirements for cold storage, and potential immune-related adverse reactions, all of which require further research (Lewis et al., 2023;Zhang et al., 2025).

Live-attenuated vaccines

Live-attenuated vaccines reduce the virulence of viruses through genetic modification, while retaining their replication capacity. This property enables live-attenuated vaccines to continuously activate the host immune system and induce a specific immune response, along with the advantages of long-lasting immune effect and low dosage (Tang et al., 2025). Previous studies have constructed single-dose live-attenuated vaccines expressing NiV F or G glycoproteins using rVSV as the vector. This vaccine can induce the production of specific neutralizing antibodies against viral surface glycoproteins, thereby providing complete protection against lethal doses of NiV infection. It also offers such advantages as rapid efficacy, suitability for post-exposure emergency prevention and control, and ease of large-scale production, making it suitable for ring vaccination strategies during epidemic outbreaks (DeBuysscher et al., 2014). However, owing to the potential risk of virulence reversion and safety concerns for immunocompromised populations, the clinical translation of NiV live-attenuated vaccines still requires long-term and strict systematic safety evaluation. Challenges and prospects

NiV-caused diseases are characterized by high pathogenicity and lethality. They have broken out many times in multiple Asian countries, such as India and Bangladesh, posing a severe threat to human health, animal husbandry development and public health security (Sahay et al., 2025). Against this backdrop, it is critical to develop a long-term NiV prevention and control system to reduce the disease burden and protect human and animal health. Preliminary progress has been made in the research and development of NiV-related vaccines, laying a foundation for its prevention and control. Nevertheless, many urgent challenges remain. For instance, the complex regulatory pathways of viral pathogenic mechanisms and the impact of genetic variation on prevention and control technologies need additional research.

The pathogenic mechanism of NiV involves promoting its own replication and spread by regulating the host cytokine network and inhibiting host immune response. Some infections are occult, confounding an understanding of the specific regulatory pathways NiV uses to evade the immune system and induce cytokine storm, demonstrably increasing the difficulty of formulating prevention and control strategies (Van den Hurk et al., 2025). NiV is a BSL-4 pathogen, which imposes high professional requirements on the research environment, experimental facilities and operators. Most scientific research institutions cannot meet these standards, seriously restricting progress in developing antiviral vaccines (Wang et al., 2025). BSL-4 laboratory constraints represent a foundational technical barrier to NiV vaccine research, yet regulatory limitations stemming from unfeasible Phase III clinical trials constitute an equally decisive roadblock to market access. Alternative approval pathways including the FDA Animal Rule and EMA Conditional Marketing Authorization are indispensable solutions; however, their exhaustive preclinical validation rules, strict post-licensure reporting mandates and cross-agency alignment challenges introduce unique, multi-year developmental delays that must be addressed alongside biocontainment limitations to accelerate NiV vaccine development.

In the future, NiV-related research needs to adopt the "One Health" concept, strengthen multidisciplinary cross-collaboration, and promote dedicated paramyxovirus research. On the one hand, to establish an integrated 'animal-human' prevention barrier, we need to design universal vaccines based on antigen-conserved epitopes of Nipah virus (NiV) strains. By combining structural biology with network pharmacology, we can proceed to explore vaccine-induced immune mechanisms, thereby enhancing cross-protection against potential mutant strains. Furthermore, it is necessary to promote the collaborative research and development of both human and veterinary vaccines. On the other hand, with the key targets of viral replication as the core, we can develop targeted antiviral vaccines with high specificity and activity, explore new delivery vectors, such as liposomes and nanoparticles, to improve vaccine efficacy, and break through the limitations of BSL-4 laboratory conditions to accelerate clinical translation of vaccine candidates. Combined with the technical means of environmental science, veterinary medicine and other disciplines, we should systematically analyze the driving mechanisms of fruit bat host behavior changes and environmental disturbances on viral spillover, improve the epidemic monitoring network, and provide a scientific basis for the prediction and prevention of viral spillover events. Dedicated NiV research will elucidate the virus's pathogenic mechanisms and transmissibility. This foundational knowledge will enable the development of precise prevention tools, optimize the 'vaccination-drug intervention-source control' system, and accelerate clinical translation, ultimately reducing the risk of cross-species and regional transmission. These steps will safeguard global public health security and promote the sustainable development of animal husbandry.
