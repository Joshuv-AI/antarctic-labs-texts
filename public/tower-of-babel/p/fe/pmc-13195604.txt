Arkangel AI, OpenEvidence, ChatGPT, Medisearch: Are They Objectively up to Medical Standards? A Real-Life Assessment of LLM Chatbots in Health Care
Authors: Natalia Castaño-Villegas, María Camila Villa, Katherine Monsalve Barrientos, Isabella Llano, Laura Velásquez, José Zea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the performance of 4 large language model chatbots in response time and quality of clinical answers, evaluated by specialists using predefined validity criteria.Participants and MethodsBetween June 1 and September 20, 2025, four clinical vignettes (orthopedics, pediatrics, gynecology, and psychiatry) were developed by independent experts and answered by 4 conversational agents: Arkangel AI, OpenEvidence, ChatGPT, and Medisearch. Each vignette included 4 questions (diagnosis, clinical management, research, and general knowledge). Responses were independently evaluated by external clinicians using an 8-criterion Likert scale assessing correctness, consensus agreement, absence of bias, adherence to standards of care, timeliness, patient safety, authenticity of cited references, and contextual appropriateness. Response times were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges.ResultsA total of 128 question-answer pairs (1024 evaluations) were analyzed. Overall satisfaction ranged from 71.1% (727) to 93% (952) across agents, with statistically significant differences (Kruskal-Wallis P<.001). Dissatisfaction was most frequent for reference authenticity in some ChatGPT modes (75% [n 91/128]-97% [n 119/128]), whereas Arkangel AI-Deep, ChatGPT-Deep, and OpenEvidence showed 100% satisfaction for this criterion. Satisfaction was high for correctness and consensus, with greater variability for bias and patient safety. Satisfaction differed by specialty, with higher scores in gynecology and lower scores in pediatrics (P<.001). Median response times ranged from 18 seconds to 12.8 minutes, with significant differences across agents and modes (Wilcoxon P<.05).ConclusionLarge language model chatbots showed substantial variability across validity dimensions when assessed using expert clinical judgment, supporting the need for standardized, multidimensional evaluation frameworks.

The expansion of large language model (LLM) chatbots has transformed health care research and practice by enabling medical information processing and synthesis at unprecedented speed and scale.1 General purpose LLM chatbots, such as ChatGPT (OpenAI), Bard (Google), and Claude (Anthropic), have gained widespread adoption in health care settings.1,2 These chatbots are being used for various clinical applications, including medical question-answering, clinical documentation, and decision support.3 However, their performance and safety in medical contexts require rigorous evaluation to ensure that they meet clinical standards.4, 5, 6

With biomedical literature multiplying and timely decision-making being critical, LLM chatbot–based conversational agents (CAs) emerge as allies for health care workers.2

Public, standardized clinical questions and answers (Q&A) datasets, such as MedQA7 and MedMCQA,8 are used to evaluate LLM chatbots. Although these resources enable progress, they are static and do not reflect the complexity of clinical practice. This limitation challenges their validity, bias, and safety.

Natural language processing (NLP) agents' medical capabilities need assessment beyond accuracy, using broader, systematic evaluation frameworks.9 The Holistic Evaluation of Language Models (HELM) proposes a structured approach to assess LLM chatbots across dimensions such as robustness, fairness, efficiency, and transparency, contributing to their accountability in real practice.5 In parallel, HealthBench (OpenAI) introduced an open-source framework designed to measure the performance and safety of CAs in medical contexts, incorporating multidimensional rubrics.10 Nevertheless, their scope is limited, and evaluation methods are not easily reproducible. No standardized benchmark exists to comprehensively assess LLM chatbots validity, effectiveness, or safety in clinical practice.

Villa et al11 developed Arkangel AI, a CA designed to answer medical questions using validated, up to date sources. Internal validation with MedQA achieved 90.26% accuracy, surpassing GPT-4o, Med-PaLM 2, and human baselines (Supplemental Table 1, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). A subsequent randomized, double-blind trial with 106 clinicians comparing artificial intelligence (AI)–assisted vs traditional search showed >50% time savings and higher validity scores (2.84 vs 2.66; P<.001).11 Building on that approach, this study evaluates direct responses from 4 CAs—ChatGPT (OpenAI),12 OpenEvidence (OpenEvidence),13 Medisearch (Weierstrass Limit Inc),14 and Arkangel AI (Arkangel AI)15—across 4 clinical specialties using an expanded 8-criterion Likert-based rubric (Table 1). The objective is to assess each agent’s strengths and limitations to inform safe and effective LLM use and support standardization for AI integration in health care.

Background

The evaluation of LLM chatbots in health care has received increasing attention in recent years. Multiple frameworks have been proposed to assess their performance in medical contexts; however, fully standardized and widely adopted benchmarks remain limited.

In parallel, a rapidly expanding body of literature has focused on benchmarking and evaluating medical LLM chatbots using both public datasets and clinically motivated simulations.1,2 Common approaches include standardized medical question-answering benchmarks (eg, MedQA, MedMCQA, and PubMedQA9), board-style examinations, and simulated clinical tasks, often emphasizing accuracy or agreement with reference answers. More recent work has highlighted important limitations of these approaches,6 including their static nature, limited contextual realism, susceptibility to hallucinated references, and insufficient assessment of patient safety, bias, and contextual appropriateness. As a result, there is growing consensus that complementary evaluation strategies—incorporating expert judgment, multidimensional criteria, and clinically contextualized scenarios—are needed to better characterize how LLM chatbots behave in health care practice.

The HELM framework provides a comprehensive approach for evaluating LLM chatbots across multiple dimensions, including accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, and efficiency. In health care applications, HELM has been extended through MedHELM, which adapts these dimensions to medical tasks and incorporates clinical relevance considerations.16

HealthBench, developed by OpenAI, represents another important contribution to the evaluation of medical LLM chatbots.10 This open-source framework is designed to assess the performance and safety of CAs in health care settings using multidimensional rubrics that capture accuracy, patient safety, and alignment with evidence-based guidelines.

Beyond evaluation frameworks, several studies have examined the performance of LLM chatbots in specific medical contexts. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have compared the diagnostic performance of generative AI systems with that of physicians,17 whereas other investigations have evaluated chatbots using specialty-specific examinations or board-style questions.18,19 Collectively, these studies emphasize the need for evaluation strategies that extend beyond accuracy to include safety, bias, and clinical utility.

Comparative assessments of medical LLM chatbots remain relatively scarce. Most existing studies focus on single-agent evaluations or comparisons against static benchmarks, rather than dynamic, clinically contextualized scenarios. The literature also identifies persistent gaps in standardized evaluation protocols, particularly with respect to reference authenticity, bias, and context awareness in clinical applications.20, 21, 22

Within this context, the present study adds to the existing evidence by providing a comparative evaluation of 4 LLM chatbots using expert clinical judgment as the reference standard. By applying a multidimensional assessment framework across 4 clinical specialties and multiple question types, this work addresses identified gaps in current evaluation approaches and offers a practical assessment methodology.

Participants and Methods

Study Design

This exploratory study was conducted from June 1 to September 20, 2025. LLM chatbots were evaluated using expert clinical judgment as the reference standard. No patient-level data were included. All evaluations were performed by independent medical specialists external to Arkangel AI, who were blinded to the identity of the chatbot and search mode used for each response. All participants provided informed consent. This study was conducted using fictitious clinical cases, and no patient information was disclosed; it was classified as minimal risk according to Resolution 8430 of 1993 (Colombia)23 and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2022).24

Clinical Vignettes and Study Setting

Independent medical specialists were invited to voluntarily develop fictitious, unidentifiable clinical vignettes. Four cases were selected, one each in orthopedics, pediatrics, gynecology, and psychiatry (Supplemental Table 2, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/), based on the experts’ availability, willingness, and experience. All had at least 5 years of clinical practice and 3 years of outpatient experience in tertiary care. They followed national guidelines for clinical records.25,26 Each vignette included 4 follow-up questions related to diagnosis, clinical management, research, and general knowledge.

LLM Chatbots Evaluated

Four LLM chatbots were evaluated in this study: Arkangel AI, ChatGPT, OpenEvidence, and Medisearch. Each chatbot was assessed using its publicly available “regular” mode. In addition, Arkangel AI and ChatGPT were evaluated using their paid advanced search modes (“Personalized” and “Deep”), because these options are offered to end users as distinct interaction modes.

All evaluations were conducted using the versions available during the study period. No system-level customization, fine-tuning, or developer-side configuration was applied beyond the default user-facing settings.

Prompting Protocol

A researcher not involved in other phases of the project conducted all prompting procedures based on the predefined clinical vignettes. Each vignette was entered verbatim into the standard user interface of each LLM chatbot, followed by the first predefined question. Subsequent questions were entered sequentially, without iterative refinement, adaptive follow-up, or additional prompting beyond the predefined question set.

Each interaction involved a single chatbot and a single question. For each vignette, one complete interaction cycle consisted of the vignette text and 4 predefined questions. All chatbots were first evaluated using their publicly available regular mode. Arkangel AI and ChatGPT were additionally assessed using their paid advanced search modes (“Personalized” and “Deep”), which are offered as distinct user-accessible interaction modes (Figure).

Q&A workflow distribution. Q&A, question-answer pairs.

To ensure reproducibility and standardization, a single prompt structure was used across all agents, clinical specialties, question types, and interaction modes. Each prompt consisted of (1) a brief instruction requesting a response based on the clinical case, (2) the complete clinical vignette text, and (3) 1 predefined question.

This structure was applied uniformly to all question types (diagnosis, clinical management, research, and general knowledge). For each question, a new chat session was initiated, following the same procedure consistently across all agents and interaction modes. The complete prompting protocol, including the exact wording of the instruction and step-by-step procedures, is provided in Supplemental Table 16 (available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Q&A Collection and Data Management

The researcher remained connected throughout the prompting process to ensure continuity and consistency. On completion of each interaction cycle (1 vignette and 4 questions), all chatbot responses were recorded in a centralized database. The variables collected are described in Supplemental Table 3 (available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

A second independent researcher verified the integrity of the collected data, including the presence and accuracy of references when provided. The complete dataset and associated search links are available in Supplemental Table 17 (available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Data were internally managed using the open-source database management system Supabase.27 An external web-based interface built with Lovable App was used to display clinical vignettes, collect chatbot responses, and record expert reviewers’ scores in a structured format.

Development of the Evaluation Tool

For the purposes of this study, an 8-criterion evaluation tool was developed to structure expert assessment of LLM chatbot responses. The tool was informed by established medical AI evaluation frameworks, including the HELM framework and HealthBench,10,16 which emphasize multidimensional assessment beyond accuracy alone.

Each criterion was designed to capture a distinct and conceptually separable dimension of medical AI performance: (1) correctness of response, assessing factual accuracy, and clinical appropriateness; (2) agreement with clinical guidelines or scientific consensus; (3) absence of demographic bias; (4) adherence to standards of care; (5) timeliness of information; (6) patient safety, reflecting potential for clinical harm; (7) authenticity of cited references; and (8) contextual appropriateness within the specific clinical scenario.

To minimize conceptual overlap, each criterion was operationally defined using an explicit evaluation guide provided to all raters before scoring. These definitions were intended to support consistent interpretation across evaluators and to ensure that each criterion addressed a distinct evaluative domain.

The evaluation tool was not subjected to an independent validation process and was not intended to function as a validated measurement instrument. Rather, it was used as a structured framework to support expert clinical judgment in a comparative, exploratory evaluation of LLM chatbots. The criteria align with core evidence-based medicine principles and contemporary medical AI evaluation practices, but results should be interpreted within the context of an exploratory study design.

Expert Raters and Scoring Procedure

All expert raters were licensed physicians with clinical experience in their respective specialties. Two specialists per field (orthopedics, pediatrics, gynecology, and psychiatry) independently evaluated the chatbot-generated responses using the 8 predefined evaluation criteria described in Table 1. Before scoring, all raters attended a brief introductory session explaining the use of the web-based evaluation platform and the operational definitions of each criterion.

Raters assessed each response based on their clinical expertise and professional judgment. To minimize bias, both the evaluators and the principal investigators were blinded to the identity of the LLM chatbot and the search mode used. Q&A pairs were presented in random order and without identifiers. A member of the research team provided technical support during the evaluation process when needed but did not participate in scoring.

Criterion 7 (“authenticity of cited references”) was operationally defined to assess whether references provided in a response corresponded to verifiable sources, such as peer-reviewed journals, government websites, or recognized professional organizations. When no references were provided, raters selected a “not applicable” option. This criterion was intended to identify the presence of hallucinated references rather than to evaluate the quality of the cited sources.

Outcomes

Time to Response

Time to response was evaluated using automatically generated system logs. Two time points were recorded: the time from question submission to response initiation (T1) and the time from question submission to completion of the full response (T2), both measured in seconds. Time metrics were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges.

Response stability was estimated by calculating the relative difference between the medians of T2 and T1, which was used as an indicator of response time variability (Table 2). Group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon test, with the significance level set at α = 0.05.

Quality and Validity

Quality and validity outcomes were assessed using the 8 predefined evaluation criteria described in Table 1. These criteria reflect key dimensions of evidence-based medical responses, including response correctness, agreement with clinical guidelines, absence of demographic bias, adherence to standards of care, timeliness of information, patient safety, authenticity of cited references, and contextual appropriateness. Each criterion was rated using a 6-point Likert scale (1=very dissatisfied, 2=somewhat dissatisfied, 3=neutral, 4=somewhat satisfied, 5=very satisfied, and 6=not applicable). For analytic purposes, scores were dichotomized into “satisfaction” (scores 4-5) and “dissatisfaction” (scores 1-2). Neutral and not applicable responses were analyzed separately, depending on the criterion. Detailed distributions for these categories are provided in Supplemental Tables 4-8 (available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Data were grouped by LLM chatbot, interaction mode, clinical specialty, and question type. Outcomes were summarized using absolute and relative frequencies and medians (interquartile ranges). Group comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dwass-Steel-Critchlow-Fligner post hoc adjustment.28 The distribution of each criterion by chatbot, specialty, and question type is presented in Table 3 and Supplemental Table 9 (available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Inter and Intrarater Consistency

Inter and intrarater consistency were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models, with chatbot treated as a fixed effect and raters as random effects. Variance components were used to estimate inter-rater reliability and overall performance variability. Results of these analyses are reported in Supplemental Tables 14 and 15 (available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). All analyses were conducted using a 2-sided significance level of P<.05.

Results

Time to a Response

Response time metrics varied across LLM chatbots and interaction modes (Table 2). Time to response initiation (T1) was short for some agents; for example, ChatGPT-Personalized initiated responses in approximately 0.5 seconds, whereas longer initiation times were observed for ChatGPT-Deep (median: 28 seconds) and Arkangel AI-Deep (median: 361 seconds, ≈6 minutes).

With respect to total time to response completion (T2), Medisearch completed responses in approximately 18 seconds, whereas ChatGPT-Deep and Arkangel AI-Deep showed median completion times of 766.5 seconds (≈12.8 minutes) and 443 seconds (≈7.4 minutes), respectively. OpenEvidence and Arkangel AI-Regular exhibited intermediate completion times with relatively limited variability. Response time variability, estimated as the difference between the medians of T2 and T1, was greater for ChatGPT-Deep (median, 737 seconds, ≈12.3 minutes) and Arkangel AI-Deep (median, 79.5 seconds, ≈1.3 minutes), whereas Medisearch showed a smaller difference (14 seconds).

Statistical comparisons indicated significant differences in response time distributions across chatbots and interaction modes (Wilcoxon test, P<.05).

Quality and Validity Outcomes

Quality and validity outcomes varied across the evaluated LLM chatbots and interaction modes (Table 3). Overall satisfaction rates ranged from 71% (n 91/128) to 93% (119/128) with values of 93% for Arkangel AI-Deep, 84% (n 107) for OpenEvidence, 80% (n 103) for ChatGPT-Personalized, Arkangel AI-Personalized, and ChatGPT-Deep, and 71% (n 91) for Medisearch. These differences were statistically significant (Kruskal-Wallis test, P<.001).

Post hoc analyses identified significant differences between several agent pairs (Supplemental Table 10, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). Specifically, Arkangel AI-Deep differed significantly from Arkangel AI-Regular, Arkangel AI-Personalized, ChatGPT-Regular, ChatGPT-Deep, and Medisearch (all P<.001), as well as from OpenEvidence (P=.022). In addition, significant differences were observed between Arkangel AI (Regular and Personalized) and Medisearch, and between ChatGPT (Regular, Personalized, and Deep) and Medisearch (P<.001). All other pairwise comparisons did not reach statistical significance.

Stratified analyses by clinical specialty and question type were also conducted. When stratified by specialty, satisfaction scores were significantly higher in gynecology and orthopedics compared with pediatrics (P<.001), whereas psychiatry did not differ significantly from gynecology or orthopedics (Supplemental Table 12, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). When stratified by question type, significant differences were likewise observed (Kruskal-Wallis test, P=.007), with clinical management questions receiving higher scores than diagnostic, general knowledge, and research questions, and research questions scoring higher than diagnostic questions (P<.001). Detailed results are provided in Supplemental Tables 11 and 13 (available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Agent Performance Profiles

Overall satisfaction rates varied across agents (Table 4). Arkangel AI-Deep achieved an overall satisfaction of 93.0% (119 of 128 satisfactory Q&A pairs). OpenEvidence achieved 84.0% (107). Arkangel AI-Personalized, ChatGPT-Personalized, and ChatGPT-Deep showed comparable overall satisfaction levels (80.5% [103] each). Arkangel AI-Regular achieved 76.6% (98), and ChatGPT-Regular 72.7% (93). Lower satisfaction was observed for Medisearch (71.1%-n 91).

Arkangel AI-Deep obtained perfect scores across most evaluation criteria, including 100% satisfaction for response correctness, agreement with clinical consensus, timeliness of information, and authenticity of cited references. Higher satisfaction was observed in gynecology, psychiatry, and clinical management and research-type questions. Lower satisfaction was observed for adherence to standards of care (criterion C4).

OpenEvidence achieved perfect scores for up to date information and authenticity of cited references (100% satisfaction for both criteria). Lower satisfaction was observed for adherence to standards of care (C4) and patient safety (C6). Higher satisfaction was observed in gynecology and in clinical management questions.

Among Arkangel AI-Personalized, ChatGPT-Personalized, and ChatGPT-Deep, distinct performance profiles were observed. Arkangel AI-Personalized showed higher satisfaction for nonbias (C3) and authenticity of cited references (C7). ChatGPT-Personalized showed higher satisfaction for patient safety and context awareness, with 100% satisfaction for patient safety, while frequently omitting references. ChatGPT-Deep showed lower satisfaction for patient safety compared with the other agents in this group. Across these agents, higher satisfaction was observed in gynecology and in clinical management and research questions.

Arkangel AI-Regular showed higher satisfaction for response correctness and agreement with clinical consensus, with higher satisfaction observed in psychiatry and research-type questions.

ChatGPT-Regular showed high satisfaction for correctness and agreement with clinical consensus, and lower satisfaction for nonbias and authenticity of cited references.

Medisearch showed higher satisfaction for nonbias, patient safety, and authenticity of cited references, with lower satisfaction observed across several other evaluation criteria. Higher satisfaction was observed in psychiatry and orthopedics, and in general knowledge and management question types.

Variance

Variance analyses showed low variability attributable to individual evaluators (Supplemental Table 14, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). The global model confirmed the presence of significant differences in performance across agents (P<.05). Model estimates showed higher values for Arkangel AI-Deep and OpenEvidence, whereas Medisearch exhibited lower values compared with other agents (Supplemental Table 15, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Discussion

This study compares 4 popular NLP agents that claim to be able to answer medical questions as support in clinical and research scenarios. These comparisons were based on the assessment of clinical-field experts, whose educated judgment was used as the reference standard. The researchers established 8 validity and quality criteria, based on essential EBM (Evidence Based Medicine items, to guide the experts' critical appraisal of agents’ responses. These responses resulted from clinical questions established as a follow-up to clinical vignettes fabricated by orthopedics, pediatrics, gynecology, and psychiatry specialists. Our results are based on and applicable to noncritical, outpatient scenarios.

Time

There was an opposite association between high-quality performing agents and time, which was compatible with greater latency and more comprehensive, referenced responses. In-depth explanations in LLM chatbots increase the computational time required to respond, a trade-off between speed and quality.29

Response time varied across agents and interaction modes. Medisearch showed the shortest median time to response completion (18 seconds), followed by ChatGPT in Personalized and Regular modes (30 seconds each), OpenEvidence (45 seconds), Arkangel AI-Regular (63 seconds), and Arkangel AI-Personalized (68 seconds). Longer response times were observed for Arkangel AI-Deep (approximately 7 minutes) and ChatGPT-Deep (approximately 13 minutes).

However, in everyday practice, agile interactions may be valued at the expense of exhaustiveness, and depth may prevail in academic or research settings despite longer response times.30

Expert Assessment: Specialty and Type of Question

Satisfaction levels by specialty were higher for gynecology and psychiatry cases. Pediatrics showed the lowest, particularly in C3, C4, and C6. These differences reflect performance heterogeneity across clinical areas, likely linked to unequal representation of training data for pediatric populations.17 It also evidences the need for specialty-specific evaluations in under-represented domains.31

All models adequately approached clinical management Q&As, some of them research-type. Diagnosis and general knowledge had lower satisfaction. This suggests that agents are better at synthesizing practical recommendations (eg, clinical guidelines) than addressing diagnostic uncertainty, which agrees with Q&A database agent evaluations demonstrating differential performance in over-represented clinical domains.32

Criteria Distribution and Hallucination

Correct information and agreement with consensus were observed across most models, confirming their ability to retrieve and synthesize information accurately.33 Criteria 7, real references, was the weakest, with extreme agent differences. This variability is consistent with the evidence on fabricated citations or unverifiable links in generalist models, undermining user trust and highlighting the need for evaluations focused on sources’ authenticity.34,35 Models that systematically included references achieved higher satisfaction and did not hallucinate (100% Arkangel AI-Deep, OpenEvidence, and ChatGPT-Deep), whereas those that frequently omitted them had the highest dissatisfaction and hallucinations (ChatGPT-Regular, 93.8% [120/128 Q&A pairs]; ChatGPT-Personalized 75% [96/128]). This suggests that bibliographic support is key to the perceived quality of responses and confirms intermodel variability of LLM chatbots in clinical contexts. Accordingly, recent studies confirm that factors such as the bibliography used for training and clinical proficiency determine the quality of responses.36,37

Strengths

The study design enables performance analysis of multiple CAs under equivalent conditions. Including different specialties and question types broadens the findings’ applicability. In addition, expert evaluation using standardized criteria adds rigor and consistency. Having 2 independent evaluators per specialty strengthened the reliability of the assessments and allowed the validation of inter-rater variability through mixed-effects modeling. Incorporating analysis by question types allows the identification of performance patterns. Including different user modes captured variability across configurations, providing better comprehension of the agents’ capabilities and limitations. Finally, evaluated criteria cover aspects that physicians increasingly recognize as critical when using AI: accuracy, calibration, and truthfulness; risk of hallucinations; fairness; workflow efficiency; and user-centered acceptability. Considering the growing need to ensure truthful, transparent, and verifiable responses, criteria C7 and C8 were added, because it was deemed that NLP agents must be able to understand the basic context of their users, especially in a society marked by violence and polarization.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The evaluation was based on 4 clinical vignettes, resulting in 128 Q&A pairs (32 questions × 4 agents/modes). Although this sample size was sufficient to support overall descriptive comparisons, it limits the ability to draw generalizable conclusions regarding performance differences across medical specialties (orthopedics, pediatrics, gynecology, and psychiatry). Subgroup analyses by specialty, question type, and agent mode should therefore be interpreted with caution due to the limited number of observations within each stratum. Future studies should expand the number and diversity of clinical vignettes to enable more robust stratified analyses.

The clinical scenarios were fictitious and primarily reflected outpatient-style cases. Although they adhered to national and international clinical guidelines and were designed to simulate real-world practice, they were not formally validated to ensure representativeness across all clinical domains. Consequently, the findings may not be generalizable to emergency settings, intensive care environments, longitudinal disease management, or complex multimorbidity scenarios. In addition, only 4 medical specialties were included, and performance across other clinically relevant fields—such as internal medicine, surgery, oncology, intensive care, or primary care—was not assessed and may differ substantially.

The evaluation tool, while informed by established medical AI evaluation frameworks, was developed specifically for this study and was not independently validated. Its use should therefore be interpreted as a structured support for expert clinical judgment rather than as a validated measurement instrument. Moreover, no qualitative appraisal of the cited references was performed, limiting assessment of their clinical relevance beyond verification of authenticity.

Response times were recorded as reported by each system without full environmental standardization, and remote participation prevented complete control over technical or network-related variability. Finally, part of the research team is affiliated with Arkangel AI, which may raise concerns regarding potential conflicts of interest. To mitigate this, all evaluations were conducted by independent, blinded clinical experts, and all data, prompts, and model responses are made publicly available to support transparency and independent replication.

Conclusion

Our evaluation of 4 LLM chatbots showed substantial variability across 8 validity criteria, as assessed by expert clinicians. Differences were most notable in response correctness, agreement with clinical consensus, patient safety, and reference verification. These results support the need for standardized, multidimensional evaluation of medical LLM chatbots and show that expert-based assessment can complement benchmark-driven approaches. Within this exploratory study, the findings provide comparative, expert-validated evidence to inform cautious integration of LLM chatbots into health care.
