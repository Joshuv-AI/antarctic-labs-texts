On the existence conditions of interaction indices in response surface models
Authors: Erhan Yumuk, Clara Ionescu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResponse-surface models (RSMs) are widely used to characterize the combined effects of two agents and to classify their interaction as additivity, synergy, or antagonism. In this context, an interaction index is frequently used as a quantitative measure. However, estimation of the interaction index outside feasible ranges may result in complex-valued or biologically implausible predictions in certain regions of the input domain, thereby limiting the validity of the model. This paper introduces a feasibility analysis framework that distinguishes between isobole-level feasibility (i.e. the existence of well-defined isoboles at a given effect level) and global feasibility (i.e. well-posedness across the entire domain). The analysis explicitly characterizes the singular sets that arise when feasibility conditions are violated, thereby explaining when and how models fail. The approach is demonstrated on three canonical models, i.e. Greco, Minto, and Finney, and supported by numerical illustrations, offering practical guidance for systematic and robust parameter selection in drug combination studies, toxicology, and process engineering.Keywords: Response surface models, Interaction index, Feasibility analysis, Singular sets, Nonlinear systems

Introduction

Response surface models (RSMs) provide a versatile framework for analyzing the combined effects of multiple agents across diverse domains, including pharmacology, toxicology, environmental sciences, and process engineering [1–3]. By linking agent doses to observed responses, such models enable systematic assessment of additivity, synergy, and antagonism. Foundational concepts such as Loewe additivity [4] and Bliss independence [5] laid the theoretical groundwork for combination assessment, while more advanced formulations including the Greco [6], Minto [7], Plummer [8], and Finney models [9], extend these ideas by introducing explicit interaction indices and flexible isobole geometries. These advanced formulations have become standard for interpreting experimental data and guiding rational design in combination studies.

The Hill equation, as one of the earliest and most influential models in quantitative pharmacology, underpins many of these frameworks and has been extensively studied both from a theoretical and a systems biology perspective [10–13]. Beyond its classical use, it has also inspired modern interaction models and extensions into systems biology and nonlinear dynamics [14]. Moreover, its role as a building block in more complex response surface formulations has provided a bridge between fundamental biochemical principles and practical modeling of drug interactions. In particular, RSMs have become standard tools in anesthesia research [15, 16] and are increasingly used to analyze multi-drug combinations in preclinical and clinical studies [17, 18]. More recent developments have further expanded their scope into computational pharmacology, including drug–drug and drug–target interaction prediction [19, 20], as well as physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling [21], highlighting the growing relevance of these models for data integration and predictive medicine. Beyond biomedicine, RSM has also found widespread applications in process engineering, including optimization of ash digestion processes [22] and biosorption studies [23], underscoring its versatility as a general multivariable modeling framework.

Despite its broad usability, several limitations of traditional formulations have been recognized. In particular, log-agent effect models introduced by Plummer [8] and Finney [9], and later extended by Kong [24], rely on the assumption of constant relative potency, which is often overly restrictive. Furthermore, representing agent interaction with a single parameter can be inadequate when synergy, additivity, and antagonism coexist in different regions of the dose–response surface. These challenges have motivated the development of more flexible approaches, such as dose-dependent interaction functions that can better capture heterogeneous behaviors across the drug combination space. Similar concerns have also been raised in applied domains such as pharmaceutical development, where response surface methodology has been employed as a practical optimization tool [25].

Despite their widespread adoption, RSMs can exhibit mathematical infeasibility in certain parameter regimes. In particular, inappropriate values of the interaction index may yield unfeasible regions or singular sets, limiting the interpretability and predictive value of the model. Although such phenomena have been noted in practice, a systematic framework for characterizing feasibility across different model classes has been lacking. As these models are increasingly applied across domains including drug development, toxicology, and process engineering, a deeper understanding of their feasibility properties has become essential for reliable application.

The present work addresses this gap by developing a feasibility analysis framework for response-surface interaction models. We distinguish between isobole-level feasibility (existence of valid isoboles at a given effect level) and global feasibility (well-posedness over the entire input domain). For each of the three models, i.e. Greco, Minto, and Finney, we characterize the conditions under which feasible solutions exist and explicitly identify the singular sets that arise when feasibility is violated. Numerical illustrations confirm the theoretical results and provide practical guidance for robust parameter selection and model interpretation.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section “Methods” reviews the response surface models considered in this study. Moreover, presents the feasibility analysis, including theorems and corollaries for each model. Section “Results” provides numerical examples illustrating feasible, boundary, and infeasible scenarios. Section “Discussion” discusses the main findings of the paper and outlines directions for future work.

Discussion

This work presented a systematic feasibility analysis framework for response surface interaction models, with the aim of clarifying under which structural and parametric conditions these models yield biologically interpretable responses. By explicitly distinguishing between isobole-level feasibility and global feasibility, the proposed framework provides a structured way to assess whether model predictions remain well-defined locally at fixed effect levels or across the entire input domain.

The model-specific analyses demonstrate that feasibility is not an inherent property of response surface formulations, but rather a consequence of the interplay between model structure and interaction parameters. For the Greco model, the results indicate that global feasibility is guaranteed only under non-antagonistic interaction regimes. While the model can formally represent both synergistic and antagonistic interactions, antagonism restricts feasibility to limited regions of the dose–effect surface, with singularity lines emerging within the positive quadrant. This highlights that practical use of the Greco formulation in pharmacological or clinical settings requires careful parameter selection to ensure biological interpretability over the relevant dose range.

A similar sensitivity to interaction strength is observed for the Minto model. Moderate interaction parameters preserve global feasibility, whereas stronger interactions induce singularities in both normalized and input domains. These singular structures reveal intrinsic limitations of the formulation and identify parameter regimes where biologically interpretable responses can no longer be guaranteed. Consequently, reliable application of the Minto model necessitates avoiding interaction regimes that give rise to such singular behavior. For the Finney model, feasibility is governed by the positivity of the logarithmic argument. While the model remains globally feasible for moderate interaction parameters, stronger antagonism introduces singular boundaries and regions with complex-valued responses. This observation underscores that, although the Finney formulation is flexible enough to capture both synergistic and antagonistic interactions, excessive antagonism fundamentally limits its domain of biological validity. The numerical illustrations support the analytical findings and demonstrate how feasibility boundaries manifest in practice. Importantly, these examples emphasize that RSMs may yield well-defined and biologically interpretable responses only within specific parameter regimes, even when the underlying mathematical expressions appear formally valid. This reinforces the need for explicit feasibility analysis when applying such models to PKPD and related applications.

The choice of Hill coefficient values in the numerical examples also merits discussion. In this study, Hill coefficients were selected for illustrative purposes to expose feasibility boundaries and to highlight the effect of increasing nonlinearity on response surface behavior. In practical PKPD applications, however, Hill coefficients are typically constrained to physiologically plausible ranges, as larger values may indicate atypical or problematic binding behavior. This distinction clarifies that the numerical examples are intended to support methodological insights rather than to represent specific pharmacological binding mechanisms.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that feasibility analysis provides a valuable complement to traditional response surface modeling approaches. By explicitly identifying parameter regimes that preserve biological interpretability and excluding regions associated with singular or complex-valued responses, the proposed framework offers practical guidance for model selection, parameter tuning, and interpretation.

Finally, while this study focused on two-agent interaction models, an important direction for future research is the extension of the proposed feasibility framework to multi-agent systems involving three or more inputs. Such extensions would require reformulating interaction terms and feasibility conditions in higher-dimensional domains, posing both mathematical challenges and practical opportunities for combination therapy and other complex multi-agent applications.
