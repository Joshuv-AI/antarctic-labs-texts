Triangulated evidence from the UK and China reveals the health impact of vegetarianism
Authors: Chen Zhu, Xiaosong Yang, Yanjun Song, Wenyan Xu, Jiakai Gong, Xiaolu Wang, Wei Si, Shenggen Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVegetarianism is widely recognised for its health and environmental benefits. However, its broader impacts on physical, mental and social well-being remain underexplored. This study investigates the health and behavioural outcomes associated with vegetarianism across diverse populations.MethodsWe analysed polygenic scores for vegetarianism (VegPGS) in 495 971 UK Biobank participants and performed phenome-wide association studies (PheWAS) on 443 health and behavioural traits. Cross-validation analyses were conducted using data from 9009 vegetarians to 486 962 non-vegetarians. One-sample and two-sample Mendelian randomisation (MR) analyses explored causal relationships. Findings were further validated in 11 642 participants from the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS). Additionally, machine-learning classification models were developed to predict vegetarian status using behavioural, physiological and genetic factors.FindingsPheWAS identified 65 health-related and 2 behaviour-related factors significantly associated with VegPGS, with cross-validation confirming these links. MR analyses supported causal effects of vegetarianism on lower basal metabolic rate, reduced body mass index (BMI), decreased fat mass and lower risk of type 2 diabetes. CHNS data confirmed associations with lower BMI and diabetes risk in East Asian populations. Machine-learning models achieved high accuracy in predicting vegetarian status (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve=0.913±0.018, area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC)=0.40).InterpretationThis study provides robust evidence supporting the metabolic health benefits of vegetarianism. The integration of multimodal genetic, behavioural and physiological data enhances understanding and prediction of dietary choices, offering valuable insights for policymakers and individuals considering a transition to plant-based diets to achieve sustainability.Keywords: Dietary patterns, Diabetes mellitus, Biomarker

Introduction

Vegetarianism is a dietary pattern associated with significant benefits for human health and environmental sustainability.1 2 A plant-based diet is linked to reduced risks of chronic diseases, including cardiovascular conditions and some cancers, while also contributing to lower greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and sustainable resource use.3 However, the broader impact of vegetarianism extends beyond these health outcomes, affecting various aspects of physical, mental and social well-being.

Existing research on vegetarianism has largely focused on specific health outcomes, such as reduced risks of cardiovascular disease (CVD), diabetes and colorectal cancer, but it has also reported potential adverse effects, such as the link with an increased risk of depression.4,8 While these studies provide valuable insights, they often rely on observational methods that are prone to confounders and may lack the rigour required to establish causal relationships.8 Moreover, the effects of vegetarianism on psychological well-being, social interactions and micronutrient deficiencies remain underexplored, which limits our understanding of the true breadth of vegetarianism’s impact.

A more comprehensive exploration that integrates causal inference and considers a broader set of health and behavioural factors is needed to deepen our understanding of vegetarianism. By broadening the scope, researchers can identify both positive and potentially adverse effects, providing a clearer, more nuanced perspective on how a vegetarian diet affects individual and public health. Additionally, incorporating economic and behavioural theories could offer valuable insights into the decision-making processes that underlie dietary choices related to vegetarianism. For instance, the random utility theory (RUT) explains individual choice by modelling decision-making as a process where individuals choose the option that maximises their perceived utility or satisfaction.9 In this theoretical framework, utility can be first constructed by demographic, socioeconomic, behavioural, physiological, genetic and even multiomic factors and then used to predict individual choices. However, applications on individuals’ decision-making behaviour related to dietary choices have been scarce.10

This study aims to address these gaps by systematically investigating a wide array of health, physiological and behaviour-related outcomes associated with vegetarianism using a triangulation framework. The goal is to provide a holistic understanding of the implications of vegetarian diets, offering insights for nutritionists, policymakers and individuals considering a transition to plant-based diets to achieve sustainability. The analysis is grounded in robust causal inference and machine-learning methodologies, addressing limitations of prior observational studies. In particular, the triangulation approach strengthens causal inference by requiring that effects be statistically significant and directionally consistent across multiple independent datasets and complementary identification strategies.11 12

Using data from the UK Biobank (UKB; N=495 971), we conducted a phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) to explore the relationships between genetic predisposition for vegetarianism—captured through polygenic scores (polygenic score for vegetarianism; VegPGS)—and 443 health/physiological and behavioural traits. We validated these associations at the phenotypic level using multivariate linear and logistic regression analyses. Furthermore, we applied both one-sample and two-sample Mendelian randomisation (MR) to investigate potential causal relationships between vegetarianism and the identified health and behaviour-related outcomes. To enhance the generalisability of our findings, we examined significant causal associations in East Asian populations using an independent cohort from the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS; N=11 642). Finally, to better understand the decision-making processes underlying individual dietary choices, we developed machine-learning classification models incorporating behavioural, physiological and genetic factors based on the RUT to improve the prediction accuracy of individual dietary choices related to vegetarianism. Methods

Study samples

We analysed two independent cohorts: the UKB (>5 00 000 adults recruited 2006–2010 across England, Scotland and Wales; NHS NRES 11/NW/0382) and the CHNS (ongoing household-based panel initiated in 1989 across multiple provinces).13 In UKB, baseline questionnaires, physical measures and biospecimens were obtained under written consent; after excluding 7848 records with implausible dietary responses, 495 971 participants remained.14,18 In CHNS, participants with a valid dietary assessment in 2004 and ≥1 health assessment during 2006–2011 were included (n=11 642); ethics approvals and consent were obtained from UNC–Chapel Hill and the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).19 20

Measurement of vegetarianism

Vegetarian status was coded as a binary variable from food-frequency data. Participants reporting no intake of processed meat, beef, pork, lamb/mutton, poultry, fish, seafood or other meats were classified as vegetarians, yielding 9009 vegetarians and 486 962 non-vegetarians in UKB, and 2658 and 8984, respectively, in CHNS.

Polygenic score for vegetarianism

A VegPGS was computed in UKB using published genome-wide association studies (GWAS) weights and standard clumping (p<5×10⁻⁶; r²<0.01 within 10 000 kb).21 Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) dosages were coded 0/1/2 and combined as a weighted sum; higher values indicate greater genetic propensity towards vegetarianism (mean=−0.572, SD=0.739).

Measurement of outcomes

We examined 443 phenotypes in UKB: 391 health/physiological traits (136 diseases; 10 blood biomarkers; 44 physical measures; 4 telomere phenotypes; 197 nuclear magnetic resonance NMR metabolomic biomarkers) and 52 behaviour-related traits spanning sociodemographics, lifestyles, mental well-being and early-life factors (online supplemental table 1). We identified the diagnosis of diseases by using the International Classification of Diseases-Tenth Revision codes. It is worth noting that vegetarian status (exposure) was recorded at baseline, and 136 disease outcomes were constructed from linked hospital inpatient records in UKB. We, therefore, analysed incident events during follow-up to reduce reverse-causation concerns (ie, pre-existing disease influencing diet at baseline).

Analytical strategies (triangulation)

We combined three complementary layers:

PheWAS: associations between VegPGS and each phenotype were tested in R V.4.4.1, adjusting for age, sex, the first 10 genetic principal components and 11 regional fixed effects (East Midlands; East of England; London; North East; North West; Scotland; South East; South West; Wales; West Midlands; Yorkshire and The Humber) derived from residential coordinates to absorb geographic/cultural heterogeneity. Two-sided tests used Bonferroni correction (α/443). Sample sizes varied by phenotype (≈28 582–458 709).

Machine-learning algorithms

To examine discriminative signatures of vegetarianism and minimizing overfitting, we trained XGBoost classifiers on a feature set reduced from 401 to 43 predictors via 100 bootstrap LASSO cycles (features retained if selected in >50% iterations).22 Tenfold cross-validation evaluated performance using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) and area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC); SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values quantified feature contributions (online supplemental tables 6–7).23

Among behavioural phenotypes, higher VegPGS was associated with a higher probability of having a college degree (p= 6.18×10−8) and higher household income (p= 4.49×10−5) that remained significant after Bonferroni correction. We did not find significant associations between VegPGS and early life factors, lifestyle factors or mental well-being after corrections for multiple hypothesis testing.

In health-related phenotypes, VegPGS demonstrated Bonferroni-significant associations with 28 physical measures, 2 blood chemistry biomarkers, 5 disease outcomes and 30 NMR metabolites. In specific, higher VegPGS was associated with lower basal metabolic rate (BMR) (p= 2.05×10−7), which measures the minimum number of calories that an individual’s body needs to perform necessary functions. Besides, VegPGS showed an identical negative effect direction with 27 fat mass-related phenotypes, including weight, BMI, arm fat mass and percentages, leg fat mass and percentages, trunk fat mass and percentage as well as whole body fat mass and fat-free mass (p=2.29×10−11 to 6.64×10−6).

For disease outcomes, higher VegPGS was associated with lower risks of all-cause death (p=5.14×10−5), obesity (p= 2.62×10−5), lower respiratory infections (p=1.16×10−5), non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (p=6.58×10−7) and volume depletion (p=4.57×10−5).

Regarding blood and metabolic biomarkers, VegPGS positively correlated with white blood cell count and several lipid-related NMR metabolites, such as concentration of large high-density lipoprotein (HDL) particles, free cholesterol in large HDL, and phospholipids in very large HDL. Conversely, it was associated with lower glucose levels in both blood chemistry and NMR metabolomics.

Online supplemental table 3 offers detailed results. Most associations identified in PheWAS were confirmed in the cross-validation analysis, demonstrating consistent effect directions. For example, vegetarianism was associated with a higher probability of having a college degree, reduced risks of obesity and non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus, lower BMR and lower fat mass-related phenotypes.

However, discrepancies were observed regarding the logarithm of household income, white blood cell and several lipid-related NMR metabolites (eg, free cholesterol in large HDL), where case–control analysis indicated negative associations with vegetarianism, diverging from PheWAS findings. These inconsistencies underscore the complexity of the interactions between genetic predisposition and phenotypic expressions of vegetarian dietary patterns. Further investigation into these discrepancies is warranted to clarify the causality and underlying mechanisms.

MR results on vegetarianism and health/behavioural outcomes

To further investigate the causal impacts of vegetarianism, we first conducted one-sample MR analyses on 56 phenotypes, significant in both PheWAS and cross-validation, using two-stage least squares. Out of these, 34 outcomes reached significance (p<0.05), with 26 remaining significant after Bonferroni correction (p<8.93×10−4; online supplemental table 4). Despite the modest variance explained by the VegPGS, first-stage strength was adequate: median first-stage F statistic=19.2, above the F≥10 threshold for reliable causal inference. Estimates for the 26 traits exceeding this benchmark can, therefore, be interpreted as potentially causal. Conversely, all 22 NMR-metabolomic traits were studied in a smaller subset (≈ 256 000), yielding F ≈ 3.9 and non-significant MR estimates; these results are exploratory.

To explore potential heterogeneous effects of vegetarianism, we also conducted sex-stratified one-sample MR estimates (online supplemental tables 8-9).24 These additional results revealed directionally consistent effects across males and females, but with larger magnitudes and tighter CIs among women. The male stratum, which comprised ~45% of the analytic sample, showed attenuated precision, consistent with reduced power rather than qualitative divergence. These subgroup analyses followed the same instrument construction and covariate specification used in the pooled models, satisfying STROBE-MR reporting standards.

To explore potential sex-specific heterogeneity,24 we reestimated all one-sample MR models separately for women and men (online supplemental tables 8-9). Effect directions were concordant across sexes, yet estimates for women were generally larger and associated with tighter CIs. By contrast, effect sizes in the male stratum—a group that accounts for roughly 45% of our analytic sample—remained similar in magnitude but displayed wider intervals, a pattern consistent with reduced statistical power rather than a true absence of effect. Nevertheless, the convergence in effect direction across strata reinforces the overall causal signal, while the diminished precision in men cautions against over-interpreting null findings in that subgroup.

Next, we performed two-sample MR analyses for all 26 phenotypes that reached statistical significance after adjusting for multiple comparison in one-sample MR estimation. After accounting for heterogeneity and pleiotropy, 22 associations remained significant, confirming robust causal relationships (figure 3c). All robust associations pertained to health/physiological phenotypes. Detailed estimation results from the random-effects IVW method (robust to the presence of heterogeneity), heterogeneity tests with Cochran’s Q statistics and MR-Egger intercept tests for horizontal pleiotropy are presented in online supplemental table 5. Scatter plots and LOO plots are presented in online supplemental figures 1-26.

Notably, vegetarianism showed strong protective effects on non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (OR=0.88, p=5.70×10−6), lower BMI (β=−0.0146, p=0.0439), lower weight (β=−0.0405, p=0.0306) and lower BMR (β=−0.0088, p=0.0327), along with reduced fat mass across 18 measures. However, no causal connections were found with educational attainment or obesity, challenging earlier observational findings. Additionally, no significant causal effects were noted on blood glucose or lipid-related NMR metabolites, suggesting prior inconsistency associations were likely spurious due to endogeneity bias.25 This comprehensive approach, integrating one-sample and two-sample MR analyses, alongside rigorous sensitivity tests, strengthens the robustness of the findings while acknowledging the complexity of health outcomes linked to vegetarianism.

Vegetarianism and health-related outcomes in East Asians

To evaluate the generalisability of our main findings, we analysed the associations of vegetarianism with diabetes and BMI using available data from the CHNS cohort, which includes 2658 vegetarians and 8984 non-vegetarians. Table 3 summarises demographic characteristics (51.2% female; mean age: 48.5 (20.0) years). Unlike the UKB cohort, CHNS vegetarians were less educated than their non-vegetarian counterparts (average years of schooling: 5.3 vs 7.5). Regression analyses revealed significant negative associations of vegetarianism with diabetes (β=−0.7766, OR=0.46, p=0.001) and BMI (β=−0.2353, p=0.020). These consistent findings provide triangulated evidence supporting our main results and enhance their applicability across different ethnic groups, reinforcing the potential global health benefits of vegetarianism.

Machine-learning classification models for vegetarianism

To differentiate vegetarians from non-vegetarians, we developed machine-learning classification models using behavioural, physiological and genetic factors. We initially considered 401 features (online supplemental table 6), and through 100 replications of LASSO regression with resampled training set, we selected 43 key predictors that were included in more than 50% of the models (online supplemental table 7). These included 11 behavioural factors (eg, college education, current drinking status, family relationship satisfaction), 27 physiological factors (eg, creatinine levels, hand grip strength, red blood cell count) and 5 genetic factors (eg, polygenic scores for height, educational attainment, rs7012814).

Three XGBoost classification models were constructed: (1) a baseline model using only behavioural factors, (2) a model incorporating behavioural and physiological factors and (3) a full model that also included genetic data. In the training cohort, the ROC-AUC curve for the baseline model was 0.744 (95% CI 0.730 to 0.758) (online supplemental figure 27). Adding physiological factors improved the ROC-AUC to 0.928 (95% CI 0.921 to 0.935) (online supplemental figure 28) and the full model incorporating genetic factors achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.932 (95% CI 0.925 to 0.939, figure 4a). In the validation cohort, the full model still exhibited strong predictive power, with an ROC-AUC of 0.913 (95% CI 0.896 to 0.931, figure 4a), compared with 0.728 (95% CI 0.699 to 0.756) (online supplemental figure 27) for the baseline model and 0.910 (95% CI 0.892 to 0.927) (online supplemental figure 28) for the model with behavioural and physiological factors.

Model performance was also evaluated with precision–recall (PR) curves, which are less prone than ROC-AUC to optimistic inflation when the positive class is rare.26 In the validation cohort, PR-AUC rose from 0.06 (baseline) to 0.37 (behavioural+physiological covariates) and 0.40 (full model), far exceeding the random expectation of 0.02 at our class prevalence (online supplemental figures 29-31). The SHAP analysis further identified key predictors, with the top five being the omega-6 to omega-3 fatty acids ratio, creatinine levels, docosahexaenoic acid percentage, glycine levels and college education (figure 4b).

Overall, the full classification model demonstrated strong discrimination performance.26 27 The combination of stringent feature selection and class-appropriate metrics indicates that the final model’s predictive signal reflects genuine, generalisable associations rather than over-fit noise, confirming that vegetarian status can be reliably predicted by a compact set of behavioural, physiological and genetic factors.

Discussion

This study integrates phenome-wide screening, cross-validated case–control analyses, and MR across two culturally distinct cohorts to appraise vegetarianism’s health and behavioural correlates. Several findings stand out. First, we observe robust protective associations with type 2 diabetes, lower BMI and reduced fat mass across multiple body regions. These patterns accord with the metabolic advantages of high-quality plant-forward eating and, crucially, are reinforced by convergent MR signals and replication in an East Asian cohort.28 29 Taken together, the triangulated evidence moves beyond simple correlation, suggesting that vegetarianism—when practised as a nutrient-dense, plant-rich pattern—contributes to more favourable metabolic profiles across different genetic and dietary contexts. Our classification experiments further indicate that adding physiological and genetic features materially improves prediction of vegetarian status, implying that dietary choice is shaped not only by preferences and constraints but also by underlying biological dispositions and phenotypic correlates.

The causal association with lower BMR is notable. A lower BMR implies fewer calories are required to maintain essential physiological functions, consistent with energy-economy adaptations sometimes observed in plant-leaning dietary patterns.30 Such adaptation may partially account for the lower adiposity signals we detect and has practical implications: individuals transitioning to vegetarian diets may benefit from counselling to avoid energy imbalance and unintended weight change, with simple monitoring of weight, appetite and fatigue during early adoption.

By contrast, our triangulated analyses yielded disciplined null findings for cancer and CVD. The broader literature is mixed—some cohorts report lower colorectal cancer risk or reduced coronary events among vegetarians, whereas others (including EPIC-Oxford) have documented higher haemorrhagic stroke incidence alongside lower ischaemic heart disease.45 7 31,34 Against this backdrop, the convergence of null results across PheWAS screening, adjusted case–control models and MR suggests that within our data, any protective effects on cancer or CVD are smaller, outcome-specific and context-dependent. Several nominal associations did not survive multiple-testing correction; maintaining stringent thresholds is essential to avoid overinterpreting chance findings in high-dimensional analyses. Similarly, we find no causal links between vegetarianism and mental-health outcomes despite some reports of elevated depression or anxiety risk in subgroups.8 35 Heterogeneity in dietary quality, motives for meat avoidance, social contexts and reverse causation (eg, appetite changes with mood) may underlie prior discrepancies.

A defining feature of our design is triangulation across two distinct ‘evidence environments’. Rather than presuming universal, transportable effect sizes, we anticipate heterogeneity and interpret it. The UK and Chinese cohorts differ in religious traditions, food environments (eg, staple legumes and soy foods vs processed meat substitutes) and potential social-desirability pressures around meat avoidance. Where estimates align across these contexts, confidence in causal interpretation is strengthened because the shared signal has survived different confounding architectures. Where estimates diverge, we treat the discrepancy as evidence of effect modification by culture, environment or baseline diet rather than as a failure of replication.

Several limitations warrant emphasis. Although we implemented one-sample and two-sample MR, IVW with random effects, MR-Egger, MR-PRESSO, leave-one-out checks and related sensitivity tests, residual horizontal pleiotropy cannot be excluded. MR instruments were generally strong but weaker for a subset of NMR-metabolomic traits; we, therefore, interpret metabolomic nulls as exploratory. Both cohorts are selective: UKB participants are healthier and more affluent than the national average, and CHNS oversamples rural and periurban communities. Our aims are aetiological rather than descriptive, so internal validity takes precedence; nevertheless, absolute effect sizes may differ in population-representative samples. Dietary exposure was ascertained by single time point food frequency questionnaires (FFQs), which are vulnerable to recall error, day-to-day variation and social desirability bias. Such non-differential misclassification would be expected to attenuate true associations towards the null; even so, residual bias could modestly influence precision and should be borne in mind. Replication across additional ethnic, socioeconomic and dietary settings will be important for establishing generalisability.

In conclusion, this study advances our understanding of the health and behavioural impacts of vegetarianism by integrating multimodal health, physiological, behavioural and genetic data. Public-health guidance should, therefore, shift from binary labels to nutrient-aware, plant-forward messages that prioritise whole grains, legumes, nuts, vegetables and fruit, while explicitly addressing potential shortfalls in vitamin B12, iron, iodine and calcium. Embedding simple screening and supplementation protocols into routine care can prevent deficiency, particularly among older adults and women of childbearing age. Finally, outcome-specific heterogeneity—for example, divergent risks for ischaemic heart disease and haemorrhagic stroke—mandates granular monitoring rather than one-size-fits-all prescriptions. Nuanced, quality-focused and outcome-sensitive policies are preferable to blanket endorsements of vegetarian diets.
