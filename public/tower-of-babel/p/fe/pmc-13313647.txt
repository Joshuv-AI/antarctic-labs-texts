Updates in mycobacterial keratitis diagnosis and treatment
Authors: Uma L. Balakrishnan, Ryan T. Wallace, Patricia Chévez-Barrios, Alejandro Arboleda, Masih U. Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mycobacterial keratitis is an uncommon but challenging form of infectious keratitis, often associated with postsurgical eyes. Clinical presentation is variable and may be difficult to recognize, resulting in delays in diagnosis and challenges in management. Traditional diagnostic techniques from corneal scrapings are limited by slow organism growth and may yield negative results. Treatment is challenging due to antimicrobial resistance and typically requires prolonged multidrug therapy. Advances in diagnostic modalities, antimicrobial therapies, surgical approaches, and adjunctive treatments such as corneal crosslinking have improved clinicians’ ability to mitigate or avoid severe visual consequences. Despite these developments, optimal management strategies remain incompletely defined, and outcomes continue to vary widely. This review highlights clinical presentations and diagnostic techniques, including slit-lamp images and microbiologic findings from patients at our institution, and discusses emerging technologies such as polymerase chain reaction. We also review the existing literature on NTM keratitis to inform clinical practice, with a focus on medical therapies, surgical management, and newer photodynamic treatment approaches. This review aims to provide a comprehensive framework to support earlier diagnosis and guide more effective management of NTM keratitis.

INTRODUCTION

Atypical mycobacterial keratitis is a rare but clinically challenging form of infectious keratitis caused by a diverse group of nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM).[123] NTM are characterized by speed of growth, where Runyon Group I–III include the slow-growing forms (17% of ocular NTM infections) and the more prevalent, fast-growing Group IV (e.g., Mycobacterium fortuitum, Mycobacterium abscessus, and Mycobacterium chelonae) (83% of ocular NTM infections).[4] The incidence of NTM keratitis depends on geographic location, but overall is low, accounting for 1.1%–7.9% of all causes of infectious keratitis at two large referral centers.[5] Exposure to organic material and corneal manipulation during surgery are commonly associated with the introduction of NTM bacteria into the corneal stroma. The diversity of organisms results in nonspecific findings, allowing the infection to masquerade as another type of keratitis and often delaying diagnosis and treatment. Difficulty culturing and antibiotic resistance further complicate the management of this disease. This review describes the current landscape in management of this challenging form of infectious keratitis, from diagnosis to medical, laser-based, and surgical interventions.

CLINICAL FEATURES

As in other forms of keratitis, patients may present initially with nonspecific complaints of foreign body sensation, eye redness, and photophobia. These symptoms are often subacute (between 1 and 3 months) in onset after exposure to organic material or surgical intervention.[26] NTM has been reported after a wide variety of keratorefractive surgeries (i.e., laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis [LASIK] and Keratorefractive Lenticule Extraction [KLEX]), cataract extraction, and endothelial and penetrating keratoplasty (PKP).[57] NTM keratitis may also be associated with contact lens use.[8]

NTM keratitis presents clinically in a variety of ways, making diagnosis especially difficult. The classic appearance is that of a “cracked windshield,” with feathery or grainy infiltrates radiating from a central ulcer with minimal epithelial defect.[59] However, long-standing infections or severe cases may present in more atypical patterns, often mimicking other forms of infectious keratitis with deep stromal infiltrates, satellite lesions, or pseudodendritic epithelial defects [Figure 1].[10] Ulceration adjacent to the limbus may also cause concomitant scleritis, further convoluting the clinical picture.[11]

Similar to other forms of microbial keratitis in post-LASIK eyes, mycobacterial keratitis may present at the flap and stromal interface, often later in the postsurgical course compared to other forms of keratitis.[11] However, even in these “typical” postsurgical NTM patients, the infection appearance has been described in a variety of ways, including a single lesion, multiple “satellite” lesions, or mimicking the feathery appearance of infectious crystalline keratopathy.[12] In the case of KLEX, diffuse flap edema, a pocket abscess, and intrastromal neovascularization have also been described.[7]

DIAGNOSTIC MODALITIES

Clinical suspicion should remain high in postsurgical cases for NTM keratitis, and imaging, including slit-lamp photos, microphotography, anterior segment optical coherence tomography, and confocal microscopy, may aid in the diagnosis. However, given the diversity of pathogenic mycobacterial species and the atypical clinical presentation, diagnosis can be challenging. Tissue sampling remains the most sensitive and specific method for distinguishing this form of keratitis from other infectious etiologies and for guiding targeted therapy.[13]

The formal diagnosis of NTM keratitis depends on the depth of involvement and extent of the disease progression. Superficial ulcers may be diagnosed with corneal scraping with direct examination of the tissue under microscopy with slides stained with Gram (Gram variable organism), specific stains including acid-fast specific stains (e.g., Ziehl– Neelsen and Kinyoun), and Fite-Faraco (Wade-Fite) stain, which is a modified acid-fast technique used to detect atypical nontuberculous Mycobacterium. The organisms are bacillary, may be beaded, and tend to interlace together [Figure 2]. The combination of special stains, including Gram, acid-fast bacillus Kinyoun, Fite-Faraco and Grocott’s methenamine silver and/or periodic acid–Schiff, which stains for fungus, may be used to identify nontuberculous versus tuberculous Mycobacterium and these from other bacillary organisms like Nocardia [Table 1]. NTM may also be isolated on culture media, including Lowenstein-Jensen agar, Middlebrook agar, blood, and chocolate agar.[14] Girgis et al. found in a review of 139 patients with culture-confirmed NTM keratitis that 91% of isolates were recovered within 10 days.[4] Although the fast-growing NTM species may grow within a week, culture media should be kept for up to 8 weeks to ensure the growth of slow-growing species.[1516]

For deeper infections, corneal biopsy, therapeutic PKP, or flap amputation in LASIK cases may serve to formally diagnose the condition, which is further discussed in the section “Surgical Approaches.”[1718] Tissue culture or histopathology may be used in processing these tissues, with one study showing equal rates of detection with either method.[18] Characteristic biopsy findings are presented in Figure 2.

Newer diagnostic techniques show promise in improving the diagnosis of NTM. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) has been shown to be sensitive and specific for the diagnosis of NTM infections, although reported performance varies widely, likely reflecting differences in infection prevalence, sample types, and assay methodologies. Ribonucleic acid- and deoxyribonucleic Acid -sequencing techniques have also been used more recently, although their limited availability in routine clinical practice restricts widespread use. Franco et al. revealed that in a study of 721 clinical samples (a wide combination of respiratory and nonrespiratory samples), two separate PCR tests for NTM infections had sensitivities of 28.17% and 21.13%, and specificities of 99.29% and 99.11% when compared to traditional culture.[19] A separate study by Smirnova et al. involving 973 exclusively sputum isolates found the sensitivity and specificity of PCR for NTM isolates was 99.7% and 100%, respectively.[20] However, neither of these studies involved NTM keratitis directly. In a study by Dhiman et al., PCR correctly identified all 16 cases of NTM keratitis, with 10 detected within 24–48 h of testing. In contrast, culture-based identification required several additional days, highlighting the advantage of earlier diagnosis with PCR.[21] However, adoption of PCR for ocular infections remains limited due to the absence of Food and drug administration (FDA)-approved assays for ocular specimens, limited onsite availability, and reliance on send-out testing, which diminishes its advantage over culture in time to diagnosis.[16]

MEDICAL THERAPIES

Medical treatment for NTM keratitis remains highly challenging, given both the resistance to traditional fortified antibiotics and varied resistance among NTM subspecies themselves. Nontuberculous mycobacteria readily develop antimicrobial resistance and can form complex three-dimensional biofilms, both of which contribute to increased virulence and hinder treatment. Multiple drug therapies have been described in attempts to control NTM keratitis, centered around the use of topical amikacin, most commonly with the addition of an oral macrolide such as azithromycin or clarithromycin.[22223] Topical amikacin remains the mainstay of treatment, with rabbit studies showing satisfactory therapeutic levels when administered topically at a concentration of 250 mg/ml (2.5%),[24] and n vitro susceptibility data showing inhibition of 70%–90% of isolates.[142526] However, highly concentrated amikacin is not well tolerated by the ocular surface and injection, chemosis, and even conjunctival necrosis have been observed as a result of topical treatment.[27] Failure rates with amikacin monotherapy may also be as high as 60%.[28] As a result, the concomitant treatment with macrolides is warranted, and both topical or systemic macrolides have been shown to be effective in some cases where the sensitivity data is favorable.[1526] Many case series describe the efficacy of oral clarithromycin (500 mg BID) and azithromycin (500 mg daily), especially in regard to deeper stromal infections.

Fluoroquinolones may also be used in cases of NTM keratitis; however, depending on the subspecies of the NTM causative organism, the efficacy and sensitivity to these agents may be more varied. One large review from cases between 1966 and 2003 showed that quinolones, especially moxifloxacin, had excellent sensitivity against M. chelonae and M. fortuitum, but poorer activity against M. abcsessus or M. massiliense.[25] A more recent study from Bascom Palmer reviewing NTM keratitis from 2008 to 2024 showed that only 14.8% of NTM strains were susceptible to moxifloxacin.[29]

New therapies may be arising in the medical treatment of NTM keratitis and could open treatment avenues previously closed in this difficult disease. Biofilm and nanoparticle targeting methods have shown some promise, with one study showing that the biofilms of M. chelonae and M. fortuitum can be targeted with a novel DNAse treatment, reducing overall resistance when paired with a topical antibiotic.[30] Further, oral clofazimine, tedizolid, and bedaquiline are being investigated for their roles in treating pulmonary NTM and may open avenues to treating the ocular isolates with similar resistance phenotypes.[31] Chang et al. showed 100% susceptibility in 17 NTM organisms tested.

Although the use of topical steroids in other forms of infectious keratitis remains controversial, available data suggest that the early use of topical steroids in NTM keratitis is correlated with worse clinical and visual outcomes. One review of 22 eyes in South Florida showed that of the eyes that failed medical treatment, 64% were treated with steroids. Of those that achieved success with medical treatment alone, 44% were on topical steroids.[26] A large systematic review of 174 articles also showed that steroid use may prolong the duration of infection and result in poorer visual outcomes.[32] As a result, very few case reports have used topical corticosteroids in their treatment protocols, and several have noted resolution of the infectious infiltrate without their use.[3334] Nevertheless, a single case study of a LASIK-associated NTM-positive infection showed that after several weeks of intense topical antibiotic treatments and flap ablation failed to resolve the patient’s infiltrative inflammation, the introduction of topical steroids very rapidly improved the inflammation and overall outcomes.[23] Given the available evidence, topical corticosteroids should not be used in nontuberculous mycobacterial keratitis. If considered, their use should be reserved only after clear evidence that the infection has been clearly eradicated. Thus, given the wide variety of species and resistance to several agents, despite its drawbacks, topical treatment with amikacin as the initial agent, with or without macrolides, remains the most used method to try to treat NTM keratitis. Sensitivity data remains key in seeking the addition of alternative therapies and adjunctive medical treatment.

PHOTODYNAMIC THERAPY AND CORNEAL CROSS-LINKING

In cases of infectious keratitis with poor response to medical therapies, adjunctive photodynamic therapies (PDTs) using a photosensitizing agent (methylene blue, rose bengal, riboflavin) activated by light can be used. This technology has a two-fold effect: (1) decreasing the bacterial load and (2) forming collagen cross-links that strengthen corneal tissue.

The cross-linking protocol developed for treatment of keratoconus (i.e., photoactivated chromophore crosslinking [PACK-CXL] and riboflavin photodynamic antimicrobial therapy [PDAT]) has been reported to be an effective method to modulating treatment for infectious keratitis associated with several organisms. A few cases of successful treatment of refractory, post-LASIK NTM keratitis with riboflavin PACK-CXL (ultraviolet-A) have been reported, where therapeutic PKP was avoided.[353637] Kymionis et al. included treatment with limited phototherapeutic keratectomy; however, further studies are necessary to determine whether this additional treatment provides benefit.[37] Another case of a presumed post-PKP suture-associated NTM keratitis was treated with adjunctive riboflavin PDAT and avoided graft failure or repeat PKP and was reported to have a clear corneal graft at 3-year follow-up.[38]

Limited laboratory studies have shown bacteriostatic and bactericidal effects with PDAT/PACK-CXL protocols treating NTM. Shih et al. demonstrated a significant 2-log reduction in corneal bacterial load in vitro in rabbit eyes treated with PDT using methylene blue (wavelength 650 nm) with amikacin compared to the amikacin-only treated eyes.[39] Trevizani Rocchetti et al. demonstrated that treatment with rose Bengal and a custom green light-emitting diode (wavelength 515 nm) inhibited M. chelonae and M. abscessus growth in vitro.[40] Further studies, ideally including randomized clinical trials, are needed, although the low incidence of atypical mycobacterial keratitis makes this challenging. Current evidence is limited to laboratory studies and case reports, but PDT and corneal cross-linking may emerge as important adjunctive treatments with further investigation. Given the limited data and the potentially devastating consequences of undertreated NTM keratitis, PDAT monotherapy is not recommended.

SURGICAL APPROACHES

In the event of failure of medical therapies and/or additional interventions such as PDAT, judicious surgical debridement may be warranted to reduce the bacterial load in the stroma. Surgical intervention provides the additional benefits of providing tissue for histopathology and facilitating antibiotic penetration, which can be a limiting factor when using drugs found to have good bacteriostatic or bactericidal effects in vitro. In most cases, NTM appears confined to the anterior one-to-two-thirds of the corneal stroma. A wide variety of surgical approaches may be used to debulk the bacterial load in the cornea and literature review of 110 cases published between 1997 and 2025 reveals a combined rate of therapeutic surgical intervention in 46% (51/110) cases [Table 2].

In the event of post-LASIK NTM keratitis, the flap should be immediately lifted, scraped to obtain cultures, and irrigated. Severe cases may require flap amputation. In cases of KLEX, corneal cap amputation was necessary for adequate penetration of antibiotics.[4142] Anterior stromal involvement without a history of keratorefractive surgery may benefit from lamellar keratoplasty, which similarly improves antibiotic delivery to the anterior and mid-stroma.[43]

Although the true rate of perforation is unknown due to the paucity of cases in the literature, cases of very deep stromal involvement may require corneal PKP or deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty. If there is severe corneal thinning, alternative surgical methods, including corneal gluing, autologous conjunctival flap, and amniotic membrane grafts, have been employed to avoid corneal transplant.[24445] Debridement with lamellar keratoplasty, with or without debridement of neighboring tissues in the case of associated scleritis, is effective at reducing bacterial load. In the case of full- or partial-thickness graft failure due to infection, repeat grafting is required.[4647] Optical keratoplasty may be required long-term after resolution of keratitis for dense corneal scars.

CONCLUSION

There are relatively few cases of atypical mycobacterial keratitis reported, and the majority of studies at this time report individual case reports or retrospective chart reviews. Given the increased prevalence of NTM keratitis with keratorefractive, corneal, and cataract surgeries, the standard of care per the Infectious Disease Society of America is to culture all postsurgical cases of infectious keratitis.[16] Delayed diagnosis and the frequent need for multidrug regimens in the case of drug-resistant atypical mycobacteria often lead to visually devastating consequences. Cases with early clinical suspicion and intervention have demonstrated good outcomes, and often, these cases can avoid PKP all together.

Prospective and randomized studies are needed to better characterize the efficacy of ancillary treatments such as PDAT and the efficacy of various surgical interventions; however, the low incidence of NTM keratitis limits the ability to perform larger studies. Liu et al. noted an overall decreasing prevalence of NTM keratitis in their 20-year review at one hospital in Taiwan, which they hypothesized was due to improved efficacy of 4th-generation fluoroquinolones.[48] However, cases of NTM keratitis continue to be reported and represent a challenging subgroup of infectious keratitis, often requiring involvement of infectious disease consultants and multimodal therapy.
