Indocyanine green-assisted liver resection for colorectal liver metastases and factors associated with resection margin: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Lei Yang, Yufan Yang, Xuechen Liu, Jingyi Xu, Wenqiang Wang, Liusheng Wu, Jun Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to compare the short-term outcomes of Indocyanine Green (ICG) assisted minimally invasive surgery (MIS) with Intraoperative Ultrasound (IOUS) against conventional MIS with IOUS alone and open surgery with tactility and IOUS for colorectal liver metastases (CRLM). And identify risk factor of R1 resection (tumor-negative margin < 1 mm) by statistical analysis.Materials and methodsA retrospective cohort analysis was conducted on 86 patients with 158 CRLM tumors undergoing hepatectomy. Patients were categorized into three groups: Group A (ICG + IOUS MIS, n = 14, tumor = 23), Group B (IOUS only MIS, n = 16, tumor = 20), and Group C (tactile + IOUS Open surgery, n = 56, tumor = 115). Statistical analysis involved univariate analysis and multivariable logistic regression were performed.ResultsGroup A demonstrated superior R0 resection (tumor-negative margin ≥ 1 mm) rates, shorter median hospital stays compared to Groups C. Intraoperatively, Group A exhibited reduced blood loss and shorter operative times (213.5 vs. 351.5 min, p = 0.02) compared to Group C. Multivariable analysis identified surgical approach, lower Ki-67 expression, higher body weight and more metastatic lesions as independent predictors of R1 resection.ConclusionICG assisted MIS significantly reduces R1 resection rates, postoperative hospitalization time and costs compared to conventional MIS or open surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04055-y.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer liver metastases (CRLM) occur in more than 50% of patients with colorectal cancer [1]. For patients with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) who are eligible for hepatic resection, surgical treatment should be offered, and perioperative chemotherapy should be considered based on the number and size of lesions [2]. Hepatectomy, as the primary curative treatment option for CRLM, is considered a radical resection for CRLM when at least a 1 mm tumor-negative margin is achieved in partial hepatectomy [3]. Achieving tumor-negative margins (R0 resection, defined as ≥ 1 mm clear margin) can significantly improve the local recurrence rate of CRLM and increase the 5-year survival rate from 10% to 50% [4–6]. Although the benefits of hepatectomy for CRLM patients have been widely validated, approximately 20% of patients still cannot achieve a R0 resection in the initial surgery [7, 8],underscoring the need for precise intraoperative techniques to optimize surgical outcomes. The number of minimally invasive surgeries for CRLM is rapidly increasing and offers immediate postoperative outcomes and long-term prognosis similar to, or even better than, traditional open surgery [7]. However, the inherent limitation of MIS—the lack of tactile feedback—poses significant challenges in tumor localization, resection margin planning, and real-time assessment of tumor boundaries. Intraoperative ultrasound (IOUS) is frequently used for tumor localization and marking of the pre-cut line. However, IOUS has limited ability to identify small tumors (< 1 cm) and may not easily detect superficial tumors, leaving room for technological innovation. ICG near-infrared fluorescence imaging (NIR) emerges as a promising adjunct to address these limitations. This special fluorescence pattern has been reported to detect small occult metastatic lesions in up to 12% of CRLM patients during surgery [9]. In this case, ICG-NIR may compensate for tactile feedback loss in MIS. Moreover, several studies have reported the application of ICG fluorescence imaging in the assessment of tumor margins during surgery [10, 11]. Nevertheless, current evidence remains confined to small-scale case reports and single-arm trials, lacking rigorous comparisons with established techniques like IOUS. Thus, the definitive clinical value of ICG-NIR in optimizing R0 resection rates warrants systematic investigation.

Materials and methods

A retrospective cohort analysis of the database from Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital was conducted, covering the period from November 2018 to December 2024. All surgeries were performed by qualified hepatobiliary surgeons. Patients that underwent local resection and pathologically confirmed CRLM were included. Those with concurrent primary liver tumors, other metastatic cancers, and patients with liver cirrhosis that could affect ICG staining results and the surgeon’s tactile perception were excluded. Cases of R1 resection (tumor-negative margin < 1 mm) due to vascular invasion were also excluded. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital (25007-4-01). The registration number for this study on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry is ChiCTR2500097138 and the study was retrospectively registered in accordance with the ethical guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (2024). The ethics committee deemed informed consent from patients unnecessary for this retrospective study.

We categorized the surgical methods used for treating CRLM into three types: minimally invasive surgery with ICG + IOUS (Group A), minimally invasive surgery with IOUS alone (Group B), and open surgery with Tactility + IOUS (Group C). All patients underwent routine cardiopulmonary function, blood, and biochemical examinations to exclude contraindications for surgery and anesthesia. Each patient received abdominal computed tomography angiography, 3D reconstruction, and enhanced MRI to confirm adequate residual liver volume postoperatively. Some patients received intravenous injection of ICG within 7 days before surgery for intraoperative near-infrared fluorescence navigation. Baseline characteristics, oncological outcomes, and postoperative short-term outcomes of patients were compared among the three approaches.

Variables: Sex, Age, Height, Weight, Body Mass Index(BMI, a measure of body fat based on height and weight), ICG-R15 (the ICG retention rate in the ICG clearance test at 15 min), CRS (Clinical Risk Score, a tool used to assess the prognosis and guide treatment options for patients with CRLM), ASA Score (American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status Classification System, a system used to assess the fitness of patients before surgery), primary site (The primary lesion is colon cancer or rectal cancer), the Ki-67 expression level of the primary CRLM lesion, preoperative CEA/CA-199 level (The tumor marker levels of the patient before hepatectomy), primary tumor T/N stage, the status of perineural invasion in the primary tumor, the number and size of metastatic lesions, the tumor margin status, length of hospital stay and hospitalization costs, duration of drainage tube placement, operative time, intraoperative blood loss and transfusion status, and duration of hepatic portal occlusion.

The primary endpoint of the study was to evaluate the short-term outcomes after surgery for CRLM, with a focus on the R0 resection rate. In this study, our definition of an R0 resection margin is that the width of the tumor-negative margin must be more than 1 mm, whereas an R1 resection is indicated when there is microscopic evidence of tumor remaining at the 1 mm resection margin.

Surgical process

In Group A, patients are administered an intravenous injection of 5 mg of ICG 7 within 7 days before surgery. During the procedure, During the procedure, the tumor boundary is clearly identified using fluorescence imaging after the tumor is exposed, which typically appears as an area of enhanced fluorescence signal. Second, we combine traditional intraoperative ultrasound (IOUS) and direct visual inspection to further confirm the tumor’s anatomical location and extent. Based on this information, the surgeon plans a transection line that is intended to be approximately 1 mm away from the enhanced fluorescence boundary to ensure an R0 resection. This planned line is designed to avoid traversing important vascular structures whenever possible. During the parenchymal transection, we continuously use fluorescence imaging to monitor the cutting edge in real-time, ensuring no residual fluorescent-positive areas (i.e., potential tumor tissue) remain. The CRLM lesions are completely excised and sent for pathological examination. The absence of active bleeding and bile leakage is confirmed before placing 1 drainage under the liver.

In Group B, the planned resection line is marked under IOUS guidance, and due to the absence of ICG, the fluorescence mode is not activated during the procedure. The remaining surgical steps, including trocars placement, instruments for liver parenchymal resection, and drain placement, are identical to those in Group A.

In Group C, an upper right quadrant reverse L-shaped incision approximately 25 cm in length is employed. The planned resection line is marked under IOUS guidance. The surgeon determines the scope of hepatectomy based on the marked resection line and tactile perception. The instruments for liver parenchymal resection and drain placement are consistent with those used in Group A.

In this study, the choice between Group A/B and Group C was primarily determined by doctors’ advices and the patients’ opinions. For patients who were candidates for minimally invasive surgery, physicians recommended the use of ICG fluorescence navigation technology during the procedure. Not all patients who underwent ICG fluorescence navigation were included in Group A. Group A consisted of patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery, received intravenous ICG injection within 7 days before surgery, and consented to the use of ICG. Group B included patients who did not receive ICG injection within 7 days, did not consent to ICG use, or had diffuse ICG staining of the entire liver during surgery that prevented effective surgical navigation.

Statistical analysis

SPSS v27.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) was used to process and analyze the data. All data were tested for standard normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) before statistical analysis (Supplementary Table 1). If the data were normally distributed, continuous quantitative data were presented as Means ± SD; comparisons between two groups were made using an independent samples t-test. If the data were not normally distributed, a nonparametric test (Mann-Whitney U test) was applied, and the data were described using M [25, 75]. For counting data, the number of cases (%) was used to describe the data. Comparisons between ranked data and categorical data were made using the χ2 test. When the conditions for the χ2 test were not met, Fisher’s exact probability method was used. Logistic regression was applied for multivariable analysis. For P values less than 0.001, they are displayed as 0.001 in the table. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

The baseline characteristics of the three groups are summarized in Table 2. There were significant differences between Groups A and B in weight (73.37 ± 12.12 kg and 62.44 ± 8.23 kg, p = 0.007), age (61.50 ± 10.27 years and 68.00 ± 8.56 years, p = 0.035), and Metastasis Diameter [1.50 (1.20;2.50) cm and 3.00 (1.50;4.65) cm, P = 0.028]. No other variables differed significantly across groups. The R0 resection margin rate in Group A was 91.30%, which was significantly higher than that in Group B (65.00%, p = 0.034) and Group C (60.87%, p = 0.004). When focusing on postoperative information, the hospital stay in Group A was 6.50 (4.00;9.00) days, which was significantly shorter than that in Group B [11.5 (6.25;16.00) days, p = 0.034] and Group C [12.00 (10.00;15.00) days, p = 0.001]. Additionally, Group A incurred lower costs [8,008.59 (7,238.41;11,995.60) USD], than Group C [(16,478.99 (11,988.13;21,259.91) USD), p = 0.001]. The total costs billed at the time of patient discharge, which therefore includes the cost of ICG itself as well as the depreciation of the fluorescence imaging equipment. Furthermore, Group A required shorter drainage duration [4.50 (3.00;7.25) days] than Group C [9.00 (6.00;11.25) days, p = 0.001]. When focusing on intraoperative performance, there were no significant differences in blood loss, operation time, occlusion time, and whether transfusion was required between Group A and Group B. However, in terms of blood loss, Group A had less blood loss [75 (20;225) ml] than Group C [254.5 (125.0;400.0) ml, p = 0.008]. And in terms of operation time, Group A was faster [213.50 (179.25;288.25) minutes] than Group C [351.50 (258.50;529.25) minutes, p = 0.002].

Among 158 resected tumors, 104 (65.82%) achieved R0 resection, while 54 (34.18%) were R1 (Table 3). Bivariate logistic regression revealed that surgical approach, weight, BMI, Ki67 and the number of tumors per patient were significantly associated. Compared with Group A, both Group B and C had a OR > 1 to achieve R1 resection, and Group C had a higher OR [6.750 (1.509–30.190), p = 0.012] than Group B [5.654 (1.016–31.478), p = 0.048]. After adjustment, multivariable regression analysis showed that surgical approaches, Ki67 [0.970 (0.945–0.996), p = 0.023], weight [1.031 (1.002–1.060), p = 0.033], and number of tumors [1.312 (1.033–1.666), p = 0.026] remained independent predictors of R1 resection. However, Group C had a lower OR [10.027 (1.905–52.779), p = 0.007] than Group B [20.754 (2.854–150.899), p = 0.003]. The increased OR for Group B in multivariable analysis suggests that minimally invasive surgery without ICG guidance may inherently pose a higher risk of R1 resection compared to open surgery when controlling for tumor biology (Ki67 index) and surgical difficulties (The patient’s body weight and tumor count).

Discussion

In the present study, we aimed to comparatively analyze the advantages of ICG fluorescence-assisted minimally invasive surgery for colorectal liver metastasis resection against conventional minimally invasive or open surgery through a series of retrospective data. Inter-group comparisons revealed that ICG-assisted minimally invasive surgery significantly outperformed the other two surgical approaches in terms of R0 resection rate and postoperative hospital stay. Previously, we believed that the tactile feedback provided by the surgeon during open surgery played a crucial assistive role. However, in minimally invasive surgery, this tactile assistance is lost. Our data comparisons suggest that the intraoperative and postoperative effects of ICG fluorescence assistance may substitute for, or even surpass, the tactile feedback of open surgery. This is primarily evident in surgical duration, intraoperative blood loss, duration of postoperative drainage tube carriage, and hospitalization costs. Both univariate and multivariable analyses identified significant factors influencing the achievement of R1 resection, namely, surgical approach, Ki-67, patient’s weight and metastatic numbers.

Interestingly, after correction with multivariable regression, we found that the OR for Group B increased and even exceeded that of Group C. The reversal of OR between univariate and multivariable analyses underscores the critical influence of confounding factors, such as tumor proliferative activity and surgical difficulties, on resection outcomes. After adjusting for these variables, the independent detrimental effect of MIS without ICG became more pronounced, potentially reflecting the technical challenges of achieving clear margins in a minimally invasive setting without fluorescence guidance. A multicenter study from Germany reported that minimally invasive surgery for colorectal liver resection demonstrated superior performance in surgical duration and hospital stay compared to open surgery, aligning with our findings [12]. Moreover, the addition of ICG fluorescence assistance further shortened the hospital stay. A meta-analysis also highlighted the advantages of minimally invasive surgery in short-term outcomes, although no significant differences were observed in long-term prognosis compared to open surgery [13]. Specifically, the use of ICG fluorescence in MIS has been shown to enhance surgical outcomes. A study assessing ICG-fluorescence-guided surgery reported higher radical resection rates and more accurate prediction of resection margins [11]. This aligns with our findings, where ICG-assisted MIS showed superior R0 resection rates and shorter postoperative hospital stays compared to conventional MIS and open surgery. It is worth noting that the sole use of ICG does not seem to yield satisfactory results. Only when combined with IOUS can it achieve nearly 100% detection rate of CRLM lesions [14]. The whole is greater than the sum of the parts. Currently, there are also methods to improve lesion identification through three-dimensional reconstruction [15]. However, the role of ICG fluorescence in assessing tumor margins was also validated in this study. Supported by real-time surgical margin assessment studies, which showed that IOUS plus ICG fluorescence assistance can effectively guide surgeons in achieving tumor-negative margins [16, 17].

Our study also identified the influence of surgical difficulties and primary tumor Ki-67 on R1 resection rates. Excessive body weight or a high number of tumors can indeed increase the difficulty of surgery, which in turn may affect the surgical margins of the tumor. Excessive body weight leads to increased visceral fat, which according to clinical experience, makes the dissection of the hepatic hilum more challenging and causes difficulties in surgical field exposure. Fat accumulation can also promote the onset and progression of cancer by modulating inflammatory factors [18, 19]. However, the safety of minimally invasive surgery for obese patients has also been confirmed. Although it is associated with a higher incidence of short-term complications, there is no clear evidence that it affects tumor resection margins [20, 21]. On the other hand, a larger number of tumors often indicates a wider distribution of lesions and greater invasiveness (with a higher possibility of nerve and vascular invasion). Existing studies have found that an increased number of tumors is an independent risk factor for prognosis in patients with colorectal cancer liver metastases [22]. Surprisingly, tumors with higher Ki-67 levels seemed more likely to achieve R0 margins. However, the expression level of Ki-67 does not appear to be entirely correlated with clinical outcomes, and some studies have proposed a relationship between low proliferative activity and high invasiveness in CRLM [23, 24]. For highly invasive tumors, the micro-infiltration of surrounding tissues may be difficult to detect by ICG, IOUS, or tactile feedback, which perhaps explains why tumors with lower Ki-67 expression are more prone to positive margins. Therefore, patients with lower Ki-67 expression may require more meticulous surgical techniques and possibly additional imaging or molecular markers to better define tumor margins.

Some studies suggest that intravenous injection of ICG 4–7 days prior to surgery can achieve optimal lesion detection, which aligns with the experience at our center [25]. However, the optimal dosage and timing of ICG injection remain a subject of debate, with the need for more precise research evidence to reach a consensus [26]. Furthermore, we have observed that research is underway to restore tactile feedback to minimally invasive surgeons, aiming to recreate this innate ability in the context of minimally invasive procedures [27].

In addition, studies have evaluated the utility of ICG in other tumor types, such as breast cancer liver metastasis and melanoma liver metastasis, albeit with limited sample sizes [28]. Given that ICG can induce peripheral fluorescence due to the compression of surrounding micro bile ducts by tumor tissue, researchers believe that this area warrants further investigation to expand the applications of ICG. Such research could potentially enhance the diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities of ICG in a broader range of oncological surgeries.

However, this study also has critical limitations that need to be emphasized. The instability of OR estimates in Group B and C, as indicated by extremely wide confidence intervals. The number of cases with R0 in Group C was indeed small, leading to the wide confidence interval, highlights the limitations inherent in studies with small sample sizes. Also, here was some selection bias in the grouping process. Patients with higher BMI and smaller tumors were more likely to be included in Group A. Additionally, the study did not report the location of liver metastases. Undoubtedly, this is a critical determinant for both surgical methods and the applicability of ICG fluorescence, which the researchers will address in subsequent studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that ICG fluorescence-assisted minimally invasive surgery for colorectal liver metastasis offers significant advantages in terms of R0 resection rates and postoperative hospital stay compared to conventional minimally invasive or open surgery. The findings suggest that ICG fluorescence can effectively compensate for the loss of tactile feedback in minimally invasive procedures, potentially surpassing the benefits of open surgery.
