Biochemical and mechanical effects of biopreservation methods on decellularized porcine skin grafts
Authors: Carolynn M Brooks, Alperen Abaci, Ruben V Oganesyan, Basak E Uygun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAs the demand for tissue and organ transplantation increases, the field of regenerative medicine is rapidly evolving to meet this critical need through innovative methods, such as employing decellularized tissues and organs, for creating engineered transplantable organ and tissue alternatives. This study aims to investigate the decellularization of porcine skin to create biological scaffolds that preserve essential extracellular matrix (ECM) properties and assesses different biopreservation techniques that best preserve morphological, biochemical and mechanical characteristics of the scaffold.ResultsWe established a decellularization protocol using 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) for efficient cellular removal, followed by an evaluation of two biopreservation methods: slow-cooling and freeze-drying. Our results indicate that slow-cooling preserves ECM integrity and composition more effectively than freeze-drying, confirmed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and biochemical assays for key ECM components. Quantitative analyses revealed sufficient retention of collagen, glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), and enhanced mechanical properties in slow-cooled samples, which closely resembled fresh decellularized controls. These findings underscore the critical need for optimized preservation techniques to ensure the viability of tissue grafts in regenerative medicine.Keywords: Decellularization, Preservation, Cryopreservation, Slow-cooling, Freeze drying, Extracellular matrix, Biomaterials

Introduction

As the demand for tissue and organ transplantation continues to grow, the need for effective transplantable solutions becomes ever more urgent. Tissue and organ decellularization have emerged as an innovative technique in regenerative medicine, enabling the fabrication of biological scaffolds that closely resemble the native extracellular matrix (ECM) [1–4]. This process removes cellular components from tissues while preserving the essential architectural and biochemical characteristics of the ECM, resulting in scaffolds that outperform those produced by conventional tissue engineering methods. The decellularized ECM provides physical support, mechanical stability, and biochemical cues necessary for tissue development, influencing key processes such as cell adhesion, migration and proliferation, vascular development, and differentiation [5–7]. Therefore, preserving the native ECM during and after decellularization is crucial for generating viable transplantable tissues and organs, requiring optimized decellularization and preservation protocols tailored for different tissue types. While considerable research has focused on maintaining ECM integrity during decellularization [8–10], studies on the preservation of decellularized tissues remain limited.

Biopreservation through cryopreservation is an effective method for the long-term storage of bioactive materials, such as cells, tissues, and organs, which is crucial for meeting transplantation demands [11–13]. However, the freezing process can lead to excessive ice formation that damages cells and disrupts the microvasculature, resulting in functional and structural alterations. To address this, various cryoprotectants are used to minimize or prevent ice formation by enabling vitrification at sub-zero temperatures [14–16]. The optimization of cryopreservation techniques for decellularized tissues has been explored in studies involving rabbit esophageal scaffolds [17] and whole rat kidney matrices, demonstrating its potential as a viable preservation method [18].

Another promising biopreservation method is freeze-drying, which eliminates the need for toxic cryoprotective agents and allows samples to be stored at room temperature. This approach has been studied for preserving decellularized porcine pulmonary heart valves [19, 20] and decellularized rat intestine scaffolds [21]. While these studies have advanced the field of decellularized tissue preservation, no research to date has investigated the preservation of decellularized skin, a material with significant potential for wound healing and skin grafting [22–24].

In this study, we demonstrated that preservation methods, specifically slow-cooling cryopreservation and freeze-drying, significantly impact the structural and quantitative composition of key ECM components, collagen, elastin, and glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) in decellularized porcine skin, ultimately influencing the tissue’s biomechanical properties. Notably, slow-cooling emerged as the superior preservation technique, effectively maintaining ECM integrity and resulting in samples that closely resembled the fresh decellularized controls. These findings provide a foundational basis for further investigation into biopreservation methods for decellularized skin, with potential implications for regenerative medicine and tissue engineering.

Materials and methods

Porcine dermal sample preparation

All animal care and surgical procedures were in accordance with the MGH Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Full thickness dorsal skin samples were collected from disease-free Yorkshire pigs (Cummings School of Veterinary Medicine, Tufts University, Grafton MA) weighing 30–40 kg. The samples were then cut into 1 cm2 squares and were shaken in a de-epithelialization solution containing 2 M sodium chloride (NaCl) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 50 mM Tris buffer (T60050, Research Products International, Mt Prospect, IL, USA), and 0.25% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 18 h. The epithelial layer from the skin was later removed manually using forceps.

Decellularization of dermis samples

Following de-epithelialization, the specimens were first washed in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) solution (0.5% or 1% w/v) for 72 h on an orbital shaker at 200 rpm. The samples were then washed in 1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 24 h followed by deionized water for another 24 h with constant agitation. After decellularization, the samples were stored in an antibiotic-antimycotic solution (100 U/mL penicillin, 50 U/mL streptomycin, and 0.25 µg/mL amphotericin B in sterile PBS) (Gibco, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) at 4 °C until further use.

DNA quantification in decellularized dermis

Using a 4-mm surgical biopsy punch, 4 samples were taken from decellularized dermis samples of each of the following groups: native, 1% SDS, and 0.5% SDS. Each sample were placed in an Eppendorf tube for digestion and purification via Invitrogen PureLink™ Genomic DNA Mini Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US). The purified DNA was then quantified through the measurement of absorbance at 260 nm using Nanodrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US).

Slow-cooling cryopreservation of decellularized dermis

Four decellularized dermis specimens were each placed in 5-mL cryovials with 2 mL of 10% (v/v) dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA) and 90% (v/v) Medium 199 (Gibco, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) solution. The cryovials were then slow-cooled in a CryoMed Controlled Rate Freezer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US). The cooling initially occurred at a rate of −1 °C/min until the internal temperature of the machine reached 0.0 °C, where the samples were held for 8 min to minimize the risk of ice crystal formation. Once this step was completed, the cooling rate was constant at −1 °C/min until the temperature reached −80 °C. The samples were then stored at −80 °C overnight until further assessment. To thaw, the samples were rapidly warmed in a 37 °C water bath. To adequately remove the cryoprotective agent, the samples were rinsed in 75 mL of PBS with constant agitation at 200 rpm for 10 min. This rinsing procedure was repeated five times.

Freeze-drying of decellularized porcine dermis

Freeze-drying of decellularized porcine dermis was done with slight modification of a previously described protocol [25]. Four 1 × 1 cm2 decellularized dermis samples were incubated in 75 mL of sterile 5% (w/v) sucrose in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA) for 18 h with constant agitation at 200 rpm. Sucrose was used as a lyoprotectant to stabilize the extracellular matrix during freeze-drying, minimizing ice crystal formation and preserving tissue structure, proteins, and overall histoarchitecture for subsequent storage. The samples were then washed three times in 75 mL of sterile 80% (w/v) sucrose solution at 37 °C with constant agitation for 15 min. The 80% sucrose was changed with new solution three times. Samples were first slow-cooled following the same steps as described above. They were then transferred on dry ice to a VirTis Benchtop Pro Freeze Dryer (SP Scientific, Gardiner, NY, US) for lyophilization for 18 h at 60 mTorr. Freeze-dried decellularized dermis samples were stored at 4 °C until further assessment. In preparation for biochemical and histological analysis, samples were rehydrated in PBS via a vacuum chamber for 15 min. PBS was replaced with fresh solution three times to allow for adequate removal of the sucrose out of the samples.

Quantitative biochemical assays

A 3-mm surgical biopsy punch was utilized to prepare samples from each group (decellularized, slow-cooled, and freeze-dried) for analysis. The amount of collagen (n = 4) was determined via QuickZyme Total Collagen Assay (QuickZyme Biosciences, Leiden, The Netherlands), total elastin content (n = 3) was measured with a Fastin™ Elastin Assay Kit (Biocolor Life Sciences Assays, Carrickfergus, UK), according to manufacturers’ instructions. Total GAG content (n = 5) was measured following a previously reported protocol [26].

Histological assessment of native and decellularized dermis

Three samples from all groups (native, 0.5% SDS, 1% SDS, slow-cooled, and freeze-dried) were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin overnight. They were then processed, embedded in paraffin, and micro-sectioning at a thickness of 5 μm. All specimens were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson's Trichrome, and Alcian Blue using standard protocols to visually confirm complete decellularization. Images of the slides were taken using a Nikon Eclipse E800 microscope at 20x magnification.

Measurement of mechanical strength of decellularized dermis

Uniaxial tensile strength test was performed on 3 × 2 cm2 samples each of decellularized, slow-cooled, and freeze-dried dermis (n = 4) using an Instron 5984 Universal Testing Machine (Instron, Norwood, MA, US) until failure, recording the tensile stress over strain. The maximum load before failure was then computed in Excel.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with three or more replicates as indicated. Statistical significance between groups was determined via Tukey’s multiple comparisons test with a p-value smaller than or equal to 0.05 considered statistically significant using GraphPad Prism 9.3.1. When reporting central tendencies, we used mean and standard error of the mean.

Results

Determination of the optimal decellularization protocol for efficient cell removal

First, we tested two different concentrations of SDS to produce decellularized porcine skin. Macroscopically, the decellularized dermis samples appeared pale white in color in both 0.5% and 1% SDS groups when compared to the warm pink of the native skin, suggesting successful removal of the cellular components (Fig. 1A). Histological review of the specimens treated with 0.5% SDS slides revealed residual nuclei in the dermis, while those treated with 1% SDS showed none, suggesting that 1% SDS was more effective in removing cellular material (Fig. 1B). Microscopic review of Masson’s trichrome and Alcian blue stained slides did not reveal any significant changes in the ECM microstructure following decellularization with either SDS concentration, while showing no signs of remaining cells (Figs. 1C and D).

Quantitative analysis of DNA content indicated that both concentrations of SDS significantly reduced nuclear material in the decellularized dermis; however, only the 1% SDS treatment reduced the DNA content to below 50 ng/mg tissue (321.7 ± 33.3 ng/mg tissue for native, 56.7 ± 20.8 ng/mg tissue for 0.5% SDS, and 45.3 ± 13.9 ng/mg tissue for 1% SDS), which is the accepted threshold for sufficient decellularization (Fig. 1E) [1].

Effect of preservation methods on the composition of decellularized dermis ECM

Following confirmation of the successful decellularization, we evaluated two biopreservation methods for long-term storage: freeze-drying and slow-cooling. Histological review of H&E-stained slides revealed significant differences in ECM structure between methods. Freeze-dried decellularized specimens appeared to have a more densely packed ECM compared to slow-cooled ones (Fig. 2A).

Quantitative analysis of B Collagen, C GAG, and D Elastin content in decellularized dermis before and after preservation. n ≥ 3, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. We also found that the collagen content of the decellularized dermis decreased after preservation using either method (Fig. 2B). Fresh decellularized dermis contained 181.0 ± 25.6 µg collagen/mg wet tissue, while the freeze-dried and slow-cooled decellularized dermis retained only 80.3 ± 10.6 and 95.1 ± 44.3 µg collagen/mg wet tissue, respectively, with these decreases being statistically significant when comparing fresh decellularized samples to both freeze-dried (p = 0.0028) and slow-cooled groups (p = 0.0075). Although the GAG content remained largely unchanged in the freeze-dried samples (6.1 ± 0.9 µg/mg wet tissue in fresh decellularized vs. 5.4 ± 0.6 µg/mg wet tissue in freeze-dried samples), the slow-cooled decellularized dermis exhibited significantly higher GAG content (8.0 ± 1.3 µg/mg wet tissue) compared to both fresh decellularized (p = 0.0236) and freeze-dried samples (p = 0.0037) (Fig. 2C). Additionally, elastin content was higher in both preserved decellularized dermis samples compared to fresh decellularized dermis (27.5 ± 9.8 µg/mg tissue for fresh decellularized, 67.4 ± 21.7 µg/mg tissue for freeze-dried, and 87.9 ± 32.1 µg/mg tissue for 1% SDS); however, the differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 2D).

Effect of biopreservation on the mechanical strength of decellularized dermis

The maximum load before failure for the freeze-dried decellularized dermis was significantly higher than that of fresh decellularized control (Fig. 3A), the values being 3.99 ± 0.35 and 2.02 ± 0.28 MPa, respectively. However, slow-cooling (2.92 ± 0.25 MPa) did not show any significant changes compared to fresh decellularized or freeze-dried samples (Fig. 3B). On the other hand, the Young’s modulus of the preserved decellularized dermis was significantly higher than that of fresh decellularized control, regardless of the preservation method: it was 11.6 ± 0.81 MPa for freeze-dried decellularized dermis and 11.09 ± 0.63 MPa for the slow-cooled sample, compared to 6.95 ± 0.51 MPa for the fresh decellularized sample. There was no statistically significant difference between freeze-dried and slow-cooled samples (Fig. 3C).

Discussion

The optimized protocol for decellularizing porcine skin utilized SDS as the primary decellularization agent, followed by Triton X-100 treatment to remove any residual SDS and cellular components from the tissue. While SDS is a widely used chemical for decellularization, its strength as a surfactant can potentially degrade essential ECM components during the removal of cellular material [27–30]. Therefore, careful optimization of SDS exposure is essential for developing an effective decellularization protocol. Even though Masson’s trichrome and Alcian blue staining demonstrated relatively preserved ECM integrity with both 0.5 and 1% SDS treatments, we showed that 0.5% SDS was less effective than 1% SDS in removing cells from the native tissue, as confirmed by quantified DNA content. Consequently, we concluded that the most effective decellularization protocol involved washing samples with 1% SDS for 72 h with constant agitation, followed by rinsing with 1% Triton X-100 for 24 h and then with DI water for another 24 h. Most skin decellularization protocols in literature typically combine enzymatic and chemical agents. A common approach involves using trypsin alongside SDS/Triton X-100, with Triton X-100 generally set at 1%, the same concentration as in our protocol [31–38]. However, SDS concentrations are often lower (0.1%) to minimize further ECM damage during the multiple treatment steps involving enzymes in these studies. Trypsin treatment methods potentially harm the ECM microstructure and there is a risk of damaging essential components like elastin and collagen, which is detrimental to skin integrity [39, 40]. To avoid using enzymes such as trypsin, Milan et al. developed a protocol utilizing 0.5% Triton X-100 followed by 0.7% SDS treatment. This approach effectively removed cells while retaining ECM integrity [41]. These findings indicate that using SDS and Triton X-100 for skin decellularization can be an effective approach without the need for the use of enzymatic agents, which is consistent with our results.

After identifying the most effective decellularization protocol, we applied two biopreservation methods, slow-cooling and freeze-drying, to the decellularized samples and assessed their effects on the ECM components. Both techniques enable long-term storage of tissues and organs; however, it’s important to note that the native tissue’s structure and composition can be altered during this process [42]. Additionally, ice crystal formation may disrupt the ECM structure, making it crucial to minimize tissue damage for efficient preservation. H&E staining of our samples indicated that the ECM in the fresh decellularized samples were dispersed, a pattern also observed in the slow-cooled samples, though to a lesser degree in the freeze-dried samples. While freeze-drying is an effective method, it requires freezing followed by sublimation to dehydrate the sample. The freezing step usually doesn’t involve cryoprotective agents to control ice crystal formation, therefore the process can result in the formation of large ice crystals that disrupt the ECM and create tissue heterogeneity [43]. As a result, more morphostructural changes are generally expected in freeze-dried samples compared to cryopreserved tissues. For instance, Diaz-Moreno et al. showed that freeze-drying decellularized rat intestine resulted in more significant alterations to ECM structure than cryopreservation [21], which aligns with our observations. However, addition of CPAs before freezing of the samples for freeze-drying biopreservation may eliminate the risk of architecture damage to the ECM.

Quantitative assessment of the ECM components showed a significant reduction in collagen content in the preserved tissues compared to fresh decellularized tissue. However, there was no significant difference in total collagen levels between the slow-cooled and freeze-dried samples. In terms of glycosaminoglycan (GAG) and elastin content, we found no significant decrease in these ECM components after either biopreservation method. Supporting our findings, Diaz-Moreno et al. reported that collagen and elastin structures can be disrupted during the freeze-drying and cryopreservation of decellularized rat intestine [21]. Similarly, Wang et al. demonstrated that freeze-drying decellularized porcine pulmonary heart valves can affect the structure of both collagen and elastin [19]. Urbani et al. also quantitatively showed that cryopreservation of decellularized rabbit oesophagi resulted in a significant drop in collagen and GAG content, while elastin was preserved to some degree [17]. These results are consistent with our findings, suggesting that collagen is more prone to loss during preservation, whereas elastin can be maintained to a certain extent. Given that elastin and collagen are critical components of skin, contributing to its mechanical properties, it is essential to preserve these proteins effectively during biopreservation [44]. There are multiple studies showing that, if the preservation protocol is not optimized, biomechanical properties of the decellularized tissues can be compromised [17, 19]. From our findings, there was no loss to biomechanical properties to the decellularized skin post preservation, and there was no significant difference between two preservation methods on maximum load before failure and Young’s modulus values. However, it was observed that there was a significant increase in maximum load before failure on freeze-dried samples. It is possible that the freeze-dried samples were not fully rehydrated after preservation, resulting in their ECM components remaining in a semi-denatured state during testing. This could explain both the rigidity of the samples observed in the tensile strength test and the packed fibers shown in the histological stains. However, reported Young’s moduli for native skin range from 4.6 to 20 MPa, and our scaffolds fall within this range both before and after preservation. This suggests that the decellularized and preserved tissues maintain physiologically relevant mechanical properties and remain well-suited for grafting applications [45].

Overall, our findings highlight the importance of selecting an appropriate preservation method for decellularized porcine skin, which can be easily applied to other types of tissues and organs. Effective preservation not only maintains the structural integrity of the extracellular matrix but also ensures that the biomechanical properties are retained. Ultimately, these advancements will contribute significantly to the development of readily available, functional transplantable tissues and organs, addressing the growing demand in regenerative medicine and transplantation.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that a 1% SDS decellularization protocol is most effective for removing cellular components from porcine dermal tissue while preserving ECM integrity. The subsequent evaluation of two biopreservation methods, slow-cooling and freeze-drying, revealed that slow-cooling better maintains ECM structure and composition. While both methods showed an increase in Young’s modulus, indicative of altered mechanical properties, slow-cooling samples exhibited ECM characteristics closer to the fresh decellularized controls. These findings highlight the importance of optimizing decellularization and preservation techniques to improve the viability of tissue grafts for regenerative applications.

Limitations

The primary limitation of this study is the lack of in vitro cell-based testing or in vivo animal models to evaluate the regenerative potential of the preserved dermal scaffolds. Without evaluating the scaffolds in a biological context, it remains uncertain whether they would perform effectively in practical applications. Furthermore, the study could be strengthened by more comprehensive histological and biochemical analyses, particularly focused on assessing the integrity and composition of key ECM components after preservation, which would provide deeper insight into the impact of the preservation methods on tissue structure. Lastly, the inclusion of growth factor analysis would be valuable in confirming the completeness of the decellularization process and ensuring that any bioactive molecules potentially affecting cellular responses were adequately removed.
