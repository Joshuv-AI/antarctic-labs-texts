Treatment of recurrent aphthous stomatitis with oral roflumilast, a multicenter observational study
Authors: Rodrigo Peñuelas Leal, Leticia Bagan, Andrés Grau Echevarría, Jose Amancio Peñuelas Ruiz, Violeta Zaragoza Ninet, Jose Luis Sánchez Carazo, Gemma Pérez Pastor, Carolina Labrandero Hoyos, Malena Finello, Sandra Martínez Fernández, Daniel Blaya Imbernon, Ángel González García, Elena Pérez Zafrilla, Miguel Martí Cabrera, Jose Bagan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSevere recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS) represents a therapeutic challenge because of its impact on the patient's quality of life. Additionally, no approved systemic therapies are available. Roflumilast, a phosphodiesterase‐4 inhibitor, has shown promise in other inflammatory dermatological conditions. This study aimed to assess the characteristics, effectiveness, and safety of roflumilast in treating RAS in routine clinical practice.MethodsThis is a single cohort ambispective observational study conducted in five Spanish centers. Twenty‐two patients with RAS treated with roflumilast participated. Data collection included demographic, clinical, and outcome variables. Statistical analysis compared the outcomes of 12 weeks of roflumilast treatment with a similar prior period without treatment.ResultsDuring treatment with roflumilast, a significant reduction in flare‐ups (88%) and oral ulcers (94%) was observed compared to the untreated period. A reduction in pain (66%) and ulcer duration (63%) was observed. Adverse effects (AEs) occurred in 13 patients, predominantly headache and gastrointestinal disturbances. Most of these were self‐limiting or manageable with dose adjustment. Treatment was withdrawn in three cases, mainly because of AEs.ConclusionsThis study suggests that roflumilast may effectively treat RAS by reducing the number of flare‐ups and ulcers, their duration, and the symptomatology produced by the ulcers. In addition, roflumilast has a good safety profile, is well tolerated at low doses, and does not require close monitoring. These characteristics and its favorable economic profile make roflumilast a promising therapeutic option in this pathology.Keywords: recurrent aphthous stomatitis, roflumilast, oral ulcer, phosphodiesterase 4, oral diseases, oral mucosa, inflammatory diseases

Introduction

Recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS) is the most common ulcerative condition in the oral cavity in humans. It is characterized by the recurrence of well‐defined, painful mouth ulcers with an erythematous border and a fibrinous base. The ulcers cannot be attributed to local trauma or systemic disease. 1 , 2 The etiology is not clearly defined, although genetic susceptibility seems to be a relevant risk factor. 1 RAS mainly affects children and up to 20–25% of young adults. It becomes less frequent with age, although it can persist 1 , 2 , 3 and cause significant impairment in the quality of life. 4

The objective of treating RAS is to improve patients' quality of life. However, there are currently no approved systemic treatments for this condition. First‐line strategies generally involve topical treatments, which reduce the severity and duration of ulceration but do not prevent recurrence. 5 , 6 The second therapeutic step consists of immunomodulatory or systemic immunosuppressive drugs, which may require routine monitoring and pose risks of adverse effects (AEs). 2 , 7 , 8 Therefore, there is a need for new therapeutic options in the management of RAS. 1

The effectiveness of roflumilast has recently been reported in one patient with RAS 9 and ulcers associated with Behçet's disease. 10 Roflumilast is a once‐daily oral phosphodiesterase‐4 (PDE4) inhibitor, approved for treating chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) since 2010. 11 , 12 Its mechanism of action is similar to apremilast and has a good safety profile. These characteristics, along with a more convenient dosage and lower price, 13 encourage the use of roflumilast in other inflammatory dermatoses where apremilast has been reported effective. 13 , 14 , 15

This study aims to describe the characteristics of patients with RAS who receive or have received treatment with roflumilast in routine clinical practice and to analyze its effectiveness and safety in this population group.

Methods

Study design

This analytical observational single‐cohort study with ambispective follow‐up involves five Spanish centers. Patient data were collected from clinical medical records and/or direct anamnesis, both retrospectively and prospectively. Demographic, clinical, and outcome variables were collected. Outcome variables included the number of flare‐ups (NFU), defined as the occurrence of at least one oral ulcer after a period of remission, the number of oral ulcers (NOU), the pain produced by ulcers assessed with the numeric pain scale (pain‐NRS), and the duration of ulcers (DU) in days.

The outcome variables collected during the first 12 weeks of treatment with roflumilast (“roflumilast treatment” (RT)) were compared with the same variables during the 12 weeks prior to receiving topical or systemic treatment (“without treatment” (WT)). NFU was recorded on a scale from 0 to 4, where 4 indicates continuous ulcers without remission. NOU, DU, and pain‐NRS (0–10) were collected as discrete numerical variables.

Additional data included the dose of roflumilast used at each time point, the presence or absence of AE, and their evolution over time. If the medication was withdrawn before 12 weeks, the reason for withdrawal was documented.

Demographic, clinical, and outcome variables were collected retrospectively during the WT and RT periods until the study was approved by the medical research ethics committee of the principal investigator's center. Subsequently, data were collected prospectively.

Study population

The study subjects were patients diagnosed with RAS who presented quality of life impairment that justified the use of systemic treatment and who were receiving or had received treatment with oral roflumilast through off‐label use.

Patient identification and selection were performed during the usual clinical consultation of the participating investigators. Patients agreed to participate in the study by signing the informed consent form.

Patients were excluded if they had oral ulceration attributed to other pathologies. These pathologies included Behçet's disease, traumatic ulcers, tumors, and infectious ulcers, including oral herpes, Crohn's disease, celiac disease, anemia, systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren's syndrome, or ulcers secondary to medication.

Endpoints

The study's main objective was to assess the decrease in NFU during the first 12 weeks of treatment with roflumilast (RT) compared to the period without treatment (WT).

The secondary objectives were to assess the decrease in NOU, pain‐NRS, and DU between the same periods. Another aim was to determine the safety of the treatment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS statistics software (version 26.0) and the OpenEpi epidemiological calculator. 16 Values were considered statistically significant if P < 0.05.

An exploratory study of the response variables was conducted using a bivariate analysis of the treatment periods (WT and RT). The non‐parametric Wilcoxon method was used to compare two samples with paired data. Incidence densities (ID) and incidence density ratios (IDR) of NFU and NOU were estimated according to the treatment periods (WT and RT) as well as the exposed attributable fraction.

Finally, NFU and NOU analysis was carried out using a repeated measure generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a Poisson distribution and a log link, with the follow‐up time added as an offset variable to model rates instead of counts. To analyze the variables pain‐NRS and duration of the oral ulcers (DU), a repeated measures linear mixed model (MML) with a normal distribution and identity link was used. Kenward–Roger REML estimation methods 17 were applied to handle violations of the assumptions of normality and low sample size.

Treatment and effectiveness up to 12 weeks

In 12 patients, roflumilast was started at 250 μg/day, in 7 patients at 125 μg/day for 7 days and 250 μg/day after that, in 2 patients at 500 μg/day and in 1 patient at 125 μg/day with no subsequent increase. In 1 patient who started at 250 μg/day, the dose was increased to 500 μg/day after 2 months to achieve greater efficacy, while in 2 patients, it was reduced to 125 μg/day to improve adherence. None of the patients received concomitant treatment during roflumilast treatment, including topical or oral corticosteroids. Clinical controls were performed every 30.7 days on average.

Safety

Thirteen patients (59%) had AEs. Headache was the most frequent AE in eight patients. Seven patients reported gastrointestinal disturbances, including abdominal discomfort in six, nausea in one, and diarrhea in one. A patient reported asthenia. In nine patients, AEs were self‐limiting, two required dose reduction, and two dropped out of treatment, one because of asthenia and one because of gastrointestinal disturbances (Table 3).

Discussion

RAS is a common pathology that affects about 20% of the population. 3 It is generally a mild disorder characterized by few flare‐ups and/or few lesions. However, it can cause severe morbidity and decreased quality of life, impairing activities such as speaking, eating, or swallowing. 18

Despite its prevalence, there are no standardized treatment guidelines. The most commonly used first‐line treatment for RAS is corticosteroids, especially topical corticosteroids (TCS), 3 as was the case in our series. TCS effectively reduces the intensity and duration of oral ulcers, whereas systemic corticosteroids are more effective in reducing the recurrence rate. 3 Nonetheless, the use of topical or oral corticosteroids in patients with persistent flare‐ups or continuous oral ulcers may be an undesirable option because of their long‐term effects. 19 Another topical treatment is amlexanox 5%, which appears to have good efficacy, although inferior to triamcinolone acetonide 0.1%. 5 Amlexanox is the only FDA‐approved prescription drug for RAS, 3 which highlights the need for new treatments. 1

In severe cases where recurrence is frequent or topical treatments are inadequate, systemic medication is recommended since it extends the periods between flare‐ups. 3 Colchicine is one of the most widely used systemic drugs and is considered the first choice in many publications. 2 , 20 Thalidomide is another recommended treatment; 3 however, it is often dismissed because of its safety profile. 21 , 22 Other drugs require close monitoring with increased visits and analytical controls, such as dapsone, or increase the risk of infections, such as azathioprine or anti‐TNF drugs. A more recent alternative is apremilast, a safe and generally well‐tolerated drug that does not require laboratory tests or close monitoring and has demonstrated promising efficacy. 23 , 24 Nevertheless, its use may be limited by its cost. 25 , 26 , 27 Another alternative with reasonable safety and economic profiles is pentoxifylline. However, an RCT test of its efficacy against a placebo showed discrete differences. 28

The results of this study suggest the possible effectiveness of roflumilast in the treatment of RAS, with statistically significant and clinically relevant decreases in all the parameters studied. There was an 88% reduction in the number of flare‐ups and a 94% decrease in the NOUs compared to the untreated period. Complete response was achieved in 8 of the 22 patients, who remained free of oral ulcers throughout the 12 weeks, while only 1 patient reported a lack of efficacy. In addition, there was a 63% reduction in the duration of aphthous ulcers and a 66% reduction in the pain caused by them. These results appear superior to those obtained with colchicine. In 1994, Katz et al. demonstrated a 74% reduction in oral ulcers during colchicine treatment at a dose of 1.5 mg/day. 29 The results in terms of pain and burning sensation reduction ranged from 50% 30 to 77% 29 and could, therefore, be similar to those obtained during treatment with roflumilast. In our series, all nine patients who had received systemic treatment were treated at some point with colchicine, but the effectiveness was partial or null.

Regarding its safety profile, roflumilast is an alternative that does not require close monitoring or regular blood tests. In our study, 13 of 22 patients experienced AEs, primarily headache and gastrointestinal disturbances, which in most cases were self‐limiting or could be controlled with dose reduction. Notably, none of the patients had significant weight loss, and three reported weight gain, contrasting with studies in COPD 12 and psoriasis. 31 This may be because of lower doses, improved RAS allowing them to eat or reintroduce food that they would have withdrawn to prevent flare‐ups, or it could be attributed to the reduced sample size and follow‐up time. These results contrast with the COPD literature in clinical practice, where up to 72% had AEs and between 49% and 68% dropped out of treatment in the first year because of AEs. 12 , 32 , 33 The better tolerability of our patients may be because of the use of lower doses, as 15 of 19 patients were maintained on 250 μg/day. In comparison, only two patients were maintained on the COPD dose (500 μg/day), and two patients even had their dose reduced to 125 μg/day to improve adherence. Medication was withdrawn in three patients who interestingly belonged to the same center, where roflumilast was withdrawn in all cases before completing 1 month of therapy. Two patients suspended treatment because of asthenia and gastrointestinal disturbances after initiating roflumilast at 250 μg/day and 500 μg/day, respectively. It is worth noting that they were ulcer‐free 3 and 5 days after starting therapy and relapsed 5 and 10 days, respectively, after withdrawal. The third patient suspended treatment on his own after 18 days, reportedly because of a single flare‐up with two ulcers. This highlights the importance of dosing, where starting with low doses, even 125 μg/day for 1 week with a subsequent increase to 250 μg/day, is probably better tolerated with a decrease in frequency and intensity of AEs. The maintenance dose is also relevant, as 89% remained well controlled without requiring escalation to the full COPD dose. Finally, to improve tolerance, it seems particularly important to carefully explain the expected AE profile to the patient, as emphasized in COPD articles, to improve adherence, 12 and to explain that AEs usually self‐resolve in the first few weeks.

Regarding its economic profile, roflumilast is low‐priced. 34 , 35 The monthly cost of treating one patient with apremilast could cover the treatment of 27 to 57 patients with roflumilast, depending on the dosage.

Previous studies have found that the number and size of oral aphthous ulcers mainly determine the impact on quality of life in RAS patients. 4 Although this study did not use scales to assess patient quality of life, since treatment with roflumilast reduces the NOUs and the duration and pain caused by them, patients are likely to have a significant improvement in quality of life.

Although more studies are needed, including long‐term and randomized clinical trials with larger patient cohorts and various dosages, all these features may allow more patients to benefit from it and make roflumilast a safe, effective, and cost‐effective therapy for treating RAS.

Limitations

This study has a reduced number of patients and is unblinded, making it subject to observer bias. In addition, the absence of a placebo control group may mean that part of the observed effect could be because of the placebo effect. The retrospective collection of some baseline data through medical records or direct interviews may be susceptible to recall bias. Observer and recall bias could increase the observed effect, while recall bias could also decrease it.

Patient consent

All patients in the study signed informed consent forms before being included.
