The influence of accessory pathway localization on left ventricular mechanics in adult patients with Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome: a speckle-tracking echocardiographic investigation
Authors: Mingxia Li, Fen Chen, Jing Yao, Xinmin Chen, Xiaoxian Wang, Beibei Ge, Fang Xu, Pingyang Zhang, Shukui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVentricular pre-excitation can cause left ventricular (LV) mechanical dyssynchrony and dysfunction. However, the specific impact of accessory pathway (AP) location on LV mechanics remains incompletely characterized. This study utilized two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography (2D-STE) to quantify and compare LV deformation and synchrony across these anatomical subgroups.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 91 patients with Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome (age 38.2±1.4 years) and 56 matched controls using 2D-STE. Global and regional LV deformation and synchrony were compared across AP-location groups: septal (n=34), right (n=38), and left (n=19). LV deformation parameters included circumferential strain (CS), longitudinal strain (LS), and radial strain (RS). LV synchrony was assessed by peak strain dispersion (PSD) of LV segments for each strain direction.ResultsAll patient groups (septal: −19.0%±0.7%, right: −19.3%±0.6%, and left AP: −19.2%±0.8%) had lower global CS (GCS) compared with controls (−23.1%±0.4%, all P<0.001). The septal AP group showed lower global LS (GLS) (−17.2%±0.5%), global RS (GRS) (30.1%±2.0%), and prolonged GLS-PSD (51.3±3.1 ms) than the left AP group and controls (P<0.05). The right AP group exhibited lower GLS (−17.5%±0.5%) than the left AP group (−19.6%±0.5%, P<0.05) and prolonged GLS-PSD (46.7±2.5 ms) compared with controls (31.6±1.1 ms, P<0.001). The left AP group showed GLS (−19.6%±0.5%), GRS (40.0%±2.6%), and GLS-PSD (40.1±2.3 ms) that were not significantly different from controls (all P>0.05). LV ejection fraction (LVEF) was lower in septal (59.2%±1.2%) and right AP groups (59.8%±1.0%) vs. controls (65.0%±0.3%; P<0.001). LVEF correlated negatively with GLS (r=−0.53, P<0.001) and with GLS-PSD (r=−0.41, P<0.001).ConclusionsLV mechanical dysfunction in WPW syndrome varies by AP location. Septal and right APs lead to widespread strain impairment, dyssynchrony, and lower LVEF, whereas left APs mainly reduce CS. Reduced LVEF is closely associated with impaired LV mechanics and dyssynchrony.

Introduction

Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome, with an estimated prevalence of 0.1–0.3%, is characterized by ventricular pre-excitation due to an abnormal atrioventricular connection (1). This accessory pathway (AP) causes premature ventricular activation, disrupting normal electromechanical coupling and potentially inducing mechanical dyssynchrony (2-4). The synchrony of electrical activation is crucial for distributing mechanical load evenly across the ventricular wall and achieving efficient global pump function.

Studies show that in early-activated myocardial regions, fiber shortening is reduced despite early contraction, while late-activated regions experience pre-stretching followed by enhanced shortening during ejection (5). The anatomical location of the AP determines the extent and site of pre-excited myocardium, which may differentially affect global ventricular function, particularly in adults with long-standing conduction.

Speckle-tracking echocardiography (STE) is now a well-established technique for accurately quantifying multi-dimensional myocardial deformation (longitudinal, circumferential, radial) and assessing mechanical synchrony (6-8). However, systematic comparisons of how different AP locations affect left ventricular (LV) deformation patterns, synchrony, and global function are lacking.

Therefore, this study aimed to apply STE to systematically quantify and compare multi-dimensional strain and synchrony parameters in WPW patients with differently located pathways. Our objectives were to elucidate location-specific mechanical alteration patterns and investigate their association with LV systolic function. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0046/rc).

Methods

Study population

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (No. 2024-438-01). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to its retrospective design. This retrospective study analyzed 115 adults with WPW syndrome from Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital and The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. All patients exhibited manifest pre-excitation on surface electrocardiography. The diagnostic criteria included: (I) PR interval <0.12 s with a normal P wave; (II) slurred upstroke of the QRS complex, representing a delta wave; and (III) secondary ST-T changes. None had sustained tachyarrhythmias at rest. After excluding patients with multiple APs (n=5), inadequate echocardiographic image quality (n=14), Ebstein anomaly (n=1), a history of prior ablation (n=2), premature atrial contractions (n=2), diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, or hypertension, 91 patients were included in the final analysis (49 men, 42 women; mean age 38.2±1.4 years).

The location of the AP was identified during the ablation procedure via standard electrophysiological mapping and served as the gold standard for subgroup classification. Patients were categorized into three groups according to AP location: septal, left free-wall, and right free-wall (Table 1).

For comparison, 56 age- and sex-matched healthy control subjects were recruited. All controls underwent comprehensive cardiovascular assessment to rule out structural or functional heart disease.

Electrophysiological study and radiofrequency catheter ablation

Electrophysiological study and radiofrequency catheter ablation followed standard protocols (9). Venous access was obtained using standard approaches. Three quadripolar catheters were placed via the femoral vein in the high right atrium, His bundle region, and right ventricular apex. A decapolar catheter was advanced into the coronary sinus through the subclavian vein.

Manifest pre-excitation was diagnosed by the presence of a delta wave on surface electrocardiogram (ECG) and a His-ventricular interval <35 ms. The AP was localized using conventional mapping techniques. Procedural success was defined as the abolition of both anterograde and retrograde conduction. Efficacy was assessed by immediate verification (sustained block for 30 minutes post-ablation) and long-term follow-up (no recurrence of tachycardia or pre-excitation on subsequent ECGs).

Based on the fluoroscopically documented ablation site, patients were classified into three anatomical subgroups: septal, right, and left AP locations.

Conventional echocardiography analysis

All echocardiographic examinations were performed before any ablation procedure, during baseline sinus rhythm with manifest pre-excitation. All participants underwent comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography using an ultrasound imaging system (Vivid 7, GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI, USA) with an M4S phased-array transducer. Patients were examined in the left lateral decubitus position, and image acquisition was gated to end-expiration to minimize respiratory motion artifact.

Standard two-dimensional grayscale imaging was systematically acquired from apical four-chamber, two-chamber, and long-axis views, as well as parasternal short-axis views at the mitral valve, papillary muscle, and apical levels. All echocardiographic data were recorded at a minimum frame rate of 50 frames per second to ensure sufficient temporal resolution for quantitative analysis.

LV systolic function was assessed according to American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) guidelines (10). LV end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) and end-systolic volume (LVESV) were measured from apical four- and two-chamber views using the biplane method of disks (modified Simpson’s rule), and LV ejection fraction (LVEF) was derived. Standard structural parameters (interventricular septal thickness, LV posterior wall thickness at end-diastole, left atrial anteroposterior diameter) were obtained from parasternal long-axis views.

LV diastolic function was evaluated by pulsed-wave Doppler analysis of early (E) and late (A) diastolic mitral inflow velocities, acquired from the apical four-chamber view. Tissue Doppler imaging was used to measure early diastolic mitral annular velocities (e’) at the septal and lateral annuli.

STE analysis

Offline speckle-tracking analysis was performed using dedicated software (EchoPAC version 204, GE Vingmed Ultrasound AS) to quantify LV mechanical function, with a focus on myocardial deformation and synchrony (3,11-13).

For LV deformation analysis, two-dimensional strain was assessed in three orthogonal directions. Longitudinal strain (LS) was derived from apical views (four-chamber, two-chamber, and long-axis). Circumferential strain (CS) and radial strain (RS) were obtained from parasternal short-axis views at basal, mid-ventricular, and apical levels. Following manual tracing of the endocardial border, the software automatically tracked acoustic speckles throughout the cardiac cycle. Global deformation parameters—global LS (GLS), global CS (GCS), and global RS (GRS)—were calculated as the average peak systolic strain across all respective segments. LV regional deformation was evaluated by dividing the LV into basal, middle, and apical regions. For each region, the mean peak systolic strain was computed for all three strain directions, yielding metrics such as bas-LS, mid-LS and api-LS.

LV mechanical synchrony, reflecting the temporal coordination of segmental contraction, was quantified as the peak strain dispersion (PSD) (14,15). This measure reflects the temporal coordination of contraction between myocardial segments. Synchrony was evaluated both globally and at the segmental level. Global parameters included the GLS-PSD, GCS-PSD, and GRS-PSD. For regional assessment, PSD was calculated separately for the basal, middle, and apical regions and presented as bas-LS PSD, mid-LS PSD, and api-LS PSD.

Reliability assessment

The reproducibility of GLS, GCS, and GRS was evaluated in 20 randomly selected subjects. Intraobserver variability was assessed by a single observer repeating measurements at 3-day intervals, while interobserver variability was determined through independent analyses by two investigators blinded to clinical data and prior results.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation following confirmation of normal distribution via the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. For comparisons among the four study groups (septal, right, and left AP groups, and controls), continuous variables with parametric distributions were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni post-hoc testing to evaluate all pairwise differences. For normally negative CS and LS, absolute values were used for intergroup comparisons. Non-parametric data were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc analysis. Categorical variables were compared using either the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient for normally distributed variables and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient for non-parametric data. Intraobserver and interobserver variability for GLS, GCS, and GRS were quantified via intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs; two-way mixed-effects model) and Bland-Altman analysis (95% limits of agreement). An ICC >0.8 was predefined as excellent agreement. A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 29.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.2.

LVEF correlated inversely with GLS (r=−0.53, P<0.001) and GCS (r=−0.42, P<0.001), and positively with GRS (r=0.40, P<0.001). LVEF also showed negative correlation with GLS-PSD (r=−0.41, P<0.001) and GRS-PSD (r=−0.42, P<0.001) (Figure 3).

Reproducibility of STEparameters

The ICCs for interobserver agreement were 0.876 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.792–0.931, P<0.001] for GLS, 0.902 (95% CI: 0.838–0.945, P<0.001) for GRS, and 0.891 (95% CI: 0.816–0.940, P<0.001) for GCS. For intraobserver agreement, the ICC values were 0.928 (95% CI: 0.871–0.963, P<0.001) for GLS, 0.915 (95% CI: 0.849–0.955, P<0.001) for GRS, and 0.921 (95% CI: 0.860–0.958, P<0.001) for GCS.

Discussion

Using two-dimensional STE (2D-STE), this study assessed the effect of AP location on LV mechanics in adults with WPW syndrome. We found that the site of pre-excitation determines a specific mechanical pattern. Septal and right free-wall APs were linked to widespread impairment in myocardial deformation, marked mechanical dyssynchrony, and a consequent reduction in LVEF. In contrast, LV mechanics in patients with left free-wall APs were less affected, showing a predominant decrease in CS while largely preserving longitudinal and radial function and global pump performance. These results quantitatively connect AP location to the severity of electromechanical dysfunction and provide a basis for more precise risk stratification.

Location-specific impact on LV mechanics and synchrony

Our results delineate a clear hierarchy in the severity of mechanical dysfunction based on AP location, with distinct patterns observed at both global and regional levels. At the global level, septal and right APs were associated with a widespread impairment of LV deformation across circumferential, radial, and longitudinal directions. This was accompanied by significant mechanical dyssynchrony. In contrast, left APs presented a more selective pattern, predominantly diminishing CS while largely preserving longitudinal and radial mechanics.

GCS was significantly reduced in all patient groups. This uniform finding suggests that circumferential deformation is affected early in patients with WPW syndrome, serving as a potential sensitive marker of electromechanical disruption (16). The mechanism for this selective vulnerability may relate to myocardial fiber architecture. The endocardial layer contains a complex weave of fibers essential for both longitudinal and circumferential shortening, and it is activated early during both normal conduction and AP-mediated pre-excitation (17,18). Histological and biomechanical studies indicate that subclinical alteration or injury confined to this layer can disproportionately impair circumferential function (19). This is because the contribution of circumferentially oriented fibers, crucial for the ventricle’s “wringing” motion, is highly dependent on endocardial integrity. In contrast, GLS, which also relies on endocardial fibers, may demonstrate relative preservation until the dysfunction extends into the mid-myocardial layers (18). In the left AP group, longitudinal and radial function were preserved, while CS was selectively impaired. This pattern corresponds to the model of endocardial vulnerability and underscores the differential impact of pre-excitation on myocardial deformation.

LV mechanical dyssynchrony was observed predominantly along the longitudinal axis. This was most evident in the significant prolongation of the time-to-peak dispersion for LS (GLS-PSD), particularly in the septal and right groups. In contrast, synchrony parameters for CS and RS did not differ significantly from controls. This difference likely arises from the underlying sequence of electrical activation (20,21). Normal longitudinal contraction depends on the rapid, synchronous activation of the endocardial Purkinje network to coordinate base-to-apex shortening (22). AP-mediated pre-excitation introduces an ectopic early activation site that directly and severely disrupts this precise temporal sequence, leading to marked dispersion in the timing of longitudinal shortening across segments. Conversely, circumferential shortening and radial thickening represent more integrated wall mechanics resulting from force summation across myocardial layers. These deformation modes are less dependent on the precise timing of initial endocardial activation. Consequently, their synchrony remains more resistant to the disorganizing effect of a single pre-excitation focus, even when deformation amplitude is reduced.

The more widespread impairment observed in septal and right AP may be explained by several interrelated mechanisms. First, septal and right AP insert near the His-Purkinje system, and their early activation profoundly disrupts the normal base-to-apex and septum-to-free-wall activation sequences. In contrast, left AP insert into the lateral basal left ventricle, a region activated late during normal conduction. Pre-excitation at this site replaces only the terminal portion of the normal wavefront, resulting in a more localized disturbance. Second, the interventricular septum serves as a mechanical anchor for biventricular interaction. Premature septal activation impairs septal contribution to ejection and alters interventricular pressure relationships. Right AP generates a pre-excitation wavefront that traverses the septum, exciting a broad LV endocardial area dyssynchronously. Left AP activates a compact lateral basal region, with the remainder of the ventricle still activated by the Purkinje network, allowing longitudinal function to remain relatively preserved.

At the regional level, patients with septal and right AP showed diffusely distributed impairment. A distinctive observation was that significant apical longitudinal dyssynchrony occurred exclusively in the right AP group. This unique apical dyssynchrony may result from the specific propagation path of the pre-excitation wavefront from the right lateral annulus, potentially disrupting the normally late-activated apex and exacerbating global inefficiency (23,24). For left AP, the mechanical alterations were more spatially confined, primarily presenting as reduced CS across regions without inducing widespread or apical-predominant dyssynchrony. Together, these regional observations confirm that the spatial pattern of mechanical disturbance closely corresponds to the anatomical origin of the AP, further supporting a location-dependent effect.

Association between LV mechanical alterations and systolic function

The observed mechanical impairments culminated in a significant reduction in LVEF in patients with septal and right APs. Efficient ventricular pumping requires synchronized contraction of all myocardial regions (25,26). The presence of an AP disrupts this synergy (27). Our correlation analysis confirms a direct pathophysiological link: the decline in LVEF was significantly associated with both the reduction in global strain (GLS, GCS, GRS) and the increase in longitudinal and radial mechanical dyssynchrony (GLS-PSD and GRS-PSD).

These correlations suggest multiple interconnected mechanisms by which pre-excitation impairs systolic performance: First, a global reduction in myocardial deformation directly limits the capacity for stroke volume generation. Second, pronounced regional dyssynchrony causes temporal dispersion of contraction, generating opposing forces between early- and late-activated regions, which wastes energy and prolongs isovolumic phases. Third, in the case of septal or right APs, abnormal early septal activation can create a functional substrate similar to that seen in paced cardiomyopathies. Chronic electromechanical dyssynchrony from these pathways may promote adverse ventricular remodeling and the development of an AP-mediated cardiomyopathy, even in the absence of frequent tachyarrhythmias (28,29). Although the present cross-sectional study does not include post-ablation follow-up data, published evidence indicates that chronic pre-excitation-induced dyssynchrony can promote adverse remodeling and the development of cardiomyopathy.

Given that patients with septal or right APs often exhibit this pattern of extensive mechanical impairment and marked dyssynchrony, close surveillance may be warranted. Beyond eliminating the arrhythmic substrate, successful ablation aims to restore physiological electromechanical synchrony, offering the potential to reverse dysfunction.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although our cohort was sufficient for an initial characterization, the sample size is modest. Larger, multi-center studies would help confirm our findings and permit more detailed subgroup analyses. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing causality or tracking the progression of mechanical dysfunction over time, including any potential influence of arrhythmia burden. Longitudinal assessments—particularly before and after catheter ablation—are needed to determine the reversibility of the observed abnormalities and to define their natural history. Future prospective studies incorporating pre- and post-ablation assessments would help address these important questions.

Conclusions

In summary, this study shows that LV mechanics are substantially influenced by the location of the AP. Septal and right APs are linked to more pronounced impairment of myocardial strain, significant longitudinal dyssynchrony, and lower LVEF. In contrast, left APs lead to a more selective disturbance, primarily affecting CS. Notably, these abnormalities occur despite preserved global LVEF, and appear driven largely by dyssynchrony.

Quantitative 2D-STE proves to be a sensitive method for detecting these often-subclinical abnormalities. Although the cross-sectional design limits immediate clinical application, combining pathway location with detailed mechanical assessment may aid risk stratification and identify patients warranting closer surveillance.
