Benchmarking antibody discovery fidelity and reproducibility with an assay-locked residue fidelity index
Authors: Michael P Weiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReproducibility in antibody discovery is undermined by dropout, paratope degeneracy, and immunogen imprinting. Although guidelines exist, the field lacks a shared, assay-locked metric to benchmark these liabilities across discovery pipelines. We propose the Residue Fidelity Index (RFI), a discovery-stage, within-assay comparative framework that consolidates fidelity liabilities into a single score. Assay-locked means RFI is comparable only within a fully specified assay configuration, uses fixed normalization and weights, and requires co-reporting of component metrics to expose drivers of the composite. RFI is not intended as a universal or optimal standard, but as one implementable example meeting basic benchmarking requirements: defined components, fixed assay context, pre-specified weights, and primary-data disclosure. RFI is reported alongside its components (D, G, I) to visualize reproducibility in parallel with affinity, humanization, and yield. To demonstrate feasibility, we applied RFI in a simulated Epivolve testbed derived from multiple studies, embedding MILKSHAKE (context retention) and Sundae (residue discrimination) validation modules into discovery. Across three campaigns (n = 25 clones), antibodies meeting provisional thresholds (dropout ≤10%, degeneracy ≤0.10, no imprinting) yielded RFI values from 0.610 to 0.982 (mean ± SD 0.922 ± 0.103; I = 1 for clones C04 and C20). These results show how fidelity can be consolidated to prioritize candidates before application-specific cellular validation. Despite limited scope and proprietary immunogens, this work provides a proof of concept for residue-level benchmarking, with per-clone primary values and RFI reported in Supplementary Table S1 for auditability.Keywords: Antibody reproducibility, residue fidelity index, dropout, degeneracy, benchmarking metrics, epivolve, MILKSHAKE, sundae

The reproducibility crisis in antibody research is well documented [1–6]. Fidelity issues such as dropout during construct transfer, paratope degeneracy, and immunogen imprinting remain common liabilities, undermining reliability across laboratories. Community initiatives have focused mainly on target validation and defined validation principles [1–6], but these remain fragmented. The field still lacks a comparative scale that is assay-locked and explicitly defined for within-assay use for benchmarking fidelity across discovery pipelines.

The motivation for this work is that improvements to antibody discovery pipelines routinely claim improvements in speed, cost, or performance, yet these assertions lack a shared, assay-comparable metric for reproducibility at the discovery stage. Existing validation guidelines focus on target confirmation and application-specific performance, leaving early-stage fidelity liabilities—such as dropout, paratope degeneracy, and immunogen imprinting, largely unquantified. This gap enables narrative superiority rather than a defensible, head-to-head evaluation.

The Residue Fidelity Index (RFI) is proposed to address this need by consolidating these liabilities into a single, assay-locked benchmark that co-reports its components (D, G, I) alongside the composite score. By foregrounding reproducibility as a quantifiable endpoint, RFI complements affinity and yield metrics and provides a transparent basis for comparing discovery workflows under matched assay conditions.

RFI is not a prescriptive standard; instead, it provides a reproducibility-oriented lens that can complement affinity and yield. In keeping with reviewer guidance, RFI is presented as one implementable example, not a universal or uniquely optimal metric. Weights for individual liabilities can be adjusted according to application priorities. For example, diagnostics may prioritize dropout control, while therapeutics may emphasize reduced degeneracy. To prevent over-generalization, RFI is computed under a fixed assay definition (antigen format, buffer, incubation, detection chemistry), a fixed normalization scheme, and a pre-specified weight set (wD*D + wG*G + wI*I); cross-study comparisons are invalid unless these conditions are matched. We additionally require co-reporting of the components (D, G, I) and disclose weights and assay conditions to enable like-for-like interpretation.

To illustrate feasibility, we applied RFI to Epivolve [7–10] (Figure 1), a testbed designed to explore fidelity benchmarking. Epivolve uses chemically defined anchor immunogens to stabilize epitope presentation, paired with two embedded assays. RFI is an assay-locked metric: all component definitions, calibrations, and weights are bound to the specific MILKSHAKE/Sundae/anchor-removal assay suite used here. Values are comparable only within assays that share these parameters; cross-assay comparisons require reestablishing component thresholds and weights. The first, MILKSHAKE (context retention) measured dropout by comparing IgG and scFv formats [11–14] while Sundae [12–14] (residue discrimination) is used to quantify residue specificity through full substitution scanning. Assay→metric linkage: MILKSHAKE provides the measured input for D (dropout), and Sundae provides the measured input for G (degeneracy); the imprinting term I is determined by anchor-removal controls. Together these inputs are consolidated by the RFI equation. These assays provide component-level binary readouts of context stability and residue discrimination. While Epivolve relies on proprietary anchored immunogen chemistry, the assays themselves are transferable and the underlying principle of staged epitope presentation using chemically stabilized anchors is transferable to alternate chemistries [15]. Immunogen constructs can be synthesized using standard peptide conjugation and purified by HPLC if necessary prior to immunization.

Epivolve immunizations have been successfully performed in chicken, murine and rabbit hosts and in phage display following standard protocols. Serum responses can be screened by ELISA against the anchor immunogen and non-target controls. If performed in animals, single B cell cloning yields panels of monoclonal IgG clones suitable for downstream characterization [7–9]. Where appropriate, advanced detection formats (e.g., photoelectrochemical or photothermal immunoassays) can interface with the same ELISA screening logic; these are methodological analogies that do not alter the assay definition used to compute RFI.

The MILKSHAKE protocol evaluates antibody specificity, especially for post-translationally modified (PTM) epitopes using surrogate proteins [11–14] (Figure 1). A modified maltose-binding protein (termed ‘MILKSHAKE protein’) is engineered to contain a C-terminal sortase conjugation site. Synthetic peptides encoding the target sequence, with or without the modification of interest and an HA tag, are covalently attached to MILKSHAKE protein using sortase A-mediated ligation [11–14] (Figures 2, 3). To illustrate RFI, across three campaigns, 25 clones were advanced to benchmarking. Figure 1 provides an overview of the Epivolve workflow, including anchor immunogen design and the integration of MILKSHAKE and Sundae validation modules. Assay→metric linkage: MILKSHAKE provides the measured input for dropout (D; context retention across formats), Sundae provides the measured input for degeneracy (G; residue discrimination via full substitution scans), and imprinting (I) is assessed by comparing anchor-scaffold versus native-context binding. These components are then consolidated by the RFI equation.

Dropout analysis was assessed using the MILKSHAKE assay, which detects loss of binding upon format transfer (Figure 3). Across all clones, mean dropout was 5.83% (Supplementary Table S1). Most antibodies retained binding within the ≤10% working cutoff, but three clones exceeded this threshold (Figure 4). This illustrates both the selectivity of the assay and the practical application of guideline-derived thresholds [1–6]; individual clone values and replicate summaries are provided in Supplementary Table S1, and per-clone points are shown where space allows in Figure 4.

Degeneracy analysis to quantify residue specificity was performed using the Sundae assay, which executes full substitution scanning at anchor residues (Figure 2). Degeneracy indices across the cohort averaged 0.095 (Supplementary Table S1). Most clones conformed to the ≤0.10 cutoff aligned with published validation tolerances, but ~12% showed broader paratope tolerance (Figure 4). These outliers highlight the diversity of specificity profiles that can emerge even under fidelity-constrained conditions and make explicit which component (G) dominates the composite for those clones.

To assess potential immunogen bias, clone binding to anchor scaffolds was compared with binding in native protein contexts. Two of 25 clones (8.0%) met the imprinting criterion (≥50% signal retention on the anchor scaffold versus native context) (imprinting positive by our conservative criterion) (Figure 4). While imprinting thresholds are less well defined than dropout or degeneracy, this conservative cutoff flagged liabilities early in the discovery process; per-clone imprinting calls (I) are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Dropout, degeneracy, and imprinting metrics were consolidated into the Residue Fidelity Index (RFI). Using fixed weights ((w_D,w_G,w_I)=(0.33,0.33,0.34)), RFI values ranged from 0.610 to 0.982 with a mean ± SD of 0.922 ± 0.103. RFI is assay-locked: cross-study comparisons are invalid unless assay definition, normalization, and weights are matched. Component values (D/G/I) are co-reported next to RFI to make the driver of the composite explicit.

These baselines are heterogeneous and not head-to-head comparators but provide context for interpreting provisional thresholds. It should be noted that these findings are illustrative rather than definitive. The sample size is small, the thresholds remain provisional, and baselines are drawn from heterogeneous literature rather than matched controls [15,16]. For these reasons, RFI should be viewed as a conceptual proposal supported by proof-of-concept data; it is one implementable example rather than a universal or uniquely optimal metric. Accordingly, comparisons are assay-locked: cross-study comparisons are invalid unless assay definition, normalization, and weight set are matched; throughout this work a single pre-specified weight triplet (wD*D + wG*G + wI*I) was held constant, with weight-grid sensitivity summarized in Supplementary Fig. S1. For transparency, we co-report component values (D, G, I) adjacent to RFI wherever the composite appears (Supplementary Table S1).

The translational significance of residue-level benchmarking lies in contexts that demand high fidelity, such as mutation-specific diagnostics and post-translational modification mapping. In these settings, reproducibility must be prioritized alongside affinity; RFI is offered as an early, discovery-stage visualization of fidelity liabilities, not as a predictor of cellular or organismal performance. By consolidating fidelity liabilities into a single scale, RFI offers a practical way to visualize reproducibility early in discovery while preserving visibility into which component (D, G, or I) dominates the composite for any given clone.

The introduction of RFI aligns with the community’s call for transparent, use-case-specific antibody validation standards, which are increasingly critical for developing high-fidelity reagents needed in demanding applications such as quantitative Western blotting and frugal point-of-care (PoC) diagnostics [17–20].

In conclusion, RFI is proposed here as a conceptual framework for benchmarking antibody fidelity. Epivolve provides illustrative data showing feasibility, but the metric remains provisional and requires broader validation. By foregrounding reproducibility as a quantifiable endpoint, RFI may help align discovery pipelines with the reproducibility goals articulated by the community [1–6,17–20]. Future work will expand cohort size, perform head-to-head comparisons with phage and hybridoma pipelines, and execute multi-laboratory replication to refine thresholds and derive statistically justified cutoffs under matched assay definitions.
