Artificial intelligence-amplified contrast enhancement in brain magnetic resonance imaging for improving image quality and lesion visualization: a prospective pilot study
Authors: Peiyuan Yin, Yishuang Wang, Liumei Zhang, Zihan Zheng, Siyu Zeng, Jian Shu, Longlin Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) algorithms synthesizing virtual standard-dose images from low-dose contrast-enhanced images of brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have been repurposed to boost contrast from standard-dose input. This study aimed to prospectively evaluate the impact of a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-cleared, deep learning-based software on contrast enhancement, lesion visualization, and diagnostic confidence for standard-dose contrast-enhanced images.MethodsThis prospective study enrolled patients undergoing contrast-enhanced brain MRI between August 2025 and September 2025. Precontrast and standard-dose postcontrast three-dimensional T1-weighted (T1w) images were acquired. AI-amplified contrast-enhanced images were generated via an FDA-cleared deep learning software based on precontrast and standard postcontrast images. Two radiologists independently performed quantitative analyses, examining contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), lesion-to-brain ratio (LBR), and contrast enhancement percentage (CEP). Qualitative assessments of lesion border delineation, internal morphology, and contrast enhancement, and diagnostic confidence were performed with a 4-point Likert scale. Comparisons between AI-amplified and standard-dose images were conducted via the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.ResultsForty-one patients (mean age 51.1±12.6 years) with enhancing brain lesions were included. For both readers, AI-amplified images, as compared with standard contrast-enhanced images, exhibited a significantly higher CNR (82.56±41.15 vs. 20.33±23.28; 87.91±50.30 vs. 20.76±12.00), LBR (2.86±0.64 vs. 1.78±0.48; 2.83±0.53 vs. 1.76±0.40), and CEP (Reader 1: 393.00%±188.40% vs. 201.20%±111.32%; Reader 2: 382.26%±176.71% vs. 197.57%±109.90%) (all P values <0.001). Compared with standard-dose images, AI-amplified images produced an approximately 400% higher CNR, a 60% higher LBR, and a 200% higher CEP. The qualitative evaluation of both readers indicated that the AI-amplified images provided significantly improved lesion border delineation (Reader 1: 3.38±0.61 vs. 3.13±0.59; Reader 2: 3.46±0.60 vs. 3.00±0.63) and contrast enhancement (Reader 1: 3.88±0.31 vs. 2.94±0.24; Reader 2: 3.85±0.42 vs. 2.90±0.49) (all P values <0.001). No significant differences were observed in lesion internal morphology scores between AI-amplified and standard-dose images (P>0.05 for both readers). AI-amplified images demonstrated significantly higher diagnostic confidence than did standard-dose images for both readers (Reader 1: 3.59±0.50 vs. 3.20±0.60; Reader 2: 3.48±0.64 vs. 3.05±0.74; both P values <0.001).ConclusionsAI-based contrast amplification significantly increases the quantitative contrast metrics, qualitative lesion visualization, and diagnostic confidence for standard-dose contrast-enhanced brain MRI without increasing the gadolinium dose. These findings support the use of AI-based contrast amplification as a complementary tool in routine clinical neuroimaging.

Introduction

Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (CE-MRI) plays a pivotal role in the detection, characterization, and follow-up of a wide spectrum of intracranial pathologies, particularly brain tumors (1,2). The administration of a standard dose (0.1 mmol/kg) of gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCAs) significantly improves lesion conspicuity by highlighting blood-brain barrier disruption. This enhancement facilitates diagnosis, treatment planning, and therapeutic monitoring (3-5). Over recent decades, advances in MRI hardware and acquisition techniques have provided a steady improvement in image quality. However, contrast enhancement remains a key determinant of diagnostic sensitivity, particularly for small or weakly enhancing lesions, such as those of inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, brain metastases, and multiple sclerosis (6-8).

Increasing the administered dose of GBCAs has been shown to improve lesion detection and the contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) in certain clinical scenarios (9-11). However, concerns regarding gadolinium retention in the brain and other tissues, as well as its environmental impact, have prompted recommendations for the use of the lowest effective contrast dose (12,13). This consideration is particularly relevant for patients requiring repeated follow-up examinations, pediatric populations, and other vulnerable groups (14). Consequently, strategies that optimize GBCA use, either through reducing the dose or enhancing CE-MRI performance, without increasing the dose have attracted substantial attention (15,16).

Several approaches have been proposed to address this challenge. These include the development of higher-relaxivity contrast agents (17,18), optimization of MRI pulse sequences (19,20), and advanced image postprocessing techniques. Among these strategies, deep learning-based artificial intelligence (AI) has demonstrated considerable potential for improving image quality, reducing noise, and enhancing lesion conspicuity (21,22). Numerous deep learning algorithms have been developed to synthesize standard-dose contrast-enhanced images from low-dose acquisitions, typically with 10–33% of the standard dose (23-26). The primary aim of these approaches is to reduce gadolinium exposure while maintaining contrast enhancement.

More recently, it has been suggested that such models may be repurposed to amplify contrast enhancement in routine standard-dose images (27-29). These algorithms generate virtual double-dose images from standard-dose input, thereby achieving contrast maximization without additional contrast administration. Despite promising preliminary results, the clinical value of AI-driven contrast amplification remains insufficiently explored. There is particular lack of research on real-world clinical settings involving commercially available, user-friendly, regulatory-authorized software. Therefore, the purpose of this prospective study was to evaluate the impact of a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-cleared, deep learning-based AI software on standard contrast-enhanced brain MRI (26). By processing pre-contrast and routine postcontrast T1-weighted (T1w) images, the AI algorithm generates contrast-amplified images without altering the administered GBCA dose. We aimed to quantitatively assess changes in contrast-related metrics and qualitatively evaluate lesion visualization. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0383/rc).

Methods

Study population

This prospective study consecutively enrolled patients who underwent contrast-enhanced brain MRI at Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital between August 2025 and September 2025. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital (No. 2023-457). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) provision of written informed consent; (II) scheduled contrast-enhanced brain MRI examination; and (III) no known contraindications or allergies to GBCAs. Meanwhile, the exclusion criteria were pronounced motion or susceptibility artifacts, incomplete imaging sequences, and absence of any enhancing brain lesions on postcontrast images. Of the 64 enrolled patients, 23 were excluded due to absence of enhancing brain lesions. Finally, the pre- and postcontrast images of 41 patients were input into the deep learning-based AI software for contrast boosting and included in the analysis (Figure 1).

MRI acquisition protocol

Contrast-enhanced MRI examinations were performed with a multichannel head coil on either a 1.5T system (MAGNETOM Aera, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen Germany) or a 3.0T system (SIGNA Architect AIR, GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA). A standardized imaging protocol was applied, including high-resolution three-dimensional T1-weighted gradient-echo (3D T1w GRE) acquisitions obtained before and after intravenous contrast administration. For 3D T1w GRE imaging, the sequence parameters for the 1.5T system were as follows: repetition time (TR), 1,770 ms; echo time (TE), 2.8 ms; flip angle, 8°; number of excitations (NEX), 1; slice thickness, 1 mm; and field of view (FOV), 24 cm × 24 cm; meanwhile, the parameters for the 3.0T system were as follows: TR, 6.2 ms; TE, 2.4 ms; flip angle, 8°; NEX, 1; slice thickness, 1.23 mm; and FOV, 25.6 cm × 25.6 cm.

Intravenous contrast was administered under the same protocol for both scanners. Gadobenate dimeglumine (MultiHance; Bracco Imaging SpA, Milan, Italy) was injected at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg body weight (equivalent to 0.2 mL/kg for a 0.5 mmol/mL formulation) with a manual bolus injection, which was followed by a saline flush of up to 30 mL. Postcontrast image acquisitions were initiated at a predefined timepoint between 2 to 5 minutes after contrast injection. The specific time window of each individual was determined based on the routine clinical MRI protocol for contrast-enhanced evaluation of the target lesions or clinical indications in our center.

AI-amplified contrast enhancement

As shown in Figure 2, all precontrast and standard-dose postcontrast 3D T1w images were processed to generate AI-amplified contrast-enhanced images with FDA-cleared, deep learning-based AiMIFY software (Bracco Imaging SpA). The underlying deep learning model is based on a pretrained U-Net-based architecture, which was originally developed to predict standard-dose contrast-enhanced images from corresponding precontrast and low-dose images (26). This approach is based on the assumption that the contrast signal in standard-dose images represents a nonlinear scaling of the noisy contrast uptake observed between the two input images (26). Through training, the model learns a nonlinear transformation that augments contrast-related information from low-dose to standard-dose images. At inference, when precontrast and standard-dose images are provided, the same learned transformation is applied and effectively extrapolates along the continuous contrast manifold, resulting in the further amplification of contrast. Thus, the model operates as a relative contrast enhancement operator rather than as a facilitator of dose-specific mapping. It generalizes to the pre-standard-dose setting without modification, and no additional retraining or fine-tuning is required to produce contrast-boosted images with higher effective contrast appearance.

Image-processing workflow with AiMIFY software for contrast amplification. AI, artificial intelligence.

The model was trained on a multi-institutional dataset acquired from various scanners and vendors to enhance generalizability (26). Specifically, 56 cases were used for training and 13 cases for validation, with careful selection being applied to ensure approximately balanced representation across institutions and scanner types. The validation set was used for hyperparameter tuning and selection of optimal loss weighting. Model training was performed over 100 epochs with the Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.001; batch size 8), and convergence was assessed based on validation loss and a visual inspection of reconstruction quality on representative cases.

Regarding preprocessing, input images underwent normalization and denoising prior to inference. Specifically, an FDA-cleared denoising module was applied to both precontrast and postcontrast images to reduce noise and improve signal consistency, which stabilized the learned contrast transformation. A second denoising step was applied after contrast boosting to further suppress residual noise and artifacts, improving overall image quality.

The model is suitable for multivendor and multi-field-strength inference, as it was trained and validated on heterogeneous data acquired from multiple institutions, scanners, and field strengths (0.3T, 1.5T, and 3T), supporting its robustness in real-world clinical settings. Importantly, for optimal performance, the pre-contrast and post-contrast inputs should be acquired using consistent imaging settings, including sequence type, orientation, resolution, and acquisition parameters. Significant differences in acquisition settings may introduce inconsistencies that can affect the learned contrast transformation. Therefore, the model is intended to operate on paired inputs that are matched in imaging protocol and spatial alignment, as is standard in routine clinical CE-MRI workflows.

The model assumes reasonable spatial alignment between precontrast and postcontrast inputs. Significant misregistration between inputs may affect contrast amplification performance and image quality.

Quantitative assessment

Quantitative analysis was independently performed by two board-certified radiologists (P.Y. and L.Z., with 3 years of experience, respectively), each blinded to the enhancement method. For each patient, regions of interest (ROIs) were manually placed on the enhanced lesion (lesion ROI) and on adjacent normal-appearing white matter of similar size (background brain ROI). Large vessels and artifacts were carefully avoided. Quantitative metrics included CNR, lesion-to-brain ratio (LBR), and contrast enhancement percentage (CEP). These metrics were calculated for both standard contrast-enhanced and AI-amplified images according to the following formulae:

❖ CNR = (SIlesion – SIbrain)/SDbrain;

❖ CNR = (SIlesion – SIbrain)/SDbrain;

❖ LBR = SIlesion/SIbrain;

❖ LBR = SIlesion/SIbrain;

❖ CEP = (SIlesion – pre-SIlesion)/pre-SIlesion.

❖ CEP = (SIlesion – pre-SIlesion)/pre-SIlesion.

In these formulae, SIlesion is the signal intensity (SI) of the lesion ROI, SIbrain is the SI of the background brain ROI, SDbrain is the standard deviation of SI of the background brain ROI, and pre-SIlesion is the precontrast SI of the lesion ROI. For patients presenting with more than three enhanced lesions, up to three representative lesions were selected based on the size and degree of enhancement. Mean values of CNR, LBR, and CEP were calculated for subsequent analysis.

Qualitative assessment

Qualitative assessment was independently performed by the same two readers, who were blinded to both the image enhancement method and clinical diagnosis. Standard contrast-enhanced and AI-amplified images were displayed side by side. Corresponding precontrast images were available as optional references. Lesion visualization was evaluated with a 4-point Likert scale for border delineation, internal morphology, and contrast enhancement. These parameters were graded as 1 [poor (internal morphology) or none (border delineation and contrast enhancement)], 2 (moderate), 3 (good), or 4 (excellent) (17,18). For each patient with more than three lesions, the mean score of up to three representative lesions was calculated for further analysis. In addition, diagnostic confidence was graded with a 4-point Likert scale as follows: 1= nondiagnostic, 2= low confidence, 3= good confidence, and 4= excellent confidence.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Quantitative and qualitative metrics between AI-amplified and standard-dose contrast images were compared via the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with STATA v. 18 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

The mean age was 51.12±12.56 years (range, 7–70 years), including 17 males and 24 females. MRI examinations were performed on 1.5T scanners in 17 patients and on 3.0T scanners in 24 patients. The majority of patients presented with a single enhancing lesion (n=36), while one patient had two lesions and four patients had three or more lesions. The mean lesion size was 31.34±16.95 mm (range, 5–75 mm). Clinical indications included meningioma (n=17), glioma (n=3), metastasis (n=9), other brain tumors (n=7), and other abnormalities (n=5). Quantitative assessment

Quantitative analysis demonstrated that AI-amplified images achieved significantly higher CNR, LBR, and CEP values compared with standard contrast-enhanced images for both readers (Table 2). For Reader 1, the mean CNR increased from 20.33±23.28 with standard-dose contrast to 82.56±41.15 with AI-amplified contrast (P<0.001), and similar improvements were observed for Reader 2, with an increase from 20.76±12.00 to 87.91±50.30 (P<0.001). The mean LBR was also significantly higher on AI-amplified images for both readers (Reader 1: 2.86±0.64 vs. 1.78±0.48; Reader 2: 2.83±0.53 vs. 1.76±0.40; all P values <0.001). The CEP showed marked increases with AI-amplified contrast, and was nearly double that observed with standard-dose contrast for both readers (Reader 1: 393.00%±188.40% vs. 201.20%±111.32%; Reader 2: 382.26%±176.71% vs. 197.57%±109.90%; P<0.001).

Qualitative assessment

The qualitative evaluation results are summarized in Table 3. AI-amplified contrast-enhanced images demonstrated significantly higher scores for border delineation and contrast enhancement as compared with standard-dose contrast-enhanced images for both readers. Border delineation scores were higher with AI-amplified contrast for Reader 1 (3.38±0.61 vs. 3.13±0.59; P<0.001) and Reader 2 (3.46±0.60 vs. 3.00±0.63; P<0.001). Contrast enhancement was rated substantially better on AI-amplified images by both readers (Reader 1: 3.88±0.31 vs. 2.94±0.24; Reader 2: 3.85±0.42 vs. 2.90±0.49; all P<0.001). However, lesion internal morphology scores did not differ significantly between AI-amplified and standard-dose images for either reader (all P values >0.05).

Discussion

In this prospective study, we demonstrated that AI-amplified contrast-enhanced brain MRI images generated with an FDA-cleared deep learning software significantly improved quantitative contrast metrics and qualitative lesion visualization compared with conventional standard-dose contrast-enhanced images. Specifically, AI-amplified images yielded markedly higher CNR, LBR, and CEP, as well as superior reader-rated scores for lesion border delineation and contrast enhancement. These findings indicate that AI-based contrast amplification can substantially enhance the visual and quantitative conspicuity of enhancing brain lesions without increasing the administered gadolinium dose.

The observed improvement in the CNR is likely attributable to the amplification of contrast-related signal differences between lesions and normal brain tissue, as well as the effective suppression of background noise provided by the denoising module. Because the CNR depends on both signal contrast and image noise, even modest noise reduction can result in a substantially increase in the CNR. In contrast, the LBR and CEP primarily reflect the relative enhancement of lesions compared with background tissue or the precontrast signal and are less influenced by denoising. Consequently, we observed a more than fourfold higher CNR but only a 1.5-fold higher LBR and a nearly twofold higher CEP after AI amplification. The degree of contrast amplification in our study is consistent with that reported previously (27-29). Haase et al. reported that the CNR, LBR, and CEP in artificial double-dose images were nearly fourfold, 1.5-fold, and twofold higher those in true standard-dose brain metastasis images, respectively, even exceeding values observed with true double-dose images (29). Fringuello Mingo et al. observed increases of 155% in CNR and 34% in LBR for virtual double-dose images as compared with standard-dose images (28). Another study that used a deep learning-based contrast-boosting approach reported increases in the CNR from 9.1 to 44.5, the LBR from 1.31 to 1.66, and the CEP from 85.6% to 112.4% (27). The consistent and significant increases across studies and readers suggest that the AI algorithm robustly enhances contrast-related information rather than producing reader-dependent or incidental effects.

Qualitative assessment further supported the quantitative findings in our study. Both readers rated AI-amplified images significantly higher in terms of lesion border delineation (3.38 vs. 3.13; 3.46 vs. 3.00) and contrast enhancement (3.88 vs. 2.94; 3.85 vs. 2.90). Improved border delineation may result in more confident lesion detection and potentially improve downstream lesion measurement and treatment planning. These results are consistent with previous analyses demonstrating that AI-amplified images provide better lesion visualization and enhance the visibility, delineation, and brightness of small or weakly enhancing lesions (28). Notably, the internal lesion morphology scores did not differ significantly between AI-amplified and standard contrast images for either reader in our study (Reader 1: 3.13 vs. 3.17; Reader 2: 2.83 vs. 3.90). This discrepancy may be partly explained by the high proportion (41.5%) of meningiomas in our cohort. Meningiomas typically exhibit homogeneous internal architecture and uniform enhancement at standard contrast doses. Consequently, AI amplification, although increasing overall SI, may not provide additional morphologic details. Moreover, the subjective evaluation of internal morphology primarily depends on the recognition of structural heterogeneity rather than on enhancement intensity alone, which may limit the incremental value of AI-amplified contrast enhancement. Future studies focusing on lesions with greater internal heterogeneity, such as brain metastases and high-grade gliomas (30,31), may further clarify the role of AI-amplified contrast in internal morphologic characterization.

Beyond quantitative metrics and lesion visualization, several studies have reported superior lesion detection performance with use of AI-amplified images as compared with standard contrast-enhanced images (27,29). Bone et al. found that deep learning-processed echo T1w magnetic resonance (MR) images from standard-dose acquisitions increased lesion detection sensitivity by up to 16% (27). A similar improvement in sensitivity in detecting brain metastases, particularly small lesions (≤5 mm), was reported by Haase et al. (29). Despite not assessing detection performance, our study evaluated diagnostic confidence to determine the incremental clinical benefit of AI-amplified images. AI-amplified images demonstrated significantly higher diagnostic confidence than did standard-dose images for both readers (Reader 1: 3.59 vs. 3.20; Reader 2: 3.48 vs. 3.05; both P values <0.001). These findings suggest that AI-amplified images may provide practical clinical benefit beyond improved conspicuity by increasing radiologists’ confidence during image interpretation. However, the improved qualitative scores and higher diagnostic confidence observed in our study should be interpreted with caution. Because lesion detection sensitivity, specificity, and overall diagnostic accuracy were not directly assessed, these findings should not be considered evidence of improved diagnostic performance. Increased lesion conspicuity and reader confidence do not necessarily translate into superior lesion detection or more accurate clinical diagnosis. Further studies incorporating lesion-level detection analysis and histopathologic or longitudinal reference standards are needed to determine whether AI-amplified contrast enhancement can improve true diagnostic accuracy and clinical decision-making.

Despite having a similar overall objective to that of previous work (26), our study provides certain novel contributions. The training and validation of the FDA-cleared AI examined in this study were conducted primarily in Western populations (26), but we evaluated its utility in a Chinese clinical cohort. External validation across different populations, healthcare systems, scanners, and imaging workflows may help determine the robustness and generalizability of this commercially available AI tool intended for clinical use. In addition, we employed a prospective design with consecutively enrolled real-world patients, which implies greater clinical applicability. Finally, diagnostic confidence was evaluated as an additional clinically relevant endpoint, providing further insights into the practical benefits for radiologists.

This study involved several limitations that should be addressed. First, the sample size was relatively small, and the study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study primarily focused on quantitative contrast metrics and qualitative visualization scores rather than on diagnostic performance endpoints. Lesion detection sensitivity, specificity, and overall diagnostic accuracy were not directly assessed. Third, the lesion size and clinical indication distribution might have been skewed, with most enhancing lesions in the cohort being relatively large. The performance of AI-amplified contrast enhancement was not specifically evaluated in small lesions, particularly those with a maximum diameter of less than 5 mm, but small and weakly enhancing lesions are known to be challenging to detect and are more susceptible to noise and partial volume effects (32). Thus, the extent to which AI-based contrast amplification may improve visualization or detection of subcentimeter lesions remains uncertain. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and a more balanced representation of small lesions are warranted to further validate these findings and to assess the clinical impact of AI-amplified contrast enhancement on diagnostic accuracy, lesion detection sensitivity, and clinical decision-making. Finally, another potential limitation relates to image registration between precontrast and postcontrast inputs. Although explicit motion correction was not applied during preprocessing, the AI model assumes adequate spatial alignment between paired images. Significant patient motion or misregistration may potentially introduce artificial enhancement or image artifacts. Although severe motion artifacts were excluded in our study, and minor misalignments may be partially mitigated by the denoising and reconstruction pipeline, the robustness of AI-amplified contrast enhancement under substantial motion conditions remains to be further investigated.

Conclusions

This prospective study demonstrated that AI-based contrast amplification performed with an FDA-cleared deep learning software can significantly enhance quantitative contrast metrics, qualitative lesion visualization, and diagnostic confidence on standard contrast-enhanced brain MRI. These results suggest that AI-amplified imaging may provide a practical approach for improving lesion conspicuity and radiologist confidence without increasing gadolinium dose. However, further studies are needed to determine whether these improvements translate into enhanced lesion detection performance and diagnostic accuracy.
