Neural Decision-Making and Affective Dynamics in Weight Regain after Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery: A Multimodal Longitudinal Computational Psychiatry Study
Authors: Zhihong Li, Qianqian Yang, Yalian Wang, Beibei Li, Yiliminuer Ahemai, Zimei Li, Shaohua Wang, Bujian Pan, Mingdong Lu, Wenwen Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo determine whether weight regain after metabolic bariatric surgery reflects a systematic reorganization of neural decision signals elicited by food cues and longitudinal affective states, and to develop an interpretable, trackable, and stratifiable multimodal computational psychiatry framework.MethodsThis study was based on a multi-timepoint longitudinal cohort of patients undergoing primary metabolic bariatric surgery, integrating a standardized food-choice task, 64-channel EEG, psychometric measures, and longitudinal affective-state data. Food stimuli were first standardized across five domains: subjective experience, health/naturalness attributes, reward-control attributes, nutritional composition, and visual features. EEG data underwent predefined quality-control procedures, after which N2, P3, centroparietal positivity, feedback-related negativity, feedback P3, and frontal theta indices were extracted. Food-choice behavior was decomposed using a hierarchical drift-diffusion model into drift rate, boundary separation, starting point, and non-decision time. A multimodal RT-by-frontal-theta interaction model was then fitted, and GIMME dynamic network modelling was used to characterize shared, subgroup-specific, and individual pathways within affect-reward-control states.ResultsFood stimuli were decomposed into 39 auditable variables, forming a continuous representational space spanning health, reward, control, nutritional, and visual-salience dimensions, with high reliability across domains. EEG quality control preserved clinically expected heterogeneity while excluding recordings at high risk of biasing ERP and time-frequency estimates. Behavioral and DDM results showed that the weight-regain group had longer mean reaction times than the maintained-weight-loss group (0.726 s vs. 0.651 s), together with lower drift rate (0.93 vs. 1.27), lower boundary separation (1.20 vs. 1.37), lower starting point (0.42 vs. 0.51), and longer non-decision time (0.31 s vs. 0.27 s). ERP findings indicated that weight-regain-related differences spanned early control/conflict monitoring, posterior stimulus evaluation, evidence accumulation, and feedback processing, with posterior P3/CPP and feedback-related components contributing the dominant late-stage separation. The multimodal interaction model showed an RT-by-frontal-theta interaction of approximately 0.92% points in the primary adjusted model (95% CI, 0.36–1.49), with a consistent direction across alternative model specifications. GIMME identified stable affect-reward-control dynamic pathways and separated three psychological dynamic subtypes: affect-reactive, reward-sensitized, and control-resilient.ConclusionWeight regain after metabolic bariatric surgery is not merely an endpoint of body-weight change, but a dynamic risk process shaped jointly by food-cue evaluation, evidence-accumulation efficiency, frontal control modulation, and longitudinal affect-reward state transitions. This multimodal computational framework reframes postoperative weight regain as a mechanistically interpretable neurobehavioral phenotype, providing a translational basis for earlier risk stratification, individualized follow-up, and interventions targeting emotion, craving, and control.

Introduction

Obesity has become one of the chronic diseases with the broadest systemic spillover effects. Global pooled data indicate that, by 2022, more than one billion children, adolescents, and adults were living with obesity, underscoring that the burden of weight-related disease has moved beyond isolated metabolic risk to encompass long-term neurobehavioral, mental-health, and care-system pressures [1]. For severe obesity and its metabolic complications, metabolic and bariatric surgery remains one of the most evidence-based and durable treatment strategies. The 2022 ASMBS/IFSO guidelines, together with subsequent reviews of long-term outcomes, emphasize that surgery should be embedded within chronic metabolic disease management rather than treated as a stand-alone anatomical intervention [2, 3]. Yet long-term weight trajectories remain markedly heterogeneous after the same anatomical procedure, making postoperative weight regain a central issue for evaluating treatment durability and designing individualized follow-up strategies. Research on weight regain has shifted from simply describing recurrent weight gain to standardizing definitions, early identification, and stratified management. Recent recommendations distinguish weight recurrence, partial response, and nonresponse to reduce interpretive bias caused by inconsistent definitions across studies [4]. Large cohort studies have also shown that different measures of weight regain vary in their association with clinical outcomes, indicating that weight regain itself should be placed within a reproducible and interpretable measurement framework [5]. Mechanistically, reduced energy expenditure, appetite regulation, adipose-tissue signalling, and neuroendocrine feedback after weight loss may jointly generate physiological pressure against weight maintenance. These pressures interact with postoperative food-cue exposure, eating control, emotional burden, and daily-life contexts, potentially shifting some patients from an initial weight-loss trajectory toward recurrent weight gain [6]. Computational models have recently begun to predict long-term postoperative weight trajectories. For example, the multinational SOPHIA cohort developed and validated an interpretable machine-learning model using baseline clinical variables to predict 5-year weight trajectories after bariatric surgery, demonstrating that body-weight outcomes can be formalized as long-term, heterogeneous, and quantifiable dynamic processes [7]. Such prediction tools are valuable for trajectory estimation, but they remain limited in explaining how patients make decisions in response to real food cues, when neural control signals deviate, and how affective states sustain risk in everyday life. In other words, postoperative weight regain requires more than risk prediction; it requires a mechanistic model that links behavior, neural signals, and longitudinal psychological dynamics. The real-world challenge faced by postoperative patients occurs in repeated food-choice contexts. Metabolic and bariatric surgery can alter gut-brain signalling, appetite hormones, taste preferences, and reward responsivity, but it does not remove patients from environments rich in high-energy-density and highly salient food cues [8]. Food-cue reactivity research shows that visual, contextual, and emotional cues can rapidly recruit reward, memory, and executive-control systems, thereby amplifying craving, promoting eating initiation, or weakening control [9]. Contemporary feeding neuroscience further decomposes eating behavior into appetitive, consummatory, and terminating phases, each shaped jointly by energy state, reward value, and control signals [10]. This provides a direct theoretical basis for the present study’s multidimensional food-stimulus standardization, food-choice task, and neural decision modeling. Existing postoperative studies, however, often rely on weight endpoints, questionnaire scores, or mean behavioral indices. These measures can quantify risk severity but have limited ability to locate when risk emerges within a single choice process or in what computational form it is expressed. The drift-diffusion model (DDM) offers a mechanistic language for food choice: health and taste-related attributes do not necessarily enter decisions simultaneously or with equal weight, but can produce separable differences in latency, evidence-accumulation rate, and boundary strategy [11]. EEG provides millisecond temporal resolution, allowing frontocentral control/conflict processing, posterior stimulus evaluation, evidence accumulation, feedback monitoring, and frontal theta control signals to be placed on a common decision timeline [12, 13]. Combining ERP, time-frequency activity, and DDM parameters therefore makes it possible to decompose surface phenomena such as slower responses or less healthy choices into latent differences in evidence quality, decision caution, non-decision processing, and control-resource recruitment.

Postoperative weight regain, however, does not occur only within a single laboratory food-choice trial. Intensive longitudinal work has shown that negative affect and loss-of-control eating exhibit time-varying relationships in daily life, and that these relationships may change across postoperative years [14]. Simple average affect scores can obscure within-person dynamics. By contrast, dynamic network approaches such as GIMME can estimate group-level shared pathways, subgroup-specific pathways, and individual-specific pathways, while distinguishing contemporaneous state associations from lagged relations between adjacent assessments [15]. This allows affect, craving, hunger, loss of control, and self-efficacy to be modeled not merely as background covariates, but as temporal processes that may drive postoperative risk persistence or recovery. Computational psychiatry provides a conceptual solution to this problem: rather than merely describing clinical phenotypes, it seeks to connect observable behavior, latent cognitive processes, neural signals, and longitudinal symptom dynamics through formal models [16]. At the same time, the translation of computational models into clinical research must confront challenges of reproducibility, parameter reliability, model diagnostics, and interpretability [17]. An auditable food-stimulus space, interpretable neural decision metrics, and ecologically grounded longitudinal affective dynamics therefore provide a more persuasive framework.

On this basis, we developed a multimodal longitudinal computational psychiatry study to test whether weight regain after metabolic and bariatric surgery is expressed at the longitudinal intersection of neural decision signals and daily affective dynamics. We first standardized food stimuli across subjective, nutritional, reward-control, and visual dimensions. We then combined clinical EEG, ERP/frontal theta measures, and drift-diffusion modeling to characterize the temporal course and latent evidence-accumulation mechanisms of food choice. Finally, using intensive longitudinal state data and GIMME dynamic networks, we identified group-level shared pathways and psychological dynamic subtypes within affect-reward-control systems. We hypothesized that weight regain would not be merely an endpoint on the postoperative weight curve, but a computable risk process jointly shaped by abnormal evidence accumulation under food cues, dysregulated control-signal modulation, and transitions in real-life affective states.

Methods

Participants

Participants were adults with severe obesity undergoing primary metabolic and bariatric surgery. Inclusion criteria were: age 18–55 years; meeting guideline-based indications for metabolic and bariatric surgery; scheduled to undergo primary Roux-en-Y gastric bypass or sleeve gastrectomy; and able to understand the study procedures and provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: contraindications to EEG recording, such as severe scalp injury or inability to cooperate with testing; a diagnosed severe psychiatric disorder, including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or active substance abuse or dependence; inability to complete questionnaires or behavioral tasks; and a history of major gastrointestinal resection. The main cohort included 141 patients. All patients completed at least baseline behavioral and clinical assessments and were included in postoperative weight-trajectory and task-behavior analyses. Across the baseline, 6-month, and 12-month visit windows, 423 EEG/behavioral assessment sessions were planned. Of these, 330 sessions had auditable raw EEG records and entered preprocessing quality control. After predefined quality gates, 246 sessions from 127 patients entered the primary EEG, ERP, drift-diffusion, and multimodal interaction analyses. These 127 patients also constituted the sample for the intensive longitudinal GIMME analysis.

Clinical Outcomes and Definition of Weight Regain

Clinical outcomes focused on postoperative weight change, including percentage total weight loss, percentage excess weight loss, and weight-regain status during follow-up. Weight regain was defined as regain of more than 15% of the excess weight previously lost after the postoperative nadir weight, and this definition was used at the 12-month follow-up to classify patients into maintained-weight-loss and weight-regain groups. Clinical covariates included age, sex, baseline body mass index (BMI), surgical procedure, comorbidities, visit time point, and postoperative follow-up adherence. All body-weight measurements were obtained using calibrated equipment and recorded to one decimal place.

Psychometric Measures and Intensive Longitudinal Assessment

Core psychometric measures included the Dutch Eating Behavior Questionnaire, the Food Cravings Questionnaire-Trait, the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-21, and the negative urgency subscale of the UPPS-P Impulsive Behavior Scale. These instruments were used to capture disordered eating tendency, trait food craving, emotional distress burden, and impulsive-control deficits under negative affect, respectively. Participants completed the questionnaires independently in a quiet setting; study staff then checked for missing items, logical inconsistencies, and abnormal completion time. Intensive longitudinal assessments focused on state variables including negative affect, stress, anxiety, craving, hunger, loss of control, and self-efficacy. Each assessment recorded state intensity and timestamp, and information on missingness, delay, and unequal intervals was retained for subsequent dynamic modeling. This design allowed between-person mean differences to be distinguished from within-person state transitions, enabling later analyses to identify shared, subgroup-specific, and individual-specific dynamic pathways.

Food-Stimulus Standardization and Food-Choice Task

Food stimuli were multidimensionally standardized before the experiment. For each image, features were extracted across subjective experience, health/naturalness attributes, reward-control attributes, nutritional composition, and visual characteristics. Subjective experience included craving, perceived healthfulness, familiarity, expected taste, pleasure, arousal, valence, consumption frequency, and portion preference. Health/naturalness attributes included health belief, naturalness, perceived additives, processing awareness, freshness, perceived protein/fiber content, nutrient density, and expected satiety. Reward-control attributes included guilt, nostalgia, craving intensity, self-control difficulty, regret, sensory appeal, visual appeal, consumption context, convenience, and price. Nutritional composition included calories, fat, sugar, sodium, energy density, and processing level. Visual features included visual complexity, color salience, luminance contrast, and target area. Stimulus reliability was assessed through rating consistency, internal consistency, and feature reproducibility. The food-choice task was conducted in a controlled experimental environment. In each trial, participants viewed a standardized food stimulus and responded according to their immediate choice tendency. The task recorded choice, reaction time, stimulus category, event timestamps, and feedback information, all synchronized with the EEG system through event markers. Extremely short reaction times, reaction times exceeding three standard deviations above the individual mean, and trials with missing responses were removed according to predefined rules. Task outputs were used to compute behavioral choice indices, mean reaction time, DDM parameters, and trial-level data synchronized with EEG time-domain and time-frequency features.

EEG Acquisition and Event Synchronization

EEG was recorded using a 64-channel system with simultaneous electrooculography (EOG) recording at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. Online filtering was kept as broad as possible to avoid unduly restricting subsequent offline analyses. Stimulus presentation, evaluative responses, and feedback events were synchronized with EEG data through event markers, and raw event logs were retained to audit trigger order and potential latency. Before acquisition, channel impedance, cap fit, operator notes, sleep status, caffeine intake, medication use, and risk of movement artifacts were recorded as clinical contextual information to support later quality control and sensitivity analyses.

EEG Preprocessing and Quality Control

EEG preprocessing followed an auditable multistage pipeline. Continuous data first underwent event-consistency checks, bad-channel detection, re-referencing, and initial artifact screening. The main ERP/CPP analysis branch used offline filtering at 0.1–40 Hz. An ICA-assisted branch used a 1-Hz high-pass copy to improve the stability of independent component separation. Components related to eye movements, blinks, cardiac activity, and muscle activity were removed on the basis of independent-component maps, time series, and topographic features. Final ERP and time-domain features were extracted from the 0.1-Hz high-pass data. When needed, data were downsampled to 250–500 Hz after anti-aliasing filtering to improve computational efficiency. Quality control was performed at the session level and included feedback-epoch retention, decision-epoch retention, number of bad channels, number of removed independent components, line-noise estimates, muscle-noise estimates, PSD noise index, and impedance distribution. Inclusion and exclusion decisions followed predefined gating rules. For each session, the bad-channel list, ICA component rejection record, PSD curve, and clinical acquisition context were retained. All quality-control metrics were manually reviewed before study analysis to reduce outcome-driven processing bias.

ERP and Time-Frequency Feature Extraction

Stimulus-locked ERPs were time-locked to stimulus onset at 0 ms, and feedback-locked ERPs were time-locked to feedback onset at 0 ms. Baseline correction was performed using a pre-event window. Core components included frontocentral N2, posterior P3, CPP, FRN, and feedback P3. N2 was defined over a frontocentral region of interest (ROI) within 220–320 ms. P3 was defined over a posterior ROI within 340–580 ms. CPP was defined over a centroparietal ROI within 320–700 ms. FRN was defined within 220–340 ms after feedback onset, and feedback P3 within 300–520 ms after feedback onset. Component amplitudes were described using ROI means, time-window means, and session-level distributions, and single-trial ERP images were used to assess whether effects were driven by a small number of extreme trials. Time-frequency analysis focused on frontal theta activity. After preprocessing, 4–8 Hz power was extracted from the frontal ROI and log-transformed and standardized at the trial or session level. Frontal theta was entered into the multimodal interaction model as a neural signal related to control/conflict monitoring and was jointly used with mean reaction time, DDM parameters, ERP components, and clinical psychological variables to explain food-choice probability.

Drift-Diffusion Modeling

Food-choice behavior was modeled using a hierarchical drift-diffusion model. The model jointly used choice outcomes and reaction-time distributions to estimate drift rate, boundary separation, starting-point bias, and non-decision time. Drift rate indexed evidence-accumulation efficiency, boundary separation indexed decision caution, starting-point bias indexed pre-response tendency, and non-decision time indexed non-decision processes such as stimulus encoding and motor response. Parameters were estimated at both individual and group levels, and posterior differences between the maintained-weight-loss and weight-regain groups were compared. Model diagnostics included chain convergence, effective sample size, posterior predictive checks, and conditional reaction-time quantile checks. The main reported outputs were group-level parameter differences, posterior uncertainty, and the agreement between observed and predicted values across response boundaries. DDM parameters were subsequently entered into the multimodal interaction model as computational decision features, jointly with EEG and psychological state variables, to explain choice risk associated with postoperative weight regain.

Multimodal Interaction Model and Robustness Analyses

The multimodal interaction model tested whether frontal theta moderated the relationship between reaction time and food choice. The outcome was adjusted choice probability or choice difference. Mean reaction time was the primary behavioral predictor, frontal theta was the moderator, and covariates included visit time, BMI, DASS-21, DEBQ/FCQ-T, UPPS-P negative urgency, EEG quality indices, ICA burden, P3/CPP, DDM drift rate, and boundary separation. The RT ? theta interaction quantified the contribution of behavioral latency to choice risk under different frontal control-signal contexts. To evaluate robustness, the model was re-estimated under multiple specifications, including the primary adjusted model, neural-interaction-only model, BMI-and-visit model, psychometric-scale model, EEG quality-control model, DDM-parameter model, and stratified models by psychological burden, visit month, weight outcome, and EEG retention quality. Heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors were used. Conditional simple slopes illustrated the predictive effect of reaction time on choice at different theta levels, and integrated area under the curve (iAUC) summarized RT-contrast effects across the follow-up trajectory.

GIMME Modeling of Intensive Longitudinal States

Intensive longitudinal psychological states were modeled using group iterative multiple model estimation. GIMME first identified group-level pathways that recurred across the majority of participants and then iteratively added subgroup- and individual-specific pathways, thereby preserving both common mechanisms and individual heterogeneity. The model included contemporaneous residual coupling within assessment prompts and lagged vector autoregressive [VAR(1)] paths between adjacent prompts, with missingness and unequal assessment intervals audited. State variables were organized into affect, reward, and control systems and included negative affect, stress, anxiety, craving, hunger, loss of control, and self-efficacy. Edge direction, sign, mean beta, and prevalence were used to describe dynamic pathway evidence. Subtype identification was based on retained pathway patterns and pathway-family structure, classifying participants into affect-reactive, reward-sensitized, and control-resilient subtypes. Subtype stability was evaluated using pathway prevalence, sign consistency, and clinical/psychological anchoring indicators.

Statistical Analysis and Ethics

Computational analyses were performed in Python and R environments. R version 4.3.1 was used for basic statistical analyses. Python version 3.8.1 was used for data cleaning and preprocessing. MNE-Python was used for EEG signal processing and analysis, the HDDM package for drift-diffusion modeling, and the gimme package for group iterative multiple model estimation. Continuous variables were described as means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges according to distributional properties. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons used t tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, or chi-square tests according to variable type. Multiple comparisons were controlled at the levels of components, time windows, and model parameters.

The study was approved by the relevant institutional ethics committee. All participants provided written informed consent. Personal information and raw EEG data were anonymized before analysis and stored using permission-based access controls.

Results

Multidimensional Food-Stimulus Standardization

To attribute subsequent neural decision effects to individual processing differences rather than stimulus-material bias, we first constructed a multilevel food-stimulus representational space. Figure 1a shows that the stimulus set was decomposed into 39 auditable variables across five domains: subjective experience, health/naturalness attributes, reward-control attributes, nutritional composition, and visual features. Subjective, health, and reward-control dimensions were derived from participant ratings, nutritional features from labels or databases, and visual features from computer-vision extraction. This structure allowed each food stimulus to be quantitatively represented at three levels: psychological value, nutritional load, and perceptual salience. Figure 1b shows that nine food categories formed continuous rather than binary gradients across the 39 variables. Fresh fruit, vegetables, whole grains, and lean proteins were advantaged on health belief, naturalness, nutrient density, and expected satiety, whereas sweets, salty snacks, fast food, and sugar-sweetened beverages carried higher loads on craving intensity, guilt, self-control difficulty, convenience, calories, fat, sugar, and energy density. This structure reframed food choice from a simple category judgment into a multivariable decision problem spanning health, reward, control, and nutritional dimensions. Figure 1c projects food stimuli into a two-dimensional space defined by a health-control index and a reward-value index. Unprocessed, minimally processed, and ultra-processed foods showed separable but not fully disconnected distributions, and the balanced subset covered the main stimulus range, reducing the likelihood that later neural effects would be dominated by extreme stimuli. Figure 1d further shows interpretable nutritional-perceptual profiles across categories for calories, fat, sugar, sodium, energy density, nutrient density, expected satiety, and visual complexity. Figure 1e shows high reliability across domains: ICCs for subjective, health-belief, and reward-control dimensions were 0.86, 0.88, and 0.84, respectively, while reproducibility indices for nutritional and visual features were 0.97 and 0.91.

EEG Preprocessing and Quality Control

A multigate quality-control procedure identified clinical EEG records suitable for neural computational modeling. Figure 2a shows that, among 330 clinical EEG sessions, feedback-epoch retention and ICA correction burden covered all sessions. Line-noise estimation and bad-channel/session gates retained 327 sessions each; the muscle-noise gate retained 310 sessions; PSD noise index and decision-epoch retention retained 287 and 286 sessions, respectively; and 246 sessions ultimately passed all analysis gates. This procedure preserved the heterogeneity expected in real clinical EEG while excluding high-risk recordings that could directly bias time-frequency and ERP estimates. Figure 2b shows that the number of bad channels, number of removed independent components, decision-epoch retention, and PSD noise were continuously distributed across baseline, 6-month, 12-month, included-session, and excluded-session strata, rather than being driven by a single visit or anomalous batch. Figure 2c orders four quality metrics across the session sequence, showing local fluctuations in bad channels, IC removal, decision retention, and PSD noise that are expected in clinical acquisition, without systematic drift. Figure 2d further shows only weak correlations between bad-channel count and decision-epoch retention: rho = -0.15 in included sessions and rho = -0.05 in excluded sessions. Figure 2e links quality gates to the acquisition context, recording clinical sources of interference such as low sleep, recent caffeine intake, sedative medication, movement artifact/high movement, poor cap fit, and protocol deviation. Movement-related issues and low sleep were more common quality-risk sources, whereas poor cap fit was relatively uncommon. Figure 2f shows that most ROI impedances clustered near or below the 20 k? target line. Figure 2g shows that the cleaning pipeline reduced broadband noise and attenuated peaks near the line-frequency range.

Drift-Diffusion Modeling Localizes Latent Decision Dynamics Associated with Weight Regain

Behavioral differences were further decomposed into latent decision processes. The left side of Fig. 3a decomposes reaction time into stimulus encoding, decision time, and motor response, showing slower responses in the weight-regain group, with a mean reaction time of approximately 0.726 s compared with 0.651 s in the maintained-weight-loss group. The right side of Fig. 3a presents the hierarchical DDM generative structure, in which choice and reaction time are jointly modeled as functions of drift rate, boundary separation, starting-point bias, and non-decision time, with parameters allowed to vary at both individual and group levels. Figure 3b shows that latent evidence-accumulation trajectories differed in direction and slope between the two groups. The maintained-weight-loss group showed a higher drift rate and larger boundary separation, with approximate parameter values of v = 1.27, a = 1.37, z = 0.51, and t0 = 0.27 s. The weight-regain group showed lower drift rate, lower boundary separation, lower starting point, and longer non-decision time, with approximate values of v = 0.93, a = 1.20, z = 0.42, and t0 = 0.31 s. Between-group differences were expressed as a 0.34 reduction in drift rate, 0.17 reduction in boundary separation, 0.08 reduction in starting point, and 0.03-s increase in non-decision time, suggesting that choices associated with weight regain involve degraded evidence quality, reduced decision stability, and delayed early or motor-related processing. Figure 3c uses conditional reaction-time quantiles to test the joint fit of the model to choice probability and reaction-time distributions. Observed and predicted values remained aligned across low/high-calorie and control-choice boundaries, indicating that the model captured not only mean reaction-time differences but also the joint structure linking reaction-time distribution, boundary direction, and choice probability. The parallel reduction in drift rate and boundary parameters advances the weight-regain phenotype from behavioral slowing to interpretable evidence-accumulation abnormality.

ERP Results Reveal Neural Differentiation during Stimulus Evaluation, Evidence Accumulation, and Feedback Processing

Millisecond-level EEG waveforms further localized the neural processing stages at which weight-regain-related decision abnormalities emerged. Figure 4a shows that frontocentral N2 was concentrated within the 220–320 ms window, with discernible waveform differentiation between groups already present during this early control/conflict-monitoring stage. Figure 4b shows that posterior P3 and CPP gradually increased within the 340–700 ms window, with the weight-regain group showing higher posterior positivity near the peak, consistent with greater food-cue evaluation or evidence-accumulation load. Figure 4c juxtaposes scalp distributions of N2, P3, CPP, FRN, and feedback P3 with standardized between-group contrasts: posterior P3, CPP, and feedback P3 showed stronger positive differences in the weight-regain group, whereas N2 and FRN reflected directional differentiation in control- and feedback-monitoring components. Figure 4d shows feedback-locked waveforms, in which the FRN and feedback-P3 windows captured negative-positive feedback differences and subsequent feedback-evaluation responses, respectively; the weight-regain group showed stronger separation during later feedback-related processing. Figure 4e shows that posterior P3 and CPP contributed the main late differences in difference waves, while frontocentral N2 and feedback FRN provided earlier or feedback-related control signals. The single-trial ERP images in Fig. 4f show that posterior amplitude structure was continuously organized along reaction-time order rather than driven by a few extreme trials. Frontal Theta Moderates the Relationship between Reaction Time and Food Choice

The multimodal interaction model tested whether frontal control signals changed the predictive role of behavioral latency in food choice. Figure 5a shows that the model integrated clinical state, food-choice behavior, EEG control signals, DDM mechanisms, and inferential-layer variables within a single analytic framework, including BMI, DASS-21, DEBQ/FCQ-T, UPPS-P negative urgency, choice rate, mean RT, feedback, frontal theta, P3/CPP, EEG quality indices, ICA, DDM drift/boundary, HC3 robust standard errors, and grouping/follow-up inference information. Figure 5b shows the adjusted choice surface, in which mean RT and frontal theta jointly shaped choice probability. Maintained-weight-loss and weight-regain sessions showed partial separation in this two-dimensional space, suggesting that reaction time and frontal theta are not independent risk indicators but instead form a joint neurobehavioral structure for predicting choice. Figure 5c shows that the simple slope of RT increased with frontal theta level, and the HC3 95% confidence interval supported a positive moderation trend. Longer reaction times translated more strongly into choice-probability differences under higher frontal theta, indicating that control/conflict-related neural activity altered the clinical meaning of behavioral slowing. Figure 5d uses a specification curve to assess the sensitivity of the interaction effect to model specification. In the primary adjusted model, the RT and theta interaction effect was approximately 0.92% points (95% CI, 0.36 to 1.49). Across the neural-interaction-only model, BMI-and-visit model, DASS/DEBQ/FCQ/UPPS model, EEG quality-control model, and DDM drift/boundary model, the direction of effect remained generally positive. Figure 5e shows that, after sorting by clinical EEG session, RT z score, theta z score, DASS-21, DEBQ dysregulation, FCQ-T, UPPS-P negative urgency, predicted choice, and 12-month status jointly formed a continuous risk gradient. Figure 5f projects the interaction onto follow-up trajectories and the iAUC metric, with the largest predicted choice differences under high psychological burden/high theta conditions and persistence across early and later follow-up.

GIMME Psychological Dynamic Subtypes

Figure 6a shows the group-level GIMME network skeleton, which identified 15 shared pathways with a median prevalence of 83%. Negative affect, stress, and anxiety formed a highly coupled affective system and were linked through positive pathways to reward and dyscontrol states such as craving, hunger, and loss of control. Control-related pathways between self-efficacy and craving/loss of control formed potential protective routes. The consensus-edge evidence plot mapped dynamic pathways into signed beta and prevalence space. Craving-loss of control, stress-anxiety, and stress-negative affect clustered in regions with higher prevalence and larger positive beta values, forming stable excitatory or persistent pathways. Self-efficacy-related pathways entered the inhibitory region, suggesting that control resources may operate by reducing subsequent dyscontrol or reward-driven escalation. This network structure organized daily affective fluctuations, craving, and loss of control into a directional and reproducible temporal process. Figure 6b shows the three psychological dynamic subtypes. In the affect-reactive subtype, pathways from negative affect to stress, anxiety, and craving/loss of control were more prominent, indicating risk amplification driven by affective reactivity. The reward-sensitized subtype centered on reward loops among craving, hunger, and loss of control, reflecting mutual reinforcement between food-reward sensitization and dyscontrol tendency. The control-resilient subtype retained stronger self-efficacy-related control pathways, suggesting protective braking of craving and loss of control. Figure 6c compresses the network into a schematic of temporal state transitions between adjacent prompts: Aff/Crv can predict subsequent Crv/LoC escalation through a GIMME lag, or shift toward SE/Ctrl recovery under a control-braking pathway.

Discussion

The central finding of this study is that postoperative weight regain can be characterized as a neurobehavioral state spanning multiple temporal scales, rather than simply a body-weight classification at the end of follow-up. Food stimuli were not reducible to healthy versus unhealthy categories; instead, they formed a continuous pressure space across reward, control, nutrition, and visual salience. Within this space, the weight-regain group showed slower responses, lower evidence-accumulation efficiency, weaker decision stability, and more pronounced late-stage food-cue evaluation and feedback-processing differentiation. These decision signals were linked to transitions in negative affect, craving, hunger, loss of control, and self-efficacy in daily life, jointly forming distinct observational windows onto the postoperative risk process. This shifts the interpretation of weight regain from whether a patient has reached a body-weight threshold to which processes are currently pushing the patient toward a recurrent weight-gain trajectory. Recent reviews and management studies of postoperative weight recurrence emphasize that anatomical factors, appetite restoration, loss-of-control eating, psychological stress, physical inactivity, and timing of treatment escalation jointly determine long-term outcomes [18, 19, 20]. The present study provides evidence closer to the mechanistic level: lower drift rate suggests reduced quality of effective information during food choice, narrower boundaries suggest reduced decision stability, and longer non-decision time suggests delay in stimulus encoding or response execution. These parameters make the choice of high-risk foods no longer merely an overt outcome, but a process that can be decomposed into information entry, evidence accumulation, threshold setting, and action output. The neural decision findings also help explain why postoperative patients may recover risk in food-cue environments even after marked weight loss. Prior studies of postoperative cognitive control and food attention suggest that general, non-food cognitive-control tasks may not consistently predict weight-loss outcomes, whereas food-cue processing, attentional bias, and reward reinforcement may be more proximal to the real challenge of postoperative weight maintenance [21, 22, 23]. In the present study, enhanced posterior P3/CPP and feedback-related components suggest that the weight-regain group was not simply under-processing food cues. Rather, these patients may have recruited greater evaluative resources under highly salient food cues without translating those resources into stable controlled choices. The convergence of P3/CPP, frontal theta, and DDM parameters turns neural signals from descriptive waveform differences into mechanistic clues about evidence accumulation and choice stability. Reaction time also cannot be interpreted linearly as greater caution or greater difficulty. Evidence-accumulation models allow the same reaction-time difference to be decomposed into combinations of drift rate, boundary separation, starting point, and non-decision time, and these combinations may carry different clinical meanings [24, 25]. In this study, the weight-regain group simultaneously showed lower drift rate, lower boundary separation, and longer non-decision time. Their slower responses therefore do not appear to reflect simple caution, but rather the superposition of inefficient evidence accumulation and processing delay. The RT and theta interaction further shows that frontal control signals change the meaning of behavioral latency: slow responses become clinically interpretable only when placed within their control-signal context. GIMME analysis further indicated that the affect-reactive, reward-sensitized, and control-resilient groups were not subjectively labelled clusters, but dynamic subtypes defined by retained pathway patterns, edge direction, edge strength, and prevalence. Recent work emphasizes that the value of these methods lies in preserving both group commonality and individual heterogeneity while auditing direction recovery, lagged structure, and contemporaneous residual relations at the model level [26, 27]. The psychological dynamic subtypes in this study should therefore not be understood as traditional questionnaire clusters, but as temporal structures describing how affect, reward, and control states drive one another in real life.

A notable implication of this work is support for an earlier and more mechanism-oriented postoperative follow-up framework. Current management of postoperative recurrence increasingly emphasizes stratified combinations of behavioral intervention, pharmacotherapy, nutritional support, and surgical evaluation, but most pathways still use body-weight change as the trigger [18, 19, 28]. The trigger point could move upstream to neurobehavioral and psychological dynamics: reduced evidence-accumulation efficiency, enhanced food-cue evaluation, abnormal RT and theta interaction, and stronger negative-affect-to-craving/loss-of-control pathways may all serve as early signals for intensified follow-up, psychological intervention, dietary-behavioral support, or evaluation for medication escalation. Patient-experience research also indicates that postoperative weight regain is often accompanied by shame, perceived failure, and delayed help-seeking; therefore, risk communication should avoid moralizing language and instead use interpretable mechanisms and actionable care plans [29]. This framework allows the findings to speak both to neural mechanisms and to the design of postoperative nursing care and long-term follow-up.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the observational longitudinal design cannot establish a causal role of neural decision signals or GIMME pathways in weight regain; future work should embed control training, craving management, emotion regulation, or pharmacotherapy within prospective intervention frameworks. Second, 12-month weight regain is closer to an early risk phenotype and cannot fully represent 2- to 5-year long-term recurrence trajectories. Third, EEG, DDM, and GIMME all depend on preprocessing, model specification, sampling density, and quality-control rules; external cohort replication, cross-center acquisition-harmonization assessment, and test-retest reliability analyses are needed. Fourth, longitudinal data may still be affected by adherence, life events, medication changes, sleep, and missing dietary records; more complete multisource data would further improve ecological validity.

Conclusion

This study shows that weight regain after metabolic and bariatric surgery is not a single body-weight endpoint, but a dynamic risk process jointly shaped by food-cue evaluation, evidence-accumulation efficiency, frontal control modulation, and real-life affect-reward state transitions. By integrating multidimensional food-stimulus standardization, clinical EEG quality control, ERP/frontal theta indices, drift-diffusion modeling, and GIMME intensive longitudinal networks within a single analytic framework, this study advances postoperative weight regain from a descriptive outcome to an interpretable, traceable, and stratifiable neurobehavioral phenotype. The framework provides an earlier mechanistic risk window for long-term postoperative follow-up and suggests that future interventions should not begin only after body weight has increased, but should prospectively and individually target evidence accumulation, food-cue reactivity, emotion regulation, and everyday self-efficacy dynamics.
