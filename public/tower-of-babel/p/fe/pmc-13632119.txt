Effects of lactic acid bacteria and grape seed tannin on enhancing intestinal supply of essential amino acids from alfalfa silage through improved preservation and rumen bypass in a multi-stage in vitro system
Authors: Guofang Chen, Jinge Deng, Xuning Miao, Chun Bai, He Du, Qiang Liu, Cong Wang, Lei Chen, Gang Guo, Wenjie Huo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The limited bioavailability of essential amino acids (EAAs) in alfalfa silage, caused by proteolysis during ensiling and extensive ruminal degradation, restricts its protein nutritional value for ruminants. This study evaluated the individual and combined effects of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and grape seed tannins (G) on the dynamic fate of essential amino acids (EAAs) throughout ensiling, ruminal fermentation, and intestinal digestion. LAB treatment accelerated acidification and improved EAA preservation during ensiling but, importantly, increased ruminal degradation of most EAAs. In contrast, tannins alone reduced ruminal degradation of key EAAs. Compared with the control, the combined LG treatment enhanced the retention of EAAs and reduced NH3-N concentration by 25.5% during ensiling. In the subsequent in vitro rumen fermentation, this treatment also suppressed the growth of proteolytic bacteria (Butyrivibrio fibrisolvens and Prevotella ruminicola) and lowered branched-chain VFA concentrations. This resulted in the lowest ruminal degradation rates for most EAAs and the highest intestinal digestibility. In conclusion, the combination of LAB and grape seed tannins optimizes the entire protein utilisation pathway from silage preservation to rumen bypass and intestinal availability, effectively enhancing post-ruminal supply of digestible EAAs. This strategy offers a practical approach to improve protein efficiency in ruminant production, pending in vivo validation.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa) is considered a foundational feed in high-producing dairy systems due to its high crude protein (CP) content [1]. Ensiling is a predominant preservation method that provides a stable year-round feed supply [2]. Alfalfa poses challenges for ensiling due to its high buffering capacity and low epiphytic lactic acid bacteria (LAB), leading to proteolysis and EAA losses [3].

To mitigate proteolysis during silage fermentation, inoculants and organic acids are commonly applied to promote a more efficient fermentation [4, 5]. Among them, biological additives, particularly homofermentative LAB inoculants, are widely favored for their cost-effectiveness and eco-friendliness [6]. By driving a rapid and efficient pH drop, LAB suppresses the activity of spoilage microorganisms (e.g., clostridia and enterobacteria) and inhibits plant proteases, thereby enhancing CP preservation and reducing ammonia–nitrogen (NH3-N) losses in alfalfa silage [7–9]. Concurrently, plant secondary compounds such as tannins have emerged as promising additives for mitigating proteolysis via their protein-binding capacity [10, 11]. Previous studies confirm that tannins not only improve fermentation quality and nutrient retention but also delay proteolysis and antioxidant degradation [12, 13], ultimately reducing non-protein nitrogen (NPN) and NH3-N content [14]. A significant practical hurdle, however, is the broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity of tannins, which can inadvertently inhibit essential LAB [15], creating a potential contradiction for their combined application.

This oversight is of central importance, as the ultimate nutritional value of silage protein for the animal is determined by the post-ruminal supply of digestible EAAs, not merely by CP preservation in the silo. In this study, three homofermentative LAB strains (Lactobacillus pentosus, Pediococcus pentosaceus, and Pediococcus acidilactici) were selected for their ability to rapidly acidify alfalfa silage, thereby inhibiting proteolysis during ensiling [3]. Recent studies have begun to explore the combined application of LAB and tannins in silage systems. Van den Bossche et al. (2024) compared autumn grass silage treated with hydrolysable tannins, LAB inoculant, or their combination, and demonstrated that tannins can form pH-reversible protein complexes that are stable in the rumen but dissociate in the abomasum, thereby increasing the flow of digestible protein to the small intestine [16]. Similarly, Guo et al. (2025) reported that the combination of tannin-tolerant Lactobacillus plantarum with tannic acid significantly reduced pH and NH3-N content in stylo silage compared with either additive alone, with the combination treatment also suppressing protease activity and altering bacterial community structure to reduce protein degradation [17]. A significant practical hurdle, however, is the broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity of tannins, which can inadvertently inhibit essential LAB [18], creating a potential contradiction for their combined application.

Therefore, this study employed a comprehensive in vitro approach integrating silage fermentation, ruminal fermentation, and simulated intestinal digestion to systematically evaluate the individual and combined effects of LAB and grape seed tannin on the dynamic fate of EAAs in alfalfa silage. Our primary objective was to elucidate the mechanisms by which these additives, individually or combined, influence EAAs preservation, rumen bypass, and intestinal availability, with the ultimate goal of developing a targeted and effective strategy for enhancing the intestinal supply of limiting amino acids in dairy production systems. We hypothesized that (1) LAB inoculation would dominate ensiling by promoting rapid acidification, thus preserving EAA integrity, (2) grape seed tannins would primarily exert their effect in the rumen by forming protein-tannin complexes, reducing EAA degradation, and (3) their combination (LG) would produce a combination effect, resulting in superior EAA preservation during ensiling, enhanced rumen bypass, and ultimately, the highest intestinal digestibility of EAAs, thereby optimizing the entire gastrointestinal supply chain of these critical nutrients.

Materials and methods

Silage preparation and experimental treatments

Alfalfa at the bud stage was harvested from Shanxi Agricultural University experimental fields. Fresh forage was field wilted to achieve a target DM content of 35%, chopped to 2 cm in length via a cutter, and homogenized. Four representative prefermented samples, 300 g per sample, were collected for baseline analysis of their chemical composition, microbial count, and amino acid composition. Three laboratory stock LAB strains (Lactobacillus pentosus KC479664, Pediococcus pentosaceus MK559726 and Pediococcus acidilactici MK559723) were activated in de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) broth at 37 °C for 24 h anaerobically. The revived cultures were mixed at a 1:1:1 volumetric ratio to form a composite inoculant of 106 colony-forming units (CFUs) per gram of fresh weight (FW). The viable count of the LAB inoculant was determined and verified by serial dilution, spread plating on MRS agar, and enumeration of colony-forming units (CFUs) in order to ensure the precise application rate of the additive. Grape seed tannins with a purity of 97%, supplied by Runze Biotechnology Co. in China, was supplemented with 3% forage DM on the basis of established protocols [19].

Four treatment groups were established: (1) control with sterile water (C), (2) grape seed tannins (G), (3) LAB composite alone (L), and (4) grape seed tannin and LAB composite (LG). The chopped alfalfa was uniformly mixed for the different treatments. 500 g treated forage was packed into sterile vacuum-sealed polyethylene bags equipped with one-way gas valves. The bags were vacuum-sealed and ensiled for 60 days at ambient room temperature, within a recorded range of 20—25℃. Each treatment included five biological replicates, resulting in a total of 20 samples. The samples ensiled for 60 days were used for the subsequent analysis of fermentation characteristics, in vitro ruminal fermentation, and simulated intestinal digestion. Samples were taken before and after ensiling for LAB enumeration. 10 g sample was homogenized in 90 mL sterile saline, followed by tenfold serial dilutions. An aliquot of 100 μL from an appropriate dilution was spread onto MRS agar and incubated anaerobically at 30 °C for 48 h. Colonies were counted and results were expressed as log10CFU/g. Three replicates were used per dilution, and two independent replicate samplings were performed. Chemical analysis

Pre- and post-ensiling samples were dried at 65 °C for 48 h, equilibrated to ambient humidity, and ground through a 1- mm sieve. In accordance with the AOAC methods [20], both the raw materials and fermented samples were analyzed for DM (934.01) and total nitrogen (TN, 984.13) contents. The CP was calculated by multiplying TN by 6.25. Fermentation parameters were analyzed from aqueous extracts prepared by homogenizing 25 g of silage with 60 mL of deionized water and incubating it at 4 °C for 24 h. The pH was measured with a digital pH meter from Thermo Scientific. The NH3-N was determined via phenol‒hypochlorite colorimetry [21], and organic acids (lactic acid, acetic acid and propionic acid) were analyzed via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (1,260, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Waldbronn, Germany) [22]. Amino acid composition was determined following acid hydrolysis in 6 M HCl at 110 °C for 24 h under a nitrogen atmosphere via an automated amino acid analyzer (Biochrom 30 +) with ninhydrin detection, measured at 570 nm for essential amino acids, according to the method described by Khumalo [23].

In vitro rumen fermentation

Rumen fluid was collected from three Holstein dairy cows (650 ± 20 kg) housed at the experimental farm of Shanxi Agricultural University (Taigu, China). The animals were privately owned by the university and were managed under standard husbandry conditions. Informed consent for the use of these animals in the study was obtained from the university’s animal facility management. The cows were fed a total mixed ration consisting of 21.5% oat hay, 17.8% Chinese wild rye, 45.8% whole corn silage, 3% soybean meal, 2.5% DDGS, 5% wheat bran, and 4.4% vitamins and minerals. The collected fluid was uniformly mixed, filtered through sterilized gauze, and stored in thermal bottles prefilled with CO2 as inoculum. Substrates (0.4 g freeze-dried silage) were weighed into preweighed nylon bags (dimensions: 35 × 75 mm; pore size: 38–40 μm), heat-sealed, and placed in 100 mL serum bottles. Then, 60 mL of inoculum prepared according to Menke’s method was aliquoted into CO2-flushed serum bottles [24]. The bottles were sealed with rubber stoppers and incubated at 39 °C in a water bath shaker for 48 h.

After ice bath, the pH of the incubation mixture was immediately measured via an electrode pH meter. All the nylon bags were subsequently removed from the bottles and manually squeezed, and the expressed liquids were subsequently transferred to their corresponding culture media. The nylon bags were rinsed under running water until the effluent ran clear and then dried at 55 °C for 60 h. The differences in DM between the substrates and undegraded residues in the nylon bags were considered nutrient degradation. The culture fluid bottles were thoroughly agitated for sampling. For ruminal NH3-N and VFA analysis,10 mL incubation fluid samples were centrifuged (12,000 × g,10 min,4 °C), and supernatants stored at −20 °C. The rumen-undegraded residues in the nylon bags were collected for determination of rumen-bypass amino acid composition and were subsequently subjected to simulated intestinal digestion. Microbial crude protein (MCP) was measured as described by Pang et al. [25]. For the in vitro rumen fermentation, four out of the five silage replicates from each treatment were randomly selected as biological replicates (n = 4). A total of 96 sample bottles (4 treatments × 4 individual samples × 3 bottles per sample × 2 runs) and 3 control bottles (rumen fluid + empty nylon bags) were prepared. The control bottles were incubated under identical conditions to account for background gas production and fermentation products originating solely from the inoculum. All values obtained from the sample bottles were corrected by subtracting the corresponding values from the control bottles to obtain substrate-specific results. No anesthesia or euthanasia was performed on the animals during rumen fluid collection. Rumen fluid was obtained via oral intubation using a flexible stomach tube, which is a minimally invasive procedure that does not require anesthesia or euthanasia.

Total DNA extraction and real-time PCR

Following thawing at 4 °C, incubation fluid samples (2 mL per bottle) were pooled and homogenized. Total DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Stool DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, China) with a repeated bead-beating protocol. DNA concentration and quality were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and samples were normalized to uniform concentrations for downstream analysis.

Microbial group-specific primers (Table 2) were commercially synthesized (BGI Life Tech, China), with specificity verified in silico via Primer-BLAST. For absolute quantification, DNA standards were generated for each target gene by conventional PCR using pooled rumen fluid DNA as template and the same primer pairs listed in Table 2. Purified amplicons were quantified spectrophotometrically, and copy numbers were calculated based on amplicon size and DNA concentration using Avogadro's number. Ten-fold serial dilutions (101 to 108 copies/μL) of each amplicon were prepared as standards. Standard curves (Ct vs. log10 copy number) were generated in each qPCR run, and sample copy numbers were determined by interpolation. Standard curves were prepared through ten-fold serial dilution of purified PCR products. All qPCR assays exhibited amplification efficiencies ranging from 92 to 105%, with correlation coefficients (R2) > 0.99 for all standard curves. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was carried out using a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Each 20 µL amplification reaction, performed in duplicate, contained 10 µL of Fast SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.4 µL of ROX Reference Dye (50 ×), 2 µL of DNA template, 6.0 µL of nuclease-free H₂O, and 0.8 µL of each primer (10 μmol/L). The amplification conditions were performed as described by Chen et al. with minor modifications [26]. Briefly, the protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at a primer-specific temperature (Table 2) for 1 min, and a final elongation at 72 °C for 30 s.

Simulated intestinal digestion

The in vitro simulated intestinal digestion method is as followed [27]. The rumen-undegraded residues recovered from the nylon bags after 48 h of ruminal incubation (Sect. 2.3) were used as the substrate for simulated intestinal digestion. Residues from each nylon bag were pooled, freeze-dried, and ground to pass through a 1-mm sieve. Samples containing 15 mg of nitrogen were weighed into 50-mL centrifuge tubes and incubated in 10 mL of 0.1 M HCl solution containing 1 g/L pepsin (Sigma P-7012) with shaking at 38 °C for 1 h to simulate abomasal digestion. After incubation, the pH was neutralized with 0.5 mL of 1 M NaOH, followed by the addition of 13.5 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) containing 3 g/L pancreatin (Sigma P-7545). This mixture was incubated at 38 °C with shaking for 24 h. The reaction was terminated with 3 mL of 100% trichloroacetic acid solution. The mixture was centrifuged at 5000 × g for 10 min, and the undigested residue was collected. The resulting residues were lyophilized for amino acid composition analysis via the methods described in Sect. 2.2. Amino acid digestibility was calculated based on the amino acid content before (rumen-undegraded residue) and after intestinal digestion.

Statistical analysis

With the additive as the main effect, one-way ANOVA (SPSS 24.0) was applied to analyze the data on the fermentation parameters after 60 days of ensiling, the ruminal in vitro fermentation parameters and the EAA components of the silage samples at different stages. The experiment was conducted using a completely randomized design. For ensiling parameters, each of the five silage replicates served as the experimental unit (n = 5). For rumen fermentation parameters, four out of the five silage replicates were used as biological replicates (n = 4); the values obtained from the three technical replicates (bottles) for each silage replicate were averaged prior to statistical analysis. Data from the two independent runs were combined after a preliminary mixed-model analysis confirmed that the run effect was not significant (P > 0.05). Discussion

Ensiling characteristics and EAA composition

Analysis of fermentation characteristics revealed that the L treatment promoted a characteristic homolactic fermentation, leading to rapid acidification [7, 28]. This acidic environment effectively suppressed protease activity and the growth of spoilage microorganisms, consequently resulting in a substantial reduction in NH3-N formation [29–31]. In contrast, the G treatment likely reduced proteolysis through the formation of protein-tannin complexes, as indicated by the lower NH3-N content compared to the control. This mechanism is consistent with previous reports on tannins in silage. [32, 33]. The LG treatment reduced NH3-N, likely due to combined acidification and tannin binding.

The analysis of EAA preservation post-ensiling revealed distinct mechanisms of action for each additive. The L treatment likely inhibited plant and microbial protease activity through rapid environmental acidification [34], while the G treatment protected proteins broadly via complex formation [35]. Notably, the LG treatment produced a protective effect, resulting in the lowest NH3-N content. We hypothesize that the underlying mechanism may involve a complementary effect: the rapid acidification driven by LAB may swiftly establish a protective environment, while the tannins concurrently bind to proteins, forming stable complexes. However, this proposed mechanism requires further validation. This coordinated dual-protection mechanism maximized EAA integrity, establishing a solid foundation for the subsequent supply of rumen-bypass amino acids.

Ruminal fermentation characteristics, microbes and EAA degradation rates

In the present study, the pH of the incubation fluid was not significantly affected by any treatment, which is consistent with the strong buffering capacity of the batch culture system and its insensitivity to changes in medium pH [36]. Despite the stable pH, all additive groups significantly increased MCP concentration while reducing the concentrations of branched-chain volatile fatty acids (BCVFAs), including iso-butyric and iso-valeric, and valeric acid, which are associated with protein degradation, especially in the L and LG treatments. Combined with the lowest NH3-N concentration in the rumen fluid observed in the LG treatment, these results indicate that the L and LG treatments effectively inhibited proteolysis in the rumen, thereby reducing the production of branched-chain fatty acids and NH3-N derived from amino acid deamination. This is consistent with previous reports that plant secondary metabolites can reduce excessive ruminal protein degradation by inhibiting proteolytic bacteria or binding with dietary proteins [37, 38]. The increase in MCP concentration may be attributed to two factors: firstly, improved NH3-N utilization efficiency providing a better nitrogen source for microbial synthesis; secondly, the inhibition of protozoa (indirectly supported by the reduction in total anaerobic fungi and certain bacteria in the G and LG treatments) may have decreased the recycling of MCP in the rumen [39].

A notable paradox emerged from our results: the LAB-only treatment (L), which excelled in preserving EAAs during ensiling, conversely increased their degradation in the rumen. While counter-intuitive, this phenomenon can be explained by the quality and form of nitrogen preserved. The rapid acidification in the L treatment effectively inhibits plant and clostridial proteases, preventing the extensive breakdown of proteins into ammonia. However, this does not mean the protein remains as intact, complex molecules. Instead, the acidic environment may render the preserved protein more soluble or alter its tertiary structure, exposing susceptible peptide bonds to rumen microbial peptidases and proteases [40]. This is consistent with the concept of high-quality silage nitrogen, which, while containing less NH3-N, may be composed of more readily fermentable peptides and free amino acids for rumen microbes [41]. Furthermore, the L treatment significantly increased the abundance of the highly fibrolytic and proteolytic bacterium F. succinogenes, which, along with potential stimulation of overall proteolytic enzyme activity in the rumen, could further contribute to the elevated EAA degradation rates observed in this group. This finding highlights that preservation efficiency during ensiling does not necessarily translate to rumen bypass, underscoring the need for a rumen-protection strategy, as achieved in the LG treatment.

Regarding changes in the microbial community, the G and LG treatments significantly reduced the abundance of total bacteria, total methanogens, total anaerobic fungi, and the predominant cellulolytic bacterium R. albus, the L treatment also reduced the abundance of total anaerobic fungi. Such reductions are generally associated with decreased ruminal organic matter fermentation rate and methane production [42]. Notably, R. albus is an important hydrogen-producing bacterium, and its decreased abundance would reduce the hydrogen available to methanogens, thereby suppressing methanogenesis. Although methane production was not directly measured in this study, the concurrent decline in the abundance of methanogens and hydrogen-producing bacteria strongly suggests a potential for methane mitigation. In contrast, the L and LG treatments significantly increased the abundance of F. succinogenes. The major fermentation product of F. succinogenes is succinate, which can be largely converted to propionate via the succinate pathway, and propionate production represents an important hydrogen sink in the rumen, contributing to reduced methanogenesis [43].

With respect to proteolysis-related microorganisms, the G and LG treatments significantly reduced the abundance of the major proteolytic bacteria B. fibrisolvens and P. ruminicola. This directly explains why these treatments resulted in significantly decreased levels of branched-chain VFAs, NH3-N, and degradation rates of various EAAs. The L treatment showed an opposite effect: although it also reduced total anaerobic fungi, it increased the degradation rates of almost all EAAs and increased the abundance of F. succinogenes. This may be because the L treatment itself stimulates proteolytic enzyme activities or alters ruminal microbial metabolic pathways, or because its chemical structure differs from that of G, resulting in a weaker protein-protective effect or even a promotion of proteolytic capacity of certain bacterial groups. Importantly, the LG treatment (combination of L and G) not only avoided the increase in amino acid degradation observed with the L treatment alone, but also significantly reduced the degradation rates of all EAAs except isoleucine and phenylalanine, and markedly decreased TAA degradation. This indicates that component G plays a dominant role in inhibiting proteolysis in the combination, and that there may be a complementary effect between L and G: suppressing undesirable excessive protein degradation without causing an over-inhibition of fibre digestion as seen with G alone (in this study, the LG treatment showed a similar degree of inhibition on R. albus and other fibrolytic bacteria as the G treatment, without further exacerbation). In terms of amino acid degradation patterns, the G treatment selectively reduced the degradation rates of lysine, histidine, valine, and leucine, whereas the LG treatment provided more comprehensive protection of most amino acids. This has important implications for increasing the flow of rumen-undegradable amino acids to the small intestine and improving host amino acid nutrition.

Intestinal digestibility of EAAs

Intestinal digestibility analysis showed that the LG treatment achieved the highest intestinal digestibility of most EAAs and total amino acids, which can be attributed to its dual advantages in silage preservation and rumen protection. Tannins improve nitrogen utilization efficiency through a pH-dependent mechanism: they form protective complexes with feed proteins at the near-neutral pH of the rumen, which subsequently dissociate in the acidic abomasum, thereby increasing the flow of digestible protein to the small intestine [44, 45]. This well-established pH-dependent mechanism helps explain why the LG treatment, which provides rumen protection, resulted in higher intestinal digestibility compared to the L treatment alone.

Regarding the balance of nutrient utilization, it is worth noting that none of the treatments consistently improved the intestinal digestibility of EAAs derived from MCP. This phenomenon may be related to the inhibitory effects of polyphenols on extracellular enzymes secreted by the ruminal microbial community, which are involved in the release of amino acids from MCP [46]. Considering that total bacterial counts in the G and LG treatments were significantly lower than in the L treatment, this inhibition of enzymatic activity may further limit the digestibility of MCP in the small intestine. Therefore, in evaluating the protective effects of tannins, it is necessary to balance the potential trade-off between increasing rumen bypass protein flow and possibly reducing MCP utilization efficiency. It is important to clarify that our simulated intestinal digestion protocol included a 1-h incubation with 0.1 M HCl and pepsin, which is designed to mimic the acidic abomasal environment and should promote the dissociation of pH-labile protein-tannin complexes [47]. Therefore, the lack of improvement in MCP digestion is not likely due to the persistence of these complexes. Instead, we propose that the observed reduction in MCP digestibility in the G and LG treatments is a downstream effect of the tannins antimicrobial activity during the ruminal phase. By inhibiting the ruminal microbial community (evidenced by the reduced total bacteria abundance), the tannins may have limited the activity of extracellular enzymes required to release amino acids from microbial cells [48], thereby reducing the subsequent digestibility of MCP in the small intestine. This presents a critical trade-off: while tannins effectively protect feed protein, their non-selective inhibition can compromise the nutritional value of microbial protein, a major protein source for the host.

We acknowledge that the limited amount of rumen-undegraded residue recovered from the nylon bags after 48 h of ruminal incubation precluded additional determinations of CP and DM disappearance, and thus we were unable to present ruminal CP disappearance or intestinal CP digestibility data in this study. The available sample material was prioritized for the analysis of individual amino acid profiles, which we consider to be the primary endpoint of this investigation. Nevertheless, we believe that the EAA degradation and digestibility data presented in Figs. 3 and 4 provide a functionally relevant assessment of protein quality, as the post-ruminal supply of individual digestible EAAs is the ultimate determinant of protein nutritional value for ruminants (as discussed in the Introduction). TAA degradation and digestibility data, presented in Figs. 3 and 4b, may serve as proxies for CP-level estimates. Future studies with larger sample volumes should incorporate CP and DM disappearance measurements to complement the EAA-level data presented here.

Although the L treatment exhibited good feed preservation during ensiling, its lack of a rumen protection mechanism resulted in higher degradation rates of most EAAs in the rumen, thereby limiting the eventual intestinal supply of amino acids. In contrast, while the G treatment increased rumen bypass protein flow through the formation of tannin–protein complexes, the optimal digestibility of these complexes in the small intestine was lower than that in the LG treatment. This may be related to the tannin dosage or complex characteristics, suggesting that not all tannin–protein complexes are completely dissociated in the abomasum.

Based on the observed combined effects, future studies should prioritize in vivo validation under physiological conditions to confirm the impacts of the LG treatment on animal production performance and nitrogen utilization efficiency. Standard molecular techniques, particularly metagenomics and metabolomics, are recommended to precisely elucidate the underlying shifts in microbial community structure and metabolic pathways responsible for the improved EAA preservation and utilization. Further investigation into the dissociation dynamics of protein-tannin complexes throughout the digestive tract is crucial for understanding their final nutritional value. Research should also systematically explore the interactions between different types of tannins and specific LAB strains to identify more effective combinations. Ultimately, developing practical strategies to optimize the balance between protein protection and overall nutrient digestibility will be key to successfully applying this combination strategy in ruminant feeding systems. These concerted efforts will provide a more comprehensive scientific basis for enhancing silage quality and protein utilization in practice.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that combining LAB with grape seed tannins (LG) improves the intestinal supply of EAAs from alfalfa silage. During ensiling, LAB accelerated acidification and reduced proteolysis, while tannins further protected proteins via complex formation, leading to the highest EAA preservation and lowest NH3-N content in the LG treatment. During ruminal fermentation, although LAB alone increased EAA degradation, the LG treatment decreased the abundance of key proteolytic bacteria (B. fibrisolvens and P. ruminicola), lowered branched-chain VFA and NH3-N concentrations, and achieved the lowest ruminal degradation rates for most EAAs without compromising microbial protein synthesis. Subsequent simulated intestinal digestion showed that the LG treatment resulted in the highest intestinal digestibility of EAAs and total amino acids. Collectively, the LAB-tannin combination optimizes the entire pathway of protein utilisation from silage preservation to rumen bypass and intestinal availability, thereby enhancing post-ruminal delivery of digestible EAAs. This strategy offers a practical approach to improve protein efficiency in ruminant production, although in vivo validation is required to confirm its effects on animal performance and nitrogen utilisation.
