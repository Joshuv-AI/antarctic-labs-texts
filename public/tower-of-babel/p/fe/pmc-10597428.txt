Three-Dimensional Printed Poly (Lactic-co-Glycolic Acid)-Magnesium Composite Scaffolds for the Promotion of Osteogenesis Through Immunoregulation
Authors: Yuqing Ren, Zheng Zheng, Yanjun Yu, Rongrong Hu, Shanshan Xu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractScaffolds play an important role in bone tissue engineering. The ideal engineered scaffold needs to be biocompatible, bioactive, and able to regulate immune cells to enhance bone regeneration. In this study, magnesium (Mg)-contained poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) scaffolds (hereinafter, referred to as PLGA-2Mg) were fabricated by 3-dimensional printing using a mixture of PLGA and MgSO4 powder. Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) scaffolds (hereinafter, referred to as PLGA) were also fabricated by 3-dimensional printing and were used as control. The biocompatibility, immunoregulatory ability, and osteogenic properties of PLGA-2Mg were analyzed and compared with those of PLGA. The results indicate that the incorporation of Mg increased the Young modulus and surface roughness of the scaffold, but did not affect its degradation. The PLGA-2Mg further promoted the adhesion and proliferation of MC3T3-E1 cells compared with PLGA, which indicates its improved biocompatibility and bioactivity. In addition, PLGA-2Mg inhibited the polarization of RAW 264.7 cells toward the M1 phenotype by down-regulating the IL-1β, IL-6, and iNOs gene expression when challenged with lipopolysaccharide stimulation. In contrast, it promoted the polarization of RAW 264.7 cells toward the M2 phenotype by up-regulating the TGF-β, IL-10, and Arg-1 gene expression without lipopolysaccharide stimulation. Finally, MC3T3-E1 cells were cocultured with RAW 264.7 cells and scaffolds using a transwell system. It was found that the expression level of osteogenic-related genes (ALP, COL-1, BMP2, and BSP) was significantly upregulated in the PLGA-2Mg group compared with that in the PLGA group. Consequently, PLGA-2Mg with increased biocompatibility and bioactivity can promote osteogenesis through immunoregulation and has the potential to be used as a novel scaffold in bone tissue engineering.Key Words: 3D printing, immunoregulation, Mg, osteogenesis, PLGA scaffolds

Repairing bone defects due to trauma, inflammation, or tumor resection in the oral and maxillofacial region remains a challenge for clinicians. Bone tissue engineering (BTE) is one of the most effective strategies to achieve ideal bone regeneration with scaffolds, cell matrices, and growth factors.1,2 The scaffolds used in BTE usually provide a 3-dimensional (3D) structure for adequate adhesion, growth, migration, and differentiation of the host cells. Therefore, a scaffold with multiple functions is of great importance in BTE.3

Due to their cytocompatibility, several natural biomaterials and synthetic polymers have been widely used to fabricate scaffolds for BTE.4,5 Natural biomaterials, such as collagen and chitosan, are highly biocompatible with bioactive cellular response; however, their insufficient structural integrity and mechanical strength limits their application.6 Comparatively, synthetic polymers, such as polycaprolactone and poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), have been extensively used to fabricate scaffolds with stronger integrity.7,8 Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) is a promising FDA-approved biomaterial for bone regeneration9 nevertheless, the poor bioactive cellular response of PLGA scaffolds results in undesirable cell adhesion, proliferation, differentiation, and immune response, and can even lead to unsatisfactory tissue repair.10 As a result, appropriate modifications in PLGA scaffolds are necessary to achieve enhanced bone repair.

One of the strategies commonly used to modify PLGA-based scaffolds is to attach biomolecules or growth factors, endowing the scaffolds with necessary biological signals, and thereby improving the bioactivity. In several studies, bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP2), vascular endothelial growth factor, and arginine-glycine-aspartate peptides have been immobilized on PLGA scaffolds11,12; however, the denaturation and degradation of such factors are uncontrolled. Several microcarriers, such as nanoparticles, can be utilized to protect these factors. Nevertheless, the fabrication process becomes more sophisticated and difficult, and the bioactivity of these factors may be compromised during fabrication.11,13,14 Surface patterning is another strategy to modify PLGA scaffolds. Electrospinning is a universal and easily operable technique with the potential to deposit polymer nanofibers onto various substrates or even the same type of material to obtain a biocompatible and bioactive surface.15,16 This obtained featured surface can have a marked effect on cellular response and contribute to enhanced cell adhesion and proliferation. However, the effect of this modified surface on differentiation and immune response is doubtful. Consequently, alternative strategies need to be developed to overcome such problems.

Magnesium (Mg) is one of the most abundant minerals in the human body, which has evolved in many important bio-reactions and is essential to bone formation.17 It has been reported that the incorporation of Mg into PLGA composite scaffolds promoted the adhesion and proliferation of cells,18 whereas the Mg2+ released from PLGA-Mg-tricalcium phosphate improved both osteogenesis and bone regeneration.19 These observations may be attributed to the activation of MAPK/ERK, PI3K/AKT, and Notch signaling pathways.17 Thus, the incorporation of Mg may potentially achieve the desired modification of PLGA scaffolds. There are various techniques to fabricate Mg-containing PLGA scaffolds, such as solvent casting/particulate leaching, freeze-drying, and phase separation.18,20,21 However, these techniques make it hard to fabricate porous PLGA-Mg scaffolds with controllable porosity and microstructure, as well as satisfactory release of the by-products, such as Mg2+. Therefore, new strategies have been developed to fabricate Mg-containing PLGA scaffolds. As far as we know, the 3D printing technique possesses the advantages to solve the above problem.

Three-dimensional printing has been integrated with craniofacial tissue engineering to fabricate scaffolds with the desired structure and achieve more satisfactory tissue regeneration or organ replacement results.22 The 3D printing technique enables the production of engineered scaffolds with desired structures that mimic the extracellular matrix, improving the cell distribution, providing a favorable microenvironment for the adhesion, migration, proliferation, and differentiation of host cells, and finally helping cells to form functional tissues.1,4 Besides, scaffolds with cortical bone-like structures and controllable Mg release can be achieved by the 3D printing technique. Therefore, in this study, the 3D printing technique was employed to fabricate Mg-containing PLGA scaffolds.

The local immune response is a major concern for scaffold application. After scaffold implantation, the local immune response, which involves a series of inflammatory events and repair processes, occurs inevitably. Generally, macrophages are first polarized to pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to help the host against pathogens, as well as react to the implant. Subsequently, macrophages are polarized to form an anti-inflammatory response to the M2 phenotype and repair damaged tissue.23 Therefore, an ideal scaffold is supposed to appropriately regulate the immune response to achieve the desirable tissue repair outcome.24 Although numerous researchers have reported that Mg-based scaffolds help with osteogenesis in vitro and bone regeneration in vivo,19,21,25 the studies about the effects of those scaffolds on macrophage polarization are limited, and the underlying mechanism of how those scaffolds regulate the polarization of macrophages remains unclear.

Herein, in this study, based on the 3D printing technique, we fabricated Mg-contained PLGA scaffolds and hypothesized these scaffolds can promote the bioactive cellular response and enhance osteogenesis by modulating macrophage towards an M2 phenotype.

METHODS

Preparation of Poly(Lactic-co-Glycolic Acid) and Magnesium-Containing Poly(Lactic-co-Glycolic Acid) Scaffolds

The scaffolds were fabricated using a 3D Bioprinter (EFL-BP-6601, Yongqinquan Intelligent Equipment Co.). In brief, PLGA (lactide: glycolide = 75:25, 110–120 kDa, Jinan Daigang Biological Engineering Co.) was dissolved in dichloromethane at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v) and stirred at 70°C and 800 rpm for 3 hours until the PLGA powder was completely dissolved. Then, the PLGA solution was loaded into a 100 μm diameter syringe, and PLGA scaffolds (hereinafter, referred to as PLGA) were 3D printed with the syringe at a flow rate of 500 mm/min and a pressure of 500 psi. Subsequently, to fabricate the Mg-containing PLGA scaffolds, after the PLGA was dissolved completely, MgSO4 powder (Sigma) was slowly added into the PLGA solution at ratios of 10:1 (w/w, PLGA:MgSO4), 10:2 (w/w, PLGA:MgSO4), and 10:3 (w:w, PLGA:MgSO4) under ultrasonic oscillation for 5 minutes, and then, the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 hours. After stewing for 30 minutes, the supernatant was loaded into a 100 μm diameter syringe, and the Mg-containing PLGA scaffolds were prepared by 3D printing; hereinafter, they will be referred to as PLGA-1Mg, PLGA-2Mg, and PLGA-3Mg, respectively. Based on their cytotoxic results (Supplemental Digital Content 2, Figure S1, http://links.lww.com/SCS/F501), PLGA-2Mg was selected for the subsequent experiments. Scaffold samples with 2 different sizes were prepared; 4×4×0.2 mm scaffolds for cell viability and cell adhesion analysis, and 10×10×0.2 mm scaffolds for the rest of the experiments.

Scaffold Characterization

The surface morphology of PLGA and PLGA-2Mg was observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; S-3400N, Hitachi) with an energy-dispersive spectrometry instrument (SEM-EDS; S-3400N, Hitachi). Before observation, the samples were sputter-coated with gold. The pore size of PLGA and PLGA-2Mg was measured from the SEM images.5 The surface roughness and Young modulus of the scaffolds were measured using an Asylum Research MFP-3D Bio atomic force microscope (Oxford Instruments Company) machine.22

Weight loss and Mg2+ ion release experiments were performed to analyze the degradation behavior of the scaffolds. More specifically, all scaffolds were dried and weighed (W0) before immersion, and then, they were placed into test tubes with ddH2O solution at a ratio of 1:10 (m/v) according to the ISO-10993-13 standard. After 7 and 21 days, the scaffolds were dried naturally and weighed again (W1). The weight loss was calculated as follows: Weight loss (%)=(W0−W1)/W0×100%. In addition, the ddH2O solution was collected on days 2, 4, 6, and 8, and the concentration of Mg2+ was detected by inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES; Avio500).

Effect of Scaffolds on Cell Adhesion and Proliferation

The mouse embryo osteoblast precursor cell line (MC3T3-E1; ATCC) and the mouse macrophage cell line (RAW 264.7; ATCC) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (Gibco) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ScienCell) supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco). In the subsequent experiments, cell passages 3 to 6 were used.

After sterilization by ultraviolet irradiation for 30 minutes for each side, the scaffolds were placed into 96-well plates, and MC3T3-E1 cells were cocultured with the scaffolds at a density of 2×103 cells/well. After 1, 4, and 7 days, the medium was removed and 10% CCK-8 (Nanjing Jiancheng) with fresh medium was added to each well. After culturing for 1 hour, the optical density (OD) values of the cells in each well at 450 nm were determined using a microplate reader (Berthold; n = 5). Other sterilized scaffolds were placed into 96-well plates and cultured with MC3T3-E1 cells at a density of 4×103 cells/well. For the other samples, after incubation for 12 hours, the scaffolds were taken out, washed with phosphate buffer solution (Gibco), and stained with rhodamine phalloidin (Cytoskeleton) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Beyotime Biotechnology) stains according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Subsequently, the adhered cells were observed with an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus U-RFL-T). For each group, 5 scopes were randomly selected to count the number of adhered cells on the scaffolds.26

Macrophage Phenotype Regulation by Poly(Lactic-co-Glycolic Acid)-2Mg

The sterilized PLGA and PLGA-2Mg samples were placed in 12-well plates. Some macrophages were activated by lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 1 mg/mL, Invitrogen) for 6 hours, then washed with phosphate buffer solution 3 times, and finally cocultured with the scaffolds at a density of 1×105 cells/well. After culturing for 3 and 7 days, the expression levels of macrophage M1 polarization-related genes (IL-1β, IL-6, and iNOs) were detected by quantitative real-time reverse-transcription polymerase chain reactions (qRT-PCR). Macrophages without LPS activation were also cocultured with the scaffolds at the same density. After culturing for 3 and 7 days, the expression levels of macrophage M2 polarization-related genes (TGF-β, IL-10, and Arg-1) were detected as well. The culture medium was refreshed every 2 days. The total RNA of cells was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and qRT-PCR was performed using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara Bio Inc.) and the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix kit (Applied Biosystems), with an ABI 7300 system (Applied Biosystems).5 The results were normalized by the cycle threshold of the housekeeping GAPDH gene. The cycle threshold values of cells cultured with PLGA were used as control (n = 4). The primers are shown in the Supplemental Table (Supplemental Digital Content 1, Table 1, http://links.lww.com/SCS/F500).

Effect of Poly(Lactic-co-Glycolic Acid)-2Mg–Regulated Macrophages on the Osteogenic Differentiation of MC3T3-E1 Cells

The transwell coculture system was employed to investigate the effect of PLGA-2Mg–regulated macrophages on the osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3-E1 cells. To this end, RAW 264.7 cells (1×105 cells/well) were incubated with the sterilized scaffolds in the upper chamber of a transwell plate (Corning), whereas MC3T3-E1 cells (5×104 cells/well) were seeded in the lower chamber. The medium was refreshed every 48 hours. At days 7 and 14, the MC3T3-E1 cells were harvested and the expressions of osteogenic-related genes (ALP, COL-1, BMP2, and BSP) were detected by qRT-PCR. The primers are shown in the Supplemental Table (Supplemental Digital Content 1, Table 1, http://links.lww.com/SCS/F500).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using the SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM Corp.). The results are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using the Student t test or 1-way analysis of variance with the Fisher least significant different post hoc analysis (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P <0.001). All experiments were repeated 3 times.

RESULTS

Scaffold Characterization

As can be observed in the SEM images, the diameter of the horizontally and vertically oriented fibers on PLGA was 250 to 300 μm, and the fiber surface was flat and smooth (Fig. 1A). In contrast, at the interconnection of the horizontal and vertical fibers, the fiber diameter was still 250 to 300 μm, but local swelling was observed (Fig. 1B), which may have resulted from the addition of the MgSO4 particles. Moreover, uneven surfaces with numerous pits and particles were observed on PLGA-2Mg (Fig. 1B). In both scaffolds, the pore size was about 200 to 250 μm; however, the shape of the pores in PLGA was almost square, whereas in PLGA-2Mg it was irregular (Fig. 1A, B). In addition, the Mg element was detected only on the surface of PLGA-2Mg (Fig. 1C, D), indicating the successful incorporation of Mg by the 3D printing technique. After the addition of Mg, the Young modulus (P < 0.001; Fig. 1E) and surface roughness (P < 0.01; Fig. 1F) of PLGA-2Mg increased significantly compared with those of PLGA.

PLGA-2Mg and PLGA scaffold characterization. (A and B) SEM images, (C and D) EDS, (F and G) AFM images, (E) Young modulus, (F) surface roughness, (G) weight loss, and (H) cumulative Mg2+ release from the PLGA-2Mg scaffold. (**P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001). AFM indicates atomic force microscope; EDS, energy-dispersive spectrometry; Mg, magnesium; PLGA, poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid); SEM, scanning electron microscopy.

According to the results of weight loss, no significant difference was found between PLGA and PLGA-2Mg after immersion in ddH2O solution for 7 and 21 days (P > 0.05; Fig. 1G), which indicated that the incorporation of Mg did not affect the degradation of PLGA. According to Figure 1H, the release of Mg2+ from PLGA-2Mg was in the range of 11 to 15 mM. No dramatic change in the concentration of Mg2+ was found over a period of 8 days (Fig. 1H).

Poly(Lactic-co-Glycolic Acid)-2Mg Promotion of MC3T3-E1 Cell Adhesion and Proliferation

The adhesion of MC3T3-E1 cells on the scaffolds was observed through fluorescence staining. More cells (blue-stained nuclei) with more stretched cytoskeletons (red-stained) were observed on PLGA-2Mg compared with those on PLGA (Fig. 2A–D). The quantitative analysis also confirmed that more cells adhered to PLGA-2Mg (P < 0.05; Fig. 2E). The effect of the scaffolds on MC3T3-E1 cell viability was assessed with CCK-8 assay. In both groups, the OD values increased with time (Fig. 2F), indicating the proliferation of the cells. The OD values for PLGA-2Mg were significantly higher than those for PLGA on days 1, 4, and 7 (P < 0.05; Fig. 2F). These results indicate that PLGA-2Mg can promote the adhesion and proliferation of MC3T3-E1 cells and is more biocompatible than PLGA.

IFM images of (A and B) DAPI and (C and D) phalloidin staining of cells adhered on the scaffolds after incubation for 12 hours. (E) Number of adhered cells (n = 5). (F) Cell proliferation in the PLGA-2Mg and PLGA groups (n = 5). (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01). DAPI indicates 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; IFM, inverted fluorescence microscope; Mg, magnesium; PLGA, poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid).

Poly(Lactic-co-Glycolic Acid)-2Mg Regulation of RAW 264.7 Cell Polarization

After the RAW 264.7 cells were stimulated by LPS, the expression level of the M1-related markers (IL-1β, IL-6, and iNOs) was determined at days 3 and 7 (Fig. 3). It can be observed that the IL-1β, IL-6, and iNOs gene expression levels decreased with time. Their expression levels in the PLGA-2Mg group were significantly lower than those in the PLGA group after 3 days (P < 0.05; Fig. 3A–C); however, no significant difference was found between the two groups after 7 days (P > 0.05; Fig. 3A–C).

Effect of PLGA-2Mg and PLGA scaffolds on macrophage polarization. Relative expression levels of M1-marked genes, including (A) IL-1β, (B) IL-6, and (C) iNOs, with LPS stimulation at days 3 and 7 in both groups. Relative expression levels of M2-marked genes, including (D) TGF-β, (E) IL-10, and (F) Arg-1, without LPS stimulation at days 3 and 7 in both groups (n = 4; *P < 0.05). LPS indicates lipopolysaccharide; Mg, magnesium; PLGA, poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid).

For RAW 264.7 cells without LPS stimulation, the expression level of the M2-related markers (TGF-β, IL-10, and Arg-1) at days 3 and 7 were detected as well (Fig. 3). In general, the expression level of these genes increased in both groups with time. In the PLGA-2Mg group, the expression levels of those genes were significantly higher than those in the PLGA group at each time point (P < 0.05; Fig. 3). These results indicate that PLGA-2Mg is able to regulate macrophage polarization with or without LPS stimulation.

Poly(Lactic-co-Glycolic Acid)-2Mg–Regulated Macrophage-Promoted Osteogenesis

After coculturing for 7 and 14 days, the expression of osteogenic-related genes was determined by qRT-PCR. At day 7, the expression levels of the ALP and COL-1 genes in the PLGA-2Mg group were upregulated compared with those in the PLGA group (P < 0.05; Fig. 4A, B). In contrast, the expression levels of BMP2 and BSP were increased in the PLGA-2Mg group compared with those in the PLGA group at both days 7 and 14 (P < 0.05; Fig. 4C, E). These results indicate that PLGA-2Mg can promote osteogenesis by regulating macrophages.

DISCUSSION

In this study, the 3D-printed PLGA-2Mg scaffolds exhibited excellent biocompatibility and enhanced the osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3-E1 cells by regulating the polarization of RAW 264.7 cells. These effects are attributed to the incorporation of Mg in the 3D printing process of the scaffolds.

Considering that the scaffold plays a vital role in BTE, compared with other fabrication techniques, 3D printing enables the development of scaffolds with desired pore structure, as well as homogeneous and interconnected microstructure, providing suitable space for cell infiltration and bone ingrowth.27 In this study, the incorporation of MgSO4 powder in the fabrication process did not affect the shape of the fibers and pores significantly, and the microstructure of PLGA-2Mg exhibited satisfactory interconnectivity. In addition, the incorporation of MgSO4 increased the surface roughness of the scaffolds, which promoted cell adhesion,28 and thus enhanced the bioactive cellular response to the scaffolds.

Apart from excellent biocompatibility and desired bioactive response, an ideal scaffold should be able to trigger appropriate and sequential responses to immune cells, such as macrophages,29 creating a microenvironment that can promote osteogenesis and enhance bone regeneration. It has been reported that the immunoregulatory effects of Mg2+ are closely related to the content of Mg2+ in the scaffold.25,30 According to Wu and colleagues, after RAW 264.7 cells were stimulated with LPS, both 2 mM and 6 mM Mg2+-contained solution down-regulated the expression of M1-marked genes of macrophages, such as TNF-α and iNOs. In contrast, 6 mM Mg2+ upregulated the expression of M2-marked genes, such as CD163 and CD206, and 2 mM Mg2+ also upregulated the CD163 expression.25 In another study, Tan et al30 used unstimulated RAW 264.7 cells and reported that Mg2+ ranging from 13 to 15 mM upregulated the expression of IL-10, and down-regulated the expression of IL-6; however, the Mg2+ ranging from 32 to 35 mM regulated macrophages towards M1 phenotypes. Moreover, Qin et al suggested that Mg2+ concentrations ranging between 10 and 15 mM are considered as the critical dose that will not inhibit cell viability.31 Therefore, the release behavior of Mg2+ from the Mg-containing PLGA scaffolds is a critical factor concerning their successful application.

Brown et al fabricated porous Mg/PLGA scaffolds using the traditional solvent casting/salt leaching technique. The variation in the mass of the Mg particles was balanced by sodium chloride particles, so that all fabricated scaffolds were maintained in the same size.21 However, the release of Mg2+ in the PLGA + 10 mg and PLGA + 20 mg groups was over 30 mM at day 1. In general, the excessive Mg2+ may be toxic to cells and not regulate macrophage polarization in a suitable way. In the present study, the release of Mg2+ from the 3D printed PLGA-2Mg scaffolds remained stable; over a period of 8 days, the concentration was 11 to 15 mM. In this concentration, Mg2+ is considered biosafe and is supposed to regulate macrophage polarization from M1 to M2 phenotypes. Furthermore, it was also confirmed that Mg2+ in such concentration can inhibit the proinflammation of LPS-stimulated macrophages.

Generally, after a scaffold was placed, M1 macrophages were dominant at the early stages (1–5 d), whereas M2 macrophages were dominant at the later stages (4–10 d).32,33 Therefore, after 7 days, the phenotype of the primary macrophages was mainly M2, and the expression of M1-marked genes may have been sequentially down-regulated; as a result, no difference in the expression level of the M1-marked genes was found in this study. At later stages, the dominant M2 macrophages secret various growth factors, such as BMP2, TGF-β, and vascular endothelial growth factor, to facilitate tissue remolding, and these factors promote the up-regulation of osteogenic-related genes and enhance bone repair.34,35 Consequently, considering that PLGA-2Mg–regulated the macrophages toward M2 phenotypes, this regulation finally resulted in the promotion of osteogenic differentiation of the MC3T3-E1 cells in the PLGA-2Mg group.

It should be clarified that this study has also certain limitations. For example, it is critical to confirm the immunoregulatory effect of PLGA-2Mg in vivo. Therefore, future work will include animal studies based on the proposed PLGA-2Mg scaffold.

CONCLUSION

In this study, PLGA-2Mg scaffolds were prepared by 3D printing with a mixture of PLGA and MgSO4 powder. The obtained PLGA-2Mg scaffolds promoted the adhesion and proliferation of MC3T3-E1 cells and enhanced their osteogenic differentiation by regulating the polarization of macrophages towards M2 phenotypes. It can be concluded that the proposed bioactive and immunoregulatory PLGA-2Mg scaffolds show potential to be used as an exciting therapeutic prospect for further applications.
