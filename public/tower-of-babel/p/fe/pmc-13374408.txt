Architecture, implementation and acceptance of performance-based resource allocation for medical education: an interview study protocol addressing medical schools in Germany and Switzerland
Authors: Marc Ninck, Yara Banz, Sissel Guttormsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPerformance-based resource allocation (PBRA) programmes have been increasingly adopted by medical schools as a strategic mechanism to optimise funding distribution, align resources with institutional priorities—particularly the delivery of high-quality education—and improve educational outcomes. In an era of budgetary constraints and growing demands for transparency and accountability in higher education, PBRA programmes offer means to enhance efficiency, incentivise quality and strengthen educational performance. Despite their widespread implementation, significant knowledge gaps remain. Specifically, there is limited evidence regarding: objectives of PBRA programmes (RQ1), structural design and key components (RQ2, RQ3), effectiveness in achieving intended outcomes (RQ4), implementation challenges and enabling factors (RQ5)This study aims to address these gaps through a comprehensive evaluation of PBRA programmes in medical schools across Switzerland and Germany, drawing insights from key stakeholders. The overarching research question guiding this work is: What are the key elements of successful PBRA programmes in Central European medical schools?Methods and analysisWe will conduct a qualitative cross-sectional study using semistructured interviews with PBRA programme managers or experts from medical schools in Germany that already have PBRA in place and Switzerland, where the existence of PBRA programmes is currently unknown. Interviews will be held online (eg, Microsoft Teams), audio-recorded and transcribed intelligent verbatim for thematic analysis. A previously piloted and validated interview guide will be used. Data will be analysed thematically following Kuckartz’s methodology, combining inductive and deductive coding, supported by NVivo (V.15). Themes will be identified, discussed and refined as a team, possibly using frameworks such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research and the Joint Committee on Standards for Educational Evaluation standards, to ensure alignment with established theoretical models. Results will be reported according to the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research.Ethics and disseminationThe study complies with the Swiss Ethical Review Act, which exempts non-sensitive qualitative research from formal approval (REQ: 2024-01001). Following study commencement and before completion of data collection, the study was registered on the Open Science Framework DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/QBDF2). Results will be published in a peer-reviewed journal, presented at conferences and shared with participating medical schools to foster collaboration and innovation in resource allocation.

Introduction

Medical schools must ensure that undergraduate education fosters effective learning, professional attitudes and clinical skills to deliver high-quality patient care in an increasingly complex healthcare environment.1 The quality of medical training directly influences patient outcomes,24 underscoring the need for continuous improvement of curricula and teaching formats. Common formats include large-group sessions (lectures), small-group teaching (eg, anatomy classes), one-to-one bedside instruction and individual supervision during internships or project work.5

Recent years have seen the development of strategies to maintain high standards in medical education,6 7 emphasising not only student performance but also educator quality. Integrating teachers’ experiences and feedback into institutional guidelines is essential.8 A survey among German medical educators identified lack of academic recognition and limited institutional and financial support as major barriers to high-quality teaching.9

Clinician-educators often face multiple demands, balancing teaching with research and clinical care.10 Financial incentives such as performance-based resource allocation (PBRA) have been widely implemented to reward research productivity.11 While these measures promote research, they may inadvertently prioritise research over education, as research activities often receive greater financial support.12

The German Science Council recognised this issue and, in 2008, issued recommendations to improve educational quality at universities, emphasising support and recognition for teaching alongside clinical research.13 It also called for research into effective instruments for PBRA in education. In response, several medical schools introduced PBRA programmes in medical education efforts in general and teaching in particular to incentivise teaching performance.1416

In PBRA for education, resources—mostly financial means—are allocated to prioritise performance and the attainment of specified objectives.17 18 In medical education, funding is typically linked to quality criteria such as student evaluations or licensing exam results.19 For example, at Hamburg Medical School, educators’ didactic performance is ranked using these indicators, and a portion of the education budget is distributed accordingly.15 This model aims to enhance educational quality by rewarding teaching excellence and motivating educators. It aligns with Deniston’s framework for programme evaluation in public health interventions, which defines programmes as structured approaches to address identified problems.20 Such a problem might, for example, be the low value given to engagement in education.

Applying Deniston’s framework, every programme (see also Nippert et al,14 Schiekirka-Schwake et al,16 Brähler and Strauß21 or Wiesemann et al22) contains specified objectives, selected and performed activities and the use of resources. Applying the key constructs of a programme to the medical school in Hamburg, the objective would be to improve the quality of education. The activities are measures to reach this objective, such as evaluating educational performance through students’ assessment, resulting in a ranking of clinical departments. Finally, the ranking then steers allocation of resources, for example, monetary incentives.

Elements of a PBRA programme

In the following, the core structure of a PBRA programme (objectives, activities and resources) is described in more detail:

Objectives of PBRA programmes

The few medical schools which publicly describe their PBRA programmes all name the improvement of quality in medical education as the primary objective.15 21 22 Additional objectives include increasing the perceived value of education and motivating educators.

However, the question arises: What do medical schools understand by ‘good quality’ in education in the context of a PBRA programme for education? Addressing this requires linking educational objectives to PBRA activities.

Activities of PBRA programmes

PBRA activities should not be confused with teaching activities. PBRA activities refer to evaluating educational efforts that guide resource allocation to improve teaching quality (the ‘objective’). Typical activities include general teaching evaluations and specific tasks such as developing tools for student feedback on faculty performance and competencies.22

Defining quality standards is essential for evaluating educational efforts. A common framework is Donabedian’s model,23 which assesses structure (eg, availability of facilities), process (eg, sequence and mix of educational interventions) and outcomes (eg, graduates’ competencies).212426 Existing guidelines for medical education evaluation highlight areas such as research training, peer teaching, formative assessment, support for self-directed learning and promotion of effective learning strategies.2732 They also stress incorporating future developments, such as artificial intelligence-based personalised learning, into educational processes.

In addition to evaluating educational efforts and initiatives, assessing teachers’ performance—that is, the quality of educational delivery—is essential. This is commonly done through student feedback on faculty competencies.22 An innovative approach by Raupach et al,24 introduced a learning-objectives-based assessment system to measure educational quality as knowledge gain, proposing its use in PBRA. Students rated their achievement of specific learning objectives before and after a module, enabling calculation of knowledge gain per objective. This method was validated as a reliable measure of educational performance. Other studies show that involving students in evaluating innovative teaching projects improves alignment with learners’ needs and interests, effectively giving students a role in resource allocation.1416

This leads to the final component of PBRA: resource allocation.

Resources of PBRA programmes

In PBRA, ‘resources’ refer to what is allocated (eg, financial means) and how allocation occurs. While monetary incentives are common, non-financial measures such as technical equipment, personnel support, awards or protected teaching time can also be effective.33 A 2019 survey of German medical schools using PBRA found that, on average, 1.6% of available funds (range: 0–6%) were distributed based on performance-based evaluations.16

Resource allocation typically follows predefined algorithms, rewarding clinical departments, educational modules or individual educators according to evaluation results. In some cases, such as in Baden-Württemberg, PBRA activities determine allocation between medical schools, creating interschool competition for education budgets.22 Elsewhere, PBRA fosters competition within medical schools, with departments competing for funds.14 15

Because PBRA activities are resource-intensive, and allocation should ideally achieve stated objectives, assessing the effectiveness of PBRA programmes is a critical next step.

Effectiveness of PBRA in medical education

According to Deniston’s framework, a programme must demonstrate effectiveness, defined as achieving its objectives through programme activities.20 In medical education, systematic evaluation and structured feedback can improve teaching quality.34 However, evidence that PBRA enhances educational quality remains scarce. This gap is critical, as effectiveness influences both implementation and acceptance.

If improving educational quality is the primary objective, establishing causality between PBRA and quality improvement is essential but challenging. Measuring perceptions of impact may be more feasible, especially when objectives include enhancing recognition and reputation within medical schools.22 33 Currently, little publicly available evidence exists on PBRA effectiveness—a gap this study aims to address. Beyond effectiveness, successful adoption depends on understanding facilitators and barriers.

Facilitators and barriers for implementation and acceptance of PBRA programmes

Facilitating factors include creating valid, transparent PBRA activities that reflect stakeholder needs and are clearly communicated to all departments and individuals competing for resources.35 Hofer et al emphasised minimising bureaucracy while maximising efficiency and ensuring broad participation, including educators from less popular disciplines such as epidemiology or informatics.33 Rewarding both—departments and individuals—for excellence in education is another key motivational factor.33

Hindering factors include evaluation methods that rely on global ratings, such as Likert-scale questions, which can be biased by students’ expectations, lecturer reputation or personal preferences rather than actual teaching quality.24 Such biases may undermine fairness and programme acceptance.

Some factors act as both facilitators and barriers depending on context. Educators’ intrinsic and extrinsic motivation is critical. Financial incentives can foster motivation,3638 but studies show that intrinsic rewards—personal satisfaction, positive student feedback, educational opportunities and career advancement—often rank higher than monetary compensation.37 For educators already receiving extra pay, financial rewards are valued more, whereas for highly intrinsically motivated educators, monetary incentives may reduce autonomy and actually diminish motivation.37

Study aim and research questions

The Medical School of the University of Bern, Switzerland, is considering introducing (financial) incentives in medical education by implementing a tailored PBRA programme. The planning process involves analysing existing PBRA models to identify success factors and barriers to implementation and acceptance. Insights from institutions and individuals with experience in PBRA will be valuable for Bern and other European medical schools.

Therefore, the aim of the project is to lay the foundation for the development of a PBRA programme for the Medical School of the University in Bern, which represents a large Swiss university. The overall guiding question is: Which are the central elements of successful PBRA programmes in central European universities? Methods and analysis

Study design

We will conduct a qualitative cross-sectional study using semistructured interviews with open-ended questions to allow insights beyond researchers’ prior assumptions.3941 Interviews will explore the design, implementation, effectiveness and acceptance of PBRA programmes in medical education, addressing the research questions directly. This open approach enables validation of known elements (deductive themes) and identification of new insights (inductive themes). Thematic analysis will follow Kuckartz’s framework.42 Development of the protocol and reporting will adhere to the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research.43 The full protocol is provided in online supplemental appendix 2.

Participants, sampling and eligibility

Although aspects related to PBRA are relevant to medical education in a global context, the particular setting of the educational system itself is very relevant to many of the research questions. Therefore, interviews will target individuals in Germany and Switzerland familiar with their medical school’s PBRA programme. Participants will include programme managers and staff with complementary expertise. Both countries share similar medical education systems; however, given Germany’s longer PBRA history, most participants will be drawn from German schools, with a smaller Swiss sample for comparative insights.

We will include medical schools with established PBRA programmes for education. Based on current information, up to 15 German medical schools meet this criterion. All German medical schools, including both public and private institutions, will be contacted. Currently the status of possible PBRA programmes in Switzerland is unknown. Therefore, Swiss participants may have limited or no experience with such programmes and may not qualify for interviews. Nevertheless, all Swiss universities, including those in French-speaking and Italian-speaking regions, will be contacted. Information regarding the absence of PBRA programmes in Switzerland is itself of scientific interest and will be reported as part of the study results. As some of the existing literature is outdated, it remains unclear how many previously described programmes are still active. Insights into discontinued or abandoned programmes may therefore be equally informative, and such medical schools will also be eligible for interviews. Medical schools with PBRA for research only will be excluded.5 The study focuses on programmes allocating resources within medical schools, not between institutions.

Sample size and recruitment

We plan qualitative interviews with representatives from up to 15 medical schools in Germany and—if PBRA programmes exist—in Switzerland, ensuring regional diversity. Depending on whether multiple participants are interviewed per institution and Swiss medical schools provide eligible interview partners, the sample will include approximately 15–25 interviewees. Purposive sampling will guide recruitment. Interviews will be conducted between summer 2025 and end of 2026.

Recruitment will leverage existing networks of educators and deans, the German Association for Medical Education (GMA), and the University of Bern’s Master of Medical Education alumni network. Additional participants will be identified through snowball sampling and direct outreach to medical schools not covered initially. Eligible candidates will receive a study description and inclusion/exclusion criteria. No financial or other compensation will be provided.

Data collection will be iterative, with ongoing analysis to determine whether additional interviews are needed. Data saturation will be assessed based on emerging inductive themes arising during the interviews and predefined deductive factors elaborated in the introduction. Possibly interviewing multiple participants from the same institution might help achieve saturation and compensate for the limited number of eligible schools.

Development of interview protocol

The interview protocol was developed following Gideon’s seven-step approach for questionnaire design,44 supported by established frameworks and best-practice guidelines.4547

Step 1

We identified key deductive constructs from the literature above: objectives, activities, resources, effectiveness, and barriers or facilitators of PBRA programmes. These will be complemented by inductive themes emerging from interviews. Validated frameworks such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR)48 and Joint Committee on Standards for Educational Evaluation (JCSEE) Programme Evaluation Standards35 may inform interpretation during analysis, if eligible. These frameworks provide structured domains and constructs for analysing implementation processes and programme effectiveness.

Step 2

Research questions were aligned with these constructs and organised into thematic clusters. Based on this structure, preliminary interview questions and prompts were formulated to be open-ended and non-directive, allowing participants to share perspectives freely.

Step 3

The preliminary questions were reviewed for relevance, logical flow, clarity and brevity. We avoided double-barrelled or negative phrasing, unnecessary repetition and leading language, while ensuring comprehensive coverage of complex topics.

Step 4

Two independent reviewers with expertise in qualitative research and medical education evaluated the protocol for relevance and appropriateness. Their feedback led to revisions, including shortening and rephrasing questions for clarity and openness. The interview protocol was further validated in two external pilot interviews with interviewees typical for the target group. We specifically asked the interviewees how they understand the questions and what possible alternative answers might be provoked.

Step 5

Based on piloting, we refined the interview protocol by adding an introduction and ice-breaker questions to establish rapport and gather background information on the interviewee’s role and views on medical education. To ensure validity, we included initial questions to check understanding of PBRA concepts. The most significant changes focused on streamlining questions to align with research objectives and improving clarity and comprehensiveness. Steps 6 and 7

We finalised the protocol and prepared supporting materials, including an invitation letter (online supplemental appendix 4) and participant instructions detailing study purpose, expected duration, confidentiality, voluntary participation and ethical considerations. An informed consent form (online supplemental appendix 5) will be sent in advance, covering agreement to audio recording, transcription and anonymisation, the right to withdraw at any time and the option to request data deletion without justification. Participants must sign the consent form before the interview. Additionally, an interview checklist (online supplemental appendix 3) was developed to ensure all relevant topics and supplementary information are addressed during the session.

Data collection

Recruitment and interviews will take place between summer 2025 and summer 2026. Interviews will be conducted online via video conferencing (Microsoft Teams or Zoom) and recorded using the platform’s built-in tools. Pilot testing indicated an average duration of approximately 1 hour. Field notes will be taken during each session. All interviews will be audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim in the original language. Participants may review transcripts for accuracy on request and will be invited to share supplementary materials, such as written documents about their PBRA programme, to support data analysis.

Interviewers

Interviews will be conducted by MN (first author) and YB (Associate Professor, MD, PhD, Head of Teaching at the Institute of Research and Education, University Hospital Bern). If needed, an additional interviewer may be included. Both MN and YB have attained extensive knowledge of PBRA programmes in medical education and qualitative interviewing49 and were closely involved in developing this protocol. SG (Professor and Director of the Institute of Medical Education, University of Bern) will provide guidance and supervision throughout the process, drawing on substantial experience in qualitative research.

For the initial interviews, MN and YB will co-conduct sessions, alternating as lead interviewer to ensure reflexivity, credibility and consistency in technique. Once alignment is achieved, interviews will be conducted independently. Interviews will primarily be conducted in German. Where German is not feasible, particularly in the French-speaking or Italian-speaking regions of Switzerland, interviews will be conducted in English. MN and YB are fluent English speakers. The interview protocol has been translated into English (see online supplemental appendix 1).

Data analysis

A thematic analysis of the interview data will follow Kuckartz’s methodology.42 Each transcript will first be reviewed by the interviewer to ensure familiarity with the data. The team will then identify initial topics inductively,50 applying CFIR and JCSEE frameworks where appropriate. To minimise bias, a subset of transcripts will undergo independent coding by another author, and discrepancies will be discussed until consensus is reached. Team coding will continue until a high level of consistency is achieved.50 SG will provide methodological oversight throughout. NVivo software (Lumivero, Denver, USA) will support the analysis.51

Next, themes will be aligned with deductive topics identified from the literature, while new inductive themes will be documented. Both sets of themes will be discussed in workshops with all authors and an external expert involved in protocol development to ensure openness and flexibility.52 These workshops aim to consolidate a robust set of themes to address the research questions.

Patient and public involvement

No patients were or will be involved in the preparation of this study protocol, or in the main study.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval

The Cantonal Ethical Review Board in Bern was informed about the study (Req 2024-01001). Under the Swiss Ethical Review Act, formal ethical clearance is not required as the study does not involve biological data or sensitive personal information (eg, ethnicity, population data).53

Benefits to participants

Participating medical schools may benefit from insights into the architecture, implementation, effectiveness and acceptance of PBRA programmes at other institutions. These findings will support critical appraisal of current funding structures and foster programme development, improvement and collaboration.

Confidentiality

All data will be anonymised. Names of schools and individuals will be replaced by ID codes during transcription, with the identification key stored separately. Quotes in publications will be anonymised to prevent identification of individuals or institutions.

Storage of data

Unanonymised data (eg, consent forms, transcripts, emails) will be stored in a password-protected system (Bern Open Repository and Information System).54 Only the authors will have access. Audio recordings will be transferred to the University’s cloud service and deleted from personal devices immediately after upload. After transcription, all audio files will be deleted. Anonymised data will be archived for at least 10 years. Anonymised data (according to the consent form for participation in the study, see online supplemental appendix 5) will be published according to the next paragraph on ‘Registration/dissemination’. Anonymised interview transcripts are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request and subject to approval of the interviewee. Full interview transcripts will not be made publicly available due to the risk of participant reidentification despite anonymisation.

Registration/dissemination

The study was registered on the Open Science Framework after commencement of the study and prior to completion of data collection (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/QBDF2).

Findings will be shared with participating medical schools and disseminated through a peer-reviewed journal publication and eventually academic seminars and presentations at relevant conferences. In addition, results will be communicated in accessible formats to a broader audience through medical education conferences and events, possibly including meetings organised by the Swiss Institute for Medical Education (Schweizerisches Institut für ärztliche Weiter- und Fortbildung) and the Society for Medical Education (GMA), medical education lecture series at the University of Bern, meetings of deans from medical schools in Germany and Switzerland and institutional university communication channels.
