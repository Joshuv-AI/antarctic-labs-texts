The Sloping-Type Adult Spinal Deformity
Authors: Yuki Mihara, Tomohiko Hasegawa, Yu Yamato, Go Yoshida, Tomohiro Banno, Hideyuki Arima, Shin Oe, Koichiro Ide, Tomohiro Yamada, Yukihiro Matsuyama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction Some adult patients with spinal deformities show a sloping spine, not kyphosis, with severe global malalignment and deterioration of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs). The purpose of this study was to elucidate sloping-type deformities on the basis of radiographic parameters and PROMs. Methods This study included participants from a health screening program with sagittal vertical axis (SVA) >40 mm. The sloping-type deformity (S group) was defined as the deformity in which all posterior vertebral walls were positioned anteriorly to the vertical line extending from the posterior end of the sacrum on standing whole-spine lateral radiographs. SVA, thoracic kyphosis (TK), lumbar lordosis (LL), L4-S angle, pelvic incidence (PI), and pelvic tilt (PT) were measured. PROMs were evaluated using the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). Results A total of 348 participants (142 men and 206 women; average age 75.8 years) were included in the study, and 50 participants (14.4%) were classified into the S group. The mean age and measured variables of the S and non-sloping-type (non-S) group were 76.1, 72.6 years; SVA 111, 79 mm; TK 24, 35°; L4-S 15, 30°; PI 58, 49°; PT 27, 21°; PI-LL 28, 14°; and ODI 22, 15%, respectively. There were 30 participants (60%) with evident lumbar anterolisthesis in the S group and 76 (25.5%) in the non-S group (p<0.001). The S group had larger SVA, PI, PT, and PI-LL (all p<0.001) and lower TK and L4-S angle (both p<0.001) than did the non-S group. The S group showed an inferior ODI to that of the non-S group (p=0.012). Conclusions The sloping-type deformity showed a significantly higher PI, and worse spinopelvic alignment and PROMs. The significant factors contributing to the incidence of sloping-type deformities were higher PI, prevalence of lumbar anteriolisthesis, and lower TK and L4-S angle.

Introduction

Rapid population aging is well recognized in developed countries. Accordingly, the need for and awareness of health care has increased. Adult spinal deformity (ASD), one of the diseases most impactful in the deterioration of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs)1), increases to 68% among older persons2). Furthermore, some useful diagnostic and therapeutic approaches have been established for ASD3-7).

Schwab et al.4) analyzed the main factors affecting sagittal spino-pelvic deformity, sagittal vertical axis (SVA), pelvic incidence (PI), lumbar lordosis (LL), and pelvic tilt (PT) and subsequently determined thresholds for each factor according to health-related quality of life deterioration. Itoi8) also reported a logical visual classification of sagittal spinal deformities: round back; increased thoracic kyphosis (TK), hollow round back; increased TK and LL, lower acute kyphosis; curves with a lowered kyphosis apex, and whole kyphosis; a single kyphotic curve. Sagittal plane deformities, most commonly presenting as uncompensated hyperkyphosis, result from accumulated degenerative changes9), such as disc or facet degeneration, ligamentous laxity, and osteoporosis with vertebral body fractures10).

However, some patients with ASD have a sloping spine (Fig. 1), which is not kyphotic, with severe global malalignment and deterioration of PROMs. Moreover, these deformities cannot be classified using the classification system of Itoi8) for kyphotic spine.

In contrast, flat back types without global malalignment have been reported in the classification of lumbar degenerative kyphosis (LDK)11). However, kyphotic, not flat back, spines were only accompanied by global malalignment in the classification. Consequently, sloping-type deformities are not classified in the classification.

Son et al.12) investigated the relationship between stooping and radiographic findings in patients with LDK. Stooping was defined using only appearance; no specific assessment of stooping was performed in patients with severe global malalignment and flat back spine. Yagi et al. reported drop body syndrome in patients with LDK, defined as PI-LL >40°, C7 SVA >100 mm, and Cobb angle <30°. However, no distinction was made between kyphotic and sloping spines in that study. Therefore, there is no similar theory regarding sloping-type spinal deformities. This study aimed to elucidate sloping-type spinal deformities on the basis of evaluations of radiographic parameters and PROMs.

Materials and Methods

The study included participants in a local health-screening program in 2012. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of our university (approval number 21-283). All the experimental investigations involving human participants reported in this manuscript were performed after obtaining informed consent.

Inclusion/Exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥50 years, (2) available whole-spine and pelvic radiographs performed in the standing position, and (3) SVA ≥40 mm regarded as global malalignment in the scoliosis research society (SRS)-ASD classification4).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) total joint arthroplasty, (2) instrumented spinal surgery, and (3) inability to stand without assistance.

Radiological assessment

The radiographic technique was standardized as follows: (1) the patient was in a standing position, staring straight ahead, with the hands on the clavicles; (2) three views were acquired (anteroposterior, lateral craniopelvic, and lateral pelvic); and (3) the x-ray tube and radiograph were positioned 1.5 m apart. The digitized radiographs were transferred to a computer as Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine data and measured using Surgimap Spine imaging software (Nemaris Inc., New York City, NY, USA). Radiological measurements were reviewed by two experienced orthopedic spine surgeons.

The sloping-type deformity

The sloping-type deformity was defined as that in which all the posterior vertebral walls were located anteriorly to the vertical line extending from the posterior end of the sacrum on standing whole-spine lateral radiographs with SVA ≥40 mm, regarded as a global malalignment in the SRS-ASD classification4) (Fig. 1).

Measured parameters

The measured parameters in the radiographs were as follows: (1) SVA, (2) TK: Cobb angle between T5 and T12, (3) LL: Cobb angle between L1 and S1 superior endplates, (4) L4-S angle: Cobb angle between the L4 and S1 superior endplates, (5) PI, and (6) PT.

Lumbar anterolisthesis

Participants with ≥3 mm anterior lumbar vertebral slips were referred for whole-spine sagittal radiographs.

Clinical evaluation

The Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) was used to assess PROMs.

Data and statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Demographic variables were analyzed using descriptive statistics. The participants were divided according to their ODI values. The higher ODI group was defined as participants with an ODI of >30%, average of +1SD. The remaining participants were defined as the non-higher-ODI group. The higher and non-higher-ODI groups were compared. Comparisons of the sloping-type (S) and non-sloping-type (non-S) groups were performed. Comparisons of the S group and the non-S group were also performed in participants with SVA ≥95 mm referring to the SVA modifiers of the SRS classification4). Differences in continuous and categorical variables between two independent groups were analyzed using an unpaired Student t test and Fisher's exact test, respectively. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify influential factors. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Results

Among the 746 volunteers for participation in this study, 398 were excluded owing to the SVA <40 mm. Eventually, this study included 348 participants (142 men and 206 women; average age 75.8 years [range 52-92 years]).

Comparisons of the higher ODI and non-higher-ODI group

There were 57 participants (16.3%) in the higher ODI group and 291 (83.7%) in the non-higher-ODI group. The mean ages of the higher and non-higher-ODI groups were 78.2 and 75.4 years, respectively. Participants in the higher ODI group were significantly older than those in the lower ODI group (p=0.007). There were 17 participants (29.8%) defined as the sloping type in the higher ODI group, and 33 (11.8%) in the non-higher-ODI group (p=0.001). Mean height, weight, body mass index, SVA, TK, LL, L4-S, PI, SS, PT, PI-LL, and ODI values are listed in Table 1. The higher ODI group were significantly taller, with larger SVA, PT, and PI-LL values (p<0.001, p<0.001, p=0.011, and p=0.002, respectively), and lower LL values (p=0.036) than those in the non-higher-ODI group. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that height (odds ratio=0.944; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.904-0.985; p=0.007), SVA (odds ratio=1.014; 95% CI: 1.005-1.023; P=0.003), and sloping type (odds ratio=2.597; 95% CI: 1.223-5.517; p=0.013) were significant factors influencing the incidence of the higher ODI group (Table 2). In this higher ODI regression model, age, height, SVA, PT, PI-LL, and incidence of the sloping type were included as independent variables.

Comparisons of the S and non-S group

There were 50 participants (14.4%) and 298 (85.6%) in the S and non-S groups, respectively. The mean ages of the S and non-S group were 76.1 and 75.8 years, respectively. Lumbar anterolisthesis was more frequently observed in the S group than in the non-S group (p<0.001), with 30 participants (60%) classified with lumbar anterolisthesis in the S group and 76 (25.5%) in the non-S group. The vertebral levels of the S group are shown in Fig. 2. The lumbar anterolisthesis presented below the L4 level in 25 participants (83.3%) in the S group. The mean values of SVA, TK, LL, L4-S, PI, SS, PT, PI-LL, and ODI are listed in Table 3. The S group had significantly larger SVA, PT, PI, and PI-LL values (all p<0.001) and lower TK and L4-S angles (both p<0.001) than did the non-S group. The S group also indicated significantly worse ODI values than did the non-S group (p=0.012). There were 27 participants (54%) with SVA ≥95 mm in the S group and 67 (22%) in the non-S group (p<0.001). Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that TK (odds ratio=0.938; 95% CI: 0.906-0.971; p<0.001), L4-S angle (odds ratio=0.904; 95% CI: 0.864-0.946; p<0.001), PI (odds ratio=1.114; 95% CI: 1.064-1.166; p<0.001), and incidence of lumbar anteriolisthesis (odds ratio=2.438; 95% CI: 1.048-5.670; p=0.039) were significant influential factors for the incidence of sloping-type deformities in the S group (Table 4). In the sloping-type regression model, age, sex, SVA, TK, L4-S, PI, and the incidence of lumbar anteriolisthesis were included as independent variables. PT was excluded from the model because of its singularity: PI=SS+PT13).

Comparison of the S and non-S group in subjects with SVA ≥95 mm

There were 27 and 67 participants in the S and non-S groups, respectively. Lumbar anterolisthesis was more frequently observed in the S group than in the non-S group (p=0.002). The mean values of SVA, TK, LL, L4-S, PI, SS, PT, PI-LL, and ODI are listed in Table 3. The S group had a significantly larger PI (p<0.001) and lower TK and L4-S angles (p=0.001 and p<0.001, respectively) than did the non-S group.

Discussion

Firstly, a sloping-type deformity was one of the factors that influenced ODI deterioration (Table 1, 2). Moreover, the S group showed significantly greater PI and worse spinopelvic alignment, and PROMs (Table 3). Average SVA (110.5 mm), PT (26.7°), and PI-LL (28.1°) corresponded to the sagittal modifiers ++, +, and ++ according to the Schwab classification for ASD4). Therefore, elucidations of pathologies of this deformity are crucial.

Secondly, a higher PI was an influential factor in the incidence of sloping-type deformities. An increased anterior tilt of the SS is associated with higher LL in healthy subjects14). Considering the geometrical relation between PI and the two positional parameters, PT and SS13), higher PI is associated with larger LL in normal spinal morphology15). Therefore, in individuals with higher PI, it is necessary to achieve greater discal extension in each intervertebral space to attain suitable LL. Because aging discopathy is characterized by disc narrowing with parallel endplates, loss of lordosis occurs16), and it might be more difficult to achieve sufficient LL in individuals with higher PI than in those with lower PI. This challenge arises owing to the varying capacities of extension within the intervertebral spaces.

Thirdly, lower L4-S angle was identified as one of the factors influencing the incidence of sloping-type deformities. Lumbar hyperextension is a crucial compensation mechanism for sagittal imbalance16). Considering the distribution of LL, L4-S accounts for 50%-80% of the total LL in normal alignment6). In individuals with lower L4-S angles, the sagittal compensation of lumbar hyperextension might be considerably impaired owing to the large proportion of L4-S.

Lastly, a high incidence of lumbar anteriolisthesis is an influential factor in the incidence of sloping-type deformities. Schuller et al.17) reported that the upper lumbar spine was more extended in patients with L4-L5 degenerative spondylolisthesis. Therefore, lower lumbar anteriolisthesis might impair the sagittal compensation of hyperextension in the lower lumbar region. Indeed, 25 participants (83.3%) in our study presented with lumbar anterolistheses below the L4 level in the S group (Fig. 2). Barrey et al.18) reported a loss of LL in patients with degenerative spondylolisthesis compared with the normal population after matching according to the PI. Lower levels of anteriolisthesis might induce a decrease in lower LL and L4-S angle (Fig. 3). Nakamae et al.19) reported that high PI was a predictor of L4 anterior listhesis, with a cut-off value of 51.3°. Several authors have also reported that patients with degenerative spondylolisthesis (DS) revealed a significantly higher PI than those in the non-DS population17,18,20-23). In this study, the S and non-S groups showed an average PI of 57.8° and 48.9°, respectively. Therefore, this variation in pelvic morphology was considered a predictor not only for anteriolisthesis but also for sloping-type deformities.

Reduction of lower lumbar lordosis induced by lumbar anterolisthesis.

In participants with severe global malalignment (SVA ≥95 mm), there was no significant difference in the SVA between the S and non-S groups (Table 5). However, higher PI and lower L4-S angles were observed in the S group, similar to the participants with SVA ≥40 mm. TK was also lower in the S group. The PI was assumed to be a fixed morphological value and defined as incapable of adjustment24,25). Therefore, the S group should be pathologically different from the non-S group in individuals with severe global malalignments (Fig. 4). However, changes in PI have been investigated in some reports26,27) showing mean changes in PI of 2.9° between anterior and posterior rotation in the resting position27), and 4.6° before and after corrective fusion surgeries using S2-alar-iliac screws, respectively28). In this study, the difference of mean PI was 11.1° between the S and the non-S groups with SVA ≥95 mm (Table 5). Therefore, this greater variation in mean PI cannot be achieved by the position of the pelvis and corrective fusion surgery.

This study had some limitations. First, the study cohort comprised Japanese patients aged ≥50 years. Therefore, the generalizability of the data to other ethnic groups is unclear. Second, most subjects who participated in this study resided in mountainous areas and were engaged in agriculture, which may differ from urban environments. Third, this study included a small number of participants, especially for the sub-analyses. Further investigation with larger sample sizes should be conducted in the future. Fourth, this study used a cross-sectional design; thus, we could not determine the pathology of the sloping-type deformity. Therefore, further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether sloping-type deformities differ from other kyphotic deformities. Fifth, the sloping-type deformity could be caused by muscle weakness of lower extremities or back muscles and by alignments of lower extremities28-30). However, assessments of the muscle mass or the muscle strength were not performed in this study. The radiographs of lower extremities also were not performed. We must evaluate these parameters in the future for elucidation of this deformity. Sixth, the hip contractures, which limit sagittal compensation through hip extension31), could affect the occurrence of this deformity. Therefore, the hip joint range of motion is deemed an important factor. However, it was not measured in this study. We must evaluate this parameter in the future. Seventh, comorbidities are considered important factors for this deformity. Parkinson's disease and lumbar spinal stenosis could induce the leaning-forward posture, respectively32,33). Indeed, comorbidities were evaluated using the check sheets filled by subjects themselves in this study. One subject with Parkinson's disease and four subjects with lumbar spinal stenosis were included. However, all five subjects belonged to the non-S group. We need to investigate this deformity in the patients with these comorbidities, not subjects in the local health-screening program, in the future.

In conclusion, the sloping-type deformities are an influential factor in ODI deterioration. The sloping-type deformity showed a significantly higher PI and worse spinopelvic alignment, and PROMs. Patients with sloping-type deformities showed a significantly higher incidence of lumbar anterolisthesis. The significant factors for the incidence of sloping-type deformities were higher PI, incidence of lumbar anteriolisthesis, and lower TK and L4-S angles.
