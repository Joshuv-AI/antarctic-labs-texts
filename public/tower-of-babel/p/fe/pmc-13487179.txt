Bromide is a surprisingly potent larvicide for Anopheles gambiae in the laboratory
Authors: Stella M Ikuzwe, Joshua I Raji, Christopher J Potter, Steven E Rokita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMosquito-borne diseases remain a major global health burden, and novel approaches to vector control are urgently needed as resistance to conventional insecticides spreads. Most mosquitoes breed in freshwater but can tolerate low levels of halogen salts. Whether specific halides exhibit selective toxicity toward mosquito larvae has not been systematically investigated. We now report on a survey focusing on Anopheles gambiae, the primary vector for malaria in sub-Saharan Africa. Bromide is selectively and potently toxic to Anopheles larvae with an LC50 below 10 µM for its sodium potassium salt. This sensitivity is more than 10-fold greater than that observed for the sodium salts of fluoride, chloride and iodide. Bromide toxicity is stage-specific with An. gambiae larvae displaying 300-fold greater sensitivity than adults while eggs and pupae remain unaffected by 5 mM sodium bromide. Anopheles stephensi larvae exhibit similar sensitivity, whereas Aedes aegypti and Culex quinquefasciatus larvae are approximately 10-fold more tolerant of sodium bromide. Mechanistically, bromide toxicity operates independently of pathways targeted by conventional insecticides. Co-administration of diflubenzuron or spinosad neither suppressed nor enhanced bromide’s effects. Bromide exposure induced overexpression of GluCl and CCC-2 ion channels without affecting Rdl, Best, or KCC expression. Chloride also protected against bromide toxicity, suggesting these halides share a common receptor. Thoracic swelling in dying larvae indicates that bromide disrupts osmotic or hydration balance. These findings are unexpected given that bromide is generally considered non-toxic to aquatic and terrestrial organisms, revealing a previously unknown vulnerability specific to Anopheles mosquitoes. The selective toxicity of bromide toward Anopheles larvae, combined with its independence from existing insecticide resistance mechanisms, may serve as a new lead for controlling malaria vectors. Targeting this bromide-sensitive pathway may complement current larvicidal approaches and provide an alternative for populations resistant to standard insecticides. Introduction

The sensitivity of mosquitoes to salinity is a key determinant of their geographic distribution and the transmission of vector-borne diseases such as malaria, dengue, Zika, and chikungunya1. Most mosquito species are restricted to freshwater habitats during their larval stages, although some have evolved tolerance to brackish environments, expanding both their ecological range and disease burden1,2. Maintaining ion and osmotic balance is essential for mosquito survival, particularly after blood feeding, which introduces a fluid volume up to ten times greater than the hemolymph3. The Malpighian tubules, functioning analogously to vertebrate kidneys, are responsible for excreting excess Na+, K+, Cl−, and water and have been identified as promising targets for novel insecticide development4,5. Conventional mosquito control strategies principally rely on neurotoxic insecticides including pyrethroids, organophosphates, and carbamates as well as chitin synthesis inhibitors and juvenile hormone analogues6–9. However, the rapid development and spread of resistance to these compounds have compromised their long-term effectiveness prompting an urgent need for alternative targets and compounds with novel modes of action10,11. Biological larvicides such as Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis and Bacillus sphaericus have emerged as important alternatives because of their selectivity, minimal environmental impact, and low propensity for developing resistance12–14. Nevertheless, many biological larvicides exhibit limited residual activity and often require repeated application, creating operational and logistical challenges for large-scale vector control programs. These limitations continue to motivate the search for additional larvicidal approaches with distinct mechanisms of action and ease of application.

Among physiologically relevant anions, chloride is both abundant and multifunctional in mosquitoes contributing to neuronal signaling, epithelial transport and osmoregulation15,16. Its intracellular concentration is tightly regulated through various ion channels and transporters3,4. By contrast, bromide is less prevalent in the environment and its biological role in mosquitoes remains largely unexplored. In mammals and fruit flies, however, bromide functions as a micronutrient essential for peroxidasin, an enzyme that catalyzes sulfilimine cross-links in collagen IV to maintain basement membrane integrity17,18. Homologues of peroxidasin are present in mosquito genomes and may likely have similar functions and an equivalent requirement for bromide. At least in Drosophila melanogaster, bromide is also used to form 3-bromo-L-tyrosine (Br-Tyr) either as a cellular signal or a consequence of oxidative stress19. This compound is degraded by a reductive dehalogenase homologous to mammalian iodotyrosine deiodinase (IYD), a flavoenzyme conserved across most metazoa20. Disruption of IYD in Drosophila leads to elevated levels of Br-Tyr and impaired fertility, suggesting a physiological importance for halotyrosine metabolism in insects.

The connection between Br-Tyr, IYD and fertility in Drosophila led us to extend our investigations to mosquitoes with a focus on Anopheles gambiae, the primary vector of Plasmodium falciparum that causes malaria in humans. During preliminary investigations, we unexpectedly discovered that bromide induced strong larvicidal effects as described in this report. An. gambiae larvae are considerably more sensitive to bromide than other halides with a hierarchy of bromide > iodide > fluoride > chloride. Characterization of this effect includes dose-response profiles, species specificity and results from co-administering chloride and insecticides.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

All halide salts were ACS reagent grade (> 99%) and purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific (sodium bromide, sodium chloride, sodium iodide and potassium bromide) or J.T. Baker (sodium fluoride). Spinosad and diflubenzuron were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (analytical standard grade). TetraMin® Tropical Fish Flakes and Spice Supreme® food coloring (red) were purchased from Amazon. All stock solutions were prepared with reverse osmosis (RO) water.

Mosquito rearing

Anopheles gambiae (Keele colony), Anopheles stephensi and Aedes aegypti were obtained from the Johns Hopkins Malaria Research Institute (JHMRI) insectary core facility at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health (Baltimore, MD). The AKDR strain of An. gambiae carrying the L1014F kdr mutation associated with pyrethroid resistance21 was obtained from BEI Resources and Culex quinquefasciatus was obtained from the Department of Entomology at the University of Maryland (College Park, MD). These were maintained in the JHMRI insectary core facility under standard conditions (27 ± 2 °C, 80% relative humidity, 12:12 light/dark photoperiod) throughout all studies described below. All solutions remained static from the beginning to end of each experiment and were never renewed, changed or refreshed. Larvae were reared in 300 mL of RO water and fed 2 mg of TetraMin fish food every two days until pupation.

Toxicity of halide salts to larvae

First instar (L1) larvae were exposed to alkali halide solutions (300 mL) at concentrations up to 1 mM in triplicate using 8 × 6 × 2 cm plastic containers. Each condition consisted of 15 larvae per replicate unless otherwise stated. Unless specified, larvae were monitored daily for survival and developmental progression, including changes in size and visible abnormalities such as pigmentation changes, pupation and eclosion. Death was defined as a lack of response to gentle agitation and was monitored until adult emergence. Acute toxicity was measured by treating fourth instar (L4) larvae as above and measuring mortality daily. Each concentration was tested in triplicate with 15 larvae per replicate unless otherwise stated, and LC50 values were calculated based on cumulative mortality for as long as 72 h.

Effect of sodium bromide on hatching of An. gambiae eggs

Eggs (200–300 eggs, aged 1–3 days) were carefully transferred with a brush to 3 × 2-inch clear cups containing aqueous sodium bromide (0–5 mM). Each condition was tested in triplicate. The extent of egg hatching was monitored daily until no additional larvae emerged.

Effect of sodium bromide on adult emergence from pupae

Newly pupated An. gambiae mosquitoes (15 per container) were transferred into 3 × 2-inch clear cups containing aqueous sodium bromide from 1.0 to 5.0 mM (40 mL). Each concentration was tested in triplicate. The cups were placed in 4 × 4 inches net-covered cages to prevent adult escape. The number of adults eclosing each day was recorded.

Effect of sodium bromide on adult An. gambiae mosquitoes

Newly emerged An. gambiae adults (20 per concentration) were transferred to cages (4 × 4 inches) containing a 40 mL glass bottle filled with 10% aqueous sucrose and supplemented with sodium halides (0–10 mM). Mosquitoes were housed for seven days under the same environmental conditions as the larvae described above and adult survival relative to control populations reared on just 10% aqueous sucrose was recorded in two replicates. The solutions were not renewed or refreshed and low levels of evaporation were constant for all comparative studies.

Chloride protection from bromide toxicity in An. gambiae larvae

Two co-exposure assays were conducted using An. gambiae larvae. In the first, larvae (L1) were exposed to increasing concentrations of sodium bromide (0–1000 µM) either alone or in combination with 1 mM sodium chloride. In the second, larvae were exposed to fixed concentrations of sodium bromide (10 or 50 µM) supplemented with sodium chloride at 10, 50, 100 or 1000 µM. Each treatment was performed in triplicate with 15 larvae per replicate in 300 mL of RO water. Larvae were maintained under standard rearing conditions and fed every two days. Survival was monitored until adulthood and percent adult emergence was recorded as the experimental endpoint.

Examination of synergy between bromide and selected larvicides using a fixed-ratio assay

Working solutions of sodium bromide and two commercial larvicides, diflubenzuron and spinosad, were prepared at concentrations similar to their individual LC50 values based on adult emergence as described above for sodium bromide. Mixtures were generated by combining the stock solutions of sodium bromide and a larvicide in volumetric ratios of 1:1, 2:1, 1:2, 1:5, and 5:1 to yield a constant final volume22. For each ratio, corresponding single-compound controls were prepared using comparable concentrations. Duplicate groups of twenty An. gambiae larvae (L1) were transferred into standard 3 × 2-inch plastic rearing cups containing 40 mL of each test solution. Larvae were maintained under standard insectary conditions and their mortality and developmental progression were recorded daily until 24 h after adult emergence. Observed mortality induced by the mixtures at fixed ratios was compared with the expected mortality predicted under independent action as calculated by the Bliss independence model (See Supplemental eq. S1)22. Synergy scores were defined as the difference between observed and expected mortality for which values near zero indicate additivity. Positive values indicate synergism and negative values indicate potential antagonism (Supplemental Tables S1 and S2).

Gene expression subsequent to sodium bromide treatment

Groups of 10–15 An. gambiae larvae (L1) were reared in the presence of 50 µM sodium bromide until L4 when they were homogenized in 500 µL of TRIzol (Invitrogen) using a sterile, RNase-free mortar and pestle cooled by ice. The homogenates were transferred to RNase-free 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and subjected to 3–5 freeze-thaw cycles in an ethanol-dry ice bath. An additional 500 µL of TRIzol was next added to each sample that was then vortexed and incubated at room temperature (5 min). For extraction, 200 µL of chloroform was added and samples were again vortexed for 20 s followed by an incubation at room temperature (10–15 min). Samples were next centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and the aqueous phase (~ 600 µL) was transferred to a fresh RNase-free tube. RNA was precipitated by the addition of an equal volume of 100% isopropanol and 3 µL of glycogen (5 mg/mL) followed by gentle mixing and then cooling to −20 °C for at least 1 h. RNA was pelleted by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, washed with 1 mL of 70% ethanol and centrifuged again at 7,500 × g for 10 min. The resulting pellets were air-dried and resuspended in 30–50 µL of RNase-free water at 60 °C. RNA concentration was assessed by its absorbance at 260 nm using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Genomic DNA was removed from these samples using a TURBO DNA-free™ Kit (Invitrogen) and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad) with 400 ng of DNase-treated RNA per 20 µL reaction. Each reaction contained 4 µL of 5× iScript Reaction Mix, 1 µL iScript reverse transcriptase and nuclease-free water to a final volume of 20 µL. Reverse transcription was performed in a thermal cycler under the following conditions for 34 cycles: 25 °C for 5 min, 46 °C for 20 min, 95 °C for 1 min. Subsequently, samples were stored at 4 °C prior to their analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was conducted using a SYBR Green-based detection system on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR platform (Bio-Rad). Each reaction contained 5 µL of SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Bio-Rad), 1 µL of the cDNA from above, 0.5 µL each of the forward and reverse primers (10 µM, Supplemental Table S3) for the gene of interest and 3 µL of nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling was performed using the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. Each reaction was carried out in six technical replicates. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method23 with Rps7 serving as the internal reference gene.

Data and statistical analysis

Experimental endpoints were selected to distinguish between acute larvicidal effects and broader developmental consequences of halide exposure across mosquito life stages. Early larval exposure experiments (L1 to adult) were used to evaluate cumulative developmental success and adult emergence under continuous halide exposure, whereas later-stage exposure (L4 to adult) was designed to assess acute stage-specific sensitivity. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 7 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Survival, developmental, and gene expression data were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) post hoc test. Because formal tests of normality and homoscedasticity were not performed for every dataset, ANOVA was used as an approximate test for treatment-level comparisons under the experimental replicate structure used in this study. Dose–response data were analyzed to determine LC50 values by nonlinear regression of percent mortality with a four-parameter logistic model in GraphPad Prism. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Bromide-induce larval mortality in An. gambiae mosquitoes

After detecting larvicidal activity of bromide in control studies on IYD, we investigated bromide toxicity by exposing newly hatched L1 An. gambiae larvae to sodium bromide concentrations ranging up to 1 mM. Larval survival was assessed daily, and successful development to adulthood is summarized in Fig. 1A. Significant mortality was observed at concentrations as low as 10 µM. Larvae consistently died before reaching the third instar (L3) when exposed to more than 600 µM bromide, and survival to L3 was only possible in the presence of sodium bromide at concentrations that were no greater than 50 µM. These data provided an estimated LC50 of 8.5 µM (Supplemental Fig. S1). To differentiate the effects of bromide from its sodium counterion, the same analysis was repeated with potassium bromide. An equivalent larvicidal activity was observed and the estimated LC50 of potassium bromide (9.8 µM) was nearly equivalent to that of sodium bromide (Supplemental Fig. S2). Thus, the potent toxicity arises from exposure to bromide independent of its counterion.

An. gambiae larvae were also examined for sensitivity to other common sodium halide salts. None expressed a toxicity at low µM levels comparable to that of sodium bromide. Exposing L1 larvae to sodium chloride at concentrations as high as 1.0 mM had no visible effect on development and emergence of adults (Fig. 1B). The lack of sensitivity to chloride is not surprising since it is highly abundant in the environment and the concentration of this ion is carefully regulated in vivo through specialized ion channels and transporters in the Malpighian tubules4. Additionally, our data on sodium chloride are consistent with those published previously24. The toxicity of sodium fluoride was only marginal at the highest concentration tested (1.0 mM, Fig. 1C). Sodium iodide was the one other salt examined in this analysis that also expressed toxicity although its potency is more than 10-fold weaker than that of sodium bromide (Fig. 1D). The stark contrast between the effects of bromide and the other halides suggests that its lethality is not due to general osmotic stress or ionic conditions. Instead, bromide appears unusual in its ability to disrupt one or more key processes required for the viability and development of An. gambiae larvae.

Acute toxicity of bromide

The use of L1 larvae to assess bromide toxicity combines the effects on early development with those of persistent exposure. As a complement, we assessed acute toxicity by exposing fourth instar (L4) larvae to bromide. L4 larvae are commonly used for toxicity assays and offer advantages of consistent feeding behavior and reduced development compared to earlier larval stages25,26. Mortality of L4 larvae was observed within 24 h when bromide was equal to or exceeded 500 µM and continued to increase during the length of exposure (72 h, Fig. 2A). The survival rate at this final time was no more than 20% for bromide exposure as low as 50 µM. With bromide at 1.0 mM, almost 80% of the larvae died within 48 h. In contrast, larvae exposed to a low concentration of bromide (10 µM) developed and pupated within 72 h with a success of 60% compared to that of the untreated control group. Exposure time and developmental stage clearly contribute to the bromide response since the LC50 for acute toxicity expressed by L4 larvae to adult emergence (43 µM, Fig. 2B, Supplemental Fig. S3) is 5-fold higher than that for L1 larvae (Supplemental Fig. S1).

Larval morphology

To demonstrate that bromide-induced mortality was not due to reduced feeding or starvation, nutrient uptake by L1 larvae was tracked by the presence of red food dye in the absence and presence of sodium bromide (10 µM, near to its LC50 value). After developing to the L4 stage, larvae with and without sodium bromide exposure contained equivalent levels of dye (Supplemental Fig. S4). As a control, the dye alone caused no obvious morphological changes or toxicity when compared to larvae reared in the absence of the dye. In contrast, larvae (L1) treated with 50 µM sodium bromide typically died by the L4 stage and presented severe swelling of the thorax and often with discoloration suggestive of impaired osmotic regulation or water retention (Supplemental Fig. S5). These effects were partially ameliorated by the additional presence of sodium chloride (see below) and no visual abnormalities were detected after exposure to sodium fluoride or sodium iodide at 50 µM consistent with their lack of toxicity under these conditions (Fig. 1).

Bromide toxicity at different life stages of An. Gambiae

To evaluate stage-specific susceptibility to bromide, we investigated its effects on the remaining stages of the life cycle including eggs, pupae, and adults. Exposure of newly laid eggs to sodium bromide at concentrations up to 5 mM had no significant effect on hatching success compared to untreated controls (Fig. 3A), suggesting that eggs are resistant to bromide-induced toxicity. Likewise, treatment of newly formed pupae with sodium bromide at concentrations as high as 5 mM did not significantly reduce adult emergence (Fig. 3B). These results are consistent with previous studies that the non-feeding stages with low surface permeability and limited physiological activity are less susceptible to insecticides27,28. Adult mosquitoes were significantly more sensitive to sodium bromide than eggs and pupae. Exposure to 1 mM sodium bromide resulted in approximately 25% mortality after 7 days and the mortality increased to 50% in the presence of 3 mM sodium bromide. Mortality progressively increased after treatment with 5 mM and 10 mM sodium bromide (Fig. 3C). Thus, bromide is also toxic to adult An. gambiae although their sensitivity is significantly lower than that of larvae.

To assess whether the effect of sodium bromide on adult An. gambiae is unique, their mortality was tested in the presence of the same set of halide salts used with larvae but the concentrations were increased to 5 mM and 10 mM. Sodium chloride and sodium iodide did not enhance mortality (Supplemental Fig. S6). Sodium fluoride expressed measurable toxicity at both concentrations (≥ 50%) and sodium bromide induced the highest mortality (≥ 60%). Except for bromide, the relative potency of the halides is not maintained in the larval and adult stages. Bromide remains the most toxic of these halides and is specific to the life stages that require feeding. An. gambiae eggs and pupae are shielded from the environment and impervious to the presence of bromide.

Bromide toxicity across different mosquito species

To assess interspecies variation in bromide susceptibility, larval toxicity studies were extended to three mosquito species relevant to public health: An. stephensi, Ae. aegypti, and Cx. quinquefasciatus, the primary vectors of malaria, dengue, Zika, chikungunya, and West Nile virus. Applying the same protocols as described in Fig. 1, one-day-old larvae (L1) from each species were exposed to increasing concentrations of sodium bromide. Sensitivity to bromide was highly species dependent (Fig. 4). An. stephensi larvae displayed a concentration dependence nearly identical to that of An. gambiae and exhibited an LC50 of 6.3 µM (Fig. 4A, Supplemental Fig. S7). In contrast, Ae. aegypti and Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae were significantly more tolerant than those of the Anopheles genus and could tolerate 10-fold higher concentrations than the LC50 of Anopheles with only 20–40% mortality. The high sensitivity of Anopheles relative to Aedes and Culex may ultimately help to define the target(s) of bromide toxicity in mosquitoes.

Insecticide resistance remains a major challenge for mosquito control, and the kdr variant in An. gambiae is among the most widespread resistance marker in anopheline populations29. In this case, mutations in a voltage-gated sodium channel reduce sensitivity to pyrethroids and DDT. To evaluate whether bromide toxicity is modulated by this adaptation directly, or more likely indirectly, we evaluated sodium bromide toxicity in resistant (AKDR) and susceptible strains of An. gambiae (L1). No significant differences were observed in mortality between the two strains at concentrations ranging from 10 µM to 1.0 mM, and the LC50 value for the AKDR strain (~ 10 µM) was equivalent to that for the other Anopheles strain (Supplemental Fig. S8). These results indicate that bromide maintains potent larvicidal activity independent of the voltage-gated sodium channel and may offer a valuable lead for alternate treatments to counter resistance accumulating in Anopheles mosquitoes.

Chloride protection from bromide toxicity

Little precedence was found in the literature to anticipate the toxicity of bromide in An. gambiae, but one report previously observed such a response to high concentrations of bromide in insecticide-resistant houseflies30. Additionally, supplemental chloride was found to counteract the effect of bromide. To test whether this protection extended to mosquitoes, An. gambiae larvae were treated with combinations of sodium bromide and sodium chloride. Both L1 and L4 larvae were exposed to the standard range of sodium bromide (0–1 mM) in the presence and absence of a fixed concentration of sodium chloride (1 mM) (Fig. 5A, B). For L1 larvae, survival was monitored until the L4 stage. During this period, the effect of sodium bromide alone was less severe than when monitored until adult emergence (Fig. 1). However, toxicity was still apparent and no larvae survived until L4 in the presence of 1 mM sodium bromide. Mortality was significantly mitigated when 1 mM sodium chloride was co-administered. Under these conditions, 80% of the larvae reached the L4 stage. Similar protection was noted when adult emergence was monitored after treating L4 larvae with the same combinations of sodium bromide and sodium chloride. In this case, no adults successfully emerged in the presence of 50 µM sodium bromide alone but emergence increased to 80% in the presence of 1 mM sodium chloride. Still, the protective effect of chloride was diminished for adult emergence since this same concentration of sodium chloride (1 mM) had little effect in overriding the toxicity of 1 mM sodium bromide.

As a complement, L1 larvae were treated with a constant concentration of sodium bromide (10 µM) near its LC50 value in the presence of increasing concentrations of sodium chloride (Fig. 5C). The protection afforded by chloride increased with concentration, but a 100-fold excess of chloride relative to bromide was necessary to enhance adult emergence from 40% to 80%. When an equivalent analysis was performed using 50 µM sodium bromide, less protection was observed, and no adults emerged unless equal molar bromide and chloride were present (Fig. 5D). The ability of chloride to act as an antagonist to bromide toxicity suggests that bromide competes with one or more chloride-dependent processes such as ion transport, osmoregulation, or neuronal signaling. The ultimate target of bromide must have high affinity for this halide since a large excess of chloride is necessary for protection of An. gambiae larvae.

The relationship between bromide and two existing larvicides, diflubenzuron and spinosad

The larvicidal activity of bromide was shown above to be unaffected by insecticide resistance caused by mutations in the voltage-gated sodium channel that are targeted by pyrethroids and DDT. To address possible connections with other widely applied methods of abatement, the mutual influence of bromide and two common larvicides, diflubenzuron and spinosad, was examined. These larvicides are well-studied and act by inhibiting chitin synthesis and disrupting nicotinic acetylcholine and GABAergic signaling, respectively31,32. A synergistic effect with bromide could indicate physiological convergence or complementary stress on shared pathways, whereas an additive effect would suggest that bromide acts independently of the chitin synthesis and neurological signaling pathways mentioned above. The Bliss model for synergy was used to distinguish any connection between the larvicides as revealed by measuring the combined pairwise toxicity in comparison to the predictive magnitude of an additive effect22. For combinations of bromide and diflubenzuron, the observed larval mortality was consistent with an additive effect across all ratios tested since the synergy scores were not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Supplemental Table 2; Supplemental Figs. S9A and S10A). Similarly, bromide and spinosad combinations displayed an additive response with no statistically significant synergy (Supplemental Table 3; Supplemental Figs. S9B and S10B). These results suggest that bromide toxicity is independent of common insecticidal targets and offers an alternative lead for larval control.

Gene expression in response to bromide exposure

Changes in gene expression may be a harbinger of the cellular processes that are directly or indirectly involved in bromide toxicity. A preliminary sample of genes was selected based on chloride-associated transport systems, ion regulation and representative detoxification genes previously implicated in insecticide response or osmotic stress in mosquitoes. Since this analysis was targeted, other genes may also respond to bromide exposure and, in the future, may be detected by a genome-wide investigation. An. gambiae larvae (L1) were reared in the presence and absence of sodium bromide (50 µM) and harvested at L4 to determine the relative levels of mRNA for the chosen genes. Expression of Rdl encoding a GABA-gated chloride channel that is involved in resistance to the neurotoxic insecticide dieldrin33 did not respond to the presence of bromide (Fig. 6). Similarly, expression of the gene for bestrophin, a calcium-activated chloride channel (best), remained constant34,35. In contrast, GluCl encoding a glutamate-gated chloride channel36 was expressed at a significantly greater level in the presence of bromide. Expression of a putative chloride channel based on homology to chloride channel protein 2 (CCP-2, LOC1273077) was similarly enhanced in the presence of bromide. Such a response was not universal since the gene for another chloride channel protein (KCC) was not affected by the presence of bromide. This result was surprising since its homolog in Ae. aegypti acts as a potassium-chloride cotransporter for excretion in Malpighian tubules37. This was initially considered an obvious target that might be sensitive to excess bromide. Cytochromes P450 were not expected to act on bromide directly but two of these were still monitored for expression. Induction of these is indicative of xenobiotic stress and, in particular, both Cyp6m2 and Cyp6p3 are most consistently upregulated in strains resistant to pyrethroids and dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane38. These cytochromes are clearly not regulated by the same stimuli since Cyp6m2 expression increased dramatically in the presence of bromide whereas the expression of Cyp6p3 remained unresponsive. The overexpression of Cyp6m2 is similar to the 7- to 12-fold increase previously observed with pyrethroid resistance in An. gambiae39 and more than a 2-fold increase of GluCl associated with emamectin benzoate resistance in the rice stem borer, Chilo suppressalis Walker40.

Discussion

Bromide is a potent larvicide for An. gambiae

The toxicity of bromide exhibited by An. gambiae was not expected since exposure to this halide at low concentrations is rarely detrimental to most organisms41. Prior studies on another species of mosquito (Culex pipiens) reported no mortality when exposed to 1 mM bromide42. A more distant member of the Diptera order, Drosophila melanogaster, exhibited a similar tolerance to 1 mM bromide43. Moreover, bromide is considered a micronutrient for its role in a peroxidasin-dependent cross-linking of collagen IV17. The high sensitivity of An. gambiae to bromide is consequently unusual and quite dramatic. The presence of as little as 10 µM sodium or potassium bromide is sufficient to prevent approximately 80% of L1 larvae from developing to adults (Fig. 1). An equivalent sensitivity was detected for An. stephensi but larvae of two other important vectors, Ae. aegypti and Cx. quinquefasciatus, demonstrated at least a 10-fold greater tolerance to sodium bromide (Fig. 4). The toxicity of bromide is also considerably higher for An. gambiae larvae than adults by 1000-fold (Fig. 3). Both eggs and pupae exhibited no sensitivity to this halide which is consistent with their barriers to the surrounding environment. However, exposure to bromide as larvae also impairs adult emergence (Fig. 2B). Such a lingering effect is relatively common and adult traits are often dictated by the prior conditions of larval growth27.

The basis for bromide toxicity

The mechanism of bromide toxicity and its site of action have not yet been identified by this initial report but are likely related directly or indirectly to at least one native process involving chloride. This hypothesis is based on the ability of excess chloride to protect An. gambiae larvae from the effects of bromide (Fig. 5). Competition may be based on halide uptake or regulation of osmolarity. The cause of larval death has not been defined but the effects of bromide treatment are visually striking. Larvae become discolored and their thoraxes swell enormously (Supplemental Fig. S5). Similar effects are not evident after exposure to the other halides and chloride has the ability to suppress this response to bromide. The inability of An. gambiae to shield itself from bromide may derive from two factors. First, the size of hydrated bromide (3.28 Å) is very similar to that of chloride (3.12 Å) and for reference much different from those of fluoride (2.67 Å) and iodide (3.52 Å)44. Second, bromide concentrations are sufficiently low in freshwater environments that a protective mechanism would not be necessary41. The maximum concentration of bromide in ground water is less than 5 µM and it ranges to a low of less than 0.02 µM whereas seawater may contain more than 800 µM of bromide41.

Bromide toxicity does not share a common mechanism with other insecticides broadly applied in the environment. Not surprisingly, the potency of bromide is also not affected by a mutation in a voltage-gated sodium channel that enables resistance to pyrethroids29. Neither synergy nor suppression of bromide’s effect was observed in the presence of diflubenzuron or spinosad31,32. Similarly, bromide did not induce overexpression of the gene Rdl that is targeted by dieldrin (Fig. 6)33. The Malpighian tubules are primarily responsible for regulating osmolytes in mosquitoes and offer an attractive target for new insecticides4,5. For adult females, their function is particularly critical after a blood meal but adults were much less sensitive to bromide than were their larvae (Fig. 3). Thus, these also seemed unlikely as a target for bromide. Additionally, the gene for a chloride channel of the Malpighian tubules (KCC) did not respond to the presence of bromide (Fig. 6). The expression of a calcium-activated channel for chloride (Best) similarly remained constant under these conditions.

In contrast, expression of two chloride ion channels (GluCl and CCC-2) responded to bromide exposure. Although the precise relationship between bromide and these transport systems remains unresolved, their upregulation is consistent with a compensatory physiological response to a disrupted chloride homeostasis and an osmotic imbalance. Such responses may reflect an attempt by larvae to restore ion transport and membrane potential following bromide accumulation. The accompanying thoracic swelling further supports disruption of osmotic regulation as a component of bromide toxicity. Further investigations will be necessary to establish the exact mechanism by which bromide is toxic.

Future directions

While we do not advocate widespread distribution of bromide into the breeding grounds of Anopheles mosquitoes, its toxicity may be used to identify a new target for larvicide development. Bromide itself may not be practical in the field but is attractive due to its simplicity, stability and low cost. Its concentration in seawater already exceeds the LC50 values for An. gambiae and An. stephensi and low concentrations in freshwater are not toxic to humans nor the typical organisms such as hydra, snails, freshwater shrimp, daphnia, fish and frogs used to evaluate environmental hazards41. Nevertheless, the current laboratory observations should be treated as a call to attention that a simple substance may ultimately reveal a new target for controlling anopholine populations.
