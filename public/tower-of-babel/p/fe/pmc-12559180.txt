Achieving improved mechanical performance in aluminum matrix composites with rebar-reinforced concrete-inspired structures
Authors: Chenwei Shao, Huicong Chen, Yu Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe strength of aluminum matrix composites (AMCs) often suffers from degradation at elevated temperatures, rendering them generally unsuitable for use in high-temperature environments. Inspired by ubiquitous rebar steel-reinforced concrete (RC) structures, we develop a series of RC-like AMCs by integrating the additive manufacturing and micro-casting methods. Such RC-AMC, with a high volume fraction of thermotolerant particulate reinforcements, effectively mitigate the strength degradation up to 500 °C. Through structural optimization, we demonstrate that such RC-AMCs achieve extraordinarily high compressive yield strength (up to 938 MPa) and specific strength (up to 235 kN·m/kg) at 400 °C — among the highest reported values for all the aluminum-based alloys and composites. The enhanced resistance to high-temperature softening in RC-AMCs is associated with abnormal thermal twinning in Al3Ti when the temperature increases. This design strategy, combining anomalous temperature-dependent deformation behavior and multiscale reinforcing architectures, offers a pathway toward structure-material integrated manufacturing for a wide range of engineering alloys and composites.Subject terms: Composites, Mechanical properties

Introduction

Many lightweight engineering materials produced through traditional methods have reached the limits of their performance, particularly in high-temperature environments1–9. High-strength aluminum (Al) alloys and aluminum matrix composites (AMCs) have been developed to replace conventional cast aluminum alloys for applications at elevated temperatures up to 150 °C10. Although the aluminum alloys and composites made by powder metallurgy approaches are designed to replace titanium alloys for applications in the temperature range of 150–300 °C11,12, they suffer from losses in strength above 300 °C12. Most commercial aluminum alloys or AMCs are unsuitable for high-temperature applications, due to dynamic recovery and recrystallization in the aluminum matrix under loading in the temperature range of 200–450 °C13,14. Wrought and aged aluminum alloys, for example, 7xxx and 2xxx series, exhibit yield strengths of ~600 MPa at room temperature, which decrease to below 120 MPa at 340 °C15,16. Similarly, for carbon nanotube- and graphene-reinforced Al2024-based AMCs, the compressive yield stress drops from ~550 MPa at room temperature to ~110 MPa at 350 °C17. Despite trialuminides (e.g., Al3Ti) improving the thermal stability of casting Al-Si matrixes, their ultimate tension strengths generally remain below 106 MPa at 350 °C18,19. To date, achieving an AMC with a yield strength over 200 MPa above 350 °C, with a high specific strength comparable to that of titanium (Ti) alloys, has been highly desirable.

Steel-reinforced concrete (RC)—composed of cement, aggregates (e.g., sand and gravel), and steel reinforcement—is ubiquitously used in architectural structures for applications such as multi-story buildings, tunnels, and bridges20–22. Such a broad application of RCs stems from its synergistic mechanical performance: the aggregates reinforced cement delivers high compressive strength, while the steel reinforcement offers tensile strength, yielding composite materials that are robust under both compression and tension23,24. Nevertheless, this RC concept has rarely been applied to engineering alloys and composites, primarily due to the limitations of traditional manufacturing processes in constructing trans-dimensional reinforcements—particularly in the context of lightweight components intended for use in high-temperature environments.

Inspired by this RC concept, we develop reinforced concrete aluminum matrix composites (RC-AMCs) by integrating structure design, additive manufacturing, micro-casting, and heat treatment. The RC-AMCs designed in this study integrate the key advantages of both particulate-reinforced and laminated AMCs—the pronounced strengthening effect characteristic of particulate composites, and the capacity to accommodate a high-volume fraction of reinforcements without introducing high porosity, a typical benefit of laminated composite architectures. We demonstrate that such RC-AMCs exhibit outstanding compressive strength at room and elevated temperatures.

Results

Microstructure and mechanical properties of RC-AMCs

The RC-AMCs were designed and fabricated using additive manufacturing (commonly known as 3D printing) and micro-casting processes, incorporating sub-millimeter-sized titanium alloy (Ti6Al4V) struts, micrometer-sized Al3Ti particles, and nano-sized precipitates (Fig. 1a, b). Figure 1c shows the macroscopic morphology and elemental distribution within the structure, confirming the presence of the titanium alloy mesh in the RC-AMCs. The Al3Ti particles in the size range of 8.4 ± 2.7 µm are discretely distributed in the connected regions between the titanium alloy struts (Fig. 1d). The nanoscale precipitates, including discoid AlSi3Ti6 and spherical silicides, are distributed throughout both the aluminum alloy matrix and the Al3Ti particles (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1). Using optimized processing parameters, the current micro-casting method enables control of porosity to below 0.5 vol% in various Al–Ti composite systems, and the interface defects are effectively minimized (see Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3).

Figure 2a presents typical compressive stress-strain curves of the RC-AMCs at room temperature, showing that the flow stress of the aluminum alloy matrix is significantly increased by introducing titanium alloy struts and Al3Ti particles. Specifically, the average yield strength (σ0.2) is increased from 103 MPa for the unreinforced matrix to over 537 MPa for the RC-AMCs, including 710 MPa of the sample along the basic vector (Direction A), 592 MPa along the face diagonal (Direction B), and 537 MPa along the body diagonal (Direction C) of the Ti6Al4V cubic cellular unit. Figure 2b compares the yield strength of various casting AMCs strengthened with different types of discontinuous reinforcements. These RC-AMCs, with their multi-scale reinforcements, exhibit superior matrix strengthening compared to conventional AMCs using single-scale reinforcements. The compressive stress-strain curves shown in Fig. 2e reveal a sharp decrease in the flow stress of the unreinforced AlSi7Mg alloy as the testing temperature increases from 25°C to 500 °C, with yield strength dropping from 103 MPa to 5 MPa. In contrast, the RC-AMC maintains a high level of flow stress even at elevated temperatures up to 500 °C (Fig. 2c). Specifically, the yield strength of the RC-AMC reaches 396 MPa at 400°C and 335 MPa at 500 °C, surpassing the aluminum alloys and AMCs reported in the literature, to our best knowledge (Fig. 2d). For comparison, the stress-strain curves at elevated temperatures for bulk Ti6Al4V, prepared using the same process parameters as the Ti6Al4V scaffold are shown in Fig. 2f. Combining the mechanical data in Fig. 2e and f, the yield strength of the AMCs, with the same percentage composition of AlSi7Mg and Ti6Al4V, is calculated by the rule of mixtures (ROM). The measured yield strength of the RC-AMCs exceeds the values predicted by the ROM (Fig. 2g), suggesting additional mechanisms strenghen the RC-AMCs. This discrepancy is attributed to the multiple reinforcement phases in the RC-AMCs, including nanosized Si particles, micron-sized Al3Ti particles, and Ti6Al4V/Al3Ti frame structures (see the detailed analysis of these multiscale strengthening mechanisms in Supplementary Note 1).

Trans-scale strengthening behavior

The trans-scale coordinated strengthening mechanisms are involved in the compressive deformation. Three typical failure modes are observed in RC-AMCs under loading along different directions (Supplementary Fig. 4). These modes include a splitting failure along Direction A (Fig. 3a), shear failure along Direction B (Fig. 3b), and a mixed splitting-shear mode along Direction C (Fig. 3c). Cracks initiate at the nodes of the Ti6Al4V struts due to microscopic shearing (Fig. 3d) and subsequently propagate through the aluminum matrix. On the micrometer-scale, various types of damage are observed in the RC-AMCs, including particle cracking, matrix-particle debonding, and matrix ductile failure (Fig. 3e). Wavy dislocation slip is the predominant plastic deformation mode in the aluminum matrix between Al3Ti particles (Supplementary Fig. 5), providing a source of strain hardening and simultaneously improving the deformation compatibility of the surrounding Al3Ti particles. As shown in Fig. 3f, a dislocation is observed bypassing the nanoprecipitate in the aluminum matrix, contributing to precipitate strengthening. Deformation twins are also observed in the brittle Al3Ti particles (Fig. 3h and i). Additionally, planar dislocation slip is activated across multiple slip systems in the Ti6Al4V scaffold, despite a relatively small global strain (Fig. 3g), suggesting a complex stress state within the scaffold.

At higher deformation temperatures ranging from 200°C to 400°C, fewer cracks are observed in the Al-Al3Ti composites, particularly near the Ti6Al4V scaffold (Fig. 4a-c). These cracks originate from the pronounced stress and strain gradients at the Ti6Al4V/Al3Ti interfaces, which are induced by the macroscale lattice structure of the Ti6Al4V scaffold (Fig. 5a). At the microscale, the forward stress within the Al3Ti phase—identified as the hard zone in the Ti6Al4V/Al3Ti interfacial system (see Supplementary Figs. 6a and b)—is primarily driven by the emission and accumulation of geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs). This forward stress not only contributes to the interfacial strengthening but also plays a critical role in crack initiation and propagation25,26. Nanoprecipitates in AlSi7Mg remained stable at 400°C (Fig. 4d). Dislocation slips in both the aluminum matrix and Ti6Al4V scaffold are enhanced (Fig. 4d and e), resulting in a higher strain-hardening capacity for the RC-AMC but a lower yield strength compared to those at room temperature. Twinning becomes more prevalent in the Al3Ti particles as the deformation temperature increases from 25°C to 400°C (compare Fig. 3h and Fig. 4f). This phenomenon contrasts with a behavior commonly observed in most metals, where twinning is generally prone to occur at lower temperatures27–29.

Deformation and abnormal thermal twinning

To further elucidate the deformation mechanisms and their impact on the mechanical properties of the RC-AMCs, we conducted finite element modeling (FEM) and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Our macroscale FEM reveals that the failure mode of the RC-AMCs is influenced by the shear stress field in the Ti6Al4V scaffold (Fig. 5a). When loading is applied along Direction A, the maximum shear stress is concentrated at the corners of the Ti6Al4V cubic cellular structure. The shear stresses of the same sign are distributed along the diagonals of the scaffold nodes, resulting in localized shear cracking at the Ti6Al4V nodes with a cracking angle of approximately 45° relative to the compressive direction (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Under loading along Direction B, the plastic strain becomes concentrated at the horizontal corners of the Ti6Al4V scaffold (Supplementary Figs. 7b and c). Compared to the deformation behavior of a standalone Ti6Al4V lattice under compression, where plastic strain predominantly concentrates at the node intersections, the incorporation of the AlSi7Mg matrix redistributes the strain and stress concentration sites from the nodes to the midsections of the lattice beams (Supplementary Figs. 7d and e). This redistribution promotes the formation of a macroscopic 45° shear band, as highlighted by the black dashed line in the center of Fig. 5a, leading to a catastrophic fracture of the RC-AMC sample (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Similarly, when loading is applied along Direction C, the maximum shear stress component in the YZ plane also aligns along a defined path; however, its magnitude is lower than that observed under Direction B loading. This reduction in sudden failure risk is attributed to obstruction of the macroscopic shear propagation path by intersecting Ti6Al4V struts oriented in other directions (Supplementary Fig. 4c).

Figure 5b illustrates the Al-51.3 vol% Al3Ti composite under quasi-static uniaxial compression, indicating that plastic deformation primarily develops in the particles near the interfaces adjacent to neighboring particles and suggesting that the contact and interaction between closely spaced particles contribute to plastic deformation within the particles. The microscale strain concentration is crucial for the twinning in Al3Ti. The corresponding experimental results of twinning are shown in Fig. 3h, i. The numerically predicted stress-strain curves and quantified failure mechanism indicate that particle cracking is first activated at a strain of approximately 0.5%, and then rapidly grows to the peak stress at a strain of around 3%, by which point approximately 60% of the Al3Ti particles are fractured (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). In the Al3Ti particles, the deformation damage initiates at the corners, which are more closely surrounded by other Al3Ti particles due to stress concentrations, and then primarily propagates parallel to the compressive loading direction (Supplementary Fig. 10). The EBSD analysis indicates that lattice distortion in Al3Ti is minimal before plastic deformation (Supplementary Fig. 11). The GNDs accumulated at the boundaries of Al3Ti particles during deformation contribute to the toughening of the material (Supplementary Fig. 12). Additionally, twinning within Al3Ti can further elevate the GND density within grains by promoting increased dislocation pile-ups at or near twin and grain boundaries30. However, the influence of GNDs in the vicinity of the Ti6Al4V/Al3Ti parallel interfaces on the overall mechanical properties is limited, due to the relatively large thickness (~100 μm) of the Al3Ti layer and the confined size of the interface-affected zone, which typically extends only a few micrometers31.

The MD simulation result reveals that dislocation slip dominates the plastic deformation in both Al and Ti, while both twinning and dislocation slip prevail in Al3Ti (Fig. 5c). The MD simulation result also reveals that elevated temperatures promote dislocation slip in both Al (characterized by wavy slip) and Ti (primarily planar slip) (Supplementary Fig. 13). Specifically, dislocations in the Al layer are predominantly of the 1/621¯1¯ and 1/211¯0 types, while deformation in the Ti layer is accommodated by 1/312¯10 and 1/311¯00 dislocations. In the polycrystalline Al3Ti layer, 1/621¯1¯ dislocations are dominant, and their motion may lead to twinning, as elaborated in the following paragraphs. The competition between slip and twinning in Al3Ti is fundamentally governed by the behavior of 1/621¯1¯ dislocations under different dislocation environments. With increasing deformation temperature, dislocation slip is suppressed while twinning is enhanced, a trend that aligns with the experimental observations from TEM (Figs. 3h and 4f) and MD simulations (Supplementary Fig. 14). The abnormal thermal twinning may contribute to an increase in yield strength at elevated temperatures, ranging from 25°C to 314°C32. Our quantitative analysis indicates that a decrease in dislocation density from 0.8 × 1016 m–2 to 0.7 × 1016 m–2 (Fig. 4g); the twin fraction increases from 3.8% to 5.0% in polycrystalline Al3Ti layer at a strain of approximately 10% when the deformation temperature increases from 25°C to 400°C (Fig. 4h). These simulation results are consistent with our experimental observations: When temperature increases from 25°C to 400°C, the GND density decreases, while the twinning fraction correspondingly increases (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 15).

Figure 6a, b present the observations of mechanical twin formation in Al3Ti particles following indentation tests. Statistical analysis using EBSD reveals that grains exhibiting deformation twinning typically show crystallographic orientations deviating from the (001) direction. An increase in testing temperature slightly reduces this deviation angle in the inverse pole figures (Fig. 6c, d), suggesting that higher temperatures promote twinning in Al3Ti during deformation. The broader orientation distribution observed in the grains without twins indicates the enhanced compatibility of deformation between the Al3Ti particles and the AlSi7Mg matrix with increasing temperature. Figure 6e–g demonstrate a pole-induced twinning mechanism in Al3Ti, involving the concurrent formation of mobile jogs and the expansion of dislocation loops. These expanding slip loops eventually interconnect, forming a continuous spiral that culminates in the nucleation of a twin embryo (Fig. 6f and h), resembling the pole mechanism commonly observed in face-centered cubic (fcc) metals33. Figure 3i shows the experimental evidence of twin growth driven by the motion of Shockley partial dislocations (SPDs) (see detailed twinning evolution and the formation of twinning partials in Supplementary Fig. 16). At the elevated temperature (675 K), the nucleation of twin embryos becomes more prevalent than the pole-induced layer-by-layer twin growth. Both twinning nucleation and growth are enhanced with increasing temperature, contributing to a notable suppression of strength degradation (Supplementary Fig. 17).

Different from typical fcc metals (i.e., those with L12 symmetry), Al3Ti in the RC-AMCs adopts a D022 crystal structure and exhibits a significantly stronger propensity for deformation twinning over dislocation slip. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 18, twinning initiates at lower strains and rapidly propagates with continued plastic deformation. This phenomenon could be rooted in the distinct tetragonal unit cell of D022 Al3Ti, with a relatively large c/a ratio of 2.234, which increases the interplanar spacing between close-packed planes and effectively lowers the critical resolved shear stress required for twinning (Fig. 6h). As a result, twinning along the 111112¯ system becomes the dominant deformation mechanism, enabling plasticity while preserving the lattice symmetry of the D022 phase.

Compared to most traditional particulate-reinforced composites, RC-AMCs with continuous skeleton reinforcement exhibit exceptionally high strength, as the Ti6Al4V skeleton directly bears the applied force, which is particularly crucial at high temperatures. The intermetallic Al3Ti provides high specific strength but limited ductility due to its D022 crystalline structure, restricting its wide engineering applications. In the RC-AMCs, the Al3Ti particles, synthesized through an in-situ reaction, strengthen the composite34,35 and, more importantly, enhance plastic deformation capacity at elevated temperatures due to the prevalent twinning in regions with sharp strain gradients. A similar beneficial effect of nanotwins on plasticity was also observed in polysynthetic twinned TiAl single crystals for high-temperature applications36. Additionally, the ductile AlSi7Mg and Ti6Al4V phases surrounding the Al3Ti particles play critical roles in suppressing cracking in the composite, further improving the mechanical performance.

Evading the trade-off of high strength-light weight

The strength of RC-AMC is higher than the combined strength of the Ti6Al4V scaffold and aluminum matrix (Fig. 2a and e, Supplementary Fig. 19a). Moreover, the specific strength, i.e., strength-to-density ratio, of RC-AMC is also higher than that of the Ti6Al4V scaffold, the aluminum matrix, and other AMCs reported in the literature (Supplementary Fig. 19b). To establish the stress–strain relationship and predict the strength of RC-AMCs under axial deformation, the Unit Cell Projection (UCP) model and the Unit Layer Compounding (ULC) model are proposed, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 20). The stress-strain relationship predicted by the UCP model aligns well with the experimental results (Fig. 7a). The strengths predicted by the ULC model are slightly lower than the experimental values, except for the yield strength prediction for Direction B (Fig. 7b). The lower measured yield strength in the Direction B sample could be due to the stress concentration caused by macroscopic shearing failure. The optimizations of scaffold structures and materials in RC-AMCs vary depending on stress states and service temperatures. Figure 7c illustrates the potential strategies for optimizing the scaffold structures. Based on these design routes, representative samples are fabricated (Fig. 7d), and Fig. 7e shows their compressive stress-strain curves under deformation at 400°C. The extended RC-AMC exhibits a density ranging from 2.8 g/cm³ to 4.0 g/cm³, which is higher than that of aluminum alloys (~2.7 g/cm³) but lower than that of titanium alloys (~4.5 g/cm³) and steels (~7.8 g/cm³). To the best of our knowledge, the present RC-AMC exhibits the highest specific yield strength at high temperatures (300–500 °C) among a wide range of engineering alloys and composites reported in the literature to date (Fig. 7f).

The RC-AMCs demonstrate superior compressive performance at elevated temperatures. Despite limited tensile ductility at room temperature—similar to conventional RC structures—RC-AMC retain high tensile yield strength (~629 MPa at RT and ~371 MPa at 400°C) due to the heat-resistant Al3Ti phase and Ti6Al4V framework (Supplementary Fig. 21). The fracture toughness of Al3Ti slightly increases with temperature and is further enhanced by the presence of ductile Ti6Al4V and Al3Ti/AlSi7Mg interfaces, supporting the integrity of composites under service conditions (Supplementary Fig. 22). While this study primarily investigates the influence of RC structural design on mechanical performance, other key factors—such as interface bonding strength37,38, reinforcement distribution uniformity39, and dislocation pinning effects40—also play roles in determining the properties of AMCs. A systematic exploration of these aspects within the RC-AMC framework may offer additional pathways to further enhance their mechanical performance and structural reliability.

Discussion

By introducing the concept of rebar steel-reinforced concrete structures to aluminum matrix composites, we demonstrate that such aluminum composites exhibit high specific yield strengths at both room temperature and elevated temperatures up to 400°C, overcoming the strength-density trade-off in lightweight engineering materials over a large temperature range. Compared to previously reported interpenetrating phase composites, our RC-AMCs—featuring a high-volume fraction of thermotolerant particulate reinforcements and controllable fine lattice structural units—exhibit significant strengthening effects and effectively mitigate the strength degradation at elevated temperatures (Supplementary Table 1). Extensive knowledge of structural design from various fields, including civil, aerospace, and automotive sectors, can be applied to further RC-AMC design. Our results suggest that the mechanical properties of RC-AMCs can be tuned by introducing scaffolds with various shapes, structures, volume fractions, and materials. The design strategy based on the RC-AMC concept enables the preparation of an AMC in a manner akin to building a house, by quantitatively reinforcing the matrix at precise locations and in specified directions. Our fabrication method integrates additive manufacturing and micro-casting technologies, enabling the creation of complex geometries and intricate structures that are difficult, or even impossible, to achieve using traditional manufacturing techniques. This design methodology facilitates the customized development of structural materials tailored to specific engineering requirements.

Methods

Material preparation

In this study, the AlSi7Mg alloy and Ti6Al4V alloy were selected as the matrix and reinforcement materials, respectively. A 3D CAD model of the Ti6Al4V mesh with a cubic structure was created using Unigraphics NX software. The unit size of the designed cubic cellular structure was 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 mm3, with the strut diameter of 0.1 mm. The Ti6Al4V mesh was produced using the selective laser melting (SLM) method. The laser power is less than 200 W, and the scanning speed is in the range of 1200-1800 mm/s. The layer information was extracted from the stereolithography (STL) file, where the geometry is approximated by a collection of triangular facets. The Ti6Al4V mesh was placed into a square Al2O3 mould, and the mesh beam surface was wetted with AlSi7Mg droplets at 800°C for 15 minutes in vacuum, before molten AlSi7Mg was poured into the mould. This pre-wetting step is to ensure the flowability of liquid AlSi7Mg within the Ti6Al4V mesh during the micro-casting process. The Ti6Al4V mesh embedded in the molten AlSi7Mg alloy was maintained at 800°C for 2 h in vacuum, and then cooled down to room temperature in an argon (Ar) atmosphere. A schematic representation of the microcasting process is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 23. Unlike conventional casting, micro-casting enables the casting of components in small, submillimeter-scale spaces and the fabrication of complex structures. Following micro-casting, the samples underwent an aging treatment consisting of annealing at 545 °C for 12 h, water quenching, subsequent annealing at 170 °C for 8 hours, and final cooling to room temperature in air.

Mechanical and structural characterization

The specimens in the dimensions of ~3.5 × 4 × 5 mm3 were used for the compressive tests, which were conducted parallel to the 5 mm direction. Uniaxial compression tests were conducted at a constant strain rate of 1 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ using a mechanical testing machine equipped with a digital image correlation (DIC) video-extensometer for room-temperature experiments and with an electric furnace for elevated-temperature tests (200–500 °C). Each test was repeated at least three times. Before performing the DIC imaging, small black paints were sprayed uniformly on the whitened surface of a sample, creating a contrasting speckle pattern for analysis. Micro-hardness tests were performed using a LECO LM310AT micro-indentation tester equipped with a heating stage. Nanoindentation tests were performed using a nanoindenter (KLA). The diameters of the Ti6Al4V scaffold struts and macro-damage defects in RC-AMCs after compression were measured using a X-ray computed tomography (CT) system, with an accelerating voltage of 600 kV and a maximum spatial resolution of 23 lp/mm. CT image reconstruction was performed using the VGStudio software. Microstructural analysis, including the morphology and damaged structure of Ti6Al4V struts and Al3Ti particles, was carried out using a field-emission SEM equipped with energy-dispersion spectroscopy (EDS) and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) detectors. Imaging of the nanoscale features was performed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM), operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV.

Prediction of mechanical performance

A unit cell projection (UCP) model was used to predict the stress-strain response of the RC structures. In the UCP model, the mechanical behavior of the entire periodic structure is derived topologically from the mechanical response of the structural unit. The featured structural unit cell of the RC structures was deliberately chosen to simplify calculations (Supplementary Fig. 24), according to the loading conditions and scaffold configuration. Additionally, a unit layer compounding (ULC) model, based on classical laminate theory, was used to describe the failure process and predict the failure strength of the composites. The ULC model accounts for the fact that any uniaxial loading condition can be realized from a basic loading condition after two sequential rotations along orthogonal axes (Supplementary Fig. 25). Detailed descriptions of the UCP and ULC models are provided in Supplementary Materials.

Multi-scale simulations

Macroscale finite element method (FEM) simulations were performed using ANSYS 2025R1 to analyze the stress/strain distribution and macroscopic deformation behavior in RC-AMCs, with a focus on the influence of the Ti6Al4V scaffold (Supplementary Fig. 26). Microscale FEM modelling was conducted to gain insights into the interactions and evolution of failure mechanisms within the matrix (i.e., the particulate Al-Al3Ti composite, as shown in Fig. 5b). Microstructure-informed representative volume elements (RVEs) were generated for meshing and applying quasi-static uniaxial compression. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were also performed using the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS, version 29 Aug 2024) to investigate dislocation and twinning behavior in Al, Ti, and Al3Ti (Supplementary Fig. 27). The resulting microstructures in MD simulations were analyzed using an Open Visualization Tool (OVITO, version 3.14.0). A detailed description of the multi-scale simulations is provided in Supplementary Materials.

Statistics & Reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analysis.
