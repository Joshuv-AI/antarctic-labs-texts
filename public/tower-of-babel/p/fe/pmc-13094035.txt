Tracheobronchial foreign body aspiration in Palestinian children in the West Bank: a 10-year retrospective analysis
Authors: Ahmad Zohud, Heba Khuffash, Qamar Hamarsheh, Masara Samara, Reem Sawafta, Nisreen Rumman, Iyad Maqboul, Wafa ’ Awad, Mousa Akram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundForeign body aspiration (FBA) is a frequent pediatric emergency with potentially serious complications. This study examines the clinical features, diagnostic challenges, and outcomes of FBA in children in the West Bank, Palestine, over 10 years, to fill a regional data gap and support improved prevention and management strategies.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional analysis was conducted on electronic health records of 292 pediatric patients (0–18 years) from multiple tertiary hospitals in the West Bank, Palestine (Jan 2014– Jan 2024), using descriptive and inferential statistics.ResultsMost cases (75%) occurred in children under 3 years of age, with males accounting for (55.8%). The most common presenting symptoms were sudden coughing (91.1%) and choking (63%). Organic foreign bodies, particularly seeds (41.1%) and nuts (10.3%), were predominant, with type significantly associated with both gender (p < 0.001) and age group (p < 0.001). The right main bronchus was the most frequent site of impaction (30.5%). Complications, including pneumonia (13.7%) and radiologic abnormalities, including atelectasis (17.1%), were linked to delayed presentation (p < 0.05). Rigid bronchoscopy successfully removed foreign bodies in 92.1% of cases.ConclusionFBA remains a notable pediatric health problem among Palestinian children. This study provides a descriptive overview of patterns of presentation, diagnosis, and management of FBA over a 10-year period and highlights gaps in hospital data recording. These findings contribute local evidence that may inform future preventive and clinical strategies.LimitationsThe retrospective design relies on the accuracy of electronic health records, and exclusion of cases with substantial missing data may have introduced selection bias.

Background

Foreign Body Aspiration (FBA) is a significant problem among children and is the fourth most frequent cause of death in early childhood [1]. Mortality is primarily attributed to hypoxic-ischemic brain injury and pulmonary hemorrhage [2]. Beyond acute complications, FBA can lead to chronic pulmonary conditions, including recurrent lower respiratory infections and bronchiectasis [3]. The condition is most common in children under 3 years of age [4], a vulnerability attributed to incomplete dentition, natural curiosity to explore objects with the mouth, fidgeting during meals, and a weakened protective laryngeal reflex [5]. The clinical presentation varies depending on the characteristics and location of the foreign object within the tracheobronchial tree, as well as the chronicity of the aspiration and imaging findings. These factors play a crucial role in guiding the selection of the appropriate techniques and methods for removing foreign bodies from the respiratory tract [2]. Rigid bronchoscope is considered the preferred method for both diagnosis and treatment [6]. While foreign objects can obstruct either the upper or the lower airway, resulting in a partial or complete blockage of the airway [7], the right main bronchus is still reported as the most frequent location of FBA [8]. The type of aspirated object varies according to age, developmental stage, and environmental exposure. Common items include food (nuts, seeds, popcorn), small toys (marbles, beads, miniature balls), household objects (buttons, coins, batteries), and miscellaneous items such as pen caps and toothpicks. Laryngotracheal aspiration occurs more commonly in infants younger than one year old due to tracheal narrowing [2].

FBA continues to be a serious public health issue in pediatrics, resulting in many emergency room visits and fatalities annually [9]. This highlights the need for more research, better education, and improved healthcare practices. In Palestine, although several centers handle cases of suspected foreign body aspiration, there’s still a noticeable gap in reported data, making it difficult to understand the true prevalence and outcomes of FBA. Regional studies provide some context: for example, a 15-year study from University Hospital in Jordan identified 103 children with FBA, including 27 deaths, with most cases occurring in boys under 3 years old, and peanuts being the most commonly aspirated object [10]. These findings suggest that Palestinian children may experience a similar burden, underlining the importance of investigating the prevalence, clinical characteristics, diagnostic methods, and complications of FBA in Palestine over the past ten years.

Materials and methods

This study is a retrospective, cross-sectional analysis conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines. It was carried out over eight months (March–November 2024), with data collected retrospectively from electronic health records spanning 10 years (January 2014–January 2024). The study was multicenter, involving several tertiary hospitals across different cities of the Palestinian West Bank, including Nablus, Ramallah, Jenin, and Hebron, which enhances the representativeness of the findings, and a standardized data collection sheet was used to ensure consistency across records.

We included Palestinian pediatric patients (0–18 years) who were hospitalized for more than 24 h, underwent bronchoscopy during hospitalization, and had confirmed foreign body aspiration (FBA). Patients were excluded if they were older than 18, if their records contained more than 60% missing information, or if their medical files were inaccessible. The > 60% threshold was chosen to minimize information bias, as highly incomplete records could compromise the validity of analyses.

In total, 292 patients met the inclusion criteria. Background variables included age, gender, and residency, while clinical variables encompassed type of foreign body, time to presentation, site of impaction, presenting symptoms and signs, radiological findings, complications, and method of removal. Data were extracted from patient histories, clinical examination notes, radiology reports, and bronchoscopy findings.

Descriptive and inferential statistical analyses were performed using Python and SPSS. Descriptive statistics were reported as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and means or medians with standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables, depending on distribution. Inferential analyses were conducted using chi-square tests for categorical variables as appropriate.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at An-Najah National University (IRB reference number: Med.Nov 2023/3), adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and complied with applicable Palestinian regulations.

Results

A total of 570 airway foreign body (FB) cases were screened across participating hospitals. Of these, 200 were excluded because the patients were older than 18 years, 58 due to more than 60% missing data, and 20 due to inaccessible medical records. After applying these criteria, 292 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Demographic characteristics

The majority of cases occurred in children aged 1–3 years representing 191 cases (65.4%), while infants under 1 year accounted for 24 cases (8.2%), and 77 patients (26.4%) were older than 3 years. Boys were more frequently affected than girls, with 163 cases (55.8%) versus 129 cases (44.2%), yielding a male-to-female ratio of 1.3:1. Most children, 160 cases (54.8%) lived in urban areas, followed by 126 children lived in rural areas (43.2%) and 6 children in refugee camps (2.1%) (Table 1).

Clinical presentation

Most patients presented within 24 h of aspiration, including 180 cases, (61.6%), while 77 cases (26.4%) presented between 1 and 7 days, 21 cases (7.2%) presented between 7 and 28 days, and 14 cases (4.8%) presented after more than 28 days. The most common symptom was sudden cough presented in 266 cases (91.1%), followed by choking in 184 cases (63.0%), shortness of breath in 98 cases (33.6%), and cyanosis in 69 cases (23.6%). Less frequent symptoms included stridor in 22 cases (7.5%), wheezing in 41 cases (14.1%), and hoarseness in 20 cases (6.8%). Signs of increased respiratory effort, such as subcostal or intercostal retractions in 38 cases (13.0%) and nasal flaring in 22 cases(7.5%), were recorded as separate findings and did not always occur together. On auscultation, decreased air entry was noted in 45 patients (15.4%), while rhonchi or crepitus were documented in 8 patients (2.7%).

Radiological findings

Chest X-rays were normal in 43 patients (14.7%). The most common abnormalities were radiological shadows seen in 90 cases (30.8%), hyperinflation seen in 60 cases (20.5%), atelectasis seen in 50 cases (17.1%), and pneumonia seen in 40 cases(13.7%). Less frequent findings included pneumothorax in 2 cases (0.7%) and the steeple sign only in one case (0.3%). These findings indicate that radiology alone was not sufficient for diagnosis, as a considerable proportion of children had normal imaging despite clinical suspicion.

Rigid bronchoscopy was the predominant removal method used in 269 cases (92.1%; 95% CI: 88.6–94.6%), followed by flexible bronchoscopy used in 17 cases (5.8%). Site of impaction

Regarding the anatomical site of foreign body impaction, the most common location was the right main bronchus, reported in 89 cases (30.5%). This was followed by the trachea in 45 cases (15.4%) and the left main bronchus in 44 cases (15.1%). Foreign bodies were impacted in the upper airway (glottis) in 25 cases (8.6%). Less commonly, impaction at the tracheal bifurcation was reported in 4 cases (1.4%). Impaction site was not reported in 76 cases (26.0%) (Table 6).

Discussion

FBA is a common, serious, and potentially life-threatening condition in children [11]. In the present study, 75% of FBA cases occurred in children under the age of 3 years. This finding aligns with the literature, which highlights the increased susceptibility of this age group, as this age group has increased vulnerability due to several factors. Immature neural mechanisms, underdeveloped chewing abilities, absence of posterior dentition, a natural tendency to explore objects orally, and behaviors such as laughing, crying, or playing during meals increase the risk of aspiration [12]. Boys were found to be more prone to FBA, with a male-to-female ratio in our study of 1.3:1, which is consistent with previous studies [7, 13]. This could be due to greater physical activity and risk-taking behavior in boys compared to girls, although the exact reasons remain uncertain [13]. The diagnosis of foreign body aspiration involves a detailed patient history, clinical examination, imaging studies, and bronchoscopy [14]. Although a history of aspiration is commonly reported, typical symptoms include sudden coughing, wheezing, and diminished breath sounds [15]. However, some patients may be asymptomatic or exhibit symptoms ranging from mild to severe respiratory distress, necessitating careful evaluation [16]. Their clinical signs and symptoms depend on the type, location, and size of the FB, as well as the duration and degree of obstruction [17]. In our study, coughing (91%) and choking (63%) were the most frequent symptoms of FBA. Decreased breath sounds are often associated with bronchial obstruction, while cyanosis may indicate significant hypoxia—a critical emergency sign. If the foreign body remains undiagnosed, symptoms may become more insidious and mimic other conditions, such as a recurrent or persistent cough that does not resolve with standard treatments, rhonchi or crepitation indicating airway irritation or secondary infection, and fever indicating superimposed infection [18–20]. In our study, the interval between aspiration and extraction varied widely, with most patients (61.6%) presenting within the first 24 h. This early presentation may reflect the acute nature of symptoms in many cases, prompting caregivers to seek immediate care. Nevertheless, nearly one quarter of patients presented several days after the aspiration event, which may be attributed to delayed symptom manifestation or difficulties in establishing an accurate diagnosis. Similar diagnostic delays have been documented in previous studies, particularly in cases with subtle or nonspecific clinical presentations [3]. Late presentation, especially beyond seven days, was associated with a higher incidence of complications, most notably pneumonia and atelectasis. This association highlights the adverse effects of prolonged foreign body retention, consistent with findings reported in earlier research [21]. Pneumonia may result from persistent airway obstruction and secondary infection, while atelectasis can contribute to longer-term respiratory morbidity. In severe cases, complete airway obstruction may lead to asphyxia or death, emphasizing the clinical importance of early recognition and intervention. Overall, timely diagnosis and prompt removal remain key factors in reducing morbidity and mortality related to foreign body aspiration [8, 9]. In this study, 14.7% of patients had normal chest X-rays, consistent with previously reported rates of 10–40%, likely reflecting the radiolucent nature of many organic foreign bodies [3, 18]. Common abnormal findings included shadows, hyperinflation, atelectasis, and pneumonia. Despite its importance, chest radiography alone is of limited sensitivity and specificity, particularly for radiolucent objects such as nuts and seeds; therefore, the absence of radiographic abnormalities cannot exclude foreign body aspiration. When clinical suspicion is high, bronchoscopy remains the definitive diagnostic modality.

The nature of aspirated FBs varies by cultural, social, and economic factors, including dietary habits [7]. In our study, 58.9% of FBs were organic, primarily seeds (41.1%) and nuts (10.3%), reflecting local dietary patterns. Inorganic FBs, including plastic (13.7%) and metal (5.1%), were more common in older children. FBs were most frequently lodged in the right bronchial tree (30.5%), which aligns with anatomical differences that the right bronchi are wider and have a more vertical structure [1]. Rigid bronchoscopy was the gold standard for FB removal in 92.1% of cases, while flexible bronchoscopy (5.8%) was used as a supplementary method. In Palestine, rigid bronchoscopy is the main modality with a high success rate for both diagnosis and treatment, which is in line with international practice, as it is also considered the gold standard in other countries such as Turkey and India [6, 8]. However, in contrast to high-income countries where flexible bronchoscopy is increasingly available for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes, Palestine faces unique challenges. Limited infrastructure, shortage of specialized pediatric anesthesiologists, and lack of advanced bronchoscopic equipment restrict the use of flexible modalities. In addition, difficulties in patient transfer between hospitals and lack of parental awareness, especially during emergencies or in geographically isolated areas, further complicate timely access to specialized care . These constraints highlight the urgent need to strengthen pediatric airway services in Palestine through investment in training, infrastructure, and equipment, as well as the development of clear referral pathways to ensure timely and safe management.

Beyond clinical management, prevention is fundamental to reducing the incidence and burden of FBA. Public health initiatives should prioritize educational and awareness campaigns targeting parents and caregivers, emphasizing the risks posed by small food items (such as nuts and seeds) and unsafe toys. Reinforcing safe practices in schools and kindergartens is also critical. At the policy level, the introduction of clear labeling regulations and the enforcement of safety standards for children’s products are essential preventive measures. Moreover, training first responders and healthcare professionals to recognize and manage choking episodes promptly can significantly improve outcomes. These strategies are consistent with previous studies that highlight prevention and education as key interventions for lowering morbidity and mortality associated with FBA [5, 7].

Limitation

While this study provides important insights into FBA among Palestinian children, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective design may have introduced recall bias, particularly for information obtained from patient histories; however, the use of hospital records and objective findings, such as bronchoscopy results, helps ensure the reliability of the core outcomes. Incomplete records that were excluded from the study may affect the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, fatal choking cases were not captured, which may underestimate the incidence of the most severe presentations. Conducting research in a conflict-affected, low-income setting also introduces challenges such as variable data quality and reduced accessibility to complete health records, which could influence the overall reliability and representativeness of the findings. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable data on clinical characteristics, types of aspirated foreign bodies, and management patterns, contributing to improved understanding and future preventive strategies in this population.

Conclusions

In summary, our study highlights that FBA is most frequent among Palestinian children under 3 years of age, with organic seeds being the most commonly inhaled objects. While our findings provide important insights, they should be interpreted with caution, given the retrospective cross-sectional nature of the study design. Future multicenter prospective studies are recommended to better define risk factors, optimize diagnostic pathways, and evaluate long-term outcomes.

From a policy perspective, targeted parental education and public awareness campaigns are essential to reduce preventable cases. In addition, strengthening primary care and emergency services through training healthcare providers to promptly recognize and manage suspected FBA could improve early detection and outcomes. Expanding pediatric airway services and ensuring access to appropriate bronchoscopic equipment and expertise should also be prioritized. Together, these strategies could help reduce FBA-related morbidity and mortality in Palestine.
