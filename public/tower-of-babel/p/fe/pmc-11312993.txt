Health-related quality of life in patients with palmoplantar pustulosis – a Swedish register study
Authors: Jenny M Norlin, Sofia Löfvendahl, Marcus Schmitt-Egenolf
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReal-world data on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in palmoplantar pustulosis (PPP) are scarce and few studies have analysed the generic HRQoL.ObjectivesTo assess HRQoL using the generic EQ-5D instrument and the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) instrument in PPP compared to plaque psoriasis.MethodsCross-sectional data from PsoReg, the Swedish National Registry for Systemic Treatment of Psoriasis (2006–2021), were examined. The study included 306 patients with PPP, out of which 22% had concomitant plaque psoriasis (n = 68), and 7041 patients with plaque psoriasis only. EQ-5D and DLQI were compared between patients with PPP and patients with plaque psoriasis, overall and stratified by sex. A subgroup analysis compared outcomes for patients with PPP vs. patients with severe plaque psoriasis (Psoriasis Area and Severity Index ≥10). Multiple regression analyses were performed to control for potential confounders (age, sex, comorbidities, lifestyle factors).ResultsPatients with PPP were to a larger extent female (79% vs. 37%, p < .01) and older (mean [SD] age 59.9 [11.9] vs. 50.7 [16.0] years, p < .01) than patients with plaque psoriasis. EQ-5D values were significantly lower (worse) in patients with PPP (mean [SD] 0.622 [0.309]) compared to patients with plaque psoriasis (mean [SD] 0.715 [0.274]). No significant difference was observed compared to patients with severe plaque psoriasis (p = .237). DLQI was comparable in PPP and plaque psoriasis patients (p = .117). In the regression analyses, PPP only and PPP with plaque psoriasis were associated with lower EQ-5D values of 0.065 (p < .01) and 0.061 points (p < .10) compared to plaque psoriasis patients.ConclusionsPPP had a substantial negative impact on patients’ generic and dermatology-specific HRQoL. Patients with PPP were worse off in terms of generic HRQoL compared with patients with plaque psoriasis when controlling for the impact of potential confounders.Keywords: Palmoplantar pustulosis, health related quality-of-life, EQ-5D, DLQI, clinical practice, register

Introduction

Pustular psoriasis describes a group of inflammatory skin disorders characterized by the presence of pustules [1,2]. Palmoplantar pustulosis (PPP) is the most common variant of pustular psoriasis [1]. PPP occurs three times more frequently in females than males [3,4]. The 1-year prevalence of PPP in the USA, Europe and Japan has been reported as 0.005–0.12% [4,5], peaking between 44 and 65 years of age [3,4,6]. PPP can co-occur with plaque psoriasis; an estimated 14.2–15.8% of patients with PPP have both diagnoses [3,5].

PPP is characterized by persistent, sterile, visible pustules on the palms of the hands and/or the soles of the feet [2,6]. Pustules are frequently accompanied by erythema, scaling and pain [1].

PPP is a chronic disease [7]. The clinical course of PPP is heterogeneous; lesions can either be persistent or follow a relapsing pattern [7]. In addition, psychiatric, cardiovascular, metabolic, articular and autoimmune comorbidities have been reported with PPP, although the relationship between PPP and systemic diseases is not yet sufficiently studied [5,7,8].

The localization and chronic-relapsing nature contribute to the overall disease burden of PPP [7]. PPP is associated with considerable impairment of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [9–11], especially for patients with coexisting plaque psoriasis [9,11]. PPP severity correlates with reduced HRQoL [9–11]. Only a few studies have provided real-world evidence on HRQoL specifically for patients with PPP [9–11], of which most studies were limited to the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) questionnaire [9–11]. Comparisons of HRQoL in patients with PPP and plaque psoriasis are lacking.

A better understanding of the generic HRQoL impairment in PPP is important from a clinical perspective by providing a more holistic picture of the patients’ health status, and from a payer perspective as it provides valuable data for health economics models. The generic EQ-5D instrument is commonly used in such analyses to estimate quality-adjusted life-years, which are essential data in cost-effectiveness analyses of new healthcare interventions [12–14].

The objective of this study was to analyse generic and dermatology-specific HRQoL of patients with PPP compared to patients with plaque psoriasis from the Swedish National Registry for Systemic Treatment of Psoriasis (PsoReg).

Materials and methods

Data source – PsoReg

This observational study utilized data from PsoReg, a registry initiated in 2006 providing real-world data from patients diagnosed with psoriasis who are using systemic treatment. Local, regional, university and private hospitals, private clinics and treatment centres managed by the patient organization PSO are enrolled in PsoReg.

The variables recorded in PsoReg include clinical types/variants of psoriasis, current and previous use of systemic treatments, general health parameters such as body mass index (BMI), lifestyle habits (e.g. smoking and alcohol consumption), physician-reported clinical measures of severity, and patient-reported HRQoL measures. Observations reflect clinical practice and visits are not protocol-driven. Questionnaires are thus distributed to the patients as they visit their physician. PsoReg is described in detail elsewhere [15].

The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board at Umeå University (Dnr 2010-194-31M, Dnr 2011-286-32M, Dnr 2016-126-32M). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in PsoReg. Data and consent were collected electronically.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

The study applied broad inclusion criteria to reflect clinical practice. All PPP patients and plaque psoriasis patients with at least one follow-up during registration on PsoReg between 2006 and May 2021 were included. Cross-sectional data based on the first observation in PsoReg for each patient were used.

Patients with concomitant uncommon clinical types of erythroderma and acrodermatitis continua suppurativa, or with no registration of either PPP or plaque psoriasis in PsoReg, were excluded. Observations without outcomes (neither DLQI nor EQ-5D) were excluded. Patients with PPP and a concurrent registration of plaque psoriasis were included in the PPP group. The diagnosis of PPP was based on the dermatologist’s clinical examination of the patient at routine clinical visits.

Patient characteristics

Data regarding patient characteristics included sex, age, BMI, waist circumference, psoriasis onset age >30 years, Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) score, smoking status, alcohol consumption (high-risk intake as defined by the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare) [16], concurrent diagnosis of other types of psoriasis (plaque psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, nail psoriasis, guttate psoriasis and non-pustular palmoplantar psoriasis), and ongoing treatment (at first observation).

Outcome measures

EQ-5D

The EQ-5D-three levels (EQ-5D-3L, hereafter EQ-5D) is a generic standardized questionnaire that measures health status in five dimensions: ‘mobility’, ‘self-care’, ‘usual activities’, ‘pain/discomfort’ and ‘anxiety/depression’ [13]. The three-level version includes three response levels: ‘no problems’, ‘some problems’ and ‘extreme problems’ [13]. The EQ-5D results in 243 health states, to which previously published utility values were attached. The utility values were derived from the general population of Great Britain [17] as no hypothetical values were available from the Swedish setting (only experience-based values) [18]. Lower EQ-5D values reflect worse HRQoL.

DLQI

The DLQI is a patient-reported questionnaire comprising 10 questions on a four-point scale relating to how the skin disease has affected patients’ quality of life over the past week [19]. The score ranges from 0 (quality of life not impaired) to 30 (quality of life severely impaired) [19]. A DLQI cumulative score of 0–1 represents no effect, 2–5 a small effect, 6–10 a moderate effect, 11–20 a very large effect, and 21–30 an extremely large effect on the patient’s life.

Analyses of HRQoL

The overall EQ-5D values and DLQI scores were analysed in patients with PPP and in those with plaque psoriasis. In addition, an alternative analysis was performed in patients with severe plaque psoriasis, defined as PASI score ≥10. A descriptive analysis (no evaluation of statistical significance of differences) was performed on the different EQ-5D dimensions and DLQI questions.

Subgroup analysis

The subgroup of patients with PPP and concomitant plaque psoriasis was compared to those with PPP only.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics (mean, SD; median, interquartile range) for DLQI and EQ-5D were generated. Differences in characteristics and outcomes between groups were analysed by statistical tests as appropriate; the analysis of variance test was used for normally distributed variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for non-normally distributed variables, and the χ2-test was used for categorical variables.

Outcomes were stratified by sex, as sex distributions differed significantly. In addition, regression analyses were performed with HRQoL (EQ-5D/DLQI) as the dependent variable and a categorical variable representing type of psoriasis (plaque psoriasis, PPP, plaque and PPP) as the independent variable. Regression analyses (ordinary least squares) adjusted for age, sex, comorbidities (psoriatic arthritis and nail psoriasis) and lifestyle factors (BMI, smoking and high risk alcohol consumption), which are known determinants of HRQoL. Plaque psoriasis was used as the reference group.

Results

From a total of 10,680 patients in PsoReg, 7041 patients with plaque psoriasis (and no diagnosis of PPP) and 306 patients with PPP fulfilled the inclusion criteria (Figure S1, Supporting Information). Of the patients with PPP, 68 (22%) had concomitant plaque psoriasis.

Forty-five percent of observations occurred in the most recent period (2016–2021), 24% occurred between 2011 and 2015 and 31% in between 2006 and 2010. No statistically significant difference was detected in the distribution of cases between PPP/plaque psoriasis between these time periods.

Compared to plaque psoriasis, patients with PPP were older, to a larger extent female and included a larger proportion with psoriasis onset age >30 years (Table 1). Among patients with PPP, 73% were either smokers or former smokers; among patients with plaque psoriasis, the corresponding proportion was 45%. In the overall study population, there were no significant differences between PPP and plaque psoriasis with respect to the frequencies of other common concurrent psoriasis types. Treatment with biologics was less common among patients with PPP (14%) compared to patients with plaque psoriasis (24%).

Patients with plaque psoriasis only had a mean PASI score of 7.86 (SD 6.71); 28% (n = 1948) had severe plaque psoriasis (a PASI score ≥10). The mean (SD) PASI score was 6.80 (6.03) in females and 8.25 (6.64) in males with plaque psoriasis.

EQ-5D

EQ-5D was significantly lower (p < .01) in patients with PPP compared to patients with plaque psoriasis, indicating lower HRQoL for patients with PPP (Figure 1, Table 2). However, since females had overall lower EQ-5D scores than males, the difference between groups was partly driven by the larger proportion of females in the PPP group. The difference in EQ-5D was statistically significant when comparing EQ-5D between female PPP and plaque psoriasis patients only, but not for male patients (possibly due to the small sample size).

Regression analyses, adjusting for sex, age, comorbidities and lifestyle factors, suggest a worse EQ-5D of 0.065 (p < .001) in PPP compared to plaque psoriasis only and a tendency of worse EQ-5D of 0.061 in patients with both and plaque psoriasis compared to plaque psoriasis only (p = .076) (Supplementary Information). Being female was associated with a decrease in EQ-5D of 0.077 (p < .001). In contrast, increasing age was associated with a small but significant increase in EQ-5D of less than 0.001 per year (p < .001); i.e. HRQoL improved with increasing age.

EQ-5D in patients with severe plaque psoriasis (mean [SD] 0.628 [0.309]) was comparable to patients with PPP (mean [SD] 0.622 [0.309]) (p = .237). No statistically significant differences between PPP and plaque psoriasis were detected in the regression analyses (supplementary file).

DLQI

Figure 2 illustrates the proportion of PPP and plaque psoriasis patients per DLQI severity level. DLQI for patients with PPP was not statistically different from that for patients with plaque psoriasis in the descriptive analyses (Figure 3, Table 2), nor in the regression analyses (Supplementary Information). Being female was associated with worse HRQoL, with 1.7 points higher DLQI (p < .001). Increasing age had a small (−0.11 per year) but statistically significant effect on DLQI (p < .001); i.e. the DLQI was lower (better HRQoL) with increasing age.

HRQoL dimensions/questions in PPP

For patients with PPP, problems related to the EQ-5D dimensions ‘pain/discomfort’, ‘anxiety/depression’ and ‘mobility’ were the most commonly experienced (Figure 4). For the DLQI questions, ‘itchy, painful’ was by far the most troublesome (Figure 5), where 86% reported problems, followed by ‘treatment’, in which 54% reported problems.

Subgroup of patients with both plaque psoriasis and PPP

No statistically significant difference was detected in terms of patient characteristics (age, sex, BMI, waist, onset age >30 years, current smoker, former smoker, high-risk alcohol consumption, guttate psoriasis, non-pustular palmoplantar psoriasis, biologic treatment) between PPP patients with (n = 68) and without plaque psoriasis (n = 238). Patients with PPP and plaque psoriasis had psoriatic arthritis and nail psoriasis to a larger extent that patients with PPP only (p < .001). No statistically significant difference was detected in EQ-5D (0.629 vs. 0.597 for PPP only and PPP with plaque psoriasis, respectively (p = .463)) nor DLQI (8.0 vs.9.1, for PPP only and PPP with plaque psoriasis, respectively (p = .163)).

Discussion

Patients with PPP had a considerable impairment in both their generic (EQ-5D) and dermatology-specific (DLQI) HRQoL. Females rated their HRQoL considerably lower than males on both measures.

Notably, a high proportion of patients with PPP experienced problems regarding the EQ-5D dimensions ‘mobility’ and ‘pain/discomfort’, which may relate to impaired mobility due to disease manifestations on the soles. A remarkable finding was the particularly large proportions (86%) of patients experiencing problems within the DLQI question about how ‘itchy and painful’ their skin had been. Furthermore, more than half of patients with PPP reported problems within the DLQI question about treatment (how much of a problem has the treatment for your skin been, for example by making your home messy, or by taking up time?).

The distribution of sex was considerably different between patients with PPP and plaque psoriasis, where the proportion of females was 79% vs. 37%, respectively. This was expected as PPP is more common in females than males, whereas severe plaque psoriasis is more common among males [6,20]. Furthermore, as some patients (n = 68) has both PPP and plaque psoriasis, it may be difficult to disentangle the impact of the different psoriasis types on HRQoL. Regression analyses, controlling for the impact of age, sex, comorbidities and lifestyle factors, suggest that EQ-5D was worse in PPP only (−0.065) and in PPP with plaque psoriasis (−0.061) compared to plaque psoriasis only. No statistically significant difference was detected in DLQI between groups.

The study findings add to previous evidence of HRQoL impairment in patients with PPP. Several observational studies and clinical trials have investigated DLQI in PPP, reporting average values ranging from 7 (6) to 12.2 (7.7) [9–11,21,22]. For example, a Turkish study of 263 patients reported a mean (SD) DLQI of 6.87 (6.08) [9]. An Austrian cross-sectional study reported a mean (SD) DLQI score of 7 (6) in patients with active PPP, with 27.3% reporting severe or very severe impairment (DLQI score >10) [10]. Nonetheless, a German open-label study showed a higher mean (SD) DLQI score of 12.2 (7.7) in 77 patients with PPP; more than half of the patients (53.2%) reported severe HRQoL impairment (DLQI score >10) [11].

We found only one other study that evaluated EQ-5D in patients with PPP; in a randomized controlled trial of the interleukin-23 inhibitor guselkumab, patients with PPP reported a slightly higher mean baseline EQ-5D of 0.7 [22] compared to 0.622 in our study. For comparison, the mean EQ-5D values reported from the general Swedish population is 0.83 for females and 0.86 for males [23], compared to 0.61 for female and 0.69 for and male PPP patients in our study.

A strength of this study is that both generic and dermatology-specific HRQoL were analysed in patients with PPP based on data from Swedish clinical practice. Moreover, access to PsoReg allowed for comparison of HRQoL in PPP patients to a cohort of patients with plaque psoriasis only.

Some limitations of this study were inherited in the register-based design. As patients were observed in clinical practice while meeting with their physician, at the time of the HRQoL assessment, some patients may have had an optimal treatment strategy, whereas others had a suboptimal treatment.

Comparisons of results between different subgroups in observational research are limited by confounding factors. We considered the impact of age, sex, psoriatic arthritis and nail psoriasis, known determinants of HRQoL [18,24], by multiple regression analyses. Three out of four (73%) PPP patients were either current or former smokers, which was expected as smoking is a known triggering factor for PPP [25]. A confounder for which we lacked information in this study was the socioeconomic status of patients, which is associated with both smoking and HRQoL [26,27].

This study shows a significant impairment of the dermatology-specific HRQoL (DLQI) in PPP; similar to patients with plaque psoriasis. The reduction of the generic HRQoL (EQ-5D) was similar to patients with severe plaque psoriasis. Consequently, this study might contribute to a better awareness of the suffering of patients with PPP. When prioritizing resources, EQ-5D could be appropriate for estimating quality-adjusted life-years in cost-effectiveness analyses of new interventions for PPP.

In conclusion, we show that PPP has a substantial negative impact on patients’ HRQoL. These results provide insight into the problems patients with PPP experience in their daily lives.
