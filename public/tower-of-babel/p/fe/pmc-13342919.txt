Reduced Activity of Triazole Antifungal Agents Against Aspergillus tubingensis From a Global Collection of Genomically‐Characterized Aspergillus Section Nigri Clinical Isolates (2020–2023)
Authors: John H Kimbrough, Marisa L Winkler, Maura H Karr, Jessica M Ewald, Mariana Castanheira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe black moulds comprising Aspergillus section Nigri are clinically important organisms causing aspergillosis and otomycosis; triazole (azole) antifungal agents represent the primary prophylaxis and treatment option for these infections. The cryptic nature of species within section Nigri presents challenges for routine identification methods, yet accurate speciation may reveal important and overlooked trends in species distribution and azole antifungal activity.MethodWe used whole genome sequencing and comparative genomics to speciate 135 clinical A. section Nigri isolates collected worldwide from 2020 to 2023 and broth microdilution susceptibility testing to assess antifungal agent activity.Results A. welwitschiae (n = 61; 45.2%) and A. tubingensis (n = 51; 37.8%) were the primary species identified. A. tubingensis accounted for 87.9% (n = 29/33) of isolates producing azole MIC values above the CLSI‐established A. niger epidemiological cutoff values (ECV). All azoles displayed high activity (≥ 93.7% wildtype [WT]) against A. welwitschiae and A. niger isolates; A. tubingensis isolates were broadly inhibited by posaconazole (100% WT). The frequency of A. tubingensis isolates with NWT phenotypes against ≥ 2 azoles and the modal MIC values for most agents increased over time; azole‐NWT isolates were present across all regions. Comparisons of protein sequences of CYP51A and CYP51B, the molecular target of azole activity, revealed unique residues in A. tubingensis. However, most azole‐NWT isolates across species lacked unique CYP51A/B alterations, suggesting mechanisms outside target site modification influence elevated azole MIC values in this section.ConclusionsOur results demonstrate the utility of speciating clinically relevant organism groups to better understand the dynamics of antifungal agent activity and species distributions.Keywords: Aspergillus tubingensis, genomics, phylogeny, triazole activity

Introduction

Global shifts in agricultural practices, climate trends, and the post‐pandemic healthcare landscape have affected the distributions of fungal species and may contribute to a rise in fungal infections [1, 2]. Invasive aspergillosis, infections caused by moulds of the genus Aspergillus, are increasingly worrisome given the environmental ubiquity of these organisms and their potential to manifest nonsusceptibility to currently available antifungal agents either through intrinsic resistance mechanisms or acquired following exposure to antifungal agents or related compounds [3, 4, 5]. Moreover, compared to the extensive options available for the treatment of bacterial infections, the current antifungal armamentarium is limited to three main classes, namely the polyenes, the echinocandins and the triazoles. Triazoles represent the primary choice for prophylaxis and treatment of Aspergillus‐associated infections due to the toxicities associated with polyenes and the lack of efficacy of echinocandins [6, 7, 8]. Triazoles act to disrupt cell membrane biosynthesis by inhibiting the activity of the lanosterol 14α‐demethylases CYP51A/CYP51B, which contribute to the biosynthesis of ergosterol, an essential and unique component of the fungal cell membrane [3, 6, 9]. However, mutations in CYP51A/CYP51B and their regulators are long recognized for their role in reducing the utility of triazoles during aspergillosis therapy.

Although A. fumigatus is consistently identified as the predominant species causing aspergillosis, members of A. section Nigri are identified as the second or third leading species in surveys of clinical aspergillosis infections [10, 11, 12], particularly among patients with otomycosis [13, 14, 15]. The Nigri section, marked by characteristic black pigmentation, contains up to 29 recognized species [16], many of which are morphologically indistinguishable [12, 16, 17]. As such, clinical isolates are typically identified using either matrix‐assisted laser desorption ionization time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (MALDI‐TOF MS) or targeted sequencing of the genes encoding β‐tubulin (benA) and calmodulin (CaM) [18]. Although both methods have been extensively employed to identify section Nigri isolates, each possesses unique challenges with identification ultimately reliant upon the availability and accurate interpretation of species reference libraries. Numerous studies have demonstrated the difficulties in the extraction procedures for MALDI‐TOF‐based identification of cryptic Aspergillus species, with the most accurate species identifications generally requiring more laborious preparation methods [19, 20]. Furthermore, the uncommon nature of such species within A. section Nigri has resulted in fewer available reference spectra for these species [21]. Similarly, while some curated databases exist for genes commonly used as molecular identification targets, these databases may lack representation of all described species within any individual section or species complex. Speciation therefore relies on the use of general databases which may contain spurious depositions or outdated taxonomic designations, confounding accurate speciation [22]. In addition to these technical and analytical challenges, both identification methods are relatively low throughput and require extensive hands‐on processing and analysis time.

Regardless of the identification ultimately achieved, clinical isolates may be defaulted to a section‐level identification to utilize established susceptibility breakpoints or epidemiological cut‐off values to assess the activities of different antifungal agents, when available [23]. For example, the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute M57S document provides epidemiological cutoff values (ECVs) to separate wildtype and non‐wildtype Aspergillus niger, but no additional species‐level ECV values exist for other members of A. section Nigri . As such, A. niger ECVs have been applied broadly to other species in the section, and such practices may have thus obscured natural variation in resistance patterns [24]. Moreover, few longitudinal studies of species distributions spanning multiple countries/regions assessing the activities of different antifungal agents against clinical A. section Nigri isolates have been undertaken, with many reports confined to the isolates obtained within single countries [25, 26, 27].

We sought to leverage the power of whole genome sequencing and comparative genomics to speciate a globally sourced collection of A. section Nigri clinical isolates obtained through the SENTRY Antifungal Surveillance program from 2020 to 2023 with a goal to more clearly define the nature of A. section Nigri species dynamics and to examine the species‐specific responses to azole antifungal agents against isolates within this cryptic species complex.

Materials and Methods

Organisms and Susceptibility Testing

A total of 135 nonduplicate Aspergillus section Nigri isolates were collected from medical centres participating in the SENTRY Antifungal Surveillance Program during 2020–2023: North America (NA; n = 46; 2 countries), Europe (EUR; n = 58; 11 countries), Asia‐Pacific (APAC; n = 30; 5 countries), and Latin America (LATAM; n = 1; 1 country) (Figure S1A, Data S1). The isolates were recovered from patients with respiratory tract infections (RTI; n = 66), bloodstream infections (BSI; n = 1), skin and skin structure infections (SSTI; n = 11), and miscellaneous other sites (OS; n = 57) as determined by local criteria (Figure S1B, Data S1).

Upon receipt, isolates were subcultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA; Remel Inc., Lenexa, KS, USA) and incubated for up to 7 days to assess purity and viability. Isolates confirmed as pure were inoculated into Sabouraud Liquid Broth Modified (Becton, Dickenson and Company, Sparks, MD, USA) and the hyphae harvested and prepared for formic acid extraction or prepared using the direct smear method followed by identification using MALDI‐TOF MS using the MALDI Biotyper if a score ≥ 2.0 was achieved (Bruker Daltronics, Billerica, MA, USA). If a score < 2.0 was achieved, identification was performed only by molecular methods.

Isolates were tested by broth microdilution (BMD) using CLSI methodologies [28, 29]. Frozen‐form microdilution panels using RPMI 1640 broth supplemented with MOPS (morpholinepropane sulfonic acid buffer) and 0.2% glucose were inoculated with conidial suspensions to a final concentration of 0.2–2.5 × 104 CFU/mL. Minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were visualized after 48 h for azoles and amphotericin B. MIC endpoints were read at the lowest concentration producing visually clear wells. Quality control was performed in accordance with CLSI M38 guidelines using Aspergillus flavus ATCC 204304, A. fumigatus ATCC MYA‐3626, Candida krusei (Pichia kudriavzevii) ATCC 6258, and Candida parapsilosis ATCC 22019. MIC values were within the QC ranges for all test replicates. Epidemiological cutoff values (ECVs) have been developed for Aspergillus niger against isavuconazole (ECV, 4 mg/L), itraconazole (ECV, 4 mg/L), posaconazole (ECV, 2 mg/L) and voriconazole (ECV, 2 mg/L) [23]. Isolates for which azole MIC results exceeded the ECV were considered non‐wildtype (NWT). For the purposes of this study, Aspergillus niger ECVs were generally applied to all isolates, regardless of species [30].

Molecular Techniques and Bioinformatic Analysis

All isolates were subjected to next‐generation sequencing for speciation beyond MALDI‐TOF MS identification. DNA was extracted from isolates grown overnight (< 24 h; 35°C) on PDA using the Qiagen DNeasy UltraClean Microbial DNA extraction kit (Qiagen, GmbH; Hilden, Germany). Total genomic DNA was used as input material for library construction prepared using the Nextera XT or the Illumina DNA Prep library construction protocol and index kit (Illumina; San Diego, California, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Library preparation was performed using either a Tecan Freedom Evo (Tecan Group Ltd.; Mannedorf, Switzerland) or Biomek i7 Hybrid (Beckman Coulter Inc.; Brea, California, USA) automated liquid handler. Sequencing was performed on a MiSeq or NextSeq Sequencer (Illumina).

Sequencing reads were error‐corrected and trimmed using Sickle version 1.33 [31]. A total of 41 publicly available genome assemblies, representative of the Nigri section, were obtained from NCBI and combined with surveillance isolates as phylogenetic reference species. A. niger strain ATCC 64974 was selected as the reference strain for variant calling using Snippy v.4.6.0 [32]. Results of the variant calling were analysed to create a core SNP alignment, and phylogeny was determined with FastTree v.2.1.10 using the generalized time‐reversible model with default settings [33]. The resulting phylogenetic tree was generated using iTOL v. 7.2.2 [34].

Nucleotide sequences corresponding to the best match allele for the genes encoding β‐tubulin (benA) and calmodulin (CaM) based on percent identity (minimum 90.0%) and maximum length (minimum 90.0% coverage) were extracted using BLAST 2.16.0. The CaM gene sequence was extracted from the NCBI reference genomes used in the whole genome SNP analysis. Analysis of benA was performed using a custom‐built database of sequences obtained from NCBI nuccore with the following parameters: Organism = “Aspergillus” and beta‐tubulin [Protein Name] OR beta tubulin [Protein Name] OR BenA [Gene Name]. Results were then filtered by entries with a maximum length of 4000 bp with a positive identification of Aspergillus. ‘Unverified’ and duplicate sequence entries were removed from the final database, which contained 6769 unique entries. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) was determined using FastANI 1.33 [35] and an in‐house curated genome database comprised of NCBI reference genomes and an additional A. floridensis reference (Aspflor1) obtained from the Joint Genome Institute database (Table S1).

CYP51A and CYP51B sequences from each reference species were used to extract the corresponding sequence from each clinical isolate using the SNP‐based speciation results. Nucleotide sequences corresponding to the exons within CYP51A and CYP51B were extracted, concatenated, translated and aligned using MUSCLE 5.1 [36] to generate a species‐level consensus sequence for each protein for comparison purposes.

Analysis was performed following isolate/patient deidentification; no further ethics committee approvals were required.

Results

Isolates were initially speciated using MALDI‐TOF MS, with most (81.5%) producing top‐score identifications of A. niger ; smaller proportions of isolates were identified as other members of section Nigri, with A. tubingensis accounting for the majority of these non‐ A. niger isolates (n = 20/25; 14.8% overall) (Figure 1, Data S1). With the limited number of validated MALDI patterns available to identify isolates to the species level within this section, many isolates produced scores ≥ 2.0 for multiple species/spectra (data not shown), necessitating further molecular methods for differentiation. We sequenced the entire genome of all isolates within our dataset and extracted the sequences of benA and CaM, commonly utilized targets for Aspergillus speciation, to increase the granularity of species assignment. Based on CaM, 61 (45.2%) and 48 (35.6%) isolates were identified as A. welwitschiae and A. tubingensis, respectively, whereas smaller proportions of isolates were identified as A. niger (n = 13, 9.6%) or the closely allied A. phoenicis ( A. niger var. phoenicis; n = 3, 2.2%); 2 (1.5%) isolates each were identified as A. vadensis, A. costaricaensis, A. brasiliensis and A. luchuensis (Figure 1, Data S1). However, benA analysis produced more diverse species assignments among all isolates, with many isolates, particularly among those speciated as A. welwitschiae by CaM, producing different species identifications (Figure 1, Data S1).

Based on the large number of discordant species assignments using β‐tubulin and calmodulin, particularly among isolates provisionally identified as A. welwitschiae via calmodulin, we leveraged the extent of our whole genome sequencing data to further clarify species representation in our isolates. We employed two methods to this end, direct comparison of each isolate to the set of 42 reference species (Table S1) to assess average nucleotide identity (ANI) and a whole genome SNP‐based approach followed by the construction of a phylogenetic tree of all isolates. Using ANI or SNP‐based methods, 62 and 61 isolates were assigned to A. welwitschiae, with the sole differing isolate identified as A. niger by SNP and CaM and as A. welwitschiae by ANI (Figure 1 and Data S1). This isolate (NA‐2023‐5) was ultimately assigned to A. niger based on its clustering with other A. niger isolates within the overall phylogeny, although its position was notable, along with isolate NA‐2022‐9, for being outside the main cluster of isolates within the A. niger clade. Isolate assignment to A. tubingensis was 100.0% concordant when using ANI and SNP‐based methods; however, 2 of these isolates were identified as A. costaricaensis and 1 as A. luchuensis by CaM or benA analysis (Figure 1 and Data S1). Calmodulin‐based identification was generally consistent with the results of ANI and the SNP‐based analysis for all isolates, with a consensus assignment of the remaining isolates as 2 isolates each to A. brasiliensis , A. luchuensis, and A. vadensis; a single isolate was ultimately speciated as A. fijiensis using calmodulin, ANI, and SNP while MALDI and benA assignments were A. japonicus and A. brunneoviolaceus, respectively (collectively ‘Other species’). Taken together, the results of each method of species identification demonstrate overall agreement between results obtained from SNP, ANI and calmodulin analysis, and we elected to assign species identifications based on the SNP‐based/calmodulin results.

Having resolved the species assignments of the isolates within our dataset, we next looked for potential geo‐temporal trends in species distribution (Figure 2). Over the study period, the frequency of A. tubingensis remained largely stable with yearly isolate totals ranging from 37.0% to 38.5% of all A. section Nigri collected whereas A. welwitschiae (36.2%–57.1%) and A. niger (2.9%–19.1%) demonstrated larger yearly variability (Figure 2A–C, All Years). Across all study years, A. welwitschiae was the predominant species contributed by medical centres in Asia‐Pacific (46.7%) and North America (43.5%), and although a similar overall percentage of isolates contributed by European medical centres was A. welwitschiae (44.8%), A. tubingensis comprised the largest proportion of A. section Nigri isolates from this region (53.4%). A niger represented a higher proportion of isolates collected in North America (26.1%) compared to other regions (Asia‐Pacific, 10.0%; Europe, 1.7%). Definitive patterns of species representation within regions across years were generally absent, although species heterogeneity tended to increase in 2022–2023 among isolates contributed by Asia‐Pacific and North American centres compared to yearly oscillations in the majority species from European centres (A. tubingenesis, 2020, 66.7%, 2022, 73.3%; A. welwitschiae, 2021, 56.3%, 2023, 60.0%).

We next evaluated the activity of four currently available azole antifungal agents (isavuconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, and posaconazole) against all isolates with attention to the specific patterns of azole activity against the primary species groups: A. welwitschiae, A. tubingensis, and A. niger . As specific susceptibility breakpoints or epidemiological cutoff values (ECVs) are not available for these species, we applied the CLSI‐approved A. niger ECVs to all isolates [23]. Against the entire set of A. section Nigri isolates, posaconazole (MIC50/90, 0.5/1 mg/L) demonstrated the highest activity, with 100.0% of isolates producing wildtype (WT) MIC values; isavuconazole (MIC50/90, 2/4 mg/L) and voriconazole (MIC50/90, 1/2 mg/L) were also active against > 90% of all A. section Nigri isolates, with 90.4% and 91.9% below the ECV, respectively (Table 1). By contrast, only 77.8% of all isolates produced WT MIC values when tested against itraconazole (MIC50/90, 2/ > 8 mg/L).

Stark differences in the activities of all azole agents were apparent across the three main species subsets. Only three total isolates from A. welwitschiae (n = 2) and A. niger (n = 1) produced non‐WT (NWT) MIC values against any azole agent, with the A. niger isolate producing NWT MIC values against isavuconazole (8 mg/L) and itraconazole (8 mg/L) and both A. welwitschiae NWT for only itraconazole (MIC, ≥ 8 mg/L) (Figures 1, 3; Data S1). Among all A. niger and A. welwitschiae isolates, 100.0% were WT for voriconazole and posaconazole, and 93.7%–96.7% and 93.7%–100.0% against itraconazole and isavuconazole, respectively (Table 1, Figure 3). By contrast, A. tubingensis isolates were poorly inhibited by most azole agents compared to the other species groups, with modal MIC values for each agent 2‐ to > 8‐fold higher, respectively, than the corresponding values when tested against A. welwitschiae or A. niger (Table 1). Only posaconazole (MIC50/90, 0.5/1 mg/L; 100.0% WT) demonstrated high activity against A. tubingensis isolates (Table 1, Figure 3), whereas isavuconazole (MIC50/90, 4/8 mg/L) and voriconazole (MIC50/90, 2/4 mg/L) inhibited ~80% of these isolates. Itraconazole (MIC50/90, 8/> 8 mg/L) was largely inactive against A. tubingensis isolates, as 51.0% produced NWT MIC values. The MIC50/90 and modal MIC values for each species group were within a single doubling dilution of one another and all isolates were WT for amphotericin B (Table 1).

Elevated azole MIC values among A. tubingensis isolates were not limited to a single region, as modal MICs for each azole were roughly equivalent across all regions (Figure S2). Importantly, the modal MIC values for itraconazole (> 8 mg/L), isavuconazole (4 mg/L), and voriconazole (2 mg/L) were equivalent to or exceeded the ECV for these agents (Table 1). Moreover, the modal MIC values for A. tubingensis tested against all azole agents (excluding posaconazole) generally increased each year (Figure S3B) as did the overall fraction of A. tubingensis isolates NWT to voriconazole (2020, 0.0%; 2023, 50.0%) and itraconazole (2020, 10.0%; 2023, 80.0%) (Figure S4A). The frequency of isolates NWT to isavuconazole was more variable (15.4%–30.0%) across study years and the frequency of posaconazole NWT isolates remained steady at 0.0% over the study period (Figure S4A). By contrast, the modal mean MIC values of azoles tested against A. niger (Figure S3A) and A. welwitschiae (Figure S3C) were largely stable or decreased slightly from 2020 to 2023.

Overall, NWT MIC values to any azole were observed for 29/51 (56.9%) A. tubingensis isolates, with 12 isolates producing NWT MIC values for ≥ 2 azoles (Figure 1). Likely owing to the overall yearly increase in modal MIC values for itraconazole, isavuconazole, and voriconazole, the frequency of isolates NWT to multiple azole agents likewise increased every year in the study period (Figure S4A). For A. tubingensis isolates NWT against a single azole agent, 82.4% were NWT against only itraconazole while the remaining 3 isolates were isavuconazole‐NWT (Data S1). These three isolates were all contributed by the same German medical centre in 2020 (Figure 1, Data S1). The geographic distribution of azole‐WT and azole‐NWT A. tubingensis was similar between azole activity and region of isolation (Figure S4B).

Outside of the three primary species groups, all A. luchuensis, A. vadensis, and A. fijiensis isolates were WT for all azoles, as was one A. brasiliensis isolate (Figure 1). A single A. brasiliensis isolate produced NWT MIC values when tested against isavuconazole (> 8 mg/L), itraconazole (> 8 mg/L), and voriconazole (8 mg/L), but was WT for posaconazole (0.5 mg/L) when using A. niger ECVs.

We then examined the targets of azole activity in Aspergillus, CYP51A and CYP51B, for amino acid variations potentially associated with the elevated MIC values observed in the 33 isolates producing NWT MIC values for any azole. We were also interested in potential natural variation in the CYP51A/B sequences of A. tubingensis isolates that may influence activity of azole agents generally, thus resulting in the elevated MIC values observed in this species. We first determined the consensus sequences for CYP51A and CYP51B in each of the three primary species groups. In CYP51A, 15 (2.9%) variant residues were identified among the three species (Figure 4A), with 14 of these positions conserved between A. niger and A. welwitschiae. Furthermore, intraspecies variation at these divergent residues was typically > 90%, with only T57 (75.0%) in A. niger and T321 (84.3%)/I503 (86.3%) in A. tubingensis falling below this threshold. Intraspecies variation within CYP51A was also low, with only 2.3%, 2.1%, and 1.6% of residues showing multiple amino acid residues in A. tubingensis, A. niger , and A. welwitschiae, respectively (Data S1). Similarly high conservation was observed for CYP51B, as only 18 (3.4%) variant positions were identified between the consensus sequences of the three species groups, with A. niger and A. welwitschiae sharing identical residues at 17 of these positions (Figure 4B). Interestingly, each species maintained a unique residue at position 267 (A. tubingensis, T267; A. niger , N267; A. welwitschiae, I267), although higher within‐species variation was present in A. niger (87.5% conserved) and A. welwitschiae (82.0% conserved) at this position. CYP51B displayed even greater intraspecies conservation than CYP51A, with only 1 (A. tubingensis) to 4 (A. welwitschiae) variable positions identified across the protein (Data S1).

Positional variation and intraspecies conservation (%) at variant residues identified in the consensus sequences of CYP51A (A) and CYP51B (B) among A. tubingensis (n = 51), A. niger (n = 16) and A. welwitschiae (n = 61) isolates.

Having established the consensus sequences of CYP51A and CYP51B for each species, we then assessed the sequences of the isolates with NWT azole MIC values for alterations potentially underlying these phenotypes. Across all three primary species groups, no alterations relative to the consensus sequence of CYP51B were identified in isolates with NWT azole MIC values. In CYP51A, a single A. welwitschiae isolate (itraconazole MIC 8 mg/L) bore a unique S270G alteration while the sole NWT A. niger isolate (itraconazole and isavuconazole MIC 8 mg/L) possessed a T57A alteration; this alteration was shared by 3 additional azole‐WT isolates from North America. Azole NWT A. tubingensis isolates similarly lacked unique alterations within CYP51A, with only a single isolate, NWT when tested against all azoles except posaconazole, bearing a unique V17A alteration. All other azole‐NWT isolates shared combinations of alterations from the consensus sequence with azole‐WT isolates, when present. Moreover, many instances of divergence from the consensus CYP51A or CYP151B sequences tended to occur among isolates closely clustered within the overall phylogeny generated through our SNP‐based analysis, suggesting these variant residues may represent natural polymorphisms. The sole azole‐NWT isolate outside the primary species groups, an A. brasiliensis isolate from North America, shared 100.0% identity for CYP51B compared to the only other A. brasiliensis isolate in our collection (pan‐azole WT), but possessed V6I, M53I and K246Q alterations in CYP51A.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the species distributions and responses to azole antifungal agents in a large, global collection of Aspergillus section Nigri isolates speciated using whole genome sequencing and comparative genomics. Our analysis identified A. welwitschiae as the most common species in our isolate set followed closely by A. tubingensis, in line with previous reports of the representation of these two species among clinical isolates [37]. However, among isolates displaying NWT MIC values against any azole antifungal agent, the primary treatment option for aspergillosis infections, A. tubingensis accounted for approximately 88% of isolates. Moreover, our results corroborate and extend previous observations of the reduced activity of azole antifungal agents generally against A. tubingensis isolates, which produced 2‐ to > 8‐fold higher modal MIC values for all tested agents compared to A. welwitschiae and A. niger [27]. However, while many isolates from the three primary species groups possessed alterations in the targets of azole activity, CYP51A and CYP51B, relative to the species‐level consensus sequences, few azole‐NWT isolates possessed unique alterations and often shared molecular profiles with azole‐WT isolates, further supporting the work of previous studies whose findings also suggest mechanisms other than target site modification of CYP51A influence elevated azole MIC values in this species group [38]. In contrast to the distinct species‐level phenotypes against azole antifungals, all A. section Nigri species groups were similarly inhibited by amphotericin B, which is the preferred alternative treatment for aspergillosis infections to azoles.

Traditional identification methods used to speciate many fungal isolates are complicated by the morphological similarities and cryptic nature of species within A. section Nigri [17]. Species‐level identification therefore relies on the use of technically and analytically challenging, low‐throughput, laborious sequencing methodology that may ultimately rely on a limited number of high‐quality references for identification. Accurate identification may also be confounded by the existence of incorrectly speciated isolates within the reference databases themselves. An extensive study by Steenwyk and colleagues similarly relied on more holistic genomic comparisons of publicly available reference isolates and catalogued numerous differences between the phylogenetic placement of these isolates relative to their deposited species [22]. Incongruencies within widely used reference databases may have influenced our initial results from the analysis of β‐tubulin as a speciation target, as identifications made by this gene were largely inconsistent with the results of the ANI‐, SNP‐, and calmodulin‐based species assignment. Specifically, 38 isolates speciated as A. welwitschiae by ANI, SNP, and calmodulin were assigned A. niger by benA (six unique NCBI benA depositions; data not shown). When we confined comparison of these benA sequences against our set of NCBI reference genomes, 28 of our isolates displayed higher percent identity with an A. welwitschiae reference strain (CBS139.54b), while the 10 remaining isolates more closely resembled the benA reference from A. niger (SAMEA3283178; data not shown). Furthermore, our benA database similarly contained the sequences for unspeciated Aspergillus and A. awamori isolates, the latter now recognized as a variant of A. welwitschiae [39]. Although some studies have proposed an overall reduction in the number of accepted species within A. section Nigri, these have typically relied on a smaller number of nucleotide and/or amino acid sequence comparisons than utilized herein. However, our benA results underscore a potential pitfall of using uncurated databases for definitive species assignments, particularly for a group of closely related species with regular updates to taxonomic assignments, and demonstrate the power of full genomic comparisons extending outside the traditional molecular identification loci.

Institution‐specific reporting structures may also generalize identifications to the section level, thereby obscuring the true representation of species in clinical settings and as such their associated responses to different antifungal agents. As currently defined, CLSI A. niger itraconazole ECVs are > 2‐fold below the modal MIC value for A. tubingensis isolates in this study [23]. Additional studies have similarly reported consistently elevated MIC values for clinical and environmental A. tubingensis isolates, particularly for itraconazole, and many have suggested it to be a natural characteristic of this species [30, 40]. All MIC values across all azoles were elevated for A. tubingensis compared to the other primary species groups, and this trend was evident across geographic regions, suggesting no region‐specific population of A. tubingensis was responsible for driving the overall increase, and the proportions of azole‐WT and NWT A. tubingensis isolates were roughly equivalent across regions.

A. section Nigri isolates in clinical invasive aspergillosis cases are less common than those of other Aspergillus species [12], namely A. fumigatus , with recent surveillance studies ranging from 6.1% to 26.9% compared to > 44% frequency for A. fumigatus in the same surveys [10, 41, 42, 43]. However, many of these studies are limited to smaller geographic regions (e.g., single countries or hospitals), shorter timescales, or specific infection types, thus limiting the overall conclusions pertaining to species distribution or antifungal activity trends [25, 26, 27, 44, 45, 46]. Although one limitation of the current study is the near complete lack of isolates from Latin American medical centres, which necessitates further studies to assess the activity of different azoles against currently circulating species in this region, we were able to examine species dynamics and azole activities over time. Shifts toward more heterogeneous species makeups were noted among isolates from Asia‐Pacific and North America, while Europe was dominated by either A. tubingensis or A. welwitschiae across study years [26, 47]. Continued species‐level surveillance of clinical specimens and environmental isolates is warranted to elucidate potential drivers (e.g., climate change, agricultural azole use) of these oppositional trends (diversification vs. stability) in these regions [1, 48].

Relatedly, upward trends in modal MIC values for all azoles, except posaconazole, when tested against A. tubingensis were also observed, although the increases in isavuconazole values were modest compared to those of itraconazole and voriconazole. Furthermore, we observed a year‐over‐year increase in the frequency of A. tubingensis isolates producing NWT MIC values to any azole as well as a yearly increase in the proportion of isolates NWT to multiple azoles, while non‐A. tubingensis isolates NWT against any azole were more sporadically encountered.

In line with other studies [49], and despite the geo‐temporal diversity of our isolate set and the large number of azole‐WT clinical isolates available for comparison, we were unable to associate any specific alteration in the targets of azole activity, CYP51A/CYP51B, to the NWT phenotype [38, 49, 50]. While deletion of CYP51A or CYP51B results in a reduction in the MIC against voriconazole in different A. niger and A. tubingensis isolates [51], no single mutation or set of mutations has been identified in natural A. section Nigri isolates as a primary driver of elevated azole MICs, as is the case in A. fumigatus [3]. Several NWT isolates did, however, contain unique CYP51A alterations (A. welwitschiae CYP51S270G; A. tubingensis CYP51V17A; A. brasiliensis CYP51V6I,M53I,K246Q) which may function to reduce azole activity. A larger outstanding question, however, is what unique genetic factors are present in A. tubingensis underlying the elevated MIC values when tested against all azoles generally, and whether such intrinsic differences in this species present as mutation‐driven resistance in the small number of azole NWT isolates from other A. section Nigri species. We attempted to assess the presence of such genomic differences between azole‐WT and azole‐NWT isolates clustered closely within our SNP‐based phylogeny, but these comparisons routinely generated thousands of differences (data not shown), thus confounding the identification of genetic factors underlying the NWT azole phenotype. These observations underscore the need for more controlled studies of the generation of azole‐NWT isolates from WT strains and the continued collection of closely related isolates from natural populations to more completely define the nature of azole activity in A. section Nigri isolates.

In summary, the results of this study collectively demonstrate the consistent presence of A. tubingensis as the primary azole‐NWT species among clinical A. section Nigri isolates collected worldwide. Troublingly, yearly increases in the frequencies of A. tubingensis isolates NWT to multiple azole antifungal agents and increases in the MIC values against most azole agents were identified, although the ultimate genetic basis underlying this trend remains incompletely understood. Our results further demonstrate the importance for continued species‐level surveillance of azole activity against A. tubingensis, and our collection of genomically‐characterized, geographically and epidemiologically diverse isolates will provide a basis for further studies of the nature of resistance to existing antifungals and assist in the development of new agents. More broadly, our results point to the power of full speciation and comparative genomics of clinical isolates, and our methods may serve as a generalized approach for future investigations monitoring species distributions and antifungal agent activity in clinical isolates belonging to different cryptic species complexes.
