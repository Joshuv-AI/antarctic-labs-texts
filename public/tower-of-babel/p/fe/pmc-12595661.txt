Transcranial photobiomodulation promotes traumatic brain injury recovery via modulating glial cell polarization and neuroinflammation: a study of 1064 nm light-emitting diodes
Authors: Guilin Chen, Zhenhua Xu, Hengchao Ma, Qiaozhen Qin, Xiaotong Li, Aojiao Yang, Yue Chen, Yifei Tan, Yuanrong Qiu, Yan Wang, Huaqiang Ruan, Xiaoxia Jiang, Jun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhotobiomodulation (PBM) utilizing 1064 nm near-infrared light, renowned for its deep tissue penetration capabilities, has demonstrated significant therapeutic potential in addressing brain disorders; however, its specific effects and underlying mechanisms in traumatic brain injury (TBI) remain poorly understood.MethodsThis study investigated the therapeutic efficacy of 1064 nm light-emitting diodes (LED) treatment on emotional and cognitive impairments in a murine TBI model, and elucidating potential molecular mechanisms. C57BL/6 mice were systematically allocated into Sham, TBI, and TBI + PBM intervention groups, with the latter receiving daily 1064 nm light treatment (25 mW/cm2, 12 min/day) for 14 consecutive days post-TBI induction. Comprehensive behavioral assessments were conducted to evaluate emotional and cognitive functions. Advanced molecular analyses encompassing transcriptome sequencing, immunofluorescence, quantitative RT-PCR, and Western blot were employed to examine brain tissue damage, neurogenesis, synaptic remodeling, and inflammatory responses.ResultsThe 1064 nm LED treatment demonstrated remarkable therapeutic effects, significantly ameliorating anxiety, depression-like behaviors, and spatial cognitive deficits in TBI mice. Behavioral improvements were evidenced by enhanced rotarod performance, increased exploratory behavior in open field and elevated plus maze tests, and improved Y-maze alternation rates. At the molecular level, PBM intervention exhibited multifaceted neuroprotective effects, including inhibition of neuronal apoptosis, reduction of brain injury, promotion of neurogenesis and synaptic remodeling, and upregulation of neurotrophic factors. Furthermore, the treatment enhanced blood–brain barrier integrity through upregulation of tight junction proteins and modulated neuroinflammation by shifting microglia and astrocytes toward anti-inflammatory phenotypes.ConclusionsThese findings collectively demonstrate that 1064 nm wavelength PBM treatment effectively promotes functional recovery and mitigates both emotional and cognitive impairments in TBI mice, providing novel mechanistic insights and a promising wavelength option for PBM-based therapeutic strategies in TBI management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07170-2.Keywords: Traumatic brain injury, Photobiomodulation, Emotional and cognitive impairments, Neuroinflammation, Cell polarization

Background

Traumatic injury (TBI) is a major global public health concern, characterized by high morbidity, mortality and disability. In the United States, approximately 1.7 million individuals suffer from TBI annually. The average mortality rate across 25 developed nations is 11.7/100,000 [1]. In China, the mortality rate associated with TBI is approximately 13/100,000 [2]. TBI often results in long-term cognitive dysfunctions and mood disorders, including irritability, aggression, anxiety, depression, attention and memory deficits, sleep disorders, and fatigue [3–6]. These sequelae are significant risk factors for chronic neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and chronic traumatic encephalopathy [7, 8]. McInnes et al. [9] reported that 50% of individuals with TBI may develop long-term cognitive dysfunction, and 15% of patients with first-time TBI may develop persistent post-concussive syndrome and long-term cognitive dysfunction. Current treatments for TBI include surgery, medication, symptomatic supportive therapy, hyperbaric oxygen therapy, and transcranial magnetic stimulation [10, 11]. However, these methods have several drawbacks, such as greater trauma, more side effects, inability to restore damaged neurological function, and a more complicated treatment process.

Photobiomodulation (PBM) is an emerging non-invasive physical therapy in the field of neuroscience, utilizing red light (600–670 nm) and near-infrared light (800–1100 nm) [12, 13]. The therapeutic effects of PBM are primarily mediated through the absorption of photons by the cytochrome c oxidase enzyme, which modulates the cellular oxidative respiratory chain, thereby alleviating inflammation, relieving pain, and promoting wound healing [12]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the neuroprotective effects of near-infrared light (NIR) in conditions such as spinal cord injury and peripheral nerve injury [14, 15]. PBM has also been applied in the treatment of TBI, with the 810nm laser being the most extensively studied wavelength [16]. However, recent evidence suggests that the 1064nm wavelength LED exhibits deeper tissue penetration compared to other wavelengths, such as 808 nm [17, 18]. Additionally, the 1064 nm LED has shown potential protective effects against neurodegenerative diseases [14, 19, 20]. Despite these promising findings, research on the regulatory effects of the 1064 nm LED on higher brain functions, particularly emotional and cognitive functions, following TBI remains limited. The underlying mechanisms of these effects also require further investigation.

In the present study, we investigated the long-term efficacy and neurobiological mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of 1064 nm LED photobiomodulation in improving outcomes following TBI. Our findings provide additional evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of PBM as a potential therapeutic intervention for TBI.

Materials and methods

Animals

C57BL/6N mice aged 8–10 weeks were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). The animals were housed in a controlled environment with a room temperature of (22 ± 2) °C, humidity of 50%–60%, and good ventilation, under a natural day/night cycle (12 h light/12 h darkness). Mice had free access to food and water throughout the experiment. Using the randomized numerical table method, the mice were divided into three groups, with 12 mice in each group: (1) Sham operation group (Sham group); (2) Traumatic brain injury group (TBI group); (3) TBI with 1064nm LED treatment group (TBI + PBM group). In the Sham group, only the brain window was opened without inducing TBI. In the TBI group, the mice were placed under a 1064 nm LED device, which remained off. In the TBI + PBM group, the mice underwent TBI induction followed by treatment with 1064 nm LED illumination. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals approved by the Beijing Institute of Basic Medical Sciences. The experimental protocols were also approved by the Ethical Review Committee of the Institution of Animal Experimentation (approval No. IACUC-DWZX-2024–560).

TBI model

The controlled cortical impact (CCI) method was used to construct the traumatic brain injury (TBI) model (Baucom et al., 2024). Each mouse was anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 2,2,2-tribromoethanol (350 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich, T48402). The animals were then placed on a stereotactic frame equipped with a temperature-controlled heating pad set at 37 °C. The scalp was depilated and sterilized with 75% alcohol before making a midline incision on the left side of the skull. After exposing the skull, a 4-mm craniotomy was performed at coordinates 3.0 mm anterior–posterior (AP) and 2.0 mm medial–lateral (ML) relative to bregma. A pneumatically operated metal impactor (diameter = 3 mm) was used to deliver a controlled cortical impact at a velocity of 3.5 m/s to a depth of 1.0 mm below the dural surface, with the impactor remaining in the brain for 400 ms. Hemostasis was achieved, and the wound was disinfected with iodine vapor before closure.

1064nm LED treatment

Mice were placed in a box and allowed to explore freely. The head was shaved, exposing the scalp over the right and left parieto-temporal region. The light device was adjusted and secured once the light intensity was confirmed to be 25 mW/cm2 using an integrating sphere spectrometer. For the TBI + PBM group, light treatment commenced 4 h after TBI induction and was administered once daily. The specific parameters included a 1064 nm wavelength LED, continuous wave mode, 12 min of irradiation time, and an energy density of 18 J/cm2, with the treatment cycle lasting for 14 days. Mice in the Sham and TBI groups were positioned similarly in the light box but without activation of the light.

Open field test (OFT)

The open field test is a widely used method to assess anxiety in mice, leveraging the rodents’ innate tendency to both fear open spaces and explore novel environments [21]. In this study, mice were first acclimated to the testing environment for 30 min. Subsequently, each mouse was placed in the center of the open field apparatus(50 cm × 50 cm × 40 cm,Zhongshi Technology, China) with its back to the experimenter, and its activity trajectories were recorded for 5 min. At the end of each trial, feces were removed from the bottom of the box, and the surface was sprayed with 75% alcohol and wiped dry with clean gauze to eliminate residual odors that could influence subsequent trials. A reduction in the time spent and distance traveled by the animals in the central area of the open field was interpreted as an increase in anxiety levels.

Elevated plus-maze test (EMPT)

The elevated plus-maze test is a well-established method for evaluating anxiety-like behavior in rodents, exploiting their inherent conflict between exploratory behavior and fear of heights [22]. Prior to the experiment, the apparatus(35 cm × 5 cm, Zhongshi Technology, China) was thoroughly cleaned and checked to ensure it was free of odors and debris, particularly feces left by previous mice. The number, date, and condition of the mice were meticulously recorded. Experimental animals were transported to the behavioral laboratory and allowed to acclimate to the environment for approximately 3 h to minimize stress. Each mouse was then gently placed at the intersection of the open and closed arms, facing the open arm with its back to the experimenter. The mouse’s activity was recorded over a 5-min period. After the test, the mouse was carefully returned to its home cage, and the apparatus was wiped with 75% ethanol and dried with paper towels to eliminate any residual odors. The time spent by the mice in the open arms was recorded and analyzed as an indicator of anxiety-like behavior.

Tail suspension test (TST)

Prior to the experiment, animals were acclimated to the experimental environment for 1 h. In a standardized experimental box (50 cm × 50 cm × 60 cm, Zhongshi Technology, China), the mice were suspended by fixing the distal 1/3 or 3/4 of their tails to the hooks using medical tape. The mice were hung with their heads downward, with the tips of their noses approximately 30 cm from the bottom of the box. Opaque baffles were used to separate the animals from each other to prevent visual interaction. The total duration of the experiment was 6 min, during which the immobility time of the mice was recorded in the final 4 min. A longer immobility time was indicative of a more severe degree of depression-like behavior.

Rotarod test (RT)

To assess the motor coordination and balance abilities of mice, we conducted the rotarod test(Ugo Basile, Italy). The mice underwent a two-day training regimen. On the first day, the rotational speed of the rotarod was set at 5 rpm for 5 min. On the second day, the speed was increased to 40 rpm for 5 min. Training was conducted four times per day, twice in the morning and twice in the afternoon, with a minimum interval of 30 min between sessions. During training, if a mouse fell off the rod, it was immediately placed back on the rod to continue the training session.

On the third day, the testing phase commenced. The rotational speed was set at 40 rpm with a time limit of 5 min. We recorded the duration that each mouse remained on the rod and the distance it traveled during this period. After each experimental trial, the rotarod was thoroughly wiped with a 75% alcohol spray to eliminate any residual odors from previous mice, ensuring a consistent testing environment.

Y-maze test

The Y-maze test leverages the innate exploratory behavior of mice to assess spatial recognition memory [23]. The Y-maze apparatus(35 cm × 5 cm × 15 cm, Zhongshi Technology, China) consists of three identical arms labeled A, B, and C, with each arm forming a 120° angle with the others. Mice were placed at the distal end of arm A, facing the center of the maze, and allowed to explore the maze freely for a period of 5 min. During this time, the sequence and frequency of entries into each arm were meticulously recorded. At the conclusion of each trial, feces were removed from the maze floor, which was then sprayed with 75% alcohol and wiped dry with clean gauze to eliminate any residual odors that could influence subsequent trials.

Immunofluorescence staining

Under anesthesia, mice were subjected to cardiac perfusion, and their brain tissues were fixed in 4% formaldehyde. Subsequently, the tissues underwent gradient dehydration using 15% and 30% sucrose solutions, respectively. After embedding with an appropriate embedding agent, 35-μm-thick frozen sections were prepared at -20°C.

For immunofluorescence staining, sections were first blocked with blocking solution in a well plate and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The primary antibody (diluted 1:200) was then added, and the sections were incubated at 4°C overnight. Following this, the sections were washed 3–5 times with PBS, each time for 5 min. The corresponding secondary antibody was added, and the sections were incubated at room temperature for 1 h, followed by three or more 5-min PBS washes.

The brain slices were carefully transferred to a clean slide, and excess water around the slices was gently removed using filter paper. DAPI antifluorescence quencher was added, and a coverslip was gently applied to avoid air bubbles. The stained brain slices were observed under a fluorescence microscope, photographed, and stored at 4°C for further analysis.

The primary antibodies used were GFAP (EMD Millipore, 3,380,386, Mouse), Iba1 (Abcam, ab178847, Rabbit), MAP2 (Abcam, ab32454, Rabbit), DCX (Cell Signaling Technology, 91,954, Rabbit), NeuN (Cell Signaling Technology, 94,403, Mouse), Cleaved Caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technology, 9664, Rabbit). The secondary antibodies were Alexa 488 (Jackson Immuno Research, 715–546-150) and Cy3 (Jackson Immuno Research, 711–165-152). The average fluorescence intensity (IOD/Area) was analyzed using ImageJ software.

High-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq)

Tissue samples were subjected to total RNA extraction using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 10,296,010), strictly following the manufacturer’s protocol. The purity and concentration of the extracted RNA were subsequently determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). For library construction, the TruSeq Stranded mRNA LT Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) was employed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis were performed by Majorbio Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sequencing was carried out on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. The raw sequencing data (fastq format) were initially processed with Trimmomatic to remove low-quality reads, thereby obtaining high-quality clean reads for downstream analysis.

Gene Ontology (GO) Annotation: Enrichment analysis was conducted using GOATOOLS (v0.6.10). Statistical significance was assessed via Fisher’s exact test. To control for multiple testing, four correction methods were applied: Bonferroni, Holm-Sidak, Sidak single-step, and False Discovery Rate (FDR). Terms with adjusted p < 0.05 (FDR-corrected) were considered significantly enriched.

KEGG Pathway Analysis: Enrichment was performed using SciPy (v1.5.2). Fisher’s exact test identified enriched pathways, with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction. Pathways with FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 were retained.

Visualization: Significantly enriched GO term and KEGG pathways were plotted as bubble charts.

Cell culture

Mouse microglial BV2 cells and astrocyte C8-D1A cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO₂ Cells were passaged when they reached 80%–90% confluence.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from BV2 cells, C8-D1A cells, or brain tissue samples using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) via chloroform extraction and isopropanol precipitation, followed by reverse transcription. qRT-PCR was performed on a ViiA7 PCR system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA) using SYBR Green PCR master mix (Applied TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). Each amplification cycle included an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min, and 72°C for 30 s. The expression levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, CD86, CD32, iNOS, ARG1, CD206, C3d, S100A10, BDNF, VEGF, ZO-1, and Bcl-2 were normalized to internal reference genes using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH and β-actin serving as internal controls. The primer sequences used for these analyses are provided in Supplementary Table 1 in the Additional file and were designed and synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Bioengineering Co.

Western blot

Cells were harvested on ice using lysis buffer (RIPA), vortexed thoroughly, and lysed on ice for 40 min. The lysates were then centrifuged at 4°C and 12,000 r/min for 10 min, and the supernatant was transferred to a clean 1.5 mL tube. After determination of the concentration, BCA protein Quantification kit (Thermo Fisher 23,225) was used, 30 μg of protein was mixed with 4 × sample buffer and boiled for 10 min. The samples were then subjected to SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis(Tanon 5200) for protein separation.

The separated proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane via electrotransfer. The membrane was blocked in TBS buffer containing 5% milk at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies against BDNF (Thermo Fisher 500-P84-1MG, Rabbit, 1:1000), VEGF(Abcam ab46154, Rabbit, 1:1000), ZO-1(Thermo Fisher 61–7300, Rabbit, 1:1000), Occludin(Abcam ab216327, Rabbit, 1:1000), Bax(Cell Signaling Technology 2772, Rabbit, 1:1000), Bcl-2(Cell Signaling Technology 2870, Rabbit, 1:1000), PSD95(Cell Signaling Technology 3450, Rabbit, 1:1000), Synaptotagmin(Synaptic Systems 105 103, Mouse, 1:1000), β-actin(Sigma-Aldrich A5441, Mouse, 1:1000), and GAPDH (ABclonal, Rabbit, 1:1000) at 4°C overnight. On the following day, the PVDF membrane was washed three times with TBS buffer. The membrane was then incubated with diluted secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. After another three washes with TBS buffer, the protein bands were visualized using a gel imaging system.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. For behavioral tests, two-way ANOVA was employed to compare outcomes across groups. In anatomical and biochemical analyses, comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using either one-way or two-way ANOVA, depending on the experimental design. Normality and homogeneity are conducted prior to ANOVA. Post hoc analysis was performed using the Bonferroni correction to identify significant differences between specific groups. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired t-tests were used. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the figures, statistical significance is denoted by asterisks: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

1064nm LED treatment ameliorates anxiety and depression-like behaviors and enhances spatial memory in mice with TBI

To investigate the therapeutic effects of 1064 nm LED treatment on TBI, a controlled cortical impact model was established in 8- to 10-week-old C57BL/6 N mice. 1064nm LED transcranial PBM was performed after TBI and a series of behavioral tests were carried out (Fig. 1A).

B Tail suspension test revealed that immobility time was significantly reduced in the TBI + PBM group compared to the TBI group. C Rotarod test showed that Log(mean fall time) was shortened following TBI and prolonged after PBM treatment. D–K Open field test and heatmaps on days 3 (D, E), 10 (H, I), and 15 (J, K) and elevated plus-maze test and heatmaps on day 4 (F, G) demonstrated that mice in the TBI + PBM group explored the center and open areas for longer durations and traveled greater distances compared to the TBI grou. L-M Y-maze test and heatmap on day 17 indicated that the alternation rate was significantly higher in the TBI + PBM group compared to the TBI group, while no significant differences were observed in the total number of arm entries or average speed among the three groups. n = 12. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, ns: not significant

The tail suspension test revealed that mice in the TBI + PBM group exhibited significantly shorter immobility times compared to those in the TBI group (Fig. 1B). Motor coordination was evaluated using the rotarod test. Although no significant differences in average movement speed were observed among the three groups, the TBI + PBM group demonstrated a longer average latency to fall compared to TBI mice (Fig. 1C). Results from the open field test and elevated plus-maze test showed that mice significantly reduced their exploration of the central and open areas on day 3 post-TBI (Fig. 1D–G). Following PBM treatment, mice spent more time and traveled greater distances in the central and open areas, with these effects persisting on days 10 (Fig. 1H, I) and 15 (Fig. 1J, K). Y-maze test results indicated that PBM-treated mice exhibited a higher rate of alternation compared to those in the TBI group (Fig. 1L, M). Collectively, these findings reveal that PBM treatment can alleviate anxiety and depression-like behavior, improve spatial cognitive dysfunction after TBI.

1064nm LED treatment effectively suppresses neuronal apoptosis and promotes brain tissue repair following TBI

To further examine the effect of PBM on injured brain tissue, murine brains from the three groups were collected at different days. As shown in Fig. 2A, 1064nm LED treatment significantly reduced the size and area of the brain injury region, with nearly complete recovery to pre-injury levels evident by day 54 (Fig. 2A). On day 4 post-injury, brain tissue samples around the injury region were collected for quantitative RT-PCR and Western blot analyses. These analyses revealed that the expression levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) were significantly higher in the TBI + PBM group compared to the TBI group (Fig. 2B–D).

On day 4 following TBI, immunofluorescence staining of brain tissue sections revealed colocalization of cleaved caspase-3 with NeuN, indicating that apoptosis mediated by cleaved caspase-3 occurred specifically in neurons. In the PBM treatment group, the count of cleaved Caspase-3 positive cells in neurons was significantly reduced (Fig. 2E). Western blot analysis demonstrated a significant downregulation of the blood–brain barrier (BBB)-associated proteins ZO-1 and occludin, as well as the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, accompanied by upregulation of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax on day 4 post-TBI. These changes were effectively reversed by PBM treatment (Fig. 2G). Moreover, these differential expression patterns remained evident on day 14 (Fig. 2H). Consistent with the protein-level findings, qRT-PCR analysis on day 14 post-TBI showed that PBM significantly decreased Bax mRNA expression and increased Bcl-2 transcript levels (Fig. 2I). Additionally, ZO-1 mRNA levels were significantly higher in the PBM group compared to the TBI group on both day 4 and day 14 (Fig. 2K, L).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PBM treatment effectively promotes the repair of the blood–brain barrier and damaged brain tissue while reducing neuronal apoptosis following TBI.

1064nm LED treatment promotes neurogenesis and synaptic remodeling after TBI

Hippocampal neurogenesis is thought to play a crucial role in certain types of learning and memory. To examine the effect of PBM on neurogenesis, we used doublecortin (DCX) to label developing and migrating neurons. The fluorescence intensity of DCX was significantly stronger in the TBI + PBM group than in the TBI group (Fig. 3A, B).

Neuronal death and diffuse axonal injury are frequent complications following TBI. Microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) is a well-established dendritic marker widely used to assess neuronal injury. To quantify mature neurons, we performed MAP2 immunostaining in the injury zone and hippocampus on days 4 and 14 post-injury, respectively (Fig. 3C–H). On days 4 and 14, the intensity of MAP2 staining in both the injury area and hippocampus was significantly reduced in the TBI group compared to the Sham group. In contrast, the fluorescence intensity of MAP2 staining in the TBI + PBM -treated group was comparable to that of the Sham group. Additionally, Western blot analysis was performed to detect the expression of the synaptic proteins PSD95 and Synaptotagmin. The results revealed increased expression of both PSD95 and Synaptotagmin in the PBM group compared to the TBI group (Fig. 3C). Collectively, these results suggest that PBM treatment promotes neurogenesis and synaptic remodeling following TBI.

1064nm LED treatment enhances the expression of genes associated with anti-inflammatory pathways in the cortex and hippocampus

To explore the mechanism of PBM promoting injury recovery, brain tissues from the peri-injured area and hippocampal region were separately collected from mice in the TBI + PBM and TBI groups for bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis. A substantial number of differentially expressed genes were identified between the two groups. Gene ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 4A–F) revealed significant differences in the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, NF-κB signaling pathway, TNF-α signaling pathway, and IL-17 signaling pathway between the TBI + PBM and TBI groups. Heatmaps (Fig. 4G–J) further illustrated the distinct patterns of upregulated and downregulated genes in these pathways within the cortex of the TBI + PBM and TBI groups. We verified the differentially expressed genes in the above four signaling pathways by qRT-PCR experiments, and the trend was basically consistent with the sequencing results (Fig. S2A–U). These findings suggest that the therapeutic effects of PBM on mood and cognitive deficits following TBI may be attributed to its ability to modulate neuroinflammatory responses.

1064nm LED treatment suppresses microglial hyperactivation, facilitates the phenotypic shift from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 polarization, and attenuates pro-inflammatory cytokine production

TBI is typically associated with the proliferation of reactive microglia. To assess microglial activation, we labeled microglia in brain tissue using Iba1. Immunofluorescence staining on day 4 revealed a significant reduction in Iba1 expression in the injury area of the TBI + PBM group compared to the TBI group (Fig. 5A, B). We further explored these effects through in vitro experiments (Fig. 5C). BV2 cells were cultured and stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL) to mimic the inflammatory environment of TBI. The TBI + PBM group received 1064 nm LED light at a power density of 12 mW/cm2, administered 12 h after LPS stimulation, with a second exposure 12 h later. Results showed that PBM significantly suppressed the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in BV2 cells (Fig. 5D–F).

To determine whether 1064nm LED treatment affects the phenotypic switch between M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory) microglia, we conducted quantitative RT-PCR analysis. The levels of M1-type microglia markers (CD32, CD86, and iNOS) were significantly higher in the TBI group and significantly lower in the PBM group (Fig. 6G–I). In contrast, M2 markers (ARG1 and CD206) were significantly higher in the PBM-treated group compared to the untreated TBI group (Fig. 6J, K). These findings suggest that 1064nm LED treatment modulates microglial polarization towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype, which may contribute to its neuroprotective effects.

1064nm LED treatment suppresses the activation of neurotoxic A1-type reactive astrocytes and facilitates their phenotypic conversion to the neuroprotective A2 phenotype following TBI

Previous studies reported that neurotoxic reactive astrocytes can be activated by microglia [24]. To further investigate the effects of PBM on astrocyte activation and the phenotypic shift between A1 (neurotoxic) and A2 (neuroprotective) astrocytes, we labeled astrocytes in brain tissues from the Sham, TBI, and TBI + PBM groups using GFAP. Immunofluorescence staining and intensity quantification revealed that astrocytes in the TBI group exhibited enlarged cell bodies, increased branching, and elevated GFAP expression levels in both the injury area and hippocampus on day 4. In contrast, GFAP fluorescence intensity in the TBI + PBM group was significantly lower than that in the TBI group and comparable to the Sham group (Fig. 5A–D).

We also conducted in vitro experiments on the astrocyte cell line C8-D1A (Fig. 5E). Cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) to induce inflammation, and the PBM group received 1064 nm LED light at a power density of 12 mW/cm2, administered 12 h after LPS stimulation, with a second exposure 24 h later. Results showed that PBM significantly inhibited the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in C8-D1A cells (Fig. 5F–H).

To explore the effects of PBM on astrocyte phenotypes, we performed quantitative RT-PCR analysis. The expression of C3d, a marker for neurotoxic A1-type astrocytes, was significantly decreased in the PBM group compared to the TBI group (Fig. 6I). Conversely, TBI induced a mild increase in the expression of S100A10, a marker for neuroprotective A2-type astrocytes, which was further enhanced by PBM treatment (Fig. 6J). These findings indicate that PBM treatment reduces the expression of pro-inflammatory markers and increases the expression of anti-inflammatory markers in astrocytes, suggesting a shift towards a more neuroprotective phenotype.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that 1064 nm light effectively alleviated mood and cognitive deficits and facilitated the repair of brain structures following traumatic brain injury. These therapeutic effects may be attributed to the modulation of astrocyte and microglial polarization towards a neuroprotective phenotype, as well as the significant suppression of neuroinflammation (Fig. 7).

Schematic representation of the effect of 1064nm LED treatment on traumatic brain injury. The light treatment promotes blood–brain barrier recovery, inhibits the activation of astrocytes and microglia, and induces their polarization towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype, thereby facilitating neuronal protection

PBM therapy has emerged as a promising non-invasive approach for various brain disorders, including ischemic stroke, Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, depression, and anxiety [5, 6, 25–28]. PBM likely exerts its effects through multiple mechanisms, with red (600–700 nm) and near-infrared (NIR, 760–1200 nm) wavelengths being most commonly used [4, 29, 30]. For example, Chao et al. [31] demonstrated that transnasal 810 nm LED light combined with transcranial PBM increased brain volume, cerebral perfusion, and functional connectivity in patients with mild traumatic brain injury. Additionally, an 830 nm laser effectively alleviated cognitive and behavioral deficits in a mouse model of autism [5], highlighting the importance of specific wavelengths in PBM. Compared to traditional pharmacological therapies, PBM is non-invasive and has minimal side effects. A single-center randomized controlled trial (RCT) with follow-up assessments found no adverse events related to PBM and observed lower Rivermead Post-Concussion Symptom Questionnaire (RPQ) scores in treated individuals [32], underscoring its safety and feasibility as a therapeutic option.

The 1064 nm wavelength penetrates deeper brain tissues more efficiently than other wavelengths due to reduced scattering and absorption by hemoglobin and water. This enhances modulation of subcortical and hippocampal regions crucial for cognition and emotion, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy [5, 32, 33]. In contrast, 810 nm light may require higher energy densities for similar outcomes. Additionally, the scattering properties of LEDs provide wider coverage with lower thermal risk, making them suitable for long-term treatments [34, 35]. These characteristics also enhance the flexibility and tolerability of 1064 nm PBM. We innovatively applied 1064 nm LED to TBI, demonstrating its efficacy in improving anxiety, depression-like mood, and spatial cognition in TBI mice. This provides a novel basis for wavelength selection in PBM for TBI and a promising direction for exploring its mechanisms.

Further supporting the observed attenuation of neuroinflammation and beneficial glial polarization, transcriptomic profiling (Fig. 4B–F) revealed significant downregulation of key pro-inflammatory pathways—specifically, the NOD-like receptor, NF-κB, TNF-α, and IL-17 signaling cascades—in the TBI + PBM group compared to TBI-only controls, across both cortical and hippocampal regions. NOD-like receptor signaling, often mediated via NLRP3 inflammasome activation, aggravates neuroinflammation by enhancing IL-1β maturation, thereby perpetuating glial activation and neuronal injury [36, 37]. In our studies, PBM downregulated this pathway, reduced levels of the A1 astrocyte marker C3d, and increased the A2 marker S100A10, supporting the inference that NLRP3 inhibition underlies the attenuation of neurotoxic astrocytic polarization. Similarly, NF-κB serves as a master regulator of neuroinflammation, promoting M1 microglial polarization and the release of cytokines including IL-1β and TNF-α [36]. Consistent with this, 1064 nm LED irradiation inhibited NF-κB activation, concurrently elevating BDNF and the M2 marker ARG1, implying a pivotal role for NF-κB suppression in facilitating M2 transition and synaptic remodeling. The TNF-α pathway amplifies inflammatory cascades and is linked to blood–brain barrier dysfunction and apoptosis [38]. We found that PBM significantly suppressed TNF-α and its downstream effector, while restoring expression of the synaptic proteins PSD95, indicating that TNF-α inhibition contributes to neuroprotection and synaptic recovery. Moreover, IL-17 signaling—implicated in chronic neuroinflammation and poor TBI outcomes [39]—was inhibited by 1064 nm PBM, which also enhanced levels of the tight-junction proteins ZO-1 and occlaudin. This suggests that PBM reinforces BBB integrity partly through curbing IL-17–mediated neuroinflammation and peripheral immune infiltration. Collectively, the coordinated suppression of these inflammatory hubs by 1064 nm PBM provides a molecular basis for the shift toward neuroprotective glial phenotypes, reduced neuroinflammation, and subsequent improvements in affective and cognitive behaviors.

Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that the M1 polarization of microglia is intrinsically linked to the pathogenesis of depression. This process exerts detrimental effects on neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity by releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby culminating in cognitive deficits and the manifestation of depressive symptoms [28]. In a similar vein, Michaela et al. [40] revealed that under pathological conditions such as inflammation, astrocytes can undergo reactive alterations, losing their neurotrophic capabilities and instead secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines. These cytokines further exacerbate neuroinflammation in the brain, ultimately precipitating depressive-like behaviors. Moreover, an imbalance in microglial polarization has been shown to be closely associated with cognitive decline in Alzheimer’s disease mouse models. Neuroinflammation impairs neuronal function and synaptic plasticity, ultimately leading to cognitive impairments [38, 39, 41]. In our study, we observed that 1064 nm LED treatment could effectively promote the conversion of microglia from the M1 phenotype to the M2 phenotype and astrocytes from the A1 phenotype to the A2 phenotype. This shift in glial cell polarization may underlie the significant improvements in mood and cognition observed following light exposure.

While previous studies have elucidated some mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of PBM in the treatment of TBI [36, 37], the precise mechanisms of action remain incompletely understood. Notably, recent research has demonstrated that near-infrared light can enhance meningeal lymphoid drainage in AD mouse models, thereby facilitating the clearance of β-amyloid and improving cognitive function in aged mice [42]. This finding suggests that the cognitive-enhancing effects of PBM in our study may be attributable to its modulation of the immune and metabolic systems within the brain. Furthermore, Wu et al. [43] reported that the neuroprotective effects of PBM may be associated with the lung-brain axis. In an in vitro model of spinal cord injury, 810 nm laser irradiation was shown to promote the secretion of various neurotrophic factors by activating the PKA-CREB signaling pathway in macrophages, ultimately facilitating axonal regeneration [44]. In our investigation, we observed that light exposure significantly enhanced neurogenesis, reduced apoptosis of brain cells, and promoted synaptic remodeling. These findings suggest that light-induced structural repair of brain tissue may be another crucial mechanism underlying the therapeutic effects of PBM.

Certainly, this study has several limitations that required consideration. First, we did not comprehensively investigate the specific molecular targets through which 1064 nm light treatment exerts its therapeutic effects. Second, our observations regarding the long-term therapeutic efficacy of PBM remain insufficient, particularly given that parameter optimization and dosage effects critically influence the therapeutic effects.

Conclusions

In summary, our data reveal that 14 days 1064 nm LED treatment can significantly inhibits the activation of astrocyte and microglial and induce their polarization towards a neuroprotective phenotype, and then facilitate brain tissue repair, alleviate mood and cognitive deficits following TBI. Our study provides a novel wavelength option and novel mechanistic insights for the application of PBM in TBI treatment.
