Tailoring dysphagia-friendly 3D printed starch-based gels: olive oil modulation of lotus root-whey protein inks
Authors: Jinfeng Wang, Xueyu Jiang, Houze Gan, Siyu Li, Hongxun Wang, Yang Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstactDeveloping dysphagia-oriented 3D-printed foods requires balancing extrudability during printing and shape retention after deposition while maintaining macronutrient balance. This study proposes a composite ink system combining whole lotus root powder, whey protein, and an unsaturated liquid oil phase to explore lipid-mediated modulation of printability and texture. Oil incorporation reduced apparent viscosity and altered viscoelastic responses, revealing a formulation-dependent trade-off between flowability and structural integrity. Based on an integrated evaluation of printing fidelity and texture performance, a printable-and-swallowable formulation window was identified, with the formulations at solid-to-oil ratios of 10:0 and 10:1 exhibiting similarly low printing deviations and improved water-holding capacity. Multi-technique characterization indicated a predominantly amorphous matrix and a redistribution of bonding environments within aqueous domains, alongside droplet-matrix interactions that facilitated extrusion and post-printing structural evolution. The present results provide mechanistic insights and a preliminary quantitative basis for understanding how structural evolution relates to rheology, printability, and quality-related properties in dysphagia-oriented 3D-printed foods.

Dysphagia, defined as a pathological condition that compromises the safety and efficiency of food transport from the oral cavity to the stomach due to impairment in swallowing-related physiological components, has emerged as a significant public health concern1. Epidemiological evidence indicates a rising prevalence of dysphagia with global population aging, substantially impacting quality of life and nutritional status in the elderly population1. Current estimates suggest that approximately one in thirteen individuals worldwide experiences swallowing impairment. In practice, dysphagia-oriented food products are commonly discussed in three broad groups. These include viscosity-modifying thickeners (e.g., dextrin- or starch-based systems) designed to reduce aspiration risk through rheological control, nutritionally enriched swallow-safe foods (such as jellies or puddings), and texture-adapted foods (including minced or pureed products) intended to facilitate oral processing and swallowing2,3. The international medical and scientific community has progressively recognized the importance of dysphagia-appropriate food products, with numerous national health authorities now formally recommending their incorporation into therapeutic dietary regimens for affected populations2.

Current dysphagia food formulations face significant limitations in ingredients and textures, resulting in restricted variety and diminished visual appeal4. Emerging 3D printing technology offers a promising solution for developing innovative food products5, enabling the preservation of texture-modified characteristics while enhancing visual presentation6. For instance, 3D printing has been used to create visually appealing dysphagia-friendly foods using red bean paste-filled green dumplings as the base material7. Additionally, high-precision, customized-shape foods for children with dysphagia have been produced using composite edible inks formulated from corn and flaxseed proteins, with fenugreek gum incorporated as a functional additive8. The nutritional implications of dysphagia are particularly concerning, as impaired swallowing frequently led to inadequate nutrient intake, potentially exacerbating swallowing dysfunction through malnutrition - establishing a detrimental cycle. Although 3D printing has successfully addressed visual and textural challenges, current formulations still exhibit nutritional limitations, necessitating complementary dietary interventions to ensure adequate nutrient diversity and quantity4.

Whole lotus root powder (WL), produced through processes such as washing, peeling, drying, and grinding, retains the starch of lotus root along with small amounts of protein, fat, carbohydrates, vitamins, minerals, and dietary fiber. WL is highly nutritious, easy to digest, and convenient to consume9,10. Beyond its health benefits, such as antioxidant, anti-tumor, and coronary heart disease prevention properties, WL also plays a significant role in improving gut health. Its applications extend beyond final products to serve as a base material for various lotus root-based foods. The gelatinization properties of WL enable formation of viscoelastic gels suitable for advanced food manufacturing applications, though its potential in 3D printing and dysphagia-specific formulations remains underexplored. Whey protein (WP), a premium byproduct-derived protein fraction, has transitioned from waste stream to high-value ingredient, exhibiting exceptional digestibility and metabolic efficiency. WP has been widely applied in the food industry due to its high nutritional value, favorable digestibility, and desirable functional properties, including gelation, emulsification, and water-binding capacity11. Upon heating, WP can unfold and expose reactive groups, which promotes intermolecular aggregation through hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and disulfide bond formation, thereby enabling the formation of a stable three-dimensional gel network. In mixed starch-protein systems, these interactions further contribute to structural reinforcement, water retention, and mechanical stability12. WP can form structurally stable gels with WL, enhancing mechanical strength, water retention, and thermodynamic stability13. Previous studies have reported that pH-modulated composite gels based on whey protein isolate and lotus root amylopectin successfully encapsulate vitamin D3, thereby improving its bioavailability14. Although this reported system differs from the WL-WP system used in the present study, it supports the potential of lotus-derived starch fractions and whey proteins in forming functional composite gels. Olive oil (OO) is a widely consumed food-grade vegetable oil derived from olive fruits, and olive cultivation in the Mediterranean dates back several millennia15. The fatty acids in OO mainly include oleic acid, palmitic acid, linoleic acid, and linolenic acid, with oleic acid being the most abundant at 55–83%. Clinical studies confirm its cholesterol-modulating effects and cardiovascular risk reduction, while its nutraceutical components contribute to broader health and dermatological benefits.

Consequently, the development of dysphagia-appropriate foods must simultaneously address three critical parameters: compliance with international texture modification standards, enhanced visual appeal, and comprehensive nutritional composition, with particular emphasis on incorporating balanced proportions of carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids as fundamental macronutrients for energy provision and physiological maintenance. However, current 3D food printing studies still lack predictive formulation-design principles that explicitly link microstructure and rheological behavior to printability and dysphagia-relevant quality attributes. As a result, formulation development remains largely empirical, limiting the rational design of nutritionally balanced and swallowing-appropriate printed foods. We hypothesize that the incorporation of OO will reorganize hydrogen bonds and promote molecular disorder, thereby facilitating the formation of a denser amorphous matrix and smoothing nanoscale interfaces, ultimately synergistically enhancing the macro-scale printability of the composite system. To address nutritional needs and explore broader possibilities for 3D-printed dysphagia-friendly foods, this study employs basic, widely available ingredients to construct a model matrix for dysphagia-oriented food boluses (Fig. 1). Guided by the IDDSI framework, the study investigates the rheological and textural properties of dysphagia food boluses with varying proportions of OO added to the WL-WP composite system. It explores the impact of OO on the 3D printing performance of whole lotus root powder and whey protein composite gels (WL-WPG) and the types of interactions within the WL-WP-OO system.Fig. 13D printing schematic diagram of WL-WPG and WL-WP-OOG.

These findings should be interpreted as system-specific rather than as a universally validated design framework, and broader validation across oils with different physicochemical properties will require future study.

Results and discussion

Rheological properties

Rheological properties play a pivotal role in evaluating the 3D printing performance and stability of food inks. The rheological characteristics of WL-WP-OOG were analyzed through frequency sweep and shear rate sweep tests (Fig. 2a–c). Both storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G”) demonstrated frequency-dependent behavior, with G’ values consistently dominating G” across the tested frequency range (0.1–100 rad/s), confirming the predominant elastic character of the composite gels. The increase in G’ with frequency indicates that the WL-WP network behaves more elastically under rapid deformation, as polymer chains have insufficient time to relax, which is characteristic of physically crosslinked starch-protein gels and favorable for extrusion-based 3D printing. Notably, the 10:0 WL-WP-OOG exhibited the maximum G’ value, indicating superior shape retention capacity critical for maintaining dimensional accuracy in 3D printed structures. Conversely, the 10:4 WL-WP-OOG sample showed markedly reduced G’, potentially compromising structural integrity during layer-by-layer deposition. The inverse correlation between olive oil content and system viscosity (from 1458 Pa s at 0.1 s−1 shear rate) suggests OO’s plasticizing effect on the starch-protein matrix. While low viscosity improves the fluidity of the material, it also makes it difficult for the material to support the 3D structure during printing. Therefore, appropriate viscosity is essential for successful 3D printing. All formulations exhibited pronounced shear-thinning behavior, facilitating smooth extrusion while maintaining post-deposition stability—a rheological signature ideal for 3D food printing applications16,17. As the OO content increased, the apparent viscosity decreased progressively, which is more plausibly attributed to lubrication and dilution effects introduced by liquid oil droplets, together with a reduction in the continuity of the WL-WP continuous phase that bears stress. In emulsion-filled biopolymer networks, dispersed oil droplets can facilitate flow under shear while simultaneously weakening post-deposition structural integrity when the droplet fraction becomes excessive, thereby narrowing the printable window. At higher OO contents, localized association and rearrangement of starch chains at the oil-biopolymer interface may occur. Three interval thixotropy test (3ITT) (Fig. 2d) and stress sweep tests (Fig. 2e) were jointly employed to elucidate the deformation-recovery behavior of WL-WP-OOG under printing-relevant shear conditions. Here, the yield stress was defined as the crossover stress (G’ = G”) from the oscillatory sweep test. The corresponding G’ in the linear viscoelastic region (LVR G’) and yield stress values were summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The crossover point marks the onset of the transition from elastic-dominant to viscous-dominant behavior, thus reflecting the resistance of the gel network to deformation-induced breakdown. The 10:0 exhibited the highest G’ in the linear viscoelastic region (1781.20 Pa) and the highest yield stress (802.00 Pa), indicating the strongest initial network strength and the greatest resistance to deformation-induced breakdown. Without an oil phase, the starch-protein matrix acted as the sole load-bearing framework. A relatively high applied stress was required to trigger large-scale structural breakdown, consistent with the behavior of physically crosslinked biopolymer gels reported in previous studies19. With increasing OO content, both the LVR G’ and the yield stress decreased progressively, indicating that OO weakened the continuous starch-protein network and made the gels more prone to deformation. At low oil contents (10:1 and 10:2), oil droplets acted as dispersed fillers embedded within the WL-WP matrix, leading to moderate decreases in LVR G’ and yield stress. Although oil droplets inherently interrupted the gel network and introduced structural defects20, adsorbed proteins at the oil-water interface participated in interfacial interactions and formed additional physical junctions. This partially compensated for network disruption and increased structural heterogeneity, as commonly observed in protein-stabilized emulsion gels21. Under the competing effects of gel-phase interruption and interfacial reinforcement, the net mechanical strength of these samples decreased relative to the oil-free system.

At intermediate oil contents, shear-induced structural damage involved simultaneous breakdown of polymer junction zones and oil-biopolymer interfacial structures. This dual-mode failure resulted in pronounced thixotropic behavior during 3ITT testing, as evidenced by the lowest recovery ratios (28.10% for 10:1 and 29.26% for 10:2). Although these samples had relatively higher LVR G’ and yield stress than oil-rich samples, reconstruction of the heterogeneous gel-emulsion network after shear was kinetically hindered as it required re-establishing both bulk gel connectivity and interfacial structures. Consequently, structural recovery was slow and incomplete, reflecting strong time-dependent behavior rather than insufficient elastic strength. In contrast, at high oil contents (10:3 and 10:4), excessive oil diluted the effective concentration of the WL-WP gel matrix and exceeded its emulsification and load-bearing capacity. Droplet aggregation occurred, leading to the loss of the active filler effect and severe weakening of the continuous network, which was manifested by substantially reduced LVR G’ and yield stress22. Correspondingly, these samples exhibited the highest apparent recovery ratios in 3ITT tests (48.18% and 52.93%). However, this rapid viscosity recovery primarily reflected facile rearrangement of a weak and loosely connected structure rather than true reconstruction of a mechanically robust gel network.

Overall, the combined stress sweep and 3ITT results demonstrate that structural recovery behavior is intrinsically linked to the initial mechanical integrity and failure mode of the gel network. Strong, continuous networks resist deformation but recover slowly once disrupted, whereas weak, oil-diluted structures recover rapidly yet lack sufficient elastic strength to support stable 3D printed architectures. These findings highlight that high recovery ratios alone do not necessarily indicate superior printability and underscore the importance of balancing elastic strength, yielding behavior, and recovery kinetics in the design of emulsion-based food inks. These rheological results suggest that OO induces a formulation-dependent transition in viscoelastic behavior, which is expected to directly influence the printable window and shape fidelity of the system.

Printing precision test

The rheological properties of printing materials are critical determinants of printing precision. To evaluate the printing performance of WL-WP-OOG, hollow cylindrical structures were fabricated and their dimensional fidelity was analyzed. As shown in Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 2, height deviation increased with higher OO content. Notably, the 10:0 and 10:1 formulations exhibited minimal deviations (hd: 1.13 ± 0.598% and 1.43 ± 0.682%, respectively), suggesting comparable printability at these ratios. In contrast, the 10:3 and 10:4 samples exhibited significant tilting (hd: 3.35 ± 0.446% and 5.17 ± 1.146%, respectively), accompanied by structural collapse (Fig. 2g). This loss of dimensional stability indicated that excessive OO content compromised the material’s ability to retain its shape during printing, likely due to reduced viscosity and insufficient structural support. Rheological analysis confirmed that the 10:0 and 10:1 samples maintained optimal viscosity, ensuring excellent shape retention and printing fidelity.

Texture analysis

The morphological characteristics of printed samples indicated that OO may have a certain influence on the physical properties of the gel. As illustrated in Fig. 3a–d, texture analysis revealed progressive decreases in hardness, elasticity, chewiness, and adhesiveness with increasing OO content. The 10:0 sample demonstrated maximum hardness, indicating a denser gel network structure and superior mechanical stability23. Within the WL-WP-OOG system, OO functioned as a lubricating agent. Elevated OO concentrations promote the development of starch-protein-lipid complexes that formed insoluble surface films. This phenomenon disrupted critical hydrogen bonding between starch and protein components, ultimately compromising gel strength and network integrity24,25. The findings of this study further suggested that OO may interfere with the hydration processes of WL and WP, as well as the subsequent gel network formation, leading to reduced adhesiveness and chewiness. Mechanistically, OO incorporation was inferred to increase the porosity of the printed samples based on the observed rheological behavior and printing characteristics, thereby reducing the effective oral cavity contact area and interfacial adhesion and ultimately weakening mastication resistance26,27. Accordingly, the optimal OO concentration was identified to be in the range of 10:1–10:2, where adequate structural integrity was maintained while achieving desirable softness. At these levels, the printed products maintained sufficient supportability to prevent collapse and deformation while achieving desirable softness characteristics, suitable for producing 3D-printed products for specific populations, including individuals with dysphagia or infants. To further explore the interaction mechanisms of the components in WL-WP-OOG, more in-depth experimental characterization was required.

Water loss test

Given that texture changes in hydrogel-based printed foods are closely associated with water retention, the water-holding behavior of the printed gels was further evaluated. As shown in Fig. 3e, the water loss characteristics of the printed samples at 50 °C revealed an inverse correlation between OO content and moisture loss, with the 10:4 formulation exhibiting significantly lower water loss rates (0.15 ± 0.008% at 30 min and 0.29 ± 0.008% at 60 min) compared to the 10:0 sample (0.28 ± 0.007% at 30 min and 0.45 ± 0.009% at 60 min). This moisture retention effect could be attributed to the dual functionality of OO as both a hydrophobic barrier and moisturizing agent. Increasing OO content promoted the formation of a more continuous and uniform lipid phase that effectively impeded water evaporation through physical encapsulation28. The inherent hydrophobicity of OO reduced the system’s overall water affinity, contrasting sharply with the strong hydrophilic character of WL and WP in the 10:0 formulation that facilitated rapid water evaporation under thermal stress. Microscopically, OO could mediate water entrapment within the protein-starch matrix through interfacial interactions, thereby enhancing moisture retention29,30. These mechanisms collectively improved the water-holding capacity of WL-WPP systems, mitigating undesirable textural changes (clumping and hardening) while better preserving product morphology and quality during thermal exposure and storage. To explain how oil incorporation gives rise to the observed changes in rheology, printability, texture, and water retention, the structural evolution of the system was further investigated from the molecular to microscale levels.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The XRD patterns of WL-WP-OOP composites (Fig. 3f) showed that all gelatinized and printed samples exhibited a broad amorphous halo near 20°, confirming complete gelatinization and a fully amorphous state. With increasing OO content, the intensity and area of the broad amorphous halo increased, indicating enhanced structural disorder in the printed gels. This trend could be attributed to two cooperative effects of OO31,32. First, the dispersed oil droplets interrupt starch-protein and starch-starch associations, thereby suppressing short-range molecular ordering and hindering the reorganization of polymer chains into more ordered arrangements. Second, OO might occupy interstitial voids within the matrix, contributing to a denser yet less ordered amorphous structure. Therefore, OO promoted a compact amorphous matrix by disrupting ordered molecular packing and redistributing internal free volume within the gel network, rather than by inducing crystallization33,34.

Hydrogen bonding interactions in WL-WP-OOP

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis was employed to investigate hydrogen bonding interactions in WL-WP-OOP composites through characterization of chemical bond vibrations35,36. As shown in Fig. 4a, distinctive peaks emerged at 1747 cm−1 (C=O stretching vibration), 3011 cm−1 (unsaturated C-H stretching vibrations), and 2748 cm−1 (saturated C-H stretching vibrations) in OO-containing samples, with intensities proportional to OO content. Second-derivative deconvolution of the FTIR spectra highlighted changes in the C-O-H band (990 cm−1), which is commonly associated with the local environment of hydroxyl groups in starch. Compared to the 10:0 control, OO-containing samples showed a shift of this band to lower wavenumbers, indicating a redistributed hydrogen-bonding environment within the remaining aqueous domains37,38. Importantly, this spectral shift does not necessarily imply an enhanced long-range network integrity. Instead, together with the observed rheological trends, the results were consistent with a scenario where OO incorporation promotes heterogeneous redistribution of water and hydrophilic groups, while hydrophobic interactions and droplet-induced lubrication reduced the long-range connectivity of the WL-WP network. This combination could reconcile the apparent FTIR shift with the macroscopic decreased in viscosity and altered viscoelastic response. The slight reversal at the highest OO level suggests additional competitive interactions at the oil-biopolymer interface.

Amorphous and helical structures of WL-WP-OOP

The 13C CP/MAS NMR spectra of WL-WP-OOP exhibited four characteristic peaks with distinct chemical shifts (Fig. 4c). As detailed in Supplementary Table 3, the resonance at 95–105 ppm was particularly indicative of starch helical and amorphous structures. Spectral deconvolution enabled quantitative determination of amorphous structures, double helices, and single helices39,40. Here, “double helices” are interpreted as ordered helical motifs reflecting short-range order, which may still exist even when the bulk matrix is predominantly amorphous. With increasing OO content, the amorphous fraction increased markedly, accompanied by a reduction in ordered helical motifs, while single helices exhibited a non-monotonic trend. These results suggested that OO modulated starch chain organization and hydration. OO levels might favor transient helical organization at interfaces, whereas higher OO levels disrupted chain association and hindered reorganization, shifting the system toward a more disordered, amorphous state. Such molecular-level reorganization was consistent with improved flow during extrusion but reduced post-deposition mechanical integrity when OO was excessive.

Small-angle X-ray scattering analysis

The small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) analysis of WL-WP-OOP composites (Fig. 4d) revealed insights into the system’s nanostructure through electron density distribution analysis. The scattering exponent α was obtained by linearly fitting the SAXS data using the Power-Law model (I ~ q-α). According to fractal theory, an α > 3 indicates a surface fractal structure, with the fractal dimension calculated as Ds = 6 - α41. A higher Ds value corresponds to a smoother interfacial surface within the material. In this study, all α for WL-WP-OOP exceeded 3, confirming the typical surface fractal characteristics of the composites (Supplementary Table 4). Notably, the Ds exhibited an increasing trend with higher OO content. This suggests that OO, acting as a filler, effectively incorporates into the voids of WL-WP network, smoothing the internal interfaces and pore boundaries of the gel matrix and thereby reducing surface irregularities42. The resulting smoother interfacial surface facilitates easier extrusion during printing by reducing internal friction. Furthermore, this structural feature enhances the shear-thinning behavior of the material under shear force. These findings are consistent with the XRD results, which indicated increased molecular-level disorder and structural densification, collectively supporting the role of OO in promoting a more homogeneous and deformable composite structure. To connect the nanoscale structural evolution with directly observable morphology, the printed gels were further examined at the microscale using SEM and CLSM.

Microstructure of 3D-printed products

The microstructural characteristic of WL-WP-OOP composites were investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), as presented in Fig. 5a, b. SEM analysis revealed that OO content significantly influenced the gel network structure. The 10:0 and 10:1 WL-WP-OOP samples exhibited a continuous porous network resembling a honeycomb structure. However, with increasing OO content, the originally porous network of the samples was gradually replaced by a continuous, dense, and homogeneous amorphous structure. The cross-section of the 10:4 sample exhibited a dense, fused glassy morphology with significantly enhanced surface smoothness. This structural transition could be attributed to OO-mediated hydrophobic interactions with starch and protein components, which reduced available starch chains for three-dimensional network formation and weakened overall intermolecular interactions. The development of this layered configuration considerably compromised the structural integrity and support capacity of the network, ultimately leading to diminished printing accuracy. Based on the conclusions derived from FTIR spectroscopy, it is hypothesized that the formation of this fused structure may be attributed to the interference of OO with the intermolecular hydrogen bonds between starch molecules. A moderate fused structure facilitates smooth and complete filament extrusion, resulting in stable layer deposition and improved printing precision. However, excessive fused structure formation could undermine the stability of the porous structure, leading to the structural collapse of 3D-printed products43. To further quantify the network evolution observed by SEM, pore size distribution analysis was performed on the cross-sectional micrographs of samples (Supplementary Fig. 1). The histograms showed that the pore aperture distribution changed with increasing OO content, indicating that oil incorporation altered the internal network architecture and pore heterogeneity of the WL-WP matrix. In the low-oil formulations, more clearly resolved pore structures were observed. This finding was consistent with a more continuous load-bearing network, which corresponded to the relatively higher G’ and lower hd observed macroscopically. As the oil content increased, the pore structure became increasingly disrupted and less regular, consistent with reduced elastic support and poorer printing fidelity. Notably, the 10:4 sample was excluded from pore-size statistics because excessive oil addition led to a fused, compact morphology in which individual pore boundaries could no longer be reliably distinguished. This observation is mechanistically meaningful. Molecular docking simulation

To gain molecular-level insights into the interactions that underpin the observed macroscopic homogeneity and structural stability, molecular docking simulations were conducted. Given the compositional complexity of WL, a simplified model system was employed, focusing on the most prevalent components: the most abundant starch in WL and oleic acid, the most abundant fatty acid in OO, as representative molecules alongside WP. The simulation provides insights into pairwise affinity trends rather than predicting exact structural or rheological properties. This targeted approach aimed to probe the fundamental pairwise affinity between the key ingredients, providing a baseline understanding of their molecular compatibility.

The results revealed a binding energy of −4.7 kcal/mol between whey protein and the representative starch and oleic acid molecules, indicating the possibility of weak non-covalent association rather than strong or specific binding. Previous studies using molecular docking have similarly identified non‑covalent binding between whey protein and lipid or phospholipid ligands with energies of −5.59 to −7.85 kcal/mol, supporting the interpretation that moderate affinity interactions can contribute to compatibility and network formation in complex food gels46. As shown in Fig. 5c, starch molecules were depicted in yellow, oleic acid in green, and WP amino acids in blue. Oleic acid molecules formed hydrogen bonds with SER-40 and ARG-360 on WP and engaged in hydrophobic interactions with ASP47, THR-369, PHE-370, PHE-359, and ALA-45. Starch molecules established hydrogen bonds with GLN-286, ASP38, and THR-362 on WP and participated in hydrophobic interactions with PHE-363, PHE-287, and ASP-301. It is critical to clarify that this moderate binding energy alone does not imply the formation of a singular, rigid “ternary complex.” Instead, it signifies a predisposition for association at the molecular interface. In the dynamic, multi-component gel system, the cumulative effect of millions of such weak interactions, alongside contributions from other WL components and other fatty acids in OO, can collectively explain the uniform dispersion and lack of phase separation observed microscopically. These simulated interactions offer a plausible theoretical foundation for the compatibility and co-localization of WL, WP, and OO, which in turn modulates the macroscopic rheology and printability by influencing network formation and interfacial properties. Accordingly, the docking results serve as complementary molecular-level support rather than primary evidence for the macroscopic mechanism.

Correlation analysis and pairwise scatter plots

To provide a more explicit quantitative linkage among microstructure, rheology, printability, and product quality, a formulation-level Pearson correlation analysis was performed. This analysis used as variables Ds, G’ (1 rad/s), recovery ratio, height deviation, water loss at 60 min, and hardness (Supplementary Fig. 2). The representative formulation-level descriptors were summarized in Supplementary Table 5. The correlation map revealed that Ds was strongly negatively correlated with G’ (1 rad/s) and hardness, while showing a strong positive correlation with height deviation. These results indicated that increasing Ds reflected structural evolution associated with weakened elastic support and reduced printing fidelity. In contrast, G’ (1 rad/s) was strongly negatively correlated with height deviation and strongly positively correlated with hardness, highlighting elastic strength as a key intermediate descriptor linking structure to both printability and post-printing firmness. Notably, water loss at 60 min was positively correlated with G’ (1 rad/s) and hardness, but negatively correlated with Ds, suggesting a trade-off in the present system. Formulations with stronger network support exhibited higher moisture loss, whereas oil-induced structural evolution improved water retention but was accompanied by reduced mechanical robustness. In addition, recovery ratio was negatively correlated with G’ (1 rad/s) and hardness, but positively correlated with height deviation. This result suggested that, in the present system, a higher recovery ratio should not be directly interpreted as better printability. Instead, it likely reflected the rapid rearrangement of relatively weak, oil-rich structures after shear. The scatter plots further supported these relationships. G’ (1 rad/s) decreased as height deviation increased, confirming that reduced elastic support was associated with poorer shape retention during printing. Ds showed an inverse relationship with G’ (1 rad/s), indicating that the structural changes captured by SAXS were closely linked to rheological weakening. Moreover, hardness increased with increasing G’ (1 rad/s), indicating that rheological strength was coupled with post-printing textural firmness. Together, these results provided a quantitative summary of how oil-induced structural evolution propagates from the microstructural level to rheological behavior, and ultimately to printability and product quality. Given the limited number of formulations, this analysis was used as an exploratory tool to visualize trend consistency rather than as a fully validated predictive framework.

IDDSI testing

After establishing the structural and rheological basis of printability, the practical dysphagia relevance of the printed gels was further evaluated using IDDSI tests. IDDSI provides a globally standardized nomenclature and testing methods for identifying foods suitable for individuals with dysphagia47. To definitively categorize our printed samples, we performed the IDDSI fork drip, spoon tilt, and fork pressure tests, explicitly mapping the results to the official criteria for Level 5 (Minced and Moist). As illustrated in Fig. 6, the fork drip test indicated that the samples remained cohesive without dripping through the fork tines. The fork pressure test demonstrated that all printed samples could be easily crushed under light pressure, with no observable blanching on the fingertips. In the spoon tilt test, the samples exhibited sufficient internal cohesion to maintain their shape on the spoon, yet detached completely upon tilting with only minimal residue. Based on these observations, the WL-WP-OOP samples met the criteria for IDDSI Level 5 Minced and Moist within the IDDSI framework. Foods at this level required minimal chewing and were primarily transported by tongue movement, making them suitable for individuals with reduced chewing ability, such as those with missing teeth or poorly fitting dentures. Given the person-specific nature of dysphagia diets, no sensory evaluation was conducted in this study. Tribological properties

Figure 6b displays the friction curves for all samples. The results indicate that the control group without OO addition exhibited the highest friction coefficient, with its maximum (μfmax = 0.61) occurring at a VR of 10.17 mm/s. With the addition of OO, the μfmax of the samples decreased significantly, and its corresponding position shifted towards the lower velocity region. For instance, 10:1 reached its μfmax of 0.40 at a VR as low as 0.08 mm/s. This phenomenon is closely related to the differences in the lubrication mechanisms between the two systems. The gel without OO relies on aqueous-based lubrication. Its surface consists primarily of hydrophilic components, where at low shear rates, water at the contact interface is readily expelled, forming only an unstable thin water film, resulting in insufficient lubrication. As VR increases to approximately 10 mm/s, shear action transports more water to the interface, thickening the water film, but its stability remains poor; consequently, the friction coefficient peaks within this velocity range. In contrast, the system with added OO exhibits an oil-film-dominated lubrication mechanism. OO effectively fills the microscopic pores and rough interfaces within the gel network formed by WL and WP, thereby smoothing the bolus surface at a microscopic scale. More importantly, OO forms a pre-existing oil film on and within the gel. This oil film can separate the friction surfaces at the initial stage of contact, providing immediate boundary lubrication. Therefore, the μfmax appears during the startup phase (VR ≈ 1 mm/s). Once motion is initiated, as the speed increases, the oil film becomes more continuous and stable, and the system gradually transitions to a mixed or hydrodynamic lubrication regime, leading to a subsequent decrease in the friction coefficient48. Based on the tribological behaviors, the two types of gels exhibited clearly different lubrication characteristics. The oil-free system was dominated by water-based lubrication and showed a higher friction coefficient. In contrast, the OO-containing systems exhibited earlier and more sustained lubrication due to the formation of an oil film. These differences might be relevant to oral lubrication behavior during bolus processing. However, the present tribological measurements should be interpreted as physical measurements of interfacial friction, rather than as direct evidence of swallowing performance or clinical suitability. Although the present tribological measurements were performed at 37 °C, they were conducted under simplified conditions and did not fully reproduce the oral phase environment. Future work should incorporate artificial saliva and oral-mucosa-mimicking contact surfaces to further strengthen the physiological relevance of dysphagia-oriented performance evaluation.

Complex model printing and sensory assessment

The visual appearance of dysphagia-oriented foods is particularly important, as color can influence both acceptability and perceived texture. Therefore, rose powder and matcha powder were selected to adjust the color of the 3D-printed samples. Based on the color comparison results shown in Fig. 7a, b, the visually most appealing formulations were identified, including 20% rose-flavored, 30% matcha-flavored, and the unmodified WL-WP-OOG, which were then used as printing inks to fabricate lotus-shaped samples. The lotus shape was chosen because it is structurally complex, featuring multiple layers and intricate details, thus providing a rigorous test of the material’s 3D printing performance. During printing, the gels containing rose or matcha powders exhibited extrusion behavior comparable to that of the unmodified gel, with no observable nozzle clogging, material dragging, or discontinuous filament deposition. As illustrated in Fig. 7c, the final printed lotus samples were well-formed, structurally stable, and exhibited uniform, visually appealing coloration. Preliminary sensory observations (Supplementary Fig. 3) suggested that all three printed samples maintained their intended shape, with well-aligned layers, no obvious collapse, and a relatively smooth texture. Participants did not report a gritty sensation, and the gels were generally perceived as easy to swallow. Taken together, these observations provided preliminary support that the addition of rose and matcha powders might improve the visual appeal of 3D-printed WL-WP-OOG without obvious adverse effects on extrusion behavior or immediate sensory acceptability. However, given the limited number of participants in this sensory assessment, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and require validation in a larger sensory study.

In summary, incorporating olive oil as a representative unsaturated liquid oil phase markedly modulated the rheological, textural, and structural properties of the WL-WP system, thereby influencing extrusion behavior and post-deposition shape fidelity of 3D-printed gels. Rather than proposing an oil-specific “best recipe,” this work identifies a printable-and-swallowable formulation window in which the ink maintains adequate flowability for extrusion while retaining sufficient structural integrity after deposition. Within this window, moderate oil addition improved water-holding performance, whereas excessive oil loading led to compromised matrix continuity and reduced printing accuracy. The addition of OO reduced the hardness, elasticity, and chewiness of printed samples, thereby improving their suitability for dysphagia patients. Furthermore, OO contributed to the formation of a hydrophobic barrier that inhibited water evaporation and enhanced the water-holding capacity of the composite gel, which was a crucial property for preserving the moisture and desired texture during storage. Multi-technique characterization consistently indicated a predominantly amorphous matrix and a reorganization of the bonding environment within aqueous domains alongside droplet-matrix interactions, providing a mechanistic basis for the observed rheology-printability-texture relationships. Microstructural observations further supported oil-induced changes in pore morphology and matrix continuity. The printed gels met IDDSI Level 5 requirements, suggesting their potential as texture-modified 3D-printed foods for dysphagia management. Olive oil was used as a model liquid oil phase; systematic validation across oils with different physicochemical properties, as well as broader sensory acceptance, storage stability, and in vivo digestion performance, will be addressed in future studies.

Methods

Materials

Lotus root (cv. Elian No. 5) was provided by Wuhan Jinshui Qiliang Agricultural Products Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). According to previously studies, lotus root powder typically contains approximately 5–10% amylose and 5–15% protein on a dry basis, although the exact composition may vary depending on cultivar and processing conditions. Whey protein (WP) was purchased from Hefei BASF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Anhui, China). Olive oil (OO) was supplied from Shanghai Kerry Food Industry Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of composite gel

Whole lotus root powder (WL) was prepared according to reported methods49. Composite printing inks with varying olive oil (OO) contents were prepared by mixing 4 g of WL and 1 g of whey protein (WP) with 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, or 2 g of OO. The sample without OO addition served as the control. Then, 25 mL of distilled water was added to prepare suspensions with mass ratios of total solute to OO of 10:0, 10:1, 10:2, 10:3, and 10:4. The mixtures were homogenized using a high-speed disperser (XHF-DY) at 5000 rpm and then placed in a 90 °C water bath, stirring at 50 rpm for 15 min to ensure complete gelatinization, resulting in WL-WP-OOG composite printing inks with different OO concentrations.

Rheology test

A rheometer (NETZSCH Kinexus Lab+, Malvern, UK) equipped with 40 mm diameter parallel plate set at 1 mm a gap width was used to test the rheological properties of samples according to our reported method with little modification. The dynamic viscoelastic properties were characterized using an oscillatory frequency sweep (0.1–100 rad/s). The strain amplitude was fixed at 0.2%, which lies within the linear viscoelastic region (LVR) as determined in our previous work for closely related WL-WP gel systems under comparable testing conditions49. The elastic modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G”) were recorded. The prepared WL-WP-OOG with different OO concentrations was loaded on the test plate at 25 °C, and the shear rate was set in the range of 0.1–100 s−1 to obtain the apparent viscosity (η) versus shear rate curve, thus observing the degree of shear thinning of the samples.

Precision testing of WL-WP-OOG printing

A food 3D printer (FOODBOT-D1, Hangzhou, China) was utilized for printing, and all printing was carried out at room temperature (25 °C). The WL-WP-OOG mixtures with different ratios were transferred into the printer’s cartridge. The printing model was a hollow cylinder (x = 24 mm; y = 24 mm; z = 36 mm), with a nozzle diameter of 1.55 mm and a printing speed of 15 mm/s. Texture properties

A texture analyzer (TMS-Pro, FTC, USA) was used to determine the texture profile analysis (TPA), including hardness, elasticity, chewiness, and adhesiveness of printed solid cylindrical samples according the following test conditions: probe selection: P10, parameter settings: 25 N induction element, test speed 60 mm/min, compression ratio 50%, trigger force 0.05 N.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The crystal structures of the samples were determined using an X-ray diffractometer (Panaco Empyrean Sharp, Netherlands) equipped with CuKα radiation. The experiment parameters were set as follow: diffraction angle range from 5° to 90°, tube pressure = 45 kV, tube current = 40 mA, scanning speed = 5 degree/min.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

FTIR spectra of WL-WP-OOP were measured using an FTIR spectrometer (Frontier, PerkinElmer, UK) over the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1, with a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 scans per sample. Samples were freeze-dried, ground with KBr, and then subjected to FTIR analysis. The spectra were processed using Omnic software.

SAXS testing

An appropriate amount of freeze-dried WL-WP-OOP was spread in a solid sample holder and placed in the SAXS sample stage (Xeuss 2.0). X-rays with a wavelength of 1.54189 Å were used, with the test temperature at 25 °C and vacuum pressure at 10−5 Pa.

13C-NMR testing

Freeze-dried WL-WP-OOP samples were placed on a 4 mm MAS solid probe for 13C CP/MAS NMR testing (JEOL-600M, Japan). The testing parameters were: resonance frequency of 100.62 MHz, acquisition time of 0.016 s, contact time of 2.1 ms, 2000 scans, and a delay time of 2 s.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The microstructure of WL-WP-OOP was observed using a scanning electron microscope (Hitachi SU8600, Tescan, Czech). The parameters were set to an accelerating voltage of 5 kV, a magnification of 1000×, and a working distance of approximately 9.4–11.7 mm. Samples were fixed on a sample holder with conductive double-sided tape and coated with a conductive layer using a vacuum coater. Cross-sectional SEM images were further analyzed using Nano Measurer to obtain pore size distributions and mean aperture values for samples with clearly resolvable pore boundaries.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy

The WL-WP-OOP (10 mg) was stained with 15 μL of freshly prepared dye mixture consisting of 0.1% Rhodamine B solution, 1% Nile Red solution, and 0.1% Nile Blue solution. After incubation in the dark at 25 °C for 1 h. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (FV1200, Olympus Corporation, Japan) was then used for observation and imaging, with excitation wavelengths of 546, 488, and 633 nm.

Molecular docking simulation

The amino acid sequence of bovine whey protein was retrieved from the NCBI database (accession number P30922.3) and modeled using Swiss-model, with the model evaluated using a Ramachandran plot50. Small molecule compounds were retrieved from the PubChem database using their CAS numbers. Molecular docking of proteins and small molecules was performed using AutoDock 4.2 and AutoDock Tools 1.5.7, and docking was carried out using the Lamarckian genetic algorithm51. The protein was dehydrated, hydrogenated, and charges were calculated. The small molecule ligands were checked for rotatable bonds. The processed protein and small molecule ligands were imported into AutoDock, and molecular docking was performed using the Lamarckian genetic algorithm with semi-flexible docking. The docking box size was 100×100×100 Å3 with a grid spacing of 0.375 Å. The lowest energy conformation from the docking results was selected for visual analysis.

Correlation analysis and pairwise scatter plots

Pearson correlation analysis was performed at the formulation level using Origin 2021. The variables included Ds, G’ (1 rad/s), recovery ratio, height deviation, water loss at 60 min, and hardness. Correlations were visualized as a lower-triangular heatmap, and representative pairwise relationships were further illustrated using scatter plots. Given the limited number of formulations, this analysis was intended to provide an exploratory quantitative overview of structure-rheology-printability-quality relationships.

IDDSI testing

The IDDSI framework was used to determine the food grade for dysphagia, including the fork drip test, spoon tilt test, and fork pressure test. The fork drip test involved lifting the sample with a smooth four-pronged fork and observing the stacking state of the food bolus on the fork and its dripping between the prongs. The spoon tilt test involved scooping the sample with a standard smooth spoon, observing the state of the food bolus on the spoon, then tilting the spoon steadily to the side to observe the sliding state of the sample and the condition of the spoon’s surface after sliding. For the fork pressure test, a printed cube (15 mm side length) was placed on a flat surface and pressed with a standard fork until the examiner’s thumbnail just turned white, reaching the target pressure defined by the IDDSI. The sample was then evaluated for ease of deformation and its ability to be squashed under this pressure.

Tribological performance

The tribological properties of the samples were evaluated using a rotational rheometer (HAAKE Mars60, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Germany). The measuring gap was set to 500 μm. Tests were conducted at 37 °C under a constant normal force of 2 N, with an angular velocity range of 0.01–20 rad/s, corresponding to a sliding velocity (VR) range of 0.1–300 mm/s. Prior to testing, each sample was equilibrated for 60 s, followed by data acquisition over a measurement duration of 60 s. The variation of the coefficient of friction (μf) with sliding velocity (VR) was recorded.

Complex model printing and sensory assessment

WL (4 g), WP (1 g), and OO (0.5 g) were taken as the base, and then rose whole powder and matcha powder were added at mass fractions of 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, and 60%. Distilled water (25 mL) was added to prepare a suspension, which was homogenized at 5000 rpm and subsequently heated in a 90 °C water bath with stirring at 50 rpm for 15 min to ensure complete gelatinization. This process yielded WL-WP-OOG with different additions of rose and matcha powders, suitable for 3D printing. Based on a color card, one gel concentration was selected to print lotus-shaped samples with dimensions of x = 55.57 mm, y = 59.97 mm, and z = 26.91 mm. The sensory evaluation was conducted as a preliminary observational assessment with 10 trained participants. The samples were rated on a 1–10 scale for appearance, taste, and swallowing sensation. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The samples provided were 3D-printed lotus-shaped gels. The objective of the sensory study was to compare differences among the samples while identifying the key food attributes that influenced participants’ positive or negative responses.

Statistical analysis

Results from three independent replicates were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All data were processed using Origin (2021) software. Duncan’s test (p < 0.05) was performed using SPSS Statistics 21.0 (IBM, New York, USA) for significance testing. The mechanism diagram (Fig. 1) was processed by Figdraw.
