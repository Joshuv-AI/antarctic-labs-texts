Assessing knowledge and counselling practices of medical personnel about surgical site infection prevention in Sudan
Authors: Thoraya Salah Murtada, Yasir Mohammed Suliman, Hiba Salah Abdelgadir, Shahad Yahya Awadelkarim, Hind Elmukashfi ShamsEldin, Ebaa Tarig Awad Abdelhadi, Mustafa Magbol, Alaa Hamid Yousif, Samar Bakri Ibrahim, Khalid Mukhtar Awad, Hamza Essam Mohamed, Abeer Ashraf Osman, Abdelfatah Abdelelah Elzain, Hind Salah Abdelgadir, Ali Elbagir Ali, Ali Mohamedahmed Ali, Osman Tarig Ahmed, Roaa Mobarak Alssiddig, Mahasen Jumaa Khalaf Alla, Mohamed Abdelhalim Mahmoud, Abrar Ahmed Hassan, Moyasar Awad Idris
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSurgical site infections (SSI) represent a significant burden on patients and healthcare systems particularly in low-income countries with limited resources. This study aimed to assess the knowledge and counselling practices of medical personnel regarding the prevention of SSI in Sudan.MethodsA cross-sectional hospital-based study was conducted. from June to December 2024. The target population included medical personnel involved in the surgical care in Sudan (surgeons, medical officers, house officers, final year medical students, operating room nurses, and ward nurses). Personnel not directly involved in surgical care were excluded. A structured, self-administered questionnaire was used to assess participants’ knowledge of SSI signs/symptoms, risk factors, and preventive techniques, as well as their counselling practices and perceived challenges to implementing SSI prevention strategies.ResultsThe study included 403 participants. The majority (72%) correctly identified hand hygiene as a crucial preventive practice. However, a significant proportion (62.5%) incorrectly believed that routine preoperative shaving is recommended, indicating a common misconception. While (70.2%) correctly recognized antibiotic prophylaxis as important for reducing bacterial load, knowledge regarding its appropriate duration was variable. Only (37.2%) reported always providing preoperative counselling. Key challenges identified included limited resources, lack of updated guidelines and inadequate training.ConclusionDespite adequate awareness of certain preventive strategies, significant gaps in knowledge and inconsistent counselling practices persist. Education and training, the provision of up-to-date guidelines and ensuring resources availability are crucial interventions to reduce SSIs rates and improve surgical outcomes in Sudan.

Introduction

Surgical site infections (SSIs) are defined as infections that develop at or near the surgical incision after surgery. They can affect the skin, subcutaneous tissue, deeper soft tissues, or any implanted material [1]. The causative pathogens often originate from the patient’s endogenous flora. The specific organisms involved typically depend on the type and location of surgery; the most common are Staphylococcus aureus, coagulase-negative staphylococci, Enterococcus spp., and Escherichia coli [2]. The primary sources of infection are the patient’s skin and, less frequently, the gastrointestinal or genital tracts [3].

The development of an SSI involves a complex interaction of factors, including the nature of the surgical procedures, patient-specific variables (e.g. age, immune status, comorbidities), and environmental factors within the hospital setting [4]. SSIs are a major global healthcare concern, affecting millions of patients annually and representing the second most common type of healthcare-associated infection [5]. They impose a substantial economic burden due to extended hospital stays, readmissions, additional surgical procedures, and long-term antibiotic therapy, further exacerbated by indirect costs from lost productivity and disability [6]. Despite advances in aseptic techniques, surgical methods, and infection control, SSIs remain a persistent challenge [7]. In low-resource settings, they are notably prevalent, affecting a significant proportion of surgical patients [8]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), risk factors associated with SSIs include both non-modifiable factors s(e.g. age and gender) and modifiable factors (e.g., nutritional status, smoking, intraoperative techniques), the control of which can significantly improve patient outcomes [9].

Existing literature reveals significant knowledge gaps among healthcare professionals. According to a study conducted at King Abdulaziz University Hospital in Saudi Arabia, physician knowledge of surgical site infections (SSIs) was found to be insufficient. While (25.2%) of physicians were aware of SSI incidence rates, only (6.7%) possessed good overall knowledge, highlighting a substantial deficit in understanding key SSI indicators [9]. A 2022 cross-sectional study in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, found that only (6.7%) of physicians had good knowledge of SSIs, while (63%) had fair knowledge and (30.2%) had poor knowledge [10]. Similarly, a 2023 study in Makkah, Saudi Arabia, reported that merely (4.1%) of participants had good knowledge, with over half (55.7%) possessing only moderate understanding [11].

A comprehensive review concluded that despite generally positive attitudes towards SSI prevention, healthcare professionals often have poor knowledge of SSI prevention [12]. This gap between knowledge and practice was highlighted in a study at a tertiary trauma center, which emphasized the need for more targeted training [13]. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis highlight the variability in healthcare professionals’ knowledge, revealing that (62%) of nurses demonstrated good knowledge of SSI prevention when assessed dichotomously, a figure which fell to (46%) on a three-point Likert scale [14]. This inconsistency underscores a significant gap that can directly impact clinical practice. Fortunately, evidence from a 2023 interventional study confirms that structured educational programs can substantially improve HCPs’ knowledge and skills in SSI detection and prevention [15].

This study aims to alleviate the burden of SSIs in Sudan. The findings will be essential for developing targeted interventions, such as policy revisions and educational initiatives—including face-to-face sessions and poster campaigns, which have proven effective [16]. Given that approximately half of all SSIs are considered preventable through evidence-based strategies [17], there is a clear imperative for such actions. Ultimately, this study will provide valuable insights for policymakers, healthcare providers, and researchers, fostering a collaborative effort to reduce SSI incidence and improve surgical care quality in Sudan.

Methods

A cross-sectional, healthcare facility–based study was conducted across various healthcare facilities involved in surgical care in Sudan from June to December 2024. The study targeted medical personnel directly involved in surgical patient care. Inclusion criteria encompassed surgeons, registrars, medical officers, house officers, final-year medical students, operating room nurses, ward nurses, and general practitioners engaged in surgical management. Exclusion criteria were medical personnel not involved in surgical care, individuals with cognitive impairments, and those unable to complete the English-language questionnaire. Medical students were instructed to skip the question regarding the frequency of SSI counselling but were permitted to answer other counselling-related questions.

The minimum sample size was calculated as 385 using the formula for a single proportion: n = z² × p × (1 – p) ÷ e², with a 95% confidence level (z = 1.96), a margin of error (e) of 0.05, and an assumed proportion (p) of 0.5 to maximize sample size. The final sample included 403 participants who completed the survey.

A structured, self-administered questionnaire was adapted from previously validated tools [13, 15] and contextualized for Sudan. The tool was piloted on 68 participants, demonstrating acceptable internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.738. The questionnaire comprised four sections:

Sociodemographic and professional characteristics.

Knowledge of SSI (definitions, classification, risk factors, transmission, and prevention).

Perception of best SSI prevention practices.

Counselling practices and perceived challenges to implementation.

Scoring and classification

Knowledge: Each correct answer was awarded one point, yielding a maximum score of 13. A score of ≥ 6 was classified as adequate knowledge.

Knowledge: Each correct answer was awarded one point, yielding a maximum score of 13. A score of ≥ 6 was classified as adequate knowledge.

Practice: Each recommended practice was awarded one point, with a maximum score of 5. A score of ≥ 2 was classified as adequate practice.

Practice: Each recommended practice was awarded one point, with a maximum score of 5. A score of ≥ 2 was classified as adequate practice.

Counselling practices were analyzed descriptively based on reported frequency and topics covered, without a composite score.

Counselling practices were analyzed descriptively based on reported frequency and topics covered, without a composite score.

Data collection and analysis:

The questionnaire was distributed online via social media platforms using a non-probability convenience sampling method. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

A total of 403 healthcare personnel participated. The largest professional groups were house officers (30.0%) and final-year medical students (30.0%), followed by general practitioners (28.3%), registrars (5.2%), nurses (3.7%), and specialists/consultants (2.7%). Half of the participants (50.1%) had less than one year of clinical experience. Knowledge of surgical site infections

Most participants (57.6%) correctly identified “young age (< 18 years)” as not a significant risk factor for SSI, and (68.5%) recognized that minimally invasive surgery carries a lower infection risk than open surgery. Regarding transmission, (59.8%) correctly identified all possible routes (airborne, contact with contaminated surfaces, and endogenous skin flora). The vast majority (80.1%) recognized all listed SSI signs and symptoms of an SSI, while (68.5%) identified “fever with purulent drainage” as the most concerning sign. Knowledge of SSI classification was highest for superficial incisional SSIs (74.9%), followed by organ-space SSIs (65.8%) and deep incisional SSIs (57.6%). Perception of best SSI prevention practices

The perception of best practices revealed both strengths and gaps. A strong majority of respondents (292, 72.5%) correctly identified proper hand hygiene as the most important preventive measure. Similarly, most participants (283, 70.2%) understood that preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis aims to reduce bacterial load and prevent infection. Antibiotic selection was also largely correct (345, 85.6%), being based on surgery type and local resistance patterns. Furthermore, most respondents (297, 73.7%,) favored tailoring the prophylaxis duration to the surgery and patient risk factors, which aligns with guidelines.

However, a significant misconception was found. A majority of participants (252, 62.5%) incorrectly perceived routine preoperative shaving as a recommended practice, while only (89, 22.1%) endorsed the evidence-based approach of avoiding shaving. SSI counselling practices and perceived challenges

Preoperative counselling on SSIs was inconsistent. Among qualified personnel (excluding medical students who were instructed to select ‘never’), only (37.2%) reported always providing counselling. The practice was reported as often (20.3%), sometimes (20.1%), or rarely (5.0%) by other respondents. A notable (26.8%) of all participants reported not counselling patients on SSIs at all.

The most common topics covered during counselling were SSI risk factors (53.8%), postoperative wound care (51.6%), and the importance of hand hygiene (46.7%).

The most frequently cited barriers to effective SSI prevention were a lack of necessary supplies (52.4%), the use of outdated guidelines (46.9%), and staffing or workload issues (43.9%). Discussion

This study provides a crucial assessment of knowledge, perceptions, and practices regarding surgical site infection (SSI) prevention among healthcare personnel in Sudan. The findings reveal a mixed landscape: while there is strong awareness of certain fundamental principles, significant and concerning gaps persist in both the perception of evidence-based practices and the consistency of patient counselling.

A key strength was the high recognition of proper hand hygiene as the most critical preventive measure (72.5%), a cornerstone of infection control consistent with global health messages. Similarly, understanding of antibiotic prophylaxis was generally sound, with most participants correctly identifying its purpose (70.2%) and the importance of selection based on surgery type and local resistance patterns (85.6%). This indicates a solid foundation upon which to build targeted interventions.

However, these strengths are offset by critical deficits. A widespread misconception was identified regarding preoperative hair removal, with (62.5%) of participants incorrectly believing routine shaving is recommended. This highlights a major disconnect from evidence showing it increases SSI risk. Furthermore, preoperative counselling was infrequent; only (37.2%) of qualified personnel always discussed SSI risks with patients. When counselling occurred, key topics like antibiotic information were often omitted, representing a missed opportunity for patient engagement in safety.

The moderate overall knowledge (mean 7.2/13) and practice (mean 3.2/5) scores are concerning compared to international benchmarks [7, 18, 19]. This pattern of knowledge gaps is not new—Labeau et al. reported similarly low scores among ICU nurses [20]—but our study confirms it extends across a broad range of health practitioners in Sudan. The persistence of these misconceptions suggests that current training is insufficient and points to a systemic failure in continuing medical education.

The situation regarding antibiotic stewardship presents a more complex picture. While theoretical knowledge was high as most participants correctly recognized the importance of local resistance patterns for antibiotic selection, it appears inconsistently applied in practice [21, 22]. This disconnect suggests that non-knowledge factors—such as drug availability, cost, and institutional protocols—often override evidence-based decision-making in resource-constrained environments.

The perceived challenges reported by participants underscore these systemic issues. The lack of necessary supplies (52.4%) and the use of outdated guidelines (46.9%) are institutional barriers that fundamentally hinder the implementation of best practices. These resource limitations mirror challenges across sub-Saharan Africa [23, 24], confirming they are widespread. Interestingly, the lower emphasis on outdated guidelines as a barrier (compared to other African studies [23]) may reflect either stronger baseline training or a normalization of working without current protocols—a distinction warranting further investigation.

In conclusion, the gap between guidelines and clinical practice revealed in this study is a problem compounded by significant resource constraints. Addressing it requires more than just training; it necessitates targeted educational interventions, coupled with systemic investment and policy reform to bridge the persistent knowledge-practice gap.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design captures data at a single point in time, preventing the establishment of causal relationships between variables. The scope was limited to medical personnel. Including patients and their families in future research would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the challenges surrounding SSI prevention. Furthermore, while the questionnaire was distributed widely, the respondent pool was not evenly distributed across specialties. It was predominantly composed of medical students, house officers, and general practitioners, with under-representation of consultants, nurses, and registrars. This sampling bias may affect the generalizability of the findings and can likely be attributed to the practical difficulties of data collection during the ongoing war in Sudan, which included severe network disruptions and access issues.

Conclusion

This study highlights critical gaps in the knowledge, perceptions, and counselling practices of Sudanese medical personnel regarding SSI prevention. While a majority demonstrated adequate awareness of certain fundamental principles, such as hand hygiene, significant misconceptions persisted—most notably the widespread belief in the benefit of routine preoperative shaving. These knowledge gaps, coupled with inconsistent patient counselling practices, are exacerbated by systemic challenges. These include a high workload, insufficient staffing, a lack of access to essential supplies, and the absence of clear, updated national guidelines. Addressing these issues is imperative to improving surgical safety and patient outcomes in Sudan.

Recommendations

To address the identified gaps, a comprehensive strategy is essential, including targeted education and training on evidence-based SSI prevention for medical personnel, ensuring consistent availability of essential supplies through proper resource allocation, developing and disseminating context-specific national SSI prevention guidelines, integrating structured patient education into preoperative counselling, fostering interdisciplinary collaboration among healthcare staff, and establishing a national SSI surveillance system alongside ongoing research to evaluate and refine interventions.
