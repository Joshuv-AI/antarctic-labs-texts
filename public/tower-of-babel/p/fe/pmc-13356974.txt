Exercise-primed Trpv4+ progenitors sense mechanical to regulate graded transition in enthesis regeneration
Authors: Shibo Xu, Yiyi Lin, Yihang Yu, Zhiquan Zhu, Pan Huang, Qizhong Xie, Yujianan Chen, Xu Tao, Mei Zhou, Zunhan Liu, Xuting Bian, Lin Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo identify the molecular mechanisms of mechanotransduction that drive functional regeneration rather than scar formation at the tendon-bone interface, addressing the high clinical failure rates of enthesis injuries.MethodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing was performed on enthesis tissues at multiple post-injury timepoints. A mouse tendon-bone insertion injury model was established with treadmill exercise versus sedentary controls. Transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4)+ cells were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting for functional assays. Mechanistic studies included bulk RNA sequencing, immunofluorescence, and in vitro mechanical stimulation. Functional outcomes were assessed by histomorphometry, micro-CT, and biomechanical testing.ResultsSingle-cell transcriptomics identified Trpv4+ cells specifically enriched in chondrogenic lineages during enthesis repair. Mechanical stimulation significantly increased Trpv4+ chondrocyte density and enhanced chondrogenic marker expression. Lineage tracing confirmed that Trpv4+ progenitors possess clonogenicity and multipotent differentiation capacity, with mechanical stimulation enhancing their chondrogenic potential. Mechanistically, Trpv4 activation facilitated calcium influx, leading to calcineurin-NFATc1 signalling cascade activation. Transplantation of exercise-primed Trpv4+ progenitor cells significantly improved cartilage matrix deposition and biomechanical properties compared to controls.Conclusion Trpv4 + progenitors constitute a mechanosensitive population critical for enthesis regeneration. Exercise enhances Trpv4-dependent chondrogenic differentiation through the calcium-calcineurin-NFATc1 axis, providing a scientific basis for optimizing postoperative rehabilitation.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(7):836–849.Keywords: Enthesis, TRPV4, Tendon bone healing, entheses, tendons, progenitor cells, chondrocytes, calcium, RNA, transplantation, biomechanical testing, histomorphometry, biomechanical properties

Introduction

The specialized tendon-bone interface, termed the enthesis, consists of a fibrocartilaginous complex divided into four reigns: tendon, uncalcified fibrocartilage, calcified fibrocartilage, and bone.1,2 This functionally graded tissue features a layered microstructure enabling transmission of muscle contraction forces across mechanically distinct tissues for joint movement.3 Such morphological grading is essential for reducing stress concentration between dissimilar tissues.4,5 Enthesis injuries are associated with high clinical severity, substantial disability rates, and an age-dependent increase in incidence.6 Surgical repair of tendon-to-bone tears suffers from high failure rates, making enthesis injury a critical challenge in sports medicine.7,8 Healing typically yields fibrovascular scar tissue, compromising mechanical integrity.9 An insufficient understanding of enthesis biology has hindered the development of regenerative strategies.

Defining the cellular contributors to tendon-bone enthesis healing is essential for elucidating underlying mechanisms and developing targeted therapeutic strategies.10 Tendon-derived stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs), recently characterized as resident progenitors within the tendon epitenon, are pivotal for tendon repair.11 Current studies characterize TSPCs using markers shared with mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) or other stem/progenitor phenotypes, including CD44, Scleraxis (Scx), Sca-1, and CD146.12,13 Substantial in vivo evidence has demonstrated the tendon regenerative capacity of specific TSPC subpopulations, particularly CD26+, Nestin+, and Tppp3+ cells.14-16 Comparatively, bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (BMSCs) have been established for high self-renewal, clonogenicity, and multilineage potential validated by decades of evidence.17 However, conventional isolation methods yield BMSCs as heterogeneous subpopulations, a limitation paralleled in enzyme-digested TSPCs which similarly comprise functionally distinct subpopulations.18 Recent studies established that moderate mechanical loading promotes tendon-bone insertion healing by remodelling the physical microenvironment.19,20 Postoperative rehabilitation requires timely and appropriate exercise, but the downstream molecular mechanisms of mechanical transduction remain unclear.21 Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enabled transcriptomic characterization of the natural healing process at single-cell resolution. Through high-throughput sequencing, we identified mechanically sensitive chondrocytes specifically emerging during healing. Common mechanosensors include Ca²+ channels, integrin signalling complexes, and primary cilia. Notably, the Ca²+ permeable channel transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4), which is highly expressed in chondrocytes, transduces mechanical cues to regulate chondrogenesis and matrix homeostasis.22

TRPV4 modulates pain and inflammation related signalling across diverse musculoskeletal tissues, including cartilage, bone, intervertebral discs, and synovium.23,24 Notably, Trpv4 deficiency impairs chondrogenic differentiation in MSCs.25 Cartilage-specific knockout of Trpv4 alleviates age-related osteoarthritis, revealing the diverse functions of this mechanosensitive channel in different tissues.26 Here, we identified six post-injury cellular subpopulations through high-throughput sequencing. Pseudotime analysis clarified Trpv4’s role in chondrogenic differentiation. Genetic deletion models demonstrated that mechanical loading promotes fibrocartilaginous enthesis reconstitution post-injury through Trpv4-dependent mechanotransduction. Inducible lineage tracing confirmed the clonogenicity and multipotency of Trpv4+ cells. Our findings reveal a distinct mechanosensitive cellular population activated during enthesis repair and a potential stem cell therapy, providing molecular insights for accelerating postoperative rehabilitation.

Methods

Single-cell isolation for RNA-seq with 10× Genomics chromium platform

Single-cell RNA sequencing experiments were performed on enthesis tissues harvested from Sprague-Dawley rats at postnatal days 3 (P3), P7, P14, and P28 following injury (n = 3 to 4 animals per timepoint for 10× Genomics processing). Tissue dissociation was carried out by enzymatic digestion using 0.2% collagenase I (17100017; Gibco, USA) and 0.4% collagenase II (17101015; Gibco) at 37°C for two hours. The cell pellet was resuspended in red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer and incubated at room temperature for five minutes to lyse erythrocytes. Cells were subsequently pelleted by centrifugation, and the washed single-cell suspension was used for downstream library construction after discarding the supernatant. scRNA-seq libraries were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell 3' Library, Gel Bead Kit v3.1 and the Chromium Single Cell G Chip Kit (10× Genomics, USA) according to the manufacturer’s requirement. Within the emulsion droplets, the released RNA was barcoded during reverse transcription. Following complementary DNA (cDNA) generation, amplification was performed. Final libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform system (Illumina, USA).

RNA-sequencing data processing and quality control

Cells exhibiting > 10% mitochondrial gene content or < 3% ribosomal gene content were excluded. Data integration was subsequently performed with the Seurat (v. 5) package. Potential doublets were identified and removed within each sample using DoubletFinder (v. 2.0.3), employing an expected doublet formation rate of 0.8% per 1,000 cells. Cells were classified into doublet-low confidence, doublet-high confidence, or singlet; only singlets were retained for downstream analysis. The remaining cells were log-normalized and scaled using a linear regression model. The top 2,000 highly variable genes were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function. Principal component analysis (PCA) was then applied for dimensionality reduction. To mitigate batch effects across samples, data integration was processed by the Harmony package. The first 20 principal components (PCs) were used for neighborhood graph construction (FindNeighbors). Following normalization, unsupervised clustering was performed using the shared nearest neighbor (SNN) algorithm with an adjusted resolution parameter. The resulting clusters were visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) through the Seurat package. For subpopulation analysis, a similar workflow was employed, encompassing feature selection identified by highly variable genes, dimensionality reduction, and cluster identification.

Gene set variation analysis

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed to identify dysregulated biological processes across distinct risk groups using the GSVA (v. 2.0.7) package (Bioconductor, USA). Gene Ontology (GO) gene sets were retrieved from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) processed by the msigdbr R package (Bioconductor). Statistically significantly enriched pathways were determined using a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, adjusted through the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Statistically significantly enriched GO terms and pathways were compiled into curated functional datasets. Functional enrichment activity across distinct temporal subpopulations was subsequently quantified using the nonparametric, unsupervised GSVA algorithm for downstream bioinformatic characterization of subpopulations for different timepoints.

Functional analysis of DEGs

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across distinct cell types were identified using the FindAllMarkers function in the Seurat (adjusted p-value < 0.05) package in R software. GO enrichment analysis of these DEGs was subsequently conducted using the clusterProfiler R package (v. 4.14.6; Bioconductor), with statistically significant terms defined by a FDR < 0.05.

Animal models

We have adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines and included the ARRIVE checklist in the Supplementary Material. Male mice and rats were housed under standard conditions with ad libitum access to food and water. The Trpv4 knockout mice, Trpv4CreERT2 mice, and Rosa26tdTomato mice were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences, USA. In Trpv4CreERT2;Rosa26tdTomato mice, Cre-mediated recombination was induced by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of tamoxifen (50 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) dissolved in sterile corn oil for five consecutive days.

Cell culture

TRPV4+ cells were isolated from the enthesis through enzymatic digestion and centrifugation. Positively selected cells were sorted by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) and subsequently cultured in complete medium consisting of Alpha Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Bone marrow was aseptically harvested from the tibiae and femora following complete soft-tissue removal. Marrow cavities were flushed with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/F12 (DMEM/F12; Gibco). For chondrogenic differentiation, cells were seeded in six-well plates and treated according to the manufacturer’s protocol using a chondrogenic induction kit. For mechanical stimulation analysis, cells were subjected to cyclic tensile strain (5%, 0.2 Hz) using a mechanical stress stimulation loading system. Silicone elastomer plates were pre-coated with fibronectin prior to cell seeding. Cyclosporine A was used at a concentration of 0.05 μM during chondrogenic induction.

Enthesis injury model

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were acclimatized for two weeks prior to procedures. Under sodium pentobarbital anaesthesia (1%, 50 mg/kg i.p.) and aseptic preparation, a tendon-bone insertion injury model was established. This involved creating a bone tunnel in the calcaneus using a bone marrow aspiration needle, passing a suture through the Achilles tendon and removing surface cartilage following dissection of the Achilles tendon-bone insertion. Postoperatively, mice received ad libitum food/water and unrestricted cage activity with random assignment to experimental groups. The treadmill-trained group initiated exercise on postoperative day 7, with the speed gradually increased from 5 to 10 m/min (30 min/day, 5 days/week, 0° incline). Mice were euthanized at designated timepoints for specimen collection.

Cell isolation and FACS

TRPV4+ cells were isolated separately from the enthesis between the tendon and the calcaneus in TRPV4CreERT2;Rosa26tdTomato mice after enthesis injury. Enthesis-derived tissues were digested, centrifuged, and filtered into polypropylene FACS tubes using sequentially smaller mesh sizes (250, 70 µm). Trpv4+ cells were isolated through FACSAria SORP cell sorter (BD Biosciences, USA) and expanded in complete medium. Expanded cells were labelled with the Qtracker 655 Cell Labeling Kit (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and subsequently injected into injured entheses post-implantation for therapeutic evaluation. Trpv4 related cells were suspended in sterile collagen type I solution at a concentration of 1 × 107 cells/ml for transplantation after enthesis injury using a 30-gauge Hamilton syringe (total volume 50 μl per injection).

Cellular proliferation assay

Following tamoxifen induction in Trpv4CreERT2;Rosa26tdTomato mice assigned to treadmill-trained or sedentary groups, cells were isolated from the Achilles tendon enthesis through enzymatic digestion at 14 days post-injury. Cell suspensions were stained with anti-CD90 and anti-CD44 antibodies during a 30-minute incubation at 4°C, washed with Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS), and subsequently incubated with DAPI at 1 mg/ml for live cell discrimination following termination of antibody binding. Sorted cell populations were cultured in complete growth medium within six-well plates at 37°C with 5% CO₂. Following 14 days of culture, functional assessment was performed through quantification of colony-forming units through 20-minute crystal violet staining alongside proliferation measurement via Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays at different timepoints.

Multipotential differentiation assays

Following tamoxifen induction in Trpv4CreERT2;Rosa26tdTomato mice, Trpv4+ cells were isolated from the Achilles tendon enthesis through enzymatic digestion at 14 days post-injury and purified by FACS. BMSCs served as positive controls. Cells were cultured in adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic induction media according to the manufacturer’s specifications after nearly 80% cellular confluency in six-well plates for multipotential differentiation.

Gait analysis and open field test

Locomotor function was assessed through gait analysis and open field testing to evaluate enthesis recovery post-injury. Before measurements, mice were acclimated to the glass walkway of the CatWalk XT system (Noldus Information Technology, the Netherlands) and underwent training sessions. For open field testing, mice were placed in a well-lit arena comprising four square zones (40 cm × 40 cm × 30 cm) with an overhead video camera recording. After five minutes of habituation, 30-minute activity processes were recorded. The arena was cleaned with ethanol between trials, and adequate ventilation was provided to eliminate residual odour. EthoVision software recorded movement trajectories and facilitated subsequent statistical analysis and data visualization.

Western blots and gene expression analysis

Fresh enthesis tissue specimens were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and homogenized in T-PER Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific; cat. no. 78510) supplemented with protease inhibitors in sonication. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 10,000 × g, and supernatants were transferred to fresh microcentrifuge tubes for storage at -80°C. Prior to analysis, samples were re-boiled and separated through sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), followed by electrophoretic transfer onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol and imaged with the gel documentation system (Vilber Fx6 EDGE Spectra; Vilber GmbH, Germany).

Similarly, cells and fresh enthesis tissue specimens from each group were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and subsequently lysed via Trizol reagent (#9109; Takara Bio Inc., Japan). Total RNA was extracted and reverse transcribed into cDNA, processed by PrimeScript RT Master Mix (#RR036A; Takara Bio Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) amplification was then performed through Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (ABclonal Technology, China). The corresponding primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Lentivirus package

The lentiviruses were purchased from GeneChem (China). The primary BMSCs were transfected with supernatants containing virus carrying the Trpv4 construct and constructs for the overexpression of Trpv4 using the HitransG reagent (GeneChem). After five days, the infected cells were selected with 3 mg/ml puromycin (MedChemExpress, USA).

Bulk-RNA sequencing analysis

Raw sequencing data underwent quality control and filtering using fastp (Shifu Chen, China). Subsequently, cleaned reads were then aligned to the reference genome with HISAT2 (v. 2.2.1; Johns Hopkins University, USA), generating BAM files from FASTQ files. Transcript assembly and gene expression quantification were then performed using StringTie software (v. 2.2.3; Johns Hopkins University). The resulting gene count matrix was used for downstream analyses. Differential expression analysis was conducted in R (v. 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria) with DESeq2 (v. 1.44.0; Bioconductor), applying thresholds of |log2FoldChange| > 1 and adjusted p < 0.05 to perform enrichment analysis and visualize the results. Functional enrichment analyses – including GO, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) – were performed using clusterProfiler (v. 4.14.0).

MicroCT analysis

Mouse enthesis samples were harvested four weeks post-surgery, with the calcaneus intact and the attached tendon tissue preserved at the insertion site. Following fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), enthesis samples were scanned using a micro-CT (μCT) system (Bruker, USA). Axial images were reconstructed and analyzed within the defined region of interest (ROI) using SkyScan CT analysis software (Bruker). After 3D reconstruction and spatial alignment, quantitative morphometric analysis was performed to assess 3D osteophyte formation and enthesis tissue calcification. Bone volume fraction (BV/TV), bone mineral density (BMD), and bone surface (BS) in the ROI were measured with CTAn (Bruker).

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Enthesis samples from all experimental groups were fixed in 4% PFA and decalcified in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). Histochemical staining – including haematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Toluidine blue (TB), and Safranin O/Fast Green (SO-FG) – was performed according to manufacturers’ protocols after standard deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through descending alcohol gradients. Histological assessment was conducted by three investigators (ZZ, YY, and PH) blinded to group allocation using a validated enthesis healing scoring system.

Similarly, for immunofluorescence staining, tissue sections underwent standard deparaffinization followed by heat-mediated antigen retrieval. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 10% FBS before overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4°C. Sections were subsequently incubated with species-matched fluorescent secondary antibodies for one hour at room temperature. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-Trpv4 (1:500, CAT: ACC-034; Alomone Labs, Israel); anti-Sox9 (1:1,000, CAT: ab185966; Abcam, UK); anti-Collagen II (1:1,000, CAT: ab34712; Abcam); anti-Collagen X (1:100, CAT: 14-9771-37; Thermo Fisher Scientific); anti-Osterix (1:1,000, CAT: ab209484; Abcam); anti-Nfatc1 (1:800, CAT: 66963-1; Proteintech, USA); anti-Ly6G (1:100, CAT: 14-5931-85; ; Thermo Fisher Scientific); anti-Tnmd (1:1,000, CAT: ab203676; Abcam); anti-RGS5 (1:1,000, CAT: 11590-1-AP; Proteintech); anti-Pvlap (1:1,000, CAT: ab27853; Abcam); anti-F4/80 (1:100, CAT: ab300421; Abcam); Cy3 AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:1,000, CAT: 711-165-152; Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA); Cy3 AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (1:1,000, CAT: 715-165-150; Jackson ImmunoResearch); Donkey anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Highly Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1,000, CAT: A-21202; Thermo Fisher Scientific); and Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Highly Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1,000, CAT: A-21206; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Following nuclear counterstaining with DAPI, protein expression was assessed using a Zeiss LSM900 confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Germany).

Biomechanical analysis of the enthesis tissue

After 28 days post-surgery, Achilles tendon-bone enthesis samples containing TRPV4+ cells and enthesis samples from treadmill-trained groups were harvested for biomechanical testing. Enthesis specimens were clamped between fixtures of the universal testing machine (MTS Exceed 40, E43.104N; MTS Systems Corporation, USA). The Achilles tendon and calcaneus were secured to opposing grips to prevent experimental interference while avoiding contact at the tendon-calcaneus junction. Enthesis samples were then stretched at a constant displacement until failure without preload.

Statistical analysis

All data statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (v. 8.0; GraphPad Software, USA). Continuous data are presented as mean (SD). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-group comparisons were evaluated using independent-samples or paired t-tests where data were normally distributed; otherwise, non-parametric tests were applied. For comparisons involving three or more groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used when assumptions were met, with non-parametric alternatives employed otherwise. Post hoc comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction following significant ANOVA results. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Exact sample sizes for each dataset are provided in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Single-cell transcriptome sequencing reveals heterogeneous cellular phenotypes after enthesis injury

To delineate cellular dynamics and transcriptional programs during enthesis healing, we performed scRNA-seq on tendon-to-bone insertion tissues isolated from the distal Achilles tendon at post-injury days 3 (P3), 7, 14, and 28. Tissue dissociation and library preparation were conducted using the 10 × Genomics platform (Figure 1a). Following stringent quality control and doublet removal, 2,800 to 7,700 high-quality cells per timepoint (1,890 to 3,379 genes/cell) were retained for downstream analysis (Supplementary Figure S1A). Integrated datasets underwent dimensionality reduction and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embedding through Seurat V5. Cell clusters were annotated based on differential expression and canonical markers, revealing six resident populations: endothelial cells, pericytes, monocyte/macrophage cells, osteoclasts, MSCs, and T cells (Supplementary Figure S1B). Each compartment exhibited distinct functional contributions to the enthesis repair microenvironment (Figure 1b).

Categorization and transcriptome analysis of cells after Achilles tendon enthesis injury based on single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). a) Achilles tendon enthesis of rat at postoperation day 3 (P3), P7, P14, and P28 were pooled, dissociated, and sorted for scRNA-seq analysis. b) Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) plot of all cells integrated from tendon entheses at P3, P7, P14, and P28. c) Percentage of cell types at different timepoints. d) Subset and categorization of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) identified by dotted outline. This subset of enthesis cells was used for the subsequent analyses. e) Heatmap of marker gene expression in different subpopulations integrated from MSCs after categorization. TBI, tendon-bone interface.

To fully grasp the dynamics of each cell type’s proportions, we conducted a comprehensive quantitative analysis of cellular composition at various timepoints (Figure 1c). Our findings reveal that the proportion of monocytes/macrophages was significantly elevated during the early stages of injury (P3 and P7), underscoring their critical role in the inflammatory response and initial repair process.27 As the injury progressed, there was a gradual decline in the proportion of monocytes/macrophages, which aligned with the restoration of tissue homeostasis.28

To validate cluster annotations, we generated a dot plot (Supplementary Figure S1C) and heatmap (Supplementary Figure S1D) depicting expression distributions of canonical marker genes across UMAP embeddings. Endothelial clusters exhibited high Plvap and Vwf expression, while monocyte-macrophage populations showed enrichment of Cd68 and Cxcl3, confirming annotation accuracy. For validation of cellular proportions, we performed immunofluorescence staining on tendon-bone insertion sites at post-injury days 7 (P7) and 28 (P28) (Supplementary Figure S2B). Complementary UMAP visualization analysis quantified variations in cell clustering over time across the dataset (Supplementary Figure S2A). Quantitative analysis of cell populations was performed using specific markers, including Ly6G (neutrophils), Tnmd (tenocytes), Rgs5 (pericytes), Pvlap (endothelial cells), and F4/80 (macrophages) in our scRNA-seq dataset (Supplementary Figure S2B). Immunofluorescence validation confirmed substantial concordance with scRNA-seq quantification. Specifically, infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages was observed at 7 DPI compared to controls, consistent with elevated monocyte/macrophage proportions in scRNA-seq data. Conversely, the number of tenocytes rebounded by 28 DPI. This spatiotemporal alignment with scRNA-seq cellular proportion dynamics further validated the robustness of our sequencing results.

Similarly, in an attempt for a deeper understanding of the heterogeneity of MSCs in the enthesis, subpopulation analyses of cells of mesenchymal source were investigated. Cell subpopulations were annotated as enthesis progenitors, enthesoblasts, mineralizing chondrocytes, tenoblasts, tenocytes, and chondrocytes (Figure 1d). Gene expression profiling revealed the molecular characteristics of different cell subpopulations. Enthesis progenitors displayed relatively high levels of Pdgfra, Tppp3, and Cd44 consistent with the molecular characteristics of previously identified tendon and muscle stem cells (Figure 1e), representing the general progenitor pool prior to lineage commitment. The identification of enthesoblasts at the enthesis was based on their stem cell transcriptional profiles, but the expression levels of these profiles were diminished relative to enthesis progenitors and early activation of chondrogenic genes. In addition, during the healing process, we identified a characteristic profile of tenoblasts (e.g. Scx, Col1a1, Tnmd, Tnc, Tnn, Thbs4), chondrocytes (e.g. Sox9, Col2a1, Bgn, Chad, Acan), and mineralizing chondrocytes (e.g. Spp1, Alpl, Ibsp, Foxa3). These findings indicated six distinct populations of enthesis cells in all enthesis MSCs at different times after injury, which synergize with each other to accomplish post-injury restoration.

Enthesis MSC differentiation trajectories reveal the direction of differentiation of enthesis progenitors

To investigate the differentiation trajectories of MSCs in a subpopulation analysis, the monocle3 algorithm was applied to scRNA sequencing data to infer differentiation pathways.29 Early-stage enthesis progenitors and enthesoblasts may serve as potential sources for post-injury repair of the enthesis. In contrast, mid-to-late stage chondrocytes and mineralizing chondrocytes are implicated in the healing process after enthesis injury (Supplementary Figure S3A). The clustering results were used as input clusters for Slingshot to perform smooth fitting of the inferred trajectories (Johns Hopkins University), further revealing the pseudotemporal positioning of distinct cell types along the differentiation paths (Supplementary Figure S3B). To validate cluster identities, we compared the expected marker expression profiles with the observed expression patterns in individual clusters via violin plots.30 As we expected, Progenitor compartments (enthesis progenitors and enthesoblasts) were characterized by the enrichment of Tppp3 and Cd44 (Supplementary Figure S3C). The chondrogenic differentiation trajectory was marked by progressive upregulation of Sox9 and Col2a1. Notably, Pdgfra and Tppp3 expression was enriched in enthesis progenitors and enthesoblasts, while chondrocyte lineage commitment was characterized by elevated Col2a1 and Pth1r in trajectory analysis, and Scx and Tnmd only in tenoblasts and tenocytes (Supplementary Figure S3D). Pseudotemporal mapping revealed the sequential positioning of enthesis-associated cell types along the differentiation trajectory, with enthesis progenitors and enthesoblasts occupying early stages and chondrocytes/mineralizing chondrocytes dominating later stages (Supplementary Figure S3E). Hierarchical clustering of DEGs along pseudotime identified distinct expression modules: a cluster of genes including Dkk3, Ccl7, Tppp3, and Igfbp5 is highly expressed at the onset of the trajectory, while another cluster involving Col2a1, Alpl, and Mustn1 exhibits gradually increasing expression in later pseudo-time stages (Supplementary Figure S3F).

To delineate stage-specific cellular functions, we then performed GSVA across pseudotime bins.31 This revealed the dynamic enrichment of key biological processes during enthesis repair. P3 was characterized by marked enrichment of macrophage activation, inflammatory apoptosis, and acute inflammatory response. As the process advanced, upregulation of the collagen metabolic process and extracellular matrix (ECM) assembly indicated structural remodelling and matrix deposition. Concurrent activation of stem cell population maintenance and chondrocyte proliferation suggested mobilization of progenitor niches and a chondrogenic differentiation trajectory. Persistent activity of endothelial morphogenesis and fibroblast proliferation at P28 highlighted ongoing vascularization and fibrotic responses (Supplementary Figure S3G).

Based on these results, we wanted to explore potential therapeutic targets after tendon-bone insertion injury. We discovered that GO terms related to cartilage development, glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis, bone development, calcium ion transport, and calcium ion homeostasis were enriched in MSCs, and interestingly Trpv4 was the only gene overlapping in these biological processes (Supplementary Figure S3H). Inspired by the unique overlapping role of Trpv4 in cellular functions, we explored its association with fibrocartilage maturation. By analyzing the expression of Trpv4 and collagen II, we unexpectedly found that Trpv4 and Col2 exhibited considerable consistency in terms of the cell types and timepoints (Supplementary Figures S3I and S3J). To validate Trpv4’s role in chondrogenic differentiation of enthesis-resident progenitors, enthesis progenitor and enthesoblast subpopulations undergoing chondrogenesis were analyzed. Monocle pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed CD44 and Thy1 expression predominantly during early differentiation stages, consistent with prior pseudotime findings (Supplementary Figures S4A and 4D). Pseudo-temporal trajectory analysis verified that Trpv4 expression directs progenitors toward mineralizing chondrocyte development. This demonstrates Trpv4’s capacity to construct graded mineralized structures during enthesis repair (Supplementary Figure S4B). Thy1+ progenitor populations exhibited low differentiation states, with a subset of Trpv4+ cells exhibiting a less differentiated phenotype (Supplementary Figure S4C). Subclustering of the population through Seurat partitioning yielded four distinct subsets. Trajectory inference analysis of these subsets using SCORPIUS predicted potential differentiation trajectories and terminal states following injury (Supplementary Figure S4D). Integrated pseudotime trajectory analysis suggested that precursor cells may differentiate toward Trpv4+ cells, which likely govern subsequent chondrogenic fate commitment. In addition, we compared the expression patterns of the classical TSPC marker Tppp3 and Trpv4 (Supplementary Figure S5A). Pseudotime analysis revealed that Tppp3 expression was predominantly enriched at the early stage of the differentiation trajectory and distributed along the tenogenic lineage (Supplementary Figure S5B), whereas Trpv4 expression occurred before the branching point and was primarily localized along the chondrogenic differentiation path (Supplementary Figure S5C). This suggests that multiple heterogeneous cell subpopulations coordinately participate in the repair process at the injured tendon-bone interface, with distinct differentiation potentials among different cell types.

Exercise promotes fibrocartilaginous complex recovery after enthesis injury

To validate the association of Trpv4 identified by scRNA-seq with collagen II in enthesis pathology, we adopted a mouse model of tendon-bone insertion injury. Given that transient receptor potential channels are a type of mechanosensitive ion channel expressed in various tissue types and cell populations and capable of transmitting a variety of stimuli inside and outside cells,23,24,32 we constructed a tendon-bone insertion injury model and let mice exercise on the treadmill to simulate mechanical stimulation (Figure 2a). Gait analysis showed improvement in stride length and swing speed of the hind limbs at 28 and 58 DPI in the exercise group compared to the sedentary group, respectively (Figure 2b). The open field test revealed a denser movement trajectory diagram for the exercise group compared to the sedentary group. Furthermore, there were no obvious blank areas in the central region of the overall movement trajectory distribution for the exercise group (Figure 2c). In the overall movement trajectory distribution, the total movement distance of the training group significantly increased, and the total velocity significantly increased at 28 and 58 DPI (Figure 2d). Histochemical staining results at different timepoints, including H&E, SO-FG, and TB staining, revealed that treadmill training promoted repair of the enthesis (Figure 2e). As time progressed, the enthesis morphology score increased (Supplementary Table S2), including maintenance of the fibrocartilage at the attachment point and increased adhesion between the tendon and bone (Figure 2f). These results preliminarily indicated that appropriate mechanical stimulation might enhance healing after enthesis injuries.

Trpv4 is critical to enthesis healing in the chondrogenesis process

Building upon our initial morphological evidence that mechanical stimulation can mediate healing after damage to the enthesis, we further designed experiments to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms and downstream mediators (Supplementary Figure S6D). Given that the critical mechanosensitive ion channel Trpv4 is widely expressed throughout the musculoskeletal system,26,33 we therefore hypothesized that Trpv4 may regulate chondrogenesis following enthesis injury. Subsequent GSEA of the Trpv4+ cluster in scRNA transcriptomics revealed significant enrichment of response to mechanical stimulus-related GO terms, glycosaminoglycan binding, and collagen-containing ECM organization (Supplementary Figure S6A). Complementary functional enrichment analysis further demonstrated pronounced involvement in chondrogenic processes, including cartilage morphogenesis, chondrocyte differentiation, and ECM organization (Supplementary Figure S6B). KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated pronounced enrichment in ECM-receptor interaction and TGF-β signalling pathways. These molecular pathways could collectively coordinate fibrocartilaginous differentiation and ECM remodelling, providing a molecular basis for mechanostimulated enthesis regeneration (Supplementary Figure S6C). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed TRPV4 localization within the cartilaginous region of the enthesis at 28 and 58 DPI. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed TRPV4 localization within the cartilaginous zone of the enthesis at 28 and 58 DPI (Supplementary Figure S6E). Quantitative analysis revealed significantly higher densities of TRPV4+ chondrocytes at the enthesis in treadmill training groups compared to the sedentary groups at both 28 and 58 DPI timepoints (Supplementary Figure S6F), indicating that mechanical stimulation enhances TRPV4-expressing chondrocyte populations during healing. Consistent with this finding, chondrogenic marker expression was markedly elevated in the trained cohort compared to controls at both stages (Supplementary Figure S6G). Given the critical load-distributing function of cartilage at the insertion point, this coordinated upregulation of chondrogenic components in treadmill training stimulated mice suggests superior regenerative outcomes at the enthesis.

To delineate the relationship between TRPV4 and Collagen II, we generated TRPV4 knockout mice (TRPV4-/-) and assessed enthesis composition (Supplementary Figures S7A and S7B). Specifically, thinning of the cartilage layer at the tendon-bone insertion was observed in TRPV4 knockout mice compared to the control group. Following enthesis injury, immunofluorescence demonstrated elevated expression of Collagen II and Collagen X in treadmill-trained mice versus sedentary controls, indicating enhanced cartilaginous matrix restoration at the enthesis (Supplementary Figure S6H). In contrast, the TRPV4-/- group markedly diminished Collagen II and Collagen X expression at the healing enthesis. The osteogenic and chondrogenic markers were downregulated in the TRPV4 knockout group from the freshly isolated sample in tendon-bone insertion injury model (Supplementary Figure S6I). In contrast, in the littermate control group of TRPV4 knockout mice, we found that treadmill training after enthesis injury promoted the regeneration of both calcified and non-calcified cartilage layers. These results indicate that TRPV4 knockout prevents mechanical stimulation from effectively facilitating regeneration of the enthesis fibrocartilage complex.

Therapeutic potential and mechanism in enthesis-derived TRPV4+ progenitors for enthesis healing

Prior studies have established TRPV4 as a mechanosensitive ion channel through which the osteochondral system perceives articular loading to maintain tissue homeostasis.25,34,35 Motivated by these prior findings and pseudotemporal single-cell analysis of the enthesis at various timepoints post-injury, we further employed the TRPV4+ cells as the latent resident source of enthesis. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a temporal increase in TRPV4+ progenitor cells following enthesis injury, with peak abundance observed at 14 DPI (Supplementary Figures S5C and S5D). Treadmill training significantly enhanced the maintenance of this progenitor population compared to sedentary controls (Supplementary Figure S5E). These cellular dynamics align with improved histological healing outcomes, suggesting that mechanoresponsive progenitor maintenance contributes to exercise-enhanced enthesis regeneration. Then, TRPV4+ cells were isolated from TRPV4CreERT2;ROSA26tdTomato enthesis after injury using FACS (Supplementary Figure S8A). After isolating TRPV4+ cells through FACS, flow cytometry revealed concomitant augmentation of proliferation and attenuation of the apoptosis ratio in TRPV4+ cells from treadmill-trained mice versus sedentary controls (Supplementary Figures S8b and 8c) . Within the TRPV4+ cellular compartment, we further isolated TRPV4+ CD90+ CD44+ subpopulations by FACS and compared their clonogenic potential across experimental groups (Supplementary Figure S8D).

Within the TRPV4+ cellular compartment, we further isolated TRPV4+ CD90+ CD44+ subpopulations by FACS and compared their clonogenic potential across experimental groups. Clonogenic assays demonstrated significantly enhanced colony-forming capacity in CD90+ CD44+ TRPV4+ cells compared to TRPV4+ cells alone (Supplementary Figure S8E). Notably, treadmill-trained mice exhibited greater clonogenicity in enthesis TRPV4+ progenitors relative to sedentary controls (Supplementary Figure S8F). Proliferation assays and CCK8 assays revealed that TRPV4+ progenitors proliferated at a higher rate than TRPV4+ cells (Supplementary Figure S8G). To assess multipotency, FACS-isolated TRPV4 cells and progenitors underwent trilineage differentiation under adipogenic, chondrogenic, and osteogenic induction. Like conventional MSCs, TRPV4 progenitors demonstrated osteogenic and chondrogenic potential but exhibited negligible adipogenic capacity (Supplementary Figure S8H). This indicates that TRPV4+ progenitors may represent a subpopulation of enthesis progenitors that express the mechanosensitive ion channel TRPV4. These cells exhibit clonogenicity, multipotency, and a specific response to mechanical stimulation. To confirm chondrogenic differentiation of TRPV4-related cells during enthesis healing, the injury model was established in TRPV4CreERT2;ROSA26tdTomato reporter mice (Supplementary Figure S7F). tdT positive cells emerged during the healing process, with treadmill-trained enthesis exhibiting increased Acan+tdT+ cells, indicating TRPV4+ cell differentiation toward chondrocytes (Supplementary Figure S7G). These findings collectively demonstrate that treadmill training enhances TRPV4+ cell-mediated reconstruction of the cartilaginous layer during enthesis repair.

To mechanistically dissect the therapeutic potential of TRPV4+ cells at the enthesis, we established TRPV4-overexpression in MSCs. Primary MSCs were isolated from murine femoral bone marrow and FACS-verified for CD90+ CD44+ expression (Supplementary Figure S9A). Femoral BMSCs were transduced with Trpv4-overexpression (Trpv4-OE) or Trpv4-KD (Trpv4-knockdown) lentiviruses. Stable polyclonal lines were established following puromycin selection, with transduction efficiency confirmed by GFP fluorescence (Supplementary Figure S9B). Following chondrogenic induction of transduced cells, messenger RNA (mRNA) analysis revealed enhanced chondrogenic gene expression (Sox9, Col2a1, Acan) in TRPV4-OE MSCs versus controls (Supplementary Figure S9C). Alcian Blue staining confirmed elevated proteoglycan deposition in TRPV4-OE MSCs after differentiation (Supplementary Figure S9D). Notably, mere pharmacological TRPV4 activation (GSK101) during MSCs chondrogenic induction paradoxically suppressed cartilage-specific gene expression versus agonist-free controls (Supplementary Figure S9E). To resolve this discrepancy, we applied cyclic tensile loading (5% strain, 0.2 Hz) to TRPV4-OE MSCs during chondrogenesis. Mechanically stimulated TRPV4-OE cells exhibited higher glycosaminoglycan accumulation and elevated Acan, Sox9, and Col2a1 mRNA than only tensile loading cultures group during differentiation duration (Supplementary Figures S9F and S9G). Subsequently, Western blot analysis was performed to determine protein content (Supplementary Figure S9H). These findings demonstrate that synergistic mechanical stimulation potentiates, rather than replaces, TRPV4-mediated chondrogenic differentiation. This in vitro mechanotransduction recapitulates treadmill training-enhanced cartilage regeneration observed in vivo, establishing combinatorial biomechanical and ion channel activation as a prerequisite for optimal chondrogenesis.

To elucidate mechanisms underlying mechanical stretch-induced chondrogenic differentiation, we performed transcriptome sequencing of a mechanically stimulated Trpv4-expressing cell line. Comparative RNA-seq analysis revealed significant transcriptional alterations in stretched Trpv4+ progenitors, visualized through a volcano plot highlighting DEGs relative to mock controls. After overexpression of TRPV4, a total of 631 DEGs were identified, of which 417 were highly expressed and 214 were lowly expressed (Supplementary Figure S10A). Subsequent KEGG and GO enrichment analyses of TRPV4-associated genes revealed preferential involvement in the calcium signalling and stem cell regulatory pathways. KEGG analysis identified significant enrichment in calcium signalling pathway and stem cell regulation (Supplementary Figures S10B and 10C). GSEA revealed that mechanically stimulated Trpv4+ related cells significantly upregulated multiple cytokine-related gene sets, including those involved in cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, cytokine activity, inflammatory response, positive regulation of cell communication, and regulation of cartilage development (Supplementary Figures S11A to S11E). Concordantly, the GO biological process analysis demonstrated pronounced enrichment in calcium-mediated signalling, calcineurin-NFAT signalling cascade, and chondrocyte differentiation (Supplementary Figure S10D). These findings indicate that the calcineurin-NFAT cascade may act as a downstream for TRPV4-mediated chondrogenic differentiation in progenitor cells. In conjunction with the activation of TRPV4, elevated cytosolic Ca2+ activates calcineurin. This may induce conformational changes exposing NFAT’s nuclear localization signals, facilitating its nuclear translocation and transcriptional regulation.36 This classical calcium signalling pathway critically regulates osteoclast activation, chondrocyte differentiation, and skeletal development and so on.37,38

Subsequently, we employed CellChat to analyze intercellular communication among various subpopulations (Wuhan University, China). The results revealed that the injured region exhibited a complex interaction network involving cell populations from distinct origins. Notably, TRPV4+ cells played a pivotal role in this network, acting as both major senders and receivers of signals (Supplementary Figure S11F). Furthermore, we observed particularly strong TGF-beta signalling in the communication between monocyte-macrophages and Trpv4+ cells (Supplementary Figure S11G), suggesting that immune cells are involved in the repair process after enthesis injury and may exert their effects through crosstalk with Trpv4+ cells. Based on bioinformatics findings, we performed functional validation experiments to clarify the mechanism of action of Trpv4 (Supplementary Figure S10F). Genetic lineage tracing of Trpv4+ cells was performed by intraperitoneal tamoxifen administration to Trpv4CreERT2;R26tdTomato mice followed by enthesis injury. Flow cytometric analysis of calcium influx intensity revealed dose-dependent enhancement in Trpv4+ cells following administration of the Trpv4 specific agonist GSK101. Notably, the sedentary group exhibited attenuated calcium influx compared to treadmill trained counterparts (Supplementary Figure S10G). As calcineurin activation initiates the calcineurin-Nfat signalling cascade, we assessed whether exercise potentiates this pathway by quantifying calcineurin phosphatase activity (Supplementary Figure S10H). Treadmill-trained mice exhibited significantly elevated calcineurin activity versus sedentary controls, demonstrating treadmill-induced potentiation of this signalling cascade (Supplementary Figure S10I).

However, the downstream NFAT isoform responsible for transduction of TRPV4-mediated mechanical stimulation in enthesis remains unidentified. We therefore intersected TRPV4-dependent genes, calcium signalling pathway-associated genes, and transcription factors, and intriguingly NFATc1 was the only one among these (Supplementary Figure S10E). This identifies NFATc1 as the key transducer coupling TRPV4 mechanosignalling to transcriptional regulation in chondrocyte differentiation. Furthermore, treadmill training significantly elevated the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio of NFATc1, indicating enhanced nuclear translocation and activation (Supplementary Figures 10J and S12A). As expected, nuclear NFATc1 protein expression in enthesis was significantly elevated in the training group compared to the sedentary group (Supplementary Figure S12B). Based on these findings, we constructed a TRPV4 gene-specific knockout mouse followed by treadmill training after enthesis injury (Supplementary Figure S12C). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed nuclear co-localization of Sox9 and NFATc1 in enthesis. Representative line scans demonstrated consistent overlap of Sox9 and NFATc1 fluorescence peaks (Supplementary Figure S12D). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between nuclear NFATc1 and Sox9 signal intensities, and the TRPV4 KO mice exhibited significantly higher nuclear expression compared to the wild type (Supplementary Figure S12E). These findings were further validated by Western blot quantification (Supplementary Figure S12F). Furthermore, we found that TRPV4 deletion reversed the increased protein expression level of Col II and Sox9, as well as attenuating the decrease in Mmp13 and Adamts5 in enthesis followed by treadmill exercise (Supplementary Figure S12G). In addition, the in vitro experimental results demonstrated that when NFATc1 was inhibited, the original chondrogenic differentiation effect was significantly suppressed, confirming that mechanical stimulus-induced chondrogenesis is mediated, at least in part, through the NFATc1 signalling axis (Supplementary Figure S11H).

Given the established mechanistic role of TRPV4+ cells in promoting chondrogenic differentiation through calcineurin activation via calcium-gated channels, we further investigated their therapeutic potential for enhancing enthesis regeneration post-injury. TRPV4+ progenitor cells were isolated from the enthesis of treadmill-trained mice following injury, expanded in vitro, and transplanted onto the enthesis injury site. Conventional histochemical staining revealed superior collagen fibre organization in TRPV4-treated specimens compared to disorganized arrangements in untreated controls. Transplantation of TRPV4+ progenitor cells into the injury site significantly enhanced enthesis regeneration, as evidenced by histomorphometry scores in treated specimens versus untreated controls (Supplementary Figure S13B). μCT analysis revealed superior bony restoration at the enthesis in treadmill-trained groups, demonstrating significantly reduced bone tissue defects at the calcaneal enthesis sites compared to untreated controls (Supplementary Figure S13C). Quantitative μCT analysis revealed significantly greater BMD and BV/TV at the enthesis site in Trpv4+ treated specimens compared to untreated controls. Notably, the treadmill training cohort exhibited significantly higher BV/TV and BS than the Trpv4+ progenitor cells treated group. These findings demonstrated that transplanted Trpv4+ intervention promotes superior mineralized tissue volume and elevated bone density at the enthesis, corroborated by significantly improved histomorphometry scores. These data indicate enhanced regenerative capacity and therapeutic efficacy of Trpv4+ treatment in enthesis repair.

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed substantially limited expression of osteogenesis and cartilaginous genes at the enthesis in the absence of TRPV4+ progenitor cell transplantation following injury. In contrast, TRPV4+ progenitor cell-treated defects exhibited markedly enhanced expression of both chondrocyte and osteoblast markers, indicating that TRPV4+ cell transplantation potentiates healing during enthesis repair (Supplementary Figure S13E). Furthermore, transplantation of treadmill-conditioned Trpv4+ progenitor cells significantly enhanced biomechanical properties of injured entheses at 58 DPI (Supplementary Figure S13F). These findings collectively demonstrate the therapeutic efficacy of exercise-primed TRPV4+ related cells in enhancing functional recovery after enthesis injury.

Discussion

The enthesis, the specialized interface between tendons and bones, serves a critical biomechanical function in the musculoskeletal system.39,40 The Achilles tendon, for instance, transmits muscular forces to the calcaneus to facilitate ankle motion.7 While previous research has elucidated the composition and function of fibrocartilaginous entheses, as well as the role of mechanical stimulation in their repair after injury, the specific mechanisms through which mechanical signals regulate chondrogenic differentiation within this fibrocartilaginous complex remain not fully understood.21

Advanced therapeutic strategies for tendon-bone interface injuries necessitate a thorough comprehension of enthesis microarchitecture. The graded transition from uncalcified to calcified cartilage provides the structural basis for physiological stress dissipation, whereas surgical repair typically yields biomechanically inferior fibrous scar tissue rather than functional regeneration.8,9,41 Notably, fibroblast-like stromal cells, commonly designated as tenocytes, populate the tendon compartment of the tendon-bone interface. On the other hand, phenotypically similar cells in ligaments, termed ligament cells, remain less characterized than their tendinous counterparts.42,43

Notably, developmentally analogous bipotent progenitors co-expressing Scx and Sox9 reside within entheses in postnatal development, demonstrating capacity for fibrocartilaginous differentiation under specific conditions. TRPV4+ cells predominantly localized to the enthesis and demonstrated self-renewal capacity with trilineage differentiation potential in TRPV4+ progenitor cells. Following tendon-bone interface injury, these cells demonstrated enhanced differentiation into chondrocytes and mineralizing chondrocytes after mechanical stimulation. Genetic depletion of TRPV4+ cells significantly reduced cartilage matrix deposition and mineralized tissue formation in the enthesis. TRPV4 functions as a mechanosensitive plasma membrane ion channel that transduces diverse mechanical stimuli—including membrane tension and stretch—into intracellular Ca²+ signalling through direct or indirect activation mechanisms.22-24,26

Moreover, research about the human anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) enthesis demonstrated that the femoral and tibial entheses exhibit distinct, graduated mineralization interfaces with site-specific biomolecular compositions. These structural gradients are finely tuned to resist distinct mechanical forces—shear stress at the femoral enthesis and tensile stress at the tibial enthesis.44 Clinical evidence substantiates that postoperative application of appropriate mechanical loading improves mechanical and biological parameters of enthesis.21,45 For example, following ACL reconstruction, patients typically initiate early, protected weightbearing within the first week in a progressive and controlled manner after which progressive, protected motion is introduced.46 Conversely, inadequate rehabilitation combined with a premature, non-objectively evaluated return to sports may limit sporting performance and predispose to subsequent injury.47 Notably, as a mechanism-focused study, we did not systematically compare the effects of varying exercise parameters—such as onset time, intensity, or duration—on enthesis healing outcomes, nor did we perform direct comparative analysis with human clinical samples. These factors limit the direct translational applicability of our findings. Future investigations incorporating multiparameter exercise regimens and cross-species comparative analyses are warranted to delineate the optimal mechanical loading conditions for postoperative rehabilitation and to enhance the clinical translatability of the current model.

At the tendon-bone interface, as evidenced in this study, TRPV4 serves as a central mechanobiological transducer, converting exercise-derived mechanical stimuli into regenerative programs that drive cartilaginous reconstruction and mineral gradient formation. To optimize rehabilitation outcomes, progressive physiological tensile loading during the postoperative remodelling phase is recommended to enhance graft-bone interface healing. Second, in cell therapy, Trpv4+ cells expanded via mechanical preconditioning significantly improved the quality of enthesis regeneration after transplantation, laying the foundation for future therapeutic strategies based on autologous mechanoresponsive cells. While Trpv4 activation promotes chondrogenesis and enthesis regeneration, its broad expression in tissues such as the synovium, sensory neurones, and vascular endothelium raises concerns regarding off-target effects. Excessive or uncontrolled activation of Trpv4 may lead to pain sensitization, aberrant inflammation, or pathological matrix remodelling. Therefore, local intervention using spatiotemporally controlled delivery systems targeting Trpv4 may minimize systemic exposure and unintended effects. Furthermore, the therapeutic efficacy of exercise-primed cell therapies may vary depending on age, baseline physical activity levels, and injury severity. Elderly or frail patients may exhibit reduced local mechanosensitivity or impaired regulation of progenitor cells, potentially limiting postoperative outcomes. Future studies should stratify patients by age and injury severity and explore adjunctive strategies, such as low-intensity pulsed ultrasound or pharmacological preconditioning, for individuals unable to tolerate active rehabilitation.

Furthermore, pharmacological modulation via local delivery of Trpv4 agonists also holds exploratory value but must be combined with mechanical stimulation to mimic physiological mechanotransduction. Notably, this mechanotransduction pathway may also contribute to heterotopic ossification in enthesis, and future therapeutic strategies should carefully balance regenerative benefits against the risk of pathological mineralization. This finding simultaneously provides novel strategies and directions for subsequent improvements in delivery systems and the more precise induction of cell differentiation.
