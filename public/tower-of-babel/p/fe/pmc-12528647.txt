Salvage use of the left hemicolon as colonic interposition for esophageal replacement: a case report
Authors: Jiaqi Ren, Yuxin Wen, Ziyan He, Xin Tang, Wenjuan Li, Xueyang Zhang, Weilin Liao, Xiaochuang Feng, Xiaosong Jiang, Dechang Diao
Published: 2025

Esophageal cancer is a prevalent malignancy within the digestive tract. According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines, surgical resection is the first-line treatment of non-metastatic esophageal cancer. Gastrointestinal reconstruction following esophagectomy serves as a critical surgical component. We present a case of a gastric cardia-esophagogastric tumor managed through gastrointestinal reconstruction. After the failure of jejunal and right hemicolon interposition, a salvage procedure using an isoperistaltic left hemicolon interposition was successfully performed, offering an alternative conduit for esophageal replacement.

Case report

On 19 February 2024, a 72-year-old man presented to The Second Clinical Medical College, Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (Guangzhou, China) with progressively worsening dysphagia and a sensation of throat obstruction. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy revealed a mass involving the gastric fundus, cardia, and esophagus (suspicious for carcinoma). Histopathologic examination confirmed malignant neoplasms of the gastric fundus and esophagus, consistent with poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma with probable neuroendocrine differentiation. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed asymmetric wall thickening extending from the distal esophagus to the gastric fundus and cardia, corresponding to a clinical stage of T4N+M0 (Figure 1A). Following three cycles of chemotherapy (Oxaliplatin plus Teysuno regimen) and concurrent neoadjuvant radiotherapy (50 Gy/25 F), the tumor regressed to ycT3N2M0 (Figure 1B). Surgical resection was performed on 3 June 2024. The procedure lasted 772 minutes, resulting in an intraoperative blood loss of 1,500 mL. The surgical field after gastrointestinal reconstruction is shown in Figure 1C. Postoperative pathological examination revealed the tumor stage as ypT3N1M0, with a tumor regression grade of 2.

(C) Renderings of gastrointestinal reconstruction. (D) Jejunal esophageal replacement. (E) Left hemicolon esophageal replacement. (F) Postoperative abdominal computed tomography scan.

The surgical procedure comprised two components: tumor resection and gastrointestinal reconstruction. The tumor resection procedure involved the mobilization of the intrathoracic esophagus and the entire stomach, clearing sentinel lymph nodes, transecting the cervical esophagus, and severing the duodenal bulb.

During the gastrointestinal reconstruction procedure, a 20-cm segment of the jejunum, located distal to the ligament of Treitz, was isolated. After separating its mesentery, the jejunum was transected. Subsequently, the jejunal stump was elevated to the level of the incision in the sternocleidomastoid muscle and was anastomosed manually. The transected duodenum was subsequently anastomosed to the distal jejunum, thereby completing the initial stage of the gastrointestinal reconstruction (Figure 1D).

However, the surgeon observed signs of congestion and necrosis in the elevated jejunum, likely induced by excessive tension that disrupted the nourishing blood vessels or venous return. The elevated jejunum continued to exhibit signs of congestion despite removal of the original esophagojejunal anastomosis and remobilization of the jejunal mesentery. Consequently, the surgeon decided to use the right hemicolon for gastrointestinal reconstruction. The right hemicolon and the terminal ileum were mobilized, and the ileocolic vessels were transected, ensuring the preservation of the left and right branches of the middle colic artery. The ileum was transected about 10 cm from the ileocecal junction, and the transverse colon was transected distal to the left branch of the middle colic artery. Unexpectedly, signs of congestion were observed in the mobilized colon, prompting the surgeon to resect the right hemicolon.

Subsequently, the surgeon mobilized the left segment of the transverse and descending colon, performing a transection of the inferior mesenteric vein and the initial branch of the sigmoid artery, while preserving the remaining left colic artery, sigmoid arteries, and superior rectal artery. The left segment of the colon was elevated, and the sigmoid colon was transected at the junction with the descending colon. Afterwards, the remaining jejunum was anastomosed to the descending colonic stump, while the terminal ileum was connected to the stump of the sigmoid colon. Additionally, the proximal colon was anastomosed to the esophagus, thereby completing the gastrointestinal reconstruction (Figure 1E).

The patient experienced complications, including pulmonary infection, left-sided pneumothorax, and pleural effusion. These issues were managed with thoracentesis, drainage, and anti-infective treatment, resulting in clinical improvement. The patient was subsequently discharged 15 days after the operation. At the most recent follow-up on 11 October 2024, the patient reported a moderate daily food intake and weight gain, and denied experiencing any symptoms, including acid reflux, nausea, or an unpleasant taste in the mouth. No disease progression was observed (Figure 1F), and the patient’s quality of life was satisfactory.

Discussion

This report discusses a case involving total gastrectomy and esophagectomy. After unsuccessful efforts to employ the jejunum and right hemicolon as esophageal substitutes, we conducted a successful “salvage” procedure using the left hemicolon as an isoperistaltic colonic interposition for esophageal replacement.

After a thorough review of our surgical notes and process, no abnormalities were identified in the jejunum or right hemicolon during mobilization. Notably, necrosis developed only after completion of the preparatory steps. Intraoperatively, the vascular arches within the mesentery appeared intact, and postoperative pathological examination of the specimens revealed no evidence of intravascular thrombosis. Therefore, it is unlikely that the necrosis in this case was caused by ischemia of the intestinal tube. According to similar cases of intestinal necrosis reported by Cheng et al. [1], we believe the congestion might be attributed to the patient’s poorly developed marginal arch vessels and insufficient venous return, which complicates the accurate assessment of the colon’s venous drainage status during the clamping test when both arteries and veins are simultaneously occluded.

Herein, we reached the following important conclusions. (i) Pre-esophageal substitution surgery requires thorough bowel preparation to ensure an alternative intestinal segment is available if needed. (ii) The blood supply of the chosen intestinal segment must be meticulously evaluated. Intraoperative clamping tests should be routinely performed to confirm whether the graft has adequate blood supply through the pulsatile flow of the marginal arteries. When necessary, angiography and three-dimensional vascular reconstruction should be employed to confirm the vascular anatomy of the selected intestinal segment. Evaluation of blood supply should consider arterial capacity, venous caliber, and permeability. (iii) During the surgical procedure, special care should be taken to protect the pedicle vessels and the vascular arcades of the colon to avoid vascular injury. (iv) If intestinal necrosis is detected, the necrotic tissue should be promptly removed to prevent severe infections or sepsis. (v) If necrosis occurs in the primary intestinal segment, use another segment to avoid jejunostomy and minimize the impact on postoperative quality of life impact. (vi) The left hemicolon is preferable as an alternative due to its adequate length, flexible mesentery, smaller lumen, consistent left colic artery, and superior marginal vein anastomosis [2–6]. Consequently, we recommend prioritizing the left hemicolon supplied by the left colic artery when using the colon as a substitute.

Based on the surgical experience from this case, we recommend that clinicians consider the left hemicolon as a viable alternative for gastrointestinal reconstruction when other intestinal grafts fail.
