Transforming waste pharmaceutical blister into sustainable concrete
Authors: Rohan Kumar Choudhary, Awdhesh Kumar Choudhary, Keshav Kumar Sharma, Bheem Pratap, Perumal Asaithambi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing global focus on sustainability and decarbonization has highlighted the urgent need for effective plastic waste management strategies, including their potential reuse in construction materials. Pharmaceutical blister packaging, primarily composed of plastic aluminium laminates, represents a growing post-consumer waste stream, further exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic due to increased reliance on solid medications. Recycling waste pharmaceutical blisters (WPBs) poses significant challenges; however, its incorporation into concrete offers a sustainable alternative for waste utilization. This study explores the feasibility of using WPB in M30 concrete by developing two mix categories through the absolute volume method: (i) direct addition of WPB (0–30% by weight of sand) and (ii) partial replacement of sand with WPB (5–30%). Compressive strength tests identified 20% substitution as optimal, with mixes achieving 92–95% of control strength. Non-destructive evaluation using the Schmidt Rebound Hammer validated destructive testing results. Water absorption analysis revealed that partial sand replacement provided better resistance compared to direct addition. Furthermore Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analyses of the optimum mix after 90 days confirmed well-developed hydration products and strong interfacial bonding between WPB fibers and the cementitious matrix. The results demonstrate that WPB can be effectively utilized in low-strength concrete, providing a promising solution for pharmaceutical waste management while contributing to circular economy and decarbonization goals in the construction sector. Future studies should investigate durability under aggressive environments, behavior at elevated temperatures, and flexural performance, along with advanced microstructural characterization to better understand interfacial transition zones.Keywords: Waste, Pharmaceutical, Blister, Concrete, SustainabilitySubject terms: Environmental sciences, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Concrete is the second most consumed material on Earth, following only water. Its importance in modern construction is unparalleled, forming the backbone of infrastructure development worldwide. In 2002, global concrete consumption stood at approximately 2.7 billion cubic meters. With accelerating urbanization and industrial growth, this figure is expected to surge to nearly 7.5 billion cubic meters by 20501–3. Concrete demand is generally divided into two categories: high-strength applications such as bridges, dams, and skyscrapers, and lower-strength uses like pavements and architectural elements4. Cement production is a major concern, contributing around 6% of global CO₂ emissions, with an annual growth rate of 1–2%, making it one of the most environmentally harmful industries5. India ranks second globally in cement production, after Europe. Increased demand for fine aggregates has intensified river sand mining, leading to soil erosion, water pollution, and ecosystem disruption. To mitigate these impacts, researchers are developing sustainable alternatives by incorporating industrial by-products and waste materials such as fly ash, slag, and recycled plastics into concrete. These approaches reduce dependence on natural resources while supporting carbon reduction, waste management, and environmental sustainability in the construction sector1–4 Estimates suggest that hospital-generated plastic waste rose nearly sixfold compared to pre-pandemic levels5,6 Following the COVID-19 outbreak, the surge in medication use accelerated pharmaceutical production, which consequently led to a substantial increase in waste pharmaceutical blisters—the dominant packaging material for most drugs7–9. People tend to prefer solid drugs over bottled medications in daily life due to restrictions on the use of bottled drugs once opened, as they deteriorate rapidly upon contact with air. Solid drugs make dosage simpler and more intuitive10. Pharmaceutical technology has evolved rapidly over the last few decades, especially in drug delivery and protection. However, blister packaging remains a primary choice for medicine packaging9,10. Blister packs are not only favoured by consumers but also by pharmaceutical companies because they are smaller, lighter, and easier to store and transport compared to typical pill bottles. The demand for blister packaging was expected to reach 5.18 thousand tonnes in 2021. The pharmaceutical blister packaging market is projected to reach US$149.3 billion by 2026, growing at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 6% between 2019 and 20296–18. Blister packaging contributes significantly to waste, accounting for 4% by weight of the total packaging waste generated daily9. Currently, the disposal options for blisters include landfilling, direct recycling, and incineration. Incineration with mixed municipal waste can generate hazardous pollutants such as nitrous oxide, sulphur oxides, dust, and chlorine-containing dioxins17,18. Recycling blisters by separating aluminium and plastic layers requires a chemical and/or physical process, which is energy-intensive and involves the use of organic solvents or corrosive reagents, offering no guarantee of sustainability Due to the structural complexity of pharmaceutical blisters, the most straightforward disposal method is landfilling19–21. However, extensive landfilling can lead to environmental problems, such as the accumulation of aluminium in soil, causing acidification and adverse effects on plants, animals, and fish18–26. Blister packages are increasingly considered plastic waste due to their high plastic content, and they are often sent to facilities where the metal is not recovered27–35. This makes landfilling an inadequate waste management solution31 A dedicated facility is needed to efficiently recycle both the plastic and metal components of blisters30 Combining two critical environmental issues CO₂ emissions from concrete production and the disposal challenges of non-biodegradable pharmaceutical blister waste into a unified solution by integrating WPB into concrete offers a promising pathway toward sustainable construction. Addressing both concrete sustainability and pharmaceutical waste management has therefore become a pressing need36–41. Several studies have investigated the incorporation of medical and pharmaceutical wastes into concrete. Kumar42 utilized geopolymer concrete with incinerated biomedical waste ash (IBWA) as a replacement for GGBS, Essam et al.6 incorporated face masks, radiology sheets, and latex gloves into fiber-reinforced concrete, Nataraja43 studied biomedical waste incinerator ash (BMIA) as a cement substitute, Matalkah Faris44 explored medical waste bottom ash (MWBA) to enhance reactivity, Katare et al.45 examined incinerated biomedical waste ash (IBMW) for strength and permeability, Kremser et al.46 investigated blister waste recycling using biogenic sulfuric acid, and Dalal et al.47 tested blister powder as a partial fine aggregate replacement. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that medical and pharmaceutical wastes can be successfully incorporated into concrete, with cement or sand typically replaced at levels of 20–30%, either by weight or by volume. However, most research has focused on incinerated or powdered forms of medical waste. Very few studies have examined the direct incorporation of intact WPB into concrete without altering its chemical properties. The potential of WPB to act as a micro-reinforcement enhancing post-crack behavior while maintaining workability remains largely unexplored. Larger blister sizes tend to form balls during mixing, while excessively small particles behave like fines and lose the intended reinforcement effect, highlighting the need for systematic evaluation. To address this gap, the present study investigates the direct use of WPB in concrete through two approaches: (i) as an additional filler material (0–30% by weight of concrete) and (ii) as a partial replacement for fine aggregate (0–30% by weight). The compressive strength of all mixes is tested to determine the optimum WPB content. The selected optimum mix is further analyzed using SEM and XRD for microstructural characterization. Both destructive and non-destructive tests (NDT) are employed to evaluate compressive strength, while water absorption is measured at 90 days. This study provides new insights into the potential of WPB as a sustainable, performance-enhancing material in concrete.

Materials and methods

Waste pharmaceutical blister (WPBs)

Pharmaceutical blister packaging is primarily composed of Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET)47 available in both amorphous and semi-crystalline forms. PET is widely used in pharmaceutical packaging due to its lightweight nature, strength, impact resistance, and effective barrier properties against water, moisture, and gases. These characteristics make it suitable for protecting medicines from environmental exposure.

For this study, WPBs were collected from local medical shops and hospitals. To ensure safety, the blisters were dry-cleaned, exposed to direct sunlight for at least seven days, and turned daily. No chemical disinfectants were applied, as the blisters were uncontaminated and chemical treatment could leave undesirable residues. Since WPBs serve only as tertiary packaging and do not come into direct contact with medicines, the risk of chemical interaction with pharmaceuticals is negligible48–51. The collected WPBs were then cut into strips measuring 65–90 mm in length and 0.5–0.8 mm in width, dimensions determined through both literature evidence and preliminary trials.

Previous research on incorporating plastic wastes such as PET, HDPE, and multilayer foils into concrete has shown that narrow strips (<1 mm wide) with moderate aspect ratios improve crack-bridging capacity and post-crack toughness while maintaining workability52. Conversely, excessively long strips tend to entangle within the mix, reducing workability, whereas very fine particles act more like fillers than reinforcements, contributing little to structural performance53,54.

As illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2, the selected strip dimensions were intended to act as micro-reinforcement, enhancing ductility and post-crack resistance without significantly affecting slump values. During preliminary trials, different size ranges (50–100 mm × 0.5–1.0 mm) were tested. Larger strips decreased dispersion and increased clumping, while smaller ones provided insufficient reinforcing action. The adopted size range of 65–90 mm × 0.5–0.8 mm therefore offered the best balance between workability, dispersion, and reinforcement efficiency. For mix design, WPBs were incorporated in two forms: width-wise strips (as filler material) and length-wise strips (as partial fine aggregate replacement). This size range can be easily achieved using simple cutting tools, making the method feasible in low-resource or field conditions without requiring specialized equipment. One practical challenge encountered, however, was the reluctance of hospitals to supply WPBs, largely due to concerns about their potential misuse for counterfeit medicines or illicit activities.

Materials

The cement used in this study is Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) of grade 53, composed of 90–100% clinker and 0–5% gypsum. It has a specific gravity of 3.11 and a fineness modulus of 4.5%. The initial setting time is 30 minutes, while the final setting time is 10 hours. The fine aggregates used are river sand, prepared according to the graded sand requirements of IS 383:2016. The sand has a particle size of less than 4.75 mm, a specific gravity of 2.67, and a fineness modulus of 2.8. The coarse aggregates range in size from 20 to 40 mm.

Experimental design and testing

In this study, two categories of M30 concrete mixes incorporating pharmaceutical blister waste (WPB) were designed using the absolute volume method as per IS 10262:2009, with a constant water-to-cement ratio (w/c) of 0.45. The slump test for the control mixes recorded values in the range of 88–105 mm, indicating good workability. For the WPB modified mixes, the optimum slump range was observed at 65–95 mm, while mixes with higher WPB content exhibited significantly reduced workability, with slump values falling below 50 mm. The first category consisted of direct incorporation of WPB, without removing any other constituent, at levels of 0%, 10%, 20%, and 30% by weight of sand. These mixes were designated as S0 (control, 0% WPB), S1 (10% WPB), S2 (20% WPB), and S3 (30% WPB). The second category involved partial substitution of sand with WPB at replacement levels ranging from 5 to 30% by weight of sand. These were designated as S4 (5% WPB), S5 (10% WPB), S6 (15% WPB), S7 (20% WPB), S8 (25% WPB), and S9 (30% WPB). The control mix S0 served as the reference for both series. Detailed mix proportions are presented in Table 1. To ensure volumetric consistency, all mixes were proportioned using the absolute volume method, maintaining the total volume at 1 m3. WPB has a higher bulk density (≈ 2710 kg/m3, due to its aluminum–plastic laminate composition)55 than natural sand (≈ 1682 kg/m3). Therefore, a simple weight-for-weight replacement would distort the fine-aggregate fraction, alter yield, and adversely affect workability. To address this, a volumetric compensation procedure was adopted: (i) the sand mass removed was first converted into its equivalent volume, (ii) the corresponding WPB mass was then determined using its measured density, and (iii) further trial adjustments were made to maintain workable mixes. During preliminary trials, full theoretical substitution of WPB led to reduced workability and compaction difficulties, owing to the smooth, hydrophobic surface of WPB particles. Optimized mixes were therefore prepared by slightly reducing the WPB content and compensating the remaining volume with sand, ensuring both yield balance and desirable workability. The replacement ratios were finalized to reflect a balance between volumetric equivalence and practical performance. These findings are consistent with previous research, where incorporation of plastic-based wastes beyond moderate levels often causes slump loss and segregation, while low to moderate levels (≈ 10–25%) can be accommodated without significant performance loss56,57. Thus, the final mix proportions in Table 1 simultaneously satisfied: (i) the M30 mix design requirements for strength and workability, and (ii) the performance-based optimization of WPB dosage for practical use. No chemical admixtures were used. For each mix, three sets of 150 × 150 × 150 mm cubes were cast, demoulded after 24 hours, and subjected to standard water curing, as shown in Fig. 2.

The physical and mechanical properties of the mixes were evaluated through a series of standardized tests. Compressive strength tests were conducted on concrete cubes at curing ages of 3, 7, 28, 60, and 90 days to monitor strength development over time. Typical crack propagation and failure patterns are illustrated in Fig. 2. In addition, a rebound hammer test was carried out on surface-dry specimens after 90 days of curing. The rebound values were averaged, and equivalent compressive strength values were calculated, enabling a direct comparison between destructive and non-destructive test results. To assess durability, water absorption tests were performed after 90 days of curing. The procedure involved oven-drying the specimens followed by water immersion, in accordance with the relevant standards. Further, microstructural characterization was undertaken. SEM and XRD analyses were performed on the specimen that achieved the highest compressive strength at 90 days, corresponding to the optimum WPB dosage. These analyses provided insights into hydration products, pore distribution, and phase composition of the modified concrete. Results and discussion

Compressive strength

Concrete gains strength over time due to ongoing hydration, with approximately 90% of its ultimate strength typically achieved by 28 days. However, strength development continues beyond this period, albeit at a slower rate. To evaluate the compressive strength development, tests were performed on cube specimens at 3, 7, 28, 60, and 90 days in accordance with Indian Standard guidelines, using three cubes per mix and reporting the average peak load. At early ages, concrete typically achieved 60–70% of its 28-day strength. For instance, 3-day strengths ranged from 40 to 55% of the 28-day strength, reflecting the influence of hydration kinetics. The strength gain of concrete with different curing periods has also been observed by other authors, suggesting that concrete continues to gain strength with age58,59. The incorporation of WPB material significantly influenced early strength gain. Moderate substitution levels, such as in specimen S2, improved pore structure and hydration, resulting in enhanced early-age strength. In contrast, higher WPB replacement levels caused a noticeable decline in strength at both 3 and 7 days. The compressive strength of all mixes followed an increasing trend over time. Control samples exhibited the highest early and later-age strength. At lower WPB replacement levels (up to 15%), both early and long-term strength remained relatively high, indicating good compatibility between the plastic material and cement. The strength gains from 7 to 28 days ranged from 57 to 80%, further confirming an effective hydration process. In the first mix design, strength differences among specimens were modest. S1 and S2 varied by 4–6%, while S3 showed a 2.5% increase. From 28 to 60 days, strength gains were smaller, with S4–S9 exhibiting increases between 1.5 and 2.8%. A more pronounced strength development occurred from 60 to 90 days. In the first mix, S1 increased by 7.8%, S2 by 11.3% (the highest), and S3 by 4.9%. In the second mix, S4, S5, and S8 recorded increases of 2.5%, 2%, and 4%, respectively, while S6 showed a 4% increase, S7 the highest at 9.3%, and S9 the lowest at 3.1%, as illustrated in Figs. 3 and 4.

Compressive strength of concrete with WPBs as a partial substitute for fine aggregate at 3, 7, 28, 60, and 90 days.

These results indicate that concrete with moderate WPB content can enhance load-bearing capacity by filling pores and improving the concrete matrix. However, excessive replacement led to reduced strength due to insufficient matrix densification and compromised structural integrity. According to standard guidelines, pozzolanic materials must achieve a Strength Activity Index of at least 75%. In this study, only specimen S2 met this requirement, while others fell short. Given the aim to promote sustainable practices by minimizing waste, a 20% replacement level was deemed optimal for both mix designs represented by S2 and S7. Consequently, SEM and XRD analyses were performed exclusively on these two specimens. For comparison, the control specimen (S0) showed a strength increase of 2.4% at 28 days and 3.09% at 90 days.

Rebound hammer test on concrete

Non-Destructive Testing (NDT) was employed in this study to evaluate the performance of WPB concrete, cross-verify results, and ensure applicability in both laboratory and field conditions. Unlike natural sand, WPBs differ in geometry and density, which can cause segregation or voids in the concrete matrix. NDT enabled the detection of internal defects such as honeycombing, poor compaction, and hidden cracks that are not visible externally but critically influence strength and durability. After 90 days of curing, concrete cubes were assessed using the Schmidt rebound hammer to non-destructively estimate compressive strength and correlate the results with conventional destructive testing. Prior to testing, the hammer was calibrated, and concrete surfaces were smoothed to ensure consistent readings. The plunger was initially set to zero with light pressure, then positioned perpendicular to the concrete surface. Gradual pressure was applied until the hammer delivered an impact, locking the plunger for measurement. To maintain stability and minimize movement, each specimen was securely positioned in a universal testing machine (UTM), as shown in Fig. 5.

Schmidt Rebound Hammer Test on Concrete positioned in a universal testing machine at 90 days.

Each cube was marked at six locations, and rebound readings were taken from these points to calculate an average value. The recorded rebound values ranged from a minimum of 22 MPa to a maximum of 38 MPa. These values closely correlated with those obtained from conventional compressive strength tests. As depicted in Figs. 6, 7, and 8, the rebound hammer results reflected the same increasing and decreasing strength trends observed in both mix designs. Although minor discrepancies were noted in a few specimens, the deviations remained within acceptable limits, confirming the reliability of the rebound hammer as a non-destructive evaluation tool. The accuracy of the rebound hammer in predicting actual concrete strength has also been questioned by other authors, suggesting that it may not provide reliable estimates of true strength60. While the test may not yield precise absolute strength values, its effectiveness in capturing overall strength trends proved valuable in identifying the optimal WPB substitution level.

Water absorption test

Water absorption in concrete is relatively easy to measure but challenging to standardize across different studies due to variations in laboratory conditions. Several factors influence water absorption, including matrix homogenization, water-to-cement ratio, cement hydration, cement type, and the presence of mineral additives. The microstructure of the cement paste, particularly capillary porosity and pore size distribution, plays a crucial role in determining absorption levels. Achieving low porosity and a dense microstructure requires proper compaction, adequate curing, and the incorporation of pozzolanic additives. High-quality concrete typically exhibits water absorption between 4 and 6%, with values below 5% considered ideal. In this study, the first mix design (Incorporating of WPBs), resulted in a gradual increase in water absorption compared to the control samples. Water absorption increased by 13.48% from S0 to S1, 25.00% from S1 to S2, and 16.50% from S2 to S3. Conversely, in the second mix design, where sand was partially replaced with WPBS, water absorption showed a decreasing trend. It declined by 7.69% from S4 to S5, 4.17% from S5 to S6, 13.04% from S6 to S7, 8.00% from S7 to S8, and 3.26% from S8 to S9, as illustrated in Figs. 9 and 10.

Water absorption percentage for concrete mixes incorporating different WPB at 90 days.

Water absorption percentage for concrete mixes with WPB as a partial substitute for fine aggregate at 90 days.

These variations suggest that external factors or systematic inconsistencies may have influenced the results. The differences in water absorption are likely attributed to improper pore size distribution, the phase composition of the cement matrix, and the hydrophobic nature of plastic. A low water absorption rate does not inherently indicate superior frost resistance in concrete, nor does a high absorption rate unequivocally denote poor frost resistance. Nonetheless, materials with lower absorption rates are often recommended by researchers to enhance safety margins in structural design61.

SEM and XRD analysis

SEM and XRD are well-established methods for investigating the microstructural features of hardened concrete. SEM allows visualization of pore structures, crack propagation, and morphological patterns, while XRD identifies and quantifies crystalline phases, enabling the tracking of hydration reactions. In this study, these techniques were applied to evaluate the influence of waste pharmaceutical blister on matrix densification and the development of hydration products. Although not the primary tools for characterizing WPB concrete, they were used to provide comparative insights into microstructural modifications. For detailed evaluation, only selected mixes were considered due to practical constraints. SEM was performed on three samples: S0 (0% WPB, control), S2 (20% WPB, optimum mix), and S7 (20% WPB), as illustrated in Fig. 11. Complementary XRD analysis was conducted only on sample S2 to confirm phase identification (Fig. 12). The combined SEM–XRD observations indicated that sample S2 contained distinct hexagonal plate-like crystals attributed to portlandite (CH). This was corroborated by clear XRD reflections at approximately 18.0° and 34.1° (2θ). In addition, fibrous, foil-like layers corresponding to calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel were observed bridging voids and surrounding particles. These morphological features were consistent with the broad amorphous hump in the 20–35° (2θ) range of the XRD spectrum. Compared with the control (S0), both S2 and S7 demonstrated a denser, more compact matrix with fewer large pores and greater amounts of C–S–H gel, highlighting the refinement effect of WPB incorporation. Nevertheless, the hydrophobic nature of WPB can hinder its adhesion with the cement matrix, potentially affecting the interfacial transition zone (ITZ). Similar microstructural responses have been reported in studies using polymeric or plastic fillers in cement-based composites57,62,63. Thus, the interpretation of Fig. 11 was not based solely on SEM images but was further supported by XRD confirmation (Fig. 12). Given the complex nature of concrete microstructure, SEM and XRD alone cannot fully describe the performance of WPB-concrete. Future research may benefit from incorporating advanced techniques such as Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) and X-ray Fluorescence (XRF) to provide more comprehensive insights, particularly in identifying microcracks and characterizing weaker ITZ regions.

Environmental and engineering perspective on WPBs

Recycling waste pharmaceutical blisters (WPBs) is a complex challenge, particularly on a small scale, due to the difficulties in collecting, sorting, and separating hazardous components. Large-scale recycling systems are more viable, yet only a few countries have developed such infrastructure. As a result, most WPBs are disposed of through municipal waste streams, primarily by incineration. This process significantly harms the environment since burning materials like polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polychlorotrifluoroethylene, and olefins releases toxic emissions including carbon monoxide, dioxins, and chlorinated furans that contribute to global warming and climate change. From an engineering standpoint, corrosion of steel reinforcement remains a leading cause of concrete deterioration, shortening service life and, in severe cases, causing structural failure. WPBs, containing durable plastics, offer a potential alternative reinforcement material that is inherently resistant to corrosion. In fiber form, WPBs are lighter and safer to handle than traditional steel, while potentially improving long-term durability. The integration of WPBs into concrete addresses two pressing concerns: sustainable waste management of pharmaceutical plastics and the promotion of a circular economy. This strategy is particularly valuable for structures where moderate load capacity is sufficient, but durability, service life, and sustainability are critical—such as in pharmaceutical facilities and other safety-sensitive environments.

Nevertheless, while the environmental and structural benefits of WPB utilization are promising, their viability has yet to be substantiated through life cycle assessment (LCA) or comprehensive economic studies. Future research should therefore include LCA and cost–benefit analyses to evaluate the environmental impact, financial feasibility, and scalability of WPB-based construction solutions.

Conclusion

The use of Waste Pharmaceutical Blisters (WPB) as a partial replacement material in concrete has shown encouraging outcomes. Replacements of up to 20% maintain acceptable workability, achieve 92–95% of the compressive strength of control mixes, and improve sustainability by reducing natural sand consumption while diverting non-biodegradable plastics from landfills and incineration. Such WPB-based concrete is particularly suitable for non-structural or low-to-moderate strength applications, such as pavement sub-bases, highway medians, and similar infrastructure, where long-term durability is prioritized over high strength. This study examines WPB utilization both as a fiber reinforcement and as a partial sand substitute for sustainable concrete production. Raw pharmaceutical blister packs with aluminum coatings were processed and incorporated into mixes at 10%, 20%, and 30% by volume. In addition, sand was partially replaced with WPB at 5%, 10%, and 15% using the absolute volume method. The objective was to reduce CO₂ emissions and mitigate the environmental challenges of WPB disposal. Compressive strength tests were conducted to determine the optimum replacement levels, supported by supplementary evaluations such as water absorption, non-destructive testing, and microstructural analyses.

Scope for future work and limitations

Future studies can focus on assessing the behavior of concrete containing WPB when subjected to aggressive exposure conditions that significantly influence durability. The influence of WPB on concrete performance under elevated temperatures also warrants further investigation. In addition, exploring flexural strength behavior will provide a broader understanding of its structural applicability. For a deeper microstructural evaluation, advanced techniques such as Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) and X-ray Fluorescence (XRF) may be employed. These tools can help in identifying the presence of microcracks and in characterizing weaker interfacial transition zone (ITZ) regions, thereby offering more detailed insights into the material’s performance. Furthermore, incorporating larger sample sizes and factorial experimental designs with ANOVA will help quantify the statistical significance of influencing factors.
