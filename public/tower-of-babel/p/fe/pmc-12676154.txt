Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) provides high diagnostic accuracy for coronary artery disease but contributes to cumulative radiation exposure, which carries a measurable malignancy risk. A recent analysis in the Journal of the American Medical Association of over 121,000 computed tomography (CT) examinations projected that, if current usage and dose levels persist, CT imaging could account for approximately 5% of new cancer cases in the United States annually. Although CCTA represents only about 1% of the projected cases, its radiation exposure is modifiable through optimization strategies.1

Evidence from multicenter studies demonstrates that substantial radiation dose reduction is achievable without compromising diagnostic quality. The PROTECTION VI trial achieved a median dose of 2.7 millisievert (mSv) across 61 centers with no increase in nondiagnostic scans,2 and a low-tube-potential subanalysis demonstrated median doses as low as 1.4 to 2.2 mSv with stable diagnostic rates and improved image quality.3 The SCOT-HEART trial reported a median dose of 4.1 mSv alongside increased diagnostic certainty.4

Baseline audits conducted within a 5-hospital health system from February to July 2024 revealed a median system-wide CCTA dose of 6.2 mSv, with significant intercampus variability ranging from 2.9 to 12.5 mSv. These findings exceeded the performance demonstrated in multicenter trials and highlighted the need for protocol optimization and standardization. Guided by this evidence, a pragmatic best-practice goal of a median CCTA dose of ≤4 mSv was set. This target is supported by the 2021 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology chest pain guideline5 and the Society of Cardiovascular Computed Tomography expert consensus,6 which—although not prescribing a fixed numeric threshold—explicitly endorse the ALARA (“as low as reasonably achievable”) principle,7 protocol standardization, and ongoing dose monitoring as essential quality metrics for CCTA programs. These findings formed the foundation for a system-wide quality improvement initiative to standardize protocols and reduce radiation exposure while maintaining diagnostic quality.

Project Rationale

Radiation dose reduction is a measurable, evidence-supported patient safety goal. Large-scale population studies have shown that cumulative radiation from medical imaging contributes to overall cancer incidence, with CT estimated to account for up to approximately 5% of new cases annually in the United States if current dose levels persist.1 While the individual risk from a single CCTA is low, the rapid growth in cardiac CT use, combined with the potential for repeat imaging, amplifies the importance of minimizing exposure wherever possible. Beyond patient safety, dose optimization is an important quality metric in cardiovascular imaging, supported by professional society guidelines that advocate for the ALARA principle without compromising diagnostic image quality.5, 6, 7

Project Description

A multidisciplinary team was convened to develop and implement a standardized CCTA protocol across all campuses in the health system. Leadership appointed a dedicated CCTA medical director to oversee the project and ensure alignment across sites. Baseline radiation dose data were collected between February and July 2024, revealing significant variability in exposure levels. Based on these findings, the team designed standardized protocols emphasizing prospective electrocardiogram gating, reduced field of view, body mass index (BMI)–tailored tube voltage (kilovoltage peak [kVp]), and the elimination of redundant imaging phases. For the single-source scanners, prospective scanning between 60% to 80% of the cardiac cycle was implemented, using 100 kVp for patients with BMI <30 and 120 kVp for BMI ≥30. For the dual-source scanners, systolic imaging between 250 to 450 milliseconds was implemented, with 100 kVp for patients with BMI <30 and 120 kVp for BMI ≥30.

In September 2024, these protocols were implemented system-wide, accompanied by hands-on training sessions for CT technologists to promote adoption and address site-specific challenges. After implementation, radiation dose data were reassessed from September 2024 through February 2025, and multidisciplinary meetings were held regularly to review performance metrics, gather feedback, and identify further improvement opportunities (Figure 1).

Project Deliverables

The project sought to align system-wide CCTA radiation doses with the ≤4 mSv best-practice goal derived from multicenter trial performance2, 3, 4 and consistent with the principles outlined in professional society guidelines.5, 6, 7 The project also aimed to reduce variability in radiation exposure between the 5 campuses (designated here as CCH, CP, GC, HP, and LM), ensuring consistent protocol adherence regardless of scanner type or site. Maintaining or improving diagnostic image quality, as measured by an independent outside source (HeartFlow CCTA fractional flow reserve derived from CT [FFRct] diagnostic acceptance rate), was an equally important deliverable.8

Project Outcome, Impact and Future Directions

Implementation of the standardized protocols resulted in a reduction of the median system-wide radiation dose from 6.2 to 4.8 mSv, representing a 23% decrease (Figure 2). Dose consistency was achieved across all 5 campuses despite differences in scanner models and baseline practices. Diagnostic quality, measured by the FFRct acceptance rate, improved overall from 90.5% to 92.7%, reflecting a 2.2% increase (95% confidence interval: 0.4%-4.0%; P = 0.019). The largest improvement occurred at the LM campus, which increased by 21.9% (95% confidence interval: 14.0%-29.8%; P < 0.001), followed by a steady gain at the CP campus of 2.1% (95% confidence interval: 0.4%-3.9%; P = 0.018). The CCH and GC campuses demonstrated modest, non–statistically significant increases, and the HP campus experienced a small, non–statistically significant decrease but remained above 90% and within confidence limits (Figure 3).

The impact of this initiative demonstrates that protocol standardization can be successfully scaled across diverse facilities without compromising image quality. Looking ahead, the health system plans to enhance protocol performance by acquiring additional dual-source CT scanners, introducing systolic phase imaging protocols to reduce the need for heart rate control medications, and appointing site-specific quality assurance technologists to maintain adherence. Continuous monitoring of radiation doses and ongoing staff education will remain central to sustaining these improvements over the long term.

Discussion

This quality improvement project demonstrates that targeted, evidence-based interventions—when supported by leadership and multidisciplinary collaboration—can significantly reduce CCTA radiation doses across a multicenter health system. Dose reduction was accompanied by maintained, and in some cases improved, diagnostic acceptance rates, underscoring the feasibility of achieving safety gains without compromising quality. Although this was a cardiology-led quality improvement initiative, it was implemented through a multidisciplinary team that included radiologists, advanced practice clinicians, radiology technologists, and nurses. The proposed protocols were developed by the cardiology imaging team and subsequently reviewed and endorsed by radiology leadership without opposition. The protocol changes were transparent to patients and did not alter their overall experience.

A critical element of the quality improvement initiative was systematic tracking of radiation doses for CCTA. Such monitoring is imperative for a CCTA program committed to quality and patient safety. Historically, radiation exposure monitoring is not consistent in all programs, limiting insight into actual performance.9 When the cardiology program assumed ownership of dose monitoring, elevated and highly variable baseline doses were identified, providing a clear impetus for change and improvement. Establishing routine dose tracking not only confirmed this performance gap but also created the infrastructure for continuous quality improvement. The resulting transparency fostered engagement from technologists, cardiologists, radiologists and administrators, and ensured that subsequent interventions could be tailored to address site-specific challenges.

This approach aligns with published literature, including the PROTECTION VI2,3 and SCOT-HEART4 trials, which demonstrated that meaningful dose reduction is possible without loss of diagnostic yield. By tailoring protocols to patient BMI, prioritizing prospective gating, and streamlining imaging phases, we were able to achieve results in real-world practice that were comparable to national benchmarks.

Scalability is supported by the success across sites with differing scanner types and baseline practices, indicating that similar health systems could reproduce these results by combining standardized protocols with structured implementation, training, and continuous monitoring.

Despite not achieving the target goal of ≤4mSv, significant improvement was achieved in the intervention phase with further work planned towards future improvement.

Conclusions

System-wide CCTA radiation dose optimization is achievable through standardized protocols, leadership engagement, and multidisciplinary education. Sustained improvement requires continuous monitoring and adaptation to evolving technology.
