Biochemical and structural differences between C3 cotyledons and C4 leaves in species of Salsoloideae (Chenopodiaceae)
Authors: Ferit Kocacinar, Faik Ceylan, Sabahattin Cömertpay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractC4 plants had evolved from C3 ancestors in at least more than 60 independent lineages of angiosperms, most probably in response to decreasing levels of atmospheric CO2 and environmental conditions promoting photorespiration. Salsola, Petrosimonia, and Cyathobasis genera of Salsoloideae subfamily contain some species with C3 cotyledons followed by C4 leaves. The aim of this study was to determine and compare the biochemical and structural differences between C3 cotyledons and subsequent C4 leaves in these genera. The results showed that there were isopalisade C3 cotyledons in Salsola grandis and isolateral C3 cotyledons in Cyathobasis fruticulosa, while salsoloid type C4 Kranz anatomy was present in subsequent leaves. Petrosimonia nigdeensis had dorsiventral C3 cotyledons and salsoloid-type C4 leaves. The proteins of C4 isoform phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) and pyruvate orthophosphate dikinase (PPDK) enzymes were absent or very sparse in C3 cotyledons of these species, whereas they were abundant in their C4 leaves. The proteins of photorespiratory isoform glycolate oxidase (GOX) and glycine decarboxylase-H subunit (GDC-H) were generally higher in cotyledons than leaves. The protein level of photosynthetic isoform ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco) enzyme was lower in C4 leaves compared to C3 cotyledons. Transcript levels of these enzymes were generally consistent with their protein content except for GOX in S. grandis and S. tragus and glycine decarboxylase complex (GDC) in S. tragus. As a result, we demonstrated that not only the protein and transcript levels of the enzymes required for C4 pathway increased, but also those levels of C3 and photorespiratory enzymes decreased during the transition from C3 cotyledons to C4 leaves. These results are considered significant in terms of shedding light on the understanding of evolutionary transition from C3 to C4 biochemical pathway in a single plant and contributing to C4 engineering.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20388-w.Keywords: C3 cotyledons and C4 leaves, Salsoloideae, Leaf anatomy, Quantitative real time-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), Western blot, Photosynthetic enzymes, C4 evolutionSubject terms: Plant sciences, Photosynthesis, C3 photosynthesis, C4 photosynthesis, Rubisco

Introduction

Photosynthesis is a series of biochemical reactions where photosynthetic organisms assimilate atmospheric CO2 and convert it to organic substances using the key enzyme, ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase-oxygenase (Rubisco). Rubisco’s carboxylation activity is partly inhibited under very low past and current atmospheric CO2 levels, due to the oxygenation reaction, where molecular O2 is fixed1.

The oxygenation reaction of Rubisco yields 3-phosphoglyceric acid (3-PGA) and 2-phosphoglycolate (2-PG). 2-PG has no use for C3 plants, and their excessive levels can be detrimental and toxic for photosynthetic enzymes, such as triose phosphate isomerase, and have to be processed in photorespiration or the so-called C2 oxidative photosynthetic cycle2,3. Because carboxylation and oxygenation take place in the same active site of Rubisco, as altering molecules for the enzyme, CO2 and O2 compete for reaction with ribulose-1,5-biphosphate. Although the adaptive value of photorespiration has not been yet elucidated completely, a critical function of photorespiration in C3 species may be defending the plants against biotic stresses, and there is a strong relationship between plant immunity and photorespiration4. On the other hand, photorespiration metabolism is metabolically costly due to the recycling of 2-PG and leads to a net loss of fixed carbon5. The inefficiency of photorespiration in C3 plants under certain conditions (high O2 and low CO2 concentrations in the atmosphere, high radiation and temperature, and water stress) had created the selective pressure for the evolution of the C4 pathway, which bypasses the photorespiration metabolism6.

Under non-stressful conditions in C3 plants, photorespiration can cause up to 25% photosynthetic inhibition at temperatures below 30 °C2. Under stressful conditions such as drought and high temperatures, however, photorespiration can result in a loss of up to 50% of the carbon fixed in C3 plants7,8. This phenomenon was not a problem earlier in geological times when C3 plants evolved at the time when atmospheric CO2 concentrations were very high and O2 concentrations very low or absent9–11. However, starting from recent geological times some 35 million years ago and onward, atmospheric conditions, mainly decreasing atmospheric CO2 and deterioration of the climate with frequent drought events and raising temperatures, favoured photorespiration significantly in C3 plants10.

C4 plants had evolved from C3 ancestors in at least more than 60 independent lineages of angiosperms, most probably in response to decreasing atmospheric CO2 levels and environmental conditions promoting photorespiration during the late Miocene12,13. Nine of all the C4 lineages belong to the family Chenopodiaceae. Subfamily Salsoloideae in Chenopodiaceae has five clades comprised in two lineages, Caroxyloneae and Salsoleae s.s12,14,15. Salsoloideae has a great photosynthetic diversity, which includes C3, C4, and C3-C4 intermediate species15, as well as species with a phenomenon known as “C3 cotyledons and C4 leaves”. This phenomenon was firstly discovered in Haloxylon aphyllum and H. persicum species16. These species possess isobilateral C3 anatomy in their cotyledons and salsoloid-type C4 Kranz anatomy in their leaves. After this discovery, the same phenomenon was observed in more species from other genera of Salsoloideae17–19.

C3 and C4 photosynthetic pathways were previously compared in closely related C3 and C4 species15,20–22. Using a broadly sampled molecular phylogeny, Schüssler et al.15. provided evidence for C3 being the ancestral photosynthetic pathway but suggested no phylogenetic evidence for the ancestry of C3-C4 intermediacy with respect to C4 in Salsoleae tribe. Siadjeu et al.23. compared transcription factors (TFs) of the closely related lineages (Camphorosmeae and Salsoleae) and revealed no C3-specific TFs higher in C2 compared with C4 species. Schulze et al.22 determined GDC enzymes and their expression levels in C3 and C4 Flaveria species. Voznesenskaya et al.17 provided early evidence for the compartmentation of key enzymes relative to photosynthetic mechanisms in leaves and cotyledons of some species in the tribe Salsoleae. In non-Kranz C3 cotyledons of Halocharis gossypina and Haloxylon persicum, Rubisco was mostly localized in chloroplasts of palisade and to lower extend in spongy mesophyll cells, where C4 pathway proteins were very low or absent17.

In other two studies, C3 cotyledons and C4 leaves in the same individuals of Salsola soda and Haloxylon ammodendron species were studied for their gene regulations in photosynthesis24,25. In these species, the increasing expression levels of genes coding the enzymes specific to C4 photosynthesis and decreasing those levels of genes coding the enzymes specific to C3 photosynthesis in differentiation from C3 cotyledons to C4 leaves were partially determined. In S. soda studied for PEPC level, which has the function of first carboxylation reaction of C4 photosynthesis in differentiation from C3 to C4, while no PEPC was detected in cotyledons, it was highly generated in mature C4 leaves24. Apart from PEPC, there has been no study comparing translation levels of photosynthetic and photorespiratory enzymes functioning in C3 cotyledons and C4 leaves in Salsoloideae subfamily.

PEPC, with a role in the regulation of CO2 flux in the C4 pathway, PPDK that has the function of regeneration of PEP in C4 pathway, GOX, with the function of converting glycolate to glyoxylate in photorespiration, and GDC, which has a central role in photorespiration, are key enzymes in biochemical pathways of C4 photosynthesis or photorespiration26–28. Beside these enzymes, Rubisco, with dual functions of catalyzing the carboxylation and oxygenation reactions, is a core enzyme in Calvin-Benson cycle (CBC)29. All of these enzymes are essential and key enzymes for getting concise clues for the biochemical pathway in each organ.

In this study, we aimed to determine structural and biochemical differences between C3 cotyledons and C4 leaves of Salsola grandis, Cyathobasis fruticulosa and Petrosimonia nigdeensis, as well as C4 cotyledons and C4 leaves of S. tragus. C3 cotyledons followed by C4 leaves, a unique phenomenon in some species, beside transcript levels, were discovered for the first time for these species in this study. We have selected few, but key enzymes in order to support the determination of the biochemical pathway in each organ. We determined the protein levels of PPDK (C4 isoform), PEPC (C4 isoform), GDC (photorespiratory isoform), GOX (photorespiratory isoform), and Rubisco (photosynthetic isoform) enzymes by Western blotting, while the transcription levels of the genes encoding these proteins were detected by qRT-PCR.

Materials and methods

Seed collection, growth conditions and sampling

Seeds of Salsola grandis Freitag, Vural & N. Adıgüzel and Cyathobasis fruticulosa (Bunge) Aellen were collected from Nallıhan, Ankara, Türkiye, while Petrosimonia nigdeensis Aellen and S. tragus L. were obtained, respectively, from Aksaray and Mersin, Türkiye. All seeds were collected in November 2017. Morphological identifications of all plant samples in this study were performed by Faik Ceylan. Voucher specimens of Salsola

grandis and Petrosimonia nigdeensis were deposited in Düzce University, Faculty of Forestry Herbarium (DUOF) with DUOF0009469 and DUOF0009466 barcode numbers, respectively. Voucher specimens of Salsola tragus and Cyathobasis fruticulosa were deposited in the personal collection of Faik Ceylan, Düzce University. Before sowing, all seeds were soaked in tap water for five hours to break dormancy. Seeds were then sown in vials containing 50% sand and 50% peat mixture. Plants were grown in a growth chamber set at 24 °C/22 °C (day/night temperature), 12 h/12 h (day/night) photoperiod, 40% relative humidity, and 500 µmol quanta m–2 s–1 PPFD (photosynthetic photon flux density) at the Faculty of Agriculture, Kahramanmaraş Sütçü Imam University. All cotyledon and leaf samples were harvested when mature. After germination, cotyledons (coty, 5-day-old) as well as first leaf pairs (1.lp, 10-day-old), second leaf pairs (2.lp, 20-day-old), and old leaf pairs (Olp, from 30-day-old plants) of five to eight individuals of S. grandis and S. tragus were pooled for RNA and protein isolations for three replicates according to Lauterbach et al.24. In C. fruticulosa and P. nigdeensis, cotyledons (coty, 5-day-old) and old leaf pairs (Olp, from 30-day-old plants) were used for the isolation of RNA and proteins for three replicates. For all species, any leaves were preferred for the isolation of DNA, while cotyledons and leaves were used for the anatomical observations.

DNA Isolation and Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) Phylogeny

After morphological identification of species studied in this work, we identified species by ITS sequencing for verification. For this, DNAs were isolated from 100 mg of fresh leaf material ground in liquid nitrogen using DNA isolation kit (ref: 740770.50, Macherey-Nagel, Germany). Quantity and purity of isolated DNAs were verified by using spectrophotometry “Thermo Scientific™ NanoDrop 2000” and their integrities were determined by running 1 µg of each DNA sample through 1.5% agarose gel via electrophoresis. Polymerase chain reactions (PCR) were set up with these DNA samples by using Internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1)30, and Internal transcribed spacer 4 (ITS4)31, primers (Table S1). The programme of PCR was set as 1 min at 95 °C, 35 cycles of 1 min. at 94 °C, 1 min. at 57 °C, 2 min. at 72 °C and finally 5 min at 72 °C. 100 ng of PCR products were run in 1.5% agarose gel and the gels were photographed by an imaging system. PCR products were sent to sequencing facility for sequencing nuclear ribosomal DNA ITS regions. Results of sequencing were blasted with ITS sequences of related species at NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information, USA) as described in Morgulis et al.32 and a phylogenetic tree was created via “Seaview4” using the method of Gouy et al.33. All sequence results were aligned with MUSCLE. Arabidopsis thaliana was used for the outgroup of the phylogenetic tree.

Light microscopy

Anatomical observations were developed with some modifications to the protocol described in Kulahoglu et al.34. Briefly, cotyledon and leaf samples were cut into 1 × 2 mm size and incubated in of 2% paraformaldehyde, 2% glutaraldehyde fixative solution for 24 h at 4 °C. Samples were then washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution and twice with dH2O. They were dehydrated with acetone series from 30% to 96%. After that, they were treated with ascending resin (mixture of araldite M, Sigma, ref: 10951, DDSA, Merck, ref: 45346 and araldite M accelerator, Sigma, ref: 10952; 20 ml, 22 ml and 1.1 ml, respectively) series (25%, 50%, 75%, 100%) and finally embedded in mold at 65 °C for 72 h. 5 μm thick cross sections were cut from polymerized resin blocks using a rotary microtome, then, toluidine-blue dye was dropped on cross sections for 20–30 s. After rinsing the samples with water, permanent slides were made by dropping some “DPX, Slide Mounting Medium” (Sigma, ref: 06522). Cross sections were examined and photographed using a Light microscope Nikon, ECLIPSE 80i (USA, New York).

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

Total RNAs of three biological replicates were isolated from 100 mg of cotyledons or leaves freshly ground in liquid nitrogen using GF-1 Total RNA Extraction Kit (Vivantis, ref: GF-TR-050) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantity and purity of isolated RNAs were verified by using spectrophotometry “Thermo Scientific™ NanoDrop 2000” and their integrities were determined by running through 1.5% agarose gel. cDNAs were synthesized from proper RNA by using 2-Step RT-PCR Kit (ref: RTPL12; Vivantis, Malaysia) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR for these cDNAs were performed using an oligo (dT) primer and Bright Green 2X qPCR MasterMix-No Dye (ABM, Canada) in 25 µl reaction volume included 5 µl cDNA. PPDK, Rubisco, glycine decarboxylase-P subunit (GDC-P), GOX and elongation factor-1 (EF-1) primer pairs were used in accordance with Lara et al.35 (Table S2). qRT-PCR cycle parameters were set up as 95 °C for 10 min; 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min at 45 cycle. Melting curves were determined from 45 °C to 95 °C. Each cDNA sample was run in duplicates with targeted primers along with duplicates of the EF-1 primer containing tubes. Relative transcript abundance of genes were normalized to the abundance of reference gene (internal control) EF-1 and calculated by comparative 2−∆∆Ct method as described by Schmittgen and Livak36. Finally, as Lara et al.35 and Lauterbach et al.24 defined, expression levels of PPDK, Rubisco, GDC-P, GOX genes of cotyledons were assumed 1.00 as control group and expression levels of PPDK, Rubisco, GDC-P, GOX genes of first (1.lp), second (2.lp) and old leaf pairs (Olp) were statistically compared to expression levels of these genes of cotyledons by one-way-ANOVA (for more than two tissues) or t-test (two tissues) using “Graphpad Prism” (CA, USA) statistical programme.

Protein extraction and Western blot

For each sample 500 mg of fresh material was ground in liquid nitrogen. Total protein of cotyledons or leaves were isolated by adding 500 µl protein extraction buffer [100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM (w/v) dithiothreitol (DTT), 5 mM (w/v) ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 2% β-mercaptoethanol, 0.5 M sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 10% glycerol, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF)] to each sample. This mixture was vortexed and centrifuged at 4 °C, 15,000 rpm for 15 min. Supernatants were collected as total proteins and kept at −20 °C until use. Concentrations of proteins were measured by Bradford assay37. For this purpose, 2.5 µl protein samples were added to 50 µl Bradford solution (Alfa Aesar, J61522) and 5 min later measured at 595 nm. Measurements without proteins were used as blanks. Proteins were denaturated at 95 °C for 5 min. For western blot, protein samples (15 µg per lane) and ladder (Thermo Fischer, ref:26619, USA; Abcam, ref: ab116029, USA) were separated through a SDS gel by their molecular weights at 100 V and were transferred to a PVDF membrane (Thermo Scientific, ref: 88520, USA) at 350 mA. A representative membrane, stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue after 15 µg proteins per lane transferred to PVDF membrane and immunoblotting, of Salsola grandis and S. tragus, and Cyathobasis fruticulosa and Petrosimonia nigdeensis is given, respectively, in Fig. S1 and Fig. S2. Membranes were treated with PEPC (Agrisera-AS09458, Sweden), PPDK (Agrisera-AS132647, Sweden), GOX (Agrisera-AS142772, Sweden), GDC-H (Agrisera-AS05074, Sweden) and Rubisco SSU (Agrisera-AS07259, Sweden) primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, following mouse anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, SC-2357, USA) secondary antibody for two hours at room temperature. Actin (Agrisera-AS132640, Sweden) antibody was used as the loading control. After that, membranes were treated with ECL substrate (Biorad, ref: 1705061, USA) to obtain chemiluminescence and the bands were visualized by UVP Imaging System (CA, USA). The intensity of the bands obtained for each protein was measured by ImageJ software (Version 1.54 g, https://imagej.net/ij/). The values measured for PEPC, PPDK, GOX, GDC-H and Rubisco bands were normalized to actin value of the same lane for adequate comparison. Amounts of PEPC, Rubisco, GDC-H, GOX proteins of cotyledons were assumed 1.00 as control group and amounts of PEPC, Rubisco, GDC-H, GOX proteins of first (1.lp), second (2.lp) and old leaf pairs (Olp) were statistically compared to amounts of these proteins of cotyledons by one-way-ANOVA (for more than two tissues) or t-test (two tissues) using “Graphpad Prism” (CA, USA) statistical programme.

Results

Verification of species

Percent identity of Salsola grandis, Salsola tragus, Cyathobasis fruticulosa and Petrosimonia nigdeensis were determined as 100%, 98%, 98% and 99%, respectively (Fig. S3). A phylogenetic tree was created using sequences and the comparison of sequences verified the species identity used in this study (Fig. S3). Results from the phylogenetic tree showed that our sequence of S. grandis was closer to S. grandis from the NCBI rather than to closely related species, S. soda. Furthermore, we were able to distinguish P. nigdeensis, C. fruticulosa and S. tragus from P. brachiata, Haloxylon ammodendron and S. kali, respectively (Fig. S3).

Anatomy of cotyledons and leaves

The study of cross sections clearly showed isopalisade C3 anatomy in cotyledons of S. grandis (Fig. 1A), isobilateral C3 anatomy in cotyledons of C. fruticulosa (Fig. 1E), dorsiventral C3 anatomy in cotyledons of P. nigdeensis (Fig. 1G) and salsoloid type C4 Kranz anatomy in their leaves (Fig. 1B, F and H). S. tragus had salsoloid type C4 Kranz anatomy both in cotyledons and leaves (Fig. 1C-D). In C4 organs of all the species studied, thick-walled bundle sheath cells were in close contact with outer mesophyll cells and completely surround the inner water storage tissue of the organ (Fig. 1B-D, F, H). Although water storage (WS) tissue was present in all C3 cotyledons and C4 leaves, cells of this tissue were highly enlarged in C4 leaves. The other conspicuous difference between the anatomy of C3 cotyledons and C4 leaves is the higher proportion of the intercellular air spaces observed in C3 cotyledons. The cells of C4 leaves are packed in close proximation than the cells in C3 cotyledons. Furthermore, among all studied species a hypodermis cell layer was observed only in C4 leaves of C. fruticulosa (Fig. 1F).

Comparison of mRNA levels of C3, C4 photosynthetic and photorespiratory enzymes among photosynthetic organs

RNAs were isolated from cotyledons and leaves of three different stages (first leaf pairs, second leaf pairs and old leaf pairs) of S. grandis (C3 cotyledons, C4 leaves) and S. tragus (C4 cotyledons, C4 leaves) and only from C3 cotyledons and C4 leaf pairs of P. nigdeensis and C. fruticulosa. qRT-PCRs were set up with cDNAs generated after RNA isolation and the RNA levels of PPDK, Rubisco, GDC-P and GOX enzymes between cotyledons and leaves were compared.

mRNA levels of PPDK enzyme in first leaf pairs, second leaf pairs and old leaf pairs of S. grandis were measured as 5.71, 5.07 and 4.39-fold higher, respectively, than the mRNA level of C3 cotyledons (Fig. 2A). Rubisco mRNA levels, on the other hand, were calculated to be 18.86-, 23.25- and 100-fold lower, respectively, in first leaf pairs, second leaf pairs and old leaf pairs of S. grandis compared with the levels in cotyledons (Fig. 2B). Compared to their C3 cotyledons, in three different developmental stages of C4 leaves of S. grandis, GDC-P mRNA levels were found 2.32-, 2.0-, and 1.88-fold lower, respectively (Fig. 2C). GOX mRNA levels in first leaf pairs, second leaf pairs and old leaf pairs of S. grandis were 3.22-, 3.22- and 2.94-fold lower, respectively, compared with the levels in cotyledons (Fig. 2D).

PPDK mRNA level in C4 leaves of P. nigdeensis was 23.59-fold higher than C3 cotyledons (Fig. 2M). Rubisco mRNA in leaves was half of the cotyledons (Fig. 2N). On the other hand, GDC-P mRNA level in C4 leaves was 2.27 times lower (Fig. 2O) and GOX mRNA level was 1.81-fold lower than in C3 cotyledons (Fig. 2P). mRNA level of PPDK increased 5.02-fold in C4 leaves of C. fruticulosa compared with C3 cotyledons (Fig. 2I). Although Rubisco mRNA was approximately 3 times higher in C4 leaves relative to C3 cotyledons of C. fruticulosa, the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 2J). However, in C4 leaves compare to C3 cotyledons, mRNA levels of GDC-P and GOX decreased significantly in C. fruticulosa (Fig. 2K-L). On the other hand, while the levels of PPDK and GOX mRNAs of leaves of S. tragus were not significantly different from the cotyledons (Fig. 2E and H), mRNA levels of Rubisco and GDC-P were significantly lower in first, second and old leaf pairs (Fig. 2F and G).

Protein quantities of C3, C4 and photorespiratory enzymes in cotyledons and leaves

The protein of PEPC enzyme was completely undetected in C3 cotyledons of S. grandis (Fig. 3A). However, the protein level of PEPC enzyme was assumed to be one unit in C3 cotyledons and its relative abundance was calculated in subsequent C4 leaf pairs and presented in Fig. 3B. The protein level of PEPC was found 10.84-, 12.87- and 15.69-fold higher than the level in C3 cotyledons in the first leaf pairs, 2. leaf pairs and old leaf pairs, respectively (Fig. 3B). A similar trend was observed for the protein level of PPDK enzyme (Fig. 3A, B). The first leaf pairs, second leaf pairs and old leaf pairs had 2.0, 2.98 and 2.93-times higher protein quantity of PPDK enzyme, respectively, than those of cotyledons in S. grandis (Fig. 3B). The protein level of GOX enzyme was significantly higher in the first leaf pairs but lower in the second and old leaf pairs compared to the cotyledons of S. grandis (Fig. 3A-B). GDC-H, another major photorespiratory enzyme, and Rubisco protein levels were significantly lower in the C4 first, second and old leaf pairs compared to the C3 cotyledons (Fig. 3A-B).

While the protein level of PEPC enzyme was undetected in both C. fruticulosa and P. nigdeensis C3 cotyledons (Fig. 3E and G), as it was the case for S. grandis, PEPC in C4 old leaf pairs of these species was highly expressed, (4.94 and 1.57 times higher in C4 leaves of C. fruticulosa and P. nigdeensis, respectively, than in C3 cotyledons (Fig. 3F and H). Similarly to PEPC, the protein of the PPDK enzyme was not detected in cotyledons of P. nigdeensis, while it was highly observable in old leaf pairs (Fig. 3G). On the other hand, the protein of PPDK enzyme in old leaf pairs of C. fruticulosa was 1.81-fold higher than its level in cotyledons (Fig. 3F). Moreover, the protein levels of GOX enzyme were found to be 2.08 and 2.34-fold lower in old leaf pairs of C. fruticulosa and P. nigdeensis, respectively, compared to their C3 cotyledons (Fig. 3F and H). A similar trend was observed for GDC-H. The protein levels of GDC-H enzyme were significantly decreased from C3 cotyledons to C4 leaves in C. fruticulosa and P. nigdeensis (Fig. 3E, F, G and H). In these species, the protein levels of Rubisco enzyme were also significantly less in C4 old leaf pairs compared to C3 cotyledons (Fig. 3F and H).

S. tragus was proven to have the same C4 photosynthetic pathway in both photosynthetic organs, cotyledons and leaves. Although some general reductions were observed for some of the enzymes studied, no significant differences in quantities of PEPC, PPDK, GDC-H and Rubisco proteins were observed between the C4 cotyledons and C4 leaves of S. tragus (Fig. 3C and D). The protein level of GOX enzyme in old leaf pairs of S. tragus, on the other hand, was determined to decrease dramatically compared to the levels in cotyledons and the first and second leaf pairs (Fig. 3C and D).

Discussion

In this study, it was hypothesized that if a plant switches its photosynthetic pathway from C3 to C4 during its life, then protein and transcript levels of C4-specific enzymes and genes should increase from C3 cotyledons to C4 leaves, while protein and transcript levels of photorespiratory and C3-specific enzymes and genes are expected to decrease at the same time. In an effort to investigate this hypothesis, two different photosynthetic organs (cotyledons and leaves), three different developmental stages of leaves of S. grandis and S. tragus, and only old leaf pairs of P. nigdeensis and C. fruticulosa were compared for their structural and biochemical differences. For this comparison, cotyledon and leaf anatomical cross sections, mRNA levels of genes coding C3, C4 and photorespiratory enzymes, and quantities of the proteins translated from these mRNAs were investigated.

Determination of photosynthetic pathways by leaf anatomy

After the discovery of the “C3 cotyledons followed by C4 leaves” phenomenon in Haloxylon by Pyankov et al.16, especially subfamily Salsoloideae gained interest in a search for more species or genera with these different photosynthetic organs. Petrosimonia, Halimocnemis, and Halocharis were some of them in Caroxyloneae lineage, and Salsola was another genus from Salsoleae s.s. lineage17,38. In our study, for the first time, we were able to determine Cyathobasis as a new genus having this phenomenon in Salsoleae s.s. lineage. This was the first anatomical evidence for the fact that P. nigdeensis and C. fruticulosa have C3 photosynthetic pathway in cotyledons and C4 pathway in leaves. Previously, Freitag et al.39 demonstrated that S. grandis exhibited C3 cotyledons and C4 leaves by using hand drawn anatomy pictures. In the present study, our anatomical observations of S. grandis supported Freitag et al.39 (Fig. 1A-B). All C4 leaves from anatomical observations exhibited typical Kranz anatomy with tightly highly packed bundle sheath cells inner to and in close contact with chlorenchyma cells and surrounding large water storage cells. While Lauterbach et al.24 showed three to four gaps in bundle sheath cells of C4 leaves of S. soda, there was not any gap in all C4 tissues of our species (Fig. 1). Voznesenskaya et al.17 showed that there are two to three palisade mesophyll cell layers in C3 cotyledons of Halocharis gossypina. In our study, all C3 cotyledons had one or two layers of mesophyll cells and relatively smaller inner water storage cells (Fig. 1). One other observed striking difference between the anatomy of C3 cotyledons and C4 leaves of the same species is the higher proportion of the intercellular air spaces in cotyledons compared with mature C4 leaves (Fig. 1A-B, E-F, G-H). Although no quantitative measurement was taken on the proportion of the intercellular air spaces, the difference between cotyledons and leaves were apparent. This observation is in agreement with the previous reports, where C3 monocots and dicots were reported to bear higher proportion of intercellular air spaces than their C4 counterparts40–42.

Gene expression and protein levels of photosynthetic enzymes

It is well known that C4 species have evolved from ancestral C3 species, and genes recruited for C4 function in C4 plants exist in C3 species having unrelated functions with C4 pathway43. It was shown in many reports that C4 species have more proteins of C4 photosynthetic key enzymes compared to those of C3 enzymes than C3 species in the same genus, such as Salsola, Cleome, and Suaeda44–47. As expected, in our study, PPDK mRNA levels were higher in C4 leaves than in C3 cotyledons of S. grandis, C. fruticulosa, and P. nigdeensis. Moreover, relative quantities of PPDK proteins were significantly higher in C4 leaves of these species compared to C3 cotyledons. This observation indicated that mRNA and protein levels of PPDK enzyme were consistent with each other in these species. In addition, another C4 cycle enzyme PEPC, which has a key enzymatic role in initial carbon dioxide (CO2) fixation reaction in C4 pathway48, was undetected in C3 cotyledons but highly expressed in C4 leaves of S. grandis, C. fruticulosa, and P. nigdeensis. We were also able to demonstrate that mRNA levels of PEPC enzyme were similar in C4 cotyledons and C4 leaves of S. tragus. Consistent with mRNA levels, relative protein amounts of PEPC enzyme were similar in C4 cotyledons and C4 leaves of S. tragus as well.

As a consequence of its kinetic properties, Rubisco is required 60–80% in less amount in C4 than C3 species of similar life forms49. In this study, we observed that mRNA levels and protein quantities of Rubisco decreased from C3 cotyledons to C4 leaves in S. grandis and P. nigdeensis. While the difference in Rubisco mRNA levels of C. fruticulosa between leaves and cotyledons was not statistically significant, a decrease in the Rubisco protein level was apparent. In agreement with our observations, Covshoff et al.50 revealed that the difference of Rubisco mRNA levels between C4 Echinochloa glabrescens and C3 rice was similar to the difference between C3 cotyledons and C4 leaves of S. grandis and P. nigdeensis. Smith et al.45 also reported that some C3 Suaeda species have higher amounts of Rubisco protein content than C4 Suaeda species. Also, Voznesenskaya et al.17 showed more protein of Rubisco in C3 cotyledons of Halocharis gossypina and Haloxylon persicum, whereas proteins of C4 enzymes such as PEPC, NAD-malic enzyme (NAD-ME), and NADP-malic enzyme (NADP-ME) were very low or undetectable. Interestingly, a decrease in Rubisco mRNA and to a lesser extend in protein levels was also observed in C4 leaves of S. tragus compared to its C4 cotyledons, but the decrease, especially in protein level, was not as significant relative to the decreased level in S. grandis (Fig. 3).

Photorespiratory enzymes were shown to be more abundant generally in C3 species compared to C4, due to the lack or the suppression of photorespiratory cycle in C4 species51. In this study, we observed that protein level of GOX enzyme, which functions in photorespiration cycle, significantly decreased from C3 cotyledons to C4 leaves in C. fruticulosa and P. nigdeensis. Schulze et al.22 and Lyu et al.52 found GDC-P and GDC-H (genes of photorespiratory enzyme complex) gene expression levels to be lower in C4 than C3 Flaveria species. Also, Siadjeu et al.23 revealed that GDC-H gene expression level was high in C3 Threlkeldia diffusa compared to C4 Bassia prostrata, but GDC-P was low. Similarly, we found that GDC-H was significantly lower in C4 leaves of S. grandis, C. fruticulosa and P. nigdeensis compared to their C3 cotyledons. GOX mRNA levels in C4 leaves of S. grandis, C. fruticulosa, and P. nigdeensis were seen to be lower than in their C3 cotyledons. However, GOX transcript abundances were similar in C4 cotyledons and in first, second, and old C4 leaf pairs of S. tragus (Fig. 2). Besides, it was determined that mRNA levels of GOX enzyme were consistent with the protein levels in those species, with the exception of first leaf pairs in S. grandis, where GOX was slightly higher. On the other hand, mRNA levels of GDC (P subunit) enzyme were lower in C4 leaves of S. grandis, C. fruticulosa, and P. nigdeensis compared to their C3 cotyledons. Although GDC-P and GDC-H are two different subunits in GDC enzyme complex of photorespiration, it was shown that there was a consistency between mRNA levels of GDC-P and protein amounts of GDC-H enzyme in these species. These results may support earlier reports by Palmieri et al.53 and Timm et al.54 claiming that GDC is a key enzyme complex in C3 compared to C4 species.

In our study, we observed that the first leaf pairs of S. grandis had slightly more protein levels of GOX enzyme than its cotyledons. Protein levels of GOX enzyme of the second and old leaves were found to decrease gradually below the levels of cotyledons. On the other hand, GOX mRNA levels in all C4 leaf pairs of S. grandis were significantly lower than C3 cotyledons. All of these results showed that GOX mRNA and protein levels in S. grandis were not consistent. A possible explanation for this inconsistency between mRNA and protein levels may be found in a report by Fortelny et al.55. They showed that predictions of protein quantities were not accurately supported by mRNA levels for most genes in human genome. In addition, Somerville and Ogren56 and Levey et al.57 also indicated that GOX could have more essential functions than expected in plants. For example, cellular H2O2 homeostasis and some other metabolic cycles, such as glyoxylate cycle could result in variation of GOX levels of first leaf pairs in this study.

An unexpected observation of our study was the fact that C4 leaf pairs of S. tragus had lower protein levels of GOX enzyme compared to C4 cotyledons. This tendency was not seen at GOX mRNA levels in this species. In S. tragus, while mRNA levels of GDC-P enzyme decreased during the differentiation from C4 cotyledons to C4 leaves, similar to what was observed in S. grandis, protein amount of GDC-H enzyme of S. tragus remained virtually constant in both photosynthetic parts.

In previous studies, it was reported that Calvin-Benson cycle (CBC) proteins, their mRNA levels and C4 key proteins increased during ontogenetic leaf development in C4 Bienertia sinuspersici35,58. Similarly, different ontogenetic leaf stages of C4 Gynandropsis gynandra had an increase in transcript levels of CBC, photosystem I (PSI) and photosystem II (PSII) proteins, as the same increase was seen in those of AspAT which is a key enzyme in the NAD-ME and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) subtype of C4 pathway34. Moreover, Li et al.25 clearly demonstrated that there was an increase in the transcript levels of C4 photosynthesis-related genes and a decrease in photorespiratory and C3 photosynthesis-related genes during differentiation from C3 cotyledons to C4 leaves in Haloxylon ammodendron. Lauterbach et al.24 also showed that S. soda had gradual photosynthetic development from C3 cotyledons to C4 leaves with an increase in the transcript levels of C4 photosynthesis-related genes and a decrease in the transcript levels of C3 photosynthesis and photorespiratory-related genes. Our current study supports the results by Li et al.25 and Lauterbach et al.24 at the gene expression level.

The differences measured between cotyledons and leaves of S. grandis, C. fruticulosa, and P. nigdeensis for the protein levels of PEPC enzyme were similar to what was observed for S. soda by Lauterbach et al.24. In addition, our western blot results showed that there was not only an increase in protein levels of C4 enzymes but also a decrease in protein levels of C3 and photorespiratory enzymes during the differentiation. Finally, as investigated before in C4 Bienertia sinuspersici, C4 Zea mays, C3 Pisum sativum, and other species at proteomics level58–60, the whole protein contents and also activities of these enzymes of S. grandis, C. fruticulosa and P. nigdeensis needed to be detected in future studies to show the expression levels and activities of more C3-, C4-, and photorespiratory pathway-specific enzymes in Salsoloideae subfamily.

Conclusions

In this study, we compared anatomical structures and mRNA and protein levels of cotyledons and leaves of Salsola grandis, S. tragus, Cyathobasis fruticulosa, and Petrosimonia nigdeensis species. Anatomical observations clearly showed, for the first time, that S. grandis, C. fruticulosa, and P. nigdeensis have C3 anatomy in their cotyledons and salsoloid type C4 Kranz anatomy in their leaves. In these species, PPDK mRNA levels, which is one of the markers of the C4 pathway, were higher in leaves than in cotyledons, and GOX and GDC-P mRNA levels of photorespiratory enzymes were higher in cotyledons than in leaves. It was observed that the Rubisco mRNA levels were lower in leaves of S. grandis and P. nigdeensis than in their cotyledons, although there was no significant change in Cyathobasis fruticulosa. On the other hand, there was no significant change in mRNA levels of PPDK and GOX enzymes of S. tragus, which were determined to have a C4 pathway in cotyledons and leaves, while a decrease was detected in Rubisco and GDC-P mRNA levels in leaves compared to cotyledons. In S. grandis, C. fruticulosa, and P. nigdeensis species, the protein levels of PEPC and PPDK enzymes were higher in leaves than in cotyledons, while those of GOX, GDC-H, and Rubisco enzymes were observed more in cotyledons than in leaves. In summary, with some exceptions, mRNA and protein levels of C4 pathway enzymes were increased, and those of C3 and photorespiration enzymes were decreased during the differentiation from C3 cotyledons to C4 leaves in Salsola grandis, Cyathobasis fruticulosa, and Petrosimonia nigdeensis species.
