Can Deep Learning Methods Differentiate Temporomandibular Joint Disorders From Healthy Joints? A 3D Artificial Intelligence Algorithm Study Based on CBCT Images
Authors: İbrahim Şevki Bayrakdar, Sevda Kurt‐Bayrakdar, Alican Kuran, Özer Çelik, Kaan Orhan, Rohan Jagtap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGiven the high difficulty of interpreting complex bone‐related temporomandibular joint disorders (TMD) on CBCT scans, developing advanced artificial intelligence models has become essential for accurate diagnosis and detailed subcategorization.ObjectivesThis study aimed to develop deep learning models capable of performing a wide range of tasks in the evaluation of the temporomandibular joint on CBCT scans.MethodsThis study followed a four‐stage design. First, an nnU‐Net v2–based model was developed for automatic segmentation of mandibular condyles on CBCT images. Second, a 3D CNN model was trained to classify healthy and pathological condyles. Third, a 3D CNN was used to differentiate five TMD types. In the fourth stage, for exploratory analysis purposes, an nnU‐Net v2–based model was implemented to grade the severity of erosion, osteophyte formation and sclerosis.ResultsThe segmentation model achieved automatic segmentation of healthy and pathological condyles with 0.87 DSC and 0.77 IoU values. The classification model achieved an F1‐score of 0.65 in distinguishing healthy condyles from TMDs. For differentiating the five TMD types from healthy condyles, the F1‐scores were 0.76 for erosion, 0.88 for flattening, 0.86 for osteophyte formation, 0.88 for sclerosis and 0.85 for subchondral cysts. In grading analysis, the highest performance was observed for Grade 1 erosion, Grade 2 osteophyte formation and Grade 3 sclerosis.ConclusionDeep learning algorithms can achieve clinically relevant performance in the segmentation of the mandibular condyle and in differentiating TMDs from healthy condyles. However, further studies with larger and more balanced datasets are needed to improve the ability of these models to classify TMD grades accurately.

Introduction

Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) are multifactorial in aetiology and represent the most common cause of non‐dental pain in the orofacial region. These disorders can affect the masticatory muscles, the osseous components of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), and the soft tissue structures [1]. While the initial classification of TMDs relies on symptoms and clinical examination, these methods are insufficient to thoroughly assess the osseous and soft tissue components of the TMJ [2]. It has been shown that clinical protocols have low sensitivity (55%) and specificity (61%) in detecting degenerative joint disease associated with TMDs [3]. Therefore, the use of advanced imaging techniques as a complement to the initial clinical assessment plays an important role in achieving a more accurate diagnosis [2].

In the imaging of TMDs, CBCT holds a significant place in dentomaxillofacial radiology due to several advantages [4, 5, 6, 7]. CBCT enables a detailed examination of all bony components of the joint, the assessment of cortical bone integrity, and the evaluation of subcortical bone resorption or formation [6, 8]. However, despite these advantages, accurate interpretation of CBCT images requires sufficient knowledge, skills and experience on the part of the clinician [9]. Furthermore, depending on the specific condition being evaluated, identifying degenerative joint changes caused by TMDs may be easier or more challenging on CBCT images, and this can vary according to the interpreter's level of expertise [10]. At this point, artificial intelligence models trained by experienced specialists with years of clinical practice and access to extensive patient datasets have the potential to offer a standardized perspective for image interpretation [11].

There have been several approaches to employing AI‐assisted diagnostic tools in the evaluation of TMJ imaging. In addition to models developed to work on two‐dimensional images [12, 13], AI models for TMJ evaluation have also been created based on three‐dimensional MRI [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20], CBCT [21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26] and CT [27] images. In the literature, there are studies focusing on the automatic segmentation of TMJ anatomical structures such as the mandibular condyle, articular eminence, glenoid fossa and articular disc [24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. In addition to the automatic segmentation of anatomical structures, efforts have also been made to develop AI models capable of identifying or distinguishing hard tissue‐related pathologies of the TMJ on CBCT images [21, 22, 23]. Studies conducted on CBCT images have demonstrated that the developed models can classify joint regions as healthy, flattened, with osteophytes, or with erosion with high accuracy, as well as detect TMJ osteoarthritis [21, 22, 23].

Although AI models show higher diagnostic accuracy than inexperienced clinicians in TMJ disorders, they still fall behind experts in complex and advanced cases. Therefore, it has become necessary to develop new AI models that can achieve higher performance with advancing technology. In this context, the aim of this study is to develop a deep learning model capable of distinguishing bone‐related TMDs on CBCT images and classifying these pathologies into their respective subcategories, as well as to evaluate the performance of this model. To the best of our knowledge, previous AI‐based studies on TMJ CBCT imaging have predominantly addressed segmentation and pathology detection as separate tasks. Although some studies have performed condylar segmentation using three‐dimensional approaches, pathology classification has largely been conducted using two‐dimensional image‐based analyses and has remained limited to binary discrimination between healthy and pathological conditions. The novelty of the present study lies in three main aspects: first, the integration of condylar segmentation, binary classification, five‐subtype classification, and severity grading within a single unified framework; second, the implementation of a fully three‐dimensional evaluation based on the entire condylar volume; and third, the use of a balanced multicentre dataset comprising both healthy and pathological condyles, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the findings across diverse patient populations and clinical settings.

Material and Methods

Study Design

In this retrospective study, a four‐step research design was established to develop an artificial intelligence model for the automatic identification of TMDs associated with the mandibular condyle. In the first step, a deep learning model was developed to perform automatic segmentation of the mandibular condyle on CBCT images. In the second step, the segmented mandibular condyle images were simply classified as healthy or unhealthy to evaluate whether the AI model could distinguish between these two conditions. In the third step, a more detailed classification was performed: the mandibular condyles were labelled into five categories relevant to TMDs—erosion, flattening, osteophyte formation, sclerosis and subchondral cysts—and the ability of the deep learning models to differentiate these pathological conditions from healthy condyles was investigated. In the fourth and final step, the models were tested for their ability to distinguish between different grades of TMDs, specifically in erosion, osteophyte formation and sclerosis. The study design and manuscript preparation followed the Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM) and the Standards for the Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) Checklist. The study protocol was approved by the Non‐Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Eskisehir Osmangazi University (Application date: June 10, 2022; Approval date: December 20, 2022; Decision number: 25; E‐25403353‐050.99‐2300001860). Additionally, approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the University of Mississippi Medical Center (UMMC‐IRB‐2023‐6). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Dataset

The dataset used in this study consisted of tomographic images retrospectively collected from the CBCT archives of the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology, Faculty of Dentistry, Eskisehir Osmangazi University, and the Division of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology, Department of Care Planning and Restorative Sciences, University of Mississippi Medical Center School of Dentistry. The dataset comprised 586 CBCT volumes and 1172 mandibular condyles, of which half were healthy, and the other half presented with one of the TMDs. In addition to including both healthy mandibular condyles and those with TMDs, strict inclusion criteria were applied. Only CBCT scans with artefact‐free images and adequate image quality to allow accurate assessment of the TMJ region were included. CBCT volumes of patients with motion artefacts or metal artefacts caused by metallic implants (e.g., placed during previous surgeries), which made the evaluation of the TMJ region impossible, were excluded from the study. The 586 pathological mandibular condyles in the dataset were classified into five TMD categories: erosion, flattening, osteophyte formation, sclerosis and subchondral cysts.

Scan Acquisition

The CBCT scans analysed in this study were obtained using Planmeca Promax 3D Mid (Planmeca OY, Finland) and CS 9600 (Carestream Dental, GA, USA) systems, with varying acquisition parameters. Patient data were reconstructed into volumetric datasets and exported in DICOM format, enabling visualization in sagittal, coronal and axial planes. Subsequently, all DICOM files were systematically converted to the Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative (NIfTI) format using a custom Python‐based script with the Pydicom, Nibabel and OpenCV libraries.

Ground Truth Labelling

After conversion of the CBCT images to the NIfTI format, they were uploaded to CranioCatch Label (CranioCatch, Eskişehir), a web‐based segmentation platform. All annotations for the four‐step study were performed on this platform by two oral and maxillofacial radiologists (ISB, RJ), each with over 10 years of clinical experience. In the first step, aimed at developing a model capable of automatically segmenting the mandibular condyle, all condyles—1172 mandibular condyles from 586 CBCT scans, including both healthy and TMD‐affected—were manually labelled on axial slices using a polygonal segmentation technique. In the second step, the condyles were categorized as either healthy or TMD‐affected to train a model to distinguish between these two groups. In the third step, the TMD‐affected condyles were further classified into five specific subcategories—erosion, flattening, osteophyte formation, sclerosis and subchondral cysts—to evaluate whether the models could differentiate these pathological conditions from healthy condyles. All assessments were performed using a three‐dimensional evaluation of the CBCT scans, incorporating axial, sagittal and coronal reconstructions. A randomly selected 20% subset of the CBCT images included in the study was used for repeated segmentation. The re‐segmentation was performed by two independent investigators, and the overlap between their segmentation masks was quantified using pixel‐wise Intersection over Union (IoU). The mean IoU was 0.90, indicating a high level of overlap for mandibular condyle segmentation.

Inter‐observer consistency for the categorical assessments of erosion, flattening, osteophyte formation, sclerosis, subchondral cysts and ‘healthy’ condyles was evaluated using Cohen's kappa. The kappa values for the two investigators' decisions across two time points ranged from 0.87 to 0.95. For ordinal grading assessments (erosion, osteophyte formation, and sclerosis), weighted kappa coefficients ranged from 0.81 to 0.88, reflecting good agreement for the grading evaluations.

Data Split and Development of the CNN Models

This study was conducted in four consecutive phases. Initially, an automatic segmentation model for mandibular condyles was developed using the nnU‐Net v2 framework. A total of 528 DICOM CBCT images were used for training and 58 for testing. The model was trained for 1000 epochs, starting with a learning rate of 0.01, which was decreased by 0.00001 at each epoch. The nnU‐Net pipeline automatically selected data‐dependent hyperparameters through its ‘data fingerprint’ approach and optimized the training for 2D, 3D and 3D‐Cascade U‐Net configurations. The best average Dice coefficient was achieved using an ensemble of different configurations.

Following segmentation, a 3D convolutional neural network (3D‐CNN) was implemented to classify segmented condyles as healthy or TMD‐affected. In this study, the classification model did not use automated segmentation outputs. Instead, ground truth expert segmentations were used to define the condylar regions. Binary segmentation masks served only for ROI extraction, and the 3D‐CNN was trained on the cropped grayscale CBCT volumes, not on binary mask images. The dataset included 1172 mandibular condyles obtained from 586 CBCT scans (586 healthy and 586 unhealthy). All images were converted to NIfTI format, and the condylar regions were cropped before being split into training (936 condyles), validation (122 condyles), and testing (116 condyles) sets. Data augmentation with random rotations and pixel normalization was applied. The model was trained in TensorFlow/Keras for 100 epochs with a learning rate of 0.0001. The architecture comprised 17 layers, including four 3D convolutional layers (64, 64, 128 and 256 filters; kernel size 3 × 3 × 3), each followed by max‐pooling (stride 2), ReLU activation, and batch normalization. The final feature maps were flattened and connected to a fully connected layer of 512 neurons for classification.

In the third phase, separate 3D‐CNN models were trained to classify five TMD subtypes: erosion (466 healthy, 466 erosion condyles), flattening (256 healthy, 256 flattening), osteophyte formation (96 healthy, 96 osteophytes), sclerosis (90 healthy, 90 sclerosis) and subchondral cysts (24 healthy, 24 cysts). All CBCT scans were converted to NIfTI format, cropped, and divided into training, validation and test sets in similar proportions. The same 3D‐CNN architecture and training parameters used in the previous phase were applied to these models.

Finally, in order to evaluate the grading of TMD severity, multiclass nnU‐Net v2 models were developed to classify erosion, osteophyte formation, and sclerosis into Grade 1, Grade 2 and Grade 3. Owing to the limited number of cases available for each grade, this analysis was considered preliminary; the corresponding training details and results are therefore presented in the Supporting Information.

Performance Metrics

The performance of the segmentation model was evaluated by calculating True Positive (TP), False Positive (FP) and False Negative (FN) values based on the agreement between the predicted voxels and the ground truth annotations. True Negative (TN) values were deliberately excluded from the evaluation, as TN represents regions that should not be segmented and were correctly left unsegmented. In three‐dimensional CBCT images, where models are trained using entire DICOM volumes and all slices, most voxels correspond to background regions that are not intended to be segmented, which leads to an artificially inflated TN count. Consequently, TN‐dependent metrics, such as Accuracy, Specificity, True Negative Rate, Negative Predictive Value, False Positive Rate and Matthews Correlation Coefficient, were not considered reliable indicators of segmentation performance. Instead, evaluation focused on metrics derived from TP, FP and FN, including Sensitivity (Recall), Precision and F1 Score, which are more appropriate for assessing medical image segmentation performance. A prediction was considered correct if the predicted segmentation overlapped with the ground truth at an Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold of 50%. Additionally, other commonly used segmentation metrics, including Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), Jaccard Index, Hausdorff Distance and 95th Percentile Hausdorff Distance (HD95), were employed to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the model's segmentation performance.

To evaluate the performance of the classification models, TP, FP, TN and FN values were calculated, and performance metrics derived from these values, including Sensitivity, Specificity, Precision, Negative Predictive Value, False Positive Rate, False Discovery Rate, False Negative Rate, Accuracy, F1 Score and Matthews Correlation Coefficient, were used.

Results

The deep learning model developed based on nnU‐Net v2 demonstrated successful segmentation of CBCT images containing both healthy and unhealthy mandibular condyles, achieving precision of 0.89, recall of 0.85 and an F1‐score of 0.87. Additionally, high agreement with expert oral and maxillofacial radiologists' ground truth annotations was observed, as indicated by DSC of 0.87 and Intersection over Union (IoU, Jaccard) of 0.77. Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive approach for the AI‐based evaluation of TMDs, including automatic segmentation of mandibular condyles, classification of healthy versus pathological condyles, differentiation of specific TMD types from healthy condyles, and segmentation of TMDs according to severity grades. In the first stage, the nnU‐Net v2–based model demonstrated high agreement with expert‐generated ground truth, achieving high DSC and IoU values and accurately delineating condyle boundaries with low 95HD values. In the second stage, the CNN model showed above‐average performance in distinguishing healthy from pathological condyles in 3D; this performance further improved when separate models were developed for each specific TMD type. However, nnU‐Net v2 models targeting segmentation according to TMD grades showed low performance or failed to segment certain grades, likely due to insufficient training data for each grade. Apart from this limitation, the study achieved successful results in condyle segmentation, healthy–pathological classification and classification of each specific TMD type with individually developed models.

Several studies in the literature have investigated the use of deep learning methods to evaluate the anatomical structures and pathologies of the TMJ on CBCT scans. The initial focus of these studies has been on the automatic segmentation of anatomical structures. In one study aiming to simultaneously segment the mandibular condyle and mandibular ramus, Celia Le et al. [29] developed a conventional U‐Net–based model using axial CBCT slices in 2D image format instead of adopting a 3D approach. Their model achieved an F1‐score of 0.91 on the test dataset; however, commonly reported segmentation performance metrics such as DSC, IoU or 95HD were not provided. Nevertheless, studies employing 3D‐based approaches are more prevalent. Nayansi Jha et al. [25] compared the performance of two deep learning models for condyle segmentation. The basic 3D U‐Net achieved DSC and HD values of 0.92 and 2.557, respectively, whereas the cascaded 3D U‐Net demonstrated slightly better performance with DSC and HD values of 0.93 and 2.452. In a similar design, Vinayahalingam et al. [26] developed a 3D U‐Net–based model to automatically segment the mandibular condyle and glenoid fossa, reporting a high DSC value of 0.97 for condyle segmentation. From a different perspective, Piaolin Hu et al. [30] proposed the MVEL‐Net model as an alternative to computationally demanding 3D networks for the automatic segmentation of various organs in 3D imaging modalities such as CBCT. The performance of MVEL‐Net for automatic TMJ segmentation on CBCT images was compared with other 3D deep learning models such as 3D U‐Net and nnU‐Net. According to their results, the proposed method outperformed the other 3D algorithms, achieving a DSC of 0.98 and a 95HD of 0.17 while requiring substantially less GPU power. Unlike many previous studies [25, 26, 29], their dataset of 88 CBCT images included both healthy and pathological TMJ cases. This approach is similar to ours, as the dataset we used for mandibular condyle segmentation also included both healthy and TMD‐affected condyles. In contrast, most other studies in the literature primarily rely on CBCT images of healthy mandibular condyles. In our study, the nnU‐Net v2–based model achieved an overall DSC of 0.87 and a 95HD of 3.16 mm for automatic mandibular condyle segmentation. These values indicate that the model can achieve performance comparable to other studies in the literature, even when applied to a dataset that includes both healthy and pathological condyles, collected from two centres and multiple CBCT devices. The slightly lower DSC observed in our study may stem from both its multicentre design and the inclusion of pathological cases representing a range of distinct TMD conditions in our dataset. As shown in Figure 2, the overall morphology of the condyle in TMD cases can differ substantially from that of healthy condyles. These morphological changes primarily affect the outer boundaries of the mandibular condyle, reducing the overlap between the model's predictions and the ground truth, which in turn increases the 95HD value. Despite these challenges, the DSC value obtained in our study remains well above the generally accepted threshold of 0.70 in the literature [31]. In medical applications, higher DSC values are often desirable, especially for critical anatomical structures. Therefore, the DSC of 0.87 achieved in our study can be considered a high‐accuracy result. Taking into account the described limitations and the multicentre nature of our dataset, this performance can be regarded as both acceptable and robust within the context of complex TMJ segmentation tasks.

In the literature, studies aiming to assist clinicians in diagnosing TMDs have predominantly focused on developing deep learning models to directly identify these disorders in CBCT images. In one such study, Lee et al. [21] analysed sagittal CBCT slices of patients exhibiting TMD‐related features such as irregular contours, bone defects, loss of cortication and flattening of the condylar head. The single shot detection (SSD) model developed in that study automatically detected areas with bone alterations in sagittal CBCT images of the condyle and classified them into three categories: Class 1, indeterminate for TMD; Class 2, TMD; and Class 3, normal. The model achieved mean accuracy, precision, recall and F1‐score values of 0.86, 0.85, 0.84 and 0.84, respectively. However, as opposed to our approach, the study did not evaluate model performance separately for each TMD type. In our study, the model achieved an F1‐score of 0.65 for differentiating TMD cases from healthy subjects, with the highest F1‐score—up to 0.88—observed in distinguishing flattening and sclerosis from healthy condyles when each TMD type was assessed individually. In another study, Eşer et al. [22] evaluated the performance of a YOLOv5‐based deep learning model in TMJ segmentation and osteoarthritis classification using 2D images derived from CBCT sagittal slices. For the classification of TMJ osteoarthritis, the condyles in the dataset were categorized as normal, flattening, erosion or deformity. The model developed in their study achieved sensitivity, precision, and F1‐scores of 1, 0.76 and 0.86, respectively. However, similar to the study by Lee et al. [21], this work employed a 2D‐based approach, differing from the three‐dimensional evaluation perspective of the 3D CNN algorithm developed in our study. Similarly, in another study also based on 2D images obtained from CBCT, Talaat et al. [23] developed a YOLOv7 Large‐based deep learning model to differentiate mandibular condyles of individuals with TMJ osteoarthritis from those of a healthy control group. The TMJ osteoarthritis group included cases of subcortical cyst, flattening, osteophyte and surface erosion. Their model was reported to distinguish these conditions with sensitivity and specificity values exceeding 96%. However, this study was likewise based on 2D imaging. In yet another study conducted using 2D images from sagittal sections of CBCT scans, a YOLOv10‐based deep learning model was developed to detect various types of TMD, including healthy, erosion, osteophyte, sclerosis and subchondral cyst. The reported F1‐scores for detecting erosion, osteophyte, sclerosis and subchondral cyst were 0.92, 0.97, 0.93 and 0.93, respectively [32]. When evaluating the results of our study, the obtained values were found to be somewhat lower than those reported in the literature. However, our study was able to distinguish the TMDs in three dimensions, a capability that has not been previously demonstrated in the literature. This difference in approach may be considered the primary reason for the performance gap between our study and existing studies. In 2D imaging, models are trained directly on slices where the TMD is most apparent, whereas in our study, the model attempts to perform a three‐dimensional evaluation of the entire mandibular condyle.

The clinical applicability of the models developed in the present study further highlights the aspects that distinguish this work from previous studies in the literature. The most important clinical contribution of the study is the presentation of a framework trained and tested on a multicentre dataset that also includes pathological condyles. Despite the heterogeneous nature of the dataset and variables that could potentially reduce model performance, the proposed approach demonstrated clinically meaningful performance within a framework beginning with automatic condylar segmentation and subsequently enabling discrimination of whether the condyle is healthy or relative to healthy condyles, which type of TMD it is associated with. This finding suggests that the model has the potential for applicability not only under controlled experimental conditions but also in scenarios that more closely reflect clinical reality. If this framework is further developed into a clinical software application, it may become possible to perform automatic TMJ segmentation and disease detection directly from unprocessed DICOM imaging data. Such an approach has the potential to be positioned within the clinical workflow. Particularly considering the high performance of the automatic segmentation component, reducing time‐consuming manual segmentation processes and contributing to the standardization of radiological assessment may become feasible. Nevertheless, when the healthy–pathological binary classification task is evaluated independently, the obtained F1‐score (0.65) appears to be insufficient at present for use as a robust screening tool in clinical settings. Moreover, it is noteworthy that this performance was lower than that achieved in the subtype classification task (F1: 0.76–0.88). One possible explanation for this finding may be that five distinct pathological conditions with markedly different morphological signatures were grouped into a single heterogeneous ‘pathological’ category, requiring the model to reduce these inconsistent patterns into a common decision boundary. In addition, the morphological similarity of mild or early‐stage cases to healthy condyles may have contributed to substantial within‐class overlap in the binary classification task.

The performance differences observed among the model components also appear to be informative in terms of clinical positioning and the biological nature of TMDs. From a broader perspective, the observed performance hierarchy (segmentation > subtype classification > binary classification > severity grading) may reflect the extent to which each task is biologically and morphologically well‐defined. Learning an anatomical shape or boundary represents a more distinct problem than discriminating subtle tissue‐density patterns. Similarly, identifying individual pathological subtypes with consistent and distinctive morphological characteristics may constitute a better‐defined task than classifying a heterogeneous pathology group under a single category. In contrast, fine‐grained severity grading inherently represents a more challenging problem because it requires differentiation among morphologically continuous changes.

Considering these findings, the current algorithm may provide clinical benefit at multiple levels of practice. First, due to its high segmentation performance, it may serve as a rapid and reliable tool in situations where automatic condylar segmentation is required. Second, it may function as a preliminary assessment or screening tool for healthy–pathological discrimination. Finally, considering its relatively higher performance in distinguishing specific TMD subtypes, it has the potential to serve as a decision‐support system or second‐reader aid for clinicians. Future studies should evaluate the effect of this algorithm on clinician performance and investigate the real‐world contribution of AI‐assisted approaches in routine clinical practice. Furthermore, the development of more comprehensive models supported by larger datasets and multimodal inputs, capable of performing segmentation, diagnosis, grading, and prognostic assessment within a single integrated system, may contribute to the advancement of AI‐assisted clinical decision‐making mechanisms in TMJ imaging.

One of the main limitations of our study was the challenge encountered in training the models developed to segment and classify TMD by severity grades. This difficulty stemmed from the imbalance in data distribution across severity grades and the relatively small dataset. Designing studies with large‐scale datasets that include ample samples for each of the three severity levels of every TMD type, without class imbalance, would address this issue observed in the fourth stage of our work and enable the generation of more detailed results. Nevertheless, in the other three stages of our study—segmenting the mandibular condyle; differentiating between healthy and pathological condyles in 3D following segmentation; and distinguishing healthy mandibular condyles from those affected by five distinct TMD features—our approach adds a new dimension to artificial intelligence applications aimed at the examination and assessment of the TMJ. Furthermore, the use of CBCT data obtained from two different centres enhances the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

Deep learning algorithms can perform all stages of CBCT evaluation of the TMJ, from the segmentation of the mandibular condyle to assessing whether it is healthy or pathological, and distinguishing different types of TMDs from healthy condyles, achieving performance comparable to that of expert dentomaxillofacial radiologists.
