In situ sol-gel-sol transformation formed by sodium alginate realizes folic acid-modified chitosan nanoparticles to deliver hops β-acids for colorectal cancer therapy
Authors: Xia Qiao, Jiayue Liu, Jing Bai, Chao Xu, Qianying Zhao, Songlin Guo, Lu Ding, Duan Ma, Xu Zhang, Bingren Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral administration can deliver targeted nanoparticles directly to colorectal cancer (CRC) lesions, bypassing systemic circulation. However, orally administered nanoparticles may become physically entrapped in the small intestine, compromising therapeutic efficacy. To overcome this limitation, we designed and prepared folic acid-modified, CRC cell-targeting nanoparticles (FA/CS/TPP/β-acids) and incorporated them into a sodium alginate hydrogel (Alg/FA/CS/TPP/β-acids). The release of β-acids from the nanoparticles was pH-dependent, with cumulative release following the order: pH 7.8 > pH 6.8 > pH 1.2. The nanoparticle/hydrogel system exhibits good biocompatibility (hemolytic activity < 5%; non-toxic to NCM460 cells) and significantly enhances cellular uptake in HCT116 and HT29 cells. In vivo studies showed that the nanoparticles were non-toxic and effectively inhibited CRC. Interestingly, the hydrogel undergoes a sol-gel transition under acidic gastric conditions (pH 1.2). Upon reaching the colorectum, where the pH is higher, it undergoes a gel-sol transition. These results demonstrate that the alginate-modified hydrogel can protect the nanoparticles in the gastrointestinal environment, enabling targeted release for CRC therapy.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04756-w.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents the most prevalent malignancy globally, with roughly 1.4 million new cases and around 774,000 fatalities annually [1]. The challenges of late-stage diagnosis and suboptimal treatment contribute to CRC’s significant economic and health burden [2, 3]. Presently, the primary clinical interventions for CRC include surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, with chemotherapy being regarded as one of the most promising modalities [4, 5]. However, conventional CRC treatments are hindered by several limitations, including insufficient efficacy, severe adverse effects, and both intrinsic and acquired resistance. Notably, patients with CRC often present with unresectable tumors that exhibit resistance to standard chemotherapy regimens. 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) constitutes a first-line chemotherapeutic drug, yet resistance to 5-FU poses a substantial clinical challenge [6, 7]. Consequently, the investigation of targeted therapies, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and immunotherapy for CRC has emerged as a pivotal advancement in cancer treatment [8–10]. Despite the heterogeneity of CRC, numerous researchers are actively developing alternative, more efficacious pharmacological interventions to enhance patient safety and health outcomes. Notably, targeted therapeutics can identify patients with receptor overexpression, thereby facilitating the selection of appropriate candidates for personalized treatment and optimizing targeted therapy outcomes [11]. Furthermore, nanoparticle drug delivery systems would significantly enhance drug efficacy while concurrently minimizing adverse side effects [12, 13]. Furthermore, nanoparticles could be modified to enhance therapeutic selectivity and specificity [14–17]. Therefore, discovering novel anti-cancer drugs that target the primary causative factors of CRC represents a significant advancement in this field [18–20].

With continued technological advances, CRC treatment strategies have also become more diverse, spurring the development of various medical materials such as hydrogels, nanoparticles, intestinal scaffolds, and degradable repair materials [21–23]. To enhance the targeting capabilities of drug carriers, nanoparticles are often modified to achieve superior anti-cancer effects. Folic acid (FA), a crucial micronutrient, functions as a key regulator in the metabolism of DNA, RNA, and proteins [24]. Generally, low or inadequate FA levels are correlated with multiple diseases, spanning neurological disorders, cancer, and cardiovascular diseases. Moreover, FA receptors are overexpressed on lesion cells (breast, ovary, uterus, kidney, colon, and lung), which makes FA a desired tumor marker [25–27]. FA receptors are expressed approximately 100–300 times more on tumor cells than on normal cells [28]. Additionally, FA offers several advantages over other ligands, including receptor specificity, efficient coupling, accessibility, and non-immunogenicity, thereby facilitating site-specific delivery of FA-modified nanoparticles [29]. When FA-bound nanoparticles interact with FA receptors on tumor cells, they thereby enhance drug retention within the tumor microenvironment [30–32].

The selection of the carrier material is a pivotal factor in the preparation of nanodelivery systems, as its physicochemical properties and its formulation into nanoparticles significantly impact the controlled release and the drug’s physiological activity [33]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that various biopolymers modified with FA can markedly enhance the effective delivery of active compounds [34]. Among naturally occurring polymeric polysaccharides, chitosan (CS) is recognized as an ideal and extensively utilized biocarrier in the food and nutraceutical sectors [35]. CS exhibits excellent biocompatibility, low toxicity, and affordability, making it highly applicable in both the food and pharmaceutical industries. Currently, CS nanoparticles facilitate the controlled release of diverse drug types, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of tumor-targeted drug delivery [36–38]. FA-conjugated CS derivative nanoparticles have the potential to enhance drug solubility and stability. Furthermore, FA-modified nanoparticles are versatile, facilitating drug delivery not only via oral administration but also through mucous membranes, such as those in the nasal cavity and lungs. These nanoparticles have enormous potential for tumor therapy.

In recent years, anti-cancer agents such as curcumin, adriamycin, thymoquinone, and vincristine have been encapsulated within FA-modified nanoparticles to enhance tumor-targeted drug delivery [39–42]. When a natural bioactive compound exhibits both antibacterial and anti-cancer properties, it can be utilized as a potential therapeutic agent. Hops, belonging to the Humulus genus in the Cannabaceae family, are perennial plants extensively cultivated globally [43]. Owing to their distinctive flavor, hops are predominantly utilized in the beer brewing industry. Various bioactive constituents have been identified in hops, demonstrating beneficial health effects and bioactivities [44]. Notably, β-acids, active compounds derived from the soft resins of hops, have been proven to possess remarkable antibacterial, antioxidant, and anti-cancer properties [45]. Consequently, β-acids hold great potential for applications within the pharmaceutical industry.

Although a large number of tumor-targeting nanoparticles have been developed at this stage, their non-targeted distribution significantly impairs drug delivery [46]. Therefore, there is a demand for the development of targeted drugs that can bypass systemic circulation and effectively deliver drugs to tumor lesions, thereby improving drug efficacy. In addition, some studies have reported that oral delivery of drugs directly to CRC sites is a promising alternative [47, 48]. So far, a variety of methods have been attempted to deliver drugs to colorectal cancer tumor sites through oral administration, such as magnetic response, pH response, ROS response, etc. [49, 50]. However, when anti-cancer drugs are delivered to CRC sites through these strategies, they are first lost prematurely in the gastrointestinal environment, thus limiting their therapeutic effect [51]. To address these deficiencies, researchers reported that hydrogels with sodium alginate (Alg) matrix undergo a sol-gel transition under different conditions [52–55]. Lee’s group loaded self-assembled drugs with targeted properties into Alg hydrogel and found that the hydrogel can reversibly shield/unshield nanoparticles during gastrointestinal tract passage, as well as activate a drug release strategy responsive to the tumor’s internal environment, thus enabling precise localized CRC treatment [48].

We hypothesize that the limitations of orally targeted nanoparticles can be overcome by harnessing the sol-gel-sol transition of alginate (Alg) in the gastrointestinal tract, combined with folic acid (FA)-mediated CRC targeting (Scheme 1). In this study, FA-modified, β-acids-loaded CS nanoparticles were synthesized to target CRC cells. Then, the nanoparticle is incorporated into Alg matrix to bypass systemic circulation for targeted, specific oral nanoparticle delivery. Compared with existing pH-responsive oral delivery platforms, our system achieves a dual synergistic effect of “hydrogel-mediated intestinal retention” and “nanoparticle-mediated active targeting,” effectively overcoming the limitations of poor stability, low targeting efficiency, and short duration of action associated with conventional oral targeted formulations. Through the aforementioned strategies, site-specific drug accumulation in CRC can be achieved without systemic circulation, offering new perspectives for CRC therapy.

Materials and methods

Materials

Chitosan (DD: 90%, MW: 70–80 kDa) was offered by Siji Technology Development Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Folic acid was supported by Bailing Wei Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Sodium alginate (MW: 1.0-1.8 MDa) was brought from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Calcium chloride was purchased from Tianjin Bohua Tong Chemical Products Sales Center. Sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP) and 5-FU were supplied by McLean Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). β-acids were extracted from hop extract in our laboratory [47]. N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N’-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), pepsin, trypsin, and trypsin were supported by Aladdin Biochemistry and Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Nanoparticle and hydrogel preparation process

The hydrogel was prepared through three successive steps: (i) synthesis of the FA/CS copolymer [57]; (ii) preparation of FA/CS/TPP/β-acids nanoparticles via ionic crosslinking [47, 56]; and (iii) incorporation of the nanoparticles into alginate matrix to form the Alg/FA/CS/TPP/β-acids hydrogel. Briefly, FA (50 mg), EDC (17.57 mg), and NHS (13.04 mg) (molar ratio: 1:1.5:1.5) were dissolved in DMSO (40 mL), and the reaction was stirred at 500 rpm in the dark at 30 °C for 3 h. Separately, CS (1 g) was dissolved in 1% acetic acid (200 mL). The resulting active ester of FA was added dropwise (1 mL/min) to the CS solution, followed by stirring at 25 °C for 24 h. The pH was then adjusted to 9.0 with 3 M NaOH, and the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The yellow precipitate was washed with saturated NaHCO3 solution and freeze-dried to afford FA/CS powder (yield: 56%). Next, the FA/CS powder (500 mg) was dissolved in 1% acetic acid (100 mL), and the pH was adjusted to 5.0 with 3 M NaOH. A β-acids/ethanol solution (1 mg/mL; 7.5, 15, or 22.5 mL) was slowly added dropwise (1 mL/min) to the FA/CS solution while stirring at 500 rpm for 30 min. Subsequently, TPP solution (2 mg/mL, 20 mL) was added dropwise (1 mL/min), and the reaction mixture was stirred at 500 rpm for 1 h. The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C and freeze-dried to obtain FA/CS/TPP/β-acids nanoparticles (yield: 78%) (Table S1).

Furthermore, the FA content in the FA/CS/TPP copolymer was determined by UV-Vis spectrophotometry. Briefly, the copolymer was dissolved in DMSO, and its absorbance was measured at 360 nm by UV-vis spectrophotometer (UV-2000, Unico). The FA content was calculated by referencing a standard calibration curve (Y = 0.1005X + 0.3018, R2 = 0.9996). In vitro drug release experiment

Nanoparticles/hydrogels (5 mg) were placed in dialysis membranes (MWCO: 3500 Da, Beijing Solab Technology Co., Ltd.) and immersed in 30 mL of PBS at different pH values (1.2, 6.8, and 7.8) at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. At predetermined time intervals, 2 mL of the release medium was withdrawn for absorbance measurement and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS (n = 3).

Cell viability assays

HCT116/HT29 cells were inoculated into 96-well plates for 12 h. β-acids, 5-FU, nanoparticles, and hydrogel were added at different concentrations and incubated for 24 h (n = 3). The supernatant in each well was replaced with fresh medium, and 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added. The plate was incubated at 37 ℃ for 1 h.

HCT116, HT29, and NCM460 cells were inoculated into 6-well plates. After the cells were fully adhered, a 10 µL of a lance tip was used to create straight-line scratches. The cells were cultured in a medium containing the nanoparticle/hydrogel and placed in an incubator at 37 ℃. After 24 h, the cells were photographed under a microscope (n = 3).

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was isolated from HCT116 cells in the control and β-acids-treated groups (n = 3 per group) by Trizol reagent. RNA integrity was then evaluated, and transcriptome sequencing was performed.

Cellular uptake

HCT116/HT29 cells were selected to study the cellular uptake. Coumarin-6 was selected as the fluorescent probe, and Coumarin-6-loaded nanoparticles/hydrogels were prepared. The cells were inoculated in 12-well plates and cultured for 24 h. The original medium was discarded; FA-containing medium and FA-free medium were added, and the mixture was treated with Coumarin-6-labeled nanoparticles/hydrogels (2 h). The medium was discarded, washed with PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde fixative. Cellular uptake was observed using laser confocal microscopy.

Apoptosis detection by flow cytometry

Apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-APC/7-AAD Apoptosis Kit (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). HCT116 and HT29 cells were treated with β-acids, 5-FU, nanoparticles, or hydrogels for 12 h. After treatment, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min, washed once with PBS, and resuspended. An aliquot of 5 × 10⁵ cells was washed again with PBS, centrifuged, and resuspended in 500 µL of 1× Annexin V binding buffer. Then, 5 µL of Annexin V-APC and 5 µL of 7-AAD staining solution were added and mixed thoroughly. The samples were incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark and analyzed by flow cytometry on a BD FACSAria III. Data were processed with FlowJo 10.8.1 software.

Hemolysis assay

Red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated from fresh rabbit blood by centrifugation at 1,500 rpm for 10 min. A 2% RBC suspension was prepared by mixing 1 mL of packed RBCs with 19 mL of PBS (pH 7.4). Nanoparticles/hydrogels were added to 2 mL aliquots of the RBC suspension to achieve final concentrations of 1.0, 0.5, 0.25, and 0.125 mg/mL, and the mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After centrifugation at 1,500 rpm for 10 min, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 541 nm. Deionized water and PBS were used as positive and negative controls, respectively.

In vivo safety exploration

Male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Xavier Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility. To evaluate the in vivo safety of nanoparticles and hydrogels, mice were assigned to the following groups (n = 6 per group): a healthy control group, and groups receiving FA/CS/TPP nanoparticles, FA/CS/TPP/22.5β-acids nanoparticles, Alg/FA/CS/TPP hydrogel, or Alg/FA/CS/TPP/22.5β-acids hydrogel. Each nanoparticle or hydrogel formulation was administered at three dose levels: high (400 mg/kg), medium (200 mg/kg), and low (100 mg/kg). The formulations were given by oral gavage every other day for 14 days, and the mice were monitored for clinical signs after each administration. At the end of the 14-day treatment period, blood samples were collected to measure red blood cell count, hemoglobin concentration, white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, and monocyte count, as well as serum levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total protein (TP), and creatinine (CREA). The heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were harvested for histopathological examination by H&E staining.

In vivo CRC model therapy

An orthotopic CRC model was established by injecting MC38 or CT26 cells (50 µL, 3 × 107 cells per mouse) into the cecal wall of male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old). Five days after inoculation, tumor formation was confirmed by laparotomy. The mice were then randomly divided into the following groups (n = 6 per group): healthy mice receiving PBS (normal group), tumor-bearing mice receiving PBS (model group), and tumor-bearing mice treated with 5‑FU, FA/CS/TPP/22.5β‑acids nanoparticles, or Alg/FA/CS/TPP/22.5β‑acids hydrogel. The drug were administered by oral gavage at 400 mg/kg every other day for two weeks, while 5‑FU (25 mg/kg) was given intraperitoneally once weekly as a positive control. At the end of the experiment, the mice were euthanized. Colorectal tissues were harvested, stored at -80 °C, and subsequently processed for H&E staining.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Before performing one-way ANOVA, normality and homogeneity of variance were verified. All data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test with GraphPad Prism. Statistical significance was indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of nanoparticle

Studies have found that the low solubility of FA can seriously affect its bioavailability; however, employing methods to increase its solubility or control pH can overcome this shortcoming [57]. In addition, FA is sensitive to light, so all reactions were carried out under dark conditions [58]. FA possesses a highly reactive γ-COOH group [58]. In this study, EDC and NHS were used to activate the γ-COOH group, which then reacted with the amino group of the chitosan backbone, and the final product was obtained by adjusting the pH (Fig. 1A). Because FA contains an active carboxyl group, Zhang et al. used a one-step esterification reaction to link the carboxyl group of FA with the hydroxyl group of hyaluronic acid (HA) to generate an HA derivative. That study demonstrated that this derivative possesses dual targeting characteristics [59]. Other research teams have employed the same method as used in this study and successfully conjugated FA with chitosan to form various nanoparticles with distinct biological activities [60, 61]. These different experimental results further indicate that this method is reproducible.

The coupling of FA and CS was verified by ¹H NMR (Fig. 1B–D). The chemical shifts were observed at 8.65, 8.13, 7.64, 6.95, 6.66, 4.49, 4.33, 2.51, and 2.03 ppm, corresponding to the FA protons H-7, H-18, H-13/15, H-10, H-12/16, H-19, H-22, and H-21, respectively [62]. After the coupling reaction between FA and CS, some absorption peaks of FA and CS overlapped. A CH peak was observed at 3.33–4.03 ppm, corresponding to hydrogens 3, 4, 5, and 6 of the CS amino ring. The signal at 2.35 ppm corresponds to the acetylamine group of CS, further confirming successful coupling of FA and CS. Furthermore, the FA content in the FA/CS/TPP copolymer was quantified as 14.46 ± 1.6 µg/mg.

The characteristic absorption peaks of CS, FA, TPP, β-acids, FA/CS, FA/CS/TPP, and FA/CS/TPP/β-acids were characterized by FTIR (Fig. 2A). CS showed a broad absorption peak at 3365 cm-1, corresponding to the -OH functional group [63]. The peak at 2876 cm-1 was attributed to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of C-H. Absorption peaks at 1655 cm-1, 1598 cm-1, and 1381 cm-1 were assigned to the amide I, II, and III bands of the O = C-NH groups in CS. Near 1194 cm-1, the C-O stretching vibration of CS was observed. FA exhibited absorption peaks at 3544 cm-1, 3324 cm-1, and 3116 cm-1, which were related to the stretching vibrations of -NH, -OH, and -CH groups, respectively. The characteristic peaks of FA were mainly located at 1696 cm-1, 1605 cm-1, and 1485 cm-1, corresponding to carboxylate vibration, amino group, and C = C or C = N stretching [64].

By comparing the FTIR spectra of FA/CS, FA, and CS, it was observed that the absorption peaks of FA/CS at 1655 cm-1 and 1598 cm-1 disappeared, while new absorption peaks appeared at 1512 cm-1 and 1609 cm-1. These new peaks were assigned to the stretching vibrations of C = C in the aromatic ring main chain [65]. These changes indicated that the -OH group of FA was linked to the -NH₂ group of CS via chemical reaction. In addition, the absorption peak at 1512 cm-1 was associated with the N-H (amide II) bending vibration in FA/CS. This evidence suggests that the -COOH group in FA reacted with the -NH₂ group of CS to form an amide bond, confirming the successful synthesis of the FA/CS conjugate.

For TPP, the absorption peaks at 1169 cm-1 and 1213 cm-1 were assigned to the O-P-O stretching vibration and the P = O stretching vibration, respectively. The peak at 900 cm-1 was attributed to the P-O-P bridge stretching vibration. In FA/CS/TPP, the peak at 1541 cm-1 corresponded to the C = O-NH stretching vibration. Regarding β-acids, a characteristic peak was observed at 3224 cm-1. Furthermore, the peak at 2971 cm-1 was attributed to C-H vibration, and the peak at 1665 cm-1 was assigned to the C = O vibration of β-acids, consistent with our previous study [56]. After loading β-acids, the FTIR absorption spectra changed. Specifically, the -OH group in β-acids interacted with the FA/CS/TPP nanoparticles, shifting the -OH absorption peak to 3403 cm-1, while the peak at 2971 cm-1 specific to β-acids was retained. Collectively, the FTIR results demonstrated that FA-targeted chitosan nanoparticles loaded with β-acids were successfully obtained.

As shown in Fig. 2B, FA/CS/TPP, FA/CS/TPP/7.5β-acids, FA/CS/TPP/15β-acids, and FA/CS/TPP/22.5β-acids were spherical or quasi-spherical but exhibited severe aggregation and adhesion. Although the nanoparticles were not uniform in size, their diameters were all slightly less than 300 nm. With increasing β-acids content, the nanoparticle surfaces became smoother. This phenomenon was attributed to the fact that the presence of β-acids improved nanoparticle adhesion to some extent and increased aggregation [66]. The adhesion of FA/CS/TPP nanoparticles was reduced compared with that of nanoparticles without β-acids. TEM was further used to analyze nanoparticle morphology (Figure S1). Similar to the SEM results, the dispersion of drug-loaded nanoparticles was severely weakened by the presence of β-acids, preventing the formation of clear boundaries between nanoparticles. This phenomenon was attributed to the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds between FA/CS/TPP particles and β-acids. Nanoparticle aggregation has been attributed to enhanced interparticle attractive forces arising from the increased surface-area-to-volume ratio. Additionally, when the core–shell structure of PEG/CS-stabilized nanoparticles dissociates, the presence of polymers can lead to a broader particle size distribution [67, 68]. Furthermore, studies have shown that the coprecipitation method often yields agglomerated particles, which are closely associated and form lumps due to electrostatic attraction between surface anions and cations [69, 70].

The crystal structure of the drug-loaded nanoparticles was characterized by XRD, which can provide information on subsequent drug dissolution from the nanoparticles (Fig. 2C). It has been reported that some drugs are dispersed in amorphous or solid form when loaded into nanoparticles [64]. The XRD diffractogram of CS revealed a distinct diffraction peak at 2θ = 21°, although the peak was not sharp, suggesting that CS exists predominantly in an amorphous state [65]. β-acids exhibited characteristic peaks at 2θ = 8.8°, 9.2°, and 30.1°, indicating its crystalline nature [56]. The XRD diffraction peaks of TPP, used as a crosslinking agent, were mainly observed at 2θ = 19.7°, 20.3°, 20.8°, 26.0°, 35.0°, 35.7°, and 36.2°. When FA and CS were crosslinked by TPP, the resulting blank nanoparticles (FA/CS/TPP) showed a diffraction peak at 2θ = 22.2°, which was broad, indicating an amorphous form. After the addition of β-acids into FA/CS/TPP, a diffraction peak reappeared near 2θ = 8.7° compared with the blank nanoparticles, confirming that β-acids were successfully loaded but existed in an amorphous form within the nanoparticles.

Analysis of the preparation process revealed that CS dissolved in an acidic solution was positively charged. Similarly, the polymer nanoparticles formed by crosslinking FA-modified CS with TPP (FA/CS/TPP) were also positively charged. When β-acids were introduced, the positively charged species decreased, indicating ionic interactions between FA/CS/TPP and β-acids during the formation of FA/CS/TPP/β-acids. These ionic interactions play an essential role in the crosslinking process and the subsequent formation of drug-loaded nanoparticles, enabling rapid crosslinking in solution to yield stable polymer nanoparticles. As a small molecule, β-acids rapidly bind to the blank nanoparticles during the formation of drug-loaded nanoparticles. Consequently, after incorporation, β-acids exist in an amorphous form within the polymer matrix.

In the examination of nanoparticle size, we found that although the β-acids content gradually increased, no significant difference was observed compared with FA/CS/TPP. The particle size distributions were as follows: 286.67 ± 4.63 nm, 266 ± 16.06 nm, 286 ± 12.03 nm, and 265 ± 8.22 nm, respectively (Fig. 2D). The nanoparticle sizes measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) were larger than those observed by TEM/SEM, which can be explained by the nanoparticles being in an aqueous, swollen state during DLS measurement, whereas they were directly dried for TEM/SEM analysis. Consequently, TEM/SEM does not accurately reflect the nanoparticle size in body fluids. Moreover, complex components in body fluids can adsorb onto the nanoparticle surface, increasing their overall size [71, 72]. Therefore, assessing particle size in solution, especially in serum-supplemented media that mimic physiological fluids, is critically important. The nanoparticle sizes determined by DLS were within an acceptable range, demonstrating the successful fabrication of nanoparticles using the ion-gel technique. Furthermore, a 7-day stability study revealed that the nanoparticles could maintain their stability under physiological conditions (Figure S2). In zeta potential measurements, FA/CS/TPP nanoparticles carried a positive charge of + 12.73 mV (Fig. 2D). β-acids are negatively charged molecules. The high positive surface charge of the nanoparticles promotes their binding to negatively charged cell membranes, rendering them an ideal platform for drug delivery [73]. Encapsulation of drugs using ionic gelation is a commonly used strategy. β-acids, as a hydrophobic drug, were stabilized by electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding. The drug loading contents (LC) of the different drug-loaded nanoparticles were 20.97 ± 0.89%, 20.79 ± 1.04%, and 32.19 ± 1.11%, and the encapsulation efficiencies (EE) were 44.61 ± 1.80%, 45.31 ± 1.49%, and 43.41 ± 1.31%, respectively (Fig. 2D). EE and LC are parameters that reflect the ability of different raw materials to encapsulate the drug and the quality of the formulation. These parameters mainly depend on the type of feedstock, drug polarity, and nanoparticle preparation method.

Alg is an anionic polysaccharide composed of mannuronic acid (M) and guluronic acid (G) [74]. In aqueous solution, it dissociates, releasing sodium ions and causing the molecular chains to expand. Upon the addition of Ca2+, ion exchange occurs. Ca2+ precisely embeds into the spatial depressions of the highly rigid GG segments. Based on the “egg-box” model, Ca2+ bridges are formed through ionic and coordination bonds between molecules, achieving physical crosslinking of multiple polymer chains and firmly locking in water, thereby rapidly forming hydrogel. The higher the G-unit content, the greater the gel strength. This crosslinking process is mild and controllable at room temperature, and the resulting gel exhibits thermal irreversibility, being decomposable only by chelating agents or high-concentration sodium salts. To characterize Alg, Alg/FA/CS/TPP, Alg/FA/CS/TPP/7.5β-acids, Alg/FA/CS/TPP/15β-acids, and Alg/FA/CS/TPP/22.5β-acids and to evaluate their interactions with cells, we explored the sol-gel-sol transition of the hydrogels in vitro. The morphological changes of the hydrogels were examined under simulated stomach (pH 1.2), small intestine (pH 6.8), and large intestine (pH 7.4) conditions to determine whether they exhibit sol-gel-sol transition (Fig. 3A). When the hydrogel was exposed to pH 1.2, it immediately formed a gel. Interestingly, this gel structure did not disintegrate upon exposure to pH 6.8 medium and maintained its non-flowing, semi-solid nature. In contrast, when exposed to pH 7.4 medium, the gel completely disintegrated, transforming back into a sol. These results confirmed that the hydrogel prepared in this study possesses sol-gel-sol transition characteristics.

We also characterized the hydrogels by FTIR. The FTIR spectrum of Alg showed an O-H stretching vibration peak at 3428 cm-1, a C-O stretching vibration peak at 1612 cm-1, and another C-O stretching vibration peak at 1417 cm-1, indicating the presence of -COOH groups in Alg [75]. When Alg and Ca2+ were successfully crosslinked to form a hydrogel, characteristic absorption peaks appeared at 1598 cm-1 and 1416 cm-1. Studies have shown that these peaks are associated with the stretching vibration of –COO⁻, most of which combine with Ca2+ to form a three-dimensional crosslinked network [76]. Compared with the FTIR spectrum of sodium alginate, the wavenumber shifted to a lower region (1598 cm-1), further suggesting an interaction between –COO⁻ and Ca2+. da Silva Fernandes et al. reported that FTIR undergoes a similar shift after calcium ions interact with Alg, and that the shift changes with varying Ca2+ content [77]. After adding blank nanoparticles to the hydrogel, no obvious shift in the infrared characteristic peaks was observed. However, upon addition of β-acids, characteristic β-acids absorption peaks (2974 cm-1, 1758 cm-1) appeared in FTIR spectrum, and the active groups in β-acids interacted with the hydrogel molecules, thereby altering the infrared absorption peaks. Among these, the O-H stretching vibration peak appeared at 3402 cm-1, the C-O stretching vibration peak at 1598 cm-1, and another C-O stretching vibration peak at approximately 1420 cm-1. These results indicate that drug-loaded nanoparticles were successfully loaded into Alg hydrogel. The SEM results (Fig. 3C) showed that the hydrogel crosslinked by Alg and Ca2+ exhibited a sparse pore structure with surface wrinkles. After adding blank nanoparticles, the wrinkles on the hydrogel surface were reduced, but smaller structures appeared. As the content of drug-loaded nanoparticles gradually increased, the hydrogels displayed a more obvious three-dimensional network structure. All these hydrogels had similar morphological characteristics, with a significant 3D network structure and larger pore size.

In the angular frequency sweep tests (1–100 rad/s) (Fig. 3D), both hydrogels exhibited frequency-dependent viscoelastic responses. All tested hydrogels demonstrated pronounced elastic behavior (G’ > G’’), with the storage modulus (G”) exceeding the loss modulus (G’’). The storage modulus G’ of the Alg/FA/CS/TPP hydrogel was higher than that of the Alg/FA/CS/TPP/β-acids hydrogel, with the introduction of β-acids primarily increasing G’ due to an enhanced crosslinking network. Furthermore, the shear recovery characteristics of the hydrogel microcapsules were investigated by applying alternating high-shear and low-shear strain cycles (Fig. 3E). Rheological analysis revealed that both blank and drug-loaded hydrogels exhibited favorable mechanical compatibility (Fig. 3F). Within the strain range of 0.01% to 1000%, G’ dominated G’’ and remained stable, indicating solid-state behavior (Fig. 3G). When G’’ exceeded G’, the hydrogels transitioned to a quasi-fluid state rather than a solid gel state.

The hydrogel prepared in this study can be smoothly shaped under external shear forces during oral swallowing and delivered into the body through the gastrointestinal tract. Upon entering the acidic environment of the stomach, the hydrogel maintains its intact three‑dimensional network structure owing to its favorable storage modulus, thereby resisting gastric erosion and preventing premature drug leakage. Once it reaches the weakly alkaline environment of the intestine, its rheological behavior changes: the hydrogel gradually swells, softens, and undergoes structural relaxation, enabling controlled and sustained drug release. Meanwhile, its moderate viscoelasticity prolongs the retention time at the intestinal lesion site, enhancing the local duration of drug action and bioavailability. Furthermore, the excellent thixotropic properties and mechanical stability of the hydrogel ensure that it does not easily disintegrate under the peristaltic forces of the gastrointestinal tract, significantly improving the in vivo delivery efficiency and targeted release performance of orally administered drugs.

To investigate the intestinal distribution of FA/CS/TPP/Coumarin-6 and Alg/FA/CS/TPP/Coumarin-6, we performed ex vivo intestinal fluorescence imaging 12 h after oral administration to visually assess drug distribution characteristics (Figure S3). The results showed that the entire small intestine of the Alg/FA/CS/TPP/Coumarin-6 group exhibited high-intensity fluorescence, with significant enrichment also observed in the colon, suggesting that Alg/FA/CS/TPP/Coumarin-6 significantly prolonged intestinal retention and slowed metabolic clearance. In contrast, the fluorescence signal in the FA/CS/TPP/Coumarin-6 group showed segmental distribution, with significantly weaker intensity than that in the Alg/FA/CS/TPP/Coumarin-6 group, indicating that FA/CS/TPP/Coumarin-6 had undergone substantial absorption or degradation, resulting in low intestinal residue.

In vitro release from nanoparticle and hydrogel

The release of β-acids from nanoparticles was investigated. In most cases, the pH of human tumors is approximately 6.8, while that of normal tissues is approximately 7.4 [78]. The cumulative percentage release of β-acids from the nanoparticles is shown in Fig. 4. The release profiles were similar under the three different pH conditions. Biphasic release kinetics were observed: an initial burst release followed by a slower sustained release. β-acids were released rapidly during the initial phase, and then more slowly through diffusion-controlled and degradation-mediated release during the later stabilization phase (Fig. 4A–C). The nanoparticle groups exhibited two-phase release patterns. The initial rapid release may be attributed to the dissolution of drug adsorbed on the nanoparticle surfaces, as well as greater diffusion across the medium due to the large surface area of the nanoparticles. The subsequent slow release may result from drug encapsulation within the polymer matrix [79]. As the pH decreased, the cumulative release of β-acids decreased, indicating that the nanoparticles degraded more rapidly in alkaline media. Experiments showed that the nanoparticles were more stable under acidic conditions. In contrast, the diffusion of β-acids increased significantly under alkaline conditions. This may be explained by the deprotonation of amino groups on the chitosan chain in alkaline environments, which weakens or eliminates the ionic interactions between cationic CS and anionic TPP, thereby accelerating the release of β-acids. For example, during the initial rapid release phase at pH 7.4, the release percentages of nanoparticles with different β-acids loadings were 17.5% (FA/CS/TPP/7.5β-acids), 16% (FA/CS/TPP/15β-acids), and 21% (FA/CS/TPP/22.5β-acids), respectively. During the subsequent stable release phase, the values were 18%, 16.5%, and 22%, respectively. During the stable release phase, both the cumulative release and the release rate of β-acids decreased markedly, which could be attributed to buffer penetration into the nanoparticles and their subsequent disintegration. Finally, β-acids diffuse out of the solubilized polymer. In summary, the in vitro release of β-acids from nanoparticles was pH-dependent.

We also studied drug release from the hydrogels. Our findings demonstrated that the release profile of β-acids from the hydrogels also followed a burst-sustained release pattern. Under pH 1.2 conditions (simulated gastric fluid), the release amounts of β-acids from the different drug-loaded hydrogels were 19.6% (Alg/FA/CS/TPP/7.5β-acids), 21.0% (Alg/FA/CS/TPP/15β-acids), and 24.3% (Alg/FA/CS/TPP/22.5β-acids). At pH 6.8, the cumulative release amounts were approximately 20.3%, 24.1%, and 24.1%, respectively. At pH 7.4, the release rates were 24.2%, 26.5%, and 32.1%, respectively. These observations indicate that in the presence of the hydrogel, the total amount of drug released is maximal at pH 7.4, suggesting that the hydrogel can accelerate the release of β-acids from the nanoparticles. This further implies that the drug-loaded hydrogel can undergo significant drug release in the intestinal tract and specifically release β-acids at the tumor site to exert an anticancer effect. Therefore, reducing the direct exposure of the drug to intestinal cells during actual administration would help overcome potential side effects. It should be noted that in vivo release is expected to differ from the in vitro situation. The present release study only measured the amount of β-acids released within 24 h, whereas in vivo release is a long-term cumulative process. Nevertheless, the in vitro release data can provide a basis for selecting appropriate hydrogels for oral delivery. The active ingredients in the FA-modified nanoparticles are shown in Table S3.

Mathematical model fitting was performed to better explain the release mechanism (Table 3). Three mathematical models (first-order, Higuchi, and Ritger-Peppas) were selected to fit the release of β-acids from both nanoparticles and hydrogels. These three models form a classic combination for fitting in vitro drug release in pharmaceutics, comprehensively covering the main release behaviors of nanoparticles. The first-order model describes dissolution-type release driven by concentration gradients; the Higuchi model describes Fickian diffusion-controlled sustained release from a matrix; and the Ritger-Peppas model can precisely determine the release mechanism (diffusion, swelling, erosion, or a combination) through the release exponent “n”. These models have simple mathematical forms, are easy to fit, and have clear physical meanings. They can mutually verify the reliability of the results and are standard methods in the field, allowing systematic elucidation of release laws and internal controlled-release mechanisms [80–82]. Based on the R² values, the Ritger-Peppas model provided a better fit than the first-order and Higuchi models. The diffusion exponent “n” was below 0.45 in all cases, indicating that the release of β-acids from both nanoparticles and hydrogels followed Fickian diffusion.

Cytotoxicity, cellular uptake, and RNA-seq analysis

To investigate the effects of the nanoparticles and hydrogel on the proliferation of HCT116 cells, we determined cell viability using the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 5A and B, and Figure S4). The results showed that β-acids, 5-FU, nanoparticles, and hydrogels all inhibited cell proliferation in a concentration-dependent manner. We used nanoparticles containing the same drug content, and the corresponding experimental results indicated that nanoparticles at concentrations greater than 40 µg/mL inhibited HCT116 cells more effectively than free β-acids. In HCT116 cells, the enhanced anti-proliferative effect of the nanoparticles can be attributed to FA-mediated targeting, which increases the intracellular delivery of β-acids into cancer cells [83, 84]. Similar experiments also confirmed that when the nanoparticle concentration within the hydrogel reached 80 µg/mL, the hydrogel exhibited greater inhibitory effect on HCT116 cells than free β-acids. In addition, similar results were obtained for HT29 cells (Figure S5). 5-FU showed a significant inhibitory effect on both HCT116 and HT29 cells (Figure S6). The IC₅₀ values of the nanoparticles/hydrogels for HCT116 and HT29 cells are presented in Table S4.

It has also been shown that FA-conjugated chitosan oligosaccharide-conjugated CS nanoparticles loaded with cytarabine could improve the cytotoxicity to cancer cells (MCF-7), and that the increase may be due to receptor-mediated endocytosis that enhances the internalization of cytarabine and enhances the effect of permeation and retention (EPR) in MCF-7 cells [63]. The endothelial cells of tumor cells have gaps around 50 ~ 500 nm, leading to penetration and accumulation of molecules smaller than 500 nm [85]. FA-modified nanoparticles improved the bioavailability of the loaded drug via active targeting and the EPR effect. Furthermore, the sustained drug release from these nanoparticles enabled prolonged drug exposure, thereby enhancing cytotoxicity. To further investigate the effect of nanoparticles (FA/CS/TPP/22.5β-acids) on cell migration, it was explored using scratch experiments. The experimental results revealed that the proliferation of HCT116 cells could be effectively inhibited in the presence of drug-loaded nanoparticles, with an effect superior to that of 5-FU, as indicated by the CCK-8 results (Fig. 5C). In addition, the study analyzed the effects of different concentrations of nanoparticles (0–40 µg/mL) on the proliferation of normal cells, demonstrating that they are not toxic to normal cells and could be selected as potential therapeutic agents. Even at 40 µg/mL, co-incubation of nanoparticles with NCM460 cells for 24 h did not affect cell proliferation, suggesting that the drug-loaded nanoparticles have a good cellular safety profile (Fig. 5D).

Many studies have shown the importance of nanocarriers in delivering sufficient amounts of anticancer drugs into cancer cells, thereby helping minimize drug side effects. To explore FA receptor targeting of the nanoparticles prepared in this study, we co-cultured the coumarin-6-loaded nanoparticles with HCT116 cells and analyzed them using confocal microscopy. It has been reported that the FA receptor is a well-known tumor marker and is highly expressed on the membrane surface of CRC cells, and that both free FA and FA-functionalized nanomaterials can be recognized and tightly bound to the FA receptor [86–88]. In addition, flow cytometry apoptosis detection discovered that both nanoparticles and hydrogel accelerated HCT116/HT29 cell apoptosis (Fig. 6A, Figure S7, Figure S8A and Figure S9). Interestingly, even if nanoparticles are loaded into hydrogel, the inhibitory effect of the hydrogel on HCT116 cells is similar to that of β-acids. It can be clearly seen in the CLSM image (Figure S10) that, after co-incubation of coumarin-6-loaded nanoparticles with HCT116 cells, strong red DID fluorescence and green coumarin-6 fluorescence were observed in the cells. This suggests that coumarin-6-loaded nanoparticles can be significantly internalized into HCT116 cells via FA receptor-mediated endocytosis. In addition, we co-incubated HCT116 cells with coumarin-6-loaded nanoparticles after pretreatment with free FA to block the FA receptor and further confirmed their recognition ability. As shown in Figure S10, the fluorescence intensity in the cells was significantly reduced, indicating that the FA receptor on the surface of HCT116 cells was coupled with free FA, thereby blocking the cellular uptake of the nanoparticles to a large extent. Similar results were also observed in HT29 cells (Figure S8B). The above results indicate that FA-functionalized nanoparticles facilitate FA receptor-mediated endocytosis and are expected to serve as targeted nanocarriers for FA receptor-positive cancers. In addition, when nanoparticles were loaded into the hydrogel, we also explored the uptake of the the hydrogel by HCT116/HT29 cells. Similar to the cell uptake experiment of nanoparticles, nanoparticles modified with FA can increase cellular uptake (Fig. 6B and Figure S8B).

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the inhibitory effects of β-acids on CRC, RNA-Seq analysis was performed on HCT116 cells samples. A total of 2786 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified (adjusted p-value < 0.05), including 1242 upregulated and 1544 downregulated genes in the β-acids-treated group. Volcano plots showing the distribution of DEGs, with upregulated genes on the right and downregulated genes (Fig. 7A). Analysis of DEGs between groups demonstrated that, compared to the control group, genes associated with the Cell cycle and MAPK signaling pathways, including CDK1, CDK2, MAPK1, and MAPK3, exhibited significant downregulation (Fig. 7B). GO enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs were primarily involved in signal transduction and transcriptional regulation. At the cellular level, they were mainly localized to the plasma membrane and nucleus. At the molecular level, nucleotide binding and ATP binding were the most enriched terms. These findings indicate that the DEGs function mainly as membrane receptors and nuclear transcription factors to regulate downstream signaling and gene expression (Fig. 7C). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment in key cell death and tumor-related signaling pathways, including cell cycle, apoptosis, pathways in cancer, MAPK signaling pathway and NF-Kappa B signaling pathway (Fig. 7D and E).

Hemolysis experiments showed that the positive control group exhibited significant erythrocyte rupture, while the negative control group showed virtually no hemolysis, indicating the reliability of the experimental system (Figure S11). For both the FA/CS/TPP and Alg/FA/CS/TPP systems, the color change of the supernatant after each concentration treatment was minimal, and quantitative results confirmed that the hemolysis rate in all samples remained below the generally accepted safety threshold of 5%. In contrast, the hemolysis rate in all groups after Alg modification was further reduced, remaining mostly below 2%, demonstrating superior blood compatibility. These results suggested that β-acids loading did not significantly increase the blood toxicity of the nano-formulation, while Alg outer-layer modification may reduce the risk of hemolysis by shielding the particle surface charge and weakening direct interactions with the erythrocyte membrane. Overall, both types of nanosystems demonstrated good blood safety, with the Alg/FA/CS/TPP system being more suitable for subsequent in vivo drug-delivery studies.

In vivo biosafety and anticancer therapy of nanoparticle and hydrogel

Seven groups’ mice (21) were administered by gavage to study the potential toxicity of nanoparticles. Body weight and behavioral changes of the mice were monitored after drug administration (14 days). All animals were found to be healthy in appearance, active, and showed no abnormal symptoms (e.g., increased or decreased activity, abnormal breathing, lethargy, etc.). Throughout the study, all animals remained alive with no fatalities observed. There was no significant difference in the body weight of animals between the experimental groups and the control group.

Following gavage, we investigated whether drug carriers/nanomedicines could alter blood levels of biochemical tissue damage or inflammatory markers (Fig. 8A). First, we measured changes in both ALT and AST levels to determine whether the nanoparticles caused hepatotoxicity. Oral administration of different doses of carriers/nanoparticles was within the normal range, even though it increased the variability in ALT and AST levels. In addition, the detection of TP can also reflect the changes in liver function. The results of 14 days of intake showed that the nanocarriers/nanoparticles did not affect TP, indicating that intake of nanoparticles did not alter liver function. Besides, renal function was assessed by measuring CREA levels. We found no significant difference between the control and experimental groups after 14 days of administration; both remained below the toxicity threshold, consistent with the literature [89].

After 14 days of administration of different doses, all organs such as heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, did not show any changes compared to the control group by visual examination (Fig. 8B). No signs of atrophy, hyperplasia, necrosis or inflammation were observed. To further explore changes in the microstructure, histological examination of the above tissues was performed by H&E staining. Compared with the control group, the microscopic parenchymal structures of the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys showed no signs of inflammation or fibrosis. These results indicated that oral administration of nanoparticles does not affect organ function.

When further studying the therapeutic effect of FA/CS/TPP/22.5β-acids nanoparticles on CRC, we selected MC38 cells to construct an in situ CRC model. Starting from day 6 after MC38 cell implantation, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 5-FU and oral FA/CS/TPP/22.5β-acids every other day for 7 consecutive days. Tumor-bearing mice were sacrificed on day 14, and the colorectum was harvested to evaluate the effects of the drug on the tumor (Fig. 8C). The experimental results showed that the colorectal status of healthy mice was normal (PBS group), and tumors were present at the colorectal site in the Control group, indicating that the modeling was successful by MC38. After treatment with FA/CS/TPP/22.5β-acids nanoparticles and 5-FU, the tumor located at the colorectal site disappeared. In the evaluation of mice’s weight, the in situ CRC model showed a trend of rising first and then flattening, whereas the 5-FU group showed a trend of rising first and then falling (Fig. 8A). Compared with the Control group, the tumor volume and number in the other dosing groups were reduced, indicating the outstanding ability of nanoparticles to inhibit tumor growth (Fig. 8C and D). As the in vitro release experiments suggest, the nanoparticles can slowly release β-acids, thereby inhibiting tumor growth. H&E staining was used to evaluate the degree of colon cancer and tumor cell apoptosis (Fig. 8E). The colon muscle layer and mucosa of healthy mice (PBS group) were clearly layered and structured, and no lesion cells were observed in the H&E-stained image at high magnification. Quite the opposite, tumor cells in the Control group severely invaded, destroying the mucosal muscular layer of colon tissue. After treatment with FA/CS/TPP/22.5β-acids, the colon section was as healthy as that of the normal mouse group and showed no tumors. The tumor invasion of the anterior mucosal structure was well preserved, and the treatment effect was significant.

In vivo anti-cancer study, nanoparticles containing FA/CS/TPP/22.5β-acids showed strong anti-cancer effects, so we chose to load them into a hydrogel to treat CRC. Similar to the in vivo safety study of the nanoparticles, oral administration of various doses of the hydrogel was within normal limits after 14 days of ingestion (Fig. 9A). Experimental results showed that intake of hydrogel did not affect changes in ALT, AST, TP, CREA. Therefore, the intake of hydrogel did not alter liver or kidney function. Besides, we performed H&E staining on various mouse organs. Corresponding results indicated that no significant toxicity was observed following administration of different doses (Fig. 9B). Consequently, we employed 400 mg/kg dose in the in situ tumor-bearing mice model. Following 14 days of treatment, mice treated with the hydrogel exhibited no significant change in body weight, whereas control mice demonstrated marked weight loss (Figure S12). Upon experiment conclusion, imaging revealed the hydrogel did not reduce colonic length in mice. Tumors implanted in the colonic region exhibited a marked reduction in size, confirming the superior tumor-growth-inhibiting capacity of the hydrogel (Fig. 9C). H&E staining (Fig. 9D) revealed characteristic tumor features in tissues from the control, 5-FU, and hydrogel treatment groups, including impairment of colonic mucosal structure. Following hydrogel treatment, histological sections demonstrated restricted tumor areas with maintained mucosal architecture at the invasion front, indicating marked therapeutic efficacy.

The immunohistochemical results (Fig. 10) demonstrated that compared to the control group, the 5-FU, FA/CS/TPP/β-acids, and Alg/FA/CS/TPP/β-acids treatment groups all exhibited enhanced expression of BAX and Caspase-3, along with reduced expression of Bcl-2 and NF-κB. These findings indicated that the treatments promote tumor cell apoptosis and inhibit the NF-κB-related inflammatory pathway to exert anti-CRC effects. Notably, the Alg/FA/CS/TPP/β-acids group showed particularly pronounced upregulation of BAX and Caspase-3 expression compared to the other treatment groups.

Conclusion

In the present work, the FA/CS/TPP/β-acids nanoparticles were positively charged with a suitable size, and the release of β-acids was pH-dependent. Cellular uptake experiments demonstrated the nanoparticles’ ability to achieve specific targeting. Cytotoxicity evaluation showed that, compared with 5-FU and free β-acids, the FA/CS/TPP/β-acids nanoparticles significantly enhanced cytotoxicity against HCT116 and HT29 cells, while exhibiting no significant effect on NCM460 cells. In addition, we employed Alg which undergoes sol-gel-sol transition under different pH conditions, to encapsulate the anti-cancer FA/CS/TPP/β-acids nanoparticles. In vivo safety and therapeutic evaluation in CRC models demonstrated that the hydrogel could treat CRC without adversely affecting the mice. These results underscore the potential of this hydrogel for oral delivery of targeted nanoparticles.

Nevertheless, this study still has several limitations: (i) in vitro experiments on mice CRC cell lines were not performed; (ii) long-term in vivo and in vitro biological safety data for the hydrogel are insufficient; (iii) control experiments using non-targeted nanoparticles are lacking; (iv) the investigation was restricted to in situ CRC models. Future work should inoculate human CRC cell lines into immunodeficient animal models to elucidate the role and mechanism of the hydrogel, evaluate its long-term (more than 3 months) biological safety, and establish diverse CRC animal models to explore the therapeutic efficacy of the hydrogel in combination with marketed drugs. The present study using the nanoparticle/hydrogel system represents only part of a preclinical investigation. Survival analysis and long-term toxicity studies are necessary to further accelerate the translational application of the drug-loaded hydrogel. In addition, pharmacokinetic analysis, biodistribution, and comparison with clinically approved colorectal cancer formulations are also essential. Collectively, these findings suggest that the oral hydrogel‑targeted nanoparticle platform has favorable translational significance and clinical potential for drug delivery and disease therapy.
