Risk and protective factors of higher-grade intrapulmonary hemorrhage in patients undergoing percutaneous computed tomography-guided microcoil localization before video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery
Authors: Yunxin Li, Yanyan Xu, Hongxiang Feng, Tongyin Zhang, Shuzhu Qin, Hongliang Sun, Zhenguo Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComputed tomography (CT)-guided microcoil localization before video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), while generally safe, carries non-negligible risks of intrapulmonary hemorrhage. This study identifies independent predictors of higher-grade hemorrhage (≥ Grade 2) caused by CT-guided microcoil localization to optimize patient selection and procedural planning.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 432 consecutive patients [2016–2020] undergoing microcoil localization prior to VATS for solitary pulmonary nodules were analyzed. Hemorrhage severity was graded radiologically (Grade 0–4). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression identified the independent factors associated with higher-grade hemorrhage (≥ Grade 2), with P<0.05 considered significant.ResultsIntrapulmonary hemorrhage occurred in 161 of the 432 CT-guided microcoil localizations (37.27%), and higher-grade hemorrhage occurred in 41 of all procedures (9.49%). The logistic regression revealed that repeat insertion [odds ratio (OR) =9.712, P<0.001] was the only risk factor for higher-grade intrapulmonary hemorrhage, while procedure-related pneumothorax (OR =0.159, P=0.005), nodule size (>5–10 mm, OR =0.216, P=0.002; >10 mm, OR =0.349, P=0.04), pleura-microcoil distance (>20–30 mm, OR =0.365, P=0.02), and lung radiodensity along the needle passage [>−900 to −850 Hounsfield unit (HU), OR =0.426, P=0.03; ≤−900 HU, OR =0.273, P=0.009] were protective factors for higher-grade intrapulmonary hemorrhage.ConclusionsRepeat insertion during microcoil localization significantly increases hemorrhage risk, while pneumothorax, larger nodule size (>5 mm), longer pleura-microcoil distance (>20–30 mm) and lower lung density (≤−850 HU) are protective. These findings enable risk-stratified patient management.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study identified repeat insertion as the independent risk factor for higher-grade intrapulmonary hemorrhage during computed tomography-guided microcoil localization (odds ratio =9.712). Protective factors included procedure-related pneumothorax, nodule size (>5 mm), pleura-microcoil distance (>20–30 mm), and lung radiodensity [≤−850 Hounsfield unit (HU)] along the needle passage.

What is known and what is new?

• Microcoil localization is widely used for video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery but carries risks like hemorrhage and pneumothorax. Previous studies focused on biopsy-related hemorrhage, while factors specific to microcoil localization remained unclear.

• This study is the first to systematically analyze risk and protective factors for higher-grade hemorrhage in microcoil localization, highlighting the critical role of repeat insertion and unexpected protective effects of pneumothorax and lung radiodensity.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• To enhance safety, clinicians should minimize repeat insertions and consider protective factors (e.g., nodule size, needle pathway density) during planning. High-risk patients (e.g., those requiring repeat attempts) may benefit from closer monitoring or expedited surgery. Further research should validate these findings in larger cohorts and explore operator skill impact.

Introduction

Recently, a preoperative tumor localization followed by video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has become the choice in the workup of pulmonary in determined nodules (1). For non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) stage IA tumors, computed tomography (CT)-guided microcoil localization is safe and effective in aiding VATS resection with adequate margins (2). It can provide a distinct mark in collapsed lung tissue for thoracic surgeons during VATS, and reduce the need for thoracotomy or anatomic resection. Compared to hook-wire and lipiodol localization, microcoil localization yields the lowest complication rates because of the structural characteristic and special mechanical property of the microcoil (3).

Considering that the workflow is relative mature with fewer complications and a shortage of medical resources in clinic (3), VATS resection for small pulmonary nodules tends to be pushed backward in surgical schedule, the interval between the microcoil localization procedure and operation is not to be closely controlled. Actually, localization-related pneumothorax and intrapulmonary hemorrhage are not rare, and the incidence summarized in our previous study was around 29.7% and 11.8%, respectively (4). Even severe hemorrhagic shock caused by preoperative microcoil localization for pulmonary nodule has been reported (5). Hence, the safety of microcoil localization needs to be reconsidered in clinic practice. Accurate prediction of intrapulmonary hemorrhage can help clinicians to screen the high-risk population who tends to develop progressive complications and adopt individualized treatment policy (such as, shortening the waiting time for surgery, adopting close monitoring in high-risk population, etc.).

Interestingly, even though relevant predictors of biopsy-related intrapulmonary hemorrhage have been mentioned in previous studies (6-9), the potential factors of microcoil localization related hemorrhage are rarely discussed. Thus, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the frequency and severity of pulmonary hemorrhage after CT-guided microcoil localization, and assess risk and protective factors associated with higher-grade pulmonary hemorrhage. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-739/rc).

Methods

The single-institution retrospective study protocol complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the China-Japan Friendship Hospital (No. 2022-KY-059), and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Study subjects

From October 2016 to June 2020, 432 consecutive patients (159 males, 273 females, median age 58 years, range, 26–82 years) with single pulmonary nodule underwent CT-guided microcoil localization prior to VATS were enrolled in. Exclusion criteria for CT-guided microcoil localization were the following: (I) nodules adjacent to the hilum or apparent vascular structure; (II) lesions located in the bronchi; (III) patients who refused to microcoil localization or VATS resection.

Of these, 79 (79/432; 18.29% ) patients were with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD); 63 (63/432; 14.58%) patients had a history of malignancy (intrapulmonary:extrapulmonary =49:14); 29 (29/432; 6.71%) patients (16 with hypertension, 5 with diabetes mellitus, 4 with hyperlipemia, 3 with coronary heart disease, 6 with coronary stenting, 2 with coronary bypass operation, 1 with cerebral infarction, 1 with cerebral artery stenting) had a history of antiplatelet or anti-coagulative drugs (e.g., aspirin, warfarin, low molecular weight heparin, etc.) and their administration was discontinued several days prior to localization (Appendix 1). None of the patients underwent diagnostic needle biopsy for the target pulmonary nodules prior to microcoil localization. CT-guided microcoil localization

Low-dose chest CT was required for all patients before localization procedures. The interventional radiologists and thoracic surgeons routinely reviewed the images together to decide the feasibility of microcoil nodule localization and VATS. All related clinical information, especially the lung function, was carefully reviewed before procedure. All procedures were performed by two experienced interventional radiologists (with 15 and 8 years of expertise, respectively) to minimize operator-related variability.

Before CT-guided localization procedures, the patients were coached to hold the breath at the relatively fixed inspiratory rather than a deep breath. All patients were scanned with a 16-detector-row scanner (Aquilion 16; Canon Medical Systems) for the planning and localization CT scans. The detailed information regarding the CT scanning is described in supplementary file (Appendix 1).

A planning CT scan was performed to select an optimal puncture point on the patient’s body surface, and then localization system including introducer needle (DCHN-18-10.0, Cook Incorporated, Bloomington, USA) and embolization microcoil (MWCE-18S-6/2, Cook Incorporated) was introduced in a stepwise manner under the guidance of CT images. A modified Powell’s method was adopted here for deploying the microcoil (4), and the detailed information regarding the localization procedure is described in Figure S1. In addition, it should be note that (I) for the patients with poor breath cooperation, interventional radiologists (15 and 8 years, respectively, in CT-guided interventional procedures) would select a new puncture point if adjusting needle angle did nothing; (II) if the radiologists predicted the microcoil was likely to dislodge or detach from the visceral pleura on postprocedural CT images, a repeat localization procedure would soon be done. For the nodules with insertion more than once (described above), the last procedure results were recorded in this study.

The severity of procedural complications was evaluated according to the Society of Interventional Radiology Standards of Practice Committee classification of complications (10). If severe complications occurred, the patients would be sent to the Emergency Room, which was close to the Radiology Department. If not, the patients would be sent back to the ward under observation by residents and waited for surgery.

Data collection and image analysis

Pulmonary hemorrhage was defined as new consolidative or ground-glass opacity on postprocedural images, and its severity was categorized five grades according to a modified hemorrhage grading scheme (9) (Figure 1). Grade 0: no pulmonary hemorrhage; Grade 1: needle tract hemorrhage with width ≤2 cm; Grade 2: hemorrhage with width over 2 cm but sublobar; Grade 3: lobar hemorrhage or greater; Grade 4: hemothorax. The classification reflects our institutional protocol where Grade 1 represents expected post-procedural findings managed with routine observation, while higher grades [2–4] trigger escalating interventions: all Grade 2 patients receive prolonged monitoring with oxygen support as needed, and Grade 3+ cases are initially transferred to the emergency observation unit for close monitoring.

Operation and pathologic analysis

Under the guidance of microcoil markings and thoracoscopy, the pulmonary nodules were intactly resected and whether hemothorax was present was also observed. A frozen section block was routinely created to evaluate the nodule histology and decide whether the extended resection was necessary. Final histologic diagnosis was assessed according to the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of lung tumors (12).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 17.0 Software (SPSS 17.0 for Windows). Descriptive statistics were shown as median (interquartile range, IQR) for continuous variables, and as frequency and percentage for categorical variables.

According to postprocedural images, all patients were divided into two groups: no or lower-grade hemorrhage and higher-grade hemorrhage group. The clinical characteristics and microcoil localization procedure-related variables were analyzed by Mann-Whitney U test, Chi-squared test, and Fisher exact test to evaluate the differences between the two groups. Variables with P<0.1 in univariate analysis were considered for multivariate modeling with Forward Likelihood Ratio method, a threshold chosen to minimize omitting potentially important predictors while maintaining model parsimony. The model fit was assessed by using area under curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and Hosmer-Lemeshow tests. All clinically plausible interactions were tested. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Totally, intrapulmonary hemorrhage occurred in 161 of 432 nodules with preoperative microcoil localization procedures (37.27%), including 120 Grade 1 hemorrhage (27.78%), 40 Grade 2 hemorrhage (9.26%) and 1 Grade 3 hemorrhage (0.23%). No hemothorax was observed in postprocedural images. Review of medical records showed that six patients with Grade 2 hemorrhage were sent back to the ward and arranged oxygen inhalation therapy after localization procedure. The patient with multi-lobar hemorrhage (Grade 3) was first sent to the Emergency Room for close observation and then transferred to operating room as soon as possible.

Three patients have been converted to open thoracotomy, two for severe pleural adhesion, and one for difficulty to perform bronchoplasty after nodule resection with a video-assisted thoracoscope. Median hospitalization duration of routine VATS patients was 7 days (IQR, 6–7 days), but three patients converted to thoracotomy were with prolonged hospitalization (median 21 days, range, 19–23 days). Three patients underwent bronchoplasty, among whom one had malignant tumors (squamous cell carcinoma, pathological T1N0). 317 (317/432; 73.38%) nodules were identified as malignant tumors, while 115 (115/432; 26.6%) nodules were benign lesions. Detailed pathological results were summarized in Table S1.

Multivariate analysis

According to univariate analysis, the potential confounders including gender, nodule size, lung radiodensity along the needle passage, patient position, needle-pleura angle, pleura-microcoil distance, procedure-related pneumothorax, and repeat insertion were introduced into binary logistic regression analysis. Nodule size (≤5/>5–10/>10 mm), lung radiodensity along the needle passage [>−850/>−900 to −850/≤−900 Hounsfield unit (HU)], needle-pleura angle (≤30°/>30°–60°/>60°), and pleura-microcoil distance (≤20/>20–30/>30 mm) were analyzed as categorical variables. Age was also introduced into analysis as the controlling variable.

The final model demonstrated good discrimination (AUC =0.796, 95% CI: 0.76–0.88) and calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow χ2=7.992, P=0.43). Bootstrap validation confirmed model robustness (optimism-corrected AUC =0.755). All clinically plausible interactions (procedure-related pneumothorax*needle-pleura angle, pleura-microcoil distance*lung radiodensity along the needle passage, needle-pleura angle*repeat insertion) have been evaluated and no significant interaction terms (all P>0.05) was found.

The results demonstrated that repeat insertion (OR =9.712, P<0.001), procedure-related pneumothorax (OR =0.159, P=0.005), nodule size (>5–10 mm, OR =0.216, P=0.002; >10 mm, OR =0.349, P=0.04), pleura-microcoil distance (>20–30 mm, OR =0.365, P=0.02), and lung radiodensity along the needle passage (>−900 to −850 HU, OR =0.426, P=0.03; ≤−900 HU, OR =0.273, P=0.009) were independent factors associated with higher-grade hemorrhage (Table 4). Repeat insertion was the only risk factors for higher-grade intrapulmonary hemorrhage, while procedure-related pneumothorax, nodule size (>5 mm), pleura-microcoil distance (>20–30 mm) and lung radiodensity along the needle passage (≤−850 HU) were more likely to protect the patients from occurring higher-grade intrapulmonary hemorrhage.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to identify the potential factors that associated with higher-grade intrapulmonary hemorrhage. The findings from our study showed that repeat insertion was the most highlighted risk factor. The other factors included procedure-related pneumothorax, nodule size (>5 mm), pleura-microcoil distance (>20–30 mm) and lung radiodensity along the needle passage (≤−850 HU) behaved as protective factors.

The original function of microcoil localization is to anchor the pulmonary lesion and provide a clear mark for the surgeons during operation (13,14). In order to implant the microcoil in place, multiple needle reorientations would be required, especially in the patients with poor breath-holding cooperation. If adjusting needle angle did nothing, interventional radiologists would have to select a new puncture point and needle passage. Then pulmonary tissue damage would inevitably occur during the procedure. The same mechanism is also applicable to the nodules with a repeat localization procedure (4).

In this study, 23.61% (102 cases of pneumothorax out of 432 total cases) of patients showed procedure-related pneumothorax on postprocedural images, and our data show that procedure-related pneumothorax may be a protective factor for intrapulmonary hemorrhage, even though they were both the common complications of microcoil localization. We speculate that this correlation might be related to an increased pressure of pleural cavity caused by pneumothorax. Local increased pressure alteration might yield a pressure hemostatic effect to adjacent lung tissue, and then reduce the occurrence of higher-grade hemorrhage. And this hypothesis requires further validation, as intrapulmonary hemorrhage was also found to be independent protective factors of pneumothorax in patients with CT-guided transthoracic fine needle aspiration biopsy (8).

During the process of microcoil implant, small vessels, fibrosis, or inflammatory exudate around the lesion would be involved and then hemorrhage might be caused. The above conditions may increase lung density, therefore the mean value of lung radiodensity along the needle passage was measured in this study. Considering the depth of implanted microcoils were variable,the regions of interest (ROIs) were different. Our results showed that lung radiodensity −850 HU or less was an independent protective factor for higher-grade intrapulmonary hemorrhage, in other word, the lower the radiodensity along the needle passage, the lower the incidence of higher-grade hemorrhage. However, it should be noted that the majority of patients enrolled in this study were non-smokers with post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume at 1 second/forced vital capacity (FEV1.0/FVC) ≥0.70 (311, 71.99%). When referring to or comparing the results of this analysis, it is necessary to take into account the differences in the clinical baseline characteristics among subjects in different studies, particularly when the study is predominantly composed of patients with COPD.

Pleura-microcoil distance (>20–30 mm) was another protective factor for higher-grade hemorrhage. The microcoil implanted at suitable position caused the lowest risk of higher-grade hemorrhage, 20–30 mm is the right distance between pleura and the endpoint of microcoil along the needle insertion pathway. Too deep a distance means the microcoil would pass through and damage more tissue, while too shallow a distance was not conductive to microcoil fixation (4). The patients with a lesion size over 5 mm were less likely to have higher-grade hemorrhage, probably because the larger the lesion, the easier to localize accurately, thus reducing the possibility of higher-grade hemorrhage.

There are several limitations in this study. First, it was a retrospective study that only enrolled patients with solitary nodule underwent preoperative microcoil localization and VATS resection procedures. Actually, simultaneous localization for multiple pulmonary nodules before VATS was not rare. Second, although mPAD measurement on CT has been used as practical method to screen pulmonary arterial hypertension, the cut-off values were inconclusive (ranging from 29.0 to 31.5 mm) (7,9). In this study, the cut-off value of enlarged mPAD was set as 29.5 mm. The potential influence on final results was not analyzed. Third, we acknowledge that operator’s experience or skill plays a vital role during localization procedure, however, it is hard for us to set it as a related variable for analysis, since there are new trainee doctors joining our interventional team all year round (15). Forth, the number of cases with higher-grade hemorrhage was limited, our results need to be confirmed with a larger cohort. Last, although operator variability was minimized through the involvement of only two highly experienced interventional radiologists, we recognize that operator skill remains an important factor in procedural outcomes that could not be fully quantified in this retrospective design. Future prospective studies should incorporate detailed operator metrics including procedure-specific volumes and years of experience.

Conclusions

In conclusion, repeat insertion was shown to be an independent risk factor for higher-grade hemorrhage in patients with CT-guided microcoil localization. Procedure-related pneumothorax, nodule size (>5 mm), pleura-microcoil distance (>20–30 mm), and lung radiodensity along the needle passage (−850 HU or less) were protective factors associated with higher-grade hemorrhage.
