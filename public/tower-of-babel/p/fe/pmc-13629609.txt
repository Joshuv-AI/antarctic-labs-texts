Generation of two induced pluripotent stem cell lines from dilated cardiomyopathy patients with TTN mutations
Authors: Jiawei Li, Anurendra Kumar, Yin-Tzu Chen, Yang Zhou, Victoria Parikh, Joseph C. Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Titin (TTN) encodes the largest protein in the human body and is essential for sarcomere assembly and muscle mechanosensation. Truncating TTN mutations are a leading cause of dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM). Here, we generated two induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) lines from female DCM patients, each carrying a heterozygous nonsense point mutation that produces a truncated titin protein. Both lines were reprogrammed from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and characterized for expression of undifferentiated human iPSC state markers, tri-lineage differentiation capacity, and genomic integrity by copy-number analysis. These lines provide a patient-derived platform for investigating the mechanobiological basis of titin-truncation DCM in vitro.

Resource utility

Truncating TTN variants are among the most common genetic drivers of dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), yet patient-specific models remain scarce. We report two iPSC lines harboring distinct nonsense mutations (p.Trp25719* and p.Trp28273*), offering a platform to interrogate how titin truncation disrupts cardiomyocyte structure and function and to screen candidate interventions.

Resource details

Titin, encoded by the TTN gene, is the largest polypeptide in the human body. It sets passive stiffness and serves as a molecular template for sarcomere assembly. Truncating variants in TTN are the most frequently identified genetic cause of DCM (Huang et al., 2023), in which the left ventricle dilates and loses systolic performance. DCM is estimated to affect approximately 1 in 250 people and is the major contributor to heart failure (Heymans et al., 2023). For these truncating alleles, disease arises from a combination of reduced full-length titin and toxicity of the truncated titin protein, which may integrate into the sarcomere or accumulate as intracellular aggregates (Fomin et al., 2021; McAfee et al., 2021). Because disease severity varies widely among carriers of the same gene, human cardiomyocyte models that retain each patient's endogenous allele and genetic background are useful for elucidating genotype-to-phenotype relationships and for preclinical evaluation of candidate therapeutics.

In this study, we derived two iPSC lines, SCVIi170-A and SCVIi171-A, from the PBMCs of two unrelated patients with clinically diagnosed DCM. SCVIi170-A carries TTN c.77157G > A (p.Trp25719*) and SCVIi171-A carries TTN c.84819G > A (p.Trp28273*), numbered against the inferred complete TTN transcript NM_001267550.2 (Table 1). Reprogramming used non-integrating Sendai-virus vectors, and clonal lines were established for characterization. The lines grew as flat, sharply bordered colonies typical of human iPSCs (Fig. 1A; scale bars, 500 μm). RT-qPCR confirmed NANOG and SOX2 expression in both lines, in contrast to the iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs) as a negative control and SCVIi273-A as a positive reference (Fig. 1B). Immunostaining showed colocalization of undifferentiated iPSC state markers SOX2, NANOG, and OCT3/4 throughout both cultures (Fig. 1C; scale bars, 100 μm). On directed differentiation, each line yielded all three germ layers, identified by OTX2 and PAX6 (ectoderm), SOX17 and FOXA2 (endoderm), and BRACHYURY and TBX6 (mesoderm) (Fig. 1D; scale bars, 100 μm). Sanger sequencing resolved each line's substitution against a wild-type reference, confirming c.77157G > A in SCVIi170-A and c.84819G > A in SCVIi171-A (Fig. 1E). Copy-number profiles from low-pass whole-genome sequencing were balanced across every chromosome, with no segmental gains or losses (Fig. 1F). The Sendai genome, quantifiable at passage 13, fell below detection by passage 28 in SCVIi170-A and passage 30 in SCVIi171-A (Fig. 1G). Both lines were negative for mycoplasma (Fig. 1H), and STR profiles were concordant between each line and its parental PBMCs (Supplementary Data). Together, these genotype-defined lines expand the resources available for studying TTN-driven DCM in patient-specific cardiomyocytes.

Materials and methods

Reprogramming

iPSCs were established as previously described (Liu et al., 2025). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were recovered from whole blood by Percoll density gradient centrifugation (Cat #17089109, GE Healthcare) and rinsed three times in PBS (Cat #14190144, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The recovered cells were expanded in StemPro-34 SFM (Cat #10639011, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 100 ng/mL stem cell factor (Cat #300–07, Peprotech), 100 ng/mL FLT3 ligand (Cat #PHC9414, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 20 ng/mL IL-3 (Cat #200–03, Peprotech), 20 ng/mL IL-6 (Cat #PHC0063, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 20 ng/mL erythropoietin (Cat #PHC9631, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reprogramming was carried out with the CytoTune-iPSC 2.0 Sendai Reprogramming Kit (Cat #A16517, Thermo Fisher Scientific) per the manufacturer's protocol. Transduced cells were transferred onto plates pre-coated with Matrigel (0.2% v/v in DMEM/F12; Cat #356231, Corning) and maintained in StemPro-34 medium with daily medium renewal. On day 7 post-transduction, the medium was transitioned to StemMACS iPS-Brew XF (Cat #130–104–368, Miltenyi Biotec) plus iPS-Brew supplement XF (Cat #130–107–087, Miltenyi Biotec) to promote colony emergence. Colonies displaying characteristic iPSC morphology arose between days 10 and 15, after which they were manually isolated, propagated, and cryopreserved.

Cell culture

iPSCs were cultured in StemMACS iPSC-Brew XF and iPSC-Brew supplement according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were maintained at 37 °C under 5% CO2 and 20% O2. At ~ 80% confluency, they were passaged onto Matrigel-coated plates at a 1:6 ratio using 0.5 mM EDTA (Cat #15575–038, Invitrogen) for 4 min. 10 μM Y-27632 (Cat #S1049, Selleck) was supplemented for 24 h after passaging.

Trilineage differentiation

iPSC pluripotency was validated through differentiation into ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm at passage 20. For ectoderm differentiation, cells were treated with the Human Pluripotent Stem Cell Functional Identification Kit (Cat #SC027B, R&D Systems). For mesoderm, cells were differentiated with RPMI media (Cat #11875–085, Gibco) supplemented with 6 μM CHIR-99021 (Cat #S2924, Selleck) and B27 minus insulin (Cat #A18956–01, Gibco). For endoderm, cells were induced with STEMdiff endoderm basal medium (Cat #05233, Stemcell Technologies) supplemented with 100 ng/mL Activin A (Cat #A2422, Selleck) and 3 μM CHIR-99021 on day 1, after which 100 ng/mL Activin A alone was given on days 2 and 3.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence was adapted from a previously described method (Liu et al., 2023). Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and then permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Cat #X100, Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min at room temperature (RT). DPBS (Cat #14190144, Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 2% BSA (Cat #A9418, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to block non-specific binding for 1 h at RT. Samples were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Following three DPBS rinses, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:2000; Cat #D1306, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min. (Table 2).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using the Direct-zol RNA MicroPrep kit (Cat #R2062, Zymo Research) and reverse transcribed with HiScript IV All-in-One Ultra RT SuperMix (Cat #R433–01, Vazyme). RNA quality was verified with a NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer by the A260/A280 ratio. Undifferentiated hPSC state markers and the Sendai virus genome were detected by TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Table 2) on a Bio-Rad CFX96. For Sendai virus genome detection, RNA was prepared from passages 13 and 28 of SCVIi170-A and passages 13 and 30 of SCVIi171-A. Passage 20 iPSCs were used to detect undifferentiated iPSC state markers.

Sanger sequencing

iPSCs at passage 20 were used for Sanger sequencing. Genomic regions of interest were amplified with KOD One PCR Master Mix (Cat #KMM-101, Toyobo) using the primer pairs listed in Table 2. PCR products were size-checked by agarose gel electrophoresis, purified with the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Cat #28104, Qiagen), and Sanger sequenced by Genewiz.

Copy number analysis by low-pass whole-genome sequencing (WGS)

2 × 106 cells at passage 20 were sent to Novogene for low-pass WGS. Sequence reads were mapped to the GRCh38 human assembly, and copy number was inferred from read counts tallied in 1 Mb bins genome-wide. As a depth-based approach, the assay detects only copy-number aberrations and is blind to copy-number-neutral events, including balanced translocations, inversions, and copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity. In addition, low-level mosaic abnormalities present in only a minority of cells may not produce a detectable shift in bin coverage. Balanced whole-genome ploidy changes can be masked by genome-wide normalization.

Short tandem repeat (STR) analysis

Genomic DNA from both lines at passage 20 was isolated with the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Cat #69504, Qiagen) and quantified with Qubit dsDNA Quantification Assay Kits (Cat #Q32851, Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA was amplified with the AmpFLSTR Identifiler Plus PCR Amplification Kit (Cat #4427368, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and submitted to Genewiz for fragment analysis on an ABI 3730 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems).

Mycoplasma assay

Mycoplasma contamination of iPSCs at passage 20 was assessed with the MycoAlert Detection Kit (Cat #LT07–318, Lonza) according to the manufacturer's instructions.
