Pediatric Neuropalliative Medicine Clinic: Five‐Year Data Characterizing a Novel Model of Outpatient Care
Authors: Lauren Treat, Lori Silveira, Kevin C Ess
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivePediatric neuropalliative medicine (PNPM) is a recently developed area of subspecialty neurology practice focused on supporting the complex emotional, psychological, and physical aspects of caring for a child with serious neurological disease. This study aims to characterize the establishment and inaugural 5 years of an academic, consultative pediatric neuropalliative medicine (PNPM) clinic at a large, quaternary care children's hospital in the US.MethodsDescriptive statistics were used to characterize the population seen in this clinic and to analyze outcomes.ResultsOver 5 years, 250 patients were seen, totaling 682 clinic visits. Primary diagnoses of patients included epileptic encephalopathy (30%), acquired brain injury (24%), and neuromuscular diseases (16%); 64% of patients had a confirmed genetic condition. The most common referral sources included child neurology (33%) and rehabilitation medicine (20%). Referral to this clinic was the first contact with palliative medicine for 82% of patients. In PNPM clinic, all families participated in facilitated narrative debriefing of their prior experiences related to serious illness. Future decision‐making considerations were highlighted, including initiation of life‐sustaining medical technology and emergency management including code status. Of the 51 patients (20%) who died within the study period, 90% had code status discussions in the clinic and 89% died in a supportive setting, including home hospice or inpatient care.SummaryPNPM clinic promotes tailored and proactive decision‐making support for families affected by serious neurological illnesses. All patients had health fragility and some of them died in childhood, though the majority of the cohort remained alive beyond the study period.Keywords: caregiver support, complex chronic conditions, medical decision‐making, palliative care, serious neurological impairment

Introduction

Serious neurological disease in childhood includes acquired, genetic, and/or congenital diseases that confer health complexity in multiple organ systems and generally result in high utilization of health care [1, 2]. Patients and families affected by neurological disease face logistical, emotional, and medical challenges, including whether to accept life‐sustaining medical technology, including feeding tubes, respiratory equipment, and acute care interventions [3, 4, 5]. In pediatrics, the role of parents of children with special healthcare needs is particularly complex, requiring both caregiving responsibilities as well as the psychological weight of making medical decisions on behalf of the child, sometimes under emergent, stressful circumstances [6, 7, 8]. Child neurologists see the impacts of these chronic and acute changes in the child and the family, whether in the inpatient setting or in the clinic over time.

Palliative medicine as a specialty has grown in response to the advancing technological capabilities of modern medicine to promote person‐centered care. By skillfully eliciting patient and family values, palliative medicine specialists help guide medical care in accordance with personal definitions of what brings meaning, joy, and connection to daily existence—helping an individual live well, not just longer. Because neurological disease often results in impairments to core aspects of identity such as the ability to speak, think, and move, the subspecialty of neuropalliative medicine has gained considerable traction as a core component of high‐quality health care [9, 10]. While there are many shared principles with adult care, pediatric neuropalliative medicine (PNPM) is a unique area of subspecialty practice that endeavors to support the quality of life (QOL) of children and families affected by neurological disorders that result in health vulnerability, as well as to allow caregivers the proactive opportunity to consider the priorities that guide their proxy decision‐making.

In the outpatient setting, PNPM is focused on debriefing prior health‐event experiences to support coping and personhood, alongside proactively discussing current or future medical needs. While all clinicians strive to promote the dignity of patients and engage in high‐quality communication, there are specific challenges related to neurological conditions, including navigating uncertainty around prognosis and responding to the complex emotional journey of parenting a child with significant motor and/or cognitive limitations. These conditions require not only robust medical treatments to mitigate threats to physical health but also foundational humanistic connection to meaningfully accompany families during acute and chronic episodes of care. Especially as technology and treatments are helping patients with neurological disease to live longer, management of advanced disease states requires greater consideration [11]. PNPM expertise is well suited to help provide meaningful support in these areas, helping families clarify their own priorities and facilitating bidirectional communication between families and their primary treatment teams. PNPM providers have training in both palliative medicine and child neurology to help address the nuances in prognostic communication as well as management of children with serious neurological diseases [12].

Here, we describe the inaugural 5 years (2020–2024) of a physician‐led, consultative PNPM clinic within a large academic, quaternary care children's hospital. This is one of the first clinics of its kind in the United States. This study aims to characterize this new clinical model, including the types of patients seen, visit data, and the outcomes for patients seen, with the hope that this information can guide additional outpatient PNPM program development and enhance the growth of this field.

Methods

Clinic Description

A physician‐led outpatient PNPM clinic was launched in January 2020 at Children's Hospital Colorado, located in the Western United States. The child neurology practice at this institution treats approximately 18 000 new patients annually between two campuses and several satellite locations, with a multi‐state catchment area including Colorado, Wyoming, Montana, South Dakota, Nebraska, Kansas, and New Mexico. Palliative medicine services at this institution have been in place formally since 2015 but have varied in staffing, with advanced practice providers being the primary clinicians alongside part‐time physician effort until 2022, when a full‐time physician presence was established. In its inaugural year (2020), the PNPM clinic began with referrals primarily from within the section of child neurology, with two primary referral criteria (Figure 1). The first criterion specified eligible diagnostic categories: presumed or confirmed neurogenetic diagnosis or acquired brain injury. The second referral criterion required that a serious medical decision was anticipated at any future time, including initiation of a life‐sustaining medical technology, consideration of a clinical trial, and/or desire for limitations on medical interventions. Since its inception, PNPM clinic has been staffed by one dual‐boarded child neurologist (LT) with hospice and palliative medicine fellowship training who also practices general neurology in a separate clinic. Given the single‐physician model, the clinic did not occur when the physician was unavailable due to other clinical obligations or time away from work. One nurse with advanced training in palliative medicine provided care coordination for the clinic, including patient outreach and follow‐up, referral processing, and bidirectional communication with community agencies. For approximately 18 months (2022–2023), a social worker with advanced certification in palliative medicine supported PNPM clinic, attending all visits. Other interdisciplinary support was available through the primary treatment team, including chaplains, child life specialists, medical assistants, and care managers. Clinic visits were initially all conducted in person and transitioned to mostly telehealth in March 2020 due to the COVID‐19 pandemic; both visit types have been offered after that date.

Clinic Registry

All patients seen in PNPM clinic between January 2020 and December 2024 (inclusive) were prospectively placed in the clinic registry via a REDCap electronic database. Data from each clinic visit were collected at the time of the visit, including the demographics of the patient, the types of medical technology used by the patient, details about their underlying illness, and whether prescriptions, referrals, or advance care planning documents were completed at the visit. Retrospective chart review was needed in some cases to provide missing data. Death information was collected via the electronic health record at the time of manuscript preparation, including date of death, location of death, any documented code status discussions or limitations, enrollment in community palliative or hospice care, and number of days of hospitalization in the last 30 days of life.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all discrete variables using frequencies for categorical variables and medians for continuous variables. Statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio Posit Software (Boston, Massachusetts).

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

This study was determined to be exempt from review by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board.

Results

The Patient Population

Over the 5‐year period, 250 patients were seen, comprising a total of 682 visits. Demographic details of patients are detailed in Table 1. The age distribution of patients seen was roughly equal across groups, with 25.6% of patients under age 3 years at the time of their first visit (24 infants, 40 toddlers ages 1–3 years, 9.6% and 16% of cohort, respectively). Fifty‐six patients (22%) were 18 years or older at the time of their first visit. Patients had an average of 1.8 follow‐up visits following their initial visit, with 95 patients (38%) completing only a single new patient visit, 131 patients (52%) having two to four visits, and 24 patients (10%) having more than four follow‐ups. Most patients were white (155, 62%), with 25% of patients identifying as Hispanic or Latino. Public insurance was the most common payor type (60% Medicaid), with 34% of families having private insurance. Parents were employed as paid caregivers for 130 (58%) of the patients seen, which is a sought‐after benefit offered by the state of Colorado, in which the hospital is located.

Characteristics of patients seen in PNPM clinic.

Patients seen in the clinic were broadly categorized as either having a congenital/genetic diagnosis or an acquired condition (Figure 1). Genetic diagnoses were confirmed in 161 patients (64%), while additional patients were presumed to have genetic etiologies and/or congenital conditions. Sixty patients (24%) had an acquired brain injury, including neonatal hypoxic‐ischemic encephalopathy (HIE). Seventy‐four patients (30%) had an epileptic encephalopathy. Thirty‐nine patients (16%) had a neuromuscular disease. At the time of the first visit to the PNPM clinic, 186 patients (74%) of patients used some form of life‐sustaining medical technology, most commonly a feeding tube or respiratory equipment. Sixteen of the remaining 64 patients began using one or more of these technologies at a subsequent time. Acute care presentations, hospitalizations, and other healthcare utilization patterns could not be accurately recorded given the range of locations of care facilities outside of the Children's Hospital Colorado network.

Clinic Metrics

Referrals to the clinic were initially from the neurology clinic but over time expanded to a variety of sources. In total over the 5 years, neurologists referred 84 patients (33%). The second most common referral source was rehabilitation medicine, which included neuromuscular/Muscular Dystrophy Association clinics (46 patients, 18%). Complex care pediatrics referred 26 patients (10%), and genetics/metabolics clinicians referred 16 patients (6%). Forty‐six patients (18%) had been seen by the palliative team on the inpatient service prior to being referred to PNPM clinic. Over the 5‐year period, 88% of visits occurred via telehealth, with in‐person visits most commonly occurring when the family's primary language was not English, when the family resided in a state for which the clinician was not licensed, or due to family preference.

Clinical Outcomes

All families seen in the clinic were offered facilitated review of their experiences related to the care and quality of life of the individual with serious neurological disease. This narrative review invites the sharing of both declarative memories about healthcare experiences and the child's life, as well as autobiographical details that are more implicit and may drive opinions and/or behavior. Follow‐up visits allow for additional debriefing of recent experiences as well as discussion of new insights from the parent about the child, medical care, or the role of parents as proxy decision‐makers. The goal outcomes for visits to PNPM clinic include reduced caregiver isolation and distress, improved meaning‐making for the family, and expanded self‐awareness and agency in future medical decision‐making encounters; all of these entities are difficult to measure but are the focus of future research. Prescriptions were generated for 21 patients (8.4%), most commonly for non‐opioid, non‐benzodiazepine medications to address discomfort and for sleep. Internal referrals to additional medical subspecialists were generated for 18 patients (7.2%), most commonly for rehabilitation medicine. Referrals to community hospice and/or palliative agencies occurred for 43 patients (17%). Code status discussion occurred in clinic for 88% of patients, which was often the first time that families were invited to dialogue about this topic with a healthcare provider.

Of the cohort, 51 patients died over the 5‐year period (20%) (Table 2). The mean age at death was 13.7 years; however, the distribution of age was skewed, with 16 patients being over age 18 and seven patients being younger than 3 years at the time of death. For six (12%) of these patients, no details are known about the circumstances surrounding their death. Of those who died, 10 (20%) had purely progressive neurogenetic conditions such as Krabbe disease and POLG1‐related disorders; 8 (16%) had a dystrophinopathy. Sixteen (31%) of the patients who died had static underlying conditions such as brain malformations, injury from prematurity, hypoxic‐ischemic encephalopathy, or other acquired brain injuries. Sixteen patients (31%) died in a hospital setting or emergency department; 12 of these deaths were after a transition to comfort‐focused care during the admission, and four died after unsuccessful cardiopulmonary resuscitation. One patient died at an inpatient hospice facility. Of the 30 patients who were known to have died at home, 26 were supported by hospice. Three patients had no available hospice agencies in their area and thus could not have home‐based hospice support. Of the 51 patients who died, 46 (90%) of their parents had had at least one code status discussion in PNPM clinic prior to their death.

Discussion

Trends in Practice

Referrals to the PNPM clinic have been robust since the clinic's initiation. Colleagues referred patients of all ages to the PNPM clinic, with patients at various stages of their illness trajectory—some were seen shortly after a new diagnosis, others after a period of refractory symptoms, and still others who were likely to be in their final phase of life. While only a few small groups had a systematic referral structure to PNPM clinic (such as all men with Duchenne muscular dystrophy over age 18 years), some referral patterns emerged, including a high percentage of patients with progressive conditions and a high percentage of family‐caregivers, which indicates a significant level of daily medical needs. These factors speak to the complex logistical and psychological implications of serious neurological disease.

Most patients seen in this clinic were in an advanced phase of their illness and/or living with serious neurological impairment that rendered them non‐ambulatory and often nonverbal. Prescriptions written by the PNPM clinician in this cohort were not abundant but when written, most often addressed problems that have root in the neurological system, such as insomnia, neuropathic pain, or visceral hyperalgesia. Rare prescriptions were generated for opioids or benzodiazepines, which were often either temporary or as a bridge to an individual being supported by hospice. Overall, prescriptions to address symptoms and referrals to other subspecialty clinics occurred at a low frequency compared with the rate of discussion‐based therapeutic intervention.

Subspecialty Expertise

The goal of PNPM clinic is to promote self‐reflection for families (and for patients themselves when possible) during periods of stability in order to build awareness about how future episodes may unfold and the decision‐making tasks that may arise in these contexts. This insight is difficult to quantify. While many patients seen in PNPM clinic also were seen in specialties like pulmonology, gastroenterology, and rehabilitation medicine, conversation in PNPM clinic aimed to promote a cohesive view of the child's condition in relation to their neurological condition—tying together the problems in other organ systems that relate to brain dysfunction such as dysmotility and respiratory insufficiency. This understanding may affect subsequent qconversations with family members or healthcare professionals. Depending on the rate of change of the child's health, impacts from PNPM clinic may be apparent quickly or may appear months to years later. Conversations in PNPM clinic may alter patterns of family decision‐making, or their decision‐making may stay the same; in either scenario, the success of PNPM clinic is not in guiding families toward comfort‐focused treatment but instead is aimed at augmenting understanding about medical fragility and disease prognosis, as well as the benefits and burdens of medical interventions. The ideal outcome from PNPM clinic is that the family feels more equipped to make goal‐concordant healthcare decisions, ideally helping to mitigate regret or guilt over decisions or outcomes. Effects may also be seen on other clinicians who care for the patient and family, including those who care for the patient in emergent or inpatient settings and those who are part of the child's outpatient treatment team. Indeed, the most commonly cited reasons for referral to pediatric palliative medicine from a recent survey of clinicians included improved support for family decision‐making and helping families feel informed about their future care decisions [13]. All of these aspects are important to query as the clinic continues but are less amenable to database metrics and retrospective review of the medical record.

Some families seen in PNPM clinic desired to shift care away from the default of full interventions and toward comfort. Comfort‐focused care is often mistakenly defined by the absence of interventions, focusing on what will not be done. Instead, a more helpful framework is to outline the ways that the child's autonomy and personhood will be prioritized, which can be complicated in advanced illness. Especially when an individual is at the end of life, ready access to a care team (so‐called attended death) either at home or in a hospital can be considered a quality metric for care [14]. While privacy is a value that many families hold during stressful periods, the availability of a medical professional and/or care assistant is often valued to help manage symptoms and help monitor the dying process. Families retain the right to decline these supports at end of life, but many find them valuable. Unattended deaths are more likely to be the ones that occur at home and are often unanticipated, though their occurrence was undoubtedly related to their underlying health fragility. In this cohort, the majority of patients who died were attended by either hospice staff or inpatient personnel, helping to ensure appropriate symptom management and guidance about the active dying process. While the rate of referral to hospice may be low compared with adult neuropalliative clinics, it is important to remember that patients referred to this clinic had many different types of conditions and that most conditions were not purely progressive, so most were not eligible for hospice services at the time of their clinic visit.

Clinic Development and Infrastructure

Initiation and maintenance of PNPM clinic has required partnership between leadership in both pediatric palliative medicine and child neurology (both housed within the Department of Pediatrics), as well as with the hospital itself. In Colorado, the School of Medicine and the children's hospital are independent entities but work closely together and are aligned in their goals for high‐quality child health care. Philanthropic support has not been attained for this clinic but ideally would be a component of a sustainable financial plan. While palliative medicine involvement can help reduce inpatient length of stay in some cases, patients in this cohort are already high utilizers of healthcare chronically, and cost‐effectiveness is challenging to demonstrate consistently. Other metrics that demonstrate the value of this work include patient/family impacts, as well as the potential to reduce burnout and improve the work of colleagues. Indeed, respondents to a recent survey indicated that having a PNPM partner allows neurologists to focus more on their area of expertise [13].

The model of care for most patients in PNPM clinic was one of co‐management with another neurologist, which was financially feasible due to the PNPM clinician being able to bill under hospice & palliative medicine subspecialty rather than neurology. The PNPM clinician took on primary neurology management only in cases where families wished to transition to a comfort focus and consolidate appointments, or the PNPM clinician first met the family in general neurology clinic. Transition of care for adolescents and young adults continues to be a major focus for the care system; however, given the complex, multi‐specialty involvement of most patients seen in PNPM clinic, many young adult patients have been allowed to continue care in the pediatric setting. For those who transitioned to adult care, referrals were made to the adult neuropalliative clinic at the neighboring facility.

The visit templates for high‐quality PNPM delivery require extended visit durations to establish the rapport that is needed for sensitive discussions about parenting a chronically ill child, and about mortality and risk tolerance. This visit structure became more financially sustainable with the advent of time‐based billing and codes that help capture the complexity of longitudinal care for patients with serious medical conditions. Productivity metrics for PNPM clinic are primarily based on clinic utilization and wait list length; billing volume and relative value unit targets are currently not an emphasis thanks to the support of the individual sections and institutional commitment to sustaining palliative medicine. The clinic was initially funded through the palliative medicine section with clinic resources (e.g., clinic space, medical assistant and registered nurse support) provided by neurology. Subsequently, the clinic became primarily supported by the neurology section, with scheduling infrastructure and RN care coordination provided via the palliative medicine section due to the uniqueness of tasks such as placing hospice referrals and supporting patients who are approaching death. This transition to primary neurology support was intentional as a way to signify that subspecialty PNPM practice is essential for a modern child neurology practice. Particularly for subspecialty neurology clinics where burdensome, resource‐intensive, and/or potentially high‐risk interventions are being discussed such as epilepsy surgery or gene therapy, integrating systematic integration of PNPM support as standard of care will help support high‐quality, patient‐centered care and shift paradigms to include PNPM in center of excellence designations. Replicating the success of this PNPM clinic at other institutions will require proactive discussions with institutional leadership to highlight the value of PNPM. Thoughtful, site‐specific planning is needed to promote sustainable and meaningful support for this highly vulnerable patient population.

Limitations and Future Directions

Limitations of this study include referral biases to a single center, though the study cohort is likely broadly representative of children with neurological disorders in the US given the large catchment area of the practice. Other limitations are the quantitative approach to characterizing a nuanced area of practice, as well as the availability of data in the electronic medical record. Data regarding patient outcomes including death are likely underestimates given that some patients moved away or received care outside of the hospital system. Data collection was based on the perspective of the treating clinician at the time of the visit; communication between visits such as requests for other prescriptions or referrals was not captured. Similarly, if a patient was supported by hospice during an interval but then disenrolled due to stabilization in health or another reason, this data point could have been missed if the referral came from a different provider and the patient was not on hospice at the time of their death.

Future directions for this work require the integration of many important perspectives. The perspectives of patients and parents who were seen in this clinic are essential to gather—not just about their level of satisfaction with care, but also about the impact on subsequent care episodes. The perspective of clinicians who refer patients to this clinic and care for PNPM clinic patients in other clinic or hospital encounters is also important and has recently been assessed, with particular emphasis on the value of the neurology expertise in the palliative clinician [13]. The degree to which patients/families feel that child neurology expertise is uniquely valuable in the palliative clinician must be characterized, as the workforce is small at present. Incorporating systems‐level data such as emergency presentations, hospitalization use, and home health services would allow more characterization of the impact of palliative care services on healthcare utilization and provide additional clarity on the financial tradeoffs seen with investment in PNPM. Additional work is needed to understand the scalability of this resource to serve a larger volume of patients given the limited workforce of clinicians with PNPM expertise.

In summary, PNPM is a recently developed area of subspecialty practice that aims to augment standard child neurology care. A core role of PNPM is to help counterbalance the quantitative data that is so often the focus of neurology clinic (e.g., seizure counts, medication doses) with qualitative knowledge about who the child is in the context of their family unit, how they thrive, and what causes their suffering. The first 5 years of experience in PNPM clinic suggest that there is robust demand from both referrers within and outside of neurology, and families engage in this care when referred. This cohort of patients had a relatively high mortality rate (20%), which speaks to the fragile health of children with neurological disease. At the same time, the majority of patients seen in PNPM clinic were alive at the time of data collection, which highlights the importance of living well and providing robust care to patients and families facing serious neurological illness.
