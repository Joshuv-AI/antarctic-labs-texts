Basic Hip Arthroscopy Part 3: Peripheral-Compartment Arthroscopy (T-Capsulotomy, Femoroplasty, and Capsular Closure)
Authors: Jorge Chahla, Juan Bernardo Villarreal-Espinosa, Salvador Gonzalez Ayala, Joshua Wright-Chisem, Ron Gilat, Shane J Nho
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver the past decade, hip preservation strategies have gained momentum, resulting in a notable increase in the use of hip arthroscopy for diagnostic and therapeutic interventions for hip-related pathology. In this 3-part series, we aim to comprehensively review the fundamentals of hip arthroscopy in the setting of femoroacetabular impingement. This Technical Note will thoroughly review the senior authors’ approach to managing the peripheral compartment of the hip in the context of femoroacetabular impingement.

Over the past decade, the incidence of sports-related hip injuries has risen, likely attributed to increasing sports participation at younger ages.1,2 Concurrently, the use of hip arthroscopy has surged as a minimally invasive method to address these injuries.3,4 Although hip arthroscopy has proved to achieve successful outcomes for various hip-related pathologies, particularly femoroacetabular impingement (FAI), it is associated with a steep learning curve.5,6

In response to the recent increase in hip arthroscopy and the associated steep learning curve, we aim to provide a comprehensive series of technical notes on the fundamentals of hip arthroscopy. This Technical Note will thoroughly review the senior authors’ (J.C., S.J.N.) approach to managing the peripheral compartment during hip arthroscopy in the context of FAI.

Anatomic and Surgical Overview

In reference to hip arthroscopy, the hip can be divided into a capsular layer and 2 associated compartments: central and peripheral (Fig 1). Acknowledging the precise location and trajectory of the iliofemoral ligament is of extreme importance. This ligament is incised to create an interportal capsulotomy and T-capsulotomy, allowing access to both the central and peripheral hip compartments (Fig 2).7

Understanding the comprehensive 3-dimensional anatomy and dynamic characteristics of the femoral neck is crucial for adequate planning of subsequent resection during FAI surgery (Fig 3). Therefore, it is our suggestion to perform a dynamic assessment by visualizing the femoral neck bony overgrowth (cam deformity) on 6 different intraoperative fluoroscopic views (extension and neutral rotation, extension and 30° of internal rotation, extension and 30° of external rotation, 30° of flexion and neutral rotation, and 30° of flexion plus 40° and 60° of external rotation).

Surgical site and fluoroscopic images of 6 views attained for enhanced femoral bony overgrowth characterization (cam lesion) during right hip arthroscopy. For the 6 views, the hip is positioned in 30° of flexion and neutral rotation, 30° of flexion and 40° of external rotation (ER), 30° of flexion and 60° of ER, extension and neutral rotation, extension and 30° external rotation, and extension and 30° of internal rotation (IR).

In addition, capsular closure should be routinely sought given the enhanced functional outcomes associated with this procedure.8 In addition to improved functional outcomes, it has been shown to decrease hip arthroscopy revision rates while decreasing total hip arthroplasty conversion rates.8 For this reason, it is the senior authors´ (J.C., S.J.N.) recommendation to consistently close the capsule.

Surgical Techniques

T-Capsulotomy

With the camera located in the anterolateral (AL) portal and while working through the modified mid-anterior portal (mMAP), radiofrequency ablation and subsequent shaver debridement of the overlying fat and synovium are performed for enhanced arthroscopic visualization of the intact portion of the capsule located on top of the femoral neck. This is continued after switching the camera to the mMAP and working through the AL portal to improve access for fat and synovial debridement and dissection of the gluteus minimus and iliocapsularis off the capsular envelope. Dissection of the referred structures is performed by peeling them off the capsule with a radiofrequency probe while they course over the acetabular rim. Identification of the anatomic safe zone between the 12- and 6-o’clock positions (anterior 12-o’clock to 6-o’clock portion in both right and left hips) is performed by placing the hip into extension and internal rotation, thus allowing visualization of the medial femoral circumflex artery and the retinacular vessels on the posterosuperior surface of the femoral neck (Fig 4). Once the neurovascular safe zone has been established, away from the posterosuperior femoral neck, the radiofrequency probe is used to perform the T-portion of the capsulotomy on the anterior portion of the capsule covering the femoral neck. The central point of the prior interportal capsulotomy should be targeted as the starting point, and the capsulotomy should extend down to the zona orbicularis, which represents the narrowest portion of the hip’s capsule and is identified as a ring of circumferential fibers at the base of the greater trochanter (Fig 5). Two No. 2 traction sutures are then placed through the medial and lateral leaflets of the capsulotomy via a suture passer (SpeedStitch; Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) for allowance of improved visualization. A third No. 2 traction suture can be placed on the most proximal aspect of the medial leaflet by using a suture passer (45° CapFix; Smith & Nephew) with the goal of further visualization enhancement (Fig 6). These traction stitches are manually tensioned from the outside while tension is maintained via a Crile hemostat fixed just over the skin to avoid skin maceration, as shown in Video 1.

Femoral Osteochondroplasty or Femoroplasty

Prior to cam resection, a 50° radiofrequency probe is used to mark the most proximal aspect of the resection over the bone at the femoral head-neck junction (Fig 7). Continuing with the same probe, the periosteum on the femoral neck is cleaned to allow for the burr to rasp over a clean and optimal bony surface during resection. Fluoroscopy is then used to visualize the cam deformity in 6 positions: extension and neutral rotation, extension and 30° of internal rotation, extension and 30° of external rotation, 30° of flexion and neutral rotation, and 30° of flexion plus 40° and 60° of external rotation.

Arthroscopic image of right hip showing identification of most proximal aspect of subsequent resection as viewed from modified mid-anterior portal.

Starting at the 12:30 clock-face position to 1-o’clock position in a right hip (11:30 clock-face position to 11-o’clock position in a left hip), a 5.5-mm burr is used to create a trough that will set the depth of resection at 30° of hip flexion (Fig 8). The depth of resection is set proximally at the femoral head-neck junction by gently burring cortical bone until the underlying cancellous bone is identified. It is crucial to always match the resection depth achieved at 30° of hip flexion. After setting the depth and prior to the start of the actual resection, the hip is brought into extension followed by femoral neck bony overgrowth (cam lesion) resection through the previously described extension positions (extension and neutral rotation, extension and 30° of internal rotation, and extension and 30° of external rotation). After fluoroscopy is used for resection depth accuracy verification, the hip is brought into 30° of flexion and neutral rotation, 30° of flexion plus 40° of external rotation, and 30° of flexion plus 60° of external rotation to accurately resect the most anterior aspect of the cam from the 1- to 3-o’clock position (9- to 11-o’clock position in a left hip). On completion of resection, the burr is used in reverse spin mode to create a smooth transition between the femoral head and neck (Fig 9). Finally, fluoroscopy is used one last time in the previously described 6 views for confirmation of an accurate 360° cam resection (Fig 10).

Capsular Closure

The camera is switched to the mMAP to begin closing the capsule. A cannula is placed in the AL portal, through which a suture passer (70° CapFix; Smith & Nephew) loaded with No. 2 Vicryl (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ) is used. Capsular closure should begin on the most distal aspect of the T-capsulotomy, and a working clear-sighted path cannula can be used to enhance knot sliding visualization for avoidance of suture entanglement. Working proximally, tension in the traction stitches is incrementally released, which allows for further plication of the capsule. Finally, the interportal capsulotomy is closed with the intention of creating a watertight closure (Fig 11). It is important to finish closing the capsule in extension to avoid the incidence of iatrogenic postoperative flexion contracture. Tips and tricks of the described technique can be found in Table 1.

Discussion

The refinement of hip arthroscopy techniques has ushered in a new era in the management of FAI. The evolution of these techniques, particularly through emerging capsular management literature and strategic use of fluoroscopy, has been pivotal in enhancing surgical accuracy, reducing complications, and improving patient outcomes.

Although capsular management has been a topic of past debate, recent literature has emerged displaying the significant benefits derived from restoring capsular integrity. Restoring the native anatomic capsule not only improves patient-reported outcomes but also impacts revision rates and, potentially, the need for total hip arthroplasty conversion.8,9 Therefore, we strongly advocate capsular repair as a means of optimizing outcomes after hip arthroscopy.

Fluoroscopy has emerged as a cornerstone in the realm of hip arthroscopy, granting surgeons the ability to perform procedures with enhanced precision. Investigations have underscored its significance in shortening the learning curve by providing real-time feedback, a feature that is indispensable in the accurate placement of portals and the resection of cam lesions.10,11 The described technique elaborates on this by detailing how fluoroscopy aids in assessment of the osteochondroplasty, aligning with the current understanding that fluoroscopic guidance is paramount in avoiding iatrogenic complications.

On completion of the peripheral-compartment portion of the procedure, the femoral bony overgrowth will have been excised and the capsule repaired. This comprehensive approach not only restores the labrum and addresses bony irregularities but also enhances stability, decreases pain, and improves quality of life.
