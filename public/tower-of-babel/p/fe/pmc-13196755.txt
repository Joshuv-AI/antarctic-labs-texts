Spin glass in quantum paraelectric KTaO3: Supercritical CO2-induced monoclinic phase
Authors: Yuning Liang, Qun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inducing magnetism in d0 nonmagnetic oxides is a central challenge in condensed matter physics and a bottleneck for developing advanced spintronic devices. KTaO3 epitomizes this challenge: Despite having strong spin-orbit coupling, its functionality is hindered by a stable nonmagnetic cubic phase. Here, we report the unlocking of magnetism in KTaO3 via supercritical carbon dioxide (SC CO2), which induces a stable monoclinic phase that has, until now, remained undiscovered. Experimentally, we demonstrate that this monoclinic phase has spontaneous magnetic moments, driving the system into a spin-glass state. Monte Carlo simulations pinpoint the origin of this glassy behavior to interfacial phase frustration, arising from the coupling between ferromagnetic facets and an antiferromagnetic bulk. To our knowledge, this work not only presents the first observation of a magnetic phase in KTaO3 but also reveals a peculiar phenomenon of SC CO2–induced interfacial phase frustration, establishing a versatile strategy for engineering complex magnetic states in quantum paraelectric.

INTRODUCTION

Spontaneous symmetry breaking is a cornerstone of condensed matter physics, leading to a rich diversity of quantum phases (1–6). The quantum paraelectric KTaO3 (KTO), however, represents a fascinating case where such transitions are frustrated, and, then, it stubbornly retains its high-symmetry cubic perovskite structure down to near absolute zero (7–13). This structural rigidity is a major roadblock for spintronics as the strong spin-orbit coupling (SOC) promises rich spin textures that are exquisitely sensitive to heterointerfaces or broken inversion symmetry. Consequently, harnessing these spin textures by virtue of fascinating extrinsic methods as interface engineering has been investigated recently (14–20).

The application of high pressure is an established method for inducing phase transitions in quantum materials, successfully driving Lifshitz transitions in Sr2RuO4, tuning the Mott metal-insulator transition in Ca2RuO4, and realizing a topological phase transition in TaSe3 (21–24). A persistent challenge, however, is that some pressure-induced phases are not quenchable; they revert to their pristine state upon pressure release. This necessitates in situ characterization and implies that many exotic physical phenomena, such as pressure-induced superconductivity, may be overlooked or remain difficult to exploit (25–27). Therefore, a critical challenge lies in developing alternative strategies that not only can introduce pressure but also can stabilize the resulting phases for ex situ investigation and application.

Herein, we overcome this challenge by introducing a methodology based on SC CO2. This approach builds upon prior work demonstrating the capability of SC CO2 in oxide phase engineering, including inducing ferromagnetism in VO2 and BaTiO3, and achieving controlled amorphization in MoO3 (28–30). While our recent work revealed that SC CO2 can stabilize high-index polar facets of KTO via polarity compensation (31), the present study ventures further to explore its potential to drive a profound bulk phase transition, characterized by quantitative changes in lattice geometry (e.g., interplanar angles). Our goal is to unlock previously unidentified magnetic functionalities that are intrinsic to the crystal structure, rather than those arising from extrinsic defects like oxygen vacancies (Ov). Our method uniquely transduces macroscopic pressure into atomic-scale stress within KTO nanopowder, whose high surface-to-volume ratio is a prerequisite distinguishing this technique from studies on bulk crystals or thin films (Supplementary Text S1). This intimate, nanoscale interaction induces a profound and irreversible transformation: The process not only exfoliates the microcrystals into two-dimensional (2D) nanosheets but also, more critically, drives the parent cubic structure into a stable, and hitherto undiscovered, monoclinic phase. This CO2-induced symmetry breaking unlocks the latent potential of KTO, fundamentally altering its electronic and magnetic properties. The emergence of the monoclinic phase shatters the crystallographic inversion symmetry, profoundly reshaping the system’s spin texture. A combination of density functional theory (DFT) calculations and magnetic measurements reveals a remarkable magnetic landscape defined by competing interactions: The monoclinic phase exposes strongly ferromagnetic (FM) facets, while its bulk retains robust antiferromagnetic (AFM) coupling. This intrinsic magnetic frustration, engineered at the nanoscale by the phase transformation itself, is the origin of a nascent spin glass (SG) state. Crucially, further Monte Carlo simulations distinguish the roles of structure versus defects, confirming that the SG is not an artifact of extrinsic defects but an inevitable consequence of the competing magnetic orders inherent to the monoclinic phase.

RESULTS

SC CO2 fundamentally alters the morphology and crystal structure of KTO. Whereas pristine KTO consists of bulk cubic microcrystals oriented along the low-index [001] direction (fig. S1), the SC CO2–treated KTO (20 MPa) transforms into 2D nanosheets exhibiting previously unobserved high-index orientations, such as [02¯1] and [112] (fig. S2 and Fig. 1). This structural transformation was directly observed using atomic-resolution high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM), which revealed significant lattice distortions along these distinct directions (Fig. 1, A, D, and G). The interplanar angles deviate from the 90° orthogonality of the cubic phase, the angle between the (100) and (012) planes becomes ~97°, and the angle between the (1¯10) and (111¯) planes becomes ~100°, both of them provide the quantitative evidence of the lattice distortion.

This distortion, further corroborated by fast Fourier transform analysis (Fig. 1, F and I), suggests a reduction in crystal symmetry to either a monoclinic or triclinic phase. To distinguish between these two possibilities, we examined the [001] zone axis and found that its interplanar angles remain strictly at 90° (Fig. 1, A and C). This specific change, where only the β angle is altered, is a definitive feature of a cubic-to-monoclinic phase transition, allowing us to unambiguously rule out the triclinic phase. Further analysis reveals that the phase transition results in a composite structure. We identify two distinct (100) interplanar spacings: 0.41 nm corresponds to a strained cubic lattice, whereas the larger 0.44 nm is attributed to the previously unidentified monoclinic phase (Fig. 1B and fig. S3). This coexistence of the strained parent phase and the nascent daughter phase is further supported by geometric phase analysis (GPA), which maps the strain fields (fig. S4) (32–34).

By combining the measured interplanar angles and spacings (Fig. 1, B, E, and H), we calculated the lattice parameters of the monoclinic phase. The entire phase transition pathway is summarized in Fig. 1J: The SC CO2 treatment first induces a tensile strain that expands the parent cubic lattice, which then releases local stress by distorting into the monoclinic phase. Critically, STEM simulations using this monoclinic model parameters show excellent agreement with the experimental data, validating this structural determination (Fig. 1, A, D, and G). This strain-driven symmetry breaking provides a direct pathway for the emergence of magnetism (3, 35, 36). In KTO, the Ta─O bonds have a mixed ionic-covalent character (37, 38). The distortion to the monoclinic phase elongates specific Ta─O bonds, which weakens electron orbital overlap and localizes unpaired electrons, thereby generating spontaneous magnetic moments. This mechanism is fully consistent with our spin-polarized density of states calculations, which show a clear spin polarization emerging in the monoclinic phase (Fig. 1, K and L).

To decouple the effects of the induced monoclinic phase from those of intrinsic Ov on the electronic and magnetic properties, we performed a series of correlated spectroscopic analyses. First, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy establishes a critical baseline, showing that the concentration of Ov, the only detectable extrinsic paramagnetic defects, remains constant after SC CO2 treatment (Fig. 2A) (39, 40). Given that interference from magnetic ions has been ruled out (fig. S5 and Supplementary Text S2), this implies that any emergent magnetism cannot be attributed to a change in Ov concentration.

However, x-ray absorption near-edge structure spectroscopy at the Ta L3-edge reveals a significant reduction in the average Ta valence state in the KTO (20 MPa) (Fig. 2B), a finding that cannot be explained by the static Ov concentration alone (41, 42). Crucially, Hall effect measurements demonstrate that this valence reduction does not correspond to an increase in mobile carrier density (table S1); instead, the carrier concentration remains comparable to the pristine state. This discrepancy between the reduced Ta valence and the low mobile carrier density provides compelling evidence that the injected electrons are localized. This localization is accompanied by a fundamental change in the local bonding environment, which is confirmed as an elongation of the Ta─O bonds by extended x-ray absorption fine structure data and bond-length fitting results (Fig. 2, C and D, and table S2).

These structural and electronic alterations are definitively linked to the phase transition by Raman spectroscopy. KTO (20 MPa) exhibits unreported phonon modes that are symmetry forbidden in the parent cubic phase (Fig. 2, F versus E) (43). These modes are a hallmark of the distorted TaO6 coordination in the monoclinic phase (mechanism I, Fig. 2G), which is distinct from the Ov-dominated environment in the cubic phase (mechanism II, Fig. 2G). Collectively, we have spectroscopically decoupled the phase transition from the Ov, demonstrating that the emergence of the monoclinic phase, rather than an increase in Ov concentration, is responsible for the additional reduction of Ta ions and the profound alteration of the local electronic structure.

While recent studies have highlighted that KTO is susceptible to lattice expansion driven by nonstoichiometry and point defects and that Ov can induce anomalous Hall signals and paramagnetism, our EPR results demonstrate that the Ov concentration does not increase after treatment (44, 45). This observation aligns with the defect-healing capability of supercritical fluids recently reported in KTO, where a reduction in Ov was observed alongside magnetic enhancement (31). Having decoupled the influence of Ov, we investigated the magnetic properties of KTO (20 MPa) to probe the role of the monoclinic phase.

Room-temperature magnetic hysteresis loops reveal a sevenfold enhancement in magnetization compared to the weak, Ov-induced paramagnetism of the pristine KTO (Fig. 3A and fig. S6). Recent theoretical work suggests that itinerant quasiparticles in cubic KTO have vanishing magnetic moments due to strong SOC, implying that simple itinerant carriers cannot account for such robust magnetism (46). Therefore, we attribute the observed magnetic enhancement to the emergence of localized moments within the magnetic monoclinic phase (47, 48). Notably, the magnetization fails to saturate even at 3 T and grows near-linearly in the high-field region, indicating the presence of strong internal AFM exchange coupling that resists the alignment of spins with the external field (49). The lack of enhancement in magnetism at low temperatures further suggests complex magnetic interactions within KTO (20 MPa) (fig. S7). In contrast to the simple paramagnetic behavior of pristine KTO (fig. S8), KTO (20 MPa) exhibits the classic hallmarks of a SG: a cusp in its zero-field-cooled (ZFC) curve at a freezing temperature Tf ≈ 40 K, with a pronounced bifurcation from its field-cooled (FC) curve (Fig. 3B) (50–53). A Curie-Weiss fit to the high-temperature susceptibility confirms the presence of strong AFM interactions, providing the necessary condition of magnetic frustration for a glassy state (54, 55).

A series of dynamic magnetic measurements provides compelling evidence for the SG nature of KTO (20 MPa). First, the Tf is systematically suppressed with increasing applied magnetic field (Fig. 3C), a key feature that distinguishes it from superparamagnetism (56, 57). The field dependence of Tf follows a power-law relationship consistent with the de Almeida-Thouless line, yielding a critical exponent Φ = 1.55 (fig. S9). This value is in excellent agreement with the mean-field theory prediction for an Ising SG (Φ = 1.5), providing strong quantitative support for our hypothesis (58, 59). Second, the nonergodicity of the SG is manifest in its slow, nonexponential magnetic relaxation below Tf (Fig. 3D) (60–62). Conclusively, the system exhibits the FC-ZFC memory effect (Fig. 3E), a quintessential fingerprint of SG dynamics, wherein the system retains a memory of its thermal history (63, 64). This behavior confirms the existence of the hierarchical energy landscape characteristic of a SG. Last, the observed exchange bias phenomenon (fig. S10 and Supplementary Text S3) provides direct evidence for the microscopic origin of this glassy behavior: interfacial coupling between competing FM and AFM domains (65, 66). This competition is the root cause of the magnetic frustration that stabilizes the SG ground state.

To theoretically validate our central hypothesis that the SG originates from competing magnetic interactions within the monoclinic phase, we performed a series of multiscale calculations. First, DFT calculations reveal that the exposed facets of the monoclinic phase have substantially larger magnetic moments than those of either the pristine or stretched cubic phases (Fig. 4A). This enhancement is a direct consequence of lattice distortion and surface reconstruction, which alter Ta─O orbital hybridization and increase spin polarization (figs. S11 and S12 and Supplementary Text S4).

Building upon these first-principles results, we constructed a physical model wherein the SG behavior arises from magnetic frustration inherent to the monoclinic domains themselves: a competition between their highly FM surfaces and a bulk that retains strong AFM coupling. We use the Ising model to describe this system. The model’s central premise is that, even in a structurally perfect crystal without atomic disorder, the geometric and competitive nature of these magnetic interactions is sufficient to create a complex energy landscape characteristic of a SG.

The system’s total energy is described by a classical Ising Hamiltonian (H) composed of three distinct terms: a surface FM term (HFM), a bulk AFM term (HAFM), and an interface coupling term (Hinterface)H=HFM+HAFM+Hinterface(1)

The interactions within the surface and bulk regions are defined asHFM=−Js∑〈i,j〉∈surfaceSiSj(2)HAFM=−Jb∑〈k,l〉∈bulkSkSl(3)

Here, S represents the Ising spin variables (±1). The exchange constant Js within the surface is positive (Js > 0), promoting the parallel alignment of spins consistent with our DFT findings of a FM surface. Conversely, the exchange constant Jb in the bulk is negative (Jb < 0), enforcing the antiparallel alignment of spins characteristic of an antiferromagnet.

The crucial element of competition is captured by the interface termHinterface=−Jint∑〈i∈surface,k∈bulk〉SiSk(4)

This term describes the interaction between spins on the surface (Si) and their nearest neighbors in the first layer of the bulk (Sk). The nature of this coupling, determined by the sign and magnitude of Jint, is the source of magnetic frustration. For instance, a FM interface coupling (Jint > 0) would force a bulk spin at the interface to align with its surface neighbor. However, this same bulk spin is simultaneously subject to AFM coupling (Jb < 0) from its neighbors deeper within the bulk. It is impossible for the spin to satisfy both of these competing energetic demands simultaneously.

This inherent conflict, or magnetic frustration, is the key mechanism for SG formation in our model. Unlike traditional SG theories that rely on “quenched disorder” (randomness in the exchange couplings Jij due to atomic defects or alloying), our model generates glassy behavior from a perfectly ordered lattice with deterministic interactions. The frustration is geometric and periodic across the entire interface.

To validate this model, we performed Monte Carlo simulations, which provide compelling evidence for a frustration-driven mechanism. A model incorporating the competing FM surface and AFM bulk interactions inherent to the monoclinic phase accurately reproduced the experimentally observed spin-freezing cusp (Fig. 4B). In stark contrast, a series of control simulations based on the cubic phase failed to show any indication of a SG transition, even with a high concentration of Ov introduced (Fig. 4C). This sharp divergence leads to an unequivocal conclusion, as illustrated in Fig. 4D: The structural phase transition from cubic to monoclinic is the essential element for engendering the SG. Our findings clarify that, while extrinsic factors like Ov can introduce local magnetic moments, this form of disorder alone is insufficient to generate the collective, geometric frustration necessary for a glassy state. Instead, it is the intrinsic competition between long-range FM and AFM order, induced by the unique structure of the monoclinic phase, that creates the highly degenerate energy landscape required to stabilize the SG ground state in KTO.

DISCUSSION

The emergence of a SG ground state in quantum paraelectric KTO highlights the critical role of structural symmetry breaking in unlocking hidden magnetic degrees of freedom. While previous attempts to induce magnetism in d0 oxides have largely relied on defect engineering, often leading to metallic conduction or extrinsic signals, our results demonstrate a distinct mechanism driven by a structural phase transition. The SC CO2–induced monoclinic distortion breaks the cubic symmetry of the parent lattice, localizing electrons into Ta 5d orbitals to form stable magnetic moments without doping mobile carriers. Crucially, the observation of glassy dynamics points to a complex magnetic energy landscape. Our theoretical modeling identifies the physical origin of this behavior as interfacial phase frustration, arising from the competitive coupling between the FM monoclinic facets and the AFM bulk. This scenario is fundamentally different from canonical dilute magnetic alloys or vacancy-driven magnetism, representing a structurally engineered intrinsic magnetic state.

Beyond the specific case of KTO, this work establishes supercritical fluid treatment as a versatile platform for manipulating order parameters in complex oxides. In contrast to conventional strain engineering or chemical substitution, which often introduce disorder or require specific substrates, SC CO2 treatment acts as a soft chemical switch that can access metastable phases hidden in the energy landscape. This capability is particularly significant for quantum paraelectrics, enabling the interplay between intrinsic strong SOC and induced magnetic order to stabilize exotic functional states. We anticipate that this methodology will open an advanced paradigm for designing future spintronic materials, enabling the on-demand creation of magnetic functionality in otherwise nonmagnetic quantum materials.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Synthesis of pristine KTO

Add 11.3 g (0.201 mol) of potassium hydroxide to 7 ml of deionized water and stir until dissolved. Subsequently, add 1.25 g (0.003 mol) of tantalum oxide (Ta2O5) to the aforementioned solution. Maintain stirring at room temperature for 1 hour to ensure thorough mixing. Transfer the mixture to a 30-ml reactor and react at 180°C for 6 hours. After the reaction, wash the product several times with deionized water to remove residual reactants and then centrifuge to collect the precipitate. Freeze-dry the precipitate, and the powder obtained after drying is pristine KTO.

Synthesis of KTO (20 MPa)

Weigh out 30 mg of untreated KTO powder and dissolve it in 10 ml of deionized water, stirring thoroughly to form a homogeneous solution. Transfer the solution to a supercritical reaction vessel, introduce carbon dioxide at different pressures, and stir at 200°C for 6 hours. After reaction, centrifuge at 4000 rpm for 30 min to remove larger particles, and then collect the supernatant. Next, centrifuge the supernatant at 10,000 rpm for 10 min and collect the precipitate. Freeze drying the precipitate for 30 hours to obtain a dried powder. The sample treated under 20 MPa SC CO2 is referred to as KTO (20 MPa).

Characterizations

HAADF-STEM observations were conducted on a JEOL ARM200F equipped with a double aberration corrector and operated at 200 kV. The thickness of the nanoplates was measured using an atomic force microscope (Bruker Dimension Icon). X-ray diffraction patterns were collected on a Bruker D8 Focus diffractometer (Bruker AXS, Germany) using Cu Kα radiation. Raman measurements were carried out on a LabRAM HR Evolution system using a 532-nm laser. EPR spectra at 300 K were recorded on a Bruker ELEXSYS E500 spectrometer. XAFS spectra were collected at the BL11B beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. The carrier concentration and mobility were measured using a Hall effect measurement system (HMS-7000). Magnetic measurements were conducted using a superconducting quantum interference device.

Details of crystal facet magnetism calculations

All calculations were carried out for the material in the framework of DFT using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (version 6.3.2) (67, 68). The generalized gradient approximation of the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof function was used to describe the exchange-correlation energy (69). The projected augmented wave method and pseudopotentials were used to describe the interactions between valence electrons and ions (70). To ensure the efficiency of the computational results and parallel computing. A 1 × 4 × 4 k-point grid under Monkhorst-Pack is used in the optimization process, and 450-eV truncation energy is set. The lattice parameters and ionic positions of all crystals were fully relaxed, and the convergence criteria for the total energy of all relaxed atoms and the final force were 10−5 eV and 0.03 eV/Å, respectively.
