Utility of real-time ultrasound-guided spinal anesthesia in severe ankylosing spondylitis - A case report -
Authors: Jedniphat Intrapongpan, Hyungtae Kim, Hajung Kim, Won Uk Koh, Se-Ung Park, Yoojeong Kwak, Jaedo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnkylosing spondylitis (AS) distorts the neuraxial anatomy and can complicate airway management. However, patients with severe AS are often considered unsuitable for spinal anesthesia, and attempts are typically avoided. We report a case of successful real-time ultrasound-guided spinal anesthesia in a patient with severe AS.CaseA 73-year-old man with severe AS and restrictive lung impairment presented with a femoral neck fracture and was scheduled for bipolar hemiarthroplasty. Cervical flexion deformity and whole spine ankylosis predicted difficult airway and hindered spinal anesthesia. Preprocedural ultrasound in the paramedian sagittal oblique view identified a single, small L3–4 interlaminar space. Real-time ultrasound guidance enabled intrathecal needle advancement and successful spinal anesthesia with uneventful intraoperative and immediate postoperative courses.ConclusionsReal-time ultrasound-guided spinal anesthesia can overcome severe anatomical distortion in patients with AS.

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic inflammatory disease that primarily affects the axial skeleton. It causes progressive stiffness and ossification of the spinal ligaments and results in severe spinal deformities and reduced spinal mobility [1]. Therefore, neuraxial anesthesia may be challenging. Cervical involvement is common in advanced AS, which challenges airway management during sedation or general anesthesia. Patients with AS often present with additional comorbidities, such as restrictive lung disease, which further influences perioperative management.

Anesthetic techniques must be carefully planned for surgical intervention. General anesthesia is often avoided in patients with AS when possible, owing to concerns regarding difficult airway management and the risk of perioperative pulmonary complications. Regional anesthesia, including spinal anesthesia, is preferred; however, spinal anesthesia is challenging in patients with severe AS. Syndesmophytic ankylosis and ossification make conventional landmark-based neuraxial techniques difficult; therefore, the implementation of alternative approaches, such as ultrasound-guided techniques, has proven to be helpful [2,3].

We report the case of a 73-year-old man with severe AS and restrictive lung disease who successfully underwent bipolar hemiarthroplasty under real-time ultrasound-guided spinal anesthesia. Despite profound spinal deformity that typically precludes neuraxial techniques, real-time ultrasound enabled precise landmark identification and safe block placement, demonstrating its value in overcoming anatomical challenges. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Asan Medical Center, University of Ulsan College of Medicine (IRB No. 2025-1103). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient.

CASE REPORT

A 73-year-old man presented with a femoral neck fracture and was scheduled for bipolar hemiarthroplasty. His medical history was notable for advanced AS, which had caused severe spinal deformity and significant mobility restriction due to extensive spinal fusion. On clinical examination, a difficult airway was anticipated owing to spinal rigidity, limited mouth opening, and a fixed posture caused by cervical kyphosis resulting from spinal fusion (Fig. 1). In addition, we confirmed whole-spine involvement with radiographic changes in AS (Fig. 2).

The patient’s comorbid restrictive lung disease further complicated the anesthetic planning. Pulmonary function tests showed a severe restrictive pattern, with a forced vital capacity of 41% predicted and forced expiratory volume in 1 s of 55% predicted. The diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide reduced to 49% predicted, consistent with significant pulmonary compromise. Although the patient had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), this restrictive physiology was attributed to AS (limited chest expansion) rather than an obstructive pattern. These findings suggest an increased risk of postoperative pulmonary complications with the use of general anesthesia.

Regional anesthesia was preferred. However, spinal anesthesia was anticipated to be extremely challenging owing to diffuse syndesmophytic ankylosis and a bamboo spine appearance with interlaminar space obliteration.

The patient was placed in the left lateral decubitus position. This posture provided the greatest possible lumbar exposure to view the completely fused spine and allowed the right-handed operator to hold the ultrasound probe with the left hand and control the spinal needle with the right hand, facilitating fine adjustments during insertion. Positioning was challenging owing to the rigid spine. The patient’s neck and head were supported with an adequately elevated pillow to maintain comfort and prevent potential cervical spine injuries.

Ultrasound scanning using the Sonimage HS2 system (Konica Minolta) equipped with a curved 2–5 MHz transducer in the paramedian sagittal oblique view identified a single accessible interlaminar space at L3–4 with a narrow acoustic window (Fig. 3A). As the in-plane approach was not feasible because of the angle, real-time ultrasound-guided spinal anesthesia was performed using an out-of-plane approach with a 25-gauge Quincke needle (BD). Needle visualization was clear; however, precise directional control of the needle toward the small interlaminar target was technically challenging.

Subarachnoid access was achieved in approximately 2 min on the first attempt (Supplementary Video 1). Clear cerebrospinal fluid was obtained, and 2.4 ml of 0.5% hyperbaric bupivacaine (no additives) was injected. The total procedure time was approximately 10 min. A bilateral sensory block to the T8 dermatome was achieved, and stable hemodynamics were maintained throughout the surgery.

DISCUSSION

Anesthetic management of patients with advanced AS is particularly demanding owing to the combined challenges of airway limitations and distorted neuraxial anatomy. In this patient, severe spinal fusion and loss of interlaminar space made conventional landmark-guided spinal anesthesia impractical. Real-time ultrasound guidance enabled visualization of the remaining accessible interlaminar window and facilitated precise needle placement.

The coexistence of severe restrictive lung disease further influenced the anesthetic plan, as it contributed to a higher risk of postoperative pulmonary complications, particularly when general anesthesia was used. Pulmonary function tests revealed severe restrictive lung disease with markedly reduced lung volumes and impaired diffusion capacity, reinforcing the decision to avoid general anesthesia. Regional anesthesia, including spinal anesthesia, is often preferable in such patients because it avoids airway manipulation and provides effective analgesia with minimal respiratory impact. Regional anesthesia can reduce postoperative complications in patients with AS and restrictive lung disease [4,5].

Nevertheless, in patients with severe AS, successful neuraxial anesthesia requires overcoming the technical challenges imposed by spinal ankylosis. Traditional landmark-guided spinal anesthesia is often impractical owing to obliterated interlaminar spaces and distorted anatomical landmarks. Similarly, a standard midline approach is impractical because of ossified interspinous ligaments and fused spinous processes. Therefore, a paramedian sagittal oblique approach is usually preferable because it provides a more favorable acoustic window for ultrasound guidance. In this context, ultrasound guidance offers a valuable alternative. Ultrasound enables real-time visualization of spinal structures, facilitating the identification of interlaminar spaces and dura mater, even in patients with severe deformities. Although technically more demanding than static ultrasound, real-time guidance provides greater accuracy in needle placement and enhances procedural safety.

The sonographic appearance of patients with AS differed markedly from that of individuals without AS. In a normal lumbar spine (Fig. 3B), the paramedian sagittal oblique view typically shows a regular “sawtooth” pattern formed by distinct laminae and wide interlaminar spaces, through which the posterior and anterior dura are clearly visualized.

However, in this patient with severe AS (Fig. 3A), the interlaminar spaces were extremely narrow, and the posterior complex appeared thin owing to syndesmophytic fusion of the adjacent vertebrae. The normal “sawtooth” contour was replaced by a continuous hyperechoic bony line, leaving only a small acoustic gap for dural visualization. With these anatomical limitations, real-time ultrasound guidance minimizes the risk of dural puncture failure or nerve injury and ensures procedural success.

In this case, preprocedural ultrasound scanning revealed a single accessible interlaminar space at the L3–4 level. The posterior and anterior dura were identified, allowing for precise placement of the spinal needle. For clinicians managing similar cases, we recommend systematic scanning from the sacrum upward to locate any potential interlaminar window. Using a curved low-frequency probe and fine adjustments of the probe angle can help optimize visualization in fused spines.

A growing body of literature supports the use of ultrasound-guided neuraxial anesthesia in patients with AS. Ultrasound guidance is associated with higher success rates, shorter procedure times, and fewer complications than conventional techniques [2,3,5]. This approach is particularly advantageous in patients with challenging anatomy, such as those with severe spinal deformities or a history of spinal surgeries [6]. However, real-time ultrasound guidance has limitations: it requires significant operator expertise, may prolong procedure time, and can prove challenging or even ineffective if no sonographic window is available in an extensively fused spine.

In conclusion, real-time ultrasound guidance should be considered a valuable tool when performing neuraxial anesthesia in patients with severe AS, particularly when complicated by severe restrictive pulmonary disease. This enables safe and reliable anesthetic management in situations where conventional techniques are not feasible.
