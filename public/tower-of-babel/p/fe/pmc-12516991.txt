Cost4Visit: a cross-sectional cohort study on hidden costs among surgical patients and relatives for in-person and video visits
Authors: Demi van Dalen, Eddy Adang, Harry van Goor, Martijn WJ Stommel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare systems face excessive pressure on sustainability due to financial, social and environmental concerns. Video visits offer an appreciated alternative for routine in-person visits in surgical subspecialties, with the potential to lower costs among hospitals and patients and carbon footprint. However, a comprehensive understanding of patients’ and companions’ costs to attend an outpatient clinic visit remains understudied. The study aims to provide insight into direct and indirect patient costs of a tertiary surgical outpatient clinic visit.MethodsA cross-sectional survey study was conducted among adult patients scheduled for a tertiary outpatient surgery clinic visit from September 2020 to September 2021. The cost of a surgical visit was assessed using a study-specific questionnaire. Visit costs between subspecialties and visit modalities were compared with generalised linear modelling.ResultsOf the 961 included surgical patients (866 in-person and 95 video visits), those who received a video visit experienced a substantial halving of visit-related costs (€67 vs €172; β=−103.65, p<0.001) and reduction in costs with increasing age (β=−1.52, p=<0.001), attributable to decreased absenteeism from work for patients and companions, and the absence of travel expenses.ConclusionDirect and indirect costs among patients and relatives for a visit to a tertiary surgical outpatient clinic are high. Reporting previously neglected, yet significant costs, including those borne by companions, could enhance awareness among clinicians and policymakers regarding the financial and societal impact of offering certain visit modalities and may influence the shared decision-making process.Keywords: Health services research, Patient-centred care, Outpatients, Cost-effectiveness, Telemedicine

Introduction

Value-based healthcare is the primary focus of policymakers nowadays to increase healthcare affordability.1 However, there has been little attention to costs incurred by patients and their relatives. To what extent the more hidden expenses, such as a mandatory contribution in healthcare costs, but also less hidden direct and indirect costs related to each hospital visit, negatively impact patients and families is underexposed.2,4 Current cost analyses often fail to incorporate all these cost variables incurred by patients and relatives and the financial impact on patient level of certain medical decisions is not well documented.5,8

During the pandemic, remote alternatives to in-person outpatient visits have increasingly been adopted to maintain quality and continuity of care.9 10 The rapid adoption of video visits led to cost and time savings for patients, improved accessibility,11 high satisfaction12,15 and reduced exposure to illnesses.16

Additionally, a substantial, unintended global reduction in the healthcare-related carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2eq) emissions of 6.4% due to restrictions was reached.17 18 Through the delivery of remote care, healthcare organisations can serve a larger population, with shorter waiting times, more efficient resource allocation and less impact on climate change.19 Although virtual care holds promise, indirect costs associated with video visits are poorly delineated and the financial burden on patients and their accompanying relatives is often overlooked.20 This study aims to assess the cost of a surgical in-person and video visit incurred by patients and their companions, based on travel expenses, informal care costs and costs associated with productivity loss and loss of leisure time. Understanding the entire cost breakdown of both visit modalities from a patient perspective is important to better tailor outpatient visits, considering patients’ medical, social and financial situation.21 Comparing outcomes and associated factors between in-person and video visits may provide valuable insights for clinics establishing value-based virtual care.

Methods

Study sample

This cross-sectional survey study was conducted among a diverse, general surgical patient cohort, aged 18 years of age and older, scheduled for a visit at the surgical outpatient clinic of a tertiary, academic hospital, between September 2020 and September 2021. The choice for a general surgical population was based on the large diversity in patients regarding age, sex, employment status and social environment, prioritising of surgical care during the pandemic and comprehensive experience with video visits at the clinic.

All outpatient clinic visits, both in-person (face-to-face consultations (F2F)) and video (video consultations (VC)), were included and choice of visit modality was made in joint decision between care providers and patients without involvement of the research team. Prematurely terminated visits or visits replaced by telephone due to technical difficulties were excluded.

Based on the historical clinical data of the past year, a sample size of 10% of the visits would yield a representative sample of the general surgical population. Subsequently, a sample of 1000 patients was proportionally selected by surgical subspecialty. The study was deemed exempt from review by the institutional review board and registered in the Dutch Trial Registry (ID NL8825). All participants provided informed e-consent. Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guideline was used in reporting this study.22

F2F patients were recruited by trained medical students in the waiting area before their visit. Recruiting was dependent on student availability while adhering to social distancing regulations. To minimise recall bias, the questionnaire was filled out immediately after the visit.

Video visits had been offered routinely by gastro-intestinal (GI)-oncological surgeons for several years, building comprehensive experience among staff performing VC.16 23 To avoid initial implementation issues among vascular and trauma (orthopaedic) surgeons affecting study outcomes, only patients with a VC performed by GI-oncological surgeons were invited for participation in the study.

3 days before the visit, VC patients received the invitation by email to voluntarily participate in the study and signed e-consent by replying to this e-mail. The morning of the VC, participating patients received a secured email providing access to the online questionnaire and were asked to fill out the questionnaire within 24 hours after the consultation to prevent recall bias. Otherwise, they were considered as dropouts and no reminder was sent.

Patient and public involvement

This study did not involve patients or the public in the design, conduct or reporting phases of the research. The nature of the study did not require direct patient involvement, as the focus was on providing insight into overlooked costs.

Measurements and outcomes

The anonymous questionnaire included queries on personal patient data (age, sex and educational status), their health status using the validated EQ-5D,24 travel and informal care expenses, employment status and productivity and leisure time loss, of both patients and their companions (see online supplemental file 1).

Baseline demographics, including zip code, type of visit, surgical subspecialty and encounter time of the visit, were abstracted from the electronic health record (EHR). In-person encounter time was calculated as the difference between check-in time at the register desk until start of the questionnaire at the waiting area. Virtual encounter time was calculated as the time between start time of appointment until closing of the clinician’s note in the EHR, as visit duration was not automatically registered.

Median household income was calculated using zip code income data and divided into low, middle and high incomes.25

Total cost of a visit was calculated through bottom-up microcosting estimating the main key costing variables: productivity loss and leisure time loss, actual travel expenses, including parking fees, and costs for informal care or childcare.26 27 Cost analyses were guided by the costing manual of the Dutch National Healthcare Institute.28 29 All reported costs were calculated by multiplying volumes with inflation-adjusted unit cost prices from 2014 to 2021 (factor 1.11067). Technology-associated costs, such as access to smartphones or Internet, were not included in the analysis. In all patients in the VC group, the possession of the necessary equipment and the ability to perform a video visit had been confirmed in advance, as a part of their care path.

To account for the total economic cost of a single hospital visit, the costs associated with a companion were included in the patient’s total cost of a visit. This ensures that all costs directly related to the surgical clinic visit are captured. A detailed description of the cost calculation is found in online supplemental file 2.

Statistical analysis

Total cost of a visit is calculated as the overall costs based on the mentioned cost components among subgroups (F2F and VC) and in terms of per visit-associated costs in **Euros. Generalised linear models (GLMs) with identity link function and gamma distribution were fitted to the data to investigate whether mean cost per visit varied among the two visit modalities (model 1), the GI-oncological subgroup (model 2) and between in-person visits of the three subspecialties (model 3). Self-reported age, sex, educational status, EQ-5D-measured functionality and participation in usual activities and income class were selected as clinically relevant variables for all models at study design based on the literature. Missing data were reported and excluded listwise in the GLM. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p<0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software (IBM SPSS Statistics for MacOS, V.28.0.1.0 Armonk, NY: IBM Corp).

Results

Most visits in general (537/961, 55.9%) and all 95 VC (95/95, 100.0%) were with GI-oncological surgery. In the GI-oncological subgroup, males were more likely to have a VC than an F2F ((49/95, 51.6%) vs 179/441, 40.6%, p<0.05), there were no other significant differences between F2F and VC.

The average cost for a single in-person visit was €165.89 (SD 144.81) and €66.32 (SD 76.80) for a VC. The cost breakdown for an F2F visit includes €142.58 (SD 131.98) for productivity losses, €26.12 (SD 61.27) for travel expenses and €8.91 (SD 18.99) for informal care. The mean cost for productivity loss and loss of leisure time for the companion, attributed to F2F visit, was €112.50 (SD 106.51). Total cost for all F2F visits amounted to €147 375.

Cost associated with one VC was €66.32 (SD 76.80) for productivity loss and €4.17 (SD 14.69) for informal care, and companion-related time costs €50.35 (SD 58.42). Travel expenses were negligible for VC. Total cost for all 95 VCs was €6373. See online supplemental table S1 for cost breakdown.

For calculation of travel expenses, 34 patients were excluded due to the unavailability of home addresses or addresses registered overseas. Travel expenses mainly comprised car travel, accounting for 87.0% (724/832) of patients, with a total travel distance of 54 452 km and 67 km (SD 63.98) on average per patient and 69 min (SD 46.3). Mean travel expense was highest for those travelling by taxi (28/832).

The distance patients with a video visit would have faced if required to travel to the hospital was 7623 km with an average of 80 per visit (SD 53.75) and 80 min (SD 54.0).

The GLM indicated that the cost of a surgical visit was €103.65 higher when provided F2F compared with VC in the total cohort (95% CI (85.07 to 122.23), p<0.001), regardless of type of specialty providing the visit. When cost differences were calculated for a visit provided by the GI-oncological subspecialty in model 2, the cost of an F2F was €108.88 higher (95% CI (90.85 to 126.91), p<0.001) than a VC. Age and low household income were associated with cost differences in models 1 and 2 as well. Cost differences between in-person visits from the three subspecialties were associated with age, male sex and secondary education (table 2).

Discussion

This study comprehensively defines the total costs incurred by patients and their accompanying relatives for visiting a tertiary surgical clinic both in-person and by video. In-person visit costs range from €36.02 to €1172.69, mainly due to productivity loss, whereas video visits save patients and companions half the amount of time off from work and over €105 per visit. Costs did not seem to differ between income classes, indicating an imbalance in financial burden to the detriment of individuals with lower incomes. Findings may increase awareness among clinicians and staff about the economic consequences of scheduling an in-person appointment, particularly for those with low income, living further from the clinic, relying on the assistance of companions or public transport and needing repeat visits.

This study brings a novel perspective to previous cost studies by considering companion and informal care expenses and defining costs associated with surgical visits. It differentiates from prior studies on surgical visits that primarily focused on travel expenses of companions, not costs associated with their productivity loss, due to a lack of data on employment status.30,32 Here, nearly 60% of patients were accompanied by individuals, many of whom were required to take time off from work to join the visit. Failing to include these costs grossly underestimates total expenses at patient (and visit) level. Companions are often essential not only for travel but also for emotional support, comprehension and decision making.33 Support by a companion seems well established and independent of visit modality. In half of the video visits, a companion, commonly a spouse, was present, aligning with similar occurrences in other studies.34 35

Less than 10% of the entire cohort arranged childcare or informal care. This cost item has not been included in previous cost calculations for an outpatient visit, but only suggested as a barrier for adherence to, for example, postsurgical follow-up.36 37 Our study’s conservative estimate might explain the relatively low cost of informal care per visit, but costs of professional childcare or disabled care can be substantial. At the individual patient level, informal care may count and should be addressed in future cost calculations in a more detailed and individualised approach.

Video visits still come with notable costs, mainly from productivity loss. However, patients and companions are significantly less likely to be absent from work during a video visit, often arranging visits during work hours. In case there is a need to arrange time off from work, this was typically half a workday or less. The difference in projected time off from work reflects the different expectations and concerns patients have with each modality. Contrary to a virtual visit, in-person visits entail uncertainties about travel time, parking availability, waiting and unplanned diagnostics or interventions. Visit encounter time, including waiting, did not differ between visit modalities in our study, although video visits had smaller ranges. This is similar to what is found in a clinical trial on video visits in patients after low-risk surgery.38 The encounter time calculation did not allow for differentiating waiting from actual visit time. One could argue that the duration of a video visit is shorter due to the inability of physical examination and less ‘small talk’.39 Conversely, duration could be prolonged by technical hurdles and more extensive interactional alignment.23 Significant virtual waiting was observed, likely attributed to mixing both modalities in the clinician’s schedule. One could argue that virtually waiting is more comfortable than in-person; however, it adds to productivity loss. Implementing separate video visit, clinic hours in dedicated areas could enhance workflow and reduce virtual waiting time.40

Round-trip travel time exceeded an hour, which is still short considering the tertiary referral nature of the clinic. Travel time, influenced by population and geography, affects the calculated travel time saved by a video visit across various surgical studies. A study in upper extremity surgery patients, conducted at an academic level-I trauma centre in Danville (USA) covering a large service area of approximately 25 000 square miles, reported 85 min travel time saved, whereas 39 min were saved in a vascular surgery patient cohort at a tertiary centre in Detroit.41 42

Besides time and financial benefits of video visits for patients, the reduction of CO2 emissions has important environmental and societal implications. Here, patient-travel emission savings in the video visit group were 1329 kg CO2−eq and 14 kg CO2−eq on average per video visit (CO2 emission factor of 0.17431/km of an average petrol-burning car).43 This concords with the reported 11.2 kg emission savings of a vascular surgery video visit and the same greenhouse gas emission savings of orthopaedic and trauma surgery video visits.32 41 Although there are other carbon-intensive hotspots in the surgical setting,44 such as material production and consumables,45 46 transportation-related carbon emissions from patient travel—which are significantly mitigated when shifting from in-person to video—will have a large impact on reducing the healthcare sectors’ carbon footprint and expenses for society.47 48

For a fair comparison, however, the IT’s footprint associated with a video visit should be considered as well, with internet data use and consumed energy being the main contributors.49 50 1 hour of video call from a desktop emits 52 g CO2, equivalent to travelling less than 1.5 km.51

The environmental benefits of VC are particularly relevant when considering the ‘alleged’ frequent need for in-person visits for certain vulnerable patient groups, such as those with advanced cancer or elderly. Studies show that patients with advanced GI cancers spend a quarter of their remaining time on in-person contact with their healthcare providers.52 Similarly, older adults in traditional Medicare spend an average of 3 weeks per year receiving ambulatory care.53 Shifting visits to video could greatly reduce the environmental impact of patients and companions travel.

Within the subgroup of in-person visits, both age and sex are associated with visit cost. With increasing age, the percentage of retired patients rises, which leads to a reduction in productivity loss costs. However, this is not compensated by an increase in leisure loss costs. The effect of sex might be explained by gender-related differences in behaviour: men were less likely to bring companions and took less time off work for an in-person visit compared with women (data not shown), which may reflect differences in expectations and perceived value of a visit. In routine medical visits, women tend to bring companions more frequently.54

The strength of this study lies in the size and diversity of our surgical patients’ sample, the inclusion of overlooked cost components and companions and the cost comparison between in-person and video visits in a patient group with comparable needs and expectations. Additionally, travel expenses were calculated using true, rather than orthodromic distances. Altogether, this comprehensive cost calculation approximates the actual costs and savings by a video visit. Limitations of this study relate to design, execution and findings.

The study was conducted in a tertiary academic hospital in a small, densely populated country with a strong digital infrastructure, a widespread public transport and a universal social security system. These contextual factors limit generalisability to regions with different digital (and healthcare) infrastructures. Technology-associated costs, such as access to smartphones and internet, were not included in the analysis. This corresponds with a direction in the costing manual being taken caution to include general-purpose costs. We are confident that the proportional share of technology in the total costs remains low with broader implementation of video visits in the region and Netherlands. This is supported by the county’s high level of digital maturity: in 2023, 98% of the Dutch citizens used internet daily, and in 2024, 83% had at least basic digital skills.55 56 Nevertheless, the availability of reliable and affordable video technology and the ability to master the particulars necessary to have a successful video visit should be pondered in cost calculation of a video visit in digitally vulnerable populations.

Video visits were limited to GI-oncological surgery patients, mostly postoperative follow-up, potentially affecting the presence of relatives and work absence, considering the generally short duration and limited emotional impact of these visits. Further, the response rate to questionnaire invitations in the video visit group was low, contrary to the high completion rate (n=95/125, 76.0%), potentially leading to self-selection bias with inclusion of, for example, patients declining to respond for privacy reasons. The pandemic context, with patients receiving numerous questionnaires outside of research, could contribute to questionnaire fatigue and further impact response rates.57

This study provides a comprehensive overview of costs incurred by patients and their companions for a surgical visit, both in-person and by video. Costs related to productivity loss and informal care are considerable, particularly considering the additional loss for relatives accompanying a patient. Video visits come with about half of in-person visit costs with potential for further savings by decreasing virtual encounter time. Substantiating hidden costs borne by patients and companions may increase awareness among clinicians, policymakers and health insurance companies about the individual and societal burden associated with outpatient care. This could support more informed decisions about appropriate use of different visit modalities.

Future research should explore how visit modalities affect patient experiences and outcomes, as this may influence the overall quality of care. Structured evaluations, such as clinical audits, could offer deeper insight into how care is currently organised, including visit frequency, modality use and underlying rationale across patient groups. These insights can guide implementation strategies by identifying care pathways where video visits are effectively embedded and where uptake remains limited while also addressing resource and financial constraints. Noteworthy is the assessment of cost share associated with purchase of necessary equipment, ICT access and training in patient populations with low digital literacy. To improve generalisability and identify context-specific barriers and facilitators, future work should extend this approach to other hospitals and clinical specialties, within, for example, broader quality improvement Projects to support further improvement of care.
