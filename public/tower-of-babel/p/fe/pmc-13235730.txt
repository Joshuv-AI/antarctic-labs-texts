Why was the cohort set up?

Pregnancy is a critical period of development, and maternal experiences and exposures before and during pregnancy can impact both the course of the pregnancy and the offspring’s lifelong cardiometabolic and neurodevelopmental outcomes [1]. The Ontario Birth Study (OBS) was designed to allow longitudinal investigation of the relationships between exposures during pregnancy and complications that develop, as well as children’s birth and neurodevelopmental outcomes. It also facilitates research into molecular mechanisms and maternal health during pregnancy.

Established in 2013, this longitudinal pregnancy cohort with ongoing recruitment is embedded in routine antenatal clinical care primarily at Mount Sinai Hospital (MSH) in Toronto, Canada, which houses one of Canada’s largest maternal–infant health programs. This integration enables the collection of research biospecimens during clinical visits and the abstraction of data from medical records to supplement study questionnaires. The original cohort description [2] includes information on the collection of biospecimens and medical, lifestyle, and diet data during pregnancy through to 10 weeks postpartum. The child extension began following up with children from OBS pregnancies in 2018, extending the follow-up period to 54 months of child age. This current extension increases the comprehensive collection of longitudinal data as the children grow, using standardized and objective measures to track development. Funding is being sought to extend follow-up beyond 54 months. Additionally, it is feasible to track mothers and children through linkage with administrative databases, as consent and health insurance numbers have been obtained.

The goal of this study is to understand the relationships of pregnancy exposures to long-term health outcomes in mothers and offspring. This new knowledge allows the development of strategies to improve women’s and infants’ health, reducing chronic diseases. Collecting data on broad exposures and outcomes alongside longitudinal biospecimen collection has allowed researchers to identify biomarkers for critical pregnancy complications and children’s early-life and health trajectory. The study’s continuous recruitment and longitudinal nature also allows the investigation of contemporary issues in real time, including the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and legalization of cannabis in Canada.

The core operation of this study is funded by grants from the Canadian Institutes for Health Research, the Lunenfeld-Tanenbaum Research Institute, the Sinai Health Foundation, the Obstetrics-Gynaecology departments of MSH, and the University of Toronto.

Who is in the cohort?

As of 1 April 2025, there were a total of 5681 participants, including 3674 unique mothers with 3897 pregnancies and 2007 participating children with at least one follow-up completed. Obstetrics patients are recruited from a subset of antenatal clinics at MSH. They are approached at an early antenatal appointment by study staff and informed about the OBS. These patients are eligible for the study if they: (i) are approached before 17 weeks gestational age (GA), (ii) are ≥18 years old, (iii) have a viable pregnancy, (iv) are receiving antenatal care at MSH, and (v) intend to deliver at MSH. In this cohort, 3555 pregnancies from 3358 unique mothers resulted in live-born neonates, including 81 live-born twins. After withdrawals and neonatal demises, 3526 live-born participant–child dyads were considered for childhood follow-ups at 8, 24, 36, and 54 months of age. They were considered eligible for these follow-ups if they: (i) had a live-born child, (ii) had completed the first questionnaire during pregnancy, and (iii) consented to future contact. From the dyads, 643 were ineligible and a further 45 of the children were too young for the first follow-up, leaving 2838 participant–child dyads eligible for contact. By 1 April 2025, 2007 of the eligible dyads had a parent who had consented to child follow-ups and completed at least one (71%). How often have they been followed up?

OBS participants are followed up throughout their pregnancy and into postpartum. Self-administered study questionnaires collect a wide range of information. The first questionnaire (Q1) is completed between 12 and 20 weeks GA, the second (Q2) between 26 and 32 weeks GA, and the last (Q3) between 6 and 10 weeks postpartum. The Canadian Diet History Questionnaire [3] (C-DHQ-II, https://epi.grants.cancer.gov/dhq2/) is completed between 19 and 25 weeks GA. Clinical data from antenatal visits and delivery are abstracted from medical records. Maternal biospecimens are collected alongside routine clinical samples at 11–15 weeks GA, 15–19 weeks GA, 24–28 weeks GA, and at delivery. More details on the OBS study process prior to the child follow-up extension have been previously reported [2].

Following the questionnaire at 10 weeks postpartum, parent participants are invited to complete interviews with study staff at 8 months (acceptable window: 7–9 months), 24 months (23–28 months), 36 months (35–40 months), and 54 months (48–55 months), mostly by telephone. When feasible, in-person visits are conducted at 24 and 54 months at the homes of study participants where anthropometric measurements are performed and parental–child interaction and child neurodevelopment are assessed as described below. Over the course of the study, there have been 65 withdrawals during pregnancy and 101 who did not wish to be contacted after the postpartum period. Of the participants completing the protocol up to the postpartum period, 71% of those eligible continued to participate, with between 62% and 76% of those invited to participate completing each follow-up. Figure 1 provides more details.

What has been measured?

During pregnancy, clinical data are manually abstracted from patient charts in PowerChart and participants complete questionnaires covering demographics, health, lifestyle, environment (Q1–3) stored on REDCap [3, 4], and diet (C-DHQ-II) [5]. Q1 collects maternal demographics data, information on the pre-pregnancy period, and early-pregnancy exposures, in which participants are asked to self-report their race from categories used by Statistics Canada [6]. Q2 adds paternal demographics, updates on exposures during the pregnancy period, and more information on maternal mental health. Q3 covers newborn information, updates on maternal condition, and breastfeeding. A summary of data fields collected from Q1–3 appears in Supplementary Table 2. Medical record abstraction includes baseline data about the mother and previous pregnancies and conditions, new diagnoses during antenatal care, a detailed record of the labour and delivery, and physician assessment of the baby. Details on the extracted data are provided in Supplementary Table 3. Table 2 summarizes the pregnancy outcomes and cohort measures.

The Biospecimen Repository—a College of American Pathologists-accredited and Canadian Tissue Repository Network-certified lab at the Lunenfeld-Tanenbaum Research Institute—stores OBS biospecimens for future research using BioBank3 to manage aliquot and dispatch details. Maternal blood collected at the time points noted above are fractionated into plasma, serum, buffy coats, and dried blood spots on protein saver cards. Also stored are infant blood spot cards collected 24 hours after birth during the routine provincial newborn screening collection.

During the child follow-ups, interview questions cover the child’s health, their activities, and family health, and are described in Supplementary Table 4. Standardized developmental measures supplement data collection at 24 and 54 months. The 24-month visit includes the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised (M-CHAT-R) [7] and the Ages and Stages Questionnaire, Third Edition (ASQ-3) [8]. An additional assessment of maternal sensitivity is also conducted in which the parent guides the child through the completion of a task [9]. A video recording of the process is made and the mother’s responsiveness to their child is subsequently coded by using a standard approach. At 54 months, the children complete the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Toolbox’s Early Child Cognition Battery—a measure of their ability to gain knowledge and comprehension—and the parents complete the Parent-Proxy Emotion Battery for their child—a set of measures detailing emotional awareness and control [10]. A summary of the children enrolled can be found in Table 3, which, together with Fig. 2, describes some outcomes of interest in the cohort using these standardized tools. Child saliva samples for DNA analysis are collected during home visits at 24 and 54 months (298 and 775 collections, respectively).

What has it found?

The OBS research platform has four main focuses of research: molecular mechanisms during pregnancy, pregnancy complications, maternal health, and early-childhood development.

Molecular mechanisms

Investigation into molecular mechanisms has provided insight into protein expression and DNA methylation. Researchers found that higher maternal ages are correlated with changes in serotonin-related protein expression in the placenta [11]. Analysis of newborn blood has identified that the use of corticosteroids during the antenatal period results in alterations in the DNA methylation profiles of genes affecting neurodevelopment [12, 13].

Pregnancy complications

Given the longitudinal nature of the OBS, researchers have investigated biomarkers and exposures throughout the pregnancy when looking at pre-eclampsia, small-for-gestational age (SGA) babies, and preterm births, all of which can impact the success of the pregnancy and the baby’s growth.

Investigations that focused on pre-eclampsia have found that elevated levels of fibronectin from placenta-derived small extracellular vesicles are a biomarker [14] and increased ferroptosis occurs in the placenta in pre-eclampsia [15]. Researchers have also found specific lipid signatures from placenta-derived small extracellular vesicles in maternal blood [16]; maternal cannabis use in early pregnancy [17] and maternal acetaminophen use solely in the pre-pregnancy period [18] are linked to SGA births.

Preterm births can have short- and long-term consequences for the baby and mother, and the OBS cohort has been used to investigate aetiologies of preterm birth. Studies of OBS biospecimens have identified specific microRNAs in extracellular vesicles [19, 20], peripheral monocytes [19], and whole blood [19], and messenger RNA signatures in maternal blood [21] and peripheral monocytes [21] that are predictive of preterm birth. Elevated plasma concentrations of interleukin-10 (IL10), interleukin-8, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, and tumour necrosis factor alpha were found to be associated with preterm labour, although increased walking time lowered IL10 levels [22]. Pregnant individuals who had a preterm birth were found to have richer and more diverse vaginal microbiomes than matched controls who had delivered at term [23].

Maternal health

Antenatal and postpartum depressions are relatively common experiences for pregnant individuals and are a critical area of research due to their impact on mother and child. The use of assisted reproductive technology in the OBS has been associated with lower levels of depression and anxiety symptoms [24] in pregnant individuals in weeks 24–28 of their pregnancy compared with those with non-assisted conception, though this association does not extend past delivery [25]. When a comparison of OBS data before and during the COVID-19 pandemic was used, those who were pregnant for any length of time during the pandemic reported an increase in depression and anxiety symptoms in the postpartum period, but not during pregnancy [26].

Child-development outcomes

A myriad of factors during pregnancy could affect child development, including maternal health during the pregnancy. In the OBS, major maternal depressive symptoms in early pregnancy have been associated with impaired child executive functioning at 54 months [27]. Maternal depression and anxiety symptoms in the second trimester have also been linked to an increased number of child awakenings at 3 years of age [28]. Children exposed to the COVID pandemic exhibited improved problem-solving skills but more personal–social difficulties compared with children not exposed to the pandemic at 24 months [29]. At 54 months of age, children with some pandemic exposure also performed better on cognitive tests than children who were tested prior to the pandemic [29].

Where biospecimens have been analysed, these results are returned to the OBS platform for use by other researchers. A full list of published papers that use OBS resources can be found at https://ontariobirthstudy.com/publications/.

What are the main strengths and weaknesses?

This study has many strengths. With comprehensive, prospective data collection at multiple time points during pregnancy and early childhood, the OBS provides a valuable research platform for a broad spectrum of research questions related to the developmental origins of health and disease. Serial biospecimens collected longitudinally empower researchers to address questions related to critical periods of development and to characterize biomarker dynamics related to a specific outcome or phenotype. The OBS is integrated into the healthcare system, providing objective data from healthcare professionals. This study is one of the few birth cohorts that conduct home visits, allowing the collection of detailed and standardized data on parental–child interactions and children’s neurodevelopment by using validated instruments (such as the NIH Toolbox) that are often not available in other birth cohorts. The NIH Toolbox cognitive battery is a direct assessment of the child and not dependent on parent report. In addition, the use of standardized, validated measures, such as the ASQ-3, allows comparisons with other populations through tools commonly used by researchers and practitioners. Retention of 71% of eligible children into the 5-year child follow-up demonstrates the enthusiasm of participants for the OBS study and strong commitment from participants enables long-term follow-up. Initiation of the study in early pregnancy enables researchers to assess in utero exposures and how they impact child development. Ongoing recruitment allows adaptation to assess contemporary changes in the environment or legislation in real time (e.g. COVID pandemic, the legalization of cannabis) on pregnancies and child development. This study collects consent from participants to link to external databases, providing a valuable ability to link to administrative health data by using Ontario Health Insurance Plan numbers and to geospatial data by using postal codes as the children grow older. To our knowledge, this is the largest active prospective birth cohort in Canada, offering broad data and biospecimen collection at repeated time points, supporting the analysis of critical exposure windows.

There are also some weaknesses. It is not known whether the patients who were approached and consented to the study differ from those who declined, as data could not be collected from those who did not take part. As with many cohorts, participants tend to have a higher level of education [85% bachelor’s or higher vs 54% (2021 Canadian census)] [30] and income compared with childbearing-age (20–44 years) women in Toronto [2] and generalization should be undertaken with caution. The impact of this issue will vary depending on the research question; however, it would not affect the internal validity. Much of the data are self-reported or parent-reported about children, bearing the associated limitations. As the main participants are mothers, paternal data were more limited and provided by the mothers. Q3 in the postpartum period has a 59% completion rate, which is lower than those of the other study questionnaires, which may be expected, as newborns require more of the parents’ time. Of the families invited to take part in interviews during childhood, 62%–76% of them participate—a potential attrition bias. Infant blood spot card collection rates are lower than those of other biospecimens, as collection occurs on the busy neonatal ward and some parents have additional reservations about providing biological data on their children. This is mitigated by collecting child saliva samples during the interviews at 24 and 54 months.

Can I get hold of the data? Where can I find out more?

Data and biospecimens collected by the OBS are available to interested researchers by applying through the OBS website. All proposals are reviewed by the OBS Steering Committee for scientific merit and compliance with participants’ consent. External researchers require a local investigator for Research Ethics Board Review and a data/material-transfer agreement. More information, the preliminary request form, and cost-recovery fees are available at https://ontariobirthstudy.com/researchers/. Questions are welcome at OntarioBirthStudy.MSH@sinaihealth.ca.
