Model-based semantic distance reveals adaptive coordination of distinct cognitive systems in flexible knowledge retrieval
Authors: Kaixiang Zhuang, Xinyu Liang, Jonathan Smallwood, Elizabeth Jefferies, Deniz Vatansever
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flexible cognition requires the adaptive retrieval of conceptual knowledge, spanning a continuum from proximal to distal semantic associations. However, the neural dynamics that facilitate this flexibility remain poorly understood. Here, combining computational linguistics with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and machine learning methods, we derive a whole-brain signature that captures graded variations in semantic distance. This domain-specific neural model revealed three distinct large-scale cognitive systems whose interactions coordinate semantic retrieval: a left-lateralised frontotemporal language and a bilateral frontoparietal control network, both recruited for distant associations, and a medial default mode memory network, facilitating access to proximal relations. Importantly, we show that adaptive retrieval across the continuum of semantic distance is facilitated by a dynamic coordination mechanism. As semantic distance increases, representational patterns converge across the three cognitive systems. These findings provide a unifying model of the neural architecture underlying semantic processing, revealing how dynamic interactions between competing cognitive systems enable flexible knowledge retrieval.

Introduction

Semantic knowledge provides the conceptual framework that enables us to understand and interact with the world around us1. From language comprehension to social interactions2,3, flexible retrieval of semantic information underlies our ability to ascribe meaning to our daily experiences and ongoing thoughts4–6, showing significant links to general intelligence and healthy aging7,8. The critical importance of this retrieval process is further revealed in disorders that impact access to semantic memory, such as semantic dementia or aphasia, leading to impairments in communication and social engagement9. As such, understanding the neural architecture that supports flexible knowledge retrieval remains essential not only for cognitive neurosciences, but also for developing targeted clinical interventions3,10.

The neural basis of semantic retrieval has been traditionally characterized through clinical observations of semantic impairment, and neuroimaging studies investigating neural responses under high semantic control demands. Converging evidence from these approaches indicates that controlled semantic retrieval consistently engages a language-rooted left-lateralised frontotemporal network, with key contributions from the left inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), implicated in controlled selection among competing semantic representations, and the posterior middle temporal gyrus (pMTG), which supports context-dependent retrieval of conceptual information3,9,10. Neuroimaging-based meta-analytic work has refined this account by identifying additional involvement of the bilateral dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) and anterior insula, reflecting the broader contribution of domain-general control processes, associated with the multiple-demand network (MDN), to the retrieval of semantic information under high executive demands11,12.

Beyond this well-characterized frontotemporal architecture, however, emerging studies also implicate an extended set of brain areas within key default mode network (DMN) regions in the retrieval of semantic knowledge13. While traditionally implicated in internally directed processes such as autobiographical memory14 and mindwandering15 during idle states of cognition, default mode engagement within semantic tasks is postulated to reflect specific contributions to integrative, automatic conceptual processing. For example, disruption to the angular gyrus, a key default mode hub, has been shown to selectively impair automatic retrieval of proximal semantic associations while leaving controlled retrieval intact16. Moreover, functional engagement across distributed default mode hubs, including the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), has been reported to track automatic access to dominant semantic memory traces17. Collectively, these findings align with systems-level neural architectures proposed for semantic cognition18,19, which draw on key regions across large-scale brain networks covering not only a functionally specialized language network, but also key contributions from domain-general multiple-demand and default mode networks20.

However, current accounts provide limited insight into how these systems adaptively coordinate their activity as semantic retrieval demands systematically fluctuate in our environments. Real-world conceptual access operates along a continuous gradient of conceptual knowledge such that increasing semantic distance shifts retrieval demands from an automatic toward a more effortful resolution of distal concepts21,22. Existing neuroimaging studies have predominantly characterized semantic retrieval through discrete experimental contrasts, comparing strong versus weak associations23,24. Consequently, the coordinated actions of key brain hubs under variable retrieval demands have remained poorly understood. Addressing this gap requires an integrative framework that captures both brain-wide activity patterns and cross-system representational dynamics during knowledge retrieval across the full length of the semantic continuum.

Recent advances in natural language processing (NLP), particularly within distributed word embedding models, now provide a principled and data-driven framework for quantifying semantic similarity along a continuous scale25,26. Within the larger field of semantic cognition, such model-based metrics have been previously employed to characterize conceptual content and the representational geometry of semantic knowledge across participants, tasks, and imaging modalities27–30. By quantifying stimulus-level semantic distance, these models allow for the parameterization of retrieval demands in a continuous manner, enabling the complementary use of advanced analytical approaches for a more precise characterization of the neural systems that differentially predict retrieval across the full range of conceptual associations. For example, machine-learning-based multivariate predictive modelling may allow the identification of brain-wide activation patterns that continuously covary with semantic distance31–33, while representational similarity analysis (RSA) provides a principled framework for probing how local representations adapt as retrieval demands increase34–36. Together, these techniques offer a more robust means of investigating how distributed neural systems encode and coordinate their activity during flexible knowledge retrieval across the semantic continuum.

Leveraging these advances, here we integrated NLP-based word embedding models with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and machine learning methods to precisely delineate the organisation and interaction of cortical systems supporting the retrieval of conceptual knowledge across the continuum of semantic associations. We first establish that semantic distance successfully predicts behavioural retrieval costs shared across individuals, with greater distance reflecting greater retrieval demands. We then employ a multivariate predictive modelling framework to derive a whole-brain neural signature that tracks continuous semantic distance, while demonstrating its generalisability across independent fMRI datasets and its specificity to semantic over domain-general executive control processes. Finally, we decompose the identified neural signature to reveal distinct large-scale systems and their interactive representational dynamics that collectively govern memory retrieval across the continuum of semantic associations. Together, our results provide a unifying model that precisely maps the adaptive neural architecture of semantic retrieval. In addition to improving our mechanistic understanding of the neural processes underlying semantic memory, our results may inform the development of more precise diagnostic markers and rehabilitation strategies for semantic memory disorders.

For robustness and generalisability, participants were divided into two groups, each completing the task with independent stimulus sets of 80 non-overlapping word pairs. Importantly, the semantic distances between probe and target words were computationally quantified via NLP-based word embedding models, enabling the definition of a continuum of semantic associations that best predicted participants’ behavioural costs and thus the variable control demands placed on semantic memory retrieval processes (Fig. 1b-c). Model-based semantic distance reliably captures flexible retrieval demands

Our initial analysis aimed to identify the word embedding model that best explained participants’ performance in the 3-AFC task, thereby capturing the continuum of varying retrieval demands. To achieve this, we selected five candidate NLP models representing diverse methodological approaches: prediction-based neural networks (Word2Vec and FastText), global word co-occurrence statistics (GloVe), knowledge-base integration (ConceptNet Numberbatch), and graph-based methods (RWSGwn). For each model, the degree of semantic associations was quantified via cosine distance (1 – cosine similarity) between vectors of probe-target word pairs. This computational linguistic approach enabled the quantification of continuous semantic distances across two independent stimulus sets, which showed normal distributions ranging from strong to weak associations (Fig. 1c). Importantly, associations between the distractors and the probe-target word pairs were minimized (Supplementary Fig. S2), ensuring their negligible influence on retrieval demands.

Across both stimulus sets, participants made highly accurate responses (List A: mean ± SD accuracy = 95.20% ± 3.28%; List B: mean ± SD accuracy = 92.08% ± 3.72%). Given this high accuracy and in line with prior investigations, we focused on reaction times across each trial as the main proxy measure, denoting control demands placed on semantic retrieval processes. When comparing the mean reaction times of split-half data, we observed that participants exhibited highly consistent retrieval patterns across different word pairs. In particular, the results demonstrated that the mean estimate of split-half correlation coefficients for reaction times across word pairs was significantly larger than the surrogate correlation coefficients in the two independent stimulus sets (List A: r = 0.82, bootstrapped 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.823 − 0.825, p < 0.0001; List B: r = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.812 − 0.813, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2a). Next, we examined the link between semantic distances and reaction times to identify the word embedding model that best characterized semantic retrieval processes. Assuming that greater semantic distances require higher retrieval demands, we expected participants to exhibit longer reaction times for word pairs with greater semantic distances. Supporting this hypothesis, we found that semantic distances across all word embedding models significantly predicted reaction times in both stimulus sets (optimal performance in List A: r = 0.46 − 0.59; in List B: r = 0.51 − 0.71; q < 0.05, false discovery rate [FDR] corrected). Across the five chosen models, ConceptNet Numberbatch demonstrated consistently superior predictive power (Fig. 2b), underscoring its previously shown advantage in modelling patterns of human behaviour37.

Interestingly, optimal model performance occurred when word vectors were made sparser to retain approximately 48% of weights, maximizing correlation across both lists of words and thereby resulting in the best behavioural fit. Based on these findings, we defined semantic distances calculated from ConceptNet Numberbatch sparse word vectors (retaining the strongest 48% of weights) as the optimal metric for reflecting the continuum of semantic retrieval demands. A general linear model (GLM) further indicated that only the quantified semantic distance between probe and target successfully predicted participants’ reaction times, while other experimental factors, such as the distance between probe and distractors or the hubness properties of probe words, did not demonstrate significant predictive power (List A: β = 0.59, 95% CI = 0.41 − 0.78, t(74) = 6.22, p < 0.001; List B: β = 0.68, 95% CI = 0.52 − 0.85, t(74) = 8.34, p < 0.001; Fig. 2c).

In summary, participants exhibited consistent behavioural patterns of semantic memory retrieval with robust reaction time reliability, indicating the existence of a latent semantic space that is largely shared across individuals. In line with this observation, despite the superior predictive power of ConceptNet, all NLP models exhibited acceptable performance. These findings underscore the utility of word embedding models in reflecting human behaviour during semantic retrieval, providing a continuous metric to assess the neural basis of variable control demands placed on semantic memory retrieval processes. This approach contrasts with the commonly employed binary categorisations and establishes a solid foundation for our subsequent predictive modelling efforts.

A robust neural signature predicts the continuum of semantic associations

Having demonstrated the ability of NLP-based continuous semantic distances in reliably and accurately capturing participants’ behavioural performance in the 3-AFC task, our next aim was to uncover a robust neural signature of semantic memory retrieval that is sensitive to systematic variations in semantic distance, and thus the control demands placed on semantic retrieval processes. In order to achieve this, we utilized a multivariate predictive modelling framework31. In particular, we employed trial-specific fMRI response estimations and Thresholded Partial Least Squares (T-PLS)38 to build a whole-brain predictive model of NLP-generated semantic distances. The neural features with significant contributions to the signature were then spatially overlapped with Haufe-transformed activation map (Supplementary Fig. S3) to define a set of critical regions39.

Using this comprehensive analytical framework, we identified a robust whole-brain neural signature of fMRI activation patterns that captured fine-grained variations in semantic distance across stimuli, incorporating the complete stimulus sets from both lists of word pairs (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. S4). This neural signature revealed a strong left-lateralised network for greater semantic distances that placed higher demands for controlled retrieval of semantic information, encompassing many regions of the semantic control network previously described in neuroimaging meta-analyses11,12. In particular, this model involved left-lateralised regions in the frontal cortex including the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, inferior frontal junction, and frontal operculum, as well as temporal and parietal regions. Bilateral structures including the anterior ventral insular cortex and inferior frontal sulcus were also engaged in this process. Moreover, the subcortical results emphasized a right-lateralised cerebellar involvement in more controlled semantic retrieval (Supplementary Fig. S5a). Specifically, activation was observed at the junction of lobules VI, Crus I, and Crus II. In contrast to highly controlled retrieval demands, however, more proximal distances, thus more automatic retrieval of dominant semantic associations, were linked to greater activation in a set of largely right-lateralised medial brain regions. Beyond isolated brain areas, however, the connectivity-based neural correspondence analysis of the semantic distance signature revealed distinct system-level selectivity that is grounded in the large-scale intrinsic organisation of the human brain (Supplementary Fig. S5b). Highly controlled retrieval across greater semantic distances was primarily associated with increased engagement of the language and frontoparietal networks. Conversely, more automatic retrieval of dominant associations within proximal semantic distances was linked to greater contribution of regions corresponding to the DMN. Together, these results highlight multiple neural networks that are associated with semantic memory retrieval across disparate ends of a continuum of semantic associations.

For a rigorous evaluation of the identified model-based semantic distance signature, we next performed a comprehensive battery of tests assessing the influence of computational approaches on model definition as well as the model’s predictive accuracy, generalisability, stability, and specificity (Supplementary Fig. S1).

To begin with, we validated the robustness of the neural signature using an alternative Support Vector Regression (SVR) model. The results demonstrated high congruence between computational approaches, confirming that the signature reflects genuine neurobiological substrates rather than methodological artefacts (Supplementary Fig. S6). Subsequently, we focused on the identified neural pattern’s predictive accuracy, and conducted evaluations separately within two independent stimulus sets (Fig. 3b). The within-sample cross-validation results indicated that the neural model accurately predicted the semantic distance across participants in both stimulus sets: for List A, individual-level r = 0.48 ± 0.03 (mean ± standard error [SE]), mean squared error (MSE) = 0.072 ± 0.003 SE, coefficient of determination (R2) = 0.15 ± 0.04 SE; group-level r = 0.48, MSE = 0.072, R2 = 0.15; for List B, individual-level r = 0.51 ± 0.02 SE, MSE = 0.067 ± 0.003 SE, R2 = 0.21 ± 0.03 SE; group-level r = 0.51, MSE = 0.067, R2 = 0.21 (Fig. 3c). In addition, an inner cross-validation revealed identical T-PLS tuning parameters, including feature sparsity and component numbers for both stimulus sets (Supplementary Fig. S7), as well as high spatial overlap between the two models (Dice coefficient = 0.96 between FDR-corrected z-statistic maps).

Beyond within-sample cross-validation, models trained on one stimulus set also displayed generalisability to another. That is, the model trained on List A accurately predicted data from List B: individual-level r = 0.52 ± 0.02, MSE = 0.066 ± 0.002 SE, R2 = 0.22 ± 0.03 SE; group-level r = 0.52, MSE = 0.066, R2 = 0.22, and vice versa: individual-level r = 0.50 ± 0.02, MSE = 0.067 ± 0.002 SE, R2 = 0.21 ± 0.03 SE, group-level r = 0.50, MSE = 0.067, R2 = 0.21 (Fig. 3d). Finally, to characterize the temporal dynamics of the neural signature within the context of the employed task, we calculated the signature response trajectory across the trial interval. The signature expression emerged approximately 2 s after stimulus onset and peaked around 10 s, consistent with a canonical hemodynamic response. This stimulus-locked temporal profile suggests that the signature is driven mainly by trial-evoked processing during task performance as opposed to pre-trial preparatory states or a post-response motor artefact (Fig. 3e).

With the aim of providing further validation to the identified neural signature, we additionally assessed the model’s cross-paradigm generalisability and specificity in an independent fMRI dataset40, which contained both semantic and executive control tasks. In the semantic control task, bilingual participants named pictures in either their native language (L1; low demand) or second language (L2; high demand). In the executive control task, they responded to arrow directions with key presses that were either congruent (low demand) or incongruent (high demand) with the required response (Fig. 3f). The results demonstrated that the neural response of the identified semantic distance signature in our study was able to accurately classify high versus low retrieval demands in the semantic control task (accuracy = 84 ± 4.1% SE, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 1.34), but failed to distinguish between different conditions in the executive control task (accuracy = 49 ± 5.7% SE, p = 0.99, Cohen’s d = 0.20). Furthermore, even when considering all condition types, the signature response could accurately distinguish between semantic control and executive control processes (accuracy = 97 ± 1.4% SE, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 2.49).

When taken together, the results indicate that the identified neural signature exhibits strong stability and sensitivity to semantic processing signals. In addition to reliably predicting semantic distances within the same sample, its predictive performance generalises across diverse samples, task paradigms, datasets, and scanning devices. Furthermore, despite containing key hubs of domain-general systems, this neural signature demonstrates high specificity to semantic control demands, showing minimal sensitivity to general executive control tasks. These findings collectively demonstrate a robust and distributed neural signature for semantic memory retrieval within the 3-AFC paradigm.

Neural signature of semantic distance consists of three distinct cognitive systems

Leveraging this robust semantic distance signature, we subsequently aimed to delineate the cognitive systems underpinning retrieval of conceptual knowledge across the semantic continuum. For this, regions of interest (ROIs) were defined based on parcels from the Multi-Modal Parcellation (MMP) atlas that showed statistically significant contribution to the identified neural signature of semantic distance. Next, a NeuroSynth-based41 meta-analytic decoding (validated through the NeuroQuery database42) was performed, generating a positive-weighted functional matrix (terms × ROIs) that was decomposed via Orthogonal Projection Non-negative Matrix Factorization (OPNMF)43. The optimal decomposition revealed three distinct cognitive systems comprising the neural signature, each with unique functional profiles (Fig. 4b) and neuroanatomical distributions (Fig. 4c). These systems were labelled based on cognitive terms showing strongest associations, namely Language, Control, and Memory. The Language system was primarily associated with terms including language, comprehension, reading, and word recognition, suggesting its involvement in core language processing operations. The Control system showed strong loadings for task demands, maintenance, monitoring, and cognitive control, indicating its role in executive processes that regulate the controlled retrieval of semantic information. Finally, the Memory system was characterized by terms such as retrieval, semantic/episodic memory, recognition, and recall, reflecting processes related to accessing stored knowledge.

Importantly, hierarchical clustering of the ROIs within the identified neural signature revealed distinct neuroanatomical distributions for each cognitive system (Fig. 4d). The Language system primarily encompassed left-hemispheric regions including the superior temporal sulcus (STSdp), posterior temporal gyrus (PH), frontal opercular cortex (FOP5), and areas within the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (IFSp, IFJa, 47 l, 45, and 44). The Control system was distributed across bilateral regions, including left intraparietal sulcus (LIPd, IP0 and IP1), bilateral anterior ventral insular cortex (AVI), bilateral medial prefrontal area 8BM, right inferior frontal sulcus (IFSp), left inferior frontal junction (IFJp), and left premotor eye field (PEF). The Memory system predominantly engaged the right-lateralised posteromedial structures, encompassing angular gyrus (PF), parieto-occipital sulcus (POS1), dorsomedial parietal cortex (7 m), posterior cingulate (31a, 23c, v23ab, 31dp, 31pv), and ventral medial prefrontal cortex (p32). Activation within each of these three systems independently scaled with semantic distance, highlighting their distinct predictive contributions (Supplementary Fig. S8).

Moreover, network affiliations revealed that both the Language and the Memory systems corresponded to well-established intrinsic brain networks within the CAB-NP (Cole-Anticevic Brain-wide Network Partition) parcellation44. While the Language system primarily aligned with the canonical Language network, the Memory system demonstrated substantial overlap with the core DMN hubs. In contrast, the Control system showed a strong correspondence with the MDN, a specialized network associated with executive control processes45. These results demonstrated high consistency across decoding and decomposition analyses based on the NeuroQuery database (Supplementary Fig. S9-S10). Moreover, the results demonstrated further generalisability to the independent task fMRI dataset. While all three systems were significantly recruited during semantic control (L2 versus L1 naming), their recruitment patterns diverged under general executive demands, where only the Control system showed significant engagement (incongruent versus congruent arrow responses; Supplementary Fig. S11).

In summary, our meta-analytic decomposition revealed three distinct cognitive systems underlying semantic retrieval with dissociable functional profiles: a left-lateralised frontotemporal Language system, a bilateral frontoparietal Control system implementing executive control, and a medial Memory system facilitating knowledge representation. Together, the results indicate that the flexible recombination of these cognitive systems with unique functional profiles and neuroanatomical distributions may collectively underlie retrieval across the continuum of semantic associations.

Retrieval across the semantic continuum facilitates cross-system coordination

In a final step, we aimed to investigate representational dynamics of the identified cognitive systems to varying semantic retrieval processes across the continuum of semantic associations. Specifically, we asked whether the identified cognitive systems comprising the semantic distance signature exhibited significant changes in their representational patterns with varying semantic retrieval demands using an RSA approach34,35. Across the two stimulus sets, we found that the three cognitive systems within the identified neural signature could be significantly discriminated by their unique representations in both low-distance (accuracy = 90.54% ± 0.46% SE; p < 0.001, permutation test) and high-distance (accuracy = 78.23% ± 0.52% SE; p < 0.001) conditions. However, the representational patterns demonstrated less pronounced discrimination between cognitive systems during high-distance trials compared to low-distance trials (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 12), indicating more overlapping representations under high demand semantic retrieval.

In order to further characterize these changes in representational patterns, we quantified the relationships across the three cognitive systems by measuring representational connectivity between ROIs using Kendall’s τA rank correlation coefficient for pairwise pattern comparisons. We then employed linear mixed-effects models (LME) to analyse how these representational connections differed between low-distance and high-distance trials. Results revealed increasing cross-system representational connectivity as semantic retrieval demands increased. Specifically, representational connections between the Language system and both the Control and Memory systems strengthened with increasing semantic retrieval demand, with the Language-Control connection showing a pronounced enhancement (Fig. 5c). In particular, left pMTG (PH) exhibited the most substantial alterations in representational connectivity among all ROIs (Fig. 5d), with statistically significant enhancements in connections observed between this region and the right retrosplenial cortex (v23ab) within the Memory system, as well as multiple regions within the Control system. Although discrete low and high semantic distance conditions were employed in this analysis to ensure the statistical robustness of the RDMs, a sliding window approach (width = 30 trials, step size = 5 trials) also captured the continuous nature of these changes, demonstrating that key representational connections (Fig. 5c; p <.01) progressively strengthened as semantic distance increased across both stimulus sets in a graded and continuous manner (Supplementary Fig. S13).

Finally, to determine whether this enhanced cross-system representational connectivity reflects a semantic-specific mechanism as opposed to a domain-general amplification of cognitive effort, we assessed systems-level coordination dynamics within the independent task fMRI dataset. Specifically, we isolated the representational connections that were significantly strengthened during the 3-AFC task and evaluated their modulation across both semantic control (L2 versus L1 naming) and executive control (incongruent versus congruent arrow responses) paradigms. The results demonstrated that extensive cross-system integration was prominently engaged during semantic control (L2 > L1; t(76) = 4.30, p < 0.001), whereas this system-level coupling remained largely absent during domain-general executive control (incongruent > congruent; t(76) = −0.07, p = 0.94; Supplementary Fig. S14), confirming that cross-system representational coupling is specifically tuned to controlled semantic retrieval, rather than reflecting domain-general task demands.

Collectively, these results suggest that while cognitive systems maintain distinguishable representational patterns, increasing semantic retrieval demands leads to greater shared representational information, thus adaptive coordination between competing large-scale cognitive systems.

Discussion

In this study, we sought to delineate the adaptive organisation of neural systems supporting memory retrieval across the continuum of semantic associations. Our findings established that semantic distances derived from word embedding models, particularly ConceptNet Numberbatch, provide a reliable, continuous measure of controlled retrieval demands, with greater distances eliciting longer response times in a forced-choice paradigm. Importantly, whole-brain multivariate predictive modelling analysis revealed a robust neural signature of semantic distance that generalised across experimental contexts while remaining specific to semantic, rather than domain-general, executive control processes. Functional decomposition, guided by meta-analytic decoding, identified three large-scale systems underlying this process: a left-lateralised frontotemporal network largely associated with linguistic operations, a bilateral frontoparietal network implicated in executive regulation, and a medial memory network overlapping with the default mode hubs. Finally, representational similarity analyses demonstrated that although these systems retain distinct patterns of activation, they become increasingly coupled as retrieval demands intensify, reflecting adaptive information integration across functional networks. Building on earlier accounts18–20,46, these findings collectively provide an integrative framework for understanding how specialized yet interacting neural systems may support flexible knowledge retrieval across the continuum of semantic associations.

Recent advances in computational linguistics have enabled accurate modelling of the vast net of conceptual knowledge embedded within the world around us. In line with this progress, our initial analysis validated computational word embeddings as effective tools for quantifying human-like semantic similarity, with ConceptNet Numberbatch demonstrating particular superiority in predicting semantic retrieval demands. This advantage likely arises from ConceptNet’s unique integration of distributional semantics and structured knowledge, enabling it to capture both linguistic and real-world conceptual associations more accurately than alternative approaches37. Furthermore, the robust split-half consistency observed in reaction times suggests that individuals rely on largely overlapping semantic representational spaces during the retrieval of common lexical concepts, thereby supporting theories that propose shared conceptual structures across individuals47. Importantly, optimal behavioural predictions emerged from sparse embeddings retaining approximately 48% of original vector weights, suggesting that human semantic retrieval preferentially engages selective, diagnostic semantic features rather than exhaustive conceptual representations. This observation aligns closely with cognitive models emphasizing efficiency through selective activation of relevant semantic dimensions, particularly under conditions requiring flexible retrieval across varying associative strengths3,7. Collectively, these findings indicate that computational semantic distances can serve as a reliable proxy for semantic retrieval demands and can be systematically tailored to accommodate various experimental designs.

At the neural level, the whole-brain signature in our study highlights a distributed network sensitive to increasing semantic retrieval demands, with a spatial organisation that substantially overlaps with classical semantic control regions3,10,11. Importantly, our results further suggest a dissociation between a left-lateralised Language from a bilateral Control system, even when both systems respond to increasing cognitive demands1,3,36. This dissociation is supported by three key lines of evidence. First, the network decomposition of these systems is distinctly different, with divisions substantiated by multimodal information in the MMP atlas. The Language system predominantly comprises regions from the Language network20, while the Control system is primarily composed of MDN regions, which include key areas within the Frontoparietal and Dorsal Attention networks45. Second, our meta-analytic findings demonstrate differential activation patterns across these systems identified in prior research. Regions within the Language system show stronger activation during tasks involving linguistic or semantic processing, whereas regions within the Control system engage in more domain-general executive functions. Third, the two systems exhibit dissociable representational patterns during semantic retrieval processes. Although this decomposition highlights distinct contributions from such separable systems, it is important to note that our results also demonstrate their capacity for dynamic cooperation. As semantic retrieval demands increase, systems that maintain distinct representational profiles under low-demand conditions become progressively aligned, adopting shared representational structures that transcend canonical network boundaries. This convergence implies that semantic control is not localized to a fixed set of regions but instead emerges from the flexible integration of multiple systems adapting to varying retrieval demands21,48,49. In this view, the semantic control network reflects a transient configuration, a state of coordinated coupling, not a static anatomical entity.

Furthermore, the positioning of regions within the representational space reflects their potential roles in semantic retrieval. Interestingly, several regions within the Language system occupy boundary positions in the representational space, closely adjacent to Control systems. Two additional vlPFC regions (IFJa, IFSp) neighbour MDN components in the frontoparietal junction (IFJp, PEF) and intraparietal sulcus (LIPd, IP0, IP1), while frontal operculum (FOP5) lies adjacent to bilateral anterior ventral insular cortex (AVI) and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (8BM). These boundary areas likely function as cross-system integrators, facilitating communication between core semantic control regions in the Language system and key attentional and executive control nodes in the Control system49,50. While IFJa/IFJp have been shown to support attentional allocation and action selection through interactions with frontoparietal regions51, FOP5 is suggested to facilitate adaptive, goal-directed behaviour by integrating salience signals from the insula with executive processing in medial prefrontal regions52. Together, this arrangement may form a multi-tiered, trans-systemic framework for semantic information processing that enables retrieval to flexibly adapt to varying task demands and cognitive contexts10.

In addition to the well-documented systems above, our findings further implicate the engagement of a Memory system that is preferentially associated with retrieval across proximal associations and primarily encompasses ventral and posterior components of the DMN. These regions include right supramarginal gyrus (PF), parieto-occipital sulcus (POS1), dorsomedial parietal cortex (7 m), posterior cingulate (31a, 23c, v23ab, 31dp, 31pv), and ventral medial prefrontal cortex (p32). Traditional perspectives have characterized these areas as task-negative regions that typically show deactivation during externally-oriented cognitive tasks53. However, emerging evidence suggests that these regions play a crucial role in memory-based cognition54,55. As such, in contrast to reflecting task disengagement, the core default mode regions in this context may facilitate efficient semantic retrieval through their role in activating internal knowledge representations and associative processing56,57, which requires further investigation.

By demonstrating a functional dissociation among the three cognitive systems, our findings are broadly consistent with a tripartite neural architecture of semantic cognition18,19. While prior work has established the overall spatial topography and functional identities of these systems18–20,46, the present study advances this framework by unveiling their task-evoked operational dynamics. Specifically, as semantic distance increased, the representational geometries of these systems progressively converged, reflecting a graded shift from segregated to integrated processing that scaled with retrieval demand. Importantly, this representational convergence was absent during domain-general executive processing, suggesting that such cross-system coupling is more closely related to semantic retrieval demands than to a generic manifestation of cognitive effort alone. This selectivity also aligns with the Controlled Semantic Cognition (CSC) framework, which posits that semantic control is neither a generic byproduct of domain-general executive demands nor reliant on the fixed recruitment of dedicated semantic modules. Instead, flexible retrieval emerges from the coordinated, context-sensitive interaction between representational and control systems3,10. Viewed through this dynamic lens, our account offers a parsimonious interpretation of established clinical dissociations. For example, frontoparietal damage may selectively disrupt the capacity for cross-system integration required for distant retrieval, while sparing the automatic access to proximal associations mediated by default mode regions9,58. Conversely, when structural damage compromises these automatic representational pathways, the system may necessitate a state of compensatory hyper-coupling driven by the control network7. Nevertheless, further work is required to disentangle the distinct computational roles of these networks and to specify how their interactions give rise to adaptive coordination during flexible semantic retrieval.

Among the three systems identified, the posterior middle temporal gyrus/inferior temporal gyrus (pMTG/ITG, PH) emerged as a distinctive component with unique functional characteristics. During low semantic retrieval demands, this region positioned itself representationally closer to Control and Memory systems while maintaining relative distance from the Language system. However, under increased semantic retrieval demands, it shifted toward a central position in the representational space, strengthening its connections with all three systems to varying degrees. Previous research indicates that pMTG/ITG occupies a critical hub position within brain functional networks, demonstrating complex connectivity patterns with visual networks, the DMN, and MDN16,46,59. From a visual processing perspective, pMTG/ITG functions as an advanced node within the ventral visual pathway, receiving and integrating information flow from primary visual cortex and contributing significantly to object recognition and visual-semantic representations56,60. Functionally, pMTG/ITG demonstrates synchronized activity with the DMN during internal representation and conceptual processing, particularly during spontaneous thought and semantic memory retrieval61,62. Simultaneously, it maintains close functional interactions with the MDN during tasks requiring higher cognitive control, with functional connectivity between these systems significantly enhanced when tasks involve multimodal information integration and semantic processing3. This distinctive cross-network connectivity profile is consistent with the possibility that pMTG/ITG acts as a key mediator between perceptual information and cognitive control systems, serving as a critical hub, facilitating the flexible allocation of processing resources across the three identified cognitive systems based on task demands.

In addition to the high predictive performance observed within the employed 3-AFC paradigm, the successful generalisation of the semantic distance signature to a bilingual naming paradigm also suggests that the model is sensitive to a shared computational constraint across retrieval contexts. Although semantic distance and bilingual language proficiency arise from different sources, both require flexible regulation of retrieval within a structured lexico-semantic space, shifting between more dominant, readily accessible representations and less accessible but task-appropriate alternatives63. From this perspective, the signature does not only track associative distance per se, but rather reflects the controlled regulation of competition within semantic space across qualitatively different behavioural demands. Nevertheless, further research will be required in order to establish the breadth of cognitive contexts in which the identified neural signature tracks semantic retrieval demands.

Several limitations of our research warrant consideration. First, our visual 3-AFC paradigm inherently involves concurrent processes, such as visual scanning and response selection, that may scale with semantic distance. Although cross-task generalisation supports the specificity of the identified signature to semantic control, the potential contribution of such task-based intrinsic confounds cannot be entirely excluded. Therefore, while the signature generalises across diverse visual formats, future validation with non-visual stimuli (e.g., auditory) is essential to fully decouple semantic retrieval from these non-semantic demands. Second, our analysis was primarily focused on cortical regions, with limited investigation of subcortical contributions. Although we observed cerebellar involvement, particularly at the junction of lobules VI, Crus I, and Crus II, during controlled semantic retrieval, consistent with previous studies demonstrating cerebellar contributions to language and semantic processing64,65, we did not systematically examine the functional interactions between these cerebellar regions and the cortical systems identified in our study. Third, while our meta-analytic and RSA findings highlight functional distinctions between the identified systems, the precise neural computations enabling their functional specialization and integration remain unclear. In particular, the role of the Language network should be explored in non-linguistic semantic tasks that require controlled retrieval, such as tasks involving non-canonical object use to establish whether the network integrating memory and control processes within the left hemisphere is specific to verbal semantic tasks, or whether it has a broader role in generating context-appropriate patterns of conceptual knowledge retrieval.

In conclusion, by integrating computational linguistics with machine learning, we identified a robust neural signature sensitive and specific to varying semantic distances and thus semantic retrieval demands in a forced-choice semantic memory retrieval task. Anatomical annotation, complemented by functional and representational decomposition, further delineated three specialized neural systems within this signature, highlighting adaptive modulation of their representational interactions. Collectively, our findings illustrate how multiple interacting neural systems collaboratively support the continuum from automatic to highly controlled semantic retrieval demands, which may have important implications for the distinct semantic deficits observed in relation to varying patterns of brain damage.

Methods

Participants

In adherence to the ethical research standards set by the Declaration of Helsinki, Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Participants, the data analysed in this study were collected under a research protocol approved by both the Department of Psychology and York Neuroimaging Centre ethics committees at the University of York17. All participants were provided with comprehensive information about the experimental procedures before giving their written informed consent.

In total, 47 participants were recruited from the local university population, who were all right-handed native English speakers with normal or corrected-to-normal vision. In order to ensure data integrity and participant safety, individuals with any pre-existing psychiatric or neurological conditions, MRI contraindications, significant claustrophobia, or recent drug use that might affect cognitive performance were excluded from taking part in this study. Volunteers received monetary compensation at a rate of £10 per hour for their participation. Following data collection, one participant’s incomplete data were removed from further analysis. The final sample, who fully completed all the required neuroimaging-based and behavioural assessments consisted of 46 participants (29 females, 17 males) with a mean age of 21.31 years (SD = 2.17, range: 18–29 years). Sex was not included as an experimental factor, and no sex-stratified analyses were performed, as the study was designed to test stimulus-level semantic distance effects and was not powered to detect sex differences.

Experimental paradigm

The main experimental paradigm in this study featured a 3-AFC semantic retrieval task that was designed to examine the retrieval of language-based semantic associations, and has been extensively employed in our prior investigations of semantic processing in the human brain6. In each trial, participants were presented with a probe word (e.g., “bee”) alongside three potential response options: one target word with a conceptual relationship to the probe (e.g., “sting”) and two conceptually unrelated distractor words (e.g., “plate,” “atom”). The systematic manipulation of the semantic distance between probe-target pairs aimed to modulate the semantic retrieval demands placed on retrieval processes.

The complete set of probe-target word pairs was selected with reference to the Edinburgh Associative Thesaurus (EAT), a large-scale empirical lexical network in which directed links from stimulus to response words reflect strengths derived from free-association norms. For each trial, two distractor words were chosen with no direct associative links to either the probe or the target, thereby minimizing distractor-driven interference and ensuring that retrieval demands are primarily determined by probe-target semantic distance.

The stimulus set was divided into two independent, non-overlapping lists of words (List A: 25 participants; List B: 21 participants), each constituting a complete version of the task. This design allowed us to test whether behavioural and neural effects generalised across distinct semantic items rather than depending on idiosyncratic properties of a single word set, and perform cross-list prediction analyses.

In the employed fMRI paradigm, the probe word appeared centrally on the screen while the three response options were displayed below in randomized positions. Participants had four seconds to select their response, followed by a jittered fixation cross (1.5–3.5 seconds in 500 ms increments). Each list independently comprised a full set of 80 experimental trials and 20 control trials. During control trials, the placeholder “XXX” was displayed in all probe and response positions, and participants were required to press any button. These control trials were included to interrupt successive semantic judgments, thereby minimizing the carry-over of a fixed decision strategy across trials and helping to maintain participants’ attention throughout the task run. The entire session lasted approximately 10.83 minutes, with all stimuli presented via PsychoPy2 (Version 1.82)66.

Evaluation of behavioural response consistency

In addition to calculating response accuracy in the 3-AFC semantic retrieval task, we aimed to quantitatively assess whether participants accessed broadly similar semantic representations during retrieval. For this, we examined the split-half consistency of reaction times using a resampling approach67. Across 5000 iterations, we randomly divided participants into two equal groups and calculated the mean reaction time for each of the 80 trials within each group, generating group-level behavioural profiles. Split-half consistency was then quantified by computing Spearman rank-order correlation coefficients between the trial-wise mean reaction times of the two randomly formed groups.

To assess statistical reliability, we conducted one-tailed tests comparing the observed mean Spearman correlation coefficient across all 5,000 iterations against a null distribution of surrogate coefficients. These surrogate coefficients were generated by randomly shuffling the trial rankings for one of the two groups before computing correlations, thereby destroying any systematic reaction time patterns while preserving the overall distribution. We hypothesized that if participants accessed similar semantic representations for identical trials, this would manifest as consistently positive split-half correlations, indicating that trials eliciting faster responses in one random group would similarly elicit faster responses in the other group.

Model-based quantification of semantic distance

In order to investigate the alignment between human semantic processing and NLP models, we employed five pre-trained static word embedding models: Word2Vec (GoogleNews-300), FastText (Common Crawl-300d), GloVe (42B-300d), ConceptNet Numberbatch (v19.08, English) and RWSGwn (JOINTC-HYB-ENES-300d), downloaded from the Hugging Face Hub (https://huggingface.co). These models represent words as dense numerical vectors in a high-dimensional space, capturing semantic relationships from large text corpora.

In further detail, Word2Vec68 employs a prediction-based neural network architecture, implementing either CBOW (which predicts target words from surrounding context) or Skip-gram (which predicts context from target words) to learn static word embeddings that represent semantic associations. FastText69 extends Word2Vec by incorporating character n-grams, thereby addressing morphological complexity and enabling processing of out-of-vocabulary words. GloVe70 leverages global word co-occurrence statistics, combining matrix factorization with context window methods to create comprehensive semantic representations. ConceptNet Numberbatch37 is constructed using an ensemble approach that integrates data from ConceptNet, word2vec, GloVe, and OpenSubtitles 2016. It employs a modified retrofitting technique to capture both linguistic associations and real-world semantic relationships. Finally, RWSGwn71 derives embeddings through random walks on WordNet’s semantic graphs, capturing lexical hierarchies and structured semantic relationships.

With the aim of assessing which word embedding model best captured semantic retrieval behaviour, we implemented a systematic evaluation approach that considers both model selection and feature sparsity optimization. For each probe-target word pair, we first calculated the semantic distance (1 - cosine similarity) between the corresponding word vectors, where smaller values indicate stronger semantic associations. Importantly, unlike previous studies that typically use complete word vectors, we examined how selective feature retention affects model performance. For that purpose, we systematically varied the sparsity threshold, retaining only the top 1% to 100% of feature weights (highest magnitude), in increments of 1%. This approach was motivated by evidence suggesting that computational models incorporating sparse semantic representations demonstrate improved performance on semantic similarity tasks benchmarked against human ratings72. By systematically varying sparsity levels, we were able to determine the optimal degree of feature retention that most accurately reflects human semantic retrieval. For each model and sparsity threshold, we calculated Pearson’s correlation between the model-derived semantic distances and participants’ reaction times in the semantic retrieval task. Higher correlation values indicate better fit between the computational model and human semantic retrieval behaviour. All reaction-time-based analyses were restricted to correct responses, and no additional trimming of reaction times was applied.

Importantly, to mitigate potential confounding variables, we quantified not only the semantic distance between each probe-target pair but also systematically assessed the semantic distances among all distractors using identical methodological parameters. Furthermore, prior research has established that lexical items with higher global connectivity in semantic networks demonstrate enhanced accessibility during semantic retrieval tasks67. To account for this potential influence, we calculated the hubness coefficient for both probes and targets, defined as the mean semantic connection strength between each word and a comprehensive lexical reference set. This reference set comprised all nouns, verbs, and adjectives from WordNet 3.1, the most recent stable release of this lexical database73.

MRI data acquisition

MRI data acquisition was performed at the York Neuroimaging Centre (YNiC) at the University of York, utilizing a 3 Tesla GE HDx Excite MRI system equipped with an eight-channel phased array head coil. Acquisition parameters for the high-resolution structural scans employing a 3D fast spoiled gradient echo sequence were: repetition time (TR) = 7.8 s, echo time (TE) = 3 ms, flip angle = 20°, matrix size = 256 × 256, 176 slices, and voxel size = 1.13 × 1.13 × 1 mm. For functional MRI during the performance of the semantic 3-AFC task, data were acquired using a single-shot 2D gradient-echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence. Acquisition parameters for the functional scans were: TR = 3.0 s, TE = 18.9 ms, flip angle = 90°, matrix size = 64 × 64, 60 slices with a voxel size of 3 × 3 × 3 mm³, and 217 volumes. To facilitate improved co-registration with functional datasets, a fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequence, with an orientation matched to the functional scans was also acquired.

MRI data preprocessing

MRI data underwent comprehensive preprocessing using the Human Connectome Project (HCP) minimal preprocessing pipelines74 (legacy option), implemented within the Qunex (Quantitative Neuroimaging Environment & Toolbox) platform75. This rigorous preprocessing sequence included correction for head motion, followed by registration to the MNI152 standard space. High-resolution white matter and pial cortical surfaces were reconstructed from the 3 T T1-weighted structural scans. The Multimodal Surface Matching algorithm optimized for sulcal features (MSMSulc) was utilized for aligning individual subject data to the HCP-S1200 32k fsLR standard surface space, enabling precise cross-subject comparisons while preserving individual cortical folding patterns. The surface-based functional data were generated in the Connectivity Informatics Technology Initiative (CIFTI) format, which integrates both cortical surface vertices and subcortical volumetric data. Each CIFTI file contained 91,282 whole-brain grayordinates (59,412 cortical surface vertices and 31,870 subcortical voxels), with an approximate spatial resolution of 2 mm throughout the brain. This grayordinate system optimizes the trade-off between spatial resolution and computational efficiency while focusing analyses specifically on the grey matter. Finally, CIFTI time series data were spatially smoothed using a 4 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel.

Estimation of single-trial fMRI responses

We employed GLMsingle, a state-of-the-art toolbox designed to reliably quantify neural responses in condition-rich experimental designs76. GLMsingle implements three key optimizations that were critical for our analyses. First, it identifies optimal hemodynamic response functions for each brain region, accounting for inter-regional variability in neurovascular coupling rather than assuming canonical responses across the brain. This fine-grained modelling approach was particularly valuable for distinguishing closely spaced neural events in our event-related design. Second, the toolbox automatically derives data-driven nuisance regressors through GLMdenoise, which identifies and removes systematic noise patterns while preserving task-related signals77. Third, custom ridge regularisation is applied per brain region using fracridge regression, with regularisation strength automatically calibrated through cross-validation to optimize the bias-variance trade-off78. The resulting GLM beta weights represent estimated percent signal change for each experimental condition. The grayordinate values were z-scored at the session level before their inclusion in the subsequent predictive modelling analyses76. This pipeline enabled robust single-trial response estimation necessary for examining trial-by-trial neural variability in the semantic memory retrieval task.

Independent generalisation dataset

A publicly available functional MRI dataset40 was employed to assess whether the semantic distance signature identified in this study generalises to paradigms beyond the 3-AFC task and to evaluate the specificity of the identified neural signature in this study to semantic control rather than executive control processes. This dataset captures the neural processing of 77 Chinese-English bilinguals (N = 77, 34 females; mean ± SD age = 22.2 ± 2.2 years, range: 19–30 years) as they engaged in both language and cognitive control tasks with hard and easy conditions.

In the semantic control task, participants were instructed to name pictures in either their native language (Chinese) or their second language (English) based on visual cues. All participants had passed the College English Test Band 4, which is a standardized English proficiency test required for Chinese college students, ensuring sufficient competency to perform the English naming condition. Self-rated proficiency scores across four language skills confirmed that participants were Chinese-dominant bilinguals with significantly higher L1 than L2 proficiency. Given this asymmetry in language proficiency, English picture naming imposed higher retrieval demands to access weaker conceptual representations (high demand) as compared to the native Chinese naming condition (low demand), thereby mirroring the variable semantic retrieval demands evaluated in our paradigm.

In the cognitive control task, participants responded to arrow directions following colour-based rules (same-side for one colour, opposite-side for the other). The opposite-side condition required higher executive control demands (high demand), as participants had to inhibit their implicit tendency to respond in the direction of the arrow and instead execute the spatially incongruent response. Conversely, the same-side condition imposed lower control demands (low demand) since participants could follow their natural inclination to respond in the same direction as the arrow, requiring minimal inhibitory control. This manipulation imposes substantial demands on general executive resources but operates independently of conceptual knowledge, providing a rigorous contrast to verify that the identified neural signature is driven specifically by semantic control demands rather than general cognitive effort. Further details on paradigm specifications, data collection, preprocessing and analysis for this dataset are provided elsewhere40.

Multivariate predictive modelling

Our primary aim in the neuroimaging data analysis was to establish a robust neural signature of semantic memory retrieval based on model-derived semantic distances in the 3-AFC task. For that purpose, a whole-brain multivariate predictive modelling framework31 was used to establish a predictive model of weighted brain activity patterns that optimally predict the semantic distance between probe-target word pairs in each trial. Here, features (i.e., predictors) consisted of 91,282 grayordinates, which were aggregated into an image × grayordinate matrix stacked across participants. The outcome variable comprised semantic distance measures, which served as a proxy for the control demands required during semantic retrieval. The predictive model was constructed using Thresholded Partial Least Squares (T-PLS)38, a methodological approach that balances computational efficiency, effective dimensionality reduction to achieve sparsity and interpretability, and superior predictive accuracy compared to alternative whole-brain decoding techniques.

A comprehensive validation framework (Supplementary Fig. S1) was employed to systematically evaluate model performance across accuracy, robustness, generalisability, and specificity: (1) Predictive accuracy was evaluated using nested Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation with Pearson’s correlation (r), mean squared error (MSE) metrics and coefficient of determination (R2). (2) Parameter optimization employed five-fold inner cross-validation to determine optimal T-PLS components (1-25) and threshold levels (0.1-1.0) based on joint r and negative MSE criteria. (3) Generalisability was assessed by training models on one stimulus set and testing predictive performance on the independent stimulus set. (4) Neural feature importance was determined through bootstrap resampling (5,000 iterations) with z-statistics and FDR correction (q < 0.05, two-tailed). In addition to the overall predictive model, models were also established and compared between the two stimulus sets to evaluate their stability. (5) The model’s cross-paradigm generalisability and specificity were assessed by applying the overall predictive model to an independent dataset comprising both semantic control and executive control tasks. Detailed procedures for each evaluation component are provided in Supplementary Information.

Identification of model-based semantic distance signature

Following predictive modelling and performance assessment, a comprehensive set of analyses were employed to establish a robust pattern of neural signature that is predictive of semantic distance between probe-target word pairs in the semantic retrieval task. First, a bootstrap test was conducted on the entire sample (List A and List B) to determine brain regions that made stable contributions to prediction across participants and stimuli, with model training utilizing consistent optimal parameters (component number = 2 and threshold = 0.65). After correcting z-statistic map using the FDR method to account for multiple comparisons, we identified cortical surface vertices and subcortical voxels exhibiting significant and consistent predictive feature weights.

To ensure accurate neurobiological interpretability of our predictive model, the predictive feature weight maps obtained from the bootstrap analysis were then transformed into activation maps using Haufe transformation39. This method yields forward-model activation maps, also known as structure coefficients, representing the direct correlation between neural activations and the predicted semantic distance outcomes. Brain regions deemed critical for semantic retrieval should exhibit significant predictive feature weights (backward model) as well as significant structure coefficients (forward model). Therefore, in line with prior literature79, the key regions within the semantic distance signature were identified through a conjunction of the significant feature weight maps and the significant activation maps (both thresholded at FDR q < 0.05 in the z-test).

A trial-wise time-course analysis was conducted to characterize the temporal dynamics of the semantic distance signature and to verify that its expression was not driven by non-stimulus-related factors (e.g., pre-trial preparatory states or post-response motor artefacts). The temporal trajectory of signature expression was quantified as the dot product between the thresholded signature map (FDR q < 0.05) and the trial-by-trial BOLD time series, allowing us to assess whether its response followed a canonical stimulus-locked hemodynamic profile.

Parcel- and network-level characterization

For the precise characterization of the identified semantic distance signature, significant grayordinates were mapped onto the Multi-Modal Parcellation (MMP) atlas80. This atlas was selected for its precision in delineating cortical areas based on multiple modalities, including cortical architecture, function, connectivity, and topography, providing a comprehensive framework that respects both structural and functional boundaries of the human cortex. For each parcel in the MMP atlas, we calculated the posterior probability of containing significant grayordinates from the semantic distance signature. To establish statistical significance, a spin-based permutation testing approach was implemented that preserves the spatial autocorrelation of the original data81. Specifically, 5,000 randomized signature maps were generated by applying random rotations to the original signature map on the spherical surface. For each randomized signature, the posterior probabilities across all parcels were used to create a null distribution of expected values under spatial randomness. Then, one-sample t-tests compared the observed posterior probabilities from the true semantic distance signature against those from the randomized signatures for each parcel. Parcels with p-values < 0.05 were designated as core regions of interest (ROIs) representing the semantic distance signature.

The same quantification of posterior probabilities was also performed across functional networks defined by the Cole-Anticevic Brain-wide Network Partition (CAB-NP)44. CAB-NP provides compatibility with the MMP atlas, allowing for direct mapping between anatomically defined parcels and their corresponding functional brain networks. This complementary approach enabled us to transition seamlessly from parcel-level to network-level descriptions while maintaining spatial consistency, thereby providing comprehensive insights into the functional network architecture underlying semantic retrieval processes.

Meta-analytic decoding

For the purpose of elucidating latent cognitive systems underlying the semantic distance signature, a comprehensive meta-analytic decoding and decomposition approach was employed. First, 27 parcels from the MMP atlas that exhibited significant semantic distance signature density were identified. Within each parcel, only vertices showing significant activation in the semantic distance signature were retained, creating functionally specific ROIs. To facilitate meta-analytic decoding, the semantic distance signature was transformed from cortical space to the standard MNI volumetric space (2 mm isotropic) via Connectome Workbench tools82.

Functional decoding was then performed using the ROI association method (discrete decoding) implemented in the Neuroimaging Meta-Analysis Research Environment (NiMARE)83. This procedure generated a functional decoding matrix representing the association between cognitive terms and ROIs. To ensure interpretability and alignment with established frameworks, we constrained our analysis to terms present in both the meta-analytic databases and the Cognitive Atlas84, following recommendations by Hansen et al. (2021)85. For robustness, we conducted parallel analyses using both NeuroSynth41 and NeuroQuery42 databases, generating two independent functional decoding matrices. Only positive values across both decoding matrices were retained, as these represent reliable positive associations between cognitive terms and brain regions. Negative values were excluded because they often reflect statistical artefacts or anti-correlations that are less interpretable in meta-analytic contexts86.

Functional system decomposition

The decomposition of the functional decoding matrices was accomplished using Orthogonal Projection Non-negative Matrix Factorization (OPNMF)43, which generates two critical components: a term-to-system mapping (terms × cognitive systems) and a system-to-ROI mapping (cognitive systems × ROIs). Terms were assigned to specific cognitive systems based on their maximum loading values within the term-to-system matrix. To determine the optimal number of cognitive systems, the similarity between decompositions derived from NeuroSynth and NeuroQuery databases was evaluated using the adjusted Rand similarity coefficient and increased reconstruction error metrics87. This cross-validation approach revealed that a three-dimensional solution (representing three distinct cognitive systems) maximized similarity between NeuroSynth and NeuroQuery-based decompositions, suggesting a robust and generalisable structure.

For organising ROIs into coherent cognitive systems, a hierarchical clustering approach was employed with the average linkage method. This process involved constructing a correlation matrix to quantify similarities in ROI loadings across the identified cognitive systems, generating a dendrogram structure to visualize hierarchical relationships, and determining cluster assignments by cutting the dendrogram at appropriate levels. This hierarchical organisation provided a data-driven parcellation of brain regions into functional systems that collectively support semantic retrieval processes.

Representational similarity analysis

A representational similarity analysis (RSA) approach was employed to characterize the representational properties of 27 cortical ROIs spanning three cognitive systems34. This methodology allowed us to examine whether the identified ROIs exhibited dissociable representational patterns and how these patterns adapted to variations in semantic distance.

First, activation patterns were extracted from each ROI across 80 trials per stimulus set. To examine condition-specific effects while maintaining sufficient statistical stability for RSA, trials were divided into two equal groups (40 trials each) based on semantic distance. For each ROI and condition, trial-by-trial representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) were computed by calculating 1 minus the Pearson correlation between activation patterns, resulting in 40 × 40 matrices. These RDMs were vectorised to serve as representational signatures for each ROI35. Furthermore, to characterize the continuous progression of system adaptation beyond this categorical division, a complementary sliding-window approach was implemented. Specifically, trials were ordered by ascending semantic distance, and RDMs were iteratively computed within a sliding window of 30 trials, advancing in steps of 5 trials. This generated a sequential series of representational matrices, enabling the parametric tracking of representational dynamics as semantic demands intensified. This comprehensive procedure was implemented consistently across all participants and stimulus sets.

In order to test whether ROIs from the identified cognitive systems exhibited dissociable representational properties, a Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) with rigorous cross-validation (100 iterations of 5-fold cross-validation) was employed. For the purpose of statistically validating our classification results, we conducted permutation testing (5000 permutations) by randomly shuffling cognitive system labels prior to LDA and recalculating accuracies using the same cross-validation procedure. P-values were determined as the proportion of permutation accuracies equal to or exceeding the observed accuracy with true labels. We then visualized these high-dimensional representational patterns using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)88 with parameters optimized to preserve topological structure (neighbours = 15, minimum distance = 0.1).

Representational connectivity analysis

We then aimed to directly quantify how representational patterns adaptively changed as a function of semantic distance. For this, we examined changes in representational connectivity between ROI pairs. Representational connectivity reflects the similarity between the representational geometries of two ROIs and was quantified using Kendall’s τA rank correlation coefficient34,35. We analysed differences in these connections between low and high semantic distance conditions using LME with condition as a fixed effect and stimulus set and participant as random intercepts.

The ROIs with the strongest adaptation effects were then identified by calculating the average LME t-value across all connections for each ROI and ranking them accordingly. Statistical significance was established using non-parametric permutation testing (5000 permutations per ROI), where null distributions were generated by randomly permuting positive and negative connections in the LME-t matrix while preserving the signed degree distribution. One-tailed permutation p-values were calculated as the proportion of permutations yielding adaptation magnitudes greater than or equal to the observed values, with FDR correction applied for multiple comparisons.
