Evaluation of corticosteroid treatment in patients with post-traumatic ascending myelopathy and spinal cord injury: three case reports and review of the literature
Authors: Ariadna Marcé-Rotger, David Farkas Reinhardt, Carles Tortajada Bustelo, Xurxo Segura Navarro, Irene Corral López, Lluïsa Montesinos-Magraner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSubacute post-traumatic ascending myelopathy (SPAM) is defined as a neurological deterioration extending several levels above the initial spinal cord injury (SCI) within the first few weeks post-injury, without associated mechanical instability or syringomyelia. This report details three cases of SPAM to highlight this condition.Cases presentationWe present three case reports who sustained SPAM over the last 7 years at the Hospital Universitari Vall d’Hebron in Barcelona (Spain). Introduction

Subacute post-traumatic ascending myelopathy (SPAM) is defined as neurological deterioration extending above the initial injury level that occurs within the first few weeks following a spinal cord injury (SCI), without associated mechanical instability or syringomyelia [1]. SPAM typically occurs within the first 3 weeks post-injury [2]. Clinically, it presents as neurological deterioration of at least 2 levels above the initial injury [3, 4]. SPAM affects approximately 1% of SCI patients [5]. Young and middle-aged male patients are most frequently affected by this complication [1].Depending on the extent of spinal cord involvement, clinical findings can vary from dysphagia, upper extremity pain, respiratory depression, and neurological deterioration with numbness and muscle weakness [2]. MRI is the gold standard for diagnosing SPAM [1], typically showing changes in T2-weighted images ascending at least 4 vertebral levels above the initial injury site [4]. Published literature provides no evidence for the efficacy or superiority of any intervention, with treatment based on supportive care, surgery, and corticosteroid treatment (either with bolus or descending taper), without a clear superiority of one protocol over another.

SPAM is an infrequent yet potentially fatal complication following traumatic SCI. Since Hospital Universitari Vall d’Hebron is considered Trauma Center Hospital and it is the reference center for acute traumatic SCI in Catalonia (Spain) with 7.9 million of people, this case series is significant because it includes not only one but three cases of SPAM, treated with different regimens of corticosteroids. One case was treated with descending taper of dexamethasone (Dx) and two cases with 1 g bolus of methylprednisolone (MP) 3 and 7 days. One patient treated with a bolus of MP showed improvement in AIS classification.

Case presentation

Three patients with SPAM were included over the last 7 years (2017–2023) at the Hospital Universitari Vall d’Hebron (HUVH) in Barcelona. The HUVH is considered Trauma Center Hospital and it is the reference center for acute traumatic spinal cord injury in Catalonia (Spain) with 7.9 million of people.

The neurological status of the patients was assessed according to the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA)/International Spinal Cord Society (ISCoS) International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury (ISNCSCI), including the determination of the ASIA Impairment Scale (AIS).

Case 1: A 41-year-old male with type I diabetes suffered a motorcycle accident and was referred to our hospital for suspected SCI due to limb weakness. Upon arrival, he was hemodynamically stable. A Body-CT showed a fracture with anterolisthesis of C6-C7 over 50% with left facet dislocation and right facet fracture, causing significant spinal canal invasion. Initial examination diagnosed a C5 neurological level SCI (AIS A), confirmed by cervical MRI (Fig. 1). Closed reduction and C6-C7 arthrodesis were performed 48 h post-injury, with no surgical complications. Postoperatively, the patient experienced poor glycemic control and hypertension in the context of a descending dexamethasone taper, as well as hypotensive episodes secondary to baclofen treatment for spasticity, requiring a switch to tizanidine. Thirty-five days post-injury, the patient reported neck pain and dysesthesias in the neck and upper dorsal region with upper extremity paresthesias. Neurological examination diagnosed a C3 SCI (AIS A), representing a 2-level increase above the initial injury. Urgent cervical MRI confirmed SPAM with new onset edema extending to C1 (Fig. 2). A descending dexamethasone taper with 4 mg at 6 h intervals for 3 days intravenously, was initiated, resulting in one-level neurological improvement at discharge, but no AIS improvement.

MRI due to neurological worsening (c, d).

Post-surgical changes include anterior arthrodesis and fracture reduction. c Sagittal T2 and d) Axial T2 sequences show ascending centromedullary edema reaching the level of C1. The axial T2 sequence (d) shows spinal cord thickening and edema, with a central hypointense focus indicating microbleeds.

Case 2: A 45-year-old female smoker with obesity was brought to the emergency department after being hit by a car. She was hemodynamically unstable, requiring vasoactive drugs and blood transfusions to maintain mean arterial pressure and intubation. A Body-CT showed a C1 (Jefferson) fracture, severe burst fracture of T2 with spinal canal invasion, basal right pneumothorax, bilateral hemothorax, right atelectasis, and a posterior mediastinal hematoma without active bleeding. The patient also had an open femoral fracture requiring external fixation. After stabilization, neurological examination confirmed a C7 SCI (AIS A) by cervico-dorsal MRI (Fig. 3). The patient experienced diabetic ketoacidosis and required bilateral chest drains before surgery. Forty-eight hours post-injury, C6-T4 instrumentation and T2-T3 reduction were performed without incidents. Postoperatively, she required prolonged mechanical ventilation and experienced sudden desaturation with bradycardia due to right basal atelectasis with apical pneumothorax, which improved with bilateral drain openings and non-invasive ventilation. After 39 days after the injury, she felt impairment at the both upper extremities. Neurological examination revealed a C4 SCI (AIS A), representing a 3-level increase. Urgent MRI confirmed SPAM with edema extending to C2 (Fig. 4). A 3-day methylprednisolone bolus (1 mg/day intravenously) was initiated. No neurological level or AIS classification improvement was noted at discharge, although muscle strength did improve.

a Sagittal T2 image shows a burst fracture of T2 with spinal canal invasion, an anterior epidural hematoma (asterisks) and spinal canal narrowing. There is extensive spinal cord contusion, thickening and edema from C7 to T3, with T2 hypointense foci indicating microbleeds (arrows). b The sagittal diffusion sequence (DWI b-800) shows subtle hyperintensity, indicating cytotoxic edema (asterisk).

MRI due to neurological worsening (c, d).

Post-surgical changes include posterior arthrodesis. c Sagittal T2 and (d) Axial T2 sequences show ascending centromedullary edema up to C2, with T2 signal heterogeneity and defined hypointense foci indicating microhemorrhages (arrow). d The axial T2 sequence also reveals significant thickening and edema of the spinal cord.

Case 3: A 40-year-old male smoker with type II diabetes was transferred to our trauma center after a workplace fall, suspected of SCI due to lower limb immobility. He was hemodynamically stable. A Body-CT showed an unstable T8 burst fracture with 30–40% spinal canal reduction and anterior spinal cord compression. Initial examination diagnosed a T7 SCI (AIS B), confirmed by dorsal MRI (Fig. 5). Pre-surgical complications included hypertension. Forty-eight hours post-injury, decompression from T7 to T9 via laminectomy and T6-T10 arthrodesis was performed. Intraoperatively, bronchospasm required bronchodilators. Postoperatively, massive left lung atelectasis necessitated fibrobronchoscopy and right lateral decubitus. The patient later developed acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) requiring prone positioning and poor glycemic control. Extubation occurred six days post-surgery. Eleven days post-injury, neurological examination revealed a T5 SCI (AIS A), a 2-level increase. Urgent dorsal MRI confirmed SPAM with edema extending from the conus medullaris to C7 and subacute cytotoxic/ischemic edema from T2-T3 to T6. (Fig. 6). A 7-day methylprednisolone bolus (1 mg/day intravenously) was initiated. At discharge, neurological improvement to T5 SCI (AIS B) was noted, with an improvement in AIS classification.

a Sagittal T2 image shows a burst fracture of T8 with slight retropulsion of the posterior wall, posterior epidural lipomatosis, and ectasia of the posterior epidural venous plexus (white arrow), leading to spinal canal stenosis and centromedullary edema at T7/T8 (gray arrow).

Post-surgical changes include laminectomies at T7, T8, and T9, and posterior arthrodesis. b Sagittal and (c) Axial T2 sequences show ascending centromedullary edema up to T2 (arrow).

No patient presented severe side effects with corticosteroid treatment applied after presenting SPAM.

Discussion

SPAM affects approximately 1% of patients with spinal cord injury [5]. At our trauma center, between 2017 and 2023, 3 of 557 patients (0.54%) developed SPAM. It predominantly affects young to middle-aged men, with a male-to-female ratio of 5:1 [1]. Our cases involved two men and one woman, aged 40–45 years, with a mean age of 42 years.

Reported SPAM risk factors include complete spinal cord injury, injury at the thoracolumbar junction [6], asymptomatic low blood pressure [5], early postoperative orthostatic mobilization [5, 7], and non-surgical treatment [1, 8]. In adults, dislocation-fracture are most common, though burst and compression fractures can also lead to SPAM [4]. In our series, one patient had a fracture-dislocation and two had burst fractures; all underwent surgical stabilization within 2 days.

Several hypotheses have been suggested, including alteration in cerebrospinal fluid circulation, thrombosis of the Adamkiewicz artery, venous thrombosis and congestive ischemia, hypotensive ischemia, infection (e.g meningitis or myelitis), apoptosis, secondary injury, arterial thrombosis, inflammatory or autoimmune reaction [1, 2, 6, 7, 9], and mechanical factors such as spinal instability [10]. Meagher et al. [9] and Planner et al. [2] highlight altered CSF circulation as the leading cause. However, its exact cause remains unknown [1]. All our patients presented complications before, during, or after surgery related to blood pressure, respiratory difficulties (atelectasis, pneumothorax, or pneumonia), and/or poor glycemic control.

Clinically, SPAM is characterized by neurological deterioration of at least 2 levels above the initial injury, typically within the first three weeks [2–4]. In our case series, deficits averaged 2.3 levels above the injury, with an onset at 28.3 days.

SPAM can be recognized by typical clinical manifestations and MRI characteristics. It can cause motor and sensory deficits ranging from partial to complete loss of limb functions, with severity increasing at higher levels [1].

Symptoms include pain in arms, shoulders, scapula, neck, chest, or trunk, paresthesias, upper limbs weakness, ascending numbness, fever and absent upper limb reflexes [1–5, 9, 10]. Brainstem involvement can lead to dyspnea or death [1]. In our series, case 1 reports cervical pain with dysesthesias at the neck and dorsal regions, and upper limbs paresthesias. Case 2 shows impared bimanual dexterity. Case 3 shows sensory deficits during systematic post-surgical evaluation.

MRI is the gold standard for diagnosing SPAM. In all our patients, an urgent MRI confirmed the diagnosis. It typically shows a central T2-weighted hyperintensity extending at least four segments above the initial injury, gradually tapering at the lesion’s upper end [1]. T1-weighted images often show minimal heterogeneous intramedullary signal [1]. Changes observed on MRI do not always match clinical-neurological deterioration [1] and they might occur without detectable neurological deterioration [1, 2, 11]. In our case series, the edema extended 4 levels above the injury in Case 1 (from C5 to C1), 5 levels in Case 2 (from C7 to C2) and 5 levels in Case 3 (from T7 to T2).

There is no evidence in the published literature for the efficacy or superiority of any particular intervention. Supportive treatment focuses on blood pressure management, neurological monitoring, bed rest to prevent orthostatic hypotension, oxygen administration, and mechanical ventilation for dyspneic patients [1. 6].

Conservative treatments such as anticoagulants, steroids, antibiotics, and osmolar therapy are also used [1, 3, 4, 10].

Surgical options aim to reduce cerebrospinal fluid pressure, including laminectomy [6], expansive durotomy [1, 12], and cordectomy [1, 9], all of with proven effectiveness. However, Visocchi et al. [13] reported no neurological improvement with early decompression.

Conservative treatment typically allows recovery to at least the initial injury level within 1–14 months [2, 4, 14].

Published articles describe cases of patients treated with corticosteroids, with no clear superiority of one protocol over another. Kaya et al. [4] present a SPAM patient treated with high doses of methylprednisolone for 3 weeks, who improved to the initial neurological level. Kandwal et al. [15] reported two cases managed with NASCIS II protocol (30 mg/kg bolus of methylprednisolone followed by 5.4 mg/kg/h over 23 h); one of these patients also received a tapering dose of 10 mg/day of prednisolone for three weeks. Both improved, but only the patient treated exclusively with NASCIS II achieved full recovery [15]. Kovanda and Horn [16] reported a case of SPAM treated with high doses of dexamethasone (4 mg every 6 h) with a descending taper over 3 weeks, showing full recovery at 11 months. Aydoseli et al. [17] presents a case of a patient with T7 lesion who developed SPAM and was treated with NASCIS II protocol combined with proton pump inhibitor therapy for 23 h, and then continued for the next three weeks. Despite some improvement after one month, full recovery was not achieved. Consequently, the author concludes that there is no clear evidence whether the neurological improvement is associated with steroid therapy or due to the natural course of SPAM [17].

Regarding our patients, one received a descending regimen of dexamethasone (Case 1),without AIS improvement. The other two received methylprednisolone: Case 3 (7-day course) improved, while Case 2 (3-day course), did not.

The prognosis of SPAM depends on the extent of myelopathy ascension. Most patients show slight neurological improvement within weeks or months, typically one or more levels below the peak deficit. Full recovery to pre-deterioration level is rare [9].

Deficits may persist or progress rapidly to tetraplegia and respiratory failure within hours or days [1, 2, 6, 9, 10], potentially leading to brain herniation with respiratory and cardiac failure [6]. Mortality is approximately 10% [1, 2]. In our case series, there were no fatalities.

A detailed neurological evaluation is essential for diagnosing SPAM [1, 6], and was systematically performed by the Spinal Cord Injury Unit in all suspected cases.

SPAM is an infrequent yet potentially fatal complication following traumatic spinal cord injury. Its cause remains unclear, though several theories exist. It should be considered in cases of neurological deterioration after a traumatic SCI, as early detection is vital. Cardiovascular risk factors may contribute, highlighting the need for close monitoring of both blood pressure and neurological status. Current literature reveals a variety of corticosteroid treatment protocols, yet no single approach has been definitively proven superior. In this limited case series, methylprednisolone appeared to be associated with more favorable outcomes than dexamethasone. Nonetheless, the small sample size precludes any firm conclusions, and further research is necessary to substantiate this potential therapeutic difference.
