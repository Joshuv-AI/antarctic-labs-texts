“From simulation to surgery: exploring variables influencing the translational impact of simulation-based education in neurosurgical competence”
Authors: Adam F. Roche, Dara O. Kavanagh, Darach Crimmins, Barry B. McGuire, Donncha O’Brien, Alessandro Perin, Lena-Mary Houlihan, Wail Mohammad, Richard Ashpole, Pablo Javier Villanueva, Laura Kehoe, Claire M. Condron
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSimulation-enhanced education (SEE) is increasingly integral to neurosurgical training, yet the mechanisms underpinning effective skill to clinical practice remain under-explored. This study, grounded in the Four-Component Instructional Design (4 C/ID) model and principles of deliberate practice investigates key variables influencing the translational impact of SEE on neurosurgical competence.MethodsA sequential explanatory mixed-methods design was employed. Quantitative data were collected via a 17-item questionnaire administered to neurosurgical residents and experts (n = 57), capturing domains such as instructional design, fidelity, skill transfer and assessment. Descriptive statistics, internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha), and exploratory factor analysis were used to identify prevailing trends and subscales. These findings informed semi-structured interviews (n = 13), which probed underlying factors affecting skill transfer. Interview data were analysed using inductive thematic analysis, with themes mapped to quantitative constructs. Triangulation was achieved by integrating both data strands, and multiple researchers contributed to coding and analysis to enhance credibility.ResultsParticipants reported that SEE improved procedural confidence, technical competence, and task fluency. However, concerns persist around the realism of simulation, it’s applicability to complex clinical scenarios, and engagement among senior residents. Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings highlighted the value of simulation for procedural readiness and objective assessment, particularly when training design aligned with cognitive learning theory. Triangulation confirmed the robustness of emerging themes and provided a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing translational impact.ConclusionSEE supports the development of neurosurgical competence, particularly when grounded in robust instructional design and deliberate practice. Addressing challenges related to fidelity, transferability, and engagement will further the predictive validity and adaptive realism of neurosurgical simulation curricula.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07814-3.

Introduction

Neurosurgery stands at the intersection of technical complexity and high-stakes decision-making, demanding not only advanced operative skills but also robust, cognitive and psychological resilience [1]. Despite advancements in patient safety and surgical education [2, 3]operator error remains a significant contributor to adverse events, with technical [4] and non-technical factors both implicated in patient harm. Traditional training models, reliant on apprenticeship and in-theatre learning alone, have come under increasing scrutiny as operative exposure for residents declines and clinical environments grow more complex [5].

In response, simulation-enhanced education (SEE) has become a cornerstone of modern neurosurgical training, offering a safe, structured platform for developing and assessing both technical proficiency and non-technical skills (NTS). Previous research underscores the importance of risk reduction at systems level [6]particularly in enhancing team communication [7] and protocol adherence [8]. The integration of non-technical skills (NTS) [9] into surgical education has emerged as a critical complement to traditional training, with high-fidelity SEE offering a structured platform for experiential learning (Fig. 1). The term coaching in surgical education is often used inconsistently, it can be understood as a structured process that guides residents toward performance improvement through targeted, task-specific feedback and reflective dialogue within a defined curricular framework [11, 12]. While mentoring emphasises career development and psychosocial support, our approach concentrated on the immediate refinement of technical and cognitive skills. While emerging studies demonstrate that coaching enhances procedural skills, self-assessment, and reflective capacity in postgraduate surgical training, its application within neurosurgical education remains underexplored. This study investigates how structured, feedback-driven simulation—when delivered through a coaching model—supports skill transfer into operative neurosurgical practice. The translational impact of SEE–specifically, the extent to which simulation-acquired skills transfer to the operating room (OR) and improve clinical outcomes–remains incompletely understood [13, 14]. Key challenges persist around the predictive validity of simulation-based assessments, the calibration of simulation fidelity to learner needs, and the integration of non-technical competencies into credentialing frameworks. While the literature affirms the value of SEE for early skill acquisition and confidence-building within a controlled learning environment [15–19]questions remain regarding its efficacy as a supplemental strategy to help prepare residents for the unpredictable, high-pressure realities of neurosurgical practice.

This study advances the field by shifting focus from simply evaluating the usefulness of simulation to exploring which variables most significantly impact the real-world effectiveness of SEE in developing neurosurgical competence. Guided by the Four-Component Instructional Design (4 C/ID) model [20]—which emphasises the interplay of learning tasks, supportive information, procedural information, and part-task practice—we employed a sequential explanatory mixed-methods approach [21]. Quantitative and qualitative strands are explicitly linked to theoretical constructs, allowing for a nuanced exploration of how simulation design, fidelity, and assessment strategies shape skill transfer and readiness for independent practice.

Through engagement with both neurosurgical residents and clinical leaders, this research seeks to clarify the mechanisms by which SEE supports operative competence, identify barriers to effective transfer, and inform future curricular and credentialing strategies. In doing so, it aims to provide actionable insights for optimising neurosurgical education and ensuring that SEE delivers lasting, measurable benefits for patient care.

Materials and methods

Design

A modified mixed-methods sequential explanatory study design was employed [21]incorporating the framework method in data analysis [22]. The initial round involved validating the quantitative data model through a custom questionnaire, followed by qualitative interviews to expand upon and contextualise findings [23]. To integrate quantitative and qualitative data with transparency, we created a joint display table aligning key questionnaire results with qualitative themes and quotes, enabling meta-inference and highlighting convergence and divergence, following best practices for mixed methods integration [24].

Participants

All participants invited to take part in this study were neurosurgeons, comprising both “residents” (n = 18) and “experts,” defined as those at the fellowship or consultant level (n = 92). In keeping with principles of effective skill transfer, experts—by virtue of their advanced clinical experience and procedural mastery—are ideally suited to serve as coaches in guided SEE. The inclusion of residents provides a diverse range of perspectives on the challenges inherent in contemporary neurosurgical education. Inclusion criteria required prior experience with SEE in neurosurgery as a learner or coach. For the initial quantitative component, purposive and snowball sampling techniques were utilised to recruit study participants throughout the world. Five co-authors (DC, AP, WM, RA, and PJV) nominated additional participants. For the qualitative component, a purposeful subset was selected for semi-structured interview. Although interview participants were actively working in public practice in the United Kingdom (UK) and Ireland. The neurosurgical training programme in these jurisdictions is jointly governed by the Intercollegiate Surgical Curriculum Programme (ISCP) [25]which is an eight-year, integrated scheme encompassing Core Surgical Training (CST) Years 1–8.

Questionnaire

Guided by the 4 C/ID model [20]the questionnaire was designed to reflect core instructional principles relevant to simulation-based training. The questionnaire was administered via Google Forms™ platform. Items addressing technical performance and theoretical understanding aligned with the 4 C/ID model’s components of learning tasks and supportive information. Questions targeting skill transfer and realism were grounded in the literature on fidelity and transfer of training, while items related to assessment utility reflected the components of procedural information and park-task practice, focusing on repeatable performance and feedback mechanisms.

The design also incorporated principles from the theory of deliberate practice, particularly structured repetition, goal orientation, and feedback-driven refinement for skill mastery [26]. These theoretical underpinnings aimed to enhance construct validity and provide a more meaningful lens for interpreting the quantitative data. Respondents rated all items using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Descriptive statistics were used to calculate median, interquartile range (IQR), and frequency distributions. Internal consistency of item groupings was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, and dimensionality was assessed via exploratory factor analysis (principal factor extraction, Kaiser criterion: eigenvalue > 1). The resulting subscales were: effectiveness, translational value, and assessment utility.

Semi-structured interviews

An interview guide (Appendix 1) was developed iteratively in response to patterns identified from initial questionnaire data responses, piloted, and then refined. The structure and content of the topic guide were further informed by the 4 C/ID model, ensuring that interview questions probed domains such as learning tasks, supportive information, procedural information, and part-task practice as they relate to SEE. Questionnaire items with high consensus were probed qualitatively to explore the basis of these perceptions, while items with moderate variability or lower ratings informed targeted follow-up questions. Interviews were conducted using Microsoft Teams™, recorded and transcribed verbatim using the live transcription function for subsequent analysis. Using inductive thematic analysis [27]data from the interviews was analysed. Pertinent information relevant to the research question was extracted using Microsoft Excel™ and preliminary codes were generated. A codebook was iteratively developed by one researcher (AR) during thematic analysis (Appendix 2), with each theme and sub-theme defined, illustrated by representative quotes, and explicitly mapped to the relevant quantitative survey constructs to ensure transparent integration of mixed-methods findings. The codebook was subsequently reviewed and checked for consistency by a second researcher (CC).

Triangulation

Triangulation

To strengthen the credibility and validity of the findings, triangulation was utilised by integrating quantitative survey results with qualitative interview data. This enabled cross-verification of emerging themes and provided a comprehensive understanding of variables influencing the translational impact of SEE.

Theme 1 – building technical confidence in a safe learning environment

Simulation was perceived as a powerful tool for developing foundational technical and cognitive skills, especially for junior residents. Participants highlighted how simulation helps reduce cognitive load by allowing procedural steps to become automated through repeated practice.

“Simulation lets them build up muscle memory, so they’re more fluent in theatre. It’s not about the complexity of the task, but the familiarity of the tools.” (P4, Expert). Consultants described simulation as preparing residents for more complex cases by isolating essential psychomotor skills from environmental stressors: “If residents have built up muscle memory of the technical steps from learning in the sim lab, then the only influential stressors are OR environmental factors.” (P3, Expert).

Alongside skill acquisition, simulation was also seen to foster a psychologically safe learning environment. Coaches play an important role in this, guiding residents stepwise through procedures, normalising mistakes, and encouraging open dialogue. These coaching behaviours enable residents to attempt unfamiliar techniques without fear of judgment. As one resident shared, “In simulation, you have time, the consultant can go through all steps to the task with you, one by one” (P10, Resident). Such practices helped co-construct a safe environment where residents could take risks, reflect openly, and receive targeted feedback—conditions that underpinned growing confidence and technical proficiency. These insights elaborate on the ‘effectiveness’ construct from the survey, where the majority of respondents agreed that simulation improves procedural confidence (see Tables 1 and Appendix 2).

Theme 2 – educational process: workflow and clinical reasoning

This theme explores how simulation serves as cognitive scaffolding, supporting the development of workflow understanding and clinical reasoning—key elements of the “supportive information” component of the 4 C/ID model. Participants emphasised that high-fidelity, immersive simulations are most effective when they mirror the entire clinical workflow, from patient preparation to procedural wrap-up, rather than isolating technical skills alone.

“Simulation should be immersive, right from entering the OR to the final stitch. It’s not just the technical part—it’s knowing how to think like a surgeon.” (P10, Resident).

Another participant described how simulation allowed for structured exploration: “Set targets to find deeper anatomical structures… that’s a learning in itself as you’re not rummaging around looking for them on a real patient first and foremost” (P6, Resident). Such reflections illustrate how coaching interactions—characterised by clear goal-setting and the normalisation of uncertainty—supports psychological safety and enables residents to engage with complexity without fear of error.

These insights elaborate on the ‘effectiveness’ and ‘translational impact’ constructs identified in the quantitative phase, where participants agreed that simulation supports not only technical proficiency but also broader clinical reasoning (see Tables 1 and Appendix 2).

Theme 3 – systemic barriers & fidelity: challenges for advanced residents

This theme highlights the interplay between learner stage, simulation fidelity, and educational value. Senior residents reported that engagement with simulation declines unless the activities are highly realistic and tailored to advanced learning needs. Perceived educational value (“yield”) and relevance are critical motivators for participation at this level.

“For residents in later stages of training, there has to be a pull factor for them to attend—there has to be yield.” (P12, Resident).

“Only thing that’s left are new surgical questions… and that can be difficult to simulate well.” (P13, Resident).

These perspectives align with quantitative findings indicating lower perceived benefit of simulation among senior residents and highlight the need for ongoing innovation in simulation design to maintain engagement and educational effectiveness (see Table 1 and Appendix 2).

Theme 4 – limits of simulation: crisis, stress, and risk

This theme illustrates the current boundaries of simulation’s instructional reach, especially in replicating stress, error consequences, and decision-making under uncertainty—elements critical to predictive validity. While simulation was broadly regarded as beneficial, participants acknowledged its limitations in replicating high-stakes elements such as crisis adaptation, anatomical variability, and the psychological pressures of real patient care.

“Simulation doesn’t have the same risk of complications such as managing excessive bleeding. You need the risk of things not going right so a resident learns to adapt.” (P1, Expert).

“Simulation doesn’t have the same risk of complications such as managing excessive bleeding. You need the risk of things not going right so a resident learns to adapt.” (P1, Expert).

These comments reinforce the quantitative finding that, while most participants value simulation for skill acquisition, there is scepticism about its ability to fully prepare residents for the unpredictable and high-risk realities of the operating room. This theme underscores the need for more immersive and scenario-based simulations that incorporate risk, unpredictability, and non-technical skills development (see Table 1 and Appendix 2).

Discussion

It is well established that today’s neurosurgical residents experience less operative exposure than previous generations, with SEE increasingly used to help bridge this gap. Given the steep and prolonged learning curve that can extend well into independent practice, SEE has emerged as a valuable tool for developing both technical and cognitive skills—especially during the early years of training. In this study, participants strongly endorsed SEE for improving procedural confidence, task fluency, and spatial awareness. However, further research is needed to clarify its specific impact on clinical performance, particularly in the later stages of training where complexity, nuanced decision-making, and high-fidelity simulation are essential.

Our findings reveal a three-step pathway underpinning effective skill transfer from SEE to neurosurgical OR: (1) cognitive load optimisation [28] → (2) fidelity calibration [29] → (3) predictive validity [30]. Early in training, residents benefit from being coached through repetitive, well-scaffolded tasks that reduced intrinsic cognitive load, aligning with the automation stage described in cognitive load theory (CLT). As they progress, greater psychological and task fidelity becomes essential to maintain engagement and foster germane load. This need was echoed in Theme 3, where participants emphasised that “there has to be yield” for continued participation, highlighting fidelity as the rate-limiting factor for advanced learning. When fidelity was well matched to resident level, both residents and experts expressed greater confidence that simulation performance would translate to operative competence—supporting its predictive validity. In contrast, low-stakes, low-consequence scenarios (Theme 4) failed to challenge learners meaningfully, undermining trust in simulation’s real-world applicability. This integrated pathway explains why confidence and workflow fluency peaked early in training, while perceptions of realism and crisis management lagged: fidelity—and thus cognitive load—was not optimally aligned with the evolving needs of senior residents.

Clinically, ventriculostomy remains a high-stakes procedure commonly performed in the OR but often required in high-acuity settings such as the neurointensive care unit (NICU) [31]where external stressors [32] such as hemodynamic instability and time pressure are pronounced. According to the 4 C/ID model [20]repeated, scaffolded practice is essential for mastering complex, recurring clinical tasks such as external ventricular drain (EVD) insertion. Our findings indicate that SEE, when structured around these principles, enables junior neurosurgical residents to build procedural fluency and automate technical skills–particularly when coached through repetition and feedback–thereby reducing intrinsic cognitive load and better preparing them to perform under pressure. Moreover, the deliberate practice framework and CLT both support the value of SEE in shifting the learning curve outside the OR [33]allowing residents to develop competence in a lower-risk, controlled environment. This approach not only enhances readiness, but also minimises patient risk [34]. Our results highlight that the predictive validity of simulation for procedures such as EVD insertion is maximised when fidelity is calibrated to learner stage and cognitive demands–ensuring that skills acquired in simulation translate effectively to clinical performance.

Our findings underscore that fidelity is not a fixed attribute, but a multidimensional concept that must align with the learner’s stage and objectives. Early in training, anatomical realism is paramount; by the senior years, residents value clinical relevance over perfect tissue feel. Fresh-frozen cadavers deliver unrivalled physical fidelity [35] but don’t include tumours, vascular anomalies, or intraoperative complications, limiting their yield for advanced residents. Purpose-built synthetic or 3D–printed models can embed exactly those pathologies [36]offering scenario fidelity that tests decision-making and technical nuance, even if the haptic feedback is less authentic. As residents progress throughout training, their expectations shift from physical mimicry to clinical relevance. Optimising simulation for advanced residents therefore means trading some anatomical accuracy for pathology-rich, complication-driven scenarios that mirror the complexity of real cases. A blended curriculum using cadavers for fine anatomical dissection and synthetic models for high-risk or rare pathology enables coaches to calibrate fidelity to the resident’s stage and the competencies being evaluated. Fidelity levels are a key variable for skill transfer, and selection flexibility would be required, as some residents reach training competence milestones quicker than others.

Simulation-based assessment presents both a critical gap and a significant opportunity to serve as a formal credentialing checkpoint in neurosurgical training. Although no studies have yet validated whether proficiency thresholds in simulation can independently license residents for operative autonomy, both experts and resident groups in our cohort strongly supported early, objective simulation assessments (typically in years 2–3) as both a safeguard and a learning accelerator [37]. We propose shifting the research agenda from simply asking whether simulation is useful to investigating whether a validated simulation-based assessment can reliably predict operative safety and justify graduated autonomy [38]. Achieving this will require high-fidelity simulation tasks that generate appropriate cognitive load; psychometrically robust assessment metrics such as error counts, motion analytics, and time-to-completion; rater calibration anchored in established validity frameworks [39] to minimise bias; and longitudinal outcome tracking to link simulator performance with real-world intraoperative results. Preliminary evidence from proficiency-based progression models, which have demonstrated up to a 50% reduction in intraoperative errors [40]suggests that simulation-based credentialing is feasible. Reframing simulation from a supplementary teaching tool to a cornerstone of credentialing could enable neurosurgery to implement a sustainable, transparent, patient-centred process for granting operative independence and ultimately improve surgical safety. The integration of simulation-based assessment into neurosurgical training is not only enhancing educational outcomes but is also shaping the future landscape of credentialing and professional development within the specialty.

SEE, while invaluable for technical skill development, remains limited in its ability to authentically replicate crisis response, acute stress, and the true consequences of surgical error—elements that are fundamental to real-world neurosurgical practice [41]. High-stakes decision-making under pressure is intrinsic to neurosurgery, and current simulation models often fall short in preparing residents for these demands. To bridge this gap, future simulation programmes should prioritize the integration of immersive non-technical skills (NTS) training, including crisis resource management (CRM) scenarios specifically tailored to the neurosurgical context. Although assessing the real-world impact of NTS training is challenging due to the individualized nature of these competencies, accumulating evidence indicates that such programmes enhance team performance and reduce the incidence of error [42]. Embedding robust NTS and CRM components within simulation curricula is therefore essential for equipping tomorrow’s neurosurgical residents to navigate the full spectrum of operative challenges and to promote safer surgical care.

Lastly, while our findings are context-specific, several elements of the simulation-based model described here may be have wider applicability. Longitudinal pairing enables residents to build trust with expert coaches and supports personalised, developmentally appropriate feedback [43]. Integration with a structured curriculum further promotes deliberate practice [26]. However, clinical expertise alone does not ensure coaching effectiveness; faculty may require targeted “train-the-trainer” preparation in educational techniques, feedback delivery [44, 45]and fostering psychological safe learning environments [46]. For institutions seeking to adopt similar models, investment in structured coach development—alongside curricular planning—will be critical to optimising both technical skills training and the overall quality of the learning environment. Effective performance feedback skills, in particular, are a crucial variable for effective learning and the successful translation of simulation gains into operative competence.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the central importance of simulation-enhanced education (SEE) in neurosurgical training, particularly for building essential technical and non-technical skills outside the operating room. To ensure that SEE translates effectively to clinical practice, future efforts should prioritise increasing the realism of simulation scenarios, establishing validated and objective assessment metrics, and integrating simulation-based assessments into formal credentialing pathways. Cost-effective, high-fidelity synthetic models offer reproducibility and scalability, as well as the ability to embed complex pathology, making them especially valuable for diverse training environments, including those with limited resources. A critical next step is to evaluate the predictive validity of these synthetic models to determine whether performance benchmarks achieved in simulation reliably translate to improved operative competence and patient outcomes. Future research should also collate validity metrics—such as reliability or construct validity—against pilot simulation-based assessments in neurosurgery. Demonstrating such impact could catalyse a cultural shift in neurosurgical education, positioning accessible simulation as a core pillar of training and credentialing, and expanding high-quality surgical education globally.

Strengths and limitations

A significant strength of this study was the use of a mixed-methods sequential design, integrating both quantitative and qualitative data collection to provide a comprehensive and well-rounded exploration of this nuanced topic. The inclusion of both neurosurgical residents and consultants allowed for a comparison of insights between different levels of experience, enhancing the robustness of the findings. Interview participants represented a diverse range of neurosurgical sub-specialties, reducing the risk of bias toward any single sub-specialty. Our research has a number of limitations. The quantitative component was limited by a relatively small sample size (n = 57) and a response rate of 52%, which may reduce statistical power and generalisability and introduces a risk of non-response bias. The clustering of survey responses at the higher end of the Likert scale indicates potential ceiling effects and acquiescence bias, which may have limited the sensitivity of the quantitative component to detect more nuanced or divergent perspectives. However, this was mitigated by qualitative follow-up that explored the basis of these positive responses. The absence of demographic data collection from questionnaire respondents increased the risk of bias in our quantitative findings. Interview respondents were based in UK and Ireland practices, thus limiting the generalisability of the interview results. For the qualitative component, while a formal audit trail was not maintained, transparency was ensured through iterative development of the codebook, independent review by a second researcher, and regular team discussions to maintain analytic rigour. Lastly, our interview sample size was relatively small. While theoretical sufficiency was achieved, a larger qualitative sample could have captured a broader range of perspectives.
