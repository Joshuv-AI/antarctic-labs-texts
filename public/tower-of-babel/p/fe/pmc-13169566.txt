Addressing the unanswered questions: a qualitative exploration focusing on community dwellers using pregabalin without prescription in Islamabad city, Pakistan
Authors: Muhammad Mujtaba Hafeez, Inayat Ur Rehman, Ruqaia Gul, Fahad Saleem, Noorasyikin Shamsuddin, Long Chiau Ming, Kashif Ullah Khan, Nada A. Alsaleh, Alaa A. Alsharif, Mohammad Bashaar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPregabalin’s non-medical use is frequently reported in the literature. However, knowledge related to this non-medical use is scarce, especially from community dwellers of a developing nation. Lacking baseline information, the current study qualitatively explored the motives and reasons for non-medical use of pregabalin among the general population of Islamabad city, Pakistan.MethodsBy adopting the phenomenological approach, a qualitative study using semi-structured interviews was conducted. Community dwellers approaching pharmacies and purchasing pregabalin without a prescription were approached for face-to-face, in-depth interviews. The interviews were audio-taped, individually transcribed (verbatim), and were then analysed for thematic contents by the standard content analysis framework.ResultsMost of the participants (10, 66.6%) were in the 18–27 age group. All participants were male and most of them worked in the private sector Islamabad city. Thematic content analysis resulted in five major themes and five subthemes. Peers at the workplace were identified as a major source of information regarding pregabalin use. Being lethargic, easing physical exertion and poor work-life balance were the reasons for pregabalin use among the study respondents. Intriguingly, pregabalin was also reported as a performance enhancer by some of the participants. The participants had no information on clinical use of pregabalin and most of them had the opinion that the drug is safe to use and has no side effects.ConclusionThis study highlights the non-medical use of pregabalin in Islamabad, driven by challenges like difficult living conditions, work-related fatigue, fear of the future, and work-life balance. The findings emphasize the need for public awareness campaigns, rigorous regulations on pregabalin sales, and accessible support for those individuals involved in non-medical use of pregabalin.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13011-026-00720-x.

Background

Pregabalin, being a GABA analogue, binds to the alpha-2-delta subunit of voltage-gated calcium channel and releases excitatory neurotransmitters. In addition to the antinociceptive and anticonvulsant effects, the drug also carries anxiolytic properties and thus has the potential for non-medical use and substance use disorder [1–3]. In addition, it is observed that communities frequently use this drug as a recreational product [4, 5]. Correspondingly, pregabalin has also incurred the attention of individuals with substance use disorders because of its euphoric and dissociative effects [6]. The evidence of euphoria produced by pregabalin is well-established in the current literature. Individuals have reported that they were using pregabalin (without prescription for relieving anxiety and pain, for withdrawal of other substances, and for enhancing the effects of other drugs [2]. Studies of the same nature confirmed that pregabalin can reduce cravings for substance use disorders and withdrawal effects of other drugs, including alcohol, and helps with sleep induction [7–9].

Within this context, Bonnet and Scherman, in their systematic review, concluded that pregabalin has a greater potential for non-medical use, and this is relatively high among individuals with a history of opioid or polysubstance use disorders [10]. Another study reported a history of trauma, socioeconomic distress, and concurrent psychiatric comorbidities in individuals with pregabalin non-medical use [11]. Pregabalin use was also reported among the US population, whereby 10% of the community dwellers were involved in intentional non-medical use or substance use disorder [12]. Additionally, pregabalin’s psychoactive effects, including euphoria and sedation, made it appealing to individuals with drug use disorders or those seeking substitutes for opioids [11]. Lately, a case study from Portugal suggested an increasing trend toward the inappropriate use linked with gabapentinoid medications, particularly pregabalin. The authors explained that the occurrence of euphoria, sedation, and dissociation at higher doses appears to be related to its non-medical use potential among migrants in Portugal [13].

The information related to the non-medical use of pregabalin in the developing world is limited and needs immediate attention. A Jordanian study among University students reported that 59% of the individuals with substance use disorders were using pregabalin without obtaining a valid prescription, and 40% were using pregabalin for non-medical purposes [14]. Zergui and colleagues, in their study from Ouargla, Algeria, concluded that pregabalin non-medical use incidents are frequent and are serious public health issues [15]. The ten-year retrospective study from Serbia again reported a continuous rise of substance use disorder cases involving pregabalin non-medical use as isolated drug ingestion [16]. Pregabalin was rated as “Punjab’s new drug menace” in India, and the local government acknowledged the alarming surge in the illicit use and trafficking of pregabalin. Significantly, only in the year 2024, pregabalin-based drug formulations worth Rs 6 crore Indian rupees (≈ Seven hundred thousand US $) were seized. Today, pharmaceutical manufacturers are now required to report all sales and purchases of pregabalin when exceeding fixed limits per invoice to the respective drug control office [17]. Pregabalin is now the second most non-medically used drug in the United Arab Emirates and is gaining popularity among individuals with substance use disorders in Jordan and Turkey. Furthermore, pregabalin non-medical use among high school and college students in Saudi Arabia was also reported [18].

Correlating the non-medical use of pregabalin and the statistics from Pakistan, the numbers are vague, and there seems to be a scarcity of information. Akber et al., in their letter to the editor, claimed gabapentinoids are becoming a target of substance use disorder by drug users in rural areas of Punjab and the agents are mostly used among polydrug addicts [19]. Frequent non-medical use of pregabalin along with gabapentin was reported from field surveillance, and the Quality Control Board of Islamabad city suggested including these drugs in Schedule G of the ICT Drug Sale Rules 2013 [20]. The report also mentioned that pregabalin, the “new Valium”, is gaining popularity among young adults, many of whom are unaware of its substance use disorder potential [20]. Pregabalin is extensively used in Pakistan, including its off-label indications; however, to the best of our knowledge and through extensive literature review, we were unable to find concrete evidence of pregabalin non-medical use in Pakistan. Based on the paucity, we planned to qualitatively assess the motives and reasoning of pregabalin non-medical use in Pakistan.

Methodology

Study design

We adopted a qualitative study design for multiple reasons. As proposed by Lune and Berg, when there is an unavailability of preceding information regarding an exploration, qualitative methods shape the approaches of choice [21]. Qualitative methods are flexible and result in a comprehensive analysis of respondents’ attitudes, experiences, and intentions [22]. Finally, qualitative methods are capable of filling the gaps that remain undiscovered, specifically in underdetermination areas of the research [23]. Consequently, considering the scarcity of information regarding the non-medical use of pregabalin in Pakistan, a qualitative design was a paramount adoption in generating concepts and hypotheses that bear added potential for research compared to other models [22].

For the current study, we used the phenomenological approach, and the rationale for adopting this method was upfront. Because our objective was to emphasize the lived experiences of participants, this approach provided rich and detailed insights into human behaviour and motivations. Through the phenomenological approach, a better understanding of the phenomenon and its impact on individuals is highlighted, aiding in developing new theories, policies, or interventions [24].

Study settings

The research was conducted at a community pharmacy located in the heart of Islamabad, Pakistan. Islamabad is the capital of Pakistan and the tenth most populous city. It has a diverse population with a high literacy rate and a significant portion residing in urban areas. The city’s population is characterized by a relatively young age structure, with a substantial number of people in the working age group.

Study participants, criteria, and sampling

The first author (MH) was involved in a pilot observation, visiting ten different community pharmacies in Islamabad city. He observed an influx of community dwellers demanding pregabalin, and the community pharmacy with the highest number of attendees was selected.

In qualitative research, particularly when emphasizing reflexivity, the interviewer’s expertise is of paramount importance. The first author (MH, Male), who conducted all interviews, holds a Master’s degree in Pharmacy and has received formal training in qualitative research methodologies, including semi-structured interviewing and thematic content analysis. He had prior experience conducting qualitative interviews in health-related research settings. The interviewer had no employment, financial, or professional affiliation with the participating community pharmacy and had no prior relationship with the participants, thereby minimizing potential role conflict and power imbalance. The second and third authors, who served as observers during interviews, also possess academic backgrounds in health sciences and prior exposure to qualitative research. Throughout the data collection and analysis phases, reflexive discussions were conducted within the research team to identify and mitigate potential biases, and discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

Adult community dwellers approaching the pharmacy and demanding pregabalin without a prescription were targeted for the study. It was also required that the selected participants were using pregabalin for a minimum of the past three months and had obtained the drug without the consultant’s prescription. Participants who used pregabalin for therapeutic purposes or had it prescribed by a consultant for managing their disease symptoms were excluded from this study. Lastly, community dwellers who did not consent to participate were also excluded.

For sample size calculation, we adopted a saturation-based approach. We continued the interviews until no additional data was retrievable and the same inference was repetitive. However, we conducted an additional two interviews to endorse category saturation as proposed in the literature [21, 23, 24].

The Interview guide (validation, reliability, and pilot study)

A semi-structured interview guide was constructed in English through an extensive literature review [7, 11, 25, 26]. Parallel, expert panel discussions and shared experience methods also aided in developing the interview guide [27, 28]. It was assured that the guide contained widely framed, open-ended questions that gave enough opportunities to the respondents. At the same time, respondents were also given the freedom to illustrate their narratives and share added information regarding pregabalin purchase and use.

Prior to the pilot study, the interview guide was translated into Urdu (The national language of Pakistan) through a forward-backwards method [29]. A panel of experts (three experienced qualitative researchers, three psychologists and three senior physicians) established the face and content validity of the interview guide. Once the validity was ensured, the guide was piloted with three community dwellers. Respondents of the pilot phase confirmed that the discussion topics were adequate and appropriately phrased to answer research questions. With minimal revisions, the interview guide was made available for the field study (Supplementary File 1). Although the core structure of the semi-structured interview guide remained consistent across interviews, the interviewer adopted a flexible and iterative approach consistent with phenomenological research. Probing questions were used to explore emerging concepts in greater depth, and minor adjustments in wording and follow-up prompts were made as new insights developed during data collection. However, no substantial structural changes were made to the interview guide after the pilot phase. Data and participants of the pilot study were not included in the final analysis.

Reflexivity and researcher positionality

The research team comprised pharmacists, a psychologist, and physicians, all of whom possess professional experience in medication use and substance-related health concerns. We acknowledge that such professional backgrounds may influence data collection and interpretation. To minimize potential bias, participant recruitment was conducted strictly according to predefined eligibility criteria, and the semi-structured interview guide was developed through extensive literature review and expert validation rather than personal assumptions. During data collection and analysis, reflexive discussions were held within the research team to critically examine how our professional perspectives and views on pregabalin misuse might shape questioning, interpretation, and theme development. Coding was conducted iteratively and themes were finalized through consensus to ensure that findings remained grounded in participants’ narratives rather than researchers’ preconceptions [30, 31].

Interview procedure, data collection, and analysis

All interviews were conducted by the first author (MH), who is male. The second author (male) and the third author (female psychologist) served as observers during the interviews. The psychologist was included to enhance the identification of psychological and emotional cues during discussions. The male interviewer conducted all questioning, while observers documented field notes and non-verbal behaviours. Given the sensitive nature of the topics discussed, we are confident that the interviewer-participant gender concordance may have facilitated open communication.

There was no prior relationship between the researchers and the participants before recruitment. Participants were approached at the pharmacy based solely on eligibility criteria. The interviewer had no clinical, financial, or personal association with the pharmacy or respondents. Prior to each interview, participants were informed about the researchers’ academic affiliations, the purpose of the study, and the voluntary and confidential nature of participation.

The interviews were conducted in a separate private room within the pharmacy premises to ensure confidentiality. No pharmacy staff or other individuals were present during the interviews. Only the first author (interviewer) and the designated observers (second and third authors) were present. All participants were informed about the study objectives before the interviews, and an exploring session was conducted at the end of the discussion. Every interview started with an ice-breaking session. The interviewer was given the independence of probing in between conversations to clarify the meanings of responses and to gain insight into the topic being discussed. The observers did not participate in questioning and were present solely to document field notes and non-verbal cues. No participant expressed discomfort regarding their presence.

During the recruitment period, 28 eligible pharmacy attendees were approached for participation. Of these, 13 declined to participate. The most expressed reasons for refusal were lack of time and unwillingness to be audio-recorded. No further demographic information was collected from individuals who declined to respect confidentiality and voluntary participation. While non-participation may introduce potential selection bias, interviews were continued until thematic saturation was achieved [32, 33].

The interviews lasted for approximately thirty minutes and were audio-recorded with the permission of the respondents. Each participant was interviewed once; no repeat interviews were conducted. Interviews were conducted until thematic saturation was reached [32, 33]. The recordings were analysed (verbatim) and presented to the participants with the finalized interview scripts [34]. Once the precision and accuracy of words, ideas, and jargon used during the script analysis were confirmed by the participants, the transcripts were translated into English [30, 35]. We used the NVivo ® for coding and analysis through iterations [36] and inconsistencies were resolved through mutual consensus. All emerging themes and subthemes were discussed among the research team for accuracy and were presented for data inference and interpretation.

Data analysis followed an iterative thematic content analysis approach informed by phenomenological principles. All interviews were transcribed verbatim and read multiple times to achieve familiarization with the data. Open coding was initially conducted to identify meaningful units of text reflecting participants lived experiences of pregabalin use. Codes were generated inductively from the data rather than imposed a priori.

Using constant comparison, codes were examined across transcripts to identify similarities and differences. Conceptually related codes were grouped into broader categories, which were subsequently refined into subthemes and overarching themes through iterative team discussions and consensus. Consistent with the phenomenological orientation, interpretation focused on capturing participants’ subjective meanings, contextual realities, and experiential accounts of non-medical pregabalin use rather than testing predetermined theoretical assumptions.

Data analysis followed an iterative thematic content analysis approach. Open coding was conducted independently by two researchers, followed by grouping of similar codes into categories. Through constant comparison and team-based consensus discussions, categories were refined into subthemes and overarching themes. An example of the coding framework illustrating this progression is provided (Supplementary File 2).

Ethics approval

The institutional review board at the Abdul Wali Khan University Mardan, Pakistan, approved the study. Written informed and publication consent was obtained from each participant prior to the study. Permission to conduct the interviews was also obtained from the proprietor of the community pharmacy. The data was kept anonymous, and participants were also informed regarding their rights to withdraw any information they shared during an interview, as well as their right to withdraw from this study at any time.

Theme 1: Peer recommendation of using pregabalin

We inquired how the participants learned about pregabalin. Almost every participant in our study reported starting pregabalin upon the recommendation of their friends at work and in social circles. Participants explained that they trusted these endorsements because they came from individuals who shared similar challenges in life. The narratives reveal diverse contexts, from alleviating physical pain to managing emotional stress, underscoring the multifaceted motivations behind its adoption.My colleague at the factory is using pregabalin for pain. As I have similar symptoms, I asked him for a recommendation. After using this medicine, I realized it worked well for me, so I am using it regularly. (Respondent 3)

My colleague at the factory is using pregabalin for pain. As I have similar symptoms, I asked him for a recommendation. After using this medicine, I realized it worked well for me, so I am using it regularly. (Respondent 3)

One interviewee stated that soon after starting his current job, he frequently faced restiveness that resulted in sleep loss. He discussed his condition with his colleagues at tea break, who confirmed that he also faced the same issues and started pregabalin on the recommendation of another friend.I was having restless nights and sleeping issues. My friend told me about pregabalin. He said that this medicine would relax me and was not dangerous like paracetamol (which causes ulcers). Since using this drug, I feel calm and, most importantly, have peaceful sleep. Because of this drug, now I am working extra hours and getting additional income. (Respondent 14)

Theme 2: Using pregabalin for chaotic routine and exertion

As seen in Table 1, most of the participants were working as private employees. Although the Government of Pakistan have established rules and regulations, work-life balance is rarely seen in the private sector. According to the European Center for Constitutional and Human Rights, most private workers are cheated out of their rightful wages in Pakistan, do not receive written contracts, and are not registered and insured in the social security system [37]. With such insecurities, private employees are under an excessive workload. This excessive workload results in physical and mental travail and hence becomes a reason to use pregabalin.

My working hours are from 8 am to 5 pm. However, I am often asked to stay for an additional hour or two. I am totally broken (creaking bones and muscles) when I reach my home. To be honest, if I don’t take this drug, I can barely get out of bed the next morning as my whole body feels weak and lethargic. (Respondent 7)

To be honest, if I don’t take this drug, I can barely get out of bed the next morning as my whole body feels weak and lethargic. (Respondent 7)

Another respondent [6] commented that:

My job is physically exhausting. I work as a porter and carry heavy loads all day. I’m always in pain, so I take this medicine to keep me pain-free.

Theme 3: Pregabalin as a performance enhancer

Although several participants framed pregabalin as enhancing productivity, one participant described deliberately using its sedative effects to avoid physically demanding tasks. This divergent account challenges the notion of performance enhancement and illustrates how the drug’s perceived benefits may also serve as a coping mechanism for occupational strain.

Subtheme 3(a): Positive performance enhancer

Pregabalin induces sensations of euphoria, calmness, and excitement. However, due to the drug’s sedative nature, the effects are not necessarily performance-enhancing and can be detrimental. Intriguingly, five of our study respondents claimed that they use pregabalin to improve their performance at their workplace. The drug helps them work overtime and thus generate additional income for themselves.

I am a daily wager. The wage I receive depends upon the number of sewing machines I assemble. By using this drug, I feel energetic and can work for late hours to generate income for myself. (Respondent 2)

I am a daily wager. The wage I receive depends upon the number of sewing machines I assemble. By using this drug, I feel energetic and can work for late hours to generate income for myself. (Respondent 2)

Subtheme 3(b): Negative performance enhancer

As mentioned, pregabalin has ecstatic effects that result in unsteadiness and lethargy. In fact, the drug is known to impact day-to-day activities and results in poor performance negatively. Fascinatingly, the side effects of the drug are shaped as a reason for frequent pregabalin use. Two of our respondents reported to use pregabalin whenever there is additional workload at their employment sides.

Once a week, we receive goods for loading, and that requires brutal physical strength. Often, I start using the medicine a day earlier. People at work can see I am not well; I look exhausted (smiling), and hence, they ask me to do something else instead of loading. This medicine saves me from heavy work (Respondent 6)

Once a week, we receive goods for loading, and that requires brutal physical strength. Often, I start using the medicine a day earlier. People at work can see I am not well; I look exhausted (smiling), and hence, they ask me to do something else instead of loading. This medicine saves me from heavy work (Respondent 6)

Subtheme 3(c): Sexual performance enhancer

Although the literature reports few individuals with increased sexual desire or arousal while taking pregabalin [38, 39], it is not a commonly documented effect. On the contrary, sexual dysfunctions induced by pregabalin is also reported in literature [40]. Nevertheless, pregabalin was mentioned as a sexual performance enhancer by a few of our married respondents.

I frequently use this medicine for improved sexual performance. This drug is economical comparing to other drugs like sildenafil (used local name) and I do not fell no shame in asking this drug from the pharmacies. (Respondent 10)

I frequently use this medicine for improved sexual performance. This drug is economical comparing to other drugs like sildenafil (used local name) and I do not fell no shame in asking this drug from the pharmacies. (Respondent 10)

Theme 4: Psychological or emotional distress

Psychological or emotional distress leads to a range of symptoms, from emotional suffering and difficulties with concentration to physical symptoms like fatigue and sleep problems. Participants of the current study reported using pregabalin to cope with psychosomatic stress. This finding is threatening as pregabalin is known to cause depression and suicidal thoughts, and therefore, a mental assessment is advisable at each visit to the healthcare [41].

My financial condition is not stable. With my current salary, I am unable to pay school fees, daily expenses, utilities, etc. I feel helpless when I see my children and their future. To stay away from such thoughts, I use this drug and find relief. (Respondent 1)

My financial condition is not stable. With my current salary, I am unable to pay school fees, daily expenses, utilities, etc. I feel helpless when I see my children and their future. To stay away from such thoughts, I use this drug and find relief. (Respondent 1)

Respondent eleven described his living conditions as precarious and unstable, often faced with severe challenges related to housing, work, and personal safety. According to him, his living situation is far from ideal and that contributes to physical and emotional stress.My job as a waiter is physically and mentally draining. Likewise, I do not see a bright future for myself. I am clueless about my future, and this causes continuous stress. I use pregabalin to keep going.

My job as a waiter is physically and mentally draining. Likewise, I do not see a bright future for myself. I am clueless about my future, and this causes continuous stress. I use pregabalin to keep going.

Theme 5: Perception regarding pregabalin use

While most participants expressed strong confidence in pregabalin’s safety, a minority reported experiencing restlessness and difficulty concentrating when not using the drug. These accounts suggest emerging dependence-like experiences that contrast with the dominant perception of safety and highlight internal contradictions within participant narratives.

Subtheme 5(a): Supreme confidence and denial of adverse effects

Most of the participants were unaware of the clinical indications of pregabalin. When asked, they had no idea about the actual use and side effects of the drug. According to them, pregabalin was a safe medicine that relieved their pain and stress.If it (pregabalin) had any side effects, then my friend who has been using it for months should have felt it. Subtheme 5(b): Confidence in pregabalin with acceptance of adverse effects

Three participants reported that they had felt certain effects while using pregabalin. These effects are augmented when they do not use the drug (away from work during long holidays). When inquired about the effects, participants described restlessness-like symptoms that force them to take pregabalin even though they are away from physical activity or stress.

Sometimes, I cannot focus on anything, and I feel weird (cannot describe). These feelings go away when I take pregabalin again. I think this medicine works, but it has some side effects, too. Anyhow, until I feel fine, I have no issues using this medicine. (Participant 7)

Discussion

Gaining insights into individuals’ perceptions and knowledge of a particular medicine provides valuable insights to social and health scientists. For that reason, the current study was conducted to try to unveil the reasons and motives for using pregabalin beyond its prescribed medical use among community members of Islamabad city, Pakistan. We are confident that this study will assist policymakers and healthcare practitioners in placing a robust vigilance on pregabalin non-medical use to safeguard the health of general people and secure the resource for rational medical use.

Most of the participants reported that they were introduced to pregabalin by either their friends or coworkers, who encouraged them to use it for relaxation or to improve their focus while working. Our findings corroborate findings from other studies where the most commonly reported source of information and suggestion to use gabapentinoids was friends and coworkers [7, 42–46]. This aspect of asking for recommendations regarding a particular disease or medicine from friends and coworkers is linked to the Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulatiom (CSM) proposed by Leventhal and associates [47]. According to this model, social interactions with friends and peers considerably shape an individual’s beliefs and understanding of their illness and remedial actions. Furthermore, people learn about their illness from various sources, including their own experiences, lay knowledge, and, importantly, from their friends and peers. A key aspect of the CSM explains that if individuals foresee positive or supportive reactions from friends and peers regarding their ailment, they experience constructive obedience and follow what is being advised [47]. This is true in our case, where the study respondents started following their peer recommendations after discussing their ailments and conditions with them. Later, while using pregabalin and experiencing relief of symptoms, the beliefs shape as a practice and hence result in continuous use of pregabalin.

In line with what is being discussed, friends and peers can offer valuable insights into personal experiences and support networks. Nevertheless, it is obvious that they do not have the same level of expertise as healthcare professionals. Community dwellers must realize that while friend and peer recommendations can be helpful for personal choices, they should not replace professional medical advice. However, with the current socioeconomical and health literacy status in Pakistan, it will be unwise to ask community dwellers to opt for professional advice of healthcare professionals. Healthcare is expensive in Pakistan, there is no concept of social security of healthcare insurance and out-of-pocket expense places a colossal burden on the population [48]. In addition, multiple opinions from different health system alternatives are also available in Pakistan, which also hinders getting professional advice. It is high time the community should be educated in masses. Parallel, medicine use without professional advice must be addressed by social and medical researchers and the outcome of such studies should be utilized in policy and decision-making by government officials and members of the health care team.

Coping with fatigue and stress was one major factor contributing to the non-medical use of pregabalin. As reported by many participants, they used pregabalin to minimize physical symptoms such as body aches and weakness and mental issues such as fear of the future and work-life balance. Other than pain, the findings of the current study are opposite to what is reported by a systematic review conducted in 2024. The authors revealed that four motives for gabapentinoid non-medical use were to get high or augmented effects of other substances, to manage pain and mood, and to substitute for another drug [49]. However, non-medical use of pregabalin was evident from our study findings, which is also reported and supported in the literature [50, 51].

An interesting finding of the study was the use of pregabalin as a performance enhancer, including sex. Pregabalin is a medication primarily used to treat neuralgia and epilepsy. Although Osman and Casey did report an enhancement of sexual desire in a patient using pregabalin, it was related to heavy intake of pregabalin and a month’s supply was taken over two days [38]. A possible explanation can be linked to pregabalin’s anxiolytic and analgesic properties rather than directly increasing libido or a direct performance enhancement. Other than that, the literature does not support the use of pregabalin as a performance enhancer in areas of human endeavour. Pregabalin use was linked to increased energy in the body (respondent 6), which helped him to do heavy work with efficiency. While pregabalin can help in improving exertion during exercise and heavy work, the effect is purely related to the analgesic activity [52]. Therefore, the pain-relieving effect is probably perceived as an augmented bodily strength by the respondent and need to be addressed before the continuous non-medical use of pregabalin becomes addictive. On the contrary, one respondent mentioned pregabalin as a negative performance enhancer, however, they mentioned using the side effects of the drug as a repetitive excuse of work. This, again, is alarming and is a clear indication for pregabalin non-medical use, and the phenomenon needs urgent attention.

Most of the participants were unaware of the clinical indication and side effects of pregabalin. They believed pregabalin is safe based on the experience of their friends and colleagues. Their lack of awareness regarding pregabalin’s side effects is one of the factors contributing to the non-medical use of this medicine. Likewise, the free availability of pregabalin at community pharmacies without a prescription plays an augmented effect. In Pakistan, it is a general perception among the local communities that medicines bought without prescriptions are relatively safe and have no side effects. The freedom to buy pharmaceuticals that registered health practitioners have not prescribed is raising serious concerns. While rules and regulations regarding the sales of medicines are devised and imposed by the Drug Regulatory Authority of Pakistan, they are of the least concern to community pharmacies. Frequently, this issue has been addressed in local newspapers and research articles; the authorities show no serious effort in providing strict surveillance towards sales of prescription drugs in Pakistan. Nevertheless, again, we implore the policymakers in Pakistan to take this issue seriously before a catastrophe occurs and becomes a threat for the entire society.

Limitations

Qualitative methods do have limitations. The limited information from the study settings made it hard to compare the data extracted from this study. Furthermore, being qualitative in nature, generalization is always an issue. We recommend conducting a quantitative study based on the identified theme with a larger group of pregabalin users to get a better insight into the non-medical use and substance use disorder related to the drug.

Strengths of study and prospects for further research

Our findings can serve as a baseline study to gain the attention of policymakers to take preventive measures to understand the alarming situation of pregabalin non-medical use. It can pave the way for the development of targeted counselling and rehabilitation interventions for individuals involved in non-medical use of pregabalin.

Conclusion

This study highlights the non-medical use of pregabalin in Islamabad, driven by challenges like difficult living conditions, work-related fatigue, fear of the future, and work-life balance. Many individuals lacked knowledge about pregabalin’s medical use and saw it as a safe medicine for the above-mentioned purposes. The findings emphasize the need for public awareness campaigns, rigorous regulations on pregabalin sales, and accessible support for those individuals involved in non-medical use of pregabalin.
