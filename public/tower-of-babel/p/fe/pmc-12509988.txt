Parental intake of high-carotenoid orange corn diet alleviated glucocorticoid and fear response but not growth in F1 Pekin ducks
Authors: Esther M Oluwagbenga, Evan Rocheford, Torbert Rocheford, Melanie M Bergman, Jenna M Schober, Gregory S Fraley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProlonged heat stress (HS) in poultry has both immediate and transgenerational effects. Carotenoids, with their antioxidant properties, may enhance stress tolerance by reducing oxidative damage. This study assessed the effects of parental HS on offspring (F1) and whether feeding high-carotenoid orange corn (OC) to breeders mitigates these effects. Breeder Pekin ducks were fed either OC or yellow corn (YC) for 3 weeks before and during a 3-week HS exposure. A control breeder group (CON) was kept under thermoneutral conditions with a YC diet. Eggs collected in the final 3 days of HS. CON eggs were collected during the same days and incubated in the same incubator as OC and YC groups. Resulting offspring was allocated to treatment based on parental treatment (n = 56/group: OCF1, YCF1, CONF1) and reared to 5 weeks of age. Weekly body weight (n = 20/group), body condition scores (n = 20 ducks/treatment), and novel object test were measured. At week 3, ducks (n = 8/injection type/group) received Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH; 0.0625 mg/kg) or saline, and blood was collected at 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 h post-injection for glucocorticoid and heterophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (HLR) analysis. Corticosterone increased at 1 h in all ACTH-injected groups (p < 0.001) and remained elevated in YCF1 at 2 h compared to the CONF1 and OCF1 treatments. Baseline cortisol was higher in F1 from HS parents than CONF1 (p < 0.01) and remained elevated at 1 and 4 h post-ACTH (p < 0.05). HLR at 3 h was higher in YCF1-ACTH compared to CONF1-ACTH (p < 0.05). Body weight was higher in CONF1 compared to OCF1 and YCF1 at weeks 2 - 5 (p < 0.05). Fearful behavior was higher in YCF1 at weeks 1 (p < 0.01), 2 (p < 0.01), and 4 (p = 0.09) compared to CONF1. In conclusion, while parental HS reduced growth in F1 ducks, OC-fed breeders produced offspring without the exaggerated physiological responses to stress. These observations suggest a transgenerational effect of parental OC diet on stress-related physiological responses in the offspring.Keywords: Parental effect, Orange corn, Carotenoid, Heat stress, Poultry

Introduction

Heat stress (HS) is a critical global challenge facing poultry production. Exposure to HS especially during breeder phase has been established to impair reproductive performance (Li et al., 2020; Yan et al., 2022), growth performance (Al-Sagan et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2014), immune function (Hirakawa et al., 2020), and overall welfare of poultry. The impact of HS on breeder poultry extends beyond the exposed generation, affecting offspring through epigenetic modifications, known as transgenerational or maternal effect (Charrier et al., 2022, 2024; Zimmer and Spencer, 2014). In poultry, these transgenerational effects are expressed as altered behavior, hypersensitive hypothalamic pituitary adrenal (HPA) axis activity, and suppressed immune responses in progeny, even when offspring are not directly exposed to HS (Oluwagbenga et al., 2023a; Santana et al., 2021; Videla et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2017). This emphasizes the importance of developing a sustainable strategy to mitigate the impact of HS across generations in poultry.

Nutritional interventions during HS events provide a promising strategy to mitigate some of these adverse effects of exposure to HS. Carotenoids, a natural pigment with potent antioxidants and immunomodulatory properties, have gained attention over the years due to their role in improving oxidative balance and enhancing immune function (Ma et al., 2024; Madkour et al., 2025). Corn, a primary energy source in poultry diets, contains a range of carotenoids including lutein, zeaxanthin, β-cryptoxanthin, α-carotene, and β-carotene (Popa et al., 2025). Orange corn (OC) has been developed to contain substantially greater concentrations of carotenoids compared to conventional yellow corn (YC) (Abraham et al., 2021). Previous studies have shown that diets formulated with high-carotenoid OC improved immune reactivity to challenges, improved footpad health, and provided more intense yolk pigmentation in broiler breeders and laying hens (Abraham et al., 2021; Nogareda et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2018). These benefits make OC a beneficial nutritional enrichment in poultry diets.

Carotenoids may exert transgenerational effects when included in parental diets in addition to the benefits towards the breeders themselves. Maternal carotenoid intake has been associated with deposition of immune-related compounds into the egg, improved antioxidant capacity during embryogenesis, and enhanced resistance to disease post-hatch (Cucco et al., 2007; Gong et al., 2023). One of the studies on maternal carotenoid intake showed that F1 offspring from breeders fed carotenoid-supplemented diet accumulated higher levels of carotenoids in their own tissues when fed carotenoid-enriched diets post-hatch, indicating a possible priming or cumulative effect (Koutsos et al., 2003). Therefore, incorporating high-carotenoid OC into breeder diet may serve as a practical and non-invasive strategy to enhance offspring stress response and welfare. The interactive effects of parental HS and dietary carotenoid intake on stress response, growth performance, and behavior of offspring is poorly understood in poultry.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the transgenerational effects of parental HS and carotenoid supplementation on offspring stress response, growth performance, and behavior in Pekin ducks. Our results showed that parental carotenoid intake suppressed glucocorticoid and fear responses, despite no dietary effect on body weight or body condition, suggesting a transgenerational effect of parental OC diet on stress-related physiological responses in the offspring.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

Parent stock. 224 hens and 56 drakes at 31 weeks of age were randomly allocated to 4 rooms with 2 pens in each room (n = 28 hens and 7 drakes/pen). The breeder Pekin ducks were fed either an OC or YC diet for 3 weeks prior to HS and during 3 weeks of HS exposure (n = 4 pens/treatment). The ducks were subjected to cyclic HS of 35°C for 10h/day and decreased to 29.5°C for the remaining 14h/day for 3 weeks. Eggs laid were collected from the last 3 days of the HS period for incubation. Nest boxes, feed, water, and lighting were provided according to standards (Chen et al., 2021). Findings related to the diet carotenoid levels, production and welfare of the breeders have been reported (Oluwagbenga et al., 2025a). All procedures were approved by the Purdue Animal Care and Use Committee (PACUC # 1223002464).

F1 stock. Eggs (n = 154 OC eggs and 206 YC eggs) from the parent were incubated according to industry standards (Chen et al., 2021) and candling was done on day 10. The resulting hatchlings were raised as a grow-out meat duck for 5 weeks while the control (CON) F1 were obtained from Maple Leaf Farms from breeders raised on an identical commercial YC diet under thermoneutral conditions (20-22°C, relative humidity ∼65 %). The breeder ducks exposed to HS and diet treatment were also obtained from the same farm. The CON F1 breeders were never exposed to any HS. All hatchlings were weighed and placed into pens based on the parental treatment (OCF1, YCF1, and CONF1). We utilized 56 ducks per treatment which was randomly allocated to pens across two rooms to minimize environmental variations. Each room contained four pens, with each pen allocated to one of the treatment groups in an alternating manner. The ducks were exposed to an 18:6 light cycle, thermoneutral temperature of 20-22°C according to standards and allowed ad lib access to feed and to water via nipple lines as per industry standards (Chen et al., 2021).

Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) challenge

ACTH challenge was done at 3 weeks of age. Ducks (n = 8/injection type/treatment) were randomly selected and injected intramuscularly with cosyntropin, a synthetic form of ACTH (amino acids 1-24; 0.0625 mg/kg). Saline with 0.1 % mannitol was used as the vehicle control. Blood samples were collected from the tibia vein of injected ducks at 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 hours relative to ACTH or vehicle injection, with 0 representing timepoint prior to injection for the determination of serum glucocorticoids (GC) and heterophil to lymphocyte ratio (HLR) as described below. This yielded 6 treatment groups which includes CONF1-ACTH, CONF1-Saline, OCF1-ACTH, OCF1-Saline, YCF1-ACTH, YCF1-Saline. Complete blood count was done to determine HLR by certified avian pathologists (Antech Diagnostics, Chicago, IL) who were unaware of treatment groups. The HLR was calculated by dividing the heterophil count by the lymphocyte count. All injected birds were euthanized with pentobarbital (Fatal Plus, 396mg/ml/kg) on the second day following ACTH administration. The liver, spleen, and bursa were removed and weighed, with organ weights expressed as a percentage of body weight (organ weight (g)/bodyweight (g) * 100).

ELISA for glucocorticoids

The corticosterone (kit #16063) and cortisol (kit #560360) assays were sourced from Cayman Chemicals and the procedure performed according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Kit verification details have been previously reported (Tetel et al., 2022a; b). In summary, samples were incubated overnight at 4°C. 250 vials of Ellman’s Reagent were reconstituted with 50 mL of ultrapure water, and 200 μL of the reagent was added to each well for plate development. Plates were then placed on an orbital shaker for 90 minutes and read at 405 nm using a SynergyLx (Biotek) plate reader.

Production and welfare assessments

All ducks were weighed on day of hatch and weekly body weight and feed intake was recorded at weeks 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. Ducks were randomly selected for body condition scores (BCS) such as foot pad quality, eyes, nostrils, feather cleanliness and quality (n = 20/treatment) at weeks 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 using a rubric previously published by our lab (Fraley et al., 2013; Karcher et al., 2013; Oluwagbenga et al., 2022). Each parameter was scored on a scale from 0 to 2, where 0 indicated the best condition, 1 represented a moderate condition, and 2 being a worse condition. Very few observations received a score of 2, therefore, scores of 1 and 2 were combined for footpad analysis.

Novel object test (NOT)

NOT was conducted at weeks 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 for all ducks in each pen (n = 31-56/treatment) to assess the ducks’ behavioral responses to red laser light. The NOT procedure was performed according to a previous report with some modifications (Uitdehaag et al., 2008) and as previously reported in our lab (Oluwagbenga et al., 2023a, 2025b). The responses of the ducks to the laser light were categorized as either “fearful” (moving or running away from the light), “passive” (looking at the light or holding still), and “approach” (moving towards the light). Video recordings were taken for 1 minute at each timepoint. Duck responses were recorded by pausing the video every 10 seconds and calculating the percentage of ducks exhibiting each behavior. For each 10 seconds observation, ducks were assigned a binary score: observations with ≥5 % response were coded as 1 (high fear, passive or approach response), and those with <5 % as 0 (low fear, passive or approach response). These binary data were then analyzed to estimate odds ratios and determine whether treatment significantly influenced the likelihood of high fear, passive and approach responses over time.

Statistical analyses

The duck was considered the statistical unit for all data. For GC and HLR data, outliers were identified and removed based on the 2-sigma rule and analyzed using a mixed model with random effect of ducks to account for repeated sampling. Post hoc analyses were performed using the emmeans package with Tukey method adjustment in R. The NOT data and body condition scores were analyzed using logistic regression models fitted by penalized maximum likelihood to generate odds ratio. Hatch body weight and organ weights were analyzed using 1-way ANOVA while bodyweight data were analyzed using 2-way ANOVA. A p ≤ 0.05 was considered significant while a trend towards significance was set at p < 0.1.

Heterophil to lymphocyte ratio

At 2 hours post-injection, HLR was significantly higher (p < 0.001) in all ACTH-injected groups compared to the saline-injected groups. By 3 hours, HLR remained elevated in the YCF1-ACTH group compared to CONF1-ACTH (p < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed between OCF1-ACTH and CONF1-ACTH. Additionally, HLR in CONF1-Saline was lower than in OCF1-ACTH (p = 0.07) and significantly lower than in YCF1-ACTH (p < 0.001). Still at 3 hours, both OCF1-ACTH (p < 0.05) and YCF1-ACTH (p < 0.001) showed higher HLR values compared to OCF1-Saline and YCF1-Saline. No significant differences were detected at 0, 1, or 4 hours relative to injection. These results suggest a more pronounced HLR response to ACTH stimulation in YCF1 compared to OCF1, specifically at the 3-hour timepoint (Table 1).

Hatch and body weight

There was no significant difference in hatch body weight among the treatment groups (Fig. 1A). However, weekly body weight in the CONF1 group tended to be higher than OCF1 at week 2 (p = 0.07) and was significantly higher than both OCF1 and YCF1 at week 3 (p < 0.05) and week 4 (p < 0.001). By week 5, body weight remained higher in CONF1 than YCF1 (p < 0.05), while OCF1 showed a tendency toward higher body weight than YCF1 (p = 0.07) (Fig. 1B). These results suggest that parental exposure to HS reduced F1 body weight, regardless of the parental diet.

Discussion

The purpose of our study was to determine the effect of parental exposure to HS on post-hatch performance, behavior, and welfare of the F1 generation and how parental exposure to OC diet could help alleviate these effects. To achieve this, we fed adult breeder ducks OC or YC diet for 6 weeks, 3 weeks before and during 3 weeks of HS. The resulting hatchlings from eggs collected during the last 3 days of HS were raised as meat type ducks and evaluated for stress-related parameters. We observed that F1 offspring from HS parents fed OC diet exhibited a significant reduction in serum corticosterone levels, lower HLR and fear responses, comparable to the CONF1 following ACTH stimulation. Parental exposure to HS decreased body weight and increased fearfulness of offspring with significant effects of parental diet on fearfulness but not body weight. Our results suggest a transgenerational effect of parental OC diet on stress-related physiological responses in the offspring.

The maternal transfer of carotenoids into the yolk and their subsequent uptake by developing embryos has been previously studied and showed the potential for embryonic and long-term physiological effects in the offspring. In another part of our study, breeder ducks fed a high-carotenoid OC diet laid eggs with more pigmented yolks, suggesting increased carotenoid deposition (Oluwagbenga et al., 2025). Similarly, Koutsos et al. (2003) reported elevated carotenoid concentrations in the egg yolks of hens supplemented with carotenoid-enriched diets. These yolk-derived carotenoids were later detected in the tissues of the offspring for up to four weeks post-hatch, indicating sustained bioavailability. Further, maternally derived yolk-carotenoid has been shown to enhance carotenoid accumulation in various tissues of the F1 when also fed a carotenoid rich diet, suggesting a cumulative effect (Koutsos et al., 2003). Hens fed carotenoid-rich alfalfa extracts laid eggs with significantly higher yolk carotenoid concentrations, resulting in chicks whose hepatic carotenoid levels were initially 29-fold higher than controls and remained elevated for at least the first week of life (Karadas et al., 2005). In our study, the rapid reduction in serum corticosterone levels observed in F1 progeny from OC-fed parents may be attributed to enhanced antioxidant capacity conferred by maternally derived yolk carotenoids. Carotenoids are known for their role in scavenging free radicals and reducing oxidative damage (Kljak and Grbeša, 2015), which are important in maintaining physiological homeostasis under stress conditions. Improved antioxidant defenses may enable more efficient HPA axis regulation, allowing birds to mount a GC response when stimulated and return to baseline levels more rapidly which likely contributes to greater stress tolerance in the offspring.

In our study, the baseline cortisol levels were elevated in the F1 from HS parents, irrespective of whether the parental diet included OC or YC, when compared to those from control parents. This finding suggests that parental exposure to HS exerts a lasting physiological influence on the stress regulatory system of the offspring. Interestingly, this observation contrasts with our previous findings, where parental HS exposure was associated with reduced cortisol levels in the offspring (Oluwagbenga et al., 2023a). It is possible that the interaction between HS and reproductive phase in the breeders induced differential epigenetic programming of the offspring’s HPA axis, leading to different outcomes. Studies have shown that maternal GC can be transferred into the egg during oogenesis or egg formation, thereby acting as an early hormonal cue to the developing embryo (Caulfield and Padula, 2020; Oluwagbenga et al., 2022, 2023b; Rettenbacher et al., 2005, 2009). This prenatal hormone exposure may play a role in the developmental programming of the HPA axis (Charrier et al., 2022, 2024; Welberg and Seckl, 2001; Zimmer and Spencer, 2014) that can manifest as heightened baseline and exaggerated GC responses to stressors later in life. These same observations have been previously reported in offspring of mammals and birds subjected to maternal stress during critical reproductive windows (Kapoor and Matthews, 2005; Oluwagbenga et al., 2023a). Serum cortisol levels in the CONF1 group injected with saline showed a significant increase from 2 hours post-injection but this pattern was not observed in serum corticosterone. This unexpected increase may be attributed to stress from repeated handling during sampling, which can transiently activate the HPA axis. Alternatively, it may reflect a random fluctuation unrelated to the treatment as the lack of a corresponding increase in corticosterone suggests hormone-specific variability. Our results support the previous studies, indicating that maternal exposure to stress may program offspring stress response and this may persist beyond the hatchling phase.

A more pronounced HLR response was observed in offspring from HS parents fed the YC diet compared to those fed the OC diet, suggesting that maternal carotenoid supplementation may have partially alleviated the hypersensitive HLR response associated with parental HS exposure. Despite this difference in HLR, we did not observe any significant changes in the relative weights of immune organs, such as the spleen or liver, among treatments. Previous findings from our laboratory showed that parental HS elevated HLR in F1 Pekin ducks, indicating changes in immune profile that persists beyond hatching (Oluwagbenga et al., 2023a). Interestingly, this observation contrasts with findings in Japanese quail, where no significant change in offspring HLR was noted following parental exposure to HS (Videla et al., 2020). In another study, the offspring from laying hens implanted with corticosterone showed a lower antibody response to challenge and lower bursa of Fabricius mass when compared to offspring from the untreated hens (Henriksen et al., 2013). Studies on the transgenerational effects of parental HS or dietary carotenoid intake on immune parameters, specifically HLR in poultry, are very limited. However, it is well established in poultry that maternal transfer of immune components including immunoglobulins and other immune factors occurs via the egg (Keyburn et al., 2013; Methner and Steinbach, 1997; Sahin et al., 2003; Yamawaki et al., 2021). Supplementation of maternal diets with carotenoids, such as β-carotene, have been shown to increase the deposition of immune-related factors in eggs (Gong et al., 2023) and enhance antibacterial properties (Cucco et al., 2007). However, the downstream impacts of these transferred immune factors on the immunocompetence of the offspring are not fully understood.

Offspring from HS parents exhibited a significantly lower body weight compared to those from control parents, irrespective of parental diet. Our observed differences in body weight are consistent with findings from previous studies, that reported reduced post-hatch weight gain in Japanese quail following parental HS exposure (Santana et al., 2021) or maternal exposure to unstable social environments (Guibert et al., 2010). One of the possible mechanisms underlying this growth impairment is the maternal deposition of GCs into the egg, that have been extensively reported during maternal stress exposure by our lab and others (Caulfield and Padula, 2020; Hayward et al., 2015; Oluwagbenga et al., 2022, 2023b; Rettenbacher et al., 2005, 2009). Several studies have reported that prenatal exposure to corticosterone results in decreased hatch weight and reduced growth rate in poultry and other avian species (Hayward and Wingfield, 2004; Heiblum et al., 2001; Love et al., 2008; Saino et al., 2005; Zhu et al., 2017). This was further supported by the findings reported by Peixoto et al. (2020) where in ovo administration of corticosterone significantly decreased the body weight of White Leghorn chicks, with differences persisting up to 17 weeks post-hatch. These alterations in phenotype of the offspring may be mediated through epigenetic mechanisms. Specifically, maternal GC exposure may induce lasting modifications in gene expression patterns within the hypothalamus, an organ central to both metabolic and stress regulation, of the developing embryo (Charrier et al., 2022, 2024; Zimmer and Spencer, 2014). Interestingly, in our study, despite the observed reduction in body weight of offspring from HS parents, feed intake and FCR remained unaffected at the corresponding time points. These observations suggest that the lower body weight is not attributable to altered feed intake or efficiency but likely rather to developmental programming. Further, parental carotenoid intake had no significant impact on the body weight of offspring, suggesting that it was insufficient to counteract or alleviate the growth-suppressive effects of parental HS in this study.

Parental exposure to HS was associated with an increase in fearful behavior in the offspring, with more pronounced fearfulness and lower approach response to novel object in the offspring from HS parents fed YC diet during weeks 1 and 5 of age. These results are consistent with our earlier findings, where parental HS exposure was linked to elevated fearfulness in the offspring (Oluwagbenga et al., 2023a). Janczak et al. (2007) reported that White Leghorn hens subjected to unpredictable feed restriction produced offspring that spent a longer duration in tonic immobility, an indicator of fearfulness. Similarly, maternal exposure to mild and repeated stressors in Japanese quails resulted in offspring that exhibited heightened caution and increased fear responses to novel environments (Guibert et al., 2010, 2011, 2013). Similar findings have been reported by studies utilizing in ovo administration of GCs. Janczak et al. (2006) and Freire et al. (2006) reported that embryonic exposure to GCs induced increased fear-like behaviors post-hatch in chickens. One of the possible mechanisms underlying these transgenerational effects may also involve stress-induced epigenetic modifications. Charrier et al. (2022) reported changes in epigenetic markers within key brain regions involved in emotional regulation, including the hippocampus, paraventricular nucleus, and dorsal amygdala. These results suggest that maternal perception of environmental instability or stress may be encoded and transmitted to the next generation, potentially as an adaptive mechanism. In contrast, parental carotenoid intake did not significantly affect fear-related behavior in offspring in the present study. Studies on the impact of parental carotenoid intake on behavioral changes in the offspring are very scarce. Cucco et al. (2006) found that dietary beta-carotene supplementation in grey partridges did not influence offspring antipredator responses which partially support our findings. The absence of parental diet effects at a later age may indicate a compensatory adaptation in offspring or that the effects of parental exposure to HS override those of the parental dietary treatments as development progresses. These results suggest that parental carotenoid intake may exert a strong influence on emotional reactivity or fearfulness in the offspring during the early phase of life, particularly when the offspring are not subjected to postnatal environmental stressors.

In conclusion, our findings showed that offspring from heat-stressed parents exhibit heightened stress, and compromised growth and behavior. However, parental OC diet intake may exert transgenerational effect on stress-related physiological responses in the offspring such as glucocorticoid and fear responses. These results emphasize the need for further research into the epigenetics changes underlying the effects of parental carotenoid intake on offspring phenotypes.
