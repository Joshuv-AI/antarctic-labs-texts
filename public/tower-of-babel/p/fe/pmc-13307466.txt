Survival prediction after cardiac resynchronization therapy: Clinical scores vs left ventricular ejection fraction
Authors: Joe Demian, Aritra Paul, WH Wilson Tang, J Emanuel Finet, Niraj Varma, Arshneel Kochar, Oussama M Wazni, John Rickard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is commonly used to characterize remodeling and estimate prognosis after cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT). Clinical scores assessed at follow-up may provide complementary prognostic information.ObjectiveThis study compared the predictive value of LVEF and clinical scores, measured at 6 months after CRT, for long-term survival.MethodsThis prospective cohort study enrolled 528 patients undergoing CRT between May 2016 and June 2022. Clinical scores, including the 6-minute walk distance, gait speed, the timed up-and-go test, the Study of Osteoporotic Fractures frailty index, and the 5-level EuroQol-5 dimension score, were evaluated at 6 months after CRT alongside LVEF. The primary end point was event-free survival, defined as survival without left ventricular assist device implantation, heart transplantation, or death. Using a nested model approach, Cox proportional hazards models assessed predictive accuracy using the concordance index (C-index).ResultsOver a mean follow-up of 3.73 years, 127 patients (24%) reached the primary end point. In models adjusted for baseline covariates, LVEF at 6 months was associated with event-free survival (C-index 0.732), whereas several clinical scores showed higher discrimination (C-index: 6-minute walk distance 0.767; Study of Osteoporotic Fractures frailty index 0.754; timed up-and-go test 0.749; 5-level EuroQol-5-dimension score 0.740). Findings were consistent when using clinically relevant cut points. In LVEF-adjusted models, high-performing clinical measures provided incremental prognostic value beyond LVEF.ConclusionAlthough LVEF remains a valuable metric, a multidimensional approach incorporating clinical scores alongside LVEF may improve long-term survival prediction after CRT. Their integration into clinical practice could enhance personalized care and risk stratification for CRT recipients.Keywords: Cardiac resynchronization therapy, Left ventricular ejection fraction, Clinical scores, Survival, Survival prediction, Heart failure

Introduction

Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) has altered the management of chronic systolic heart failure, leading to a substantial decrease in cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Its impact on cardiac functional metrics, particularly left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), is well documented.1,2 The reverse remodeling attributes of CRT are well recognized and described in patients with symptomatic and mildly symptomatic heart failure.1,3, 4, 5 A commonly used definition of response is the presence of reverse ventricular remodeling, partly described by changes in LVEF.6 Some studies have even demonstrated a significant association between clinical response and LVEF improvement specifically.7 Thus, LVEF is frequently used in clinical practice as a marker of therapeutic response and as a surrogate for prognosis after CRT.

Notwithstanding the proven benefits of CRT, not all patients exhibit a traditional positive response to the therapy. This realization has led to numerous studies attempting to predict favorable outcomes after CRT.8 Some of these studies evaluated patient response based on various outcome measures, such as hospitalization rates and survival.8,9 All-cause and heart failure–related mortality have been used as criteria to define nonresponse.9 This approach seems valid, given that sustained reverse remodeling of the failing left ventricle after CRT has been known to decrease mortality.5 An association between baseline echocardiographic parameters and mortality and other cardiac events has already been established, especially for LVEF.10,11

However, despite its widespread use, LVEF alone may not fully capture the complexity of myocardial and systemic remodeling processes induced by CRT. Consequently, clinical scores may offer complementary predictive value by providing a more comprehensive risk stratification. In this study, we aimed to compare clinical scores and LVEF—assessed at 6 months after CRT—as predictors of long-term survival after CRT.12

Methods

Study population

Patients undergoing CRT between May 2016 and June 2022 at the Cleveland Clinic in Cleveland, OH, were enrolled in this prospective cohort study. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were designed to capture a broad cohort and did not impose requirements for LVEF or QRS duration for participation in the final cohort. Before CRT device implantation, each patient underwent a comprehensive evaluation, which included a 12-lead surface electrocardiogram to measure QRS complex duration and an assessment of New York Heart Association (NYHA) class. All participants were on optimal medical therapy in accordance with current clinical guidelines for at least 3 months before undergoing the CRT procedure and for the whole study duration. In addition, all eligible patients who received a defibrillator device met the guideline criteria for implantable cardioverter defibrillator therapy.13 The research reported in this paper adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study received approval from the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board, and all participants signed written informed consent before enrollment.

Patient evaluation

Detailed and paired echocardiographic data were collected at baseline and at the 6-month follow-up. Clinical scores were assessed at the 6-month follow-up. Echocardiograms were digitized to measure left ventricular end-diastolic volume and left ventricular end-systolic volume using Simpson’s method of discs, in accordance with the guidelines of the American Society of Echocardiography. LVEF was determined through visual inspection, supplemented by Simpson’s calculation.14 Functional capacity was evaluated using the 6-minute walk distance (6MWD) (ft), whereas frailty was assessed using gait speed (m/s), the timed up-and-go test (TUG) (seconds), and the Study of Osteoporotic Fractures (SOF) frailty index. Quality of life was measured using the 5-level EuroQol-5 dimension (EQ-5D-5L) score. Clinical cutoffs commonly used in routine practice at our center for this patient population included LVEF of ≤35%, 6MWD of <1200 ft, gait speed of <0.8 m/s, TUG of >12 seconds, SOF frailty index of ≥2, and EQ-5D-5L score of >5.

CRT system

In the overall cohort, CRT devices were predominantly implanted transvenously by electrophysiologists, with the left ventricular lead positioned in a lateral or posterolateral vein whenever possible. In cases where transvenous lead placement was not feasible owing to procedural challenges, a minimally invasive epicardial lead was implanted via a mini thoracotomy by a cardiothoracic surgeon. CRT devices were typically programmed with an atrioventricular sensed delay of 100 ms and a paced delay of 130 ms, with optimization conducted in accordance with the Cleveland Clinic’s standard protocols. Medications were documented immediately before CRT device implantation, with subsequent adjustments made at the discretion of patients’ treating physicians.

Primary end point

The primary composite end point was long-term event-free survival. Events included death from any cause or a nonfatal heart-failure event, whichever came first. A nonfatal heart-failure event was in turn defined as either heart transplantation or left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation. Heart-failure events were diagnosed by physicians and adjudicated by a committee.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges and categorical variables as frequencies with percentages. Time zero for all analyses was the date of CRT implantation, with outcomes captured from the time of implantation onward. In rare instances where a patient was unable to complete a specific clinical assessment owing to symptom severity, that measure was treated as missing and the corresponding model was fit excluding the observation in question. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to compute cumulative event-free survival and the cumulative incidence for each event separately. For the sake of this study, we established a nested multivariate model of a priori determined predictors of long-term survival, which we will refer to as the base Cox proportional hazards model. The assumptions of the Cox proportional hazards model were respected. Key baseline variables that were statistically significant and/or clinically relevant were included in the model.

Given that the base model served as the benchmark for evaluating improvements when the additional predictors were added, its concordance index (C-index) was calculated before the inclusion of said predictors. Then, each parameter of interest (ie, the clinical score or LVEF at 6 months) was individually incorporated into the base model to determine which one provided the best model fit for predicting long-term survival free of LVAD or heart transplant. Their respective predictive accuracy was compared using each subsequent model’s respective C-index, and the improvement in C-index they yielded was compared with the base model. Parameters that produced the greatest C-index improvement were determined to be the best model fit and hence the most appropriate predictors of long-term survival free of LVAD or heart transplant. For each model, 95% confidence intervals were estimated using bootstrap resampling. Nested Cox proportional hazards models were further compared using likelihood ratio tests as a sensitivity analysis. To test whether associations differed by CRT response status, score × responder interaction terms were evaluated using likelihood ratio tests and included in Cox models. To assess incremental prognostic value beyond LVEF and whether combined models improve performance, additional nested Cox models were constructed using a reference model that included the base covariates and LVEF. The parameters demonstrating the strongest prognostic contribution in the main analysis were subsequently added individually and in combination, and models were compared using likelihood ratio tests. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.2).

Results

Population overview

A total of 528 patients were enrolled in the study and successfully underwent CRT. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. The median age of the population was 70.60 years (62.34–77.82), with a median body mass index of 27.77 kg/m2 (24.50–32.14) and the majority being male (65.7%). Median LVEF at baseline was 29% (23–35). Frequent comorbidities included ischemic cardiomyopathy in 35.4% of patients, atrial fibrillation/flutter in 41.1%, and diabetes mellitus in 22.5%. Most patients were categorized as either NYHA functional class II (49.4%) or III (40.7%), and 55.7% had left bundle branch block. Furthermore, 80.1% of patients received CRT devices equipped with a defibrillator. These characteristics reflect a diverse yet clinically typical population of patients with heart failure undergoing CRT. Median time-to-clinic-visit was 7.43 months (interquartile range 6.14–9.08). At follow-up, LVEF increased to a median of 40% (31–47) from 29% (23–35) at baseline.

Model fit

When each parameter of interest was individually added to the base model, most clinical scores seemed to exhibit better model fit than LVEF given that they provided greater improvement to the base model’s C-index. Among the clinical scores, the 6MWD was the strongest predictor of long-term survival, improving the C-index to 0.767, followed by the SOF frailty index, which improved the C-index to 0.754. Other scores, such as the TUG and the EQ-5D-5L, also enhanced predictive performance, with C-indices of 0.749 and 0.740, respectively. LVEF, although still a significant predictor, yielded a smaller improvement in model performance, increasing the C-index to 0.732. Gait speed, the weakest performer among the clinical scores, showed a marginal C-index increase to 0.722. Each subsequent model’s C-index and the relative improvement each parameter yielded when incorporated into the nested model are presented in Table 3. In the likelihood ratio test sensitivity analysis (Supplemental Table 1), 6MWD (likelihood ratio statistic [LR χ2] = 37.9), TUG (22.5), SOF frailty index (19.8), and EQ-5D-5L (14.7) each improved overall model fit more than LVEF (10.1) when added individually to the base model. Associations for 6MWD (P = .193), SOF frailty index (P = .630), and EQ-5D-5L (P = .593) were similar in echocardiographic responders and nonresponders, whereas gait speed (P = .022) and TUG (P = .019) showed evidence of effect modification by response status (Supplemental Table 2).

Clinical cutoffs

To further assess the utility of the parameters of interest, clinical cutoffs were incorporated into the nested model. These cutoffs provided additional insights into long-term risk stratification, as presented in Table 4. A 6MWD of less than 1200 ft and an SOF frailty index of 2 or higher were among the most effective thresholds, with C-indices of 0.760 and 0.755, respectively. These cutoffs performed relatively better than an LVEF of ≤35%, which yielded a C-index of 0.728. Even gait speed, which seemed to be a weaker predictor when analyzed as a continuous variable, demonstrated higher predictive performance (C-index of 0.744) than LVEF when categorized using their respective cutoffs. In the corresponding sensitivity analysis of cutoff-based models (Supplemental Table 1), the model-fit improvement was greater for the cutoffs of 6MWD (LR χ2 = 26.3), TUG (25.3), gait speed (24.2), and SOF frailty index (19.6) than for that of LVEF (7.9) when adding each to the base model individually.

Combined models

Clinical measures that demonstrated the strongest prognostic contribution in the main analysis also provided incremental value beyond LVEF when incorporated into LVEF-adjusted models (Table 5). Compared with the reference model including the base covariates and LVEF, inclusion of 6MWD was associated with the greatest improvement in model fit (LR χ2 = 32.9; P < .001), followed by TUG (LR χ2 = 17.8; P < .001), SOF frailty index (LR χ2 = 16.4; P < .001), and EQ-5D-5L (LR χ2 = 13.3; P < .001), in line with the primary analysis. Consistent with its comparatively weaker contribution earlier, gait speed did not add meaningful prognostic value once LVEF was already accounted for (LR χ2 = 0.28; P = .59). Importantly, combined models incorporating multiple high-performing clinical measures further strengthened prognostic performance compared with LVEF alone, including models combining LVEF with 6MWD and SOF frailty index (LR χ2 = 30.4; P < .001) or with 6MWD and EQ-5D-5L (LR χ2 = 32.9; P < .001) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study evaluated the predictive accuracy of clinical scores and LVEF for long-term survival after CRT. Traditionally, LVEF has been a cornerstone metric in assessing cardiac function and guiding therapeutic decisions in CRT recipients. However, our findings suggest that relying on LVEF alone may not fully capture post-CRT risk, and clinical scores assessing functional capacity, frailty, and quality of life may improve post-CRT prognostic assessment by providing a more comprehensive characterization of patient status at follow-up.

Predictive value of clinical scores

The primary results demonstrate that although LVEF is a significant predictor, clinical scores such as the 6MWD, SOF frailty index, the TUG, and the EQ-5D-5L demonstrated higher predictive accuracy in forecasting long-term survival. In particular, the 6MWD, which measures functional capacity, emerged as a strong predictor when incorporated into a nested Cox proportional hazards model, providing more incremental predictive value than LVEF. These findings align with previous evidence suggesting that post-CRT survival correlates strongly with measures of both reverse ventricular remodeling and overall functional improvement rather than with symptomatic relief alone.15 In interaction analyses stratified by echocardiographic response category, the associations of 6MWD, SOF frailty index, and EQ-5D-5L with outcomes were similar across subgroups, supporting their role as post-CRT prognostic markers regardless of response status. Gait speed and TUG showed evidence of effect modification, warranting confirmation in independent cohorts.

Added value with clinical cutoffs

The use of cutoffs further reinforced the robustness of clinical scores. Established thresholds, such as a 6MWD of less than 1200 ft and an SOF frailty index of 2 or higher, delivered better predictive performance than the widely used LVEF cutoff of ≤35%. Notably, even gait speed, which showed limited predictive power as a continuous variable, achieved better model fit than LVEF when categorized using a cutoff of 0.8 m/s. This underscores the utility of clinically relevant cutoffs in enhancing post-CRT survival prediction.

Incremental value beyond LVEF

Although LVEF remains an important post-CRT prognostic marker, the improved performance of models that incorporate multiple measures suggests that clinical scores contribute complementary, nonoverlapping information. The strongest measures in our primary analyses remained informative when considered alongside LVEF, and combining select measures further strengthened survival prediction compared with relying solely on LVEF.

Implications for patient risk stratification

Our findings raise important considerations for risk stratification in CRT recipients. Clinicians often prioritize metrics such as LVEF at follow-up. However, these measures alone may not fully capture the multifaceted nature of heart failure, which encompasses systemic, functional, and quality-of-life dimensions. Clinical scores offer a complementary perspective, promoting a multidimensional evaluation of patient status that extends beyond myocardial function.

Composite end point approach

The composite end point used in this study—death from any cause or a nonfatal heart-failure event—adds further validity to our findings. This approach is widely used in heart-failure trials and reflects clinically meaningful outcomes.16 The inclusion of clinical scores alongside LVEF in predictive models not only improves accuracy but also aligns with the broader goal of optimizing patient-centered care in CRT.

Contribution to CRT research

To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first and the largest investigations comparing the predictive accuracy of clinical scores and LVEF for long-term survival in a cohort of CRT recipients. Previous studies in the context of CRT have often focused on clinical scores as end points rather than predictors.17, 18, 19 Their use case as predictors of survival remains less explored. By demonstrating their potential to improve survival prediction, this study presents compelling evidence toward integrating clinical scores into routine post-CRT evaluation protocols.

Future directions

Echocardiographic measures of LVEF and clinical assessments remain observer-dependent metrics, so relying solely on them introduces intra- and interobserver variability that may affect reproducibility. Future research could explore combining clinical scores and LVEF into a single standardized composite score, potentially yielding a more reliable and comprehensive measure of post-CRT risk.

Limitations

Some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our cohort was drawn from a single high-volume tertiary care center, meaning that the findings may not fully generalize to patients treated in community settings or lower-volume centers. Second, because clinical scores were collected only at the 6-month follow-up and not at baseline, we were unable to assess changes over time. Therefore, these measures should be interpreted as post-CRT prognostic markers rather than direct indicators of CRT response. Future studies should incorporate both baseline and follow-up measurements. Finally, the study did not account for additional determinants of CRT outcomes, including myocardial substrate and procedure-/device-related factors, which could further refine prognostic performance in future investigations.

Conclusion

In this prospective cohort, clinical scores assessing functional capacity, frailty, and quality of life were significant post-CRT prognostic markers and provided incremental value beyond LVEF for predicting long-term survival. Although LVEF remains a valuable metric, a multidimensional approach incorporating clinical scores alongside LVEF may improve prognostic assessment by providing a more comprehensive evaluation of patient status at follow-up. Integrating these measures into post-CRT evaluation may enhance risk stratification and support more personalized heart-failure management.
