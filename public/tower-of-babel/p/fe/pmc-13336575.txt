Near-infrared fluorescence cholangiography in emergency cholecystectomy: a team-based adjunct rather than a standalone safety tool
Authors: Jie Zhang
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy in the setting of acute cholecystitis remains technically challenging, as inflammatory edema, exudation, and adhesions frequently distort anatomical planes and obscure key structures within Calot’s triangle, thereby increasing the risk of bile duct injury. In this context, recognizing anatomical uncertainty while maintaining operative progress is central to intraoperative decision-making and procedural safety. Near-infrared fluorescence cholangiography, which allows real-time visualization of biliary anatomy without ductal cannulation, has consequently attracted increasing interest in emergency cholecystectomy, although its effectiveness and appropriate role in inflamed operative fields remain to be clearly defined.

Pesce and colleagues conducted a prospective study applying indocyanine green–based near-infrared fluorescence imaging during emergency laparoscopic cholecystectomy, integrating biliary assessment into the operative workflow and evaluating visualization at predefined stages of dissection[1]. Using the intraoperative AAST severity grading system, the authors demonstrated that visualization of the cystic duct and common bile duct improved as the dissection of Calot’s triangle progressed, indicating that the utility of near-infrared fluorescence cholangiography is closely dependent on prior anatomical exposure. Rather than replacing conventional anatomical identification early in the procedure, fluorescence imaging appears to function as an adjunct that enhances spatial orientation during ongoing dissection, reinforcing that it complements – rather than circumvents – established safety principles such as the Critical View of Safety.

In contrast, patients with gangrenous or complicated acute cholecystitis consistently demonstrated poorer fluorescence visualization, even after partial dissection. This suggests that in the most severe inflammatory settings – where anatomical distortion is greatest – the adjunctive value of fluorescence imaging may be limited. Clinically, this observation is instructive, as it underscores that near-infrared fluorescence does not resolve anatomical uncertainty in high-risk cases and may instead prompt earlier consideration of bailout strategies or alternative operative approaches.

From a clinical standpoint, the evidence supports positioning near-infrared fluorescence cholangiography as an anatomical adjunct rather than an independent safeguard of operative safety. Its value lies not only in the successful visualization of biliary structures but also in situations where inadequate fluorescence or persistent anatomical ambiguity encourages a more cautious reassessment of the risks associated with continued dissection. Accordingly, the evaluation of this technology should be embedded within a broader surgical safety framework rather than based on visualization metrics alone.

When interpreting the practical utility of near-infrared fluorescence cholangiography, it is also important to recognize its reliance on effective team coordination. The success of this technique does not depend exclusively on the operating surgeon but is influenced by the standardization of perioperative processes and the degree of intraoperative collaboration. In the preoperative phase, circulating nurses play a key role in ensuring appropriate timing of indocyanine green administration, medication verification, and monitoring for adverse reactions, all of which are prerequisites for obtaining stable intraoperative fluorescence signals. During surgery, the familiarity of scrub and circulating nurses with the imaging system, as well as their efficiency in switching between white-light and fluorescence modes, directly affects whether fluorescence information can be accessed at critical anatomical junctures. Moreover, the maintenance of a clear operative field through timely suction, irrigation, and management of bleeding or bile contamination objectively influences signal detectability. In complex cases or when transition to a bailout strategy is anticipated, early preparation of relevant instruments and drainage materials by the nursing team may shorten the interval between decision-making and execution, thereby reducing the likelihood of prolonged high-risk dissection. Although these team-related factors are not explicitly captured by the study’s primary endpoints, they represent important contextual elements that influence the real-world effectiveness of the technology.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The lack of a control group limits interpretation, as biliary visualization is a surrogate rather than a definitive safety endpoint; consequently, the study cannot determine whether near-infrared fluorescence cholangiography reduces major bile duct injury, conversion to open surgery, or severe complications. In addition, visualization assessment is inherently subjective and dependent on surgeon experience, imaging settings, and team familiarity. Although no major bile duct injuries were observed, the occurrence of minor bile leaks and postoperative complications underscores that, in acute cholecystitis, adjunctive techniques cannot fully eliminate operative risk.

In summary, this study provides a pragmatic appraisal of near-infrared fluorescence cholangiography in emergency laparoscopic cholecystectomy. The findings support its role as an adjunct for anatomical clarification and risk awareness within established dissection strategies and coordinated team practice, rather than as an independent guarantee of safety. Further multicenter randomized trials focusing on clinically meaningful outcomes, along with analyses of patient selection and workflow integration, are required to better define its role in acute cholecystitis[2].
