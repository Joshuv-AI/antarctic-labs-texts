ABSTRACT
AbstractCross-cultural pragmatic reasoning requires computational systems to interpret implicit meanings and culturally-specific communication patterns beyond literal semantic comprehension. This study proposes a cognitive salience feature-driven multi-task deep learning model that integrates attention-based mechanisms mirroring human perceptual priorities with culture-conditioned gating for cross-cultural pragmatic inference. The model jointly optimizes four pragmatic reasoning tasks—implicature detection, speech act classification, politeness assessment, and cultural appropriateness evaluation—through dynamic task weighting and gradient conflict resolution strategies. Comprehensive experiments conducted on a multilingual corpus spanning eight cultural groups demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves 83.2% overall accuracy and 77.5% cross-cultural transfer accuracy, representing substantial improvements of 9.7 and 12.7% points respectively over standard multi-task learning baselines. Ablation studies confirm that cognitive salience mechanisms contribute 5.2% performance gain, while culture-conditioned processing enhances cross-cultural generalization. The model’s learned salience patterns exhibit strong correlation (0.81) with theory-driven pragmatic markers, validating interpretability and theoretical grounding. This work advances computational pragmatics by demonstrating that human-inspired cognitive mechanisms significantly enhance artificial systems’ cross-cultural reasoning capabilities.Keywords: Cross-cultural pragmatics, Cognitive salience, Multi-task learning, Deep learning, Natural language understanding, Cultural adaptationSubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Cross-cultural pragmatic reasoning represents a critical challenge in natural language understanding, requiring systems to interpret implicit meanings, contextual cues, and culturally-specific communication patterns beyond literal semantic comprehension1. The increasing globalization of digital communication platforms has amplified the need for automated systems capable of processing pragmatic nuances across diverse linguistic and cultural contexts2. Cognitive salience, defined as the prominence of particular features in perception and processing, plays a fundamental role in how individuals from different cultural backgrounds interpret and generate pragmatic meanings3. Recent advances in deep learning architectures have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in capturing complex linguistic patterns, yet their application to cross-cultural pragmatic inference remains substantially underexplored4.

Current research in pragmatic reasoning predominantly focuses on monolingual or mono-cultural contexts, employing single-task learning frameworks that treat different pragmatic phenomena as isolated problems5. Multi-task learning approaches have shown promise in natural language processing by leveraging shared representations across related tasks, thereby improving generalization and reducing data requirements6. However, existing multi-task models rarely incorporate cognitive salience features explicitly, limiting their ability to capture the attention mechanisms and perceptual priorities that guide human pragmatic interpretation7. Cross-cultural studies in pragmatics have primarily relied on manual annotation and rule-based systems, which lack scalability and struggle to handle the variability inherent in natural language use across cultures8.

Several critical challenges impede progress in this domain. First, the scarcity of annotated cross-cultural pragmatic datasets constrains the development and evaluation of data-driven models9. Second, conventional deep learning architectures fail to account for the cognitive mechanisms underlying salience detection, treating all input features with equal weight regardless of their perceptual prominence10. Third, the transfer of pragmatic reasoning capabilities across cultural contexts remains problematic, as models trained on one cultural group often exhibit significant performance degradation when applied to others11. These limitations underscore the necessity for novel computational frameworks that integrate cognitive principles with multi-task learning paradigms to address cross-cultural pragmatic reasoning systematically12.

The present study addresses these gaps by proposing a cognitive salience feature-driven multi-task deep learning model specifically designed for cross-cultural pragmatic inference. Rather than merely assembling existing techniques, this work introduces a principled cognitive-computational bridging paradigm that translates established findings from attention psychology and salience detection research into operationalized neural mechanisms. Our approach draws directly from Posner’s attentional orienting theory13 and Kahneman’s resource allocation framework14, implementing computational analogs of endogenous and exogenous attention shifts that cognitive scientists have documented in human language processing.

The primary innovations of this work include three interconnected contributions. First, we develop a theoretically-grounded cognitive salience module that differs fundamentally from standard attention mechanisms. While conventional attention computes relevance weights through learned parameters without cognitive constraints, our salience computation incorporates empirically-derived parameters from eye-tracking studies and reaction time experiments, enforcing biologically plausible temporal dynamics and competitive inhibition patterns observed in human salience detection15. Second, the culture-conditioned gating mechanism operationalizes cross-cultural psychology findings about differential attention allocation across individualist and collectivist societies, moving beyond post-hoc cultural classification to embed cultural modulation directly within feature extraction. Third, the multi-task optimization framework captures the inherently interconnected nature of pragmatic phenomena, reflecting psycholinguistic evidence that speech act recognition, implicature derivation, and politeness assessment share overlapping cognitive substrates16.

We acknowledge that the individual technical components—attention mechanisms, multi-task learning, gating networks—have been extensively studied. However, their systematic integration for cross-cultural pragmatic reasoning represents unexplored territory. Previous attempts using single techniques have demonstrated consistent limitations in this domain: attention-only models fail to capture cultural variation, culture-classification approaches lack pragmatic depth, and single-task models miss crucial inter-task dependencies11. This research contributes both theoretical insights into how cognitive salience shapes pragmatic processing across cultures and practical advances toward more robust cross-cultural communication systems.

Cross-cultural pragmatic reasoning theory

Pragmatic reasoning encompasses the cognitive processes through which language users derive intended meanings beyond literal semantic content by integrating linguistic input with contextual knowledge, social conventions, and inferential mechanisms17. The theoretical foundation of pragmatic inference rests primarily on Grice’s cooperative principle and conversational maxims, which posit that communication participants adhere to implicit rules governing informativeness, truthfulness, relevance, and clarity18. However, these principles exhibit substantial variation across cultural contexts, as different societies prioritize distinct communicative norms and interpretive strategies.

Cross-cultural pragmatic differences manifest along multiple dimensions, including directness versus indirectness in speech acts, high-context versus low-context communication styles, and varying tolerance for ambiguity19. Cultural dimensions such as individualism-collectivism and power distance systematically influence pragmatic interpretation by shaping expectations about politeness strategies, information structure, and face-saving behaviors. Cognitive salience feature mechanism

Cognitive salience refers to the perceptual prominence of specific stimuli or features that capture attentional resources and receive prioritized processing in human cognition22. This concept finds its roots in Gestalt psychology and attention theory, where salience emerges through interplay between bottom-up sensory properties and top-down cognitive expectations. The resulting dynamic weighting system determines which information elements dominate conscious awareness and working memory. Neural competition among representations forms the psychological foundation of salience—stimuli with distinctive attributes or contextual relevance achieve heightened activation levels and gain preferential access to downstream cognitive processes23.

What distinguishes our cognitive salience formulation from standard neural attention requires careful articulation. Traditional attention mechanisms in deep learning, while inspired by psychological concepts, lack explicit grounding in cognitive processing constraints. Our approach differs in three essential ways that align with established cognitive science findings. First, we incorporate temporal dynamics consistent with the 150-300ms salience detection window documented in ERP studies24, implementing this through constrained attention span parameters rather than unconstrained learned weights. Second, our model enforces competitive inhibition patterns observed in the salience network, where attending to one feature necessarily suppresses processing of competing features—a constraint absent from standard softmax attention25. Third, we integrate the distinction between stimulus-driven (exogenous) and goal-directed (endogenous) attention identified in Corbetta and Shulman’s influential framework26, implementing separate pathways for bottom-up feature detection and top-down task relevance.

Multi-task deep learning methods

Multi-task learning (MTL) constitutes a paradigm wherein models simultaneously optimize multiple related objectives, leveraging shared representations to improve generalization performance and data efficiency compared to single-task approaches31. The foundational principle underlying MTL posits that related tasks contain complementary information, and joint learning enables models to discover common structural patterns while maintaining task-specific adaptations. The evolution of multi-task learning has progressed from early neural network implementations with hard parameter sharing to contemporary architectures employing sophisticated attention mechanisms, meta-learning strategies, and dynamic task weighting schemes32.

Knowledge sharing in multi-task frameworks operates through architectural components that enable information transfer across tasks while preserving task-specific processing pathways. Overall model architecture design

The proposed cognitive salience feature-driven multi-task deep learning model integrates cognitive principles with advanced neural architectures to address cross-cultural pragmatic reasoning through a hierarchical processing pipeline. Overall architecture of the cognitive salience feature-driven multi-task deep learning model for cross-cultural pragmatic reasoning.

The input layer processes multilingual textual data alongside contextual metadata including speaker cultural background, discourse context, and situational parameters. Raw text undergoes tokenization and embedding through pre-trained multilingual encoders, generating contextualized representations that preserve cross-lingual semantic alignments37. Cultural identity vectors are concatenated with linguistic embeddings to provide culture-specific conditioning signals throughout the network. This dual-stream input design ensures that both linguistic content and cultural framing receive explicit representation in subsequent processing stages.

The feature extraction layer incorporates cognitive salience detection mechanisms through attention-based weighting modules that dynamically identify pragmatically significant elements within input sequences. Multi-head self-attention layers compute contextual dependencies while salience prediction networks assign prominence scores to linguistic units based on learned salience patterns from human annotation data. These salience weights modulate feature activations through element-wise multiplication, effectively amplifying cognitively prominent features while suppressing peripheral information. The salience-enhanced representations flow into shared encoder blocks implemented as transformer layers, which extract hierarchical linguistic abstractions applicable across multiple pragmatic reasoning tasks38.

The multi-task learning layer orchestrates joint optimization across four primary tasks: implicature detection, speech act classification, politeness assessment, and cultural appropriateness evaluation. Task-specific attention modules operate on shared representations to generate task-adapted features, while cross-task interaction gates facilitate bidirectional information exchange between related tasks. This architecture leverages complementary signals across tasks, enabling the model to exploit correlations between different pragmatic phenomena. Hard parameter sharing in lower encoder layers promotes learning of universal linguistic features, whereas soft parameter sharing in higher layers accommodates task-specific requirements through constrained divergence of task networks.

The output layer contains dedicated prediction heads for each task, employing fully connected layers followed by task-appropriate activation functions. Classification tasks utilize softmax outputs, while regression-based tasks such as politeness scoring employ linear activations. Task-specific loss functions aggregate into the total training objective through learnable task weighting coefficients that adapt dynamically based on task difficulty and learning progress.

The architectural complexity warrants explicit justification, as each component addresses specific limitations that simpler alternatives cannot resolve. We arrived at the current design through systematic pilot experiments that evaluated component necessity. Experimental design and results analysis

Experimental setup and dataset

The experimental evaluation utilizes the Cross-Cultural Pragmatic Inference Corpus (CCPIC), a multilingual dataset constructed specifically for this study by aggregating and extending existing pragmatic annotation resources across eight linguistic communities. We built CCPIC by integrating five publicly available datasets: the DIPLOMAT dialogue corpus48(CC BY 4.0 license), the Cross-Cultural Speech Act Database from Georgetown University (research use agreement obtained), the Japanese Politeness Corpus49(CC BY-NC 4.0), the Arabic Pragmatic Markers Dataset from King Saud University (written permission granted), and the Multilingual Implicature Collection compiled by the European Language Resources Association (ELRA end-user license). For proprietary datasets, we obtained explicit written permissions from original creators, with correspondence documented in Supplementary Materials.

The dataset comprises 47,320 instances spanning English, Chinese, Arabic, Spanish, Japanese, German, Hindi, and French, with each instance annotated for four pragmatic reasoning tasks: implicature detection, speech act classification, politeness assessment, and cultural appropriateness evaluation48. Annotation inconsistencies across source datasets required systematic reconciliation. We developed a unified annotation schema that mapped original labels to standardized categories, with three bilingual annotators independently reviewing all instances where source annotations conflicted with our schema. Instances showing irreconcilable conflicts (approximately 4.2% of aggregated data) were excluded rather than arbitrarily resolved. All retained instances underwent verification by native speakers of the respective languages. Data collection followed a stratified sampling strategy ensuring balanced representation across high-context cultures (East Asian, Middle Eastern) and low-context cultures (Western individualist societies), addressing the systematic bias toward Western linguistic data prevalent in existing natural language processing benchmarks.

Data preprocessing implements a standardized pipeline beginning with language-specific tokenization using multilingual tokenizers trained on CommonCrawl corpora. Text normalization handles punctuation, casing, and special characters while preserving pragmatically significant markers such as exclamation points and question marks that convey illocutionary force. Each instance undergoes sentence segmentation, dependency parsing, and named entity recognition to extract structural features potentially relevant to salience detection.

Cultural metadata encoding requires careful methodological consideration. We employ Hofstede’s cultural dimensions framework50 to generate 6-dimensional cultural vectors representing individualism-collectivism, power distance, uncertainty avoidance, masculinity-femininity, long-term orientation, and indulgence-restraint scores. We acknowledge the substantial criticism this framework has received in cross-cultural psychology, particularly regarding its essentialist treatment of national cultures, ecological fallacy in applying country-level scores to individuals, and dated empirical basis51,52. However, we selected this framework for three pragmatic reasons: its widespread adoption enables comparison with prior work, its quantitative operationalization permits computational implementation, and no alternative framework offers comparable coverage across our target languages.

To mitigate essentialist assumptions, we implement several corrective measures. First, rather than assigning fixed cultural vectors based on language alone, we compute instance-level cultural adaptation scores that account for contextual factors including register formality, interlocutor relationship markers, and situational cues present in each utterance. Second, we introduce variance parameters around Hofstede dimension scores, acknowledging that any individual instance may deviate substantially from national averages. Third, for bilingual or multicultural speakers identified through metadata (approximately 12% of instances), we assign blended cultural vectors weighted by contextual language choice and topic domain. These modifications represent partial remediation rather than complete solutions—we recognize that assigning any cultural vector to individual utterances involves simplification that future work should address through more sophisticated cultural identity modeling.

Disagreement resolution followed a structured adjudication protocol rather than simple majority voting. When annotators disagreed, a senior annotator with expertise in pragmatics and the relevant language reviewed the instance alongside written justifications from each original annotator. The senior annotator either selected the most defensible annotation or, when disagreement reflected genuine ambiguity, assigned a “contested” flag that triggered discussion among all annotators until consensus emerged. This deliberative process, while resource-intensive, ensures that retained annotations represent considered judgments rather than arbitrary tie-breaking.

We further conducted a label isolation experiment: training the model with randomly shuffled labels for one task while maintaining correct labels for others, then measuring performance degradation on the shuffled task. If models primarily exploited inter-task label leakage, shuffling should minimally impact the shuffled task (since other task labels remain informative). Results showed substantial degradation (12–18% accuracy drop) when any task’s labels were shuffled, indicating that the model learns task-specific representations rather than merely inferring labels from correlated tasks. These correlations reflect genuine linguistic relationships—polite utterances do tend toward indirect speech acts across cultures—rather than annotation artifacts. The multi-task architecture exploits these natural co-occurrences while maintaining distinct task-specific processing pathways.

Implicature detection labels instances as containing conversational implicature, conventional implicature, or no implicature. Speech act classification employs Searle’s taxonomy encompassing assertives, directives, commissives, expressives, and declarations. Politeness assessment uses a 5-point Likert scale aligned with Brown and Levinson’s politeness theory53. Cross-cultural comparability of Likert scales presents a recognized methodological challenge, as response styles and scale interpretation vary systematically across cultures54. East Asian respondents, for instance, often exhibit central tendency bias, while Western respondents show greater use of scale extremes55. To address this concern, we implemented anchoring vignettes—standardized scenarios with known politeness levels—that calibrated annotator responses within each cultural group. Annotators first rated five anchor scenarios before proceeding to target instances, and we applied linear transformation to raw scores based on each annotator’s anchor ratings. This anchoring procedure, while not eliminating all cross-cultural variance, substantially reduces systematic differences in scale usage. Additionally, we report within-culture relative rankings alongside absolute scores for analyses where cross-cultural comparison is essential, acknowledging that ordinal relationships may prove more robust than cardinal values across cultural contexts.

The experimental infrastructure employs NVIDIA A100 GPUs with 40GB memory for model training, utilizing PyTorch 2.0 framework with CUDA 11.8 acceleration. Distributed training across 4 GPUs implements data parallelism with gradient accumulation to accommodate large batch sizes. The software environment includes Python 3.9, Transformers library version 4.30, and custom implementations of salience extraction modules. Evaluation metrics encompass task-specific measures and aggregate performance indicators. Classification tasks employ accuracy, macro-averaged F1 score, and per-class precision-recall to account for class imbalance. Politeness assessment uses mean absolute error (MAE) and Pearson correlation coefficient. Cultural transfer capability is quantified through cross-cultural accuracy, measuring performance when training on one cultural group and testing on others. The overall model quality metric combines task performances through weighted averaging based on task difficulty57.

Discussion

The experimental results reveal that cognitive salience integration constitutes a fundamental mechanism for enhancing cross-cultural pragmatic reasoning, operating through selective amplification of contextually-relevant linguistic features that carry pragmatic significance. The proposed model’s superior performance stems from its explicit modeling of attention allocation patterns that mirror human cognitive processing, where salient elements receive prioritized computational resources and exert disproportionate influence on interpretation outcomes.

The cognitive plausibility of learned salience patterns merits direct examination. Our human-model alignment study (correlation 0.73) provides quantitative evidence that the model’s attention distribution resembles human pragmatic focus, yet this correspondence admits multiple interpretations. The alignment might reflect genuine cognitive simulation, or alternatively, both humans and models might converge on statistically optimal feature weighting without sharing underlying mechanisms. Several observations favor the cognitive interpretation. First, the model shows stronger alignment for pragmatic markers than content words, matching psycholinguistic findings that pragmatic processing prioritizes functional over semantic elements63. Second, cultural modulation of salience patterns follows directions predicted by cross-cultural psychology—Japanese instances show elevated attention to honorific markers while German instances prioritize propositional content64. Third, the model’s error patterns cluster around cases that also challenge human annotators, suggesting shared processing limitations rather than orthogonal failure modes.

However, we must acknowledge the distance between our computational instantiation and actual neural mechanisms. The cognitive salience module implements functional analogs rather than biologically faithful simulations. Human salience detection involves distributed processing across multiple brain regions with complex temporal dynamics that our feedforward architecture cannot capture22. Our constraints on attention parameters, while empirically motivated, represent coarse approximations of nuanced cognitive phenomena. Future work should explore recurrent architectures that better capture the temporal unfolding of salience computation and integrate findings from neurolinguistic studies more directly into model design.

This architecture captures the observation that pragmatic comprehension does not treat all linguistic input uniformly but rather focuses cognitive effort on markers signaling speaker intentions, social relationships, and contextual assumptions. The culture-conditioned salience gates provide a principled mechanism for representing how different cultural schemas modulate feature prominence, explaining the model’s robust cross-cultural transfer capabilities compared to approaches lacking explicit cultural modeling.

The advantages of the multi-task learning framework extend beyond simple parameter efficiency to encompass qualitative improvements in learned representations. Joint optimization across implicature detection, speech act classification, politeness assessment, and cultural appropriateness evaluation enables the model to discover shared pragmatic abstractions applicable across tasks while maintaining specialized processing pathways for task-specific requirements. This architectural design reflects the interconnected nature of pragmatic phenomena, where speech act recognition informs implicature detection, and politeness judgments constrain cultural appropriateness assessments. The dynamic task weighting mechanism addresses inherent differences in task difficulty and learning dynamics, preventing scenarios where easily-learned tasks dominate gradient updates at the expense of more challenging objectives requiring sustained optimization.

Comparison with existing approaches highlights the present work’s distinctive integration of cognitive principles with neural architectures. Traditional rule-based pragmatic systems achieve high interpretability but lack scalability and struggle with linguistic variability, while standard deep learning models demonstrate strong pattern recognition capabilities without principled attention mechanisms grounded in cognitive theory. The proposed framework bridges this gap by implementing learnable salience computation that adapts to data while maintaining alignment with theoretical constructs from pragmatic linguistics and cognitive psychology. Unlike prior cultural adaptation methods that rely solely on language-specific encoders or post-hoc cultural classifiers, this work embeds cultural conditioning directly into feature extraction, enabling culture-aware salience weighting throughout the processing pipeline.

The model exhibits differential adaptability across cultural contexts, performing optimally on low-context cultures where pragmatic conventions display greater explicitness and conventionalization. High-context cultures present challenges due to heavier reliance on implicit shared knowledge, situational context, and non-verbal cues absent from text-only inputs. This performance asymmetry suggests that cross-cultural pragmatic reasoning difficulty scales with the degree of implicitness and context-dependency characteristic of target cultures. The model’s relatively stronger performance on formal registers compared to informal discourse reflects the increased standardization and reduced variability of pragmatic markers in professional communication contexts.

Despite substantial improvements over baselines, several limitations warrant consideration. The model’s dependence on pre-annotated salience patterns constrains its ability to discover novel pragmatic markers not represented in training data. Highly conventionalized language-specific expressions lacking compositional transparency remain problematic, requiring expanded lexical coverage or incorporation of culture-specific pragmatic lexicons. The absence of prosodic, gestural, and paralinguistic information limits performance on pragmatic phenomena where non-verbal channels carry critical interpretive signals. Future research should address these constraints through multimodal integration, unsupervised salience discovery mechanisms, and expanded cross-cultural annotation efforts capturing diverse pragmatic conventions across linguistic communities.

Conclusion

This study proposes a novel cognitive salience feature-driven multi-task deep learning model for cross-cultural pragmatic reasoning, addressing fundamental challenges in computational pragmatics through principled integration of cognitive mechanisms with neural architectures. The research develops a hierarchical processing framework comprising salience-aware feature extraction, culture-conditioned attention mechanisms, multi-task learning with dynamic optimization, and task-specific prediction heads designed for implicature detection, speech act classification, politeness assessment, and cultural appropriateness evaluation. Comprehensive experiments conducted on a multilingual corpus spanning eight cultural groups demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves substantial performance improvements over baseline methods, with overall accuracy reaching 83.2% and cross-cultural transfer accuracy of 77.5%, representing gains of 9.7 and 12.7% points respectively over standard multi-task learning approaches.

The primary innovations of this work include three key contributions. First, the explicit modeling of cognitive salience through attention-based feature weighting that dynamically identifies pragmatically significant linguistic elements while suppressing peripheral information, mirroring human attention allocation patterns during pragmatic comprehension. Second, the culture-conditioned gating mechanism that modulates salience distributions based on cultural schemas, enabling adaptive feature prioritization across high-context and low-context communication cultures. Third, the comprehensive multi-task optimization strategy incorporating dynamic task weighting, gradient conflict resolution, and hybrid parameter sharing that balances knowledge transfer with task-specific specialization.

The theoretical significance of this research lies in demonstrating that computational models benefit substantially from incorporating cognitive principles, validating the hypothesis that human-inspired attention mechanisms enhance artificial systems’ pragmatic reasoning capabilities. The work advances cross-cultural pragmatics by providing quantitative evidence for systematic cultural variations in feature salience and establishing computational methods for modeling culture-specific pragmatic processing. Practically, the model enables diverse applications including machine translation quality enhancement, intercultural communication training systems, automated content moderation requiring cultural sensitivity, and cross-cultural dialogue systems. The interpretability of learned salience patterns facilitates human understanding of model decisions, supporting deployment in contexts requiring transparent reasoning processes.

Several limitations constrain the current work’s scope and effectiveness, requiring transparent acknowledgment. The model’s reliance on text-only inputs excludes prosodic, gestural, and paralinguistic information critical for comprehensive pragmatic interpretation. Limited coverage of language-specific conventionalized expressions restricts performance on highly idiomatic pragmatic constructions. The requirement for annotated training data poses scalability challenges for extending coverage to additional languages and cultural contexts. Performance asymmetries across cultural groups suggest differential model capacity for handling varying degrees of contextual implicitness.

Beyond these technical constraints, methodological limitations deserve explicit discussion. Our cultural modeling relies on Hofstede’s framework despite well-documented criticisms regarding its essentialist assumptions, ecological fallacy, and dated empirical basis51,52. Assigning cultural vectors to individual utterances necessarily involves simplification that obscures intra-cultural variation, hybrid identities, and context-dependent cultural positioning. The remediation measures we implemented—instance-level adaptation scores, variance parameters, blended vectors for bilingual speakers—represent partial solutions rather than fundamental resolutions. Similarly, the cross-cultural comparability of our Likert-based politeness annotations remains imperfect despite anchoring vignette calibration; future work should explore culture-specific politeness taxonomies rather than imposing uniform scalar measures across diverse societies.

The CCPIC dataset, while substantially larger than prior cross-cultural pragmatic resources, aggregates from sources with heterogeneous annotation standards that our reconciliation procedure imperfectly harmonizes. Some cultural groups (Hindi, Arabic) contain fewer instances than others, potentially limiting model capacity for these languages. Our inter-task correlation analysis suggests that label leakage does not drive performance gains, yet the interconnected annotation of all tasks on identical instances creates dependencies that fully independent evaluation cannot assess. These limitations collectively indicate that our results, while representing meaningful advances, require replication with independently constructed datasets and refined cultural modeling approaches before informing practical deployment.

Future research directions include multimodal extension incorporating speech prosody and visual cues to capture non-verbal pragmatic signals, unsupervised salience discovery methods reducing dependence on annotated data, zero-shot cross-cultural transfer through meta-learning enabling adaptation to entirely unseen cultural contexts, integration with large language models to leverage pre-trained knowledge while maintaining culture-aware processing, and longitudinal studies examining model performance on evolving pragmatic conventions. Expanding dataset coverage to include low-resource languages and underrepresented cultural groups would enhance equity in cross-cultural natural language processing systems. These advancements promise more robust, interpretable, and culturally-sensitive computational pragmatics systems capable of supporting increasingly diverse global communication needs.
