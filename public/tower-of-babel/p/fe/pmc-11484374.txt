Neutrophil N1 polarization induced by cardiomyocyte-derived extracellular vesicle miR-9-5p aggravates myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury
Authors: Yeshen Zhang, Xinzhong Li, Yining Dai, Yuan Han, Xiaomin Wei, Guoquan Wei, Weikun Chen, Siyu Kong, Yu He, Haobin Liu, Ning Ma, Jianping Bin, Ning Tan, Pengcheng He, Yuanhui Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Neutrophil polarization contributes to inflammation and its resolution, but the role of neutrophil polarization in myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury remains unknown. Cardiomyocytes (CMs) participate in cardiac inflammation by secreting extracellular vesicles (EVs). Therefore, we investigated the role of neutrophil polarization in myocardial I/R injury and the mechanism by which CM-derived EVs regulated neutrophil polarization. In the present study, our data showed that N1 neutrophil polarization enlarged cardiac infarct size and exacerbated cardiac dysfunction at the early stage of myocardial I/R. Further, CM-EV-derived miR-9-5p was identified as a mediator inducing neutrophils to the N1 phenotype. Mechanistically, miR-9-5p directly suppressed SOCS5 and SIRT1 expression, resulting in activating JAK2/STAT3 and NF-κB signaling pathways in neutrophils. Importantly, we confirmed that serum EV-derived miR-9-5p levels were independently associated with cardiovascular mortality in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention. These findings suggest neutrophil polarization is a promising therapeutic target against myocardial I/R-induced inflammation and injury, and serum EV-derived miR-9-5p is a promising prognostic biomarker for cardiovascular mortality in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-024-02902-w.

Introduction

Inflammation plays a crucial role in myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury [1, 2], but the underlying mechanisms are not well characterized. Inflammatory response during myocardial I/R is characterized by the infiltration of different types of immune cells, including neutrophils [3, 4]. In response to myocardial I/R, high levels of reactive oxygen species, proteinases, and cationic peptides released by the infiltrating neutrophils mediate tissue damage [5, 6]. Neutrophils also secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which promote further neutrophil recruitment and activation, and trigger the inflammatory cascade at the injury site [7]. Intriguingly, accumulating studies revealed that neutrophils exhibit significant plasticity and are categorized into N1 neutrophils (pro-inflammatory) and N2 neutrophils (anti-inflammatory) with opposing immuno-modulatory functions [8]. The proportion of N1 neutrophils is higher at the early stage of myocardial infarction (MI) and exert pro-inflammatory effects, whereas the proportion of N2 neutrophils is higher during later stage of MI and play a significant role in alleviating inflammation, promoting angiogenesis, and injury repair [9–11]. Accordingly, neutrophil functions depend on polarization status, and enhancing our understanding of neutrophil phenotypic transformation will advance comprehension of the mechanisms responsible for inflammatory response during the process of myocardial I/R injury.

Most eukaryotic cells release extracellular vesicles (EVs), which are small, membrane-bound structures that play an important role in intercellular communication. Mounting evidence has demonstrated that EVs derived from the myocardium-resident cells, including cardiomyocytes (CMs), cardiac fibroblasts (CFs), endothelial cells, and immune cells regulate cardiac inflammation under physiological and pathophysiological conditions [12, 13]. CMs are the main cell type in the mammalian heart, and CM-derived EVs (CM-EVs) play an important role in the progression of cardiovascular diseases [14]. Previous study demonstrated that cardiac EVs were mainly derived from CMs during MI, which would be taken up by infiltrating monocytes to regulate local inflammatory responses [15]. A study by Almeida Paiva et al. demonstrated that EVs derived from the ischemic CMs polarized the macrophages into a pro-inflammatory phenotype, thereby impacting healing and post-infarction remodeling of the myocardium [16]. However, it has not been previously investigated whether and how CM-EVs alter neutrophil polarization during myocardial I/R injury. Therefore, understanding of CM-EVs responsible for shifting neutrophil phenotype will help identify novel effective therapy against myocardial I/R injury.

EVs mediate intercellular communication by transferring biomolecules such as microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), lipids, and proteins [14]. It has been shown that miRNAs are the most abundant non-coding RNAs (18–25 bp in length) in the EVs that repress the expression of target genes [17]. Several studies have reported that CM-EV-derived miRNAs alter myocardial physiology and pathophysiology by regulating cellular apoptosis, angiogenesis, inflammation, and myocardial fibrosis [14, 18–20]. However, it is unclear whether these CM-EV-derived miRNAs modulate neutrophil polarization during myocardial I/R and the underlying mechanisms. Therefore, we hypothesized that CM-derived EVs could transfer essential miRNAs to mediate CM-neutrophil communication to impact inflammatory response in I/R hearts.

In the present study, we provided the first evidence that CM-EVs deliver miR-9-5p from CMs to neutrophils during myocardial I/R, which results in neutrophils to shift toward the N1 phenotype and further exacerbate the myocardial I/R injury. These findings reveal a molecular mechanism for the inflammation during myocardial I/R from the perspective of neutrophil polarization and provide a promising therapeutic target for myocardial I/R-induced inflammation. Importantly, we confirmed that serum EV-derived miR-9-5p levels were independently associated with cardiovascular mortality in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), providing a promising prognostic biomarker for such population.

Materials and methods

Establishment of murine myocardial I/R model

We obtained 8–10-week-old male wild-type C57BL/6J mice from the Experimental Animal Center of Southern Medical University. Myocardial I/R model was established in mice as previously described [1]. Briefly, the adult mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (5% for induction and 2% for maintenance) and ventilated using a rodent respirator. Mice in the GW4869 group received intraperitoneal injection of 2.5 mg/kg GW4869 (D1692, Sigma, USA) 1 h before I/R surgery [21]. Subsequently, left thoracotomy was performed and the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery was ligated for 45 min using an 8-0 silk suture. Immediately after removing the silk suture, the reperfusion region was injected with 5 × 106 neutrophils or 1 × 1010 particles EVs in accordance with the experimental design [22, 23]. The periods of reperfusion varied according to the experiments. Mice in the sham-operated group underwent the same surgical procedure but the LAD was not ligated. Before or after surgery, all the mice were maintained in a pathogen-free room under a 12-h light–dark cycle at 22 ± 1 °C and 65%–70% humidity, and were provided with free access to food and water.

Measurement of cardiac infarct size

Cardiac infarct sizes were assessed as previously described with minor modifications [24]. Briefly, after 24 h reperfusion, the LAD was re-ligated again and 0.5% Evans Blue dye (AAPR215-500, Pythonbio, China) was injected into the ascending aorta to delineate the ischemic area (area-at-risk, AAR) from the non-ischemic area. Then, the hearts were harvested, frozen at − 80 °C for 20 min, and sectioned into 5 slices. The heart tissue sections were incubated in 1.5% triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) buffer (T8877, Sigma, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C to delineate the infarct size (IS). We measured the left ventricular (LV) region, AAR, and IS using the Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, Bethesda, MD, USA). The ischemic and infarct areas were expressed as percentages of AAR/LV and IS/AAR, respectively.

Measurement of cardiac functional parameters

Cardiac function was analyzed with Vevo 2100 high-resolution echocardiography (RMV-707B, VisualSonics, Toronto, ON, Canada) before surgery and 3 days after reperfusion. After anesthesia with inhaled isoflurane, the left ventricular end diastolic diameter (LVEDD) and the left ventricular end systolic diameter (LVESD) were measured along the short axis views. The left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular fraction shortening (LVFS), left ventricular internal diameter in systole (LVIDs), and left ventricular internal diameter in diastole (LVIDd) were also calculated to determine the cardiac functional status.

Estimation of in vivo CM necrosis

CM necrosis is characterized by the rupture of the plasma membrane and estimated using the Evans blue dye uptake assay as described previously [25]. Briefly, the mice were administered 16 h before I/R with the Evans blue dye (0.1 mL/10 g) by intraperitoneal injections. The mice were sacrificed at 24 h post I/R. The hearts were harvested and cut into 4.5 μm thick slices after embedding in the optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound. The heart sections were then incubated with the mouse anti-cardiac troponin T antibody (cTnT, ab33589, Abcam, USA). The stained sections were imaged under a confocal microscope. The percentage of necrotic CMs (Evans blue+ cTnT+) were quantified with the Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. In addition, necroptotic molecular markers including receptor-interacting protein 3 (RIP3), mixed-lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL), phosphorylated RIP3 (p-RIP3), and phosphorylated MLKL (p-MLKL) were determined by western blotting.

Estimation of serum cTn-I and CK-MB levels

Blood samples were collected from the mice subjected to different treatments. The samples were processed and the serum samples were separated and stored at − 80 °C. The levels of cTn-I (JM-03071M2, Jingmei Biotechnology, China) and CK-MB (JM-03084M2, Jingmei Biotechnology, China) in the serum were measured using the ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Isolation, culturing, and treatment of primary murine cells

We obtained 1-day-old neonatal wild-type C57BL/6J mice from the Experimental Animal Center of Southern Medical University. The primary neonatal CMs were isolated as previously described [26]. Briefly, we isolated hearts from the 1-day-old C57BL/6 mice, cut into small pieces, and digested with 0.25% trypsin (Gibco, USA) at 4 °C for 12 h. Then, the heart tissues were further digested with 0.1% type II collagenase (17101-015, Gibco, USA) in 1% BSA (Sigma, USA) at 37 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, the tissue extracts with CMs were centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min at 25 °C. The cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA). To purify the CMs, the cell suspension was subsequently cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator for 2 h. Then, the supernatant with CMs was carefully collected and centrifuged. The CMs were then resuspended with the DMEM/F12 medium and seeded in petri dishes at an appropriate density for subsequent assays.

Bone marrow-derived neutrophils (BMDNs) were isolated using the Mouse Neutrophil Negative Selection Kit (19762, Stemcell, Canada) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the adult wild-type C57BL/6 mice were sacrificed. The femurs and tibias were harvested immediately and placed in a petri dish with sterile pre-cooled PBS. Bone marrow cells were flushed out into a 5 mL tube with a syringe using the EasySep™ Buffer (20144, Stemcell, Canada) and sequentially incubated with the enrichment cocktail, biotin selection cocktail, and the magnetic particles (all kit components) at 4 °C for 15 min each. BMDNs were then separated using a magnet, resuspended with the RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS, and cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. Freshly isolated BMDNs were defined as N0 neutrophils. BMDNs were stimulated with 1 µg/mL LPS and 20 ng/mL IFN-γ for 4 h to induce N1 polarization (CD11b+Ly6G+CD206−) and incubated with 20 ng/mL IL-4 to induce N2 polarization (CD11b+Ly6G+CD206+).

For the hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) experiments, the CMs were washed twice with PBS, resuspended in serum-free DMEM medium without glucose (Gibco-BRL, USA) and incubated in a hypoxic environment with 1% O2 and 5% CO2 for 12 h. Then, they were resuspended in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin and then incubated in a normoxic environment with 21% O2 and 5% CO2 for 6 h.

For the co-culture experiments, CMs or CFs were seeded into the 0.4 μm pore inserts of a 6-well plate (Corning, USA) and subjected to H/R treatment. Subsequently, the CMs or CFs were co-cultured with the BDMNs, which were previously seeded into the 6-well plates and assessed after 24 h. CMs were treated with 10 μM GW4869 (D1692, Sigma, USA) in the 6-well plate inserts for 24 h to inhibit production of EVs. Then, they were subjected to H/R treatment by resuspending the cells in glucose-free DMEM containing 10 μM GW4869 [24]. Subsequently, the CMs were co-cultured with the BMDNs. DMSO was used as the vehicle control.

Cell viability assays

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release by CMs in the culture medium was spectrophotometrically measured using a kit (BL1405A, Biosharp, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The viability of BMDNs were determined by trypan blue staining (AAPR162-20, Pythonbio, China).

Isolation and characterization of EVs

EVs were isolated from the CM-conditioned medium by ultracentrifugation as previously described [27]. In brief, CM-conditioned media from different treatments were sequentially centrifuged at 300×g for 10 min, 2000×g for 20 min, and 10,000×g for 40 min at 4 °C to eliminate the cells. Then, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Millipore) to remove the cellular debris. The filtered supernatant was then centrifuged at 120,000×g for 90 min at 4 °C (Optima L-80 XP, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) to pellet the EVs. The supernatant was removed. The pelleted EVs were resuspended with pre-cooled PBS and recentrifuged again at 120,000×g for 90 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed and the EV pellets were resuspended in PBS and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent use in the experiments.

According to the guideline of the International Society of Extracellular Vesicles [28], EVs from the CM-conditioned medium were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and western blotting. Morphology of the freshly isolated EVs was assessed by TEM. We pipetted 15 μL of the EVs onto a carbon-coated copper grid and air dried for 1 min. Then, the sample was incubated with 2% uranyl acetate for 1 min at room temperature. After removing excess uranyl acetate and drying, images were captured using the FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit TEM (Tecnai). The size distribution and concentration of the EVs were estimated by NTA (NanoSight NS300, Malvern, UK). EV sample was prepared by diluting 1 μL of the EVs with 29 μL of 0.22 μm filtered sterile PBS, and then samples were injected into the NanoSight instrument. The motility of the EVs were recorded for 30 s at least 3 times with the screen gain of 10 and the camera level of 12, and the following parameters were used for the analysis: a detection threshold of 4 and a screen gain of 10. The results were analyzed by the NTA analytical software (NanoSight NTA3.4). Western blotting analyses of the CMs and EV samples were performed using antibodies against EV-specific markers such as CD9, CD81, and TSG101.

EV uptake assay

CM-derived EVs were labeled with the red fluorescent dye PKH26 (MINI26, Sigma, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The PKH26-labeled EVs were isolated using ultracentrifugation again to clear out the free PKH26 dye. BMDNs (1 × 107 cells) were seeded in the poly-l-lysine–coated confocal dishes. Then, after 3 h, BMDNs were incubated with 1 × 1010 PKH26-labeled EVs at 37 °C for 6 h. The nuclei of BMDNs were stained with DAPI (10 μg/mL) for 10 min before the BMDNs were observed by confocal microscopy (Leica, Germany). The PBS was also incubated with PKH26 and purified to serve as a negative control.

For assessing the uptake of EVs by the cardiac-infiltrating neutrophils in vivo, CM-derived EVs were labeled with the red fluorescent dye DiR (UR21017, Umibio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DiR-labeled CM-derived EVs were isolated using ultracentrifugation again to eliminate the unbound DiR dye. Then, immediately after removing the silk suture, the reperfusion region was injected with 1 × 1010 DiR-labeled EVs. The PBS was also incubated with DiR and purified to serve as a negative control. The fluorescence of DiR-labeled EVs was measured using the Bruker In Vivo FX Pro system (Bruker, MA, USA) at 0 h and 24 h after the injection of DiR-labeled EVs. Furthermore, heart samples were collected after 24 h and single cell suspensions were prepared. Flow cytometry was performed to determine the uptake of DiR-labeled EVs in the cardiac-infiltrating neutrophils.

Preparation of single-cell suspensions from the murine hearts

Single-cell suspensions of the murine hearts were prepared as described previously [10]. Briefly, LV tissues were harvested, minced, and incubated with a cocktail of collagenase II (600 U/mL, Worthington) and DNase I (60 U/mL, AppliChem) at 37 °C for 45 min. The tissue debris and cell clumps were removed by filtering the cell suspension through a 70 μm cell strainer. The single cell suspensions were placed on ice and used immediately to isolate the EVs or perform flow cytometry.

Flow cytometry

Peripheral white blood cells of mice were obtained after lysis of peripheral blood with red blood cell lysis buffer (AAPR27-A500, Pythonbio). Peripheral white blood cells, single-cell suspensions of LV tissues, and BMDNs subjected to different treatments were washed with pre-chilled PBS. To label different neutrophil phenotype, they were incubated with PE-tagged anti-CD11b, APC-tagged anti-Ly6G and Alexa Fluor 700-tagged CD206 antibodies (all from BD Biosciences, USA) in pre-chilled PBS containing 1% BSA for 15 min on ice in the dark. In addition, peripheral white blood cells and single-cell suspensions of LV tissues were incubated with PerCP/Cyanine5.5-tagged anti-CD3, FITC-tagged anti-CD4, Alexa Fluor 647-tagged anti-CD8 and PE-tagged anti-NK-1.1 (all from BioLegend, USA) to label different lymphocyte subsets (CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and natural killer cells). Flow cytometry analysis was performed using the FACScan flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). The flow cytometry data was analyzed using the FlowJo version 7.6.1. software.

In vitro transfection and in vivo administration

The miRNA mimic, inhibitor, antagomir, and agomir used in this study were synthesized by Ribobio (Guangzhou, China). BMDNs were isolated from the mouse bone marrow and seeded in 6-well plates. After 3 h, they were transfected with the miRNA mimic or inhibitor using the RNA transfection reagent (HB-RF-1000, HANBIO, China). The transfection efficiency was analyzed by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). The primary neonatal CMs were isolated from the hearts of 1-day-old C57BL/6 mice, and CMs were transfected with the miRNA mimic or inhibitor using the RNA transfection reagent. For knockdown or overexpression of miR-9-5p in vivo, the reperfusion region was injected immediately with antagomiR-9-5p (3 mg/kg) or agomiR-9-5p (1 mg/kg) after removing the silk suture [29].

Sequencing analyses of EV-derived miRNAs

Total RNA was extracted from the normal control (NC) or H/R-treated CM-derived EVs using the exoRNeasy Maxi Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). MiRNA sequencing was performed using the Illumina NextSeq 500 system (Aksomics, China). The differentially expressed miRNAs were screened based on the expression change ratio ≥ 1.5 or ≤ − 1.5 and P value < 0.05 as threshold parameters. The miRanda, miRTarbase, Targetscan, and miRDB databases were screened to identify the candidate target genes of miR-9-5p. The KOBAS-i (http://bioinfo.org/kobas), an online web tool, was used for gene functional annotation of the candidate target genes of miR-9-5p [30].

RNA pull-down assay

The probe-coated beads were generated by incubating the streptavidin-coupled magnetic beads (15942-050, Invitrogen) with the biotin-coupled miR-9-5p probes and the oligo probes overnight at 4 °C. Then, the cell lysates were prepared from approximately 1 × 107 BMDNs and incubated with the probe-coated beads for 1 h at room temperature. The probe-coated beads were then washed with the buffer and RT-qPCR was performed to detect the pulled down RNAs.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted from the cell or tissue lysates using the TRIzol reagent (R6830-01E.Z.N. A, OMEGA). Total RNA was extracted from the EVs using the exoRNeasy Maxi Kit (77164, QIAGEN). Total RNA was transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan) or the miRNA 1st strand cDNA synthesis kit (AG11717, AG) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, qPCR analysis was performed with the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Takara, Japan) using the LightCycler 480 System (Roche, Germany). The relative expression levels of the miRNAs were normalized to the expression levels of U6 RNA. The expression levels of target mRNAs were normalized to the expression levels of GAPDH mRNA. The relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method. The primers used for the RT-qPCR analysis in this study are listed in Tables S1, S2.

Western blotting

Total protein extracts were prepared from the EVs, cells, and LV tissues using the RIPA Lysis buffer (BL504A, Biosharp, China) supplemented with the Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (C0001, Pythonbio) and the Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (AAPR176-1, Pythonbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total protein concentrations were estimated using the BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by 10–12% SDS-PAGE electrophoresis. The separated proteins were transferred onto the PVDF membranes. The membranes were then blocked with 5% BSA. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4℃ with the following primary antibodies: anti-RIP3 (505431, Zen-bioscience, China), anti-MLKL (612662, Zen-bioscience, China), anti-pRIP3 (R30283, Zen-bioscience, China), anti-pMLKL (R30265, Zen-bioscience, China), anti-CD9 (AF1192, Beyotime, China), anti-CD81 (ab79559, Abcam, USA), anti-TSG101 (AF8259, Beyotime, China), anti-TNF-α (17590-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), anti-IL-1β (26048-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), anti-IL-6 (bs-0782R, Bioss, China), anti-IL-10 (82793-16-RR, Proteintech, USA), anti-Arg1 (16001-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), anti-TGF-β1 (26155-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), anti-SOCS5 (bs-13664R, Bioss, China), anti-SIRT1 (bs-0921R, Bioss, China), anti-JAK2 (YT2426, Immunoway, USA), anti-STAT3 (10253-2-AP, Proteintech, USA), anti-p65 (80979-1-RR, Proteintech, USA), anti-pJAK2 (YP0155, Immunoway, USA), anti-pSTAT3 (bs-1658R, Bioss, China), anti-acetylp65 (bs-23216R, Bioss, China), anti-β-actin (20536-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), and anti-GAPDH (10494-1-AP, Proteintech, USA). Next, the membranes were incubated with the HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (sc-2004, 1:10,000, SantaCruz, USA) for 1–2 h at room temperature. Then, the protein bands were developed using ECL and analyzed using the ImageJ software.

Serum EV-derived miR-9-5p in patients with STEMI undergoing PCI

Patients with STEMI undergoing PCI were enrolled in this study from Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, and the diagnostic criteria for STEMI were in accordance with the 2013 American College of Cardiology Foundation/American Heart Association guideline [31]. Patients were excluded when: (1) patients with cardiogenic shock; (2) pregnant women; (3) patients with severe liver dysfunction or end-stage nephropathy; (4) patients with severe organic disease, infection, or inflammatory disease; (5) patients with malignancy. The whole blood samples (10 mL from peripheral venous blood) were collected from enrolled patients within 24 h after PCI. The whole blood samples were centrifuged at 500×g for 8 min at 25 °C to acquire serum, and serum EVs were subsequently isolated using ultracentrifugation. Then, the serum EV-derived miR-9-5p levels were detected by RT‐qPCR analyses. The primary endpoint was follow-up cardiovascular mortality, which was defined as the deaths attributed to heart diseases and cerebrovascular diseases. The secondary endpoints were follow-up all-cause mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs, defined as the composite of all-cause death, stroke, recurrent myocardial infarction, and target vessel revascularization). All clinical adverse events were obtained through telephone interviews conducted by trained nurses or physicians.

Statistical analysis

The data are represented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. The data was compared between two groups using the unpaired t-test and for more than 2 groups using the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The variables were analyzed at different time points using the Bonferroni-corrected repeated measures ANOVA. Univariate and Multivariate Logistic regression models were constructed to explore the associations between serum EV-derived miR-9-5p levels and the incidence of clinical adverse events. Non-linear correlations were explored using restricted cubic splines. Receiver operating curve (ROC) analysis was used to determine the prognostic value of serum EV-derived miR-9-5p. All the statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS 21.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and the R software (version 4.2.2). Two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Neutrophils show high plasticity after myocardial I/R injury

To determine the plasticity of neutrophils after myocardial I/R injury, we generated a mouse model of myocardial I/R injury (Fig. S1a), and analyzed the cardiac-infiltrating neutrophils on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 post-I/R by flow cytometry. There were very few neutrophils in the heart before I/R; the number of cardiac-infiltrating neutrophils peaked on day 1 post I/R, gradually decreased on days 3 post I/R, and returned to normal levels by day 7 post I/R (Fig. 1a, b). The percentage of N1 neutrophils was highest on day 1 post I/R and returned to baseline levels on day 7 post I/R (Fig. 1a, b). In contrast, the percentage of N2 neutrophils were significantly low on day 1 post I/R, but gradually increased after day 3 post I/R, and returned to baseline levels on day 7 post I/R (Fig. 1a, b). Next, to investigate the correlation between the neutrophil phenotype and myocardial I/R injury, we performed Pearson correlation analysis on day 1 post I/R. The percentage of N1 neutrophils showed negative correlation with LVEF and LVFS and positive correlation with the serum cTn-I and CK-MB levels (Fig. 1c–f). Conversely, the percentage of N2 neutrophils showed positive correlation with LVEF and LVFS and negative correlation with the serum cTn-I and CK-MB levels (Fig. 1c–f). N1 neutrophils aggravate murine myocardial I/R injury

Next, we investigated the effects of different neutrophil phenotypes on the status of myocardial I/R injury. Towards this, we isolated and purified BMDNs and then stimulated the BMDNs into pro-inflammatory N1 or anti-inflammatory N2 neutrophils (Fig. S1b–d). To evaluate the potential for intracardiac injection of exogenous neutrophils to induce a host anti-graft reaction, we initially conducted flow cytometry analysis to examine changes in peripheral and cardiac CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and natural killer cells in normal mice following the intracardiac injection of exogenous neutrophils. Our results showed that intracardiac injection of exogenous neutrophils did not induce a significant change in peripheral and cardiac CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and natural killer cells (Fig. S2a–d). Subsequently, we injected the N1 or N2 neutrophils by intracardiac injections into the myocardial I/R model mice immediately after reperfusion. Our RT-qPCR data demonstrated that the expression levels of pro-inflammatory genes such as Il1β, Il6, and Tnf-α were significantly increased in the LV tissues after intracardiac administration of N1 neutrophils compared with the I/R+PBS mice, whereas intracardiac administration of N2 neutrophils significantly repressed the expression of these pro-inflammatory genes in the LV tissues after I/R (Fig. 2a). Conversely, the expression levels of anti-inflammatory genes such as Il10, Arg1, and Tgf-β1 were increased greatly in the LV tissues after intracardiac administration of N2 neutrophils (Fig. 2b). These results were verified by western blotting (Fig. S2e, f). Previous studies have shown that approximately 50% of CMs undergo necroptosis during myocardial I/R episodes [32]. Therefore, we investigated whether these two different types of neutrophils play a differential role in the necrosis of CMs during myocardial I/R by performing Evans blue dye staining of the heart tissues from different groups of mice. Our results showed that administration of N1 neutrophils increased myocardial necrosis at 24 h after reperfusion based on the positive Evans blue dye-stained area, but administration of N2 neutrophils significantly reduced the positive Evans blue dye-stained area (Fig. 2c, d). Similar results were also observed in the protein expression of necroptotic molecular markers including p-RIP3 and p-MLKL (Fig. S2g, h). Furthermore, consistent with the necrosis data, I/R mice injected with N1 neutrophils showed significantly increased levels of serum myocardial damage markers (cTn-I and CK-MB) and higher IS/AAR ratio compared with the I/R mice injected with PBS following 45 min of ischemia and 24 h of reperfusion; I/R mice injected with N2 neutrophils showed significantly decreased levels of the serum myocardial damage markers and a low IS/AAR ratio compared with the I/R mice injected with PBS following 45 min of ischemia and 24 h of reperfusion (Fig. 2e–g and Fig. S2i). Moreover, we observed significant decline in the cardiac functional parameters on day 3 post I/R after intracardiac administration of N1 neutrophils compared with the I/R mice administered with PBS; however, we observed improved cardiac functional parameters on day 3 post I/R after intracardiac administration of N2 neutrophils (Fig. 2h, i). Collectively, these results confirmed that neutrophil plasticity was associated with the extent of myocardial I/R injury. Inhibition of EV production suppressed N1 neutrophil polarization and attenuated myocardial I/R injury

EVs play an important role in human health and disease by regulating inflammation and immune responses [33]. In this study, we investigated whether EVs regulated neutrophil polarization and cardiac injury on day 1 post I/R. GW4869 is a widely used pharmacological inhibitor of EV biogenesis and release [21], and the doses of GW4869 used in this study had no appreciable cytotoxic effects on cardiomyocytes in vivo and in vitro (Fig. S3a, b). We confirmed that treatment with GW4869 significantly reduced the production of EVs from the I/R hearts (Fig. S3c), and GW4869 had minimal effect on neutrophil polarization in vitro (Fig. S3d, e). LV tissues of the GW4869 group mice showed significantly lower expression levels of pro-inflammatory genes, including Il1β, Il6, and Tnf-α compared to the PBS or DMSO group mice (Fig. 3a). Furthermore, GW4869 treatment significantly reduced infiltration of neutrophils in the cardiac tissues after myocardial I/R; importantly, compared with the controls, hearts of the GW4869 group mice after myocardial I/R showed decreased proportion of N1 neutrophils and increased proportion of N2 neutrophils (Fig. 3b, c). Moreover, we found that the proportion of N1 neutrophils was significantly increased in the peripheral blood and heart of myocardial I/R model mice, especially in the heart (Figs. 1a, b and S3f, g). Evans blue dye staining of the murine heart tissues showed that GW4869 treatment reduced myocardial necrosis in response to myocardial I/R (Fig. 3d, e). In addition, GW4869 treatment significantly reduced the levels of serum myocardial damage markers (Fig. 3f) and the IS/AAR ratio (Figs. 3g, h and S3h). The heart functional parameters were significantly higher in the GW4869 treatment group mice after myocardial I/R compared with those receiving PBS or DMSO (Fig. 3i, j). Therefore, we isolated primary CMs and CFs from the neonatal wild-type C57BL/6J mice (Fig. S4). The CMs and CFs were subjected to H/R and then co-cultured with BMDNs for 24 h (Fig. 4a). CM-EV-derived miR-9-5p promotes polarization of neutrophils into the N1 phenotype

MiRNAs are the most abundant non-coding RNAs in the EVs and are associated with human health and disease [36, 37]. EV-derived miRNAs are involved in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases, and are considered as potential prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for the cardiovascular diseases [36]. Therefore, we performed miRNA sequencing of the EVs derived from the H/R-treated and the normal CMs to identify potential miRNAs that regulate N1 polarization of the cardiac-infiltrating neutrophils. We compared the miRNA profiles of EVs derived from the H/R-treated and the normal CMs and identified 19 differential expressed miRNAs, including 12 downregulated miRNAs and 7 upregulated miRNAs in the EVs derived from the H/R-treated CMs (Fig. 6a, b). Among these differentially expressed miRNAs, we selected the high-expressed miRNAs in the EVs derived from the H/R-pretreated CMs and verified them by RT-qPCR (Fig. 6c). Then, we analyzed the expression levels of the top three highly expressed miRNAs (miR-9-5p, miR-195a-5p, and miR-338-5p) by RT-qPCR in different phenotypes of neutrophils. Our analysis showed that only miR-9-5p was significantly up-regulated in the N1 neutrophils but remained unchanged in the N2 neutrophils compared with the N0 neutrophils (Fig. S5a). Moreover, the expression of miR-9-5p was higher in the H/R-treated CMs compared to the normal CMs and the N0 neutrophils (Fig. S5b), and miR-9-5p was also up-regulated in the hearts of mice subjected to myocardial I/R compared to hearts from the sham mice (Fig. S5c). Besides, miR-9-5p was more enriched in H/R-treated CMs compared to H/R-treated vascular endothelial cells (human umbilical vein endothelial cell) and CFs (Fig. S5d). RT-qPCR assays revealed increased expression of miR-9-5p in the lower layer BMDNs co-cultured with miR-9-5p-overexpressing CMs compared to the controls, but it could be attenuated by GW4869 (Fig. S5e). SOCS5 and SIRT1 are key players in the JAK2/STAT3 and NF-κB signaling pathways [38, 39], and NF-κB and JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathways are implicated in the inflammatory responses underlying several human diseases [40, 41]. Thus, we investigated the effects of miR-9-5p on the NF-κB and JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathways. Western blotting analysis demonstrated that miR-9-5p mimic significantly increased the levels of acetylated P65 (acetyl-P65), phosphorylated JAK2 (p-JAK2), and phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) in the BMDNs in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 7f, g). Furthermore, miR-9-5p mimic enhanced activation of NF-κB and JAK2/STAT3 in the BMDNs that were incubated with EVs derived from the H/R-treated CMs, but these effects were reversed by treatment with the miR-9-5p inhibitor (Fig. 7h, i). Taken together, these results suggested that miR-9-5p induced N1 neutrophil polarization by activating SIRT1/NF-κB and SOCS5/JAK2/STAT3 signaling.

Furthermore, treatment with antagomiR-9-5p decreased the proportion of N1 neutrophils and increased the proportion of N2 neutrophils in the LV tissues after myocardial I/R (Fig. 8b, c). Evans blue dye staining of the heart tissues showed that administration of antagomiR-9-5p decreased myocardial necrosis after I/R (Fig. 8d, e). Western blotting results further showed that administration of agomiR-9-5p significantly increased the protein levels of p-RIP3 and p-MLKL in the LV tissues after myocardial I/R (Fig. S7b, c). Serum EV-derived miR-9-5p serves as a prognostic biomarker for patients with STEMI undergoing PCI

As CM-EV-derived miR-9-5p aggravates myocardial I/R injury in mice, we next examined whether serum EV-derived miR-9-5p levels are associated with adverse cardiovascular events in patients with STEMI undergoing PCI. A total of 294 patients with STEMI undergoing PCI were enrolled, and Table S4 provides detailed characteristics of the patients included in this study. Serum EV-derived miR-9-5p levels were significantly negatively correlated with LVEF within 24 h after PCI, but not significantly correlated with the levels of serum cTn-T, CK-MB, and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) (Fig. S8a–d). During a median follow-up period of 49 months (interquartile range, 36–51 months), 21 incident cases of cardiovascular mortality, 32 incident cases of all-cause mortality, and 49 incident cases of MACEs were observed in patients with STEMI undergoing PCI. The incidence of cardiovascular mortality was significantly decreased with increasing -lg(miR-9-5p) tertile (Fig. S8e–g). Further, we found that serum EV-derived miR-9-5p levels were independently associated with higher cardiovascular mortality in patients with STEMI undergoing PCI (Tables S5, S6), and the restricted cubic splines revealed a non-linear association of the serum EV-derived miR-9-5p levels with cardiovascular mortality (Fig. S8h). Moreover, the area under the receiver operative curves of serum EV-derived miR-9-5p for cardiovascular mortality in such patients was 0.67, which was comparable to the area under the curve of LVEF, serum cTn-T and CK-MB levels, NT-proBNP, C-reactive protein, and serum creatinine levels within 24 h after PCI (Fig. S8i–n). Together, these results indicated that serum EV-derived miR-9-5p was a promising prognostic biomarker for cardiovascular mortality in patients with STEMI undergoing PCI.

Discussion

Neutrophil-mediated inflammation plays a significant role in myocardial I/R injury [42]. Recent studies have reported that cardiac-infiltrating neutrophils exhibit anti-inflammatory as well as pro-inflammatory phenotypes during myocardial injury [9–11]. Several studies have demonstrated the role of neutrophils in myocardial I/R injury [4, 43, 44], but the effects of neutrophil plasticity on myocardial I/R injury remain unclear. In this study, we showed that cardiac-infiltrating neutrophils displayed significant plasticity during myocardial I/R. N1 neutrophils were predominant on day 1 post myocardial I/R, whereas the proportion of N2 neutrophils gradually increased after day 3 post myocardial I/R. Then, our results confirmed that N1 neutrophils aggravated cardiac inflammation, enlarged cardiac infarct size and exacerbated cardiac dysfunction at the early stage of myocardial I/R. Our study further demonstrated that miR-9-5p from the CM-derived EVs induced polarization of the neutrophils towards the N1 phenotype by activating SIRT1/NF-κB and SOCS5/JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathways. Importantly, we confirmed that the serum EV-derived miR-9-5p levels were independently associated with cardiovascular mortality in patients with STEMI undergoing PCI. Therefore, we demonstrated neutrophil polarization is a promising therapeutic target against myocardial I/R-induced inflammation and injury, and serum EV-derived miR-9-5p is a promising prognostic biomarker for cardiovascular mortality in patients with STEMI undergoing PCI.

Excessive infiltration of neutrophils after reperfusion is significantly associated with myocardial I/R injury [45]. Several studies have demonstrated that depletion of neutrophils reduced myocardial injury and infarct size after myocardial reperfusion [46, 47]. However, a recent study in the myocardial infarction model mice demonstrated that the depletion of neutrophils was associated with significant decline in cardiac function, enhanced myocardial fibrosis, and increased expression of biomarkers associated with heart failure [48]. Neutrophils are associated with anti-inflammatory, angiogenic, and repair functions [11, 49, 50]. Therefore, the requirement for effective therapy to alleviate myocardial I/R injury by targeting neutrophil-mediated inflammation includes reducing cardiac inflammation and preserving the pro-angiogenic and repair functions of the neutrophils. Ma et al. reported the presence of the pro-inflammatory N1 neutrophils and the anti-inflammatory N2 neutrophils in the heart tissues of MI mice; moreover, they demonstrated that the N1 neutrophils were associated with ventricular wall thinning after MI [10]. Therefore, modulation of neutrophil polarization is a potential therapeutic strategy for reducing neutrophil-mediated myocardial I/R injury. In this study, our data showed the presence of N1 and N2 neutrophils in the cardiac tissues after I/R, with the N1 neutrophils being the predominant subtype of neutrophils at the early stage of reperfusion. Several studies have shown that neutrophils are plastic and play an important role in the pathogenesis of human diseases, including cardiovascular diseases. Baban et al. reported that renal I/R injury caused N1 polarization of the neutrophils, but treatment with cannabidiol restored the balance between N1 and N2 neutrophils and significantly improved renal function [51]. Reprogramming of neutrophils into the N2 phenotype alleviated inflammation in the animal models of stroke, MI, and rosacea [52–54]. Our data also demonstrated that the changes in the proportion of N1 and N2 neutrophils were associated with cardiac injury after I/R. Intracardiac injection of N1 neutrophils enhanced inflammation, aggravated myocardial injury, worsened cardiac dysfunction, and enlarged cardiac infarct size at the early stage after reperfusion, but the N2 neutrophils reversed the adverse effects of the N1 neutrophils. Therefore, our study provides significant evidence for the role of neutrophil polarization in myocardial I/R injury.

To date, several studies have demonstrated that EVs are involved in the inflammatory response after myocardial I/R injury [55]. We also investigated the role of EVs in neutrophil polarization after myocardial I/R by inhibiting the synthesis of EVs with GW4869. We demonstrated that GW4869 treatment alleviated myocardial I/R injury by suppressing the production of EVs. This data was in line with the findings of a previous study, which demonstrated that inhibition of EV production alleviated myocardial I/R injury [21]. Furthermore, our data showed that inhibition of EVs decreased cardiac N1 neutrophils in response to myocardial I/R, thereby reducing cardiac inflammation. Importantly, our results found that EVs also had a strong chemotaxis effect on neutrophils during myocardial I/R episodes. However, our data showed that the proportion of N1 neutrophils in the heart was significantly higher than that in the peripheral blood of myocardial I/R model mice, suggesting that EVs were involved in the N1 polarization of cardiac-infiltrating neutrophils rather than merely recruiting N1 neutrophils to the heart at the early stage of myocardial I/R injury. Previous studies have reported that CMs exposed to ischemia release large quantities of EVs with immunomodulatory functions [14, 15, 33]. We further demonstrated that the CM-derived EVs were taken up by the neutrophils and was related to N1 polarization of the neutrophils. Several other studies have also confirmed that the CM-derived EVs are significantly associated with immunomodulatory effects that are responsible for the pathophysiological processes underlying heart diseases [16, 56, 57].

MiRNAs play a significant role in several physiological and pathological processes by regulating the expression levels of their target genes. Previous studies have demonstrated that several miRNAs participate in macrophage reprogramming [58]. However, the role of miRNAs in the regulation of neutrophil polarization has not been reported in detail. In this study, we identified miR-9-5p from the CM-derived EVs as a key player in the polarization of neutrophils to the N1 phenotype. Previous studies have reported that miR-9-5p is associated with inflammation [59, 60]. High expression of miR-9-5p is associated with the polarization of macrophages to the M1 phenotype [61, 62]. Our study showed that miR-9-5p facilitated polarization of neutrophils into the N1 phenotype. Furthermore, we observed positive correlation between expression levels of miR-9-5p and the pro-inflammatory genes in the neutrophils. Moreover, rescue experiments with miR-9-5p inhibitor and H/R-treated CM-derived EVs further clarified that N1 neutrophil polarization mediated by the CM-EV-derived miR-9-5p played a significant role in the myocardial I/R injury. NF-κB and JAK/STAT are two important inflammatory-regulatory signaling modules [63, 64]. In osteoarthritis, miR-9-5p promotes M1 macrophage polarization via SIRT1/NF-κB pathways [65]. Our study also showed that inhibition of SIRT1 by miR-9-5p activated NF-κB, an important factor that induced polarization of neutrophils to the N1 phenotype. In addition, our study also indicated that CM-EV-derived miR-9-5p mediated neutrophil polarization by targeting the SOCS5/JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. It is well established that activating JAK2/STAT3 signal pathway have a directly cardioprotective effect in CMs mainly through anti-apoptosis [66]. However, our results showed that overexpression of cardiac miR-9-5p aggravated myocardial necroptosis at the early stage of myocardial I/R, which implies that miR-9-5p has more detrimental than protective effects at the early stage of myocardial I/R. This also suggests, to some extent, that miR-9-5p mainly activates JAK2/STAT3 signal pathway in neutrophils rather than CMs at the early stage of myocardial I/R. Therefore, both the SIRT1/NF-κB and SOCS5/JAK2/STAT3 signaling axes regulated neutrophil polarization and inflammation in myocardial I/R injury. However, the crosstalk between the SIRT1/NF-κB and SOCS5/JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathways needs to be analyzed further.

In this study, we demonstrated that CM-EV delivered miR-9-5p from the CMs to the neutrophils during myocardial I/R. This resulted in the polarization of neutrophils to the N1 phenotype and aggravated myocardial I/R injury. Our study demonstrated a new mechanism for enhanced cardiac inflammation after myocardial I/R because of pro-inflammatory polarization of the neutrophils. This provides new avenues for the prevention and treatment of myocardial I/R injury. Importantly, we also demonstrated the association of the serum EV-derived miR-9-5p levels with cardiovascular mortality in patients with STEMI undergoing PCI, providing a promising biomarker for the risk of cardiovascular mortality in such population. However, there are still some issues that need to be investigated further. Firstly, we found that the CM-neutrophil interaction modulated the inflammatory microenvironment in the I/R hearts. However, the cardiac tissues are composed of CMs, CFs, endothelial cells, macrophages and other cell types. Therefore, future studies are necessary to determine the interaction between different cardiac cell types and the neutrophils to refine the regulatory network that modulates neutrophil polarization. Secondly, although the intracardiac injection of 5 × 106 N2 neutrophils could show an effect on myocardial I/R injury, we did not perform dose–response experiments to choose the optimal dose of N2 neutrophil treatment in the present study. Future studies are needed to confirm the optimal dose of N2 neutrophil treatment for myocardial I/R injury. Thirdly, this study suggested an increased proportion of N2 neutrophils at the late stage of myocardial I/R, but we failed to investigate the effect of N2 neutrophil polarization on cardiac repair in this study. A previous study reported that neutrophils modulated cardiac repair by polarizing the macrophages towards a reparative phenotype [47]. Therefore, in the future, we need to further investigate the role of N2 neutrophils on cardiac repair after myocardial I/R injury and the crosstalk between neutrophil reprogramming and macrophage reprogramming to further unravel the pathophysiological mechanisms of myocardial I/R injury. Fourthly, we identified CM-derived EVs as a mediator of the feedback loop between N1 polarized neutrophils and damaged CMs. However, damage associated molecular patterns from damaged CMs also contribute to neutrophil polarization [10]. Whether CM-derived damage associated molecular patterns or EVs play a dominant role in regulating neutrophil polarization remains to be elucidated in future studies. Fifthly, miR-9-5p from CM-derived EVs was identified as a key regulator of neutrophil reprogramming in this study. Notably, EVs contain several factors that may play a role in neutrophil polarization and inflammation in myocardial I/R injury. Further investigations are necessary to discover these factors and mechanisms. Sixthly, since serum EV-derived miR-9-5p levels were examined at a single time point in this study, it is unclear whether the dynamics of the serum EV-derived miR-9-5p are associated with cardiovascular mortality. Future researches should be carried out to evaluate the prognostic value of serum EV-derived miR-9-5p at different time points to determine the optimal time point for monitoring. Finally, the present study had limited sample size, and future studies with a large sample size are required to confirm the clinical implications of serum EV-derived miR-9-5p and to assess the efficacy of serum EV-derived miR-9-5p detection in combination with clinical parameters as a prognostic biomarker in patients with STEMI undergoing PCI.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that neutrophil N1 polarization induced by CM-EV-derive miR-9-5p aggravated myocardial I/R Injury. These findings suggest neutrophil polarization is a promising therapeutic target against myocardial I/R-induced inflammation and injury, and serum EV-derived miR-9-5p is a promising prognostic biomarker for cardiovascular mortality in patients with STEMI undergoing PCI.
