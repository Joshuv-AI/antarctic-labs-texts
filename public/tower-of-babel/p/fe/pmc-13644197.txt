Human-mediated determinants of trypanocide use in dogs in Southeastern Nigeria: a cross-sectional survey with implications for resistance and One Health
Authors: Chukwunonso Francis Obi, Romanus Chukwuduruo Ezeokonkwo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTrypanocide resistance is an escalating threat to African animal trypanosomosis control, yet how dogs are actually treated for trypanosomosis, by who, with what drugs, and under what oversight, remains poorly characterized despite the documented presence of resistant trypanosome populations in this neglected host compartment.MethodsIn a cross-sectional survey interpreted within a One Health framework, grounded in data from Enugu North Senatorial Zone, Southeastern Nigeria, we integrate previously published molecular and epidemiological data on canine trypanosomes with original cross-sectional survey data from 120 dog-owning households and 250 dogs. Associations are descriptive and inferential; no new resistance assays or transmission studies were undertaken.ResultsSurvey data revealed that 87.5% of treated dogs received treatment without prior parasitological confirmation, 83% received doses estimated without weight-based calculation, and 76.7% of households obtained trypanocides from unregulated sources. Only 38.3% had geographic access to a licensed veterinarian, and 60% reported no community access to diagnostic testing. Inter-household dog movement was documented in 44.2% of households and 57.5% reported livestock co-habitation. These findings are consistent with sustained selective pressure for resistance, interpreted alongside published evidence of diminazene-resistant and multidrug-resistant Trypanosoma isolates from the same zone.ConclusionsDogs emerge as plausible reservoirs and amplifiers of drug-resistant trypanosomes within a One Health framework, posing risks to livestock productivity and public health. The molecular identification of Trypanosoma brucei gambiense in study-area dogs warrants structured surveillance investigation. Direct transmission pathways were not evaluated. Addressing these drivers requires robust veterinary drug regulation, expanded veterinary service access, community education, and integration of companion animals into national trypanosomosis surveillance frameworks.

Introduction

Canine African trypanosomosis is increasingly recognized as a substantial, though historically under-reported, burden in dog populations across endemic regions of Nigeria. A cross-sectional survey of 450 dogs in Enugu North Senatorial Zone, the same study area examined here, found an overall trypanosome prevalence of 11.3%, with Trypanosoma brucei as the predominant species, alongside T. congolense, T. evansi, and the first reported canine T. vivax infection in Nigeria [10]. Infection was associated with clinically relevant haematological changes, confirming that canine trypanosomosis represents an active disease burden rather than an incidental finding in this population. Notably, Trypanosoma brucei gambiense, the causative agent of human African trypanosomiasis, has subsequently been molecularly confirmed in dogs from the same region [14], underscoring the potential epidemiological significance of the canine compartment within the local transmission landscape.

The study zone has large dog population, kept predominantly for guarding, hunting, and companionship, which live in close, frequently unsupervised contact with their owners, and routinely co-habit with livestock, creating substantial and repeated opportunities for both vector exposure and human-directed drug treatment. Despite this documented burden and exposure, the behavioural and structural determinants of how dogs are actually treated for trypanosomosis, by whom, with what drugs, and under what oversight, have not previously been systematically surveyed in this or comparable Nigerian settings, even though analogous owner- and farmer-level surveys have long informed resistance management in livestock trypanosomosis [4, 6].

Trypanocide resistance constitutes an escalating threat to the sustainability of African animal trypanosomosis control across sub-Saharan Africa. Despite decades of chemotherapy-led control programmes, in which diminazene aceturate and isometamidium chloride form the backbone of treatment, the long-standing reliance on a limited drug arsenal is associated with the emergence of resistant trypanosome populations increasingly reported across endemic regions [2, 11, 15]. Research into resistance drivers has focused predominantly on livestock systems, leaving companion animals, particularly dogs, as a critically neglected host compartment despite their intensive drug exposure, high mobility, and structural proximity to both humans and livestock [5, 3, 15].

While parasite-intrinsic mechanisms of resistance are well-characterized, including alterations in drug transporters and P2-type adenosine transporter function [1, 2, 8, 15], it is increasingly recognized that these mechanisms operate within a broader socio-ecological context shaped by drug-use practices, regulatory environments, and health system limitations [4, 12]. In endemic settings, patterns of treatment based on clinical signs alone, without parasitological or laboratory confirmation (hereafter, presumptive treatment), informal drug markets, sub-therapeutic dosing, and limited veterinary oversight collectively structure drug pressure and thereby influence the emergence, persistence, and dissemination of resistant parasite populations, yet these upstream, human-mediated drivers remain systematically understudied in companion animal populations.

Despite extensive livestock studies, companion animals, particularly dogs, remain largely neglected in trypanosomosis resistance research, even though they are frequently treated and occupy unique epidemiological niches. In endemic areas, dogs are highly susceptible to infection, frequently treated presumptively, without prior diagnostic confirmation, and often managed outside formal veterinary structures [11]. Their close association with humans, high mobility, and participation in informal trade networks enhance their potential as reservoirs and disseminators of resistant trypanosomes. Recent studies from southeastern Nigeria indicate that dogs harbour diverse Trypanosoma species, including diminazene- and multidrug-resistant populations [14, 10, 11].

The present study does not re-present previously reported prevalence or resistance phenotypes. Rather, it reports a cross-sectional survey of dog-owner treatment practices and veterinary service access, interpreted alongside primary cross-sectional survey data that integrates (i) previously published molecular, epidemiological, and resistance data on canine trypanosomes from Southeastern Nigeria [10, 11, 14] with (ii) original cross-sectional survey data on veterinary drug access, treatment practices, and availability of professional veterinary services collected from 120 dog-owning households and 250 dogs. The objective of this survey is to identify, characterize, and interpret the human-mediated and systemic determinants of trypanocide use that may favour resistance emergence in dogs, and to situate these determinants within a One Health framework that links human behaviour, animal health systems, and parasite evolution.

Materials and methods

Study design and conceptual framework

This study is a cross-sectional survey of dog-owning households, interpreted within a One Health conceptual framework that integrates two complementary evidence streams: (i) previously published molecular, epidemiological, and trypanocide-resistance data from domestic dogs in Enugu North Senatorial Zone, southeastern Nigeria [10, 11, 14], and (ii) original cross-sectional observational data on drug-use practices and veterinary service access generated through a structured household and dog-level survey conducted concurrently during field sampling for the molecular studies. This interpretation is explicitly descriptive and inferential rather than experimental. No new resistance assays, pharmacokinetic studies, or experimental transmission studies were conducted. Study area

The study was conducted in Enugu North Senatorial Zone, Enugu State, Southeastern Nigeria. The zone comprises six Local Government Areas (LGAs), namely Igboetiti, Igbo-Eze North, Igbo-Eze South, Nsukka, Udenu, and Uzo-Uwani. The region is characterized by a derived sub-humid savannah ecology with a distinct wet season (April–October) and dry season (November–March). The area supports both smallholder livestock production (cattle, sheep, and goats) and substantial dog populations kept for guarding, hunting, and companionship purposes. The zone has been established as an endemic focus for canine trypanosomosis [10], with active transmission confirmed across multiple communities. Sampling was conducted in communities distributed across four LGAs to ensure geographic coverage. Communities were selected to represent variation in rurality, livestock co-habitation density, and access to veterinary services.

Cross-sectional survey: sampling and recruitment

A total of 120 dog-owning households and 250 dogs were enrolled in the cross-sectional survey of trypanocide treatment practices; consistent with the descriptive design stated above, dogs were not individually tested for trypanosome infection as part of this survey. The cross-sectional survey was conducted between January and August 2016, concurrently with field sample collection for the studies reported by Obi et al. [10, 11]. Household-level sampling was conducted using a convenience sampling approach, guided by availability during field epidemiological visits and community-leader referral within selected communities. Households were eligible for inclusion if they owned at least one dog and if the dog owner or primary caregiver was present and consented to participation at the time of the visit. No household was visited more than once. Dogs were enrolled from participating households up to a maximum of five dogs per household, dogs were eligible if they were adults (estimated age > 6 months), and present at the household at the time of the visit. Each enrolled dog served as the individual unit of reference for owner-reported treatment-history data (Table 1): for every enrolled dog, the owner or caregiver was asked about that specific dog's prior trypanocide treatment, dosing, and drug source, generating the dog-level proportions reported in Tables 2 and 3. Dogs were identified and counted by the field team to establish this unit of reference but were not clinically examined, weighed, or sampled as part of this survey.. Data collection instrument

Contextual observations were recorded using a structured observation and interview checklist administered at the point of sample collection by trained veterinary field personnel. The checklist was designed in English and administered by bilingual veterinary field personnel, who orally translated questions into Igbo, the predominant local language in the study area, where this aided respondent comprehension; no separate back-translation procedure was applied, as the checklist was administered as a structured interview rather than a self-completed instrument. In addition to the six domains summarized in Table 1, basic household demographic information (household size and the primary occupation of the dog owner or caregiver) was recorded but was not analysed in detail in this report. The checklist was developed through a review of the published literature on trypanocide resistance and drug-use practices in endemic settings [4, 12, 6] and local veterinary practice guidelines. It was pre-tested in a pilot subset of 10 households not included in the main study sample, and refined for clarity, feasibility, and cultural appropriateness prior to full deployment. The checklist captured data across six domains summarized in Table 1. Data were recorded contemporaneously during the household visit to minimize recall bias.

Statistical analysis of survey data

Cross-sectional survey data were entered into Microsoft Excel (version 16.0) and analysed using SPSS version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize household- and dog-level variables as frequencies and proportions. No inferential statistical comparisons were performed between groups, as the study was designed as a descriptive observational survey. The integration of survey proportions with published resistance phenotype data from the same study population [11] was performed descriptively within the One Health conceptual framework and does not imply statistical association or causal linkage between specific household practices and resistance outcomes at the individual animal level.

Results

Across the 120 surveyed households and 250 dogs, three broad patterns emerged from the survey data, detailed in Tables 2, 3 and 4 and summarized narratively below. First, treatment of dogs for suspected trypanosomosis was overwhelmingly presumptive and imprecisely dosed: most treated dogs received drugs without parasitological confirmation or weight-based dosing. Second, drug acquisition occurred predominantly outside licensed veterinary channels, reflecting both a structural shortage of accessible veterinary services and a reliance on informal markets. Third, dogs were structurally connected to both the wider community and co-habiting livestock through frequent inter-household movement and shared living space, creating plausible pathways for any resistant parasites they harbour to disseminate beyond the individual animal. Trypanocide exposure and presumptive treatment

The mean number of dogs per household was 2 (range: 1–5). Adult males constituted the majority of the dogs sampled (148/250, 59.2%), whereas adult females accounted for 102 (40.8%). Just over a third of surveyed dogs (88/250, 35.2%) had been treated with a trypanocide in the preceding 12 months, most often with diminazene aceturate, the compound known to predominate in the informal veterinary drug market [7] in southeastern Nigeria (Table 2). Among treated dogs, treatment without prior parasitological confirmation was the dominant pattern, accounting for 77 of 88 cases (87.5%), and over a third of treated dogs that relapsed were retreated without repeat testing (Table 3). These patterns, rather than the precise frequencies, are the focus of interpretation below.

Drug access, sources, and prescription practices

Across the 120 surveyed households, drug acquisition was concentrated in unlicensed channels: agro-veterinary shops and informal market or street vendors together accounted for 82 households (68.3%), compared with 27 households (22.5%) sourcing trypanocides from a licensed veterinary outlet (Table 4). Close to half of households (58/120, 48.3%) obtained trypanocides without a veterinary prescription, decoupling drug availability from professional diagnostic oversight.

Dosing practices

Weight-based dose calculation with scale measurement was reported in only 15 of 88 treated dogs (17%), while 73 (83%) received doses estimated by the owner or drug vendor without weight-based measurement (Table 3). Dosing guidance was obtained from a licensed veterinarian in only 15 cases (17%); the majority received guidance from agro-veterinary vendors (26.1%) or community members and self-practice (56.8%). Full treatment course completion was reported for 60 dogs (68.2%).

Veterinary service access

Access to licensed veterinary services was limited across the study sample (Table 4). Only 46 of 120 households (38.3%) had access to a licensed veterinarian within 5 km of their community, and only 48 (40%) reported consulting a licensed veterinarian for their dog at least once in the preceding 12 months. Eighty-seven households (72.5%) reported professional veterinary care as unaffordable, and 81 (67.5%) relied exclusively on self-medication or informal community advice for management of dog health problems. Seventy-six households (60%) reported no access to trypanosomosis diagnostic testing within their community.

Dog movement and livestock co-habitation

Inter-household movement of dogs through informal trade or gifting networks was documented in 53 (44.2%) of 120 households (Table 4). Co-habitation with livestock species (cattle, sheep, or goats) was recorded in 69 households (57.5%). Dogs sharing grazing or pasture space with livestock were documented in 32 households (26.7%). Notably, 27 households (22.5%) reported that treated dogs moved to a new household within 30 days of treatment, a specific resistance-dissemination pathway in the canine compartment.

Discussion

Human-mediated drug pressure and resistance selection

Cross-sectional survey data from 120 households and 250 dogs revealed a pattern of sustained, frequently inappropriate trypanocide exposure that may favour selection for resistance. Treatment without parasitological confirmation, documented in 77 dogs (87.5% of those treated), and repeat dosing without diagnostic re-testing following relapse (32 dogs, 36.4%), are the most prevalent drug-use practices observed and are practices that, in principle, could contribute to resistance selection. When interpreted alongside published evidence of diminazene-resistant and multidrug-resistant Trypanosoma isolates in dogs from the same zone [11], these practices describe a plausible, though unconfirmed, pathway from drug pressure to resistance phenotype, broadly consistent with established selection models [8, 12]. Direct linkage between specific practices and specific resistance phenotypes within the same animals was not assessed, the associations described here are therefore systems-level associations rather than individual causal effects. The observed dosing practices, with 83% of dogs dosed without weight-based calculation, could theoretically generate sub-therapeutic plasma drug concentrations capable of favouring selective survival and expansion of partially resistant parasite subpopulations [8, 13],however, direct pharmacokinetic verification was not performed, and this interpretation remains speculative.

Unregulated drug access, documented in 92 of 120 households (76.7%) obtaining trypanocides from an agro-veterinary shop, informal market, itinerant vendor, or community member rather than a licensed veterinary outlet, removes professional oversight from treatment decisions and may further increase selective pressure. The predominance of agro-veterinary shops and informal vendors as drug sources (82 households, 68.3% combined) creates a structural environment in which dosing accuracy, diagnostic guidance, and post-treatment monitoring are consistently absent. These dynamics closely parallel those documented in cattle-keeping communities in Uganda [6] and West Africa [4], where informal market drug access was identified as a primary driver of accelerated resistance emergence.

Veterinary service gaps as structural amplifiers

Limited access to licensed veterinary services is a plausible structural amplifier of the drug-use practices documented above, operating alongside rather than independently of them. In many rural and peri-urban communities within the study area, professional veterinary services are both geographically inaccessible and economically prohibitive, compelling dog owners to rely on self-medication, informal drug sources, and community advice. Only 38.3% of households had access to a licensed veterinarian within 5 km of their community, and 60% reported no access to trypanosomosis diagnostic testing. These access barriers are consistent with, and may help explain, the drug-use patterns described above. In the absence of professional diagnosis, clinical signs of trypanosomosis may be confused with those of other illnesses, leading to treatment of non-trypanosomal illness with trypanocides; this scenario, while not directly assessed in the present survey, would expose parasite populations to drug pressure without therapeutic justification.

Inter-household movement of dogs through informal trade or gifting networks, documented in 44.2% of households, represents a plausible mechanism by which any drug-resistant trypanosomes carried by dogs could disseminate across community boundaries, although this study did not track parasite genotypes alongside dog movements. We suggest that the structural exclusion of companion animals from formal veterinary oversight systems, rather than the behaviour of individual dog owners alone, is an important amplifying factor in this system, though both operate together and cannot be fully disentangled with the present data.

Several features of the local drug market plausibly explain why dog owners obtain trypanocides without veterinary consultation. Agro-veterinary shops and informal vendors are typically closer to dog-owning households than licensed veterinary clinics, charge substantially less than a formal veterinary consultation and treatment fee, and require no appointment or travel, making them the most accessible option for owners with visibly sick dogs. Dosing at these outlets is generally guided by the vendor's own experience or by standard adult-dog quantities recommended informally by drug distributors, rather than by individualized weight-based dosing; this is consistent with the low proportion of dogs in our sample that were weighed before treatment (17%). These structural and economic incentives, rather than simple unawareness on the part of owners, likely sustain the self-dosing pattern observed in this survey. Other behavioural factors may also reinforce this practice. Owners who have previously observed apparent clinical improvement following trypanocide treatment may become confident in recognising the disease and treating their dogs without professional guidance. Perceived familiarity with the clinical signs of trypanosomosis, convenience, ready availability of trypanocides, reliance on advice from drug vendors or other community members, and limited awareness of the importance of appropriate dosing and laboratory confirmation may collectively encourage continued self-treatment. These interacting influences suggest that self-treatment reflects a combination of behavioural, socioeconomic, and health-system factors rather than poor owner knowledge alone [4, 6, 12].

Contextualization within the comparative literature

The patterns of human-mediated drug pressure documented in this study align closely with findings from analogous investigations of trypanocide resistance in livestock systems across sub-Saharan Africa. Kasozi et al. [6] documented widespread presumptive treatment, unregulated drug access through informal markets, and dosing practices inconsistent with manufacturer recommendations in Southwestern Uganda, structural parallels consistent with the present findings despite differences in host species and geographic ecology. Grace et al. [4] similarly identified informal drug market access and farmer-directed presumptive treatment as the dominant resistance drivers in cattle trypanosomosis management in West Africa. Richards et al. [12] framed the challenge as a 'pharma to farmer' gap, a failure not of drug pharmacology but of the systems governing drug delivery, dosing accuracy, and diagnostic access. The present findings suggest that a similar dynamic operates in the companion animal compartment, where institutional oversight is essentially absent, no quantitative comparison of intensity between host compartments was performed, and any suggestion that the dynamic is more pronounced in dogs than in livestock would require a head-to-head study designed for that comparison.

At the continental scale, Tchamdja et al. [13] documented drug-resistant Trypanosoma populations in communities with high rates of informal drug access in northern Togo, a pattern structurally similar to the drug-access pattern observed here. Ungogo and de Koning [15] explicitly identified unregulated drug markets as a primary resistance driver and call for One Health approaches that integrate animal management into resistance governance frameworks, a recommendation for which the present survey provides, to our knowledge, one of the first owner-level, quantified datasets from the canine perspective in Nigeria.

Integration of previously published molecular evidence

The present cross-sectional survey was designed to complement our previously published molecular investigations conducted in Enugu North Senatorial Zone. Whereas the molecular studies focused on parasite detection, species identification, and trypanocide susceptibility, this survey examined owner treatment practices, veterinary service access, and dog-management factors that shape the behavioural and structural context of trypanocide use. Together, these datasets provide a more comprehensive systems-level understanding of canine trypanosomosis and trypanocide resistance than either approach alone.

Obi et al. [10] reported an overall molecular prevalence of 11.3% (51/450), with Trypanosoma brucei predominating alongside T. congolense, T. evansi, and T. vivax. Subsequently, Obi et al. [11] characterised the drug susceptibility of isolates recovered during the same sampling period (January–August 2016), demonstrating the occurrence of diminazene-resistant and multidrug-resistant T. brucei. While these studies established the presence of resistant trypanosome populations in dogs, they did not investigate the treatment practices or veterinary service factors that may generate or sustain the selection pressures acting on these parasites.

A third related molecular study from the same population adds a further dimension to this evidence base. Umeakuana et al. [14] screened naturally infected dogs presenting at the University of Nigeria Veterinary Teaching Hospital, Nsukka, within the same study zone, and used subspecies-specific PCR targeting the TgsGP and AnTat 11.17 genes to confirm Trypanosoma brucei gambiense, the causative agent of human African trypanosomiasis, in a subset of the dogs examined. This finding is distinct from, and complementary to, the resistance-phenotype data of Obi et al. [11]: whereas the latter establishes that drug pressure in this population coincides with phenotypically resistant parasites, Umeakuana et al. [14] establishes that the canine compartment in this same zone also harbours a parasite of direct human public health relevance. Read together with the present survey's findings, this raises a structural concern that extends beyond resistance management alone: the same conditions we identify, presumptive treatment, weak veterinary oversight, and frequent inter-household dog movement, that plausibly sustain drug pressure on canine trypanosome populations also describe a setting in which a zoonotic parasite could circulate largely unmonitored. We treat this as a hypothesis-generating observation rather than evidence of an active transmission pathway, since neither this survey nor Umeakuana et al. [14] assessed human cases or vector infection rates directly.

The findings of the present survey help address this gap. The high frequency of presumptive treatment, non-weight-based dosing, repeat treatment without parasitological re-evaluation, and widespread procurement of trypanocides through informal drug outlets collectively describe a behavioural and service-delivery environment that is biologically consistent with mechanisms known to favour trypanocide resistance selection [8, 12]. However, because individual treatment histories were not linked to the resistance phenotypes of specific parasite isolates, these findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating a direct causal relationship. Rather, they provide complementary lines of evidence supporting the biological plausibility of a systems-level pathway linking human-mediated drug-use practices with the emergence and persistence of resistant trypanosome populations, and, taken together with the zoonotic finding of Umeakuana et al. [14], they identify the same structural gaps in veterinary oversight as relevant to both drug-resistance selection and zoonotic risk monitoring in this host compartment..

One health implications: canine reservoir dynamics and zoonotic risk

Building on the integrated molecular and epidemiological evidence presented above, the present findings also have important One Health implications. With 57.5% of dog-owning households reporting livestock co-habitation and 44.2% documenting inter-household dog movement, dogs are structurally situated within both the livestock interface and intra-community dissemination networks simultaneously (Fig. 2). When interpreted alongside published evidence of diminazene-resistant and multidrug-resistant Trypanosoma isolates in dogs from the same zone [11], this convergent finding supports the positioning of dogs as potential reservoirs and amplifiers of drug-resistant trypanosomes within shared ecological spaces. Multidrug-resistant strains detected in dogs from Enugu North Senatorial Zone are phenotypically resistant to both diminazene aceturate and isometamidium chloride. In livestock systems, these two compounds are deliberately alternated, a practice known as the ‘sanative pair’ strategy, on the principle that parasites resistant to one drug typically remain susceptible to the other, thereby prolonging the useful life of both drugs [5]. The two compounds constitute this sanative pair. If such strains are maintained and amplified in the canine compartment, where drug pressure is intense and diagnostically unsupervised, and if cross-species transmission occurs via shared Glossina or other vector exposure, the integrity of sanative pair rotation in co-habiting livestock populations may be compromised. A finding of particular public health significance is the molecular identification of Trypanosoma brucei gambiense, the causative agent of West African human African trypanosomiasis (HAT; sleeping sickness), in naturally infected dogs in Nigeria by Umeakuana et al. [14]. Dogs are recognised as plausible reservoir hosts and potential sentinels for T. b. gambiense in Central and West African foci [9], and their proximity to humans in the household and community setting makes them epidemiologically relevant at the animal-human interface. The co-occurrence of a confirmed T. b. gambiense reservoir (dogs) and conditions conducive to tsetse transmission in the study area constitutes a scientifically legitimate signal that warrants structured investigation. Recommended research priorities include: (i) active HAT case-finding surveillance in communities within the study zone,(ii) tsetse fly infection rate surveys targeting palpalis group vectors,(iii) longitudinal monitoring of T. b. gambiense seroprevalence in sentinel dog populations; and (iv) subspecies-specific molecular typing of trypanosome isolates to establish phylogenetic linkage.

Policy and control considerations

Effective mitigation of trypanocide resistance in the canine compartment requires interventions that extend beyond parasite biology to address human behaviour, structural service gaps, and regulatory weaknesses. Community-level education on appropriate trypanocide dosing, the dangers of presumptive repeat treatment, and basic principles of drug rotation represents the most immediately actionable intervention. Periodic subsidized veterinary outreach clinics targeting communities where diagnostic access is most limited represent a second high-feasibility short-term measure. In the medium term, formal recognition of companion animals within regional and national animal-health surveillance frameworks is critical, routine reporting of suspected trypanocide treatment failures and multidrug-resistant infections in dogs should be integrated into existing livestock monitoring systems. Sustainable resistance control in the long term requires substantive reform of veterinary drug regulation in Nigeria, including coordinated action between the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) and Veterinary Council of Nigeria (VCN) to reclassify trypanocides as strictly prescription-only veterinary medicines enforceable at the point of retail sale. Contributions to the literature

This study makes three specific contributions beyond what is available in the existing literature. First, it provides what is, to our knowledge, the first systematically collected, owner-level dataset on trypanocide treatment practices, drug sourcing, and veterinary service access for dogs in a Nigerian setting; comparable owner- or farmer-level surveys have previously been conducted only in livestock systems [4, 6]. Second, it interprets these practice-level survey data alongside previously published molecular evidence of trypanocide-resistant trypanosome populations from the same dog population and geographic zone [10, 11], [14], offering a descriptive, systems-level link between upstream human practices and downstream resistance phenotypes that has not previously been articulated for companion animals in this region. Third, by quantifying the proportion of households relying on unregulated drug sources (76.7%) and lacking access to a licensed veterinarian (61.7%), the study provides baseline figures that can inform the design and prioritization of the community education, outreach, and regulatory interventions outlined above, and against which future interventions in this zone could be evaluated.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that must be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional survey data were derived from a non-probability, convenience-based sample of 120 households and 250 dogs; household- and dog-level proportions should not be interpreted as population-level prevalence or incidence estimates for the zone or Southeastern Nigeria. The convenience sampling approach, while appropriate given the field-epidemiological context in which data were collected, introduces potential selection bias: households that were accessible and whose owners consented to participation may differ systematically from non-participating households in ways that could affect the proportions reported. Second, self-reported information on drug-use practices, particularly presumptive-treatment behaviour, dosing estimation, and drug source, is subject to social desirability bias, recall bias, and measurement imprecision. Direct quantification of administered drug doses (mg/kg) or plasma drug concentrations was not performed; the association between reported dosing practices and sub-therapeutic drug exposure therefore remains inferential. Third, no experimental resistance assays, pharmacokinetic confirmation studies, xenodiagnosis experiments, or vector competence studies were conducted. Relationships between observed human-mediated practices and documented resistance phenotypes are therefore interpreted at the systems level rather than as causal effects. Fourth, the analysis presented here is descriptive; cross-tabulation of practice variables against one another (e.g., drug source against veterinary access) or demographic variables were not performed in this report and may reveal additional structure in the data that future re-analysis of the record-level dataset should explore. Fifth, the temporal scope of the cross-sectional survey limits assessment of seasonal variation in drug-use practices. Sixth, the interpretation offered here draws substantially on the authors' own previously published molecular evidence base, all from the same study area, which limits comparative generalizability. Seventh, regarding the T. b. gambiense finding, the zoonotic implications cannot be quantified within the present evidentiary framework, and any inference of current active zoonotic transmission risk would be premature.

Future research directions

The associations described in this survey are descriptive and systems-level; confirming them, and translating them into evidence-based interventions, will require several lines of follow-up work. Longitudinal cohort studies that track individual dogs and their owners over time would allow seasonal and treatment-history effects on drug-use practices to be assessed, which the present cross-sectional design cannot capture. Paired studies that combine household-level practice surveys with concurrent parasitological and molecular resistance testing of the same dogs are needed to move from systems-level association toward direct evidence of whether specific practices, such as repeat dosing without re-testing, are associated with resistance phenotypes within individual animals. Pharmacokinetic studies of diminazene aceturate and isometamidium chloride dosed without weight-based calculation in dogs, as opposed to cattle, would clarify whether the sub-therapeutic exposure hypothesized here is pharmacologically plausible in this species. Intervention studies evaluating community education, subsidized outreach clinics, or point-of-sale dosing tools at agro-veterinary outlets would test whether the structural drivers identified here are modifiable in practice. Finally, expanded molecular and genomic surveillance of trypanosome populations circulating in dogs, livestock, and vectors within the same households would help to establish whether inter-host transmission of resistant strains, suggested but not demonstrated by the present data, is actually occurring.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional survey advances understanding of trypanocide resistance by documenting, with quantified survey evidence, how human-mediated drug-use practices, treatment without parasitological confirmation (87.5% of treated dogs), estimation-based dosing without weight calculation (83%), and unregulated drug access through informal channels (76.7% of households), plausibly contribute to the conditions favouring resistance selection in canine trypanosome populations in Enugu North Senatorial Zone, Southeastern Nigeria. By integrating these primary survey findings with published molecular evidence of diminazene-resistant and multidrug-resistant Trypanosoma isolates in dogs from the same zone [11], the study offers a convergent, systems-level argument for considering companion animals as epidemiologically relevant actors in regional trypanocide resistance dynamics, while recognizing that this argument rests on association rather than experimentally confirmed causation.

The identification of T. b. gambiense in dogs from the study area [14] introduces an additional dimension that elevates the public health significance of these findings beyond trypanocide resistance management, positioning dogs as potential sentinels for HAT risk in a region where surveillance is limited. At the policy level, the findings support a tiered intervention strategy from immediate community education and outreach diagnostics, through medium-term integration of dogs into national surveillance frameworks, to long-term regulatory reform engaging NAFDAC and VCN to restrict informal trypanocide access. Resistance is not merely a biological phenomenon but an outcome of interacting behavioural, structural, and regulatory dynamics within local health ecologies. This survey contributes a systems-oriented, One Health perspective on that dynamic, with implications for intervention design, surveillance architecture, and regulatory reform that extend beyond Southeastern Nigeria to comparable endemic settings across sub-Saharan Africa.
