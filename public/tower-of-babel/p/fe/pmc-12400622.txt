Hepatoprotective effects of Acanthopanax trifoliatus standardized leaf extract and its formulations in a HepG2 model
Authors: Khanit Sa-ngiamsuntorn, Piyanuch Rojsanga, Pakatip Ruenraroengsak, Montree Jaturanpinyo, Chutima Petchprayoon, Somnuk Bunsupa, Veena Satitpatipan, Yongyut Pewkliang, Piyanoot Thongsri, Pongtip Sithisarn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcanthopanax trifoliatus (L.) Voss or phak-paem, a traditional Thai plant in the Araliaceae family, has been reported to promote adaptogenic effects for a long time. The leaves have been used as a tonic to improve general weakness and to treat tuberculosis, lung hemorrhages, bruises, ulcers and contusions. The young leaves and the shoots are also eaten as vegetables.MethodsA. trifoliatus leaf extract was prepared, and its physical and chemical properties were qualitatively controlled. Three formulations, tablets, drinks and bubbles, were developed from A. trifoliatus standardized leaf extract. The in vitro antioxidant effects of the A. trifoliatus leaf extracts and formulations were evaluated using a DPPH scavenging assay. The hepatoprotective effects of A. trifoliatus leaf extract and formulations were evaluated in the HepG2 cell line using a hepatoprotective assay, the detection of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and the quantification of apoptotic cells and necrotic cells. The mechanism of action for hepatoprotective effects was determined by detecting antioxidant and apoptosis markers using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR).ResultsStandardized extracts of A. trifoliatus leaves were prepared from quality-controlled raw material. The physical properties of the extract were controlled, including loss on drying, total ash, and acid-insoluble ash. It had specific chromatographic fingerprint with total phenolic and total flavonoid contents of 13.44 ± 0.22 g of chlorogenic acid equivalent in 100 g of extract (g% CAE) and 6.14 ± 0.45 g of rutin equivalent in 100 g of extract (g% RE), respectively. According to high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis, the extract contained mono-caffeoylquinic acid 1.33 ± 0.00 g% CAE, di-caffeoylquinic acid, 1.82 ± 0.00 g of a cynarin equivalent in 100 g of extract (g% CYE) and flavonoid 0.48 ± 0.00 g% RE. Tablets, drinks and bubbles were developed from A. trifoliatus standardized leaf extract. A. trifoliatus leaf extract, tablets and drinks showed strong DPPH scavenging effects, with EC50 values of 38.88 ± 1.19, 62.99 ± 1.80 and 81.19 ± 1.18 µg/mL, respectively. Treatment with the A. trifoliatus extract at a concentration of 12.5–100 μg/mL reduced hepatocyte toxicity by 30–40% in a dose-dependent manner, as determined by a tert-butyl hydroperoxide (t-BHP)-induced cytotoxicity assay. The three formulations of A. trifoliatus extracts also showed hepatoprotective effects of approximately 5–25% at concentrations ranging from 12.5–100 μg/mL. The mechanisms of action for hepatoprotective effects were found to include a reduction in intracellular ROS production; the prevention of t-BHP-induced apoptosis; an increase in the expression of antioxidant genes such as catalase, superoxide dismutase-1 (SOD-1) and NADPH quinone dehydrogenase-1 (NQO-1); a reduction in the expression of apoptotic genes, including the proapoptotic genes AIFM-1 and BAK-1 and the apoptotic genes CASP-8 and CASP-3; and the upregulation of the antiapoptotic gene BCL-2.ConclusionStandardized A. trifoliatus leaf extract and its formulations, tablets, drinks and bubbles were prepared and quality controlled. They promoted in vitro antioxidant activities with hepatoprotective effects in HepG2 cell line.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05025-3.

Introduction

Thai traditional practice has been used for a long time to prevent, diagnose, and treat imbalances between human bodies and minds. In traditional Thai medicine, herbs with adaptogenic or ginseng-like effects, such as detoxification, blood purification, jaundice curing, hepatoprotection, tonics and hematotonics, aphrodisiac, antifatigue, nourishment and longevity promotion [1], should be considered. Acanthopanax trifoliatus (L.) Voss, or phak-paem, a traditional Thai plant in the ginseng family (Araliaceae), has been reported to promote adaptogenic effects for a long time. Root bark and stem bark have been used to treat rheumatism, ostealgia, lumbago and impotence [2]. Stem bark promotes antiflatulence effects, has been used for the treatment of emaciation and neurosis [3] and has also been used as a CNS stimulant, memory enhancer and tonic [2]. The leaves of A. trifoliatus have been used as a tonic to improve general weakness and to treat tuberculosis, lung hemorrhages, bruises, ulcers and contusions [4–7]. Moreover, young leaves and shoots are popularly consumed as vegetables in traditional Northern Thai cuisine [8]. Previous studies have reported the biological activities of the extract from the leaves of A. trifoliatus, including antioxidant activities [9–11], anti-inflammatory activity [9], antianxiety effects in animal models [12], anti-cholinesterase activity [1, 8], ameliorative effects on cognitive and emotional deficits in animal models [8] and antidepressant effects in animal models [8]. Phenolic acids and flavonoids were found to be the major constituents of A. trifoliatus leaf extracts. Mono-caffeoylquinic acid, such as chlorogenic acid; di-caffeoylquinic acid, such as 3,5 di-caffeoylquinic acid and 4,5 di-caffeoylquinic acid; and flavonoids, including rutin, isoquercetin and quercitrin, in A. trifoliatus leaf extracts have been identified and quantitatively analyzed by HPLC [13, 14]. Acute toxicity tests in an animal model of A. trifoliatus leaf extract, which contained 16.26 g% chlorogenic acid equivalent (CAE) and 1.31 g% rutin equivalent (RE) of total phenolics and total flavonoids, respectively, showed no signs of toxicity (LD50 > 2 g/kg body weight) [1]. Standardization of herbal medicines has been used to quality control of natural products nowadays. It is the process of prescribing a set of standards or inherent characteristics, constant parameters, definitive qualitative and quantitative values that carry an assurance of quality, efficacy, safety and reproducibility [15]. It is the process of developing and agreeing upon technical standards [15]. The standardized extract can be the extract with the known amounts of active phytochemicals or the extract prepared from the guaranteed manufacturing process for the presence of a certain percentage of marker compounds which are not indicators of therapeutic activity or quality of the herb [15]. Based on previous research on biological activities related to ethnomedicinal uses, this study investigated the hepatoprotective effects of standardized A. trifoliatus leaf extract and its formulations on a human liver cancer cell line (HepG2) model.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Leaves of Acanthopanax trifoliatus were purchased from Chaing Mai Province in 2016 (Voucher No. AC-CNX-S01-2016). The voucher plant specimen was kept at the Department of Pharmacognosy, Faculty of Pharmacy, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand. Plant samples were identified according to their botanical and taxonomical characteristics using the identification key presented in Flora of Thailand [16]. The plant sample was cleaned and dried in a hot-air oven (50 °C) for 6 h and powdered using an electronic mill (20-mesh sieve).

Quality control of A. trifoliatus leaf raw materials

Macroscopic analysis

The macroscopic characteristics of the A. trifoliatus leaf samples were evaluated according to the guidelines described by Evans [17] and Upton et al. [18] for shape, size, taste, fracture, color and odor.

Microscopic analysis

Using previously described methods [18, 19], powdered A. trifoliatus leaf samples were studied under a light microscope (Leica ICC50 W; Leica Camera Inc.; USA). The powder was cleared in chloral hydrate. Then, the cleared powder was stained with aniline sulfate reagent. Then, small amounts of the stained powders were mounted on glass slides with glycerin water and examined under a light microscope. The cells in the plant powder were recorded using a camera connected to a light microscope under the management of the Leica Application Suite v4.12 software (Leica Camera Inc.; USA).

Evaluation of physical properties

The physical characteristics of the A. trifoliatus leaf samples were evaluated using methods described in the 2016 Thai Herbal Pharmacopoeia [20], including loss on drying, total ash, acid-insoluble ash, water-soluble extractive and ethanol-soluble extractive methods.

Loss on drying

A glass-stoppered weighing bottle was dried in a hot-air oven at 105 °C for 30 min, followed by cooling in a desiccator and accurately weighing. Two grams of A. trifoliatus leaf powders were added into the weighing bottle then the stopper was replaced and the bottle containing the plant powders was accurately weighed. The loaded weighing bottle was placed in the hot-air oven with the stopper removed and left to dry in the oven at 105 °C for 2 h; then, it was cooled in a desiccator and accurately weighed. The drying and cooling processes were repeated until the difference between two consecutive weights of the loaded weighing bottle with the stopper was less than 2.5 mg/g plant powder. The percentage loss on drying was calculated.

Total ash

Two grams of A. trifoliatus leaf powders were incinerated in a tared silica dish at a temperature not exceeding 450 °C in a furnace until free from carbon; then the silica dish was cooled in the desiccator and weighed. The percentage of total ash was calculated.

Acid-insoluble ash

Twenty-five milliliters of dilute hydrochloric acid were added into the silica dish obtained from the final step of the total ash evaluation; then, it was boiled in a water bath for 5 min after which, the solution was passed through ashless filter paper and washed with hot water until the filtrate was neutral. The obtained residue and ashless filter paper were transferred to the tared silica dish and incinerated at 500 °C in a furnace for 3 h. The silica dish was cooled in the desiccator and weighed. The percentage of acid-insoluble ash was calculated.

Water-soluble extractive

Five grams of A. trifoliatus leaf powders were macerated with chloroform water (100 mL) in a closed flask for 24 h, shaking frequently during the first 6 h and then allowed to stand for 18 h. The extraction solution was rapidly filtered and 20 mL of filtrate was evaporated in a tared evaporating dish in a water bath. The evaporating dish containing the dried extract was dried at 105 °C in a hot-air oven until a constant weight was obtained. The percentage of water-soluble extractive was calculated.

Ethanol-soluble extractive

Five grams of A. trifoliatus leaf powders were macerated with 95% ethanol (100 mL) in a closed flask for 24 h, shaking frequently during the first 6 h and then allowed to stand for 18 h. The extraction solution was rapidly filtered and 20 mL of filtrate was evaporated in a tared evaporating dish in a water bath. The evaporating dish containing the dried extract was dried at 105 °C in a hot-air oven until a constant weight was obtained. The percentage of ethanol-soluble extractive was calculated.

Preparation of A. trifoliatus leaf extract

A. trifoliatus leaf powders were boiled with distilled water (1:10, w/v) for 3 h and then filtered. The filtrate was dried by spray drying method. The dried extract was kept in the sealed aluminium foil bag in the room temperature until used.

Standardization of A. trifoliatus leaf extract

The processes for standardization of A. trifoliatus leaf extract composed of the quality control of A. trifoliatus leaf raw material which had been previously described, evaluation of the chromatographic fingerprint using thin-layer chromatography, quantitative analysis of phytochemicals including determinations of total phenolic, total flavonoid and phytochemical contents and determination of in vitro antioxidant activity by the DPPH scavenging method.

Evaluation of the chromatographic fingerprint using thin-layer chromatography

A. trifoliatus leaf extract solution (5 mg/mL) was applied as a band with a length of 6.0 mm on precoated silica gel 60 GF254 aluminium sheet using a mixture of ethyl acetate:acetic acid:formic acid:water (100:11:11:26 v/v/v/v) as the mobile phase. The TLC plate was detected under white light, UV 254 nm, UV 366 nm, and the plate was also sprayed with the NP/PEG spray reagent and detected under UV 366 nm. The TLC-visualization of the mount digital camera was carried out using CAMAG manual winCATS software.

Quantitative analysis of phytochemicals

The total phenolic and total flavonoid contents of the A. trifoliatus leaf extracts were quantitatively analyzed using the Folin-Ciocalteu and aluminium chloride methods, respectively. The phenolic and flavonoid contents in the extracts were also quantitatively analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

Determination of total phenolic content using the Folin-Ciocalteu method

Using a previously described method [21], A. trifoliatus leaf extract solution was mixed with Folin-Ciocalteu reagent in a 96-well plate. Distilled water and 20% sodium carbonate solution were added. The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 765 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan, USA). The samples were analyzed in triplicate. The total phenolic content was calculated from the standard curve of chlorogenic acid and was expressed as g of the chlorogenic acid equivalent in 100 g of the extract (g% CAE).

Determination of total flavonoid content using the aluminium chloride method

Using a previously described method [21], the A. trifoliatus leaf extract solution was reacted with the same volume of a 2% aluminium chloride solution. The absorbance was measured at 415 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan, USA). The samples were analyzed in triplicate. The total flavonoid content was calculated with the standard curve of rutin and was expressed as g rutin equivalent in 100 g of the extract (g% RE).

Analysis of phytochemical contents by HPLC

The contents of phenolics and flavonoids in the A. trifoliatus leaf extract were quantitatively analyzed using a validated HPLC method previously reported [13]. Evaluation of the system suitability, including the relative standard deviation (%RSD) of the retention time, %RSD of the peak area, tailing factor, number of theoretical plates (N) and resolution, was conducted. The amounts of mono-caffeoylquinic acid, di-caffeoylquinic acid and flavonoids were quantitatively analyzed using standard curves of chlorogenic acid, cynarin and rutin, respectively. The samples were analyzed in triplicate. The mono-caffeoylquinic acid, di-caffeoylquinic acid and flavonoid contents were calculated and are expressed as g of chlorogenic acid equivalent in 100 g of extract (g% CAE), g of cynarin equivalent in 100 g of extract (g% CYE) and g of rutin equivalent in 100 g of extract (g% RE), respectively.

Determination of in vitro antioxidant activity by the DPPH scavenging method

DPPH solution was mixed with various concentrations of A. trifoliatus leaf extract solutions in the same volume according to methods described in a previous study [10]. The absorbances of the reaction solutions were determined at a wavelength of 517 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan, USA). Each experiment was conducted in triplicate. The EC50 values (µg/mL) of the extracts are expressed as the means ± SDs.

Development of A. trifoliatus leaf extract formulations

A. trifoliatus leaf extract tablet

In our previous study, the active dose for antidepressant, anti-anxiety and memory and cognitive enhancement effects in an animal model of A. trifoliatus leaf extract was 500 mg/kg body weight [8, 11]. The human equivalent dose (HED) for 60 kg people was calculated to be 250 mg/day. Therefore, A. trifoliatus leaf extract tablets were formulated to contain 250 mg of A. trifoliatus leaf extract/tablet. Using the wet granulation method, tablets containing A. trifoliatus leaf extract and other components, such as disintegrating agents (croscarmellose sodium, sodium starch glycolate, starch or crospovidone), binders (polyvinylpyrrolidone, starch, hydroxypropyl methyl cellulose, gelatin or methyl cellulose), lubricants (magnesium stearate, stearic acid, sodium stearyl fumarate or polyethylene glycol) and diluents (microcrystalline cellulose, lactose, starch, dicalcium phosphate, sorbitol or mannitol), were developed. The physical properties of the obtained tablets, including weight variation, thickness, disintegration, friability and hardness, were evaluated [22].

Weight variation

Twenty of A. trifoliatus leaf extract tablets were individually weighed. The average and standard deviation of the weight were then calculated. According to USP 40-dietary supplement, the weight variation was accepted when less than 2 tablets had the weights differ to the average weight more than 5% and no tablet with the weight 2 times higher than 10% compared to the average weight [22].

Thickness

Ten A. trifoliatus leaf extract tablets were evaluated for the thickness by using automated tablet tester (EasyCheck, Erweka®, Geramany). The average and standard deviation of the thickness (mm) were calculated.

Disintegration

The disintegration time of A. trifoliatus leaf extract tablets were evaluated using disintegration apparatus. Six A. trifoliatus leaf extract tablets were randomly sampled for evaluation of disintegration time using water as the media and temperature of 37 ± 2 °C. The disintegration of the tablet was accepted when all the tablets were completely disintegrated within 30 min.

Friability

The friability of A. trifoliatus leaf extract tablets was determined in accordance with USP 40 guidelines by using Roche friabilator machine. The friability of the tablet was accepted when it was less than 1% and no broken tablet was found.

Hardness

The hardness of A. trifoliatus leaf extract tablets were evaluated using automated tablet tester (EasyCheck, Erweka®, Geramany). Ten A. trifoliatus leaf extract tablets were evaluated for the hardness. The average and standard deviation of the hardness (kp) were calculated. The hardness of A. trifoliatus leaf extract tablet was adjusted to be 7–12 kp.

A. trifoliatus leaf extract drink

The drink was formulated with 100 mg/100 mL A. trifoliatus leaf extract in distilled water. The physical properties, including color and appearance, were evaluated.

A. trifoliatus leaf extract bubble

A. trifoliatus leaf extract bubbles were formulated with a concentration of 1.6 mg/pill. The physical properties, including appearance, hardness, bounciness, stickiness and crispiness, were evaluated. The hepatoprotective effects of the A. trifoliatus leaf extract, A. trifoliatus leaf extract tablet, A. trifoliatus leaf extract drink and A. trifoliatus leaf extract bubble were determined using a tert-butyl hydroperoxide (t-BHP) assay, and the effect on reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels was determined using 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA). A. trifoliatus leaf extracts were also studied for their apoptotic and necrotic effects and effects on antioxidant and apoptotic gene expression in the t-BHP-treated HepG2 cell line.

Evaluation of hepatoprotective effects

Cell culture

HepG2 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). HepG2 cells were cultured in 1:1 DMEM/F12 media (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (HyClone), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin (HyClone), and 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco). The cells were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere. Cells were subcultured every 3–5 days or whenever they reached 80% confluence, with 0.125% trypsin–EDTA (Gibco).

Cell viability assay

HepG2 cells were seeded onto 96-well plates at 5 × 104 cells/well and treated with 0–100 μg/mL A. trifoliatus extract, 0–100 μg/mL lecithin, 0–500 μg/mL silymarin (Sigma) and 0–2 mM t-BHP (Sigma) diluted with Williams’ E (Sigma) supplemented with 10% FBS (HyClone), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (HyClone), and 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco) for 24 h. Cell viability was examined by an MTT colorimetric assay. MTT [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] (Sigma‒Aldrich) was added to the medium, and the cells were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The medium was removed, and the formazan crystals were then dissolved in DMSO (Sigma‒Aldrich). The formazan concentration was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm by a Tecan Infinite®200 PRO microplate reader (TECAN).

Hepatoprotective assay

A hepatoprotective assay was performed following a previous report [23]. HepG2 cells were seeded onto 96-well plates at 5 × 104 cells/well and incubated with 0–100 μg/mL A. trifoliatus extract and its formulated products, tablets, drinks and bubbles, and 0–100 μg/mL lecithin diluted with basal Williams’E media (without FBS) at 100 μL/well for 2 h. The 50 μg/mL sylimarin served as the positive control. After 2 h of incubation, the media was removed, and the cells were incubated with 0.064 mM (CC50) t-BHP in basal Williams’E media at 100 μL/well for 2 h. Cell viability was determined using PrestoBlue™ (Invitrogen). The fluorescent signal was measured by a CLARIOstar multimode plate reader (BMG LABTECH) using excitation at 535 nm and emission at 595 nm. Detection of intracellular ROS levels

The detection of intracellular ROS was performed as described in a previous study [24]. HepG2 cells were seeded onto 6-well plates at 1 × 106 cells/well and incubated with 50 μg/mL A. trifoliatus extract and its formulated products, namely, tablets, drinks and bubbles, lecithin or silymarin, in basal Williams’E media for 18 h. After treatment, the media was replaced with basal media containing 5 mM t-BHP for 5 min. Next, the media containing t-BHP was discarded, and the cells were incubated with 50 μM H2DCFDA (Invitrogen) for 30 min. The cells were subsequently washed twice with PBS and harvested by trypsinization. The fluorescent product (2',7'-dichlorofluorescein (DCF)) was detected by a flow cytometer (FACSVerse, BD Biosciences) with an excitation wavelength (λEx) of 480 nm and an emission wavelength (λEm) of 526 nm. The results are presented as the percentage of ROS inhibition versus that of the untreated control.

Quantification of apoptotic cells and necrotic cells

HepG2 cells were seeded onto 6-well plates at 1 × 106 cells/well and treated with 50 μg/mL A. trifoliatus extract and its formulated products, namely, tablets, drinks and bubbles, lecithin or silymarin in complete Williams’ E medium for 24 h. After treatment, the media was discarded, and the media was replaced with complete media containing 128 μM t-BHP for 24 h. Next, the cell pellets were collected by trypsinization and washed with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were stained with Annexin V-conjugated FITC and propidium iodide (PI) (Dojindo). The percentages of apoptotic and necrotic cells were detected by flow cytometry (FACSVerse, BD Biosciences).

Detection of antioxidant and apoptosis markers using real-time qPCR

HepG2 cells were seeded onto 6-well plates at 1 × 106 cells/well and treated with 50 μg/mL A. trifoliatus extract or 50 μg/mL silymarin in complete Williams’ E media for 72 h. After treatment, the media was replaced with complete media containing 12.5 μM t-BHP for 24 h. Next, the cell pellets were collected by trypsinization. Total mRNA was then extracted with an Illustra RNAspin Mini RNA isolation kit (GE Healthcare, Asia Pacific). Total mRNA was converted to cDNA using the ImProm-II™ Reverse Transcription System (Promega). The primers used to quantify mRNA expression included antioxidant and apoptotic markers. All primer sequences and conditions are listed in Table 1. qPCR was performed with KAPA SYBR® FAST qPCR Kits (Kapa Biosystems) at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 20 s, using a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Each sample was measured in triplicate. PCR amplicons were confirmed by melting curve analysis. The expression levels were calculated as the fold change relative to the control using the 2−ΔΔCt method and were normalized to the expression of GAPDH. Statistical analysis

All the experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data are expressed as the means ± SDs. qPCR data were analyzed for differences using one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s test for multiple comparisons between the treatments and their respective controls. A p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results and discussions

Quality control of A. trifoliatus leaf raw materials

Macroscopic analysis

A. trifoliatus was described in the Flora of China in 2004 as a shrub, scandent or climber that usually reaches a height of 7 m. The branches are scattered with recurved prickles. The inflorescence is a terminal raceme, umbel or compound umbel. The fruit is globose and laterally compressed with a size of 3–4 mm. [16, 25]. In this study, the young leaves of A. trifoliatus were used and described as compound leaves with 3 or 5 papery leaflets and a green color. The leaflets are ovate, elliptic-ovate, or oblong, base cuneate, margin serrulate, apex acute or acuminate. The observed botanical characteristics of A. trifoliatus leaves corresponded to the information reported in the Flora of Thailand in 2019 [16]. Standardization of A. trifoliatus leaf extract

Evaluation of the chromatographic fingerprint using thin-layer chromatography

The A. trifoliatus leaf extract showed specific TLC fingerprints. There were chromatographic bands that positively appeared as dark quenching bands on the green fluorescence background under UV 254 nm, and some appeared as dark and fluorescence bands under UV 366 nm, suggesting the presence of compounds with chromophores and conjugated double bonds. After detection with the NP/PEG spraying reagent and observation under UV 366 nm, some chromatographic bands appeared as bright blue and orange fluorescence bands, suggesting the presence of phenolics and flavonoids. Compared with the reference compounds, the leaf extract of A. trifoliatus exhibited chromatographic bands corresponding to cryptochlorogenic acid (4-caffeoylquinic acid) and chlorogenic acid (3-caffeoylquinic acid) at Rf = 0.50, neochlorogenic acid (5-caffeoylquinic acid) at Rf = 0.52, quercitrin (quercetin-3-rhamnoside) at Rf = 0.67, isochlorogenic acid C (4,5-di-caffeoylquinic acid) at Rf = 0.76 and isochlorogenic acid A (3,5-di-caffeoylquinic acid) at Rf = 0.90 (Fig. 3). The chromatographic profile of the A. trifoliatus leaf extract corresponded to previous reports, which also suggested that there were 3 main groups of phytochemicals in this extract, including mono-caffeoylquinic acids such as chlorogenic acid; di-caffeoylquinic acids such as isochlorogenic acid C and isochlorogenic acid A; and flavonoids such as quercitrin and rutin [1, 10, 11]. There are reports about free radical scavenging activities, inhibitory effects on lipid peroxidation of phenolics and flavonoids [11, 26] and metal chelating effects [27]. Moreover, di-caffeoylquinic acids showed cytoprotective effects [28], as did chlorogenic acid and its isomers, which showed antioxidant and DNA-protective effects [29]. Determination of the in vitro antioxidant activity of A. trifoliatus leaf extracts via the DPPH scavenging method

Using the DPPH scavenging method, the EC50 value of the A. trifoliatus leaf extract was 38.88 ± 1.19 µg/mL, while the EC50 value of ascorbic acid in the same experiment was 4.70 ± 0.10 µg/mL. The in vitro antioxidant activities of A. trifoliatus leaf extracts were previously reported. The EC50 values of the leaf extracts prepared by different extraction methods varied from 14 to 100 µg/mL; decoction provided the extract with the strongest DPPH scavenging effect [10], while another report suggested that the EC50 value of the leaf extract from this plant was approximately 24 µg/mL [11]. Another study suggested that the EC50 value of A. trifoliatus leaf extract obtained from a DPPH scavenging assay should be 30–55 µg/mL [14]. The extract showed in vitro antioxidant effects when tested by other methods, including the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) method [11, 14], and showed in vivo anti-inflammatory effects in a carrageenan-induced rat paw edema model [10]. From TLC and HPLC analysis, some phenolics and flavonoids were found in the A. trifoliatus leaf extract. These compounds have been previously reported about their antioxidant activities. Chlorogenic acid, cynarin, rutin, quercitrin, isoquercetin have been reported to promote antioxidant activities [14, 31, 32]. Moreover, there was a report indicated the high relations of total phenolic and total flavonoid contents with DPPH scavenging activity of A. trifoliatus leaf extract [14].

Evaluation of hepatoprotective effects

Cytotoxicity of the A. trifoliatus extract

The cytotoxicity of the herbal extract and related compounds was determined by MTT assay in HepG2 cells after 24 h of treatment. The A. trifoliatus extract and lecithin had no cytotoxic effects on HepG2 cells, with a 50% cytotoxic concentration (CC50) > 100 μg/mL (Fig. 6A, 6B, respectively). Silymarin is a flavonolignan that has been reported to be an effective hepatoprotective agent [35]. The CC50 value of silymarin in HepG2 cells was 174.9 μg/mL (Fig. 6C), which was equivalent to that in a previous report (CC50 > 150 μg/mL) [36]. t-BHP is an organic peroxide that is widely used for oxidation processes. The cytotoxicity profile of t-BHP in HepG2 cells was evaluated, with a CC50 value of 0.064 mM (Fig. 6D). t-BHP induces cytotoxicity by forming covalent bonds with biomolecules, causing leakage of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and generating malondialdehyde (MDA) in hepatocytes [37]. Therefore, t-BHP can induce DNA damage and reduce cellular glutathione levels [38]. These mechanisms recapitulated the oxidative stress conditions that arise in cells and organs. A. trifoliatus extract has hepatoprotective effects on t-BHP-induced cytotoxicity

This study aimed to compare the hepatoprotective effects of A. trifoliatus extract and 50 μg/mL sylimarin on t-BHP-induced hepatocyte injury. HepG2 cells were preincubated with 0–100 μg/mL lecithin or A. trifoliatus extract for 2 h and then treated with 0.064 mM (CC50) t-BHP for 2 h. The results revealed that 50 μg/mL sylimarin reduced t-BHP-induced cytotoxicity by 80–90%. Lecithin and A. trifoliatus extract reduced hepatocyte toxicity by 30–40% in a dose range of 25–100 μg/mL (Fig. 7A) and 12.5–100 μg/mL (Fig. 7B), respectively, in a dose-dependent manner. Sylimarin is a plant-derived product from Silybum marianum (L.) GAERT. Silymarin, which is composed of polyphenolic flavonoids from S. marianum, contains flavonolignans, silibinins (silybins), silychristin and silydianin. Silybins are 70–80% abundant bioactive compounds in silymarin that have hepatoprotective and antioxidant properties [39]. A silymarin concentration of 10–50 μg/mL was employed as a control and standardized herbal extract for hepatoprotective and antioxidant effects in t-BHP-induced HepG2 cells [38]. Lecithin, an essential phospholipid, is found in various tissues, such as the brain, blood, and nerves. A previous study revealed that 50 and 100 mg/kg body weight lecithin can mitigate D-galactosamine-induced hepatotoxicity through the mitochondrial pathway involving BCL-2 and Bax in Wistar albino rats [40]. Furthermore, lecithin, a constituent of essential phospholipids within the phytocomposition, has been reported to prevent the development of cytolytic-cholestatic liver damage caused by carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) [41]. The three formulated A. trifoliatus products, tablet (C), bubble (D), and drink (E), showed hepatoprotective effects of approximately 5–25% in the dose range of 12.5–100 μg/mL (Fig. 7C, 7D and 7E, respectively). The cytotoxicity data indicated that t-BHP-induced cytotoxicity was inhibited by A. trifoliatus extract in a dose-dependent manner. In this study, we first reported that A. trifoliatus extract and its three formulated products alleviated hepatocyte toxicity in t-BHP-induced HepG2 cells. The chromatographic profiles confirmed three main groups of compounds in this A. trifoliatus extract, including chlorogenic acid, isochrogenic acid A and flavonoids. Several studies have reported the hepatoprotective effects of chlorogenic acid against chemical-induced hepatotoxicity by lipopolysaccharide (LPS), aflatoxin B, and CCl4 based on in vitro and animal models [42–44]. Isochlorogenic acid was extracted from Laggera alata (D. Don) Sch. Bip. ex Oliv. has demonstrated hepatoprotective and anti-HBV properties [45]. A. trifoliatus extract and its formulated products reduced intracellular ROS production in response to t-BHP treatment

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are naturally produced in organisms as byproducts of normal cellular metabolism or are induced by environmental factors such as substances, pollution, and toxins. ROS play essential roles in various physiological processes. However, an imbalance between ROS production and neutralization can lead to oxidative stress, causing damage to cellular components such as proteins, lipids, and DNA and contributing to various diseases. To clarify the potential mechanism by which A. trifoliatus extract reduces t-BHP-induced hepatotoxicity, ROS accumulation was induced by t-BHP in HepG2 cells, and the redox-sensitive fluorescent dye H2DCFDA was used to stain intracellular ROS. Treatment of HepG2 cells with 5 mM t-BHP resulted in 100% ROS-positive cells (Fig. 8A). The percentage of ROS-positive cells largely decreased to 30.50% and 56.24% after prophylactic treatment with silymarin (Fig. 8B) and lecithin (Fig. 8C), respectively. Prophylactic treatment with A. trifoliatus and its products diminished the number of ROS-positive cells after challenge with t-BHP. The A. trifoliatus extract reduced the percentage of ROS-positive cells to 53.30% (Fig. 8D), and the tablet, bubble and drink formulations decreased the percentage of ROS-positive cells to 51.60% (Fig. 8E), 85.55% (Fig. 8F) and 75.16% (Fig. 8G) as representative data, respectively. The average percentages of intracellular ROS reduction for silymarin, lecithin, A. trifoliatus, and three formulated products from A. trifoliatus extract were determined (Fig. 8H). The reduction of ROS by A. trifoliatus was consistent with the DPPH scavenging activity of the extract and formulated tablet and drink, which had EC50 values of 38.88 ± 1.19, 62.99 ± 1.8, and 81.19 ± 1.18 µg/mL, respectively. The reduction in the number of ROS-positive cells depends on the quantity of bioactive compounds in the A. trifoliatus extract and its products. Interestingly, a previous study reported that at least three major compounds found in A. trifoliatus extracts, such as cryptochlorogenic acid and chlorogenic acid, strongly inhibited intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [48, 49]. A. trifoliatus extract increased antioxidant gene expression in t-BHP-treated HepG2 cells

The effects of A. trifoliatus extract on cellular redox and apoptosis markers were investigated by measuring the expression of genes associated with antioxidant activities, such as Nrf2, catalase, SOD-1, GPx, NQO-1, HO-1, GSTP-1, GCLC, and GCLM. Real-time qPCR was used to evaluate the mRNA levels in HepG2 cells incubated with A. trifoliatus extract or silymarin and subsequently treated with t-BHP. Nrf2 was downregulated in the treated group compared with the control group (Fig. 9A). The upregulation of catalase, SOD-1 and NQO-1 was observed in HepG2 cells after treatment with A. trifoliatus extract (Fig. 8B-8D). Compared with silymarin, prophylactic treatment of HepG2 cells with A. trifoliatus extract increased catalase expression more strongly (Fig. 9B). However, compared with the other treatments, silymarin increased SOD-1 and NQO-1 expression more strongly (Fig. 9C-9D). HO-1 was not significantly changed by the treatment (Fig. 9E). In contrast, the levels of glutathione-related enzymes, including GPx, GSTP-1 and GCLM, were decreased (Fig. 9F-9H), while the level of GCLC was increased compared with that in the control group (Fig. 9I). The antioxidant activity of the A. trifoliatus extract was a consequence of increasing catalase, SOD-1, and NQO-1 gene expression, similar to the effects of silymarin. SOD changes superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide, and then, catalase converts hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen [51], while NQO-1 plays an important role in the reduction of free radicals in cells and the detoxification of xenobiotics [52]. In a previous study, cryptochlorogenic acid, a phenolic compound found in various plants, was shown to induce the activity of a broad spectrum of antioxidant enzymes [53]. In addition, the leaf extract of Moringa oleifera, which contains a predominant component, cryptochlorogenic acid, increased catalase and SOD-1 mRNA expression in HEK293 cells treated with H2O2. Furthermore, stem and leaf extracts of Polygonatum zanlanscianense Pamp., containing chlorogenic acid, increased SOD, catalase, and NQO-1 expression in t-BHP-induced HepG2 cells [54]. In addition, we investigated the effect of A. trifoliatus extract on matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which are involved in the breakdown of the extracellular matrix. The results demonstrated that MMP-1 expression was slightly reduced by t-BHP treatment but was mainly inhibited by A. trifoliatus extract and silymarin treatment compared with that in the control (Fig. 9J). MMPs play essential roles in the invasion and metastasis of cancer cells, including liver cancer cells [55]. Plant polyphenols, especially chlorogenic acid, have been reported to have anti-invasive and anti-metastatic activities [56]. Therefore, A. trifoliatus extract, which contains chlorogenic acid, strongly reduced MMP-1 expression. For example, chlorogenic acid, the major component of coffee, has been shown to have anticancer effects, especially by showing an inverse association with liver cancer. The underlying mechanism by which chlorogenic acid inhibits DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) in human hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has been investigated [57].

A. trifoliatus extract prevented t-BHP-induced apoptosis in HepG2 cells

Increasing ROS production in response to t-BHP treatment induced cell apoptosis, which eventually caused cell death. The detection of annexin V/PI by flow cytometry was performed to evaluate apoptosis events (Fig. 10A-10B). HepG2 cells were prophylactically treated with silymarin, lecithin or 50 μg/mL A. trifoliatus extract for 24 h and then treated with 128 μM t-BHP for an additional 24 h. Cell pellets were collected, stained with Annexin V/PI for 15 min, and detected by flow cytometry. The results are reported as the percentage of positively stained cells relative to total cells. Compared with the control, the 50 μg/mL A. trifoliatus extract did not alter cell apoptosis (Fig. 10 C). However, treatment of HepG2 cells with 128 μM t-BHP strongly increased apoptosis by 40% in the early stage of the cell population (Fig. 10D). Prophylactic treatment of HepG2 cells with silymarin, lecithin, or A. trifoliatus extract for 24 h prior to t-BHP treatment decreased the percentage of early apoptotic cells to 9%, 16% and 14%, respectively (Fig. 10E-10G, 10 K). All the formulated A. trifoliatus products reduced the percentage of early apoptotic cells from 40% to 11% (tablet), 18% (bubble), and 17% (drink) (Fig. 10H-10J, 10 K). These data indicated that prophylactic treatment with the compounds reduced early and late apoptosis compared to t-BHP treatment (Fig. 10K-10M). The results indicated that A. trifoliatus extract efficiently reduced t-BHP-induced apoptosis in HepG2 cells.

Conclusion

Decoction extracts from the leaves of phak-paem (A. trifoliatus) were prepared and controlled for physical, chemical and biological qualities, including antioxidant and hepatoprotective effects. Phak-paem tablets, phak-paem drinks and phak-paem bubbles were prepared from phak-paem standardized extracts. The developed products showed good physical characteristics. Phak-paem extract, tablets, and drinks showed strong DPPH scavenging effects and promoted moderate hepatoprotective effects on hepatic cells treated with the free radical-generated substance t-BHP. The investigation into the mechanism of the hepatoprotective effect showed that a 50 µg/mL concentration of phak-paem extract increased the expression of antioxidant genes, including catalase, superoxide dismutase-1 (SOD-1), and NADPH quinone dehydrogenase-1 (NQO-1). It also decreased the expression of apoptotic genes such as AIFM-1 and BAK-1, as well as CASP-8 and CASP-3. Additionally, it led to the upregulation of the anti-apoptotic gene BCL-2.
