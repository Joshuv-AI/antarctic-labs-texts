Mitochondrial membrane potential and compartmentalized signaling: Calcium, ROS, and beyond
Authors: Nada Ahmed Selim, Andrew P Wojtovich
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondria are central to cellular function, acting as metabolic hubs that regulate energy transduction to communicate cellular status. A key component of this energetic regulation is the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), a charge separation across the inner mitochondrial membrane generated by the electron transport chain. Beyond MMP's canonical role in driving ATP synthesis, MMP acts as a dynamic signaling hub. MMP rapidly adjusts to acute changes in cellular energy demand and undergoes sustained modifications during developmental processes, such as neuronal remodeling. Changes in MMP influence reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, calcium handling, and mitochondrial quality control, enabling localized and time-sensitive regulation of cellular function. In neurons, changes in MMP coordinate synaptic plasticity by linking metabolic state to structural changes at synapses. This review highlights the non-canonical roles of MMP in signal integration, spatial organization, and stress adaptation, providing a broader framework for understanding mitochondrial contributions to health and disease.Keywords: Mitochondria, Mitophagy, Metabolic specialization, Neuron plasticity, Bioenergetics

Mitochondrial membrane potential generation and dissipation

Cells require a constant supply of energy to function, and many energetic demands are largely fulfilled by mitochondrial ATP production. The production of ATP depends on an electrochemical potential gradient known as protonmotive force (PMF) [1,2]. The PMF serves as an intermediary, converting energy from food into useable cellular energy. PMF is generated by proton pumping across the mitochondrial inner membrane (IM) by electron transport chain (ETC) complexes I, III, and IV (Fig. 1). During this process, the ETC oxidizes metabolites, transfers electrons through a series of reduction-oxidation reactions ultimately to molecular oxygen, and pumps protons from the mitochondrial matrix to the intermembrane space establishing PMF [3]. The PMF consists of two components: an electrical gradient, known as mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and a chemical gradient (ΔpH). These components are connected, with the MMP arises from pumping positively charged protons across the membrane creating an electrical charge difference, while the ΔpH reflects differences in proton concentrations, which create a chemical gradient (Fig. 1) [4]. Under physiological conditions, cells maintain a modest pH gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane. Typically, the pH diffference (ΔpH) is approximately 0.4 units, with the cytosolic pH around 7.4 and the mitochondrial matrix pH around 7.8. This corresponds to roughly a 2.5-fold difference in proton concentration [5]. In comparison, the MMP is generally around −180 mV, which generates a larger driving force, equivalent to a 1000-fold difference in proton concentration across the membrane [6]. Thus, ΔpH contributes only around a quarter of the total PMF under physiological conditions. For this reason, MMP serves as the primary contributor to the PMF in most biological conditions and generally mirrors changes in PMF. Due to the ease with which commonly available potentiometric dyes that allow measurement of the MMP, this biophysical characteristic is most referenced in the literature. In this review, we treat changes in MMP as indicative of PMF shifts, while appreciating the nuanced distinction between them.

MMP typically powers ATP synthesis, but mitochondria can also release this potential as heat through a process called mitochondrial uncoupling [7,8]. Uncoupling proteins (UCPs) are a family of proteins that act as a safety mechanism to dissipate MMP to prevent dielectric breakdown of the IM [9]. The controlled release of energy helps maintain a balance in energy transduction, preventing excessive buildup of MMP that could lead to energetic failure [10]. Investigating single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and genetic variants in UCPs can reveal how MMP generation and dissipation impact downstream physiological processes. UCP3 polymorphisms have been linked to obesity [11]. In contrast, variants in other UCPs, such as UCP2, have been linked to conditions beyond metabolism, including neurodegenerative disease and ischemic injury [12,13]. UCP4 has also been implicated in neurological disorders; for example, an intronic variant is associated with increased risk of late-onset Alzheimer's disease and frontotemporal dementia [14]. Similarly, mutations in genes that alter mitochondrial uncoupling are tied to diseases like frontotemporal dementia and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [15]. Examining SNPs that directly influence MMP and its dissipation reveals that MMP extends beyond energy and metabolism alone. Work on UCPs helped advance MMP to the forefront by showing that small, regulated MMP changes can reprogram output and downstream signaling, not just alter energy and heat production. Thus, in this review, we will explore the non-energetic functions of MMP, challenging the oversimplified view that MMP acts as a battery for ATP synthase. We aim to highlight MMP structural properties and its broader physiological roles such as metabolic specialization and critical neuronal adaptations, focusing on how its dynamic properties influence diverse cell functions through second messengers such as calcium and reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Fig. 2).

MMP in cellular physiology

MMP as a driver of mitochondrial quality control and metabolic specialization

Mitochondria are dynamic organelles that can function as independent bioelectric units or integrate into an interconnected network (Fig. 3). To maintain network integrity and cellular health, mitochondria undergo regulated processes such as mitophagy and intracellular trafficking [16]. Mitophagy is essential for removing damaged mitochondria, while intracellular trafficking facilitates the distribution and positioning of mitochondria within cells, ensuring they adapt effectively to changing cellular conditions [17,18].

Dysfunctional mitochondrial components are electrically isolated from the mitochondrial network, driven primarily by a loss of MMP. Reduced MMP leads to the accumulation of PINK1, which in turn recruits Parkin and LC3, marking mitochondria for degradation via mitophagy [19,20]. In this way, a decrease in MMP acts as a clear, direct signal to eliminate damaged mitochondria (Fig. 2).

Mitochondrial fission and fusion dynamically remodel the network to segregate dysfunctional regions and maintain organelle quality [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. Fusion refers to the merging of two separate components into one, while fission involves the division of a single component into two. Together, fusion and fission regulate the size, shape, and function of mitochondria, allowing them to adapt and respond to changing conditions within the cell. At first glance, observing mitochondrial fission may appear stochastic, as fission serves two critical roles: generating new mitochondria and eliminating dysfunctional mitochondria. However, recent studies have identified patterned fission events during the mitochondrial life cycle that help determine mitochondrial fate [25]. MMP of each daughter mitochondrion plays role in this decision. Fragments that retain higher MMP relative to baseline often re-fuse with the network or support its expansion, while those with lower MMP are more likely to be targeted for degradation via mitophagy [26]. The binary outcome implies the existence of MMP threshold that directs mitochondria toward either biogenesis or clearance. However, the mechanisms by which proteins sense changes in MMP, isolate mitochondrial regions depending on their MMP and determine the appropriate signaling pathway require further investigation. One possibility lies in the regulation of mitochondrial protein import.

Most mitochondrial proteins are made in the cytosol and must be transported into the mitochondrion [6]. This import process depends on MMP. Proteins carrying a positively charged targeting signal at their N-terminus are pulled across the inner membrane into the mitochondrial matrix by an electrical driving force provided by MMP [27]. Similarly, proteins destined for the inner mitochondrial membrane rely on MMP to be correctly inserted into place [28,29].

Although it remains unclear whether protein import machinery directly determines mitochondrial fate, protein import dependence on MMP may reflect local differences in mitochondrial function and composition [29,30]. Regional variation in MMP could influence how mitochondrial fragments are sorted, either returning to the network or being marked for degradation. Whether the import machinery plays an active role in sensing and responding to changes in MMP remains an open question.

In addition to structural adaptations, mitochondria also specialize metabolically to meet diverse cellular needs. One way mitochondria achieve this specialization is through the formation of distinct mitochondrial subpopulations, each dedicated to specific metabolic roles. Classic work in cardiac muscle revealed two spatially and functionally distinct groups. Subsarcolemmal mitochondria are positioned beneath the plasma membrane, while interfibrillar mitochondria are located between myofibrils [31]. These mitochondrial populations differ in respiratory capacity, protein composition, and sensitivity to metabolic stress, demonstrating that even within a single cell type, mitochondria can diversify to serve specialized roles [32]. We do not infer constitutive differences in MMP between the two mitochondrial populations; rather, local workload and substrate supply can produce transient differences in mitochondrial energetics, contributing to MMP distinctions [9,33].

This concept of metabolic compartmentalization extends beyond structural positioning. In addition to spatial separation, mitochondria also differ in their biochemical output, engaging in metabolic programs. The metabolic reactions occurring within mitochondria can be broadly categorized into two primary types: oxidative and reductive [34,35]. Oxidative reactions involve loss of electrons exemplified by the electron transport chain transferring electrons to oxygen and generating the MMP used by oxidatice phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to produce ATP [36]. Conversely, reductive reactions entail the addition of electrons to various acceptor molecules, often dedicated to synthesizing molecular precursors necessary for cellular replication [37].

While oxidative and reductive pathways have been individually studied, their coordination within mitochondria remains unclear. Recent work suggests that this coordination arises from the selective partitioning of metabolic enzymes into distinct mitochondrial subpopulations [38]. Partitioning into ATP-producing and substrate-producing mitochondria is influenced by changes in MMP which shape the activity of specific metabolic enzymes. For example, pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthase (P5CS) catalyzes the first step of proline biosynthesis and acts as a regulatory hub for mitochondrial metabolic switching between oxidative and reductive metabolic pathways [39]. P5CS activity is enhanced under elevated MMP relative to baseline, promoting the formation of filamentous assemblies of P5CS that drive reductive biosynthesis [38]. Whereas reduced MMP inhibits this filamentation and limits substrate production. In low MMP conditions, mitochondria may shift toward maintaining core energetic functions such as OXPHOS and ATP generation. The dynamic interplay facilitates the emergence of specialized mitochondrial subpopulations, each tailored to specific metabolic demands.

Nonetheless, it is still uncertain whether the establishment of these distinct mitochondrial populations is regulated by intrinsic mitochondrial signals or is directed by cellular processes. Considering that metabolically distinct mitochondria compete for similar nutrients, the mechanisms governing the allocation of cellular resources in this context remain insufficiently understood. Further research is needed to determine whether MMP sits at the top of the signaling chain, controlling P5CS activity and nutrient allocation, or if P5CS regulates MMP in return. Dynamic partitioning of mitochondria enhances our comprehension of MMP, highlighting its function as a regulator of metabolic specialization. Metabolic specialization holds particular importance in pathological conditions such as cancer, where augmented substrate production is vital for supporting rapid cellular proliferation, thereby presenting novel opportunities for targeted therapeutic strategies [40].

MMP-mediated mitochondrial signaling

Mitochondria are now recognized as regulators of signal transduction, integrating various cellular cues and shaping physiological responses [41,42]. Communication of mitochondrial functional status to the cell is partly achieved though reactive oxygen species (ROS) release and calcium (Ca2+) signaling, both intimately linked to MMP [43]. Beyond ROS and Ca2+, accumulating evidence demonstrates that MMP directly influences mitochondrial signaling through additional mechanisms, including protein localization, activation, and mitochondrial protein import [[44], [45], [46]]. These important mechanisms have been reviewed extensively elsewhere [47,48]. In this review, we briefly focus on Ca2+ and ROS signaling as foundational examples before transitioning to the emerging, nuanced roles of MMP in cell signaling.

ROS production is closely linked to MMP. Elevated MMP typically slows electron flux through the ETC, enhancing ROS formation due to increased resistance against proton pumping [49]. Conversely, during ATP demand or in the presence of protonophores, MMP decreases, leading to an increase in electron flux through the ETC to maintain MMP, which subsequently lowers ROS formation [50]. While this provides a cursory view, it highlights how MMP and ROS are intricately interconnected. The influence of MMP on ROS production varies depending on when, where, and for how long MMP is altered, suggesting that the relationship is complex and context-dependent [51,52].

Historically, ROS has long been postulated to mediate cellular damage [53]. In this line, damage is primarily driven by ROS generated in mitochondria as a by-product of oxidative metabolism. One challenge in the field is accurately defining how mitochondrial ROS contribute to damage, as ROS surges have been often used as a proxy for observed damage in pathology or aging. Correlative relationships have been drawn due to limitations in tools capable of establishing direct cause-and-effect. Correlative models also tend to ignore UCPs, which act as a physiological rheostat. Regulated proton leak by UCPs tempers MMP, stabilizes electron flow, and limits ROS production [9]. At low, regulated levels, mitochondrial ROS function as signals rather than damage [54]. In this range, ROS can transiently modify redox-sensitive targets and adjust metabolic and stress responses [55]. UCP-tuned proton leak helps maintain this signaling window, preventing bursts while preserving a tonic ROS signal.

Recently developed methods have allowed more precise observation and control over mitochondria ROS in their natural state in living cells or in whole organisms [51,[56], [57], [58]]. These technological advances have highlighted the limitations of earlier models, which could not fully explain the complexities of ROS behavior. Once formed, ROS can move between mitochondrial compartments and into the cytosol [59]. ROS movement is not uniform and depends on where along the ETC the ROS is produced. Proteins complexes release ROS in different mitochondrial compartments, with some into the matrix, others toward the intermembrane space, and some at the outer mitochondrial membrane [60]. Production rates also differ across sites [61]. Local antioxidant buffering capacity, membrane permeability, and protein crowding further shape how ROS spreads and where it accumulates [62]. Together, these features determine how far and how fast ROS signals propagate. Recently, it has been shown that ROS produced in mitochondria exhibit directionality, rather than simply diffusing throughout the cell with preferential retention or escape set by the site of origin. This explains recent evidence suggesting that supraphysiological ROS levels produced in mitochondria may not induce direct nuclear DNA damage [63]. Therefore, it is important not to oversimplify ROS as the sole causative factor of pathology but to investigate the spatial and temporal constraints that govern ROS production and their contribution to pathophysiology.

Another way MMP regulates mitochondrial signaling is through Ca2+, which acts as a key cellular second messenger. Mitochondria control the uptake, storage, and release of Ca2+ within the mitochondrial matrix via MMP [4,64]. A higher MMP enhances mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake, reducing cytosolic Ca2+ levels and attenuating calcium-dependent signaling cascades. In contrast, lower MMP decreases mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake, resulting in elevated cytosolic concentrations and heightened calcium-dependent signaling [65] (Fig. 2).

The dynamic relationship between Ca2+ and MMP is important for physiological functions, especially in neurons. Neurons, with their three distinct compartments—the soma, dendrites, and axons—communicate by transducing electrical impulses into chemical signals at synapses [66]. The entry of Ca2+ into mitochondria is triggered by electrical signals propagating down the axon (i.e., action potentials) [16]. Maintaining electrochemical gradients, regulating synaptic vesicle release and recycling, and coordinating these processes require substantial energy and Ca2+ buffering for homeostasis, all of which are tightly regulated by MMP [67].

A substantial portion of neuronal energy is devoted to ATP-dependent ion pumps that restore ionic gradients after electrical activity. These include the Na+/K+ ATPase, which resets membrane potential [68], and Ca2+ pumps such as PMCA and SERCA, which clear calcium from the cytosol [69].Collectively, these ion pumps account for an estimated 30–40 % of total cellular ATP consumption [70,71]. Mitochondria must therefore continuously match ATP output to fluctuating demands imposed by electrical activity and ion transport.

A recent study showed that upon neuronal excitation, Ca2+ rapidly enters mitochondria, followed by an increase in mitochondrial ROS production. This ROS signal leads to the downregulation of excitatory synaptic channels expression, which appears to reduce the energy burden associated with sustained action potential firing. This sequence suggests that mitochondrial Ca2+ and ROS work together to modulate synaptic excitability and protect against metabolic stress during periods of high neuronal activity [72]. While the direct connection between Ca2+ influx and ROS production was not explored, MMP likely integrates calcium influx as an input, driving the production of mitochondrial ROS as an output. Ca2+ influx causes rapid mitochondrial depolarization, necessitating continuous regulation to restore MMP. Evidence shows that Ca2+ activates the ETC, which increases MMP [73]. Higher MMP, in turn, promotes ROS production. This forms a self-regulating feedback loop where Ca2+ influx modulates MMP, MMP regulates ROS production, and ROS feeds back to influence neuronal excitability. Through this loop, MMP dynamically links electrical activity with redox signaling and metabolic homeostasis. Thus, the interplay between ROS and Ca2+ signaling places MMP at the core of the regulatory network.

MMP-driven mechanisms in neuronal adaptation

A growing body of evidence highlights that precise mitochondrial morphology, positioning, and signaling are critical determinants of cellular physiology and pathology [74]. However, the mechanisms underlying how mitochondria contribute to the wide spectrum of cellular outcomes remain incompletely understood. In this section, we explore noncanonical mitochondrial signaling and its diverse roles in physiology, with a focus on how the dynamic nature of MMP influences neuronal function.

Cellular adaptation, guided by energetic needs, can be seen as a macroscopic manifestation of microscopic changes in MMP. At the mitochondrion level, rapid transitions between higher and lower MMP relative to local baseline at specific locations and times influence neuronal physiology, impacting key functions such as synaptic activity and cellular energy regulation. Synapses between neurons are dynamic biochemical structures that adjust their strength based on the type of information they receive, a phenomenon referred to as synaptic plasticity [75]. One of its key forms is long-term synaptic plasticity. Long-term plasticity is fundamental to learning and memory. Recent research has revealed that mitochondria undergoing fluctuations in MMP play a role in long-term synaptic plasticity [76]. Changes in polarized states of MMP were not entirely stochastic. Instead, specific spatial and temporal requirements were observed to stabilize the enlargement of dendritic spines [77]. Spines serve as specialized structures where dendrites receive signals from neighboring neurons [78]. Mitochondrion-to-spine signaling was only effective within 30 min after spine stimulation and within a distance of a few micrometers.

During spine enlargement, the dramatic increase in cellular volume necessitates a reorganization of neuronal resource management, a process referred to as decentralization. Decentralization involves redistributing biological functions traditionally confined to the soma, allowing processes to occur in other regions of the neuron [79]. One aspect of decentralization is the relocation of protein synthesis to dendrites and axons, enabling localized translation. This shift in translation supports efficient responses to stimuli and facilitates the structural and functional changes required for synaptic plasticity [80].

Localized translation in dendrites is important for activity-dependent processes, where protein synthesis is triggered by synaptic signaling. This mechanism ensures that newly synthesized proteins are available to support rapid structural adaptations. Emerging evidence highlights that spine morphological plasticity not only relies on local translation but also requires the recruitment of mitochondria to dendritic spines, linking ATP production to the regulation of activity-dependent translation [81].

Mitochondria with a higher MMP are selectively transported to regions of high energy demand through microtubule-based mechanisms involving motor proteins and their adaptors [82,83]. Contrary to expectations, mitochondrial ATP production does not appear to broadly support basal translation in dendrites under resting conditions. Instead, mitochondrial recruitment is specifically required for activity-dependent translation [81]. Although changes in MMP are often thought to signal through ROS, cytosolic ROS levels in dendrites appear minimal under these conditions, suggesting that any ROS-mediated effects are likely confined to mitochondrial microdomains or immediate surroundings. Moreover, specific redox-sensitive targets linking ROS to translational control in dendritic compartments have yet to be identified. This suggests that MMP regulates local translation through alternative, ROS-independent mechanisms.

MMP might regulate local translation by modulating the production of mitochondrial metabolites critical for biosynthesis. For example, dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH), a key enzyme in de novo pyrimidine synthesis, is directly coupled to the ETC and highly sensitive to changes in MMP [84,85]. Lower MMP impairs DHODH activity, potentially limiting nucleotide availability and compromising mRNA synthesis or stability [85]. Thus, beyond ATP supply and ROS signaling, mitochondria may regulate local translation through MMP-sensitive metabolic control points. This adds an additional layer of precision to how neurons match bioenergetic state with synaptic function.

While we highlight neuron-focused examples, MMP coupling to mitochondrial signaling is not neuron-specific. The same interplay between MMP, Ca2+, ROS, and metabolism operates in other cells, with tissue context shifting kinetics and readouts due to differences in geometry, workload, and mitochondrial organization. For example, skeletal muscle has distinct mitochondrial arrangements and adaptations that tune these relationships, as discussed later in the review.

Intramitochondrial PMF

To preserve functionality, mitochondrial networks employ mechanisms to contain disruptions, including the strategic isolation of PMF disturbances. In this section, we will explore the most recent safeguarding mechanisms that have been discovered.

PMF isolation

Mitochondria exhibit morphological variations depending on the specific tissue environment in which they reside [86,87]. There are several excellent reviews published recently that discuss in depth the molecular architecture and signaling processes that underlie mitochondrial morphologic remodeling [[88], [89], [90]]. In striated muscle, mitochondria develop in a network configuration, known as a mitochondrial reticulum, to meet the high energy demands and accommodate the physical constraints of the tissue [91]. The mitochondrial reticulum demonstrates highly coordinated behavior akin to an electrical power grid, transmitting MMP within skeletal muscle [92]. When disruptions occur in the mitochondrial reticulum, neighboring mitochondria can rectify these disturbances by exchanging electrical gradients, limiting dysfunction spread [93] (Fig. 3). In skeletal muscle reticulum networks, malfunctioning mitochondria are isolated from the network through detachment processes [94]. Regional mitochondrial subnetworks aid in containing the impact of local dysfunction, with damaged mitochondria being expelled from the network within seconds. While the exact mechanisms are still under investigation, two explanations have been proposed. The first involves the formation of tubular protrusions called mitochondrial nanotunnels [95]. These evolutionarily conserved structures enable direct communication between mitochondria. Nanotunnels, composed of double membranes, typically form on the surface of stationary mitochondria or within tissues with limited mitochondrial mobility, such as skeletal muscles [96]. The protrusions allow for the exchange of mitochondrial contents, including matrix and membrane proteins, and facilitate the transport of smaller molecules like ions, RNA, and metabolites [97]. The second hypothesis centers on specialized contact sites called intermitochondrial junctions (IMJs). IMJs serve as stable connections between adjacent mitochondria and can be likened to electrical wires in a network, facilitating communication and coordination among mitochondria within the mitochondrial reticulum [93,98]. While the molecular composition and functional properties of IMJs are not fully known, they are characterized by the close alignment of both the inner and outer membranes of two neighboring mitochondria, exhibiting distinct electron density. Structurally, this phenomenon can be attributed to the abundance and alignment of cristae at the IMJ between neighboring mitochondria [99]. It is hypothesized that the alignment of cristae may enable the transmission of the electric field across the junction, contributing to mitochondrial connectivity. Both hypotheses suggest a safety mechanism that isolates disruptions at their initial site within the mitochondrial reticulum, like a circuit breaker in an electrical system. A circuit breaker prevents electrical disturbances from spreading throughout a network and this biological mechanism ensures that disruptions within the mitochondrial reticulum are contained, preserving cellular function and homeostasis [100].

Cristae PMF autonomy within a mitochondrion

The circuit breaker analogy can be extended to the microscopic scale of mitochondrial microdomains. Within the inner mitochondrial membrane lie distinct subcompartments known as cristae and the inner boundary membrane (IBM) [101]. Cristae are inward projections into the mitochondrial matrix, while the inner boundary membrane runs alongside the outer mitochondrial membrane [102,103] (Fig. 1). The ETC resides in the cristae where it contributes to the MMP by pumping protons into the intermembrane space. The MMP has canonically been viewed as a homogeneous entity across the inner membrane [104,105]. Recent studies have indicated that individual cristae within a mitochondrion exhibit electrical autonomy, maintaining a distinct MMP from neighboring cristae (Fig. 3). Using advanced microscopy techniques, cristae showed an increased MMP in contrast to the adjacent IBM. The observed electrochemical disparity among segments of the inner membrane implies the potential for individual cristae to operate independently to some degree [106,107]. However, the mechanism that isolates the MMP in individual cristae is unknown.

A key aspect to consider is that the MMP is made up of protons, which must translocate to power the rotary motion of ATP synthase and generate ATP. With the high rate of proton translocation, it has been proposed that proton transport is aided by the Grotthuss mechanism [108]. Because of how easily protons modify substrates based on their unique biophysical properties, most of their protein-mediated effects occur through proton hopping, often using proton modifiable amino acids as conduits. In this context, protons are not like other electrolytes. Recent cryo-electron microscopy studies have supported Grotthuss proton-hopping mechanism in which protons move through a chain of aligned water molecules and neighboring amino acid residues [109]. For the Grotthuss mechanism to operate efficiently within cristae, the complete closure of the MICOS (mitochondrial contact site and cristae organizing system) is essential. Alterations to MICOS result in the loss of MMP heterogeneity across the mitochondrion, as MICOS forms the critical contact sites linking cristae to IBM. Without MICOS, the structural integrity of mitochondrial cristae is compromised. Therefore, MICOS is necessary for maintaining localized proton gradients within specific cristae regions. Overall, the model suggests that cristae and IBM operate as separate electrochemical domains. These domains can be susceptible to damage, leading to the loss of functionality in certain areas, while others may continue to maintain MMP preserving their function (Fig. 3).

In support of isolated cristae MMP, there are reports of pH gradients along the IM. While MMP plays a role in various biological processes, it is just one component of the broader PMF. ΔpH within mitochondria also contributes to the PMF, albeit to a lesser degree. Recent research has revealed a heterogeneous distribution of ΔpH within the mitochondrion. Utilizing local pH sensors revealed lateral, transmembrane, and radial pH gradients between the ETC and ATP synthase within individual cristae [110]. ATP synthase consumes the MMP by facilitating proton movement across the IM. However, there exists a physical separation between the source of protons in the ETC and ATP synthase. Variations in proton concentration occur not only in a linear direction (lateral gradients) but also across different regions of the membrane (radial gradients). Consequently, the proximity of ATP synthase also plays a role in the diversity of MMP. While the presence of such pH gradients seems to challenge the compatibility with the Grotthuss mechanism, this remains an unresolved conundrum, as proton movement via this mechanism is thought to require a more uniform proton gradient across the membrane.

Although it may seem counterintuitive, the pH difference near ATP synthase is minimal during ATP production because most of the proton flow is driven by electrical charge across the membrane, rather than the pH gradient. ATP synthase relies on MMP to operate in the forward direction and avoid entering a futile cycle. However, under conditions where MMP collapses, ATP synthase may operate in reverse, hydrolyzing ATP to pump protons out of the matrix in an attempt to restore the gradient.

This futile cycling can be further exacerbated by defects in the ETC. ATPase inhibitory factor 1 (IF1) regulates ATP synthase by binding to it and inhibiting its ATPase activity, thus preventing ATP wastage [111]. IF1 also influences the spatial-temporal behavior of ATP synthase, impacting mitochondrial cristae structure [112]. Notably, the levels of IF1 vary across tissues, contributing to differences in regulation. However, studies suggest that IF1 may not effectively inhibit ATPase reversal under anoxia [113,114], where ETC activity is severely impaired. Instead, other evidence proposes that IF1 primarily serves as an assembly factor for ATP synthase [112]. This dual role of IF1, as both a potential regulator of ATPase activity and an assembly factor, remains a topic of ongoing research.

BTB06584 is a selective inhibitor of the reverse activity of ATP synthase and has been used to investigate when and how ATP synthase reversal might occur [115]. However, whether ATP synthase can operate in reverse under physiological conditions remains debated. One challenge is the difference in charge between ATP and ADP, which affects how these molecules are exchanged across the mitochondrial inner membrane. To maintain cytosolic ATP levels, the adenine nucleotide translocase (ANT) exports newly synthesized ATP from the mitochondrial matrix in exchange for cytosolic ADP. [116]. When MMP is intact, this exchange is energetically favorable and supports forward ATP synthesis. When MMP is lost, the exchange becomes energetically unfavorable, raising the possibility that ATP synthase reversal may not be feasible in cells as it might need steady supply of ATP [117].

Together, these findings suggest that mitochondrial structure is tightly linked to electrochemical function. The formation of distinct domains through cristae morphology, MICOS organization, and spatial enzyme distribution permits localized control of proton gradients and MMP. This compartmentalization allows mitochondria to sustain function in select regions even when others are compromised. Understanding how mitochondria maintain, adapt, or lose this electrochemical architecture is central to understanding mitochondrial resilience in both physiological and pathological contexts.

Assessment of MMP measurements

The different macroscopic levels of mitochondrial heterogeneity have been mostly assessed by measuring the MMP. Absolute values reported in the literature are typically measured in either isolated mitochondria in suspension or in live intact cells in situ, with values found in the range of approximately −165 to −135 mV [118]. Spectral fluorescent methods are commonly used for this purpose, where cationic dyes upon addition, redistribute between the inner mitochondrial membrane and cytoplasm, theoretically following the Nernst potential [119]. Fluorescent cations detect changes in MMP as an indirect measure of cellular energetic state. Without calibration, these dyes do not provide absolute millivolt values. Absolute estimates require Nernst calibration with ionophores to equilibrate the dye which isolates MMP but moves the system away from native conditions [120]. This review does not aim to comprehensively summarize MMP methodologies. Instead, in this section, we highlight often-overlooked caveats, such as how the fluorescence of potentiometric probes is influenced by factors beyond their Nernstian distribution. Probes commonly used to assess MMP typically include a hydrophobic component, enabling them to non-specifically associate with phospholipid membranes across cellular compartments, particularly with mitochondrial membranes [121]. This binding is influenced significantly by the membrane composition, which can vary widely depending on cell type and tissue. To mitigate non-specific binding, measuring dye uptake in the absence of MMP can serve as a control, although variability in this uptake may still occur across different tissues.

Another consideration is that potentiometric dyes must first cross the plasma membrane before reaching mitochondria. Thus, any alteration in plasma membrane potential alone can cause fluorescence changes independently of MMP, complicating interpretation [122,123]. Additionally, potentiometric dyes do not respond linearly to changes in MMP. This means the relationship between dye fluorescence and membrane potential is not directly proportional [123]. For example, a 50 % decrease in fluorescence intensity does not always reflect the same absolute change in membrane potential. Instead, it depends significantly on the mitochondrion's starting potential, making quantitative comparisons inaccurate.

When interpreting MMP data obtained using conventional dyes, a low MMP is frequently concluded as an indication of mitochondrial dysfunction or bioenergetic deficiency. While this can suggest ETC impairment, alternative explanations are possible. For instance, a reduced dye signal may also arise from decreased mitochondrial abundance due to disruptions in mitophagy or mitochondrial biogenesis pathways. One approach to account for mitochondrial content when measuring MMP is to use dyes such as Rosamine-based MitoTracker, which are MMP-independent [124]. These dyes undergo a series of conversions, transitioning from non-fluorescent compounds to positively charged fluorescent molecules that localize to mitochondria. However, while MitoTracker is often used to indicate mitochondrial content independently of MMP, it still depends on the presence of MMP to target and accumulate within mitochondria. Therefore, under conditions of low MMP, the dye may not localize effectively to mitochondria, limiting its accuracy in reflecting true mitochondrial content. A more robust solution may involve using fluorescently tagged mitochondrial proteins to visualize mitochondrial content [125,126]. This approach directly targets resident mitochondrial proteins, offering a means to normalize for mitochondrial mass independently of MMP and enhancing the reliability of bioenergetic assessments.

Concluding remarks and future perspectives

While MMP plays an important role in cellular bioenergetics, the expanding of its functions necessitates a shift in how we conceptualize its involvement in mitochondrial and cellular physiology. The discovery of MMP heterogeneity especially at cristae level, highlights the nuanced regulation of mitochondrial function that cannot be fully captured by traditional models. MMP is not simply a binary measure of cellular health; it is a dynamic and context-dependent parameter that shapes mitochondrial responses to cellular signals, stress, and metabolic demands.

Despite significant progress, several key questions remain unresolved in understanding the true role of MMP in cellular processes. One of the foremost conceptual challenges is how MMP heterogeneity, both across mitochondrial populations and within individual mitochondria, impacts the physiological outcomes at the cellular level. The precise mechanisms by which different mitochondrial regions maintain distinct MMP gradients and how these gradients influence mitochondrial function in both healthy and diseased states are not well understood. Localized differences in MMP likely contribute to specific mitochondrial functions and play a protective role, though the full extent of these effects remains unclear.

Moreover, while low MMP is commonly associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, emerging evidence suggests that MMP changes can have context-specific consequences, depending on cell type, tissue, and the degree of disruption. The current dichotomy of “high equals good, low equals bad” fails to capture the complexity of MMP dynamics. Alterations in MMP, both increases and decreases, likely serve different roles depending on the physiological or pathological context. It is possible that subtle fluctuations in MMP, even within normal ranges, contribute to fine-tuning mitochondrial responses and cellular signaling.

Another area of interest is how MMP modulates mitochondrial communication, including through mechanisms like mitochondrial nanotunnels and intermitochondrial junctions. These structures seem to act as part of a broader network that coordinates mitochondrial function in a way that mimics a highly integrated electrical grid. Disruptions in this network can have profound consequences on tissue-specific mitochondrial function and adaptation, especially in tissues with high-energy demands, such as muscle and neurons. The long-term impact of these network dynamics on cellular function and disease progression requires further exploration.

Finally, although advancements in methodology have significantly improved our ability to measure and visualize MMP in live cells, these techniques still fall short of capturing the full complexity of MMP dynamics. Current approaches often involve complete ablation of MMP, a method that does not accurately mimic physiological or pathological conditions. Moreover, many studies on MMP variations are correlative in nature, making it difficult to establish definitive causal relationships. To truly understand the role of MMP in cellular function, more refined approaches to control MMP are needed that can capture its dynamic changes in real-time and in a more context-dependent manner.

In conclusion, advancing our understanding of MMP will require a conceptual shift that embraces its complexity as a regulator of mitochondrial function and cellular physiology. By addressing these outstanding questions, we can deepen our appreciation of MMP's multifaceted role and open new avenues for therapeutic strategies targeting mitochondrial dysfunction.
