Preliminary Outcomes of NephSIM Nephrons: A Virtual Mentorship Program to Foster Interest in Nephrology
Authors: Samira S Farouk, Matthew A Sparks, Jessica Joseph
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite concerted efforts by the nephrology community to increase the quality of nephrology education via a plethora of free open access medical education tools and efforts by national nephrology societies, interest in nephrology has mostly remained stagnant.MethodsA 6-month virtual learning and mentoring program (NephSIM Nephrons) was launched in January 2021 to increase interest in nephrology careers among medical students and residents. Trainees were assigned one faculty mentor and a small group with faculty and other trainees. Learning opportunities consisted of both live and virtual sessions and an asynchronous curriculum. Feedback was collected from trainees at the end of the program each year through an online, anonymous survey. For trainees in the 2021 and 2022 cohort, residency/fellowship status for the July 2024-2025 academic year was assessed by survey.ResultsOf 319 and 315 eligible applications received for the 2021 and 2022 programs, 111 and 108 trainees were accepted, respectively; 31 participants in the 2023 cohort completed the exit survey (response rate: 31/84, 37%) and rated the mean overall NephSIM Nephrons experience as 4.7 of 5. All respondents were very likely or somewhat likely to recommend the experience to other trainees. In the 2021 and 2022 cohorts, 37 of 49 (77%) and 29 of 38 (76%) trainees, respectively, who were eligible to match into adult or pediatric nephrology fellowships by July 2024 successfully matched. Similarly, among the 2021 and 2022 cohorts, 11 of 19 (58%) and 21 of 23 (91%), respectively, who could be internal medicine or pediatrics residents in July 2024 successfully matched.LimitationsLow survey response rate.ConclusionsEarly outcome analysis of the NephSIM Nephrons program shows promising results, and individuals who participated had high rates of enrollment in nephrology pipeline residencies and nephrology fellowships. More work is needed to first continue rigorous follow-up of program participants, obtain qualitative program feedback, and improve participant and mentor engagement.Index Words: Medical education, nephrology, training

Increasing trainee interest in nephrology careers remains a top priority for our field, given the ongoing challenges evidenced by unfilled nephrology training positions.1 A trainee’s medical specialty/subspecialty choice is likely multifactorial.2 Several studies have sought to answer the elusive question of “Why not nephrology?,” with findings including lack of mentorship, poor educational experiences, subject complexity, suboptimal compensation, and work-life balance.3, 4, 5 Despite concerted efforts by the nephrology community to increase the quality of nephrology education via a plethora of free, open access medical education (FOAMed) tools and efforts by national nephrology societies,6 interest in nephrology has mostly remained stagnant. A program for medical students and residents was implemented to try to increase interest in nephrology careers, and short-term outcomes are reported here.

Methods

A 6-month virtual learning and mentoring program (NephSIM Nephrons) was designed and launched in January 2021 to increase interest in nephrology careers among medical students and residents. The program structure is outlined in Table 1. As nephrology educational experiences for both medical students and residents are often limited to physiology/pathophysiology preclinical coursework and inpatient clinical experiences during residency, the curriculum aimed to expose early trainees to diverse nephrology educational experiences and allow opportunities to connect with nephrologists. Several educational sessions highlighted FOAMed resources like NephSIM, NephMadness, Renal Fellow Network, and Arkana Live Nephropathology sessions. Both the educational sessions and an annual career panel highlighted the diversity of career options within nephrology (eg, private practice, interventional nephrology, critical care, palliative care, transplant, glomerular diseases, and physician-scientist).

A brief online application was disseminated via social media/email, and the submission deadline was November. Applicants were selected based on alignment of trainee goals for their participation in the program with program objectives, as conveyed through their personal statement. In January, accepted trainees joined small groups (Tubules) of 6-8 trainees and 2-3 faculty members. Tubules communicated via WhatsApp and breakout rooms during virtual, live sessions. Since 2022, trainees have been paired with individual mentors based on interests and met twice during the program. Each mentor was assigned 2-3 trainees. Mentor/mentee expectations were outlined in the first session, and mentors received resources for self-evaluation and effective mentoring.7,8 Trainees were asked to join at least one 1-hour live, virtual session per month and to coordinate 30-minute meetings with their assigned mentor at least twice during the 6-month program.

In addition to one-on-one meetings with their mentees, faculty were asked to attend 3 prespecified live sessions to participate in breakout sessions. Trainees were asked to initiate communication with their mentor to arrange the one-on-one meetings and were counseled on potential meeting topics including their career goals and interests, their mentors’ career trajectory and interests, perceptions of nephrology, tips for interview season, interesting nephrology cases, or challenging topics. Similarly, mentors were advised to set expectations with mentees, try to respond promptly to trainees, maintain confidentiality, connect mentees with others when appropriate, and stay active in WhatsApp Tubule chats. All virtual sessions were held at 8 PM EST. In 2024, an asynchronous, themed, monthly, FOAMed-based online curriculum was introduced for trainees (Table 1, Item S1).

The 2024 faculty consisted of 36 US-based nephrologists with diverse backgrounds and career paths; 13 of 36 (36%) have participated annually since 2021. A recent collaboration with the National Kidney Foundation (NKF) has allowed for administrative program support and free NKF membership for faculty and trainees. Support from NKF includes marketing and program promotion; management of logistics including communications with applicants, accepted trainees, and faculty; assistance with live session scheduling; implementation of the asynchronous curriculum; and close collaboration with program directors to discuss strategies to improve the program. As of January 2024, NKF began to provide free registration for trainees to attend the Spring Clinical Meeting. Eleven participants applied for and received Spring Clinical Meeting travel grants which partially supported their travel to the 2024 meeting.

Age, gender, and race/ethnicity data were collected from trainees after they were accepted into the program. Feedback was collected from trainees at the end of the program each year through an online, anonymous survey. For trainees in the 2021 and 2022 cohorts who were medical students or residents in the United States or Puerto Rico, residency and fellowship status for the July 2024-2025 academic year was by assessed by reviewing residency and fellowship directories, Doximity, LinkedIn, public match lists posted by institutions, and the National Provider Identifier database. Those in other countries or not enrolled in physician training programs were excluded from this analysis. Medical students who had not graduated by June 2024 were excluded. This study was determined to be exempt from Institutional Board Review and informed consent according to the Mount Sinai Human Research Determination policy.

Results

Out of 319 and 315 eligible applications received for the 2021 and 2022 programs, 111 and 108 trainees were accepted, respectively. Ineligible applicants were those who reported that they were already enrolled in or had completed nephrology training. Cohort sizes ranged from 84 (2023) to 111 (2021), with participation limited to those located within the United States and Puerto Rico from 2023 onward to enhance virtual session attendance.

Early feedback after the 2021 program launch was positive.9 Thirty-one participants in the 2023 cohort completed the exit survey (response rate: 31 of 84, 37%). They rated the mean overall NephSIM Nephrons experience as 4.7 of 5. All respondents were very or somewhat likely to recommend the experience to other trainees. Since 2021, trainees have commented in the survey that they liked the exposure to the diversity of nephrology, casual nature of the program, and being part of a community. Selected trainee comments from the 2024 cohort are included in Table 2. Areas for improvement have included more: mentor-mentee/Tubule interactions, discussion of literature and potential research opportunities, and responsiveness from some mentors.

Discussion

This initial analysis suggests that NephSIM Nephrons participants are likely to pursue nephrology fellowships, though an inherent selection bias in program applicants is present. Given the dearth of nephrology interest, potential applicants may struggle to build peer-peer/trainee-mentor relationships that may inspire them to pursue a career in nephrology. A 2012 survey of adult nephrology fellows found that nearly 70% of trainees chose nephrology as a subspecialty to pursue during their residency training.10 Trainees who complete their residencies at smaller hospitals that do not offer robust nephrology services may have minimal exposure to the field. NephSIM Nephrons provides a mechanism for trainees to be part of the nephrology community and potentially encourage them to become nephrologists.

Two other programs designed to achieve similar goals have recently reported participant outcomes: the Kidney Disease Screening and Awareness Program (KDSAP)11,12 and American Society of Nephrology American Society of Nephrology Kidney Tutored Research and Education for Kidney Scholars (TREKS) Program.13 Like NephSIM Nephrons, these programs were designed to increase trainee interest in nephrology careers. In 2019, a study found that 43% (40 of 94) of surveyed KDSAP alumni currently on a physician career path were considering or had chosen nephrology as their future career.12 One important difference between NephSIM Nephrons and KDSAP is the trainee level. KDSAP participants, who are high school, college, medical school, and other graduate school students, have a significantly higher number of career options available than those who have already committed to medical school or an internal medicine or pediatrics residency. A 2024 study13 of TREKS participants found that medical students who participated in the program chose a nephrology pipeline residency at a higher rate than medical students overall (57% vs 31%, P = 0.01), like the proportion of 2021 NephSIM Nephrons medical students who chose a nephrology pipeline residency. Unlike KDSAP and TREKS, NephSIM Nephrons does not yet include a robust in-person component. Unfortunately, funding is a challenging barrier to promote trainee travel to in-person gatherings. To build some “real life” community, NephSIM Nephrons events have intermittently been held at national nephrology meetings.

The trainee exit survey response rate was low, despite regular communication with the cohort via email, WhatsApp, and at virtual sessions. Incentives, such as raffle prizes, may improve the survey response rate in the future. Future studies including focus groups and qualitative data analysis will allow for more granular understanding of trainee and faculty perspectives to improve the program.

Comprehensive follow-up and qualitative data are needed for a deeper understanding of outcomes and trainee experiences. Continued follow-up is needed not only of trainees who completed later NephSIM Nephrons programs but also of those included in this analysis. At the time of enrollment, trainees are asked for noninstitutional email addresses so they may be more effectively contacted in the future. Among residents who did not match into nephrology fellowships, 7 chose hospitalist careers (Supplement). Although these data were not obtained, these individuals may pursue nephrology fellowships in the future.

Prospective program adjustments may involve restricting applicants to junior trainees (eg, medical students, junior residents) who may be less committed to nephrology. Enhancing mentor incentives may also boost mentor engagement, in addition to free NKF membership ($375 per year for physicians). An adjunct academic appointment at the program director’s home institution was discussed as a potential option for NephSIM Nephrons faculty, though anecdotally, this was not thought to be an attractive option. If funds can be secured in the future, a stipend would likely be preferable to other mechanisms.

Virtual community-building can be a challenge and relies on the participation of all members. Some trainees have shared that their Tubule chat was not as active as they had hoped. For future programs, mentors will be given more direct guidance and tips on how to engage their small groups, and strategies to guide engagement will be implemented including specific examples of topics that can be discussed, sharing of a “question of the week” for all groups, and suggesting that faculty discuss their own engagement strategy with their co-faculty at the start of the program. Potential future sessions include interactive case-based group discussions, which were included in prior years, journal clubs, and other gamification activities. Although data were not collected on individual session attendance, anecdotally, trainee engagement decreased for some over the duration of the program. Individual session attendance will be tracked moving forward, with potential incentives for those with the highest participation (eg, gift cards). Individual mentor evaluations from trainees have not been collected, although this may also present another opportunity to improve the mentor-mentee experience.

In conclusion, early outcome analysis of the NephSIM Nephrons program shows promising results. Individuals who participated in the 6-month program with tailored mentorship had high rates of enrollment in nephrology pipeline residencies and nephrology fellowships. Overall, the program has been well received by participants. More work is needed first to continue rigorous follow-up of program participants, obtain qualitative program feedback, improve participant and mentor engagement, and ultimately contribute to the development and maintenance of a robust nephrology workforce.
