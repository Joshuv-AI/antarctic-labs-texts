RehabMate: an explainable framework for action detection and corrective feedback in pediatric stroke rehabilitation
Authors: Shitao Huang, Zhiwei Chen, Yiming Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pediatric stroke is a sudden cerebrovascular condition with a disability rate of up to 70%, often requiring long-term, structured rehabilitation. However, in home and community environments, limited access to professional rehabilitation resources poses significant challenges for recovery. To address this gap, we present RehabMate, an explainable framework specifically designed for lower-limb pediatric stroke rehabilitation, aimed at providing clear and actionable support to physiotherapists in delivering effective rehabilitation training. Unlike traditional ”black-box” AI models, RehabMate integrates a multimodal graph-based architecture with a spatial-temporal attention mechanism to enable interpretable assessment of movement quality and detection of incorrect rehabilitation actions. This interpretability allows physiotherapist to trace AI decisions back to specific spatial-temporal features and body segments, providing auditable and rehabilitation-meaningful insights to support individualized training planning and adjustments. In addition, RehabMate incorporates an enhanced language model that generates real-time, personalized corrective feedback and motivational support by combining action recognition results with a corpus curated by rehabilitation professionals. The feedback is adapted according to the child’s pain level, mobility, and age, ensuring safe and effective rehabilitation guidance tailored to individual needs. When evaluated on our custom PSP2 dataset, RehabMate achieved 93.3% top-1% accuracy in cross-view action recognition, significantly outperforming baseline models. By bridging the gap between model interpretability and practical applicability in physiotherapy rehabilitation, RehabMate demonstrates the potential of explainable AI to deliver trustworthy and implementable lower-limb rehabilitation support for pediatric stroke patients.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is widely expected to bring transformative changes to rehabilitation decision-making and service delivery in home and community settings. However, AI applications in practical rehabilitation scenarios remain in a stage of “great potential but limited adoption.” One key barrier is the lack of interpretability and transparency in traditional “black-box” models (such as deep neural networks)1. Users, such as physiotherapists, often see only the results without understanding how they are generated, which creates information asymmetry in rehabilitation guidance, undermines effective collaboration between human decision-makers and AI, and hinders AI’s transition from the laboratory to real-world deployment. In recent years, “explainable artificial intelligence” (XAI) has been recognized as a critical pathway for enhancing the usability of AI in home and community rehabilitation2. Explainable models can provide information on relevant variables, potential causal relationships, and decision rationales–not only helping to understand recovery progress but also supporting auditing and safety monitoring, thereby increasing user trust3. However, XAI in the rehabilitation field still faces challenges: the absence of standardized metrics for explanation quality, a lack of publicly accepted benchmark datasets, and persistent trade-offs between interpretability and model performance4.

Pediatric stroke is an acute neurological event caused by sudden interruption of cerebral blood circulation and ranks among the top ten causes of death in children, with a disability rate as high as 70%5–7. Systematic, long-term motor rehabilitation training is essential for improving motor function impairments in affected children. Owing to their strong neuroplasticity, children often have greater rehabilitation potential than adults, and they tend to show higher initiative and compliance in training. However, traditional rehabilitation methods often require frequent hospital visits and dependence on professional therapists, imposing heavy financial and logistical burdens on families8. Therefore, a key societal challenge is how to provide professional, continuous rehabilitation assessment and training guidance at lower cost in home or community settings.

In the rehabilitation process, promptly identifying and correcting abnormal or improper movements can not only improve training outcomes but also help prevent secondary injuries. At the same time, rehabilitation therapists provide emotional support and motivation to help children maintain their engagement in recovery9. However, most AI-based rehabilitation systems currently struggle to fully replicate this role10–13. On one hand, many AI models lack interpretability, preventing healthcare professionals from understanding the rationale behind decisions, which reduces their credibility and usability in clinical environments. On the other hand, existing models are often trained on adult datasets, making it difficult to accurately capture the anatomical and motor pattern differences specific to children14–16. Thus, there is an urgent need for an explainable AI system specifically designed for pediatric rehabilitation scenarios17,18.

To address these challenges, this paper proposes RehabMate, an explainable framework for lower-limb pediatric stroke rehabilitation training. The system integrates action recognition and natural language generation modules to provide movement quality assessment, personalized corrective guidance, and emotional encouragement. RehabMate adopts a three-stream attention-based feature pyramid graph convolutional network (3s-AFPGCN), fusing multimodal graphs constructed from motion and skeletal data collected by multiple sensors, enabling precise recognition of pediatric rehabilitation action types and their quality evaluation. Based on a specialized rehabilitation corpus, we incorporate an enhanced language model to generate detailed action descriptions and personalized corrective suggestions tailored to the child’s age, pain level, and activity capacity. Each AI-generated feedback is designed to be understandable, traceable, and adjustable by medical professionals, supporting more scientific and individualized rehabilitation strategies.

Our system not only achieves strong performance in action recognition accuracy and feedback generation but also is designed to meet the practical requirements of clinical AI, namely being usable, trustworthy, and professional. Through intuitive presentation of results and clear explanation of decision rationales, RehabMate enables physicians to accurately monitor a patient’s rehabilitation performance and understand the basis of the system’s recommendations, thereby making AI a truly reliable tool for rehabilitation support. Related works

Large language models and multimodal technologies in stroke

With the development of artificial intelligence technology, especially the progress of deep learning, language models have been widely applied in various fields such as text generation, machine translation, and speech recognition, and have shown potential in stroke-related diagnosis and treatment.

In the field of stroke treatment, the application of language models can be broadly divided into two categories. The first category involves using language models to assist doctors in analyzing brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). For instance, Aziz et al. combined language models with machine learning techniques to parse free-text radiology reports of brain MRIs and predict the functional outcomes of patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) within three months16. Miller et al. used language model technology to analyze radiology reports of acute ischemic stroke patients, identifying key clinical outcomes such as edema and midline shift17. Kent et al. demonstrated the use of language models to detect covert cerebrovascular diseases in neuroimaging reports, linking these conditions to an increased risk of dementia, highlighting the potential of language models in improving early diagnosis and preventive healthcare in neurology18. However, despite these advances, such systems often operate as ”black boxes,” providing results without transparent reasoning, which limits their trust and adoption in physiotherapy practice.

The second category of application involves using language models to analyze electronic health records (EHR). Sung et al. showed that combining language models with machine learning techniques can predict functional outcomes for stroke patients, with advanced language models like BERT significantly improving prediction accuracy19. Kogan et al. used language models and machine learning to predict scores on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), allowing the integration of stroke severity into observational studies through scores derived from EHR data, thereby enhancing the understanding and management of stroke outcomes20. However, in most cases, the reasoning behind these predictions remains opaque, lacking interpretability, which restricts their clinical usability.

In the field of stroke rehabilitation, multimodal technologies have also been applied to some extent. Rathee et al. combined a motor attempt (MA)-based brain-machine interface with a robotic exoskeleton to improve upper limb motor function in patients with hemiparesis after stroke21. Su et al. proposed an adaptive hybrid brain-computer interface (BCI) system that combines two different neurophysiological signals, motor imagery (MI) and steady-state visual evoked potentials (SSVEP), to study the neurophysiological patterns of stroke patients and help with hand function rehabilitation22. However, these multimodal technologies have not yet been applied to pediatric stroke rehabilitation, and existing systems rarely integrate explainable AI frameworks that allow physiotherapists to trace and validate system outputs.

To fill this gap, our research combines two different modalities–motion recognition results and a professionally curated rehabilitation corpus–into a language sub-model to evaluate movement quality and generate personalized rehabilitation feedback for pediatric stroke patients. This combination not only provides personalized rehabilitation guidance for patients but also makes the feedback process more transparent and traceable, ensuring the system’s practical application in home and community settings.

Skeleton-based action recognition

With the advancement of sensor technology, skeleton data has become popular due to its efficient computation and robustness to lighting and background changes compared to RGB video and other data types. Deep learning methods using skeleton data, like 3D convolutional neural networks (CNNs), and long short-term memory networks (LSTMs), are effective for activity recognition in post-stroke rehabilitation23. However, CNNs and LSTMs face challenges in capturing complex dependencies in skeleton data. Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs), on the other hand, improve action recognition accuracy and motion quality evaluation by using information from neighboring nodes. This makes GCN more suitable than CNN and LSTM for analyzing skeleton data24.

Action recognition using graph convolution treats human skeletal sequences as graphs to identify actions. Yan et al. introduced Spatial-Temporal Graph Convolutional Networks (ST-GCNs), which model spatial-temporal dynamics by applying graph convolutions to joint configurations over time and space. However, the fixed structure of the graph limits its ability to capture multi-level semantic information25. To address this, Two-Stream Adaptive Graph Convolutional Networks (2s-AGCNs) introduce an adaptive mechanism to learn both physical connections and second-order information, such as bone lengths and directions26. Our work builds on 2s-AGCN by adding dynamic angle information, improving the recognition of similar actions through a new adaptive graph structure.

To highlight important information, Multi-Stream Attention-Enhanced Adaptive Graph Convolutional Networks (MS-AAGCN) use a Spatial-Temporal-Channel (STC) attention module to adjust joint, frame, and channel activations. This boosts performance with minimal added parameters27. Methods like GAT and DySAT use self-attention to capture dependencies between graph nodes28,29. Our method improves on this by calculating importance weights for frames and features based on skeleton data, enhancing the flow of information in the graph.

Our proposed method

This chapter systematically explains our proposed RehabMate system, describing the overall process from the collection of action data to the final generation of detailed feedback.

System architecture

The architecture of the RehabMate system is composed of three core components: the data collection layer, the data fusion layer, and the data analysis layer. As illustrated in Fig. 1, we depict the aforementioned three layers and their interconnections.

Fig. 1RehabMate system overall architecture diagram.

RehabMate system overall architecture diagram.

The data collection layer employs two types of nodes. The first type is the Standalone Node30, primarily designed for gathering motion data from the human body. It consists of four wireless inertial measurement units (IMUs, model: Xsens MTw Awinda) and a mobile device A. This model of IMU communicates using the Awinda protocol, and its built-in three-axis accelerometer, three-axis gyroscope, and three-axis magnetometer can synchronously acquire data at a maximum update rate of 60Hz. The four IMUs are worn on the subject’s bilateral knee and ankle joints, respectively, and their physical locations correspond to the lower limb nodes in the multimodal graph constructed by the data fusion layer, as shown in Figure 3. Mobile device A is responsible for synchronously acquiring human RGB video data.

The second type is a data annotation node, primarily used for interaction with the patient. This node consists of a mobile device B. Device B acts as a data aggregation center, connecting to the four IMUs via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) technology to receive their motion data, and communicating with device A and the cloud server via the IEEE 802.11 (WiFi) standard. Before rehabilitation training begins, patients record information such as the type of training movement to be performed, pain level, mobility, and age on the app on device B. This information, along with the collected sensor data, is then uploaded to the data analysis layer. By harnessing the language model and action recognition model within the data analysis layer, we can evaluate the patient’s movement quality, generate action correction feedback based on the patient’s condition, and craft encouraging text to motivate the patient to persist in rehabilitation training.

The following is a detailed block diagram of the data flow structure involving action recognition and language modeling in the data analysis layer, as shown in Figure 2.

Figure 2 presents the detailed architecture of the Data Analysis Layer, which comprises two main components: the 3s-AFPGCN action recognition model (left) and the language generation module (right).

The 3s-AFPGCN processes the multimodal graph through three parallel streams: J-Stream (joint features), B-Stream (bone features), and AM-Stream (angular motion features). Each stream applies Adaptive Graph Convolutional Networks (AGCN) and Adaptive Dilation Temporal Convolutional Modules (ADTCM) to extract spatial-temporal representations. Spatial-Temporal Attention Maps (STAM) fuse information across streams, and the final classification head outputs the recognized action class. The 3s-AFPGCN is trained to recognize four lower-limb rehabilitation action classes: walking, standing, climbing up the stairs and walking down the stairs. These actions represent fundamental movements in pediatric lower-limb stroke rehabilitation, selected in consultation with pediatric physiotherapists.

The language generation module receives both the action class and the patient’s profile (pain level, mobility, age). The Retrieve-Copy-Generate Network (RCGN) retrieves relevant action descriptions from a professional corpus, a curated collection of professional rehabilitation action descriptions and corrective guidance texts compiled by licensed physiotherapists. These retrieved descriptions are then fed into LLaMA 3.1 along with the action class to generate personalized corrective and incentive feedback.

Data fusion layer

Early fusion fuses inertial measurement unit (IMU) data with skeletal data before the feature extraction stage. Compared with late fusion, this method retains more detailed and comprehensive data information while maintaining high computational efficiency. Following the example of reference31, we fuse IMU signals and skeletal sequences into a single graph, which is then processed using a graph convolutional network (GCN) to capture the interdependencies in human structure and motion dynamics.

Before constructing the multimodal graph, we perform rigorous standardization preprocessing on the raw data from different sensors. This step is crucial, aiming to eliminate differences in dimensions, numerical ranges, and coordinate reference systems between different data sources, ensuring that subsequent models can perform effective and fair feature learning. The preprocessing process is mainly divided into two parts: skeletal data preprocessing and IMU data preprocessing, strictly following the input representation format defined at the beginning of the chapter.

Action recognition algorithm in the data analysis layer

In this segment, we are going to describe the motion recognition model employed for assessing the quality of motion in RehabMate. Adaptive dilation temporal convolutional module

In the field of skeletal temporal graph research, many studies employ one-dimensional convolution with a fixed kernel size to extract temporal features of actions. This approach struggles to capture diverse temporal receptive fields across different layers and datasets. Moreover, it plays a minor role in capturing various temporal scales, which is crucial for distinguishing between similar actions.

To overcome these limitations, a novel adaptive dilation temporal convolutional module is proposed, which dynamically selects features generated by 1D convolutions with varying dilation rates through channel weighting. The structure of the adaptive dilation temporal convolutional module is depicted in Figure 5. Given an input feature X with the shape [C, T, N], it is split along the channel dimension into 3 segments, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{i}$$\end{document}, where i ranges from 1 to 3.Fig. 5The structure diagram of the Adaptive Dilation Temporal Convolutional Module. ”C” represents the concatenation of feature maps along the channel dimension, indicating the formation of a new feature map after dividing the feature maps by the channel dimension. The value of d represents the dilation rate of the 1D convolution, with d being 2, 3, and 4, respectively.

Multi-scale features can capture spatio-temporal information at different granularities, while adaptive weights avoid the limitations of fixed fusion, enhancing the model’s robustness.

Spatial-temporal attention pyramid module

In graph convolutional neural networks (GCNNs), we achieve long-range dependencies among nodes by stacking multiple layers of graph convolutions. However, node features face a recognized challenge when propagating (i.e., ”diffusion”) through network layers: the ”oversmoothing” problem. This means that as the range of information propagation (number of convolutional layers) increases, the features of originally different nodes in the graph gradually converge, diluting and weakening the influence of each node’s unique local structural information. This weakens local structural information, thus affecting the accuracy of action recognition.

To address these issues, we propose a Spatio-Temporal Attention Pyramid Module (STAPM), inspired by Feature Pyramid Network (FPN)34, as shown in Figure 7. In the STAP module, the feature map output by the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(m-1)$$\end{document}-th layer of the spatio-temporal attention module and the output feature map of the m-th layer are element-wise added to obtain the feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F^{m}$$\end{document} of the m-th layer. This design allows the spatio-temporal attention module to share weights between the J-Stream and AM-Stream at the same level33, enabling the simultaneous consideration of changes in joint angles and spatial coordinates to identify keyframes and key nodes. Similarly, the spatio-temporal attention module also shares weights between the B-Stream and AM-Stream at the same level, allowing the simultaneous consideration of changes in joint angles and skeletal information to select keyframes and key nodes. Experiments

Datasets

PSP2 dataset

The PSP2 (Pediatric Stroke Patients) dataset is a specialized dataset collected in this study for evaluating rehabilitation movements in pediatric stroke. A total of 32 volunteers were recruited (age range: 6–12 years; average age: 8.5 years; gender: 18 males, 14 females). All volunteers were diagnosed with mild to moderate stroke sequelae and were in the rehabilitation phase. The dataset consists of 960 video clips covering 4 core rehabilitation movement categories: walking (on a 10-meter flat straight path), standing (maintaining a static standing position for 30 seconds), climbing up the stairs (continuously climbing 12 standard-height steps), and walking down the stairs (continuously descending 12 standard-height steps). Each movement was performed by each volunteer in an indoor environment and recorded simultaneously by 3 cameras from different angles to ensure the multi-view nature of the data.

To evaluate the generalization ability of the model in different scenarios, two rigorous partitioning protocols were adopted on the PSP2 dataset: cross-subject partitioning (X-sub) and cross-view partitioning (X-view). The dataset contains a total of 960 video samples. The specific partitioning methods are as follows:

1. Cross-subject Partitioning (X-sub): This protocol aims to evaluate the model’s generalization ability to new subjects, simulating the scenario of assessing new rehabilitation patients in real-world settings. The obtained skeleton consists of 17 joints, with each joint represented by a 3D coordinate and confidence score.

VATEX dataset

The VATEX dataset39 is an innovative multilingual video description dataset. Uniquely crafted, the dataset provides descriptions in both Chinese and English for each video, with 10 detailed descriptions under each language, greatly supporting cross-lingual video understanding research. The latest version of the VATEX dataset draws resources from Kinetics-600, encompassing 41,269 video clips that include over 410,000 English text sentences, covering a wide range of scenarios from daily life to professional activities, providing researchers with rich and detailed visual content. All video descriptions are meticulously created by human annotators, ensuring the naturalness and diversity of the text. This high-quality dataset not only poses challenges for machine learning models in understanding complex visual scenes and emotional expressions but also provides a solid foundation for practical applications such as video retrieval and automatic caption generation.

Ablation study of language model

To comprehensively evaluate the quality of generated action descriptions, we employed four standard text generation evaluation metrics: CIDEr, BLEU-4, Rouge-L, and Meteor. These metrics measure the semantic, lexical, and syntactic consistency between the generated text and the reference text (professional descriptions compiled by physical therapists) from different dimensions. Since most samples in the latest VATEX dataset come from Kinetics-600, we used the InternVideo2 model to identify the action categories contained in each sample in the VATEX dataset. We then trained the language model using textual forms containing and excluding action categories; the results on the test set are shown in Table 5.

By incorporating action recognition results as prior information, the metrics showed a differentiated but complementary improvement trend. CIDEr improved from 57.5 to 57.6 (+0.1), indicating a slight improvement in overall clinical consensus and terminology usage in the generated descriptions. BLEU-4 saw a significant improvement from 33.5 to 34.2 (+0.7), the largest increase, indicating that action category information allows the model to more accurately generate phrases and 4-grams consistent with standard rehabilitation terminology, and to use specific action descriptions more precisely. Rouge-L slightly decreased from 50.1 to 49.1 (−1.0). This is not a quality degradation, but reflects a change in the model’s generation behavior: with the introduction of explicit action categories, the output descriptions tend to be more focused and precise, rather than verbose and covering all possible details, resulting in a slight sacrifice in recall, but in exchange for higher precision and relevance. This ”precision-first” characteristic is desirable in medical rehabilitation scenarios, avoiding information overload. Meteor improved from 23.7 to 23.8 (+0.1), showing a slight improvement in semantic equivalence, indicating that the model is more robust in terms of vocabulary selection and word order rationality.

In summary, the introduction of action category information yielded the most significant gain on BLEU-4, validating its core value in improving terminology accuracy and descriptive fluency. The steady improvements in CIDEr and Meteor further confirm the professionalism and semantic fidelity of the model’s output. Although Rouge-L decreased slightly, this is a reasonable result of the model’s optimization from ”generalized coverage” to ”precise description,” meeting the high requirements of accuracy and safety in rehabilitation feedback. Overall, the experimental results consistently support integrating action recognition results into language models to generate higher-quality rehabilitation descriptions with greater clinical applicability.

Quantitative verification of interpretability

To quantitatively evaluate the interpretability of RehabMate, we designed verification experiments at the following three levels to ensure that the model decisions are not only accurate but also transparent and traceable to human users (physiotherapists).

Validation of spatio-temporal attention weight effectiveness

We recruited 3 senior physiotherapists to label the key joints for 50 randomly selected motion samples from the PSP2 test set. This quantitative result directly supports the clinical workflow of physiotherapists tracing motion defects through IMU data.

Traceability evaluation of feedback generation

We constructed an indicator called Traceability Accuracy (TA) to evaluate whether the generated corrective suggestions can be accurately traced back to the original entries in the knowledge base. For 100 pieces of feedback generated by Llama 3.1, three physiotherapists independently judged the corresponding pain level, activity ability level and motion category index of each suggestion. TA is defined as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {TA} = \frac{\text {Number of feedback with correct traceability}}{\text {Total number of feedback}} \times 100\% \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The experimental results show that the TA reaches 94.6%, indicating that physiotherapists can understand the model decision logic with high accuracy. For the 5.4% of cases that cannot be fully traced, the analysis found that the main reason is the semantic generalization of the language model leading to slight differences in the expression of suggestions, but the core medical logic is still consistent with the knowledge base. In addition, we counted the distribution of knowledge base call frequency: low-demand suggestions account for 68.2%, medium-demand for 27.5%, and high-demand only for 4.3%. This is consistent with the clinical reality that children patients in the PSP2 dataset generally have relatively high pain scores (> 4 points), further verifying the rationality of the system in practical applications.

Through the above quantitative indicators, we systematically verified that the interpretability of RehabMate is a system characteristic verified by clinical experts and measurable, which cannot be provided by traditional black-box models.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose RehabMate, an explainable framework for pediatric stroke rehabilitation. By combining motion recognition technology with a curated rehabilitation corpus, the system generates personalized, real-time feedback and ensures transparency and traceability in the feedback process. This enables physiotherapists to understand and validate system decisions, providing tailored guidance and fostering patient trust in rehabilitation through clear, actionable clinical instructions. These capabilities lay the foundation for translating AI research into effective rehabilitation solutions.

By focusing on explainability, RehabMate bridges the gap between AI’s potential and its real-world application, ensuring that these systems are not only accurate but also reliable and accessible for improving patient outcomes. However, current research focuses on lower limb rehabilitation movements, and future research needs to expand to upper limb and whole-body coordination training. The PSP2 dataset has a moderate sample size and needs to be further expanded to include children at more disease stages and with complications to improve the model’s generalizability. Overall, RehabMate provides a solution from technology to application for home and community rehabilitation of pediatric stroke patients through a systematic process of motion recognition, personalized feedback guidance, and interpretable retrospective analysis.
