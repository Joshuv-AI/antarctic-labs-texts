Gender-specific biomechanical effects of smartphone use with backpack load during standing and walking
Authors: Yi-Lang Chen, Wei-Han Tang, Yi-Xuan Guan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmartphone use and backpack carrying are common among young adults, yet their combined effects on neck and shoulder biomechanics remain unclear. This study systematically examined the three-way interaction between gender, smartphone use, and backpack load under static and dynamic conditions. Thirty university students (15 males, 15 females) completed a repeated-measures protocol involving three backpack loads (0%, 5%, and 10% of body weight [BW]) and two postures (standing, walking), with and without smartphone use. Head–neck posture and muscle activity of the cervical erector spinae (CES) and upper trapezius (UTZ) were measured using motion capture and surface electromyography. Significant smartphone × load interactions were found for posture and muscle activation, especially during walking. Male participants showed greater head angles (19.3° vs. 14.6°) and upper trunk angles (28.8° vs. 26.4°) than female participants, whereas female participants consistently exhibited higher CES activity (7.9 vs. 6.8% of maximum voluntary contraction [MVC], p < 0.001). Gender differences in UTZ activation emerged only during smartphone use, with female participants showing disproportionately higher values when carrying 10% BW (15.6 vs. 13.8% MVC, p < 0.001). Critically, during walking with smartphone use and a 10% BW load, female participants’ UTZ activity reached 16.8% MVC—exceeding the established 15% MVC fatigue threshold—while male participants approached this limit (14.5% MVC). UTZ activation increased progressively with backpack load in both genders, particularly during walking. These results provide the first empirical evidence that the simultaneous use of smartphones and backpacks can push neck–shoulder muscle activity to near-fatigue levels, especially in female participants. To reduce musculoskeletal strain, backpack loads should be kept below 10% BW during smartphone use, particularly when walking. Introduction

The widespread adoption of smartphones has profoundly transformed daily life, with users increasingly engaging in mobile activities across diverse settings. Global statistics show that over 90% of individuals in developed countries own smartphones, with Taiwan exhibiting particularly high usage rates—95% of internet users access content via mobile devices1. Research has identified prolonged smartphone use as a significant risk factor for musculoskeletal disorders, particularly in the neck and shoulders, due to sustained forward head posture (FHP) and increased mechanical loading on the cervical spine2–6. Additionally, excessive screen exposure has been linked to visual strain and altered postural control, further exacerbating musculoskeletal discomfort7.

The pervasiveness of smartphone use extends beyond stationary settings into dynamic environments. Surveys indicate that approximately one-third of smartphone users in the United States regularly engage in non-voice activities such as browsing, texting, or gaming while walking8. Similarly, in Taiwan, nearly 40% of smartphone users habitually use their devices while walking9. This growing trend underscores the integration of smartphone use into daily locomotor activities, creating new ergonomic challenges as users attempt to maintain visual focus on screens while navigating their surroundings.

Backpack use remains prevalent among students and young professionals as a primary means of carrying daily essentials. Observational studies indicate that over 80% of university students use backpacks, often exceeding recommended weight limits10,11. Many studies recommend limiting load carriage to 10% of body weight (BW); exceeding this threshold—such as carrying 15% of BW—has been shown to result in postural adaptations, including forward head tilt and posterior trunk lean12–14. These adjustments not only affect back muscles but also increase cervical erector spinae (CES) and upper trapezius (UTZ) muscle activity, contributing to discomfort, fatigue, and a higher risk of musculoskeletal disorders14–17. Prolonged exposure to these biomechanical loads may result in postural misalignment and chronic pain18. While extensive research has examined these factors individually, the combined effects of smartphone use and backpack carriage—particularly under dynamic conditions such as walking—remain underexplored.

Recent evidence indicates that smartphone use while walking alters gait patterns and postural stability, leading to reduced step length, slower walking speed, and increased cervical flexion19,20. Additionally, cognitive distractions from smartphone use may further compromise postural control, as users prioritize screen engagement over biomechanical stability21. The simultaneous engagement in smartphone use and backpack carrying may create compounded effects, exacerbating musculoskeletal strain beyond the sum of their individual impacts22,23. Understanding these interaction effects is essential for developing evidence-based ergonomic guidelines and preventive strategies to mitigate associated health risks.

Gender differences in musculoskeletal responses to smartphone use and backpack carrying have also been observed. Studies suggest that females exhibit higher neck muscle activity and report greater discomfort despite displaying less pronounced FHP compared to males24,25. This paradox may stem from differences in muscle strength, anthropometry, cervical spine mobility, and postural adaptation strategies26–28. Additionally, females may be more susceptible to fatigue-related musculoskeletal issues due to higher relative activation levels in cervical muscles29,30. Research has also highlighted gender-based variations in trunk and head stabilization strategies31which may influence postural compensation mechanisms under increased external loads. However, the extent to which gender influences responses to the combined effects of smartphone use and backpack load remains unclear.

This study investigates the combined effects of smartphone use, backpack weight, and posture (standing vs. walking) on neck and shoulder biomechanics in young adults. We hypothesized that (1) simultaneous smartphone use and backpack carrying would produce synergistic effects on head–neck posture and muscle activity, (2) these effects would be more pronounced during walking than standing, and (3) notable gender differences would emerge, with female participants experiencing greater relative muscular demands. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically quantify the three-way interaction among gender, smartphone use, and backpack load under both static and dynamic conditions. By examining posture, muscle activity, and visual parameters in an integrated framework, this research addresses a critical gap in ergonomic literature. The findings provide novel empirical evidence to guide the development of gender-specific ergonomic recommendations for reducing musculoskeletal risks associated with common multi-tasking scenarios such as walking while using a smartphone and carrying a backpack.

Methods and materials

This study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University, Taiwan (Approval code: NTU-REC 202312EM051), ensuring adherence to ethical standards and guidelines. All procedures adhered to the 2013 World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, including permission for the publication of identifying information and images.

A priori sample size calculation was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) to determine the required participant number. For a mixed factorial design with one between-subject factor (gender) and two within-subject factors (backpack weight and posture), assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25), an alpha level of 0.05, a power of 0.80, a moderate correlation among repeated measures (0.5), and accounting for potential sphericity violation (ε = 0.75), a minimum of 24 participants (12 per gender group) was required. The final sample of 30 participants (15 males, 15 females) exceeded this requirement, ensuring sufficient statistical power for detecting medium-sized effects.

Participants

Thirty university students participated in the study, with an average age of 20.2 ± 1.3 years for males (n = 15) and 20.1 ± 1.1 years for females (n = 15). All participants were regular smartphone users with no self-reported history of musculoskeletal disorders in the neck, shoulder, or back region. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the experimental protocol was approved by the ethical review. Participants were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) age 18–26 years, (2) daily smartphone use of at least one hour, (3) routine backpack use, and (4) no history of musculoskeletal disorders, injuries, or surgeries in the neck, shoulder, or back within the past year. Exclusion criteria included: (1) ongoing neck, shoulder, or back pain, (2) history of cervical spine surgery or significant injury, (3) diagnosed scoliosis or spinal abnormalities, and (4) inability to stand or walk continuously for 10 min.

Postural measurement

Head, neck, and upper trunk postures were recorded using the MacReflex motion analysis system (Qualisys, Gothenburg, Sweden) with a digital camera positioned perpendicular to the sagittal plane. Reflective markers were placed at the tragus (T), canthus of the eye (C), seventh cervical vertebra (C7), seventh thoracic vertebra (T7), and the midpoint of the smartphone (M). Figure 1 schematically illustrates marker placement and anatomical landmarks used for angle and distance calculations. To ensure T7 visibility when wearing a backpack, a 1.5-cm diameter, 25-cm plastic rod was mounted perpendicular to the sagittal plane on the upper back, with the T7 marker attached to its end. All angles were measured in the sagittal plane according to established biomechanical conventions. Posture angles were derived from 3D motion capture data, ensuring high measurement precision. Images were captured during the final 30 s of each trial, with frames extracted every 5 s (seven frames per trial) and analyzed using Qualisys Track Manager (QTM version 2025.1, Qualisys, Gothenburg, Sweden; https://www.qualisys.com/software/qualisys-track-manager/). The average of these seven frames was used for subsequent analysis.

Muscle activity measurement

Surface electromyography (EMG) signals were recorded from the cervical erector spinae (CES) and upper trapezius (UTZ) muscles on the dominant side using a TeleMyo2400 system (Scottsdale, AZ, USA) with an expanded 8-channel capacity. Surface electrodes (Ag/AgCl, 1 cm diameter) were placed with a 2 cm inter-electrode distance along the Longitudinal axis of each muscle. Electrode placement followed established guidelines for cervical musculature. For the CES, electrodes were positioned approximately 2 cm lateral to the spinous process at the C4 vertebral level, targeting the semispinalis and splenius capitis muscles. For the UTZ, electrodes were placed at the midpoint between the C7 spinous process and the lateral edge of the acromion, aligned with the muscle belly. This placement methodology adhered to the Surface Electromyography for the Non-Invasive Assessment of Muscles (SENIAM) guidelines32 and incorporated recommendations from relevant literature33–36.

Prior to conducting EMG measurements, participants were instructed to perform standardized maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) exercises for muscle-specific normalization of the EMG data collected in each trial, in line with the protocol outlined by Vera-Garcia et al.37. For the MVC assessments, participants were seated. The MVC of the CES was assessed through resisted neck extension, while the MVC of the UTZ was determined by resisted shoulder elevation, as described in previous studies28,34. Participants were instructed to exert their maximal effort (> 2 s) to resist neck extension and shoulder elevation against single and two fixed straps, respectively, with each MVC test lasting for a gradual exertion duration of 5 s. Each participant completed three trials of resisted exertion for each muscle group. To prevent muscle fatigue, each MVC test for the same muscle group entailed maintaining maximum exertion for more than 2 s, followed by a rest period of at least 20 s before the next trial. Additionally, there was a 5-min break before testing the other muscle group, adhering to established MVC testing protocols by Greig et al.33 and Namwongsa et al.38.

The maximum EMG amplitude of each muscle across MVC techniques was calculated using a 0.5 s moving average window, following established protocols for MVC determination28,37. The highest recorded value from the three trials was used as the MVC value for subsequent analysis, providing robust normalization reference values. Intra-trial reliability of MVC measurements was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), demonstrating excellent reliability (ICC > 0.9) for both CES and UTZ muscles across the three trials.

The electrical signals collected from both the MVC tests and the experimental trials were subjected to band-pass filtering (20–600 Hz) and sampled at 1200 Hz. Following signal rectification, integrated EMG (IEMG) values were calculated rather than root mean square values, as IEMG provides a direct measure of total muscle activation over the measurement period. A normalization procedure compared the IEMG data from each experimental trial with the MVC IEMG data for a matching 2-s interval, ensuring that muscle activation was expressed relative to each participant’s maximum capacity and accounting for inter-individual differences in muscle strength and electrode placement. All muscle activation values are reported as percentages of the MVC IEMG data. EMG data were collected during the final 30 s of each 2-min condition, consistent with the postural measurement protocol.

Experimental design and procedure

A mixed factorial design was used, with one between-subject factor (gender: male vs. female) and two within-subject factors (backpack weight: 0%, 5%, and 10% of body weight [BW]; posture: standing vs. walking), under two smartphone-use conditions (using vs. not using). Each participant completed 24 trials (12 experimental conditions × 2 repetitions). Trial presentation followed a fully randomized sequence generated using a random number generator, with each participant receiving a unique randomization order to minimize systematic order effects. To minimize learning and fatigue effects, participants completed a 5-minute familiarization with the texting task and treadmill walking, took mandatory 2-minute rests between trials, were continuously monitored for fatigue during the 3-hour session, and underwent post-hoc trial order analysis, which showed no significant order effects.

Backpack weights were selected based on prior studies recommending a 10% BW upper limit for young adults13,14. The 5% BW condition was included to assess potential dose–response relationships. Participants wore a standardized double-strap backpack (50 × 30 × 18 cm3, 0.2 kg net weight), commonly used by Taiwanese students (Fig. 2), and loaded with textbooks to achieve target weights. MVC measurements were conducted, and reflective markers were affixed. For each condition, participants either stood naturally or walked on a treadmill (CS-5728, Chanson, Taipei) at their individually determined preferred walking speed, established during the familiarization period and maintained constant throughout all walking trials.

Test combinations used in the study, with the backpack containing two weight types: 5% and 10% of body weight.

During smartphone use, participants completed a standardized 2-min texting task via LINE (Z Holdings, Tokyo, Japan) with a research assistant at a natural pace, exchanging predefined messages that randomly included basic personal information (e.g., sex, age, height) and casual topics (e.g., weather, learning, recent news). The duration of each testing trial was selected primarily with reference to previous studies employing similar measurements over a 1.5–2-min period6,20,28,38. Backpack straps were adjusted for proper load distribution. Each trial lasted 2 min, with data collection occurring in the final 30 s. Participants rested for at least 2 min between trials, with extended breaks upon request. The full session lasted approximately 3 h per participant. Figure 2 illustrates the various experimental conditions, depicting participants in both standing and walking postures with and without smartphone use, under backpack weight conditions of 0%, 5%, and 10% BW. Quality control involved repeating trials if texting was interrupted, technical issues arose, or posture deviated from natural alignment. All participants received identical instructions, and testing was conducted in a controlled laboratory with motion analysis and EMG equipment calibrated before each session.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) with α = 0.05. Descriptive statistics (means ± SD) were calculated. Given the factorial design, we employed mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA) for conditions with and without smartphone use separately. Gender was treated as a between-subject factor, while backpack weight and posture were within-subject (repeated) factors. This approach appropriately accounted for mixed effects, including cross-level interactions between gender and repeated measures. Normality was assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test, and Levene’s test evaluated variance equality. Dependent variables included HA, NF, UTA, GA, VD, and CES/UTZ EMG (with GA and VD excluded in the non-smartphone condition). Significant main effects were followed up with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests and independent t-tests. Interaction effects were explored using simple effects analyses. Statistical power was reported per Cohen’s guidelines (≥ 0.2 small, ≥ 0.5 medium, ≥ 0.8 large).

Posture effects

Table 2 shows that posture had a significant main effect on several measured variables, depending on smartphone use, whereas GA and VD were unaffected by posture. Significant posture × backpack weight interactions were observed for UTZ activity under both smartphone use conditions (p < 0.01), indicating that the effect of posture on UTZ activation varied with load and warrants further analysis. CES muscle activity during smartphone use was significantly higher in walking than standing (10.5 ± 2.8 vs. 11.7 ± 3.8% MVC, p < 0.05; Fig. 4). When not using a smartphone, walking resulted in significantly greater HA (p < 0.01), NF (p < 0.05), and UTA (p < 0.001) compared to standing (Fig. 5). However, during smartphone use, only UTA remained significantly different (p < 0.001), while the differences in HA and NF diminished.

Backpack weight significantly affected HA in both smartphone and non-smartphone conditions (p < 0.001; Table 2). Without smartphone use, HA increased progressively with each weight increment, whereas during smartphone use, significant differences were found only between no-backpack and backpack conditions, with no difference between 5% and 10% body weight (BW) (Table 3). UTA was influenced by backpack weight only during smartphone use (p < 0.05), while NF was unaffected in all conditions.

Interaction effects

A significant posture × backpack weight interaction was observed for UTZ activity in both smartphone and non-smartphone conditions (p < 0.01), with standing–walking differences becoming more pronounced when backpacks were carried (Figs. 6 and 7). In smartphone use conditions, a significant gender × backpack weight interaction was also found (p < 0.001): female participants showed disproportionately higher UTZ activity at 10% BW (15.6 ± 2.1% MVC) compared with male participants (13.8 ± 1.9% MVC). This gender difference was minimal without a backpack (7.6 ± 1.1% vs. 7.4 ± 1.0% MVC) but became pronounced at 10% BW (Fig. 8).

Discussion

This study investigated the combined effects of smartphone use, backpack weight, and posture on neck–shoulder biomechanics in young adults, with particular attention to gender differences. Female participants exhibited greater cervical erector spinae (CES) activation despite showing less forward head posture, and their upper trapezius (UTZ) activity exceeded the fatigue threshold (15% of maximum voluntary contraction [MVC]) when walking while using a smartphone and carrying a 10% body weight (BW) backpack. Male participants showed lower muscle activity but still approached this threshold. These findings confirm our hypotheses that smartphone use and backpack carriage interact synergistically, that their effects are more pronounced during walking than standing, and that gender-based differences are evident in both postural and muscular responses. To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify the three-way interaction of gender, smartphone use, and backpack load—a common daily scenario—demonstrating that load carriage further modifies neck–head–trunk posture and muscle activity beyond the effects of smartphone use alone.

Gender differences

A key finding was the gender-specific adaptation strategies during smartphone use with backpack carriage. Although several postural measures differed, neck flexion (NF) converged to about 34° in both genders, suggesting a consistent viewing strategy that balances gaze angle (GA) and viewing distance (VD). This value aligns with previous reports of 33°–45° during mobile device use39and the posterior load of the backpack may have encouraged a slightly more upright cervical posture than typical.

Male participants displayed greater head angle (HA) and upper trunk angle (UTA) across all conditions, indicating a global forward-leaning posture of the head, neck, and trunk. In contrast, female participants maintained a more upright trunk and relied on localized cervical adjustments, consistent with earlier findings that females adopt more upright postures31.

As part of our post-hoc interpretation, we calculated the craniovertebral angle (CVA) from NF and UTA values using the formula CVA = 90°– (NF + UTA). This composite measure, validated in smartphone ergonomics research, is a clinically relevant indicator of forward head posture (FHP)40. Our data revealed distinct patterns: during smartphone use with a 10% BW backpack, males had a CVA of 25.6 ± 4.2°, compared with 31.3 ± 3.8° in females. Without smartphone use, the values were 32.4 ± 3.9° (males) and 36.1 ± 4.1° (females). Smartphone use reduced CVA in both genders, with males showing larger absolute changes. Since CVA < 48° indicates FHP3and previous studies have reported ~ 36° after 1–5 min of smartphone use42,43the notably lower values observed here suggest an additional effect of backpack loading. Thus, males exhibited a more pronounced FHP than females, with both genders falling below the recommended range of 48°–50°41 and the values typically reported for smartphone use without backpacks42,43. This is clinically important because FHP increases cervical mechanical stress and elevates the risk of neck pain40,44–46.

Despite less severe FHP, females consistently showed higher CES activity, indicating greater relative muscular effort to maintain comparable neck postures—consistent with the higher prevalence of neck pain among female smartphone users25,47. Gender differences were particularly evident in UTZ activation at heavier loads: during smartphone use with a 10% BW backpack, females reached 15.6 ± 2.1% MVC, compared with 13.8 ± 1.9% MVC in males (p < 0.001). This threshold effect may reflect the lower absolute strength of the neck and shoulder muscles in females29,30,48.

Postural mechanisms

This study highlights the complex postural adaptations that occur when smartphone use is combined with backpack carriage. Without smartphone use, walking produced significantly greater HA, NF, and UTA compared with standing. In contrast, during smartphone use, only UTA differed between postures, while GA (~ 60°) and VD (34–38 cm) remained stable. These results suggest that visual engagement with the device, rather than locomotor demands, primarily determined head–neck positioning.

Backpack weight also influenced posture differently depending on smartphone use. Without smartphone use, HA increased progressively with load. However, during smartphone use, significant differences appeared only between the no-backpack and backpack conditions, with no further change from 5% to 10% BW. NF remained stable across loads, indicating that participants prioritized maintaining a preferred GA and VD regardless of backpack weight. These results differ from earlier reports of progressive postural shifts with heavier loads12,13 and support the idea that visual demands dominate head–neck positioning when smartphones are used during load carriage. The observed increase in VD with heavier backpacks may represent a compensatory strategy, bringing the head’s center of mass closer to the trunk’s vertical axis and potentially offering visual ergonomic benefits49,50.

Muscle activity and fatigue thresholds

The dose–response relationship between backpack weight and muscle activation was most evident in the UTZ. During walking with smartphone use, UTZ activation increased from 7.7 ± 1.4% MVC with no backpack to 11.7 ± 1.8% MVC at 5% BW and 15.7 ± 2.4% MVC at 10% BW. Under the most demanding condition (walking + smartphone use + 10% BW), females reached 16.8 ± 2.3% MVC, while males reached 14.5 ± 2.1% MVC—a 16% difference in relative demand. Notably, females exceeded the 15% MVC fatigue threshold proposed by Rohmert51and males approached it, indicating that this task combination can push individuals close to physiological limits and elevate the risk of fatigue-related neck and shoulder discomfort with prolonged exposure.

UTZ activation was consistently higher than CES activation across all conditions. As noted by Pardeshi et al.52, excessive smartphone use reduces the CVA, producing fatigue in the UTZ but not the CES. Our findings are consistent with this mechanism, as decreased CVA likely contributed to the greater UTZ loading observed.

Task prioritization and visual demands

The stability of GA (~ 60°) and VD (34–38 cm) across backpack loads during smartphone use suggests that participants maintained consistent visual alignment with the device, even as posture and muscle demands changed. This pattern indicates that visual requirements shaped head–neck positioning more strongly than locomotor or load-related factors. Such prioritization aligns with the task prioritization hypothesis, which proposes that attention is directed to tasks considered most critical—in this case, sustaining clear visual access to the smartphone screen. Woollacott and Shumway-Cook53 emphasized that postural control depends on limited attentional resources, and under dual-task conditions, stability adjustments may be compromised to preserve performance in the primary visual–manual task.

Muscle activity patterns, particularly in the UTZ, support this interpretation. UTZ activation remained higher during walking, reflecting the additional stabilization needed to counteract vertical gait accelerations21,54. These demands increased further with backpack loading55,56 and were compounded during smartphone use, when attentional resources were partially diverted from postural control. This finding is consistent with Andersson et al.57, who reported that dual-task interference between postural stability and cognitive tasks reflects general capacity limitations. The UTZ response also displayed a clear dose–response relationship with load, underscoring its biomechanical sensitivity to external weight.

Clinical and ergonomic implications

This study is the first to show that simultaneous smartphone use and backpack carriage produce synergistic biomechanical effects that exceed the risks of either activity alone. Female participants reached the critical UTZ fatigue threshold (16.8% MVC) during a common daily scenario—walking while using a smartphone and carrying a 10% BW backpack—whereas male participants remained below this limit. Although a 10% BW threshold is often regarded as safe14,58–60our findings suggest that this guideline may be excessive for females under multitasking conditions, highlighting the need to either reduce carried weight or incorporate rest breaks to mitigate fatigue-related discomfort and injury risk.

NF stabilized around 34° during smartphone use, regardless of backpack load, reflecting a consistent visual adaptation strategy. This suggests that ergonomic interventions should address not only load reduction but also visual ergonomics—for example, elevating the device screen or using posture-training aids to promote a more neutral cervical position. Despite showing less pronounced FHP, females demonstrated higher CES activity, indicating greater relative muscular effort to sustain comparable postures. This underscores the importance of strategies that target muscle exertion as well as postural correction.

The observed three-way interaction between gender, smartphone use, and load challenges conventional ergonomic assessments that consider these activities in isolation. By recognizing their combined effects, guidelines can be refined to provide more comprehensive and gender-specific recommendations for multitasking contexts, ultimately reducing technology-related musculoskeletal risks among young adults.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the sample consisted of young, healthy university students, which limits generalizability to older populations or individuals with musculoskeletal conditions. Second, the trials lasted only 2 min, capturing short-term responses but not Long-term adaptations. Third, backpack weight was capped at 10% BW for ethical reasons, which may underestimate the loads carried in real-world contexts. Fourth, the absence of subjective discomfort ratings prevents direct associations between biomechanical changes and perceived strain. Finally, the controlled laboratory environment, including treadmill walking, may not fully represent natural gait and posture in daily settings.

Despite these constraints, the study provides valuable insights into the interplay between smartphone use, backpack carriage, and posture. Future research should examine long-term effects, include subjective discomfort assessments, and test heavier loads and varied walking conditions to enhance ecological validity.

Conclusions

This study quantified the combined effects of smartphone use, backpack load, and gender on neck–shoulder biomechanics. Male participants adopted a more forward-leaning posture, whereas females relied on localized cervical adjustments but exhibited higher muscle activity. Critically, in females, walking while using a smartphone with a 10% body weight backpack elevated upper trapezius activation to 16.8% MVC of maximum voluntary contraction [MVC], surpassing the 15% MVC fatigue threshold. These findings indicate that current load carriage guidelines may be excessive for females under multitasking conditions. From an ergonomic perspective, backpack loads should be kept below 10% of body weight during smartphone use—particularly in females—to reduce neck–shoulder strain. More broadly, ergonomic recommendations should consider the combined effects of concurrent daily activities rather than assessing each in isolation.
