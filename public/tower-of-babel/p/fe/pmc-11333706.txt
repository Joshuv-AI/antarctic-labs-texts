Sweetened caffeine drinking revealed behavioral rhythm independent of the central circadian clock in male mice
Authors: Yu Tahara, Jingwei Ding, Akito Ito, Shigenobu Shibata
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaffeine consumption is associated with the evening chronotype, and caffeine administration in mice results in prolonged period of the circadian rhythm in locomotor activity. However, as caffeine is bitter, sweetened caffeine is preferred by humans and mice; yet, its impact on the circadian clock has not been explored. In this study, mice were provided with freely available sweetened caffeine to investigate its effects on behavioral rhythms and peripheral clocks. Mice that freely consumed sweetened caffeine shifted from nocturnal to diurnal activity rhythms. In addition to the light-dark entrained behavioral rhythm component, some animals exhibited free-running period longer than 24-h. Intraperitoneal administration of caffeine at the beginning of the light phase also acutely induced diurnal behavior. The behavioral rhythms with long period (26–30 h) due to sweetened caffeine were observed even in mice housed under constant light or with a lesioned central circadian clock located in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus; however, the rhythmicity was unstable. PER2::LUCIFERASE rhythms in peripheral tissues, such as the kidney, as measured via in vivo whole-body imaging during caffeine consumption, showed reduced amplitude and desynchronized phases among individuals. These results indicate that consumption of sweetened caffeine induces diurnal and long-period behavioral rhythms irrespective of the central clock, causing desynchronization of the clock in the body.Subject terms: Physiology, Risk factors, Feeding behaviour

Introduction

Caffeine is a widely consumed substance, known for its arousal effects. The impact of caffeine on health is an ongoing subject of research with both supportive and opposing findings. Cohort studies have linked caffeine intake to decreased incidence of cardiovascular diseases1, lower risk of diabetes2, and reduced mortality rates3. However, it is crucial to acknowledge that caffeine is addictive and may negatively affect sleep quality and overall well-being4. Excessive consumption of caffeine-rich beverages is associated with decreased sleep quality5. Individuals who lean towards a night-oriented lifestyle often have high caffeine consumption6. Reports also suggest that a night-oriented lifestyle may contribute to the development of caffeine use disorder7. The disruption of sleep patterns due to caffeine may arise from its immediate stimulatory effects and preference for nighttime activities.

The internal body clock is a sophisticated system that governs the body’s sleep-wake cycle, creating an approximately 24-h rhythm8,9. Irregular circadian rhythms are linked to various diseases and health issues9,10. Internal body clock comprises a central regulator located in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus and peripheral regulators distributed across all other organs11. The SCN is primarily entrained by light, aligning the body’s internal clock with the natural day-night cycle, ensuring the synchronization of clocks in peripheral tissues through neuronal and humoral outputs8. It has been shown that constant light (LL) disrupts circadian organization by desynchronizing the SCN neurons12. The circadian clock in each tissue consists of expressions of the clock genes and clock-controlled genes, with approximately 24 h of rhythmicity. Transcriptional and translational negative feedback by core clock genes, such as Period1/2 (Per1/2), Cryptochrome1/2 (Cry1/2), Bmal1, and Clock, generates a well-organized rhythm at the single-cell level, which in turn regulates many physiological signaling through regulating the tissue-specific clock-controlled genes9.

Caffeine acts as an antagonist of the adenosine receptor, a phosphodiesterase inhibitor, and a clock modulator with multiple functions. Chronic caffeine or coffee consumption dose-dependently extends the period of the circadian locomotor rhythm under conditions of constant darkness, but not under a light–dark cycle in mice13,14. A prolonged circadian period by caffeine was also found in mammalian cell lines, mouse liver or SCN explants, and Neurospora, and the effect was mediated by adenosine receptor signaling13–16. Caffeine administration during the active phase of mice inhibits or promotes the phase change of behavioral rhythm by light-pulse treatment depending on the caffeine concentration17–19. This caffeine-induced enhancement of light entrainment involves adenosine A1/A2A receptor signaling19,20 or neural firing by ryanodine-sensitive intracellular Ca2+ channels21–23. Acute caffeine injection in mice causes phase changes in clock gene expression rhythms in the liver or kidney, but not in the SCN, in a time-of-day-dependent manner14. Intraperitoneal administration of caffeine at the end of the active period for three consecutive days delayed the peripheral clock phase. In humans, similar to mice, consuming two cups of espresso 3 h before their usual bedtime delays the circadian secretion of melatonin15. Taken together, these results indicate that caffeine modifies photic entrainment in the SCN, lengthens the circadian clock period, and causes a transient phase shift in the peripheral circadian clock.

However, the effects of usual caffeine intake on behavioral rhythms and clock gene expression need to be carefully analyzed. This is because, as in humans, mice tend to be averse to the bitter taste of caffeine, which activates several type 2 bitter receptors24. Two-bottle preference test showed avoidance of caffeine choice in over 1 mM (>0.02%) concentration of caffeine in mice, suggesting that mice sense the bitterness of caffeine25. The above animal studies utilized caffeinated water for chronic treatment, and the drinking amount might have been reduced because of the bitter taste. In humans, sweetened coffee and energy drinks are associated with a high risk of addiction to alcohol, cigarettes, and drug use26–28. Thus, in the current study, we examined the characteristics of male mice behavior during sweetened caffeine water drinking and accidentally found interesting behavioral changes. Therefore, we further analyzed the behaviors from the perspective of circadian clock research.

Methods

Ethical approval

All experiments were approved by the Committee for Animal Experimentation of the School of Science and Engineering at Waseda University and the Committee for Animal Experimentation at Hiroshima University. The experiments were performed according to law (No. 105) and notification (No. 6) of the Japanese government.

Animals and treatment

C57/BL6J male mice (8-week-old) were purchased from Tokyo Laboratory Animals Science Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan), and PERIOD2::LUCIFERASE (PER2::LUC) knock-in male mice (8-12 week-old; ICR background)29,30 were bred at Waseda University. Mice had ad libitum access to food and water. The mice were housed under controlled conditions, with a room temperature of 23 ± 2 °C, humidity maintained at 50 ± 20%, and lighting (lights on from 08:00 to 20:00). Zeitgeber time 0 (ZT0) was defined as the light-on time and ZT12 was defined as the light-off time in normal light-dark (LD) conditions. Caffeine was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (MO, USA). Chronic caffeine treatment was administered with free access to drinking water containing 0.05–0.1% caffeine, 1–5% sucrose, or 0.1–0.5% saccharine. Moreover, 0.05–0.1% caffeine concentration was found to significantly impact the circadian free-running period of behavioral rhythm13,14. Water bottles were changed once a week. Acute treatment was administered through intraperitoneal injections of caffeine (i.p.) at doses of 20, 50, or 100 mg/kg, as a dose of 25 mg/kg caffeine has previously been utilized as a sleep deprivation model31. In addition, 20 mg/kg caffeine caused a peripheral clock phase-shift14.

Measurement of locomotor activity

Spontaneous locomotor activity was recorded individually using an infrared radiation sensor (SE-10; Akizuki Denshi Tsusho Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), according to our previous method30. Double-plotted actograms of locomotor activity are shown with 3 min epochs, and circadian rhythmicity of activity was evaluated via χ2 periodogram analysis with P = 0.001 as the significance threshold using CLOCKLAB software (Actimetrics, IL, USA). Onset and offset were detected and calculated using CLOCK LAB software.

Measurement of PER2::LUC rhythm from peripheral tissues using in vivo imaging system

To measure in vivo PER2::LUC emissions in mice, experiments were conducted using a gas anesthesia system (XGI-8; Caliper Life Sciences, USA) and an In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS; Caliper Life Sciences, USA), as described previously30. d-Luciferin (Promega, USA) was diluted to 30 mg/10 mL in PBS and administered subcutaneously at a dose of 5 mg/kg body weight. A CCD camera was used to measure luminescence in the kidneys 8 min after d-luciferin administration, and in the liver and submandibular gland 10 min after administration. To measure the luminescence rhythm for 1 d, the above operations were repeated every 4 h (at ZT3, 7,11, 15, 19, and 23).

SCN lesion (SCNx)

Bilateral thermal lesions of the SCN were performed stereotaxically (RWD, TX, USA) under midazolam/xylazine anesthesia, as described previously30. A stainless steel electrode (0.35 mm in diameter) was inserted bilaterally into the SCN (±0.2 mm lateral to bregma and 5.7 mm from the skull surface) with DC power supply (SLG-40-10; N■SIC Japan Stabilizer Industry Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan). A lesion was made by maintaining a current of 0.5 A (with approximately 10 V) for 10 s. Sham-operated animals were treated by opening the skull surface without electrical stimulation. After the experiment, the brains were collected and sliced to check for SCN lesions. Mice with incomplete SCN lesions were excluded from analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 6.03 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Normal distribution and equal variation were verified using the D’Agostino–Pearson test/Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and F-value test/Bartlett’s test, respectively. Parametric analysis was conducted using one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test, Sidak’s test, or Student’s t-test for post hoc analysis. Non-parametric analysis was performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s test or the Mann–Whitney U test for post-hoc analysis. Sine wave fitting analysis was performed on the in vivo imaging data to identify rhythm parameters (amplitude, peak phase, and goodness of fit). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Chronic sweetened-caffeine water treatment delayed locomotor activity rhythms

Mice were randomly assigned to water, 0.1% caffeine, 1% sucrose, 0.1% saccharine (non-caloric sweetener), 0.05% caffeine with 1% sucrose, and 0.1% caffeine with 1% sucrose, for a period of one to several weeks (Fig. 1A, B). Increased locomotor activity during the beginning of the light phase (ZT0-6) with only delayed onset was seen in the 1% sucrose. Increased locomotor activity at ZT0-6 with delayed both onset and offset was seen in sweetened caffeine treated mice, and 1% caffeine showed larger effect than 0.05% caffeine (Fig. 1C, D). This experiment was repeated with 1% caffeine plus sucrose or saccharin under normal LD or constant darkness (DD) (Fig. 2). The results showed that caffeine-related behavioral changes were not related to sucrose metabolism, as caffeine with non-caloric sweetener treatment showed similar effects (Fig. 2A–C). Although the onset and offset were fluctuated in the caffeine-treated mice, average onset was significantly delayed in each caffeine group compared with control group (Fig. 2D). Regardless of the lighting conditions, free-running behavior with over 24-h period was detected in some caffeine-treated mice (e.g., sucrose + caffeine #2 and # 4; saccharine + caffeine #1–3). For the saccharine + caffeine #2, 24.0, 26.7, and 28.05 h of the periods were detected in LD, and 24.05 and 28.95 h of periods were detected in DD (Fig. S1D). In DD, the calculated period of behavioral rhythms by chi-square analysis was 24.10 ± 0.03 h for water, 24.22 ± 0.12 h for sucrose + caffeine, and 24.09 ± 0.04 h for saccharine + caffeine, respectively (no significance among the groups). The body weight was lower in the caffeine group than that in the water group (Fig. S1A). The amount of sweetened caffeine consumed was also lower than the water intake, independent of sucrose or saccharine concentration (Fig. S1B). In addition, water intake showed no difference between 0.1% caffeine and 0.1% caffeine + 1% sucrose group (Fig. S1E). Food intake was comparable between the groups (Fig. S1C).

Caffeine-induced diurnal behavior was not related to the SCN clock

After chronic caffeine treatment, the mice were housed with normal water under DD conditions (Fig. 3). Interestingly, the onset of activity immediately appeared at the normal phase (near ZT12) of nocturnal activity rhythm when caffeine was removed and transferred to DD. The average activity waveform showed two peaks of activity around ZT12 and ZT0 in DD, suggesting the two different rhythm components continued (Fig. 3B). Similar behavioral changes were observed in mice acutely injected with caffeine (Fig. 4). The mice were administered 20 or 50 mg/kg of caffeine or saline via intraperitoneal injection at ZT0, ZT10, or ZT18 for 5 days. However, instead of 50 mg/kg, 100 mg/kg of caffeine was administered at ZT10. High doses of caffeine (100 mg/kg) decreased the locomotor activity (Fig. 4B upper panel; p < 0.001 for the 24-h total activity counts between saline and 100 mg/kg groups by Student’s t-test), which is consistent with a previous report32. Treatment with 20 mg/kg caffeine increased locomotor activity independent of light. Notably, caffeine injection at ZT10 advanced the onset and offset of activity rhythm, and injection at ZT18 or 0 delayed them (Fig. 4). In addition, the advanced or delayed activity rhythm recovered to the normal phase immediately on the next day of the final treatment, suggesting that the caffeine-induced acute behavioral change is a temporary change and independent of the light-entrained master clock located in the SCN. To examine further, we conducted LL-induced weakening of the SCN output (Fig. 5 and Fig. S3) and SCNx (Fig. 6 and Fig. S4). In LL, the free-running period of the activity rhythm was extended, as previously described12. Although there were individual differences and difficulties in detecting the free-running period owing to the lower rhythm amplitude, the period of activity rhythm tended to be extended further in the chronic caffeine treatment group compared with water treatment (Fig. 5B and Fig. S3). These results suggest that caffeine remained effective under LL conditions. In addition, SCNx mice, which showed arrhythmic behavior after surgery, also exhibited rhythmic behavior for periods longer than 24 h during chronic caffeine treatment (Fig. 6 and Fig. S4). However, the rhythmic behavior was not stable. Interestingly, a stable activity rhythm with over 24-h long period was observed in mice that underwent partial SCN lesion surgery (Fig. S4). These results suggest that caffeine regulates the SCN-independent clock system to generate rhythms of locomotor activity with extremely long periods.

Chronic caffeine-sucrose water treatment changed peripheral clock oscillations

As caffeine treatment causes time-dependent effects on peripheral clocks14, we measured PER2::LUC rhythms in the kidney, liver, and submandibular gland of caffeine-treated mice using an in vivo imaging system (Fig. 7). Measurements were conducted when the mice showed diurnal behavior (Fig. 7A). In comparison with water treatment, chronic caffeine-sucrose water treatment resulted in disorganized bioluminescence rhythms in all peripheral organs, with significantly decreased amplitude and a large individual difference in the peak phase in each tissue (Fig. 7B–E). Some tissues showed a higher goodness of fit (>0.1), which indicated lower rhythmicity with a lower fit to the sine wave (Fig. 7C). The average peak phase did not differ significantly between the groups (Fig. 7E). Individual coordination of the tissue phases was calculated as the standard deviation of the peak phase among the three tissues in each mouse. A large standard deviation was observed in the caffeine-treated mice (water: 1.20 ± 0.13 h; caffeine: 3.58 ± 0.69 h, p < 0.01 using Student’s t-test), suggesting the internal desynchronization of peripheral clocks in each mouse.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated for the first time that administration of a mixture of caffeine and sucrose/saccharine to mice resulted in a diurnal and long-period behavioral rhythms. Although it is difficult to immediately apply the current results to human, the concentration of caffeine used in this study, 0.1%, was almost two times higher than that typically found in regular coffee (e.g., 60 mg/100 ml), but one-half that of espresso (0.2%; 200 mg/100 ml). Sucrose concentration in this study (0.1%) was 10 times lower than the energy drink or sugar-sweetened beverage (e.g., 10 g/100 ml).

Although caffeine is known to affect the central clock17–19, the diurnal behavioral changes observed in this study were likely to be central clock-independent. The reasons for this include: (1) the emergence of normal nocturnal behavioral rhythms and continue of caffeine-induced behavioral rhythms after caffeine cessation (Fig. 3); (2) the ability to induce the same diurnal behavior as acute administration at ZT0 (Fig. 4); and (3) the immediate return to nocturnal behavior upon discontinuation of this acute administration (Fig. 4). Acute exposure to light or drugs can induce acute changes in behavioral rhythms, a phenomenon known as “masking”33. In this study, the induction of diurnal behavior through caffeine injection could be considered as a masking behavior resulting from the acute arousal stimulus of caffeine administration (Fig. 4). This suggests that the diurnal behavior observed in caffeine-drinking mice (Figs. 1–3) may have been generated by consuming caffeine at around ZT0. In fact, the caffeine drinking increased at around ZT0, similar as the behavioral increase (Fig. S2). In contrast, the decrease in activity at the beginning of the dark phase following caffeine consumption or injection may be attributed to phase delays in the circadian clock14,15 and the impact of sleep deprivation due to increased arousal during the light phase34,35. In fact, the presence of residual activity around ZT0 even after caffeine withdrawal (Fig. 3) suggests that it is not just a result of acute masking but rather a high likelihood of phase delays in the circadian rhythm. Similar changes in behavioral rhythms were observed even when transitioning from time-restricted feeding during the light phase to ad libitum feeding in mice36. During time-restricted feeding at the light period, daytime activity increased, whereas nighttime activity decreased. However, upon returning to ad libitum feeding, the behavioral rhythm quickly reverted to nocturnal behavior. This behavior is SCN-independent because the clock gene expression rhythm in the SCN does not respond to feeding times37,38. Changes in feeding-related behavioral rhythms have also been observed in mice with SCN disruption39. Therefore, the diurnal behavior by sucrose-caffeine consumption observed in this study is likely to be SCN-independent.

It is also difficult to assert that the behavioral rhythms with long period (26–30 h) induced by sweetened caffeine are SCN-dependent. The reasons for this include: (1) the observation of both SCN-dependent free-running periods and caffeine-dependent long periods in constant darkness (Fig. 2 and Fig. S1D); (2) the additive presence of caffeine-induced long periods in the weakened SCN under constant light (Fig. 5 and Fig. S3; the same result was reported already in mice with only caffeine treatment40); and (3) the appearance of long-period behavior, albeit unstable, even after SCN lesion (Fig. 6 and Fig. S4). This caffeine-induced behavioral rhythm may resemble methamphetamine-induced long-period behavioral rhythms41–44. Behavioral rhythm dependent on methamphetamine persists even after SCN lesion42 and exhibits long periods under constant light conditions45. Interestingly, Masubuchi et al. recently reported the induction of an unstable free-running behavioral rhythm through caffeine consumption in Cry1/2 double knockout mice lacking endogenous behavioral rhythms in DD46, which supports the current data. However, the location and mechanism of methamphetamine-related oscillation systems are not yet well understood43,44, and the results from this caffeine study open the door to future research directions.

Because sweetened caffeine consumption was still lower than that of control water (Fig. S1) and increasing sweetness did not result in changes in water consumption (Fig. 2, Fig. S1B, and Fig. S1E), suggesting the amount of caffeine intake did not change by adding sweetness. Sucrose/saccharine intake itself might have a role on the long-period behavior. In the current and previous studies, diurnal behavioral changes were not observed with 0.1% caffeine alone or sucrose alone13,14,19,47, suggesting the important role of sweetness in this behavior. Methamphetamine, caffeine, and sucrose/saccharine share a common factor: dopamine. “Dopaminergic ultradian oscillator” might be involved in the current result, because the similar splitting free-running behavior with LD-entrained rhythm and long-period rhythm was also seen in dopamine transporter (Slc6a3) knockout mice48,49. Caffeine increases long-term potentiation in the striatum, leading to mitochondrial enhancement and increased membrane potential in dopaminergic neurons50. Moreover, A2A receptors in the striatum and nucleus accumbens along with dopaminergic neurons are involved in the regulation of performance and fatigue50–52. Both sucrose and saccharine evoke dopamine in the nucleus accumbens53. Sweetened caffeinated beverages, such as energetic drinks, have been shown to increase consumption and enhance alcohol dependence in both mice and humans54, suggesting the effect of addiction by those beverages. In future, it is necessary to investigate whether the observed behavioral changes in this study are a result of changes in the dopaminergic signaling using the specific inhibitor of dopamine receptors (i.e., haloperidol)48, or using other dopaminergic stimulations (i.e., high-sucrose diet or l-dopa) with caffeine treatment.

Peripheral PER2LUC rhythms in the sweetened caffeine-treated mice showed lower amplitude and bigger internal phase differences among tissues (Fig. 7). This result is reminiscent of the changes observed in the peripheral clocks of mice injected with caffeine at approximately ZT014. In our previous study, a 3-d administration of caffeine at ZT1 resulted in a reduction in the amplitude of PER2LUC in the kidney, liver, and submandibular gland, with increased individual differences in phase. Moreover, this timing is prone to induce “singularity”, as peripheral clocks can experience forward or backward shifts in individual cells55,56, a phenomenon also observed under acute physical stress exposure in vivo57. Therefore, the consumption of caffeine at bedtime is likely to disturb peripheral clock rhythms. The mechanism by which caffeine induces a phase shift in the peripheral clock is not yet fully understood. The antagonistic effects of caffeine on the adenosine receptor result in the regulation of the clock through the Ca2+ and CREB pathway20. The CREB signaling pathway is crucial for the regulation of the peripheral and central clock58–60. In addition, the inhibition of phosphodiesterase by caffeine may also play a key role. The PDE inhibitor rolipram regulates the clock in cultured cells61. Furthermore, the activation of the sympathetic nervous system by caffeine has the potential to influence the circadian clock regulation pathway62,63. Thus, caffeine exerts a strong effect on the peripheral circadian clock via various pathways.

In this study, the successful induction of diurnal behavioral rhythms in mice by administering sugar-containing caffeine to drinking water resulted in dissociation of the central clock and disorganization of the peripheral clocks. Given the observed positive association between evening chronotypes and caffeine consumption in humans6, there is a high likelihood that caffeine abuse contributes to the disorganization of circadian rhythms. Sugar-containing caffeine is also associated with alcohol and drugs26–28, both of which affect circadian rhythms64,65. Moreover, caffeine intake upon waking has been shown to suppress high-fat diet-induced obesity better than consumption before bedtime66. Therefore, actively reducing caffeine intake at night is crucial to prevent a negative cascade of effects in daily life.
