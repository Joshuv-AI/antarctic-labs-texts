Research Note: Exploratory analysis of temporal changes in ceca and plasma metabolite profiles of broiler chickens utilizing untargeted metabolomics
Authors: Philip M Campos, Katarzyna B Miska, Kouassi R Kpodo, Monika Proszkowiec-Weglarz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gastrointestinal tract and its microbiota are researched for their impact on the health and performance of broiler chickens, which have been selected for rapid growth rate to meet demand. Untargeted metabolomics is one method that can potentially link changes in the gut microbiota to changes in metabolites and assist in revealing mechanisms affecting health outcomes. In this study, untargeted metabolomics via Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (UPLC-MS) was performed to characterize changes in ceca and plasma metabolite profiles during early development (d 1, 3, and 10 post-hatch), a time period known for rapid changes in the cecal microbiota. PCA, PLS-DA, OPLS-DA, and fold change analyses revealed shifts in metabolite profiles and individual metabolites over time. The results suggested that shifts in cecal metabolites occurred mostly from d 1 to d 3, while plasma metabolites shifted more gradually from d 1 to d 10. Metabolites linked to KEGG pathway enrichment and potentially influential to growth, such as 5-Glutamyl-taurine and stachyose, greatly increased in concentration in the ceca. These findings show promise in utilization of metabolomics for time-dependent studies on broiler chicken gut health and integration with gut microbiota analysis.Keywords: Broiler, Metabolomics, Cecum, Plasma

Introduction

Demand for chicken meat and selection of broilers for rapid growth rates has led to research addressing challenges which may affect broiler health and performance. The gastrointestinal tract (GIT) and intestinal microbiota play key roles in poultry health by impacting nutrient uptake, immune system maturation, and pathogen exclusion (Aruwa et al., 2021). Diet, pathogens, and environmental stressors such as heat stress are among the factors often researched for their effects on the gut microbiota. Widespread usage of 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing has assisted in characterizing GIT bacteria and discovering correlations in bacterial abundance and treatments. However, the most common 16S amplicon sequencing methods are limited to genus-level classifications, and predictions of function are limited in accuracy, especially in non-human systems. Therefore, questions remain on the function of GIT bacteria and the mechanisms leading to health outcomes due to the limitations of 16S sequencing. Metabolomics, the study of small molecule metabolites, is a methodology which may bridge the gap between bacterial abundance and health outcomes by showing how changes in bacterial profiles may correspond with changes in metabolite concentrations. Metabolomics involves the use of liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC-MS) and can either be performed in an untargeted manner, detecting all known and unknown metabolites in a sample, or in a targeted manner, measuring a defined set of known metabolites. In recent years, studies have integrated both untargeted metabolomics and 16S gut microbiota data in poultry experiments to simultaneously assess alterations in gut microbiota and metabolite profiles (Zhang et al., 2023).

The cecal microbiota is characterized by high bacterial diversity and is known to rapidly change during broilers’ early development from days 1-3 post-hatch and further increasing in taxonomic diversity after around day 7 (Ballou et al., 2016). The intestinal microbiota generates metabolites that can play an important role in growth. Temporal metabolome studies have shown changes in enriched pathways in the serum and egg yolk metabolome during embryonic development up until hatch, and the liver metabolome has been compared at days 4 and 20 post-hatch (Zhang et al., 2024). However, the cecal metabolome at early stages post-hatch is not well understood. In addition, plasma metabolites may also serve as a useful complement to intestinal metabolite measurements, being important biomarkers for growth. For example, Su et al. (2022) reported potential plasma metabolite biomarkers found by comparing high and low feed efficiency broilers. In this study, we performed an exploratory analysis to assess the effectiveness of an untargeted metabolomics approach in distinguishing chicken ceca and plasma metabolomic profiles over three early development time points at d 1, 3, and 10 post-hatch. These results will provide an initial understanding of ceca and plasma metabolite profiles at early development and may inform future experimental design of temporal-based studies on the effects of diet, infection, and environmental stressors.

Materials and methods

Animal care

All animal care procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC, protocol #22-15) of the Beltsville Agricultural Research Center (BARC). Fertile Ross 708 eggs obtained from Longenecker’s Hatchery (Elizabethtown, PA) were incubated for 21 days under standard conditions. After hatch, birds were divided into 8 experimental replicates and placed in floor pens with heating lamps. Birds had ad libitum access to a standard commercial type corn-soybean meal starter diet up to day 10. The starter diet contained 23% crude protein, 3000 kcal/kg, 5.6% fat, 1% calcium, and 0.77% phosphorus. Ceca digesta and mucosal scrapings were collected at 1, 3, and 10 days post-hatch (each n = 3) for untargeted metabolomics analysis. In addition, plasma samples were collected from the same 3 birds at each time point, plus 1 additional bird at days 1 and 3.

Sample preparation

Ceca and plasma samples were sent to Creative Proteomics (Shirley, NY) for untargeted metabolomics processing. After thawing the samples, 50 mg of each sample was weighed and added to a tube with 1.5 mL of chloroform/methanol (2/1, v/v). All samples were vortexed for 1 min and grounded for 180 s at 65 Hz. 200 μL of water was added and sonication was performed for 30 min at 4°C. Each sample was kept at −20°C for 1 h, vortexed for 30 s, and then kept at 4°C for 30 min. Next, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4°C for 15 min. Lastly, 200 μL of supernatant and 5 μL of DL-o-Chlorophenylalanine (0.2 mg/mL) were transferred to a vial for LC-MS analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared to evaluate the methodology. The same amount of extract was collected from each sample and pooled as QC samples (n = 3), which were prepared using the same procedures.

Measurement of metabolites

Ultra-Performance Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (UPLC-MS) with Electrospray Ionization (ESI) was utilized to detect metabolites. Separation was performed by an ACQUITY UPLC (Waters) combined with a Q Exactive Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). The LC system was comprised of an XBridge BEH HILIC column (3.5 μm, 4.6 × 150 mm). The mobile phase was composed of 10 mM Ammonium formate in water (solvent A) or in acetonitrile (solvent B) with a gradient elution (0-1 min, 85% B; 1-12 min, 85%−30% B; 12-13.5 min, 30% B; 13.5-13.6 min, 30%−85% B; 13.6-16 min, 85% B). The flow rate of the mobile phase was 0.65 mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 40°C. The sample manager temperature was set at 4°C. A 5 μL aliquot of each sample was injected into the column.

ESI parameters were set as follows for both ESI+ and ESI- modes: Heater Temp at 300°C, Sheath Gas Flow rate at 45 arbitrary units (arb), Aux Gas Flow Rate at 15 arb, Sweep Gas Flow Rate at 1 arb, and Capillary Temp at 350°C. ESI+ mode had a spray voltage of 3.0 kV and a S-Lens RF Level of 30%, while ESI- mode had a spray voltage of 3.2 kV and a S-Lens RF Level of 60%. The mass scan mode of the machine was set to Full Scan (mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) 70∼1050, Resolution: 70,000) and dd-MS2 (TopN = 10, Resolution: 17,500), and the collision mode was set to HCD.

Data analysis

The raw peak intensity data were obtained and analyzed in-house using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca) (Pang et al., 2024). First, the Statistical Analysis [one factor] module was selected and the raw data was uploaded as a peak intensity table. The peak intensity data was formatted by listing metabolites based on the m/z value and the retention time of the ion signals. Data filtering was performed as recommended for untargeted datasets, utilizing the low-repeatability filter to exclude features with relative standard deviations (RSDs) greater than 20% based on QC samples, the default low-variance filter to filter out 40% based on interquartile range, and the default low-abundance filter of 0%. Next, normalization, transformation, and scaling were performed utilizing normalization by median, log transformation (base 2), and Pareto scaling, respectively. To compare metabolite profiles at the 3 time points, two ordination analyses were performed: Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Partial Least Squares – Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA). Data were then narrowed down for two-group comparisons: D1 vs. D3, D3 vs. D10, and D1 vs. D10. Orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA) was performed on each comparison to expand on PLS-DA by separating orthogonal variation from the predictive component. Variable Importance in the Projection (VIP) scores from OPLS-DA analysis indicated compounds with the most contribution to the predictive model (e.g. VIP ≥ 1.5). Volcano plots, highlighting features passing both a Fold change (FC) analysis threshold of 2.0 (|log2FC| ≥ 1) and a T-test P-value threshold of < 0.05 with FDR correction, were produced for each comparison.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the MetaboAnalyst Functional Analysis [LC-MS] module with D1 and D10 data, repeating the data processing steps described for statistical analysis. Pathway enrichment plots were produced utilizing the mummichog 2.0 algorithm with the Gallus gallus (chicken) KEGG pathway libraries selected and p-value cutoffs corresponding to the top 300 significant peaks (cecum positive: 0.00196, cecum negative: 0.00106, plasma positive: 0.0461, plasma negative: 0.0331). Metabolites from volcano plots and VIP scoring were cross-referenced to a list of aligned compounds provided by Creative Proteomics to identify known compounds with the largest changes between D1 and D10. The aligned compounds data was obtained using Compound Discoverer 3.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) to search against mass spectral libraries, including mzCloud, mzVault, and the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, https://www.hmdb.ca) (Wishart et al., 2022) using molecular weights. Lastly, to discuss selection of sample sizes and study design in future studies based on current data, the Power Analysis module in MetaboAnalyst was used. Steps for all analyses were repeated as necessary to process data from both the positive and negative ion modes and to process both the cecum and plasma datasets.

Results and discussion

Ceca

The PCA showed clear clustering and distance between D1 vs. D3 and D1 vs. D10 samples, in both positive and negative ion mode data (Fig. 1A and 1F, respectively). Statistically, groups were different overall based on PERMANOVA (positive mode: R2 = 0.83, P = 0.01; negative mode: R2 = 0.85, P < 0.01), however, pairwise comparisons (D1 vs. D3, D3 vs. D10, and D1 vs. D10) were not considered significant (adjusted P > 0.05 in both modes). This experiment was an exploratory study, and the lack of significant pairwise results could be due to the limited sample size for ceca (n = 3). Therefore, larger sample sizes are recommended for full-scale studies to improve statistical power in pairwise comparisons. In addition, we observed variation in the D3 samples in the PCA, and a higher sample size could clarify if we should expect consistent clustering of D3 from D10 in a larger population. PLS-DA also showed clear clustering by timepoint, with D1 more distant from D3, while D3 was closer to D10 on the Component 1 axis in both modes (Fig. 1B and 1G). OPLS-DA was performed on pairwise comparisons to separate orthogonal variation from the predictive component, and clear clustering was observed in each comparison (Fig. 1C-E and 1H-J). The predictive component explained greater variation for D1 vs. D3 (positive mode: 59.6%, Fig. 1C; negative mode: 57.5%, Fig. 1H) compared to D3 vs. D10 (positive mode: 26.8%, Fig. 1D; negative mode: 32.6%, Fig. 1I). We hypothesized that greater shifts in cecal metabolites may be observed from D1 to D3 as supported by these visualizations in the current study due to rapid development in the cecal microbiota from D1 to D3 (Ballou et al., 2016).

Ordination analyses showing clustering of metabolite profiles by timepoint post-hatch for A-J) ceca samples (positive ion mode: row 1, negative ion mode: row 2) and K-T) plasma samples (positive ion mode: row 3, negative ion mode: row 4). Each row of plots depicts the following analyses from left to right: PCA (all timepoints), PLS-DA (all timepoints), OPLS-DA (D1 vs. D3), OPLS-DA (D3 vs. D10), and OPLS-DA (D1 vs. D10). X- and y-axis labels indicate the percentage of variation explained by the axis.

At the metabolite feature level, significant shifts in metabolite concentrations occurred from D1 to D3 or D1 to D10, similar to the observation from ordination analyses. The volcano plot incorporating Fold Change (FC) analysis (2.0 threshold) and t-test significance (P < 0.05 with FDR) for positive mode, revealed that out of 2,820 features, 345 features significantly increased and 406 features significantly decreased from D1 to D3 (data not shown). There were no significant changes from D3 to D10 (data not shown). From D1 to D10, 740 features significantly increased and 859 features significantly decreased (Fig. 2A). For negative mode, there were 2,899 features, with 242 features significantly increased and 372 features significantly decreased from D1 to D3 (data not shown). There were no significant changes from D3 to D10 (data not shown). From D1 to D10, 752 features significantly increased and 965 features significantly decreased (Fig. 2C). Large proportions of the significant changes from D1 to D10 (positive mode: 695/1599 [43.5%], negative mode: 571/1715 [33.3%]) overlapped with changes from D1 to D3, showing that many changes began in the first few days of post-hatch development and then persisted or continued until D10.

Volcano plots (left side) showing fold changes (log2FC on x-axis) and t-test P-values (log10 scale on y-axis) in metabolites from D1 to D10 post-hatch for A) ceca samples in positive ion mode, C) ceca samples in negative ion mode, E) plasma samples in positive ion mode, and G) plasma samples in negative ion mode. Significantly increased metabolites (darker red as log2FC increases on positive scale) are located in the top right division of the dotted lines, and significantly decrease metabolites (darker purple as log2FC increases on negative scale) are located in top left division of the dotted lines. Cutoffs for significance were |log2FC| ≥ 1 and a t-test P-value < 0.05 (FDR corrected). The corresponding KEGG pathway enrichment plots (B, D, F, H) are labeled with the top 5 significantly enriched pathways by P-value (log10 scale on y-axis) from mummichog 2.0 analysis using the Gallus gallus pathway library. Enrichment Factor (x-axis) is the ratio of significant metabolite hits in a pathway compared to the number of hits expected by chance.

Pathway enrichment analysis of D1 and D10 samples was performed using the mummichog algorithm and the KEGG pathway library for Gallus gallus. The top 5 significantly enriched pathways based on P(Gamma) values from positive mode were Galactose metabolism (P = 0.002), Starch and sucrose metabolism (P = 0.003), Metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450 (P = 0.004), Steroid biosynthesis (P = 0.004), and Taurine and hypotaurine metabolism (P = 0.005) (Fig. 2B). From negative mode, the top 5 significantly enriched pathways were Taurine and hypotaurine metabolism (P = 0.001), Glutathione metabolism (P = 0.001), Biotin metabolism (P = 0.001), Citrate cycle (TCA cycle) (P = 0.001), and Glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism (P = 0.001) (Fig. 2D). Seven pathways were consistently significant (P < 0.05) in positive and negative mode data, including Metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450, Taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, Pentose and glucuronate interconversions, Tryptophan metabolism, Glutathione metabolism, Inositol phosphate metabolism, and Porphyrin metabolism. Some metabolites identifiable through HMDB had high FC and VIP scores consistent with the pathway enrichment results. 5-Glutamyl-taurine (log2FC = 11.54, P < 0.01, VIP = 2.2, positive mode) levels increased from D1 to D10, likely contributing to taurine metabolism activity. A study has shown that taurine supplementation improved the growth performance, antioxidant capacity, and lipid metabolism of broilers (Han et al., 2020). Oxidized glutathione (log2FC = 9.34, P < 0.01, VIP = 1.91, negative mode) and glutathione (log2FC = 7.15, P = 0.01, VIP = 1.76, negative mode) were enriched at D10 and may play a role in toxic waste disposal (Wishart et al., 2022). Stachyose, a metabolite in the Galactose metabolism pathway, was identified with consistently large increases at D10 in both ion modes (positive mode: log2FC = 8.95, P = 0.01, VIP = 2.01; negative mode: log2FC = 8.93, P < 0.01, VIP = 1.93). Stachyose is an oligosaccharide found in soybeans, and its presence in the cecum is likely explained by the broilers’ diet. When supplemented in certain amounts, stachyose may promote growth, increase numbers of beneficial bacteria, impact nutrient utilization, and reduce unwanted odorous compounds in the cecum, possibly in interaction with aromatic amino acids levels (Fu et al., 2024).

Plasma

The D1, D3, and D10 timepoints showed clear clustering in PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA in both negative and positive mode data (Fig. 1K-T). Groups were statistically different overall based on PERMANOVA using PCA distances (positive mode: R2 = 0.82, P < 0.01; negative mode: R2 = 0.90, P < 0.01). Pairwise comparisons indicated all three groups were significantly different from each other for negative mode data (D1 vs. D3: R2 = 0.82, P = 0.03; D3 vs. D10: R2 = 0.87, P = 0.03; D1 vs. D10: R2 = 0.91, P = 0.03). For positive mode data, D1 and D3 were significantly different to D10 (D3 vs. D10: R2 = 0.75, P = 0.03; D1 vs. D10: R2 = 0.91, P = 0.03), however, there was only a trend of difference between D1 and D3 (R2 = 0.58, P = 0.06). The significant results for pairwise comparisons possibly suggest that the modest increase to 4 plasma samples from D1 and D3 added statistical power compared to our ceca analysis (n = 3). In contrast to the ceca, the predictive component from OPLS-DA explained greater variation for D3 vs. D10 (positive mode: 39.7%, Fig. 1N; negative mode: 41.5%, Fig. 1S) compared to D1 vs. D3 (positive mode: 28.5%, Fig. 1M; negative mode: 32.2%, Fig. 1R). These results may further support that the distance in metabolite profiles from D1 to D3 in the ceca are unique because of rapid development in the cecal microbiota, while changes in plasma metabolite profiles may be expected to occur more gradually.

The volcano plot for positive mode revealed 2 features that significantly increased from D1 to D3, and the other 1489 features had no significant change (data not shown). From D3 to D10, 3 features significantly increased and 31 features significantly decreased (data not shown). From D1 to D10, 157 features significantly increased and 75 features significantly decreased (Fig. 2E). For negative mode, 20 features significantly increased and 23 features significantly decreased from D1 to D3 out of 2,350 features (data not shown). From D3 to D10, 28 features significantly increased and 38 features significantly decreased (data not shown). From D1 to D10, 225 features significantly increased and 141 features significantly decreased (Fig. 2G). These numbers show the longest interval of time was required to observe more significant changes in the plasma, unlike in the ceca where many changes were already significant from D1 to D3.

In pathway enrichment analysis, the top 5 significantly enriched pathways in positive mode were Valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation (P = 0.007); Pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis (P = 0.008); Vitamin B6 metabolism (P = 0.008); Alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism (P = 0.009); and Histidine metabolism (P = 0.009). Overall, these pathways involve amino acids and vitamins which may be involved in major muscle and GIT organ growth during early development. For negative mode, the top 5 significantly enriched pathways were Pentose phosphate pathway (P = 0.001), Primary bile acid biosynthesis (P = 0.001), alpha-Linolenic acid metabolism (P = 0.001), Taurine and hypotaurine metabolism (P = 0.001), and Metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450 (P = 0.001). In 35 day old broilers, alpha-Linoleic acid metabolism and Linoleic acid metabolism have been reported as enriched pathways in comparison of cecal metabolomes in high and low body weight groups, possibly connected to results showing high lipid-associated metabolites in high body weight broilers (Zhang et al., 2023). Lipid and lipid-like molecules are among the most commonly found as plasma metabolites, as shown in a plasma metabolomics study on 7 week old broilers with differing feed efficiency (Su et al., 2022). Ten pathways were significant (P < 0.05) in both positive and negative modes, including Alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism; Histidine metabolism; Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism; Tryptophan metabolism; Pentose phosphate pathway; Purine metabolism; Arginine and proline metabolism; Pentose and glucuronate interconversions; beta-Alanine metabolism; and Taurine and hypotaurine metabolism. In contrast to the increase in 5-Glutamyl-taurine in the ceca, this metabolite decreased from D1 to D10 in the plasma (log2FC = −1.84, P < 0.02, VIP = 1.56, positive mode). An increase of 5-Hydroxy-tryptophan from D1 to D10 (log2FC = 4.70, P = 0.02, VIP = 2.51, positive mode) was consistent with the enrichment of tryptophan metabolism. 5-Hydroxy-tryptophan is an aromatic amino acid naturally produced by tryptophan and is a precursor of the neurotransmitter serotonin, which regulates behavior and intestinal movements (Wishart et al., 2022).

Summary and future research

Visualization via PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA cluster analyses together with fold change analysis on metabolite abundances showed promise in distinguishing metabolite profiles and identifying differential metabolites at multiple timepoints post-hatch. Results showed that we may expect large shifts in metabolite profiles and individual compounds in the ceca very early in development (D1 to D3), while shifts in plasma metabolite profiles may be more gradual, with more shifts in individual compounds over a longer period (D1 to D10). Statistical analysis results on PCA data showed that the multiple group comparison of the timepoints could likely be improved with larger sample sizes. At least 5 samples per group is a minimum recommendation in microbiome statistics communities for PERMANOVA analysis, however, even larger sample sizes can improve power to detect effects of smaller effect size (Kers and Saccenti, 2022). A power analysis is a tool that can be used to estimate required sample sizes from a pilot study or existing data. For example, running the MetaboAnalyst power analysis on our D1 and D3 cecal positive data showed a predicted power of 0.8 and FDR of 0.05 could be achieved by approximately 15 samples per group. Power analyses also supported the idea that shifts in metabolites in the plasma would be more likely observed in longer durations (D1 to D10), with the positive data results suggesting 60 samples per group would be required for a D1 vs. D3 comparison compared to only 20 samples per group for D1 vs. D10. Despite the limited sample sizes, our results showed promise in detecting temporal changes in the ceca and plasma and identifying compounds in the ceca which may be related to the gut microbiota and growth. Further research with integration of metabolomics and microbiota data may be an effective method to determine temporal changes from factors such as diet, infection, and environmental stress. Various studies have characterized the effects of these factors on the gut microbiota, however, a greater understanding of microbial function and metabolite production will be crucial for developing effective solutions to manage poultry health.
