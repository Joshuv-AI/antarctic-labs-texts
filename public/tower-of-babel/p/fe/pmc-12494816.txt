Hepatic fatty acid profile and gut microbiome responses of ducks induced by dietary n-6/n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid ratio
Authors: Zhi Cao, Laidi Wang, Yang Zhang, Guobin Chang, Guohong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOptimizing the dietary fatty acid profile has been recognized as an effective strategy for improving meat quality traits in poultry. The present study aimed to investigate how different dietary n-6/n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) ratios influence hepatic fatty acid composition and gut microbiota in ducks, and to identify key microorganisms involved in gut-liver axis regulation. In this study, Runzhou white-crowned ducks were used as a model to explore the effects of dietary n-3 PUFAs content on liver fatty acid composition and intestinal microbiota, and to screen out key microorganisms that mediate gut-liver axis signaling. A total of 112 28-day old Runzhou white- crowned ducks fed with 2 types of diets (with n-6/n-3 PUFA ratios of 1.97 and 17.37) were randomly divided into 2 treatments with 4 replications (14 ducks per replication) for 28 days. A low n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio significantly increased hepatic n-3 PUFA levels (P < 0.05) while reducing n-6 PUFA synthesis. High-throughput 16S rRNA sequencing showed that n-3 PUFA supplementation altered the microbial composition in both the cecum and colon. It also supported intestinal development by maintaining villus structure and overall gut integrity. At the genus level, Bacteroides were enriched in the cecum (P < 0.05), whereas in the colon, n-3 PUFA increased Campylobacter abundance and reduced Pseudomonas. Using a random forest model, 20 and 50 key microbial taxa were identified in the cecal and colonic communities, respectively. Integration with Spearman correlation analysis revealed strong associations between hepatic n-3 PUFA levels and microbial changes. Notably, Caecibacterium_sporoformans showed a positive correlation with multiple n-3 PUFAs, and its abundance in both the cecum and colon increased significantly (P < 0.05). In summary, a low dietary n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio promoted hepatic n-3 PUFA accumulation, reshaped gut microbial communities, and supported intestinal health, highlighting the gut-liver axis as a key pathway for nutritional regulation in ducks.Key words: Correlation analysis, Ducks, Gut microbiota, N-3polyunsaturated fatty acids, 16S rRNA

Introduction

Duck meat is one of the most widely consumed meat products globally and serves as an important dietary source of protein, lipids, and other nutrients. Compared with conventional red meats such as pork, beef, and lamb, duck meat is particularly rich in n-3 and n-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), which play essential roles in lipid metabolism, anti-inflammatory responses, and the maintenance of cardiovascular and neurological health (Djuričić and Calder, 2021; Schulze et al., 2020). Nutritional studies recommend that the dietary n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio should be maintained between 5 and 10 (Angelakis et al., 2010; Shen et al., 2014). However, the n-6/n-3 ratio in duck meat typically exceeds 15, making it less suitable for long-term consumption. Therefore, optimizing the fatty acid (FA) composition of duck meat has both nutritional and practical significance. Linseed oil, a rich plant-based source of n-3 PUFAs, has been widely applied to modify fatty acid profiles in livestock and poultry products (Lam et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2022). It was shown that adding linseed oil to the diet significantly increased the content of n-3 fatty acids such as α-linolenic acid (ALA), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) in duck meat, and that the expression of genes related to fatty acid transport and binding in muscle tissue was upregulated (Zhang et al., 2023). Among them, the fatty acid transporter gene 1 plays a key role in the process of cellular uptake of n-3 PUFAs (Shehata et al., 2022). It can specifically recognize long-chain fatty acids and promote the entry of exogenous fatty acids into cells under the synergistic action of long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase (Brown et al., 2023; Shehata et al., 2022).

In poultry, the liver is the central organ for fatty acid synthesis, storage, and oxidation, coordinating overall lipid homeostasis. Fat metabolism in the liver mainly comes from three pathways: dietary fat (direct absorption), body fat decomposition and fatty acid synthesis in the liver (synthesis of fatty acids from non-lipid substrates). Poultry hepatocytes possess robust lipogenic capacity, converting non-lipid substrates into saturated fatty acids (SFAs), which can then be enzymatically converted into monounsaturated (MUFAs) and PUFAs (Zaefarian et al., 2019). The liver also stores triglycerides (TGs) and packages them into lipoproteins for distribution to peripheral tissues for energy storage or phospholipid biosynthesis. Typically, exogenous fatty acids are digested and absorbed in the small intestine (Tancharoenrat et al., 2014), and are directly transported to the liver via the portal vein. Subsequently, the fatty acid transporter family uses small transmembrane proteins on hepatocytes to absorb exogenous fatty acids into cells. It should be emphasized that the absorption efficiency of fatty acids in the small intestine is not only affected by the lipid composition of the diet, but also closely related to the intestinal microecological system. Before being absorbed, exogenous fatty acids must first interact with the intestinal microbial environment, in which the intestinal microbiota plays a key role in regulating lipid absorption and host metabolism (Brown et al., 2023).

The gut microbiota is a central regulator of host metabolism. The composition and function of the gut microbiota are dynamic and affected by diet properties such as the amount and composition of lipids. Consequently, dietary lipids can affect host physiology through interactions with the gut microbiota (Schoeler et al., 2019). Poultry gastrointestinal tracts harbor trillions of microbes that are essential for health and growth, contributing to carbohydrate hydrolysis and fermentation, antimicrobial compound production, pathogen suppression, maintenance of intestinal structure, and regulation of energy metabolism (Khan et al., 2020; Shehata et al., 2022). The majority of bacteria associated with ducks have been found in the cecum (Best et al., 2017). Most of the bacteria identified by 16S rRNA community analysis belong to the phyla Bacteroidota and Bacillota (Vasaï et al., 2014), which underpin the association with gut microbiome in ducks and have been linked to health states of individuals (Angelakis et al., 2010). n-3 PUFAs play crucial roles in immune-regulation, cardio-protection and neurodevelopment. Diet composition influences gut microbiota and subsequent fermentation products that impact the host (Shen et al., 2014). Nevertheless, the specific impacts of n-3 PUFAs on the duck gut microbiota and their underlying mechanisms remain largely unexplored. However, the specific impacts of n-3 PUFAs on the duck gut microbiota and their underlying mechanisms remain largely unexplored.

In this study, Runzhou white- crowned ducks were used to examine the effects of diets with different n-6/n-3 PUFA ratios on hepatic fatty acid composition, intestinal morphology, and gut microbiota. By combining Random Forest (RF) modeling with multi-omics correlation analyses, we further identified key microbial taxa that may participate in gut–liver axis signaling. Together, these results provide new insights into how n-3 PUFA composition influences liver fatty acid metabolism and intestinal function, and enhance our understanding of host–microbiota interactions in poultry.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and bird management

All experimental animals and procedures involved in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Yangzhou University (License No. YZUDWSY379-2024).

The feeding experiment was conducted at Tiancheng Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd. in Zhenjiang, China, which provided 112 28-day-old Runzhou white-crowned ducks (average weight 753.35 ± 12.94 g). All birds were randomly divided into two groups (half male and half female) and fed diets with varying n-6/n-3 PUFA ratios. The diets were formulated based on a corn-soybean meal-based basal diet, consistent with the nutritional requirements of broiler ducks. The experimental diets were supplemented with either duck fat or linseed oil. The specific nutrient levels and PUFA compositions are shown in Appendix 1. Linseed oil contains 35.21 % α-linolenic acid (ALA), while duck fat contains 0.67 % ALA. Furthermore, the n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio of the treated (linseed oil) diet was 1.97, significantly lower than the 17.37 of the control diet, ensuring a high n-3 PUFA content in the treated diet. All ducks were fed the same basal diet from 1 to 28 days of age. After 28 days of age, 2 % duck fat was added to the basal diet of the control group poultry, and 2 % linseed oil was added to the basal diet of the treated group poultry. The experimental period was 28 days, from 28 days of age to 56 days of age. Each group had 4 replicates, with 14 birds in each replicate. During the experiment, all birds were kept under the same environmental conditions, with an average temperature of 22 °C and a humidity of 67 %. Each pen was a replicate, with free access to water and food. Experimental date: May 16-June 12, 2024.

Sample collection

At the end of the 28-day feeding trial, six ducks (half male and half female) were randomly selected from each replicate group on June 12, 2024. All ducks were fasted for 12 hours and weighed (BW). They were then electroshocked with a 40V shock at a frequency of 900 Hz for 5 seconds to render them unconscious, and finally sacrificed by severing the carotid artery. The sacrificed ducks were immediately transported to the laboratory for sample collection. From the left lobe of the liver, a tissue sample of approximately 3 × 3 cm was excised, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent hepatic fatty acid analysis. Additionally, a portion of liver tissue measuring approximately 2 × 1 × 1 cm was fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for hematoxylin and eosin staining to assess hepatic lipid deposition. Intestinal content samples were collected from one side of the cecum (Ce) and a portion of the colon (CR) of each duck, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for microbiota analysis. Furthermore, a segment of the cecum and colon was randomly selected, the contents removed, and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for subsequent staining and histological examination. All samples were collected on the same day under identical conditions across all replicate groups to ensure comparability of the data.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining method

Liver and intestinal tissue samples were fixed in 4 % formaldehyde solution for 24h to maintain the integrity of tissue structure and cell morphology. Then, the samples were dehydrated using 70 %, 80 %, 95 % and 100 % ethanol solutions respectively. Xylene solution was used for permeabilization of intestinal and liver tissues, and this step was repeated twice, each time for 5min. The permeabilized tissues were embedded and fixed in paraffin solution, and then made into wax blocks and cut into slices of about 4-6 µm using a microtome. The slices were dried in an environment of 60°C, and then dewaxed and rehydrated in xylene and gradient ethanol (100 %, 100 %, 95 %, 70 %) in sequence. Next, hematoxylin staining solution was used for 3-5 minutes. After washing with distilled water, the samples were rapidly differentiated with 1 % hydrochloric acid-ethanol solution for 30s. The alkaline solution was washed for 5min, and the cell nuclei after this step appeared blue. Finally, eosin staining solution was used for 1-2min. After staining, use gradient ethanol (70 %, 95 %, 100 %) for dehydration and xylene permeabilization again. Add a small amount of neutral gum sealing medium, cover with a coverslip, and observe under a microscope.

Fatty acid analysis

For FA analysis, approximately 100 mg of feed, or liver was weighed and hydrolyzed, extracted, saponified, and methylated. The FA methyl esters were subjected to Trace1310-ISQ gas chromatography and mass spectrometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) using an HP-88 column (100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.20 μm) (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) for fatty acid separation. The column oven temperature was maintained at 100 °C for 15 min after sample injection and was programmed to increase from 100 to 190 °C at 15 °C/min and maintained at 190 °C for 25 min. Then the temperature was increased to 235 °C at a rate of 2.5 °C/min and held for 4 min. The injector and detector temperatures were 240 and 280 °C, respectively. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Fatty acids were identified and quantified by comparing the retention times and peak areas with those of standard components (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). The results were recorded as a percentage of fresh feed or breast muscle.

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

For cecal content samples, microbial genomic DNA was extracted using QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the instructions of kits. Then, NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, NC) was used to detect DNA concentration, andagarose gel electrophoresis was performed to detect DNA quality. DNA concentration of each sample was diluted to 10 ng/ul using ddH2O. The specific primers (338F: 5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′; 806R: 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) were performed to amplify the V3-V4 region of 16S rDNA. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA) by Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), which generated 250 bp paired-end reads.

Paired-end reads were merged using fast length adjustment (FLASH) (V1.2.7). Quality filtering on the raw reads was performed under specific filtering conditions to obtain the high-quality clean tags according to the quantitative insights into microbial ecology (QIIME) (V1.7.0). Chimeric sequences were filtered using undetected chimeras (UCHIME) algorithm. We obtained the feature table and feature sequence. Alpha diversity and beta diversity were calculated by normalizing to the same sequences randomly. The representative sequence was used to annotate the taxonomic data based on the GreenGene Database. Then feature abundance was normalized using the relative abundance of each sample. Alpha diversity is applied for analyzing the complexity of species diversity for a sample through 5 indices, including Chao1, Observed species, Goods coverage, Shannon, Simpson, and all these indices were calculated using QIIME (version 1.7.0). Beta diversity was calculated by QIIME (version 1.7.0), the graphs were drawn by R package. Blast was used for sequence alignment, and the feature sequences was annotated with SILVA database foreach representative sequence. Other diagrams were implementedusing the Software (version 2.15.3). To investigate the similarity between different samples, clustering analysis was performed using the unweighted UniFrac method to construct a hierarchical clustering tree. The Metastat method was applied for preliminary exploration of microbial abundance differences between and within groups. The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) algorithm was performed using the non-parametric factorial Kruskal–Wallissum-rank test with P < 0.05. LDA score (>2.5) was used to examine the differences in bacterial abundance between groups. PICRUSt was used to standardize the abundance of operational taxonomic units (OTUs), and each standardized OTU was mapped to the corresponding gene evolution tree to obtain the corresponding KEGG information.

Random forest model

All analysis processes for RF model construction were implemented using R (v4.3.2) software, and receiver operating characteristic curves were generated for each machine model. The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70 %) and a test set (30 %) for model training and validation. The number of trees was set to 500 according to experimental requirements. RF analysis was based on a tree-based ensemble method, and feature importance was assessed by mean reduction impurity (Gini index). The stability and reliability of the model were evaluated using different numbers of important features. As the number of important features increased, the model prediction ability tended to stabilize.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normal distribution of the data, and Levene’s test was applied to examine homogeneity of variance. When the data met both normality and homogeneity assumptions, differences between two groups were analyzed using independent sample t-tests; otherwise, non-parametric tests were employed. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with 24 biological replicates per group. Spearman’s rank correlation test was used to evaluate associations among gut microbial species, body weight, and hepatic fatty acid composition. Data visualization was carried out using GraphPad Prism 9 and R software (v4.3.2). * P < 0.05; ** P < 0.01; *** P < 0.001.

Results

Dietary n-6/n-3 PUFAs ratio affected hepatic fatty acid profiles in ducks

To analyze the effects of dietary n-6/n-3 PUFAs ratio on hepatic fatty acid profiles, we measured the changes in the compositions of fatty acids. As shown in Table 1, the decreased dietary n-6/n-3 PUFAs ratio led to a significant increase in total fatty acid (P < 0.01), SFAs (P < 0.05), PUFAs (P < 0.01), and n-3 PUFAs (P < 0.01). And n-6/n-3 PUFAs in duck liver was significantly decreased from 14.3 to 4.28 (P < 0.01). Liver HE staining results showed that a high-content n-3PUFAs diet promoted fat deposition in the liver (Fig. 1A). However, there was no significant difference in the total amount of MUFAs and n-6PUFAs (P > 0.05), and supplementing more n-3PUFAs did not affect the body weight of ducks. Next, we further explored the changes in n-3 and n-6PUFAs families. Specifically, feeding an n-3PUFAs diet significantly promoted the deposition of EPA, DHA, and ALA (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1B), while inhibiting the synthesis of the n-6PUFAs family, including dihomo-γ-linoleic acid (DGLA) and arachidonic acid (ARA) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1B). It is worth noting that we observed that a high n-3PUFAs diet instead promoted the synthesis of linoleic acid (LA) in the n-6 family (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1B), thereby disturbing the balance between n-3/n-6. This result suggests that the effect of n-3 PUFAs supplementation on the n-3/n-6 ratio has a dynamic change characteristic.

The alpha complexity of the gut microbiome

Dietary PUFAs are absorbed in the small intestine via passive diffusion or lipid transporter–mediated mechanisms. In this study, a high n-3 PUFA diet improved intestinal villus development and preserved structural integrity (Fig. 2A), while appropriate PUFA supplementation promoted intestinal epithelial cell proliferation (Fig. 2B). These findings suggest that n-3 PUFAs contribute to reducing intestinal permeability and limiting pathogen invasion. To further determine whether these effects are associated with modulation of gut microbial diversity and abundance, we performed 16S rRNA sequencing of cecal and colonic microbial communities. At the same time, after appropriately merging the raw data and filtering low-quality sequences, a total of 3,969,261 clean reads were obtained from the two groups of samples, and clustered into 8152 OTUs according to the 97 % sequence similarity standard (Supplementary Table 1 and 2). Rarefaction curves and rank abundance curves tended to saturation platforms, indicating that the microbiota of the two groups of samples were large enough to estimate the phenotypic richness and microbial community diversity (Fig. 2C and Fig S1). To assay the α-diversity of cecal microbiota, Chao 1 and Observed were employed for the richness of microbial community, and Shannon and Simpson were examined for diversity of the microbial community. As compared with the control diet, lower dietary n-6/n-3 PUFAs ratio conspicuously declined Observed and Chao 1 estimators (P < 0.001), while there were no significant differences in Shannon and Simpson indices (Fig. 2D). However, we observed that supplementation with n-3 unsaturated fatty acids significantly reduced Simpson's values in the CR group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2E).

The top 10 phylum level and top 10 genus level in the Ce and CR are presented in Fig. 2F and 2G. Members of the phyla Bacteroidota, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Spirochaetota were the most abundant in Ce, collectively accounting for over 80 % of the microbiome. The overall abundance of the microbiome remained relatively stable at both the phylum and genus levels under different treatments. However, in the CR microbiome, in addition to Bacteroidota, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Spirochaetota, Campylobacterota also showed a high abundance, and its relative abundance further increased after high n-3PUFA treatment (Fig. 2G). In addition, from the perspective of samples within the group, the differences between samples in the Ce group were small, while the abundance of the microbiome between samples in the CR group fluctuated greatly (Fig. H). This also explains that although linseed oil treatment caused a significant change in the relative abundance of CR microbiota, there was no statistical difference in α-diversity between the two groups.

The beta complexity of the gut microbiome

β-diversity analysis is used to measure the differences in microbial composition between different samples or groups. Here, we observed significant changes in the gut microbiota structure after n-3PUFAs treatment, including Ce and CR (Fig. 3A). As shown in Fig. 3B, although these groups shared more than 50 % of OTUs, they still retained their own unique microbiota characteristics. Similarly, CCe and HCe showed different clustering patterns after PCA dimensionality reduction, and their distances on the two-dimensional plane intuitively demonstrated the community differences between samples (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the clustering patterns of the CCR and HCR groups showed certain similarities. However, despite the short straight-line distance in the PCA plot, this similarity did not mask the microbiota differences between the two groups, and the effect of n-3 PUFAs treatment on the CR microbiota structure still existed. Therefore, in order to evaluate the similarity between different samples, we performed cluster analysis on the microbial communities of the Ce and CR groups and constructed a sample clustering tree (Fig. 3D). The results showed that compared with Ce, the changes in the microbial community in the CR group before and after treatment were more significant, with a cluster tree distance of 0.16, mainly reflected in Campylobacterota and Proteobacteria. The changes in the microbial community in the Ce group were mainly concentrated in Firmicutes. Further, we used MetaStat analysis to analyze the effects of n-3PUFAs treatment on microbial abundance at the genus level, revealing significant differences in specific microbial communities under different treatment conditions (Fig. 3E and Fig. S2).

Significant variations in microbial diversity between groups

Next, we used the Kruskal-Wallisem algorithm to identify taxa that changed significantly between groups, covering the phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species levels. The stability of these taxa within the group was further verified by the Wilcoxon rank sum test. In addition, we used LDA to evaluate the contribution of these significantly different bacterial communities to the microbial community structure between groups to identify the key differential bacterial communities. The results showed that with the increase of n-3PUFA in the diet, the abundance of Bacteroides_plebeius, Brachyspirales, Brachyspiraceae, Brachyspira, and Brachyspirae in the Ce group increased significantly (Fig. 4A), and these changes were mainly concentrated in Bacteroidota and Spirochaetota. In contrast, the changes in the microbial community in the CR group were mainly reflected in the enrichment of Campylobacterota, including Campylobacter_canadensis, Campylobacteraceae, and Campylobacter (Fig. 4B).

The analysis showed that the intake of n-3PUFA mainly affected the fatty acid metabolism of the microbial community, among which the two metabolic pathways of Long-chain fatty acid transport protein and Lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis changed significantly in both the Ce group and the CR group.

Identification of key microbial species using random forest model

Based on 16S rRNA sequencing data, we performed RF analysis to identify key taxa driving changes in gut microbial communities under different treatment conditions. In the Ce group, the area ubder the curve (AUC) value gradually increased with the increase in the number of features and stabilized at 20 feature factors (Fig. 5A), indicating that the model had achieved the best discrimination ability at this time. In addition, the cross-validation error rate also decreased with the increase in feature factors, indicating the importance of these key features in the classification task. Based on this analysis, we finally selected 20 feature factors to construct the classification model, and the results showed that the AUC value reached 100 % at this time, indicating that the model performance had reached the best state (Fig. 5B). Fig. 5C shows the top 10 taxa of these feature factors (Supplementary Table 3). In the CR group, the impact of different numbers of feature factors on the model prediction ability showed greater volatility. The AUC value stabilized after 50 feature factors (Fig. 5D), and the cross-validation error rate dropped to the lowest level (Fig. 5E), indicating that the model avoided the interference of redundant features while maintaining the classification ability. The final RF model reached 72.81 % ∼ 100 % within the 95 % confidence interval, indicating that its predictive ability has strong robustness. Finally, we showed the 10 key microbial species that contributed the most to the classification model of the CR group (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 4).

Correlation analysis between microbiota and polyunsaturated fatty acids

To clarify how n-3 PUFAs mediate changes in hepatic fatty acid profiles through the regulation of intestinal microbiota, thereby achieving metabolic modulation of the gut–liver axis. We performed Spearman correlation analysis based on the relative abundance of bacterial genera, together with sex, body weight, and hepatic fatty acid composition (Fig. 6A). The results showed that BW, LA, ALA, EPA, DHA, and hepatic n-3 PUFA levels were all closely associated with the intestinal microbiota. In the Ce group, these fatty acids were significantly correlated with Campylobacterota, Actinobacteriota, Euryarchaeota, Desulfobacterota, and Firmicutes (P < 0.05). By contrast, in the CR group, the intestinal microbiota was mainly correlated with hepatic DGLA, ARA, ALA, EPA, and DHA levels (P < 0.05). Fig. 6B presents the top 10 key microbial species in the CR group and their relationships with DGLA, ARA, ALA, EPA, and DHA. By integrating Pearson correlation, Mantel correlation, and Mantel P values, the analysis revealed that Caecibacterium sporoformans and Weissella cibaria were significantly positively correlated with DHA, EPA, and ALA. Notably, the relative abundance of Caecibacterium sporoformans was markedly elevated following n-3 PUFA intervention (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6C).

(D) Correlation between key bacterial groups in the cecum and body weight, LA, total n-3 PUFAs, ALA, EPA, and DHA. Similarly, we analyzed the top 20 characteristic microorganisms in the Ce group and evaluated their correlations with BW, LA, ALA, EPA, DHA, and hepatic n-3 PUFA levels (Fig. 6D). The results showed that eight microorganisms were significantly associated with LA, ALA, EPA, DHA, and n-3 PUFAs (Fig. 6E). Among them, Caecibacterium sporoformans, Corynebacterium xerosis, Campylobacter canadensis, Gallibacterium anatis, and Bacterium 'New Zealand D' were positively correlated with hepatic n-3 PUFAs (ALA, EPA, and DHA) (P < 0.05), and their relative abundance significantly increased after n-3 PUFA treatment (P < 0.01). Importantly, in both the CR and Ce groups, Caecibacterium sporoformans showed a significant increase in abundance following n-3 PUFA intervention. This shift may indirectly promote hepatic n-3 PUFA deposition by reshaping the intestinal microbial community.

In summary, these findings further demonstrate the role of n-3 PUFAs in the metabolic regulation of the gut–liver axis and identify Caecibacterium sporoformans as a potential key mediator influencing hepatic fatty acid metabolism through modulation of the intestinal microbiota (Fig. 7).

Discussion

This study examined the effects of dietary n-6/n-3 PUFA ratios on liver fatty acid composition and gut microbiota in ducks. Lowering the dietary n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio had no significant impact on body weight, consistent with previous reports in broilers and laying hens, where n-3 PUFA supplementation showed limited effects on growth performance (Mašek et al., 2014). In contrast, liver fatty acid composition was markedly influenced by dietary fatty acid profiles. Ducks fed the low n-6/n-3 PUFA diet had significantly higher hepatic levels of n-3 PUFAs, including ALA, EPA, and DHA, while hepatic n-6 PUFA levels remained largely unchanged. These results indicate that dietary n-3 PUFA enrichment effectively promotes n-3 PUFA deposition in the liver and lowers the hepatic n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio. These findings indicate that a diet enriched in n-3 PUFAs can effectively enhance the deposition of n-3 PUFAs in duck liver and reduce the hepatic n-6/n-3 PUFAs ratio. This is consistent with previous studies in broiler chickens (Lam et al., 2015), laying hens (Kuda et al., 2000) and Peking ducks (Caesar et al., 2015), all of which showed that n-3 PUFA supplementation increased n-3 PUFAs levels in liver and muscle while reducing the n-6/n-3 PUFAs ratio.

The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in regulating host energy metabolism, lipid absorption, and immune function (Zhu et al., 2020). In this study, 16S rRNA sequencing was used to assess the effects of dietary n-6/n-3 PUFA ratios on the gut microbiota of ducks. Dietary n-3 PUFA supplementation supported intestinal villus development and maintained gut structural integrity. After reducing the n-6/n-3 PUFAs ratio in the diet, the α-diversity of the intestinal flora decreased. However, β-diversity analysis showed that there were significant differences in the composition of the flora between the two groups. The cecal microbiota was dominated by Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, accounting for over 80 % of the total community. In ducks fed a low n-6/n-3 PUFAs ratio diet, the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes significantly increased, whereas Firmicutes significantly decreased. Previous studies have indicated that the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio is a key indicator of gut microbial homeostasis, with a higher F/B ratio often associated with obesity, inflammation, and metabolic disorders, while a lower F/B ratio is linked to improved metabolic health (Yang et al., 2022). In this study, the reduction in the F/B ratio in the low n-6/n-3 PUFAs group suggests that n-3 PUFAs may improve host lipid metabolism and energy balance by modulating gut microbial composition (Wu et al., 2020). At the genus level, the abundance of Bacteroides increased significantly after n-3 PUFAs supplementation in the diet, while the abundance of Lactobacillus decreased significantly. Bacteroides is one of the most important Gram-negative bacteria in the intestine. It can decompose complex carbohydrates and produce short-chain fatty acids, such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate, and plays an important role in maintaining intestinal health (Neijat et al., 2020). On the other hand, Lactobacillus is a typical probiotic that can produce lactic acid and short-chain fatty acids, inhibit the colonization of pathogens, and enhance host immune function (Sheh et al., 2020). This study found that a low n-6/n-3 PUFAs ratio led to a decrease in the abundance of cecal Lactobacillus, which may be related to the inhibitory effect of n-3 PUFAs on intestinal flora. However, the specific mechanism of the effect of n-3 PUFAs on probiotics still needs further study (Brennan et al., 2019).

Notably, we observed that dietary supplementation with n-3 PUFAs led to a significant increase in the abundance of Campylobacterota in the colon, alongside a marked decrease in Proteobacteria. Campylobacterota, a conditional pathogen, has been associated with intestinal inflammation and host immune responses (Wu et al., 2017), and plays a role in maintaining mucosal function and regulating host metabolism (Awad et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2024). The enrichment of Campylobacterota in the treatment group may reflect the reshaping of gut microbial structure by n-3 PUFAs, potentially promoting the colonization of certain beneficial taxa. In contrast, Proteobacteria is often considered a marker of dysbiosis and inflammation (Hughes et al., 2017; Shin et al., 2015). The significant reduction of Proteobacteria following n-3 PUFAs supplementation suggests that these fatty acids may contribute to the restoration of gut microbial homeostasis by suppressing potentially pathogenic bacteria (Costantini et al., 2017). Collectively, these findings imply that n-3 PUFAs may influence host intestinal immune function indirectly through modulation of the gut microbiota (Parker et al., 2020).

To identify key gut microbial taxa associated with n-3 PUFA supplementation, we applied a Random Forest (RF) model for feature selection based on intestinal microbial composition. As a supervised ensemble learning algorithm built from multiple decision trees, the RF model is well-suited for high-dimensional biological datasets, effectively reducing overfitting and allowing quantitative assessment of feature importance (Fan et al., 2019; Phan and Tomasino, 2021). Using this approach, we identified 20 and 50 key microbial taxa in the cecal and colonic communities, respectively, which exhibited strong discriminatory power between the treatment and control groups, suggesting their potential involvement in or response to dietary n-3 PUFA supplementation. Further, we explored the relationship between intestinal flora and liver PUFAs composition. The results showed that BW, LA and n-3 PUFA (ALA, EPA, DHA) levels were significantly correlated with the abundance of cecal flora. Previous studies have shown that the intestinal microbial ecosystem plays a key role in the host lipid metabolism process (Wu et al., 2023a), and dietary lipids mediate fatty acid metabolic balance through microbial lipases, short-chain fatty acids and the gut-liver axis (Wu et al., 2023b). In addition, n-3 PUFAs can shape the intestinal microecological environment by changing the physical and chemical properties of intestinal morphology and mucosal structure, thereby affecting the composition and diversity of the flora(Xu et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). At the same time, n-3 PUFAs may also promote the colonization of certain beneficial flora by directly inhibiting the proliferation of some Gram-negative bacteria (Bellenger et al., 2019; Tian et al., 2023). It is worth noting that the colon flora is not only significantly correlated with n-3 PUFAs (ALA, EPA, DHA), but also closely related to n-6 PUFAs (DGLA and ARA). This result suggests that some intestinal flora may be involved in the metabolic regulation of n-3 and n-6 fatty acids at the same time. Studies have shown that n-6 PUFAs can indirectly regulate the ecological structure of the intestinal flora by regulating the immune status and barrier function of the intestinal mucosa (Magalhães et al., 2022; Pinchaud et al., 2022).

Building on these observations, we integrated correlations between intestinal microbial abundance and liver n-3 PUFA levels (ALA, EPA, DHA) to identify key taxa potentially mediating gut–liver axis signaling. The results revealed that Caecibacterium sporoformans showed significant positive correlations with multiple n-3 PUFAs in both the cecum and colon, and its relative abundance was markedly increased in the treatment group. Originally isolated from the chicken cecum, Caecibacterium sporoformans is a butyrate-producing, spore-forming bacterium (Onrust et al., 2017). Although phylogenetically classified within the Firmicutes phylum and Clostridium cluster XIVa, it displays Gram-negative staining, indicating atypical cell wall structures and unique physiological features. Caecibacterium sporoformans uses butyrate as its main metabolite. Studies have shown that butyrate has advantages in maintaining intestinal epithelial barrier function, regulating mucosal immune response and inhibiting the proliferation of potential pathogens (El‐Saadony et al., 2022; Zha et al., 2023). Importantly, butyrate has been shown to regulate hepatic lipid metabolism and improve lipid distribution via activation of the LKB1-AMPK-Insig signaling pathway (Zhao et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2023). It also enhances liver mitochondrial function, promoting mitochondrial respiration and fatty acid oxidation, thereby alleviating hepatic lipid metabolic disorders (Carotenuto et al., 2017). Therefore, we speculate that Caecibacterium sporoformans may play a bridging role in the gut-liver axis by synergistically regulating the local intestinal environment and liver lipid metabolism through its metabolite butyrate.

In summary, lowering the dietary n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio can markedly enhance hepatic n-3 PUFA composition and may influence host lipid metabolism and immune function by modulating intestinal microbiota. However, the precise mechanisms through which n-3 PUFAs affect the gut microbiota, and the specific role of Caecibacterium sporoformans in the gut–liver axis, remain unclear. Future studies integrating metagenomics, metabolomics, and host transcriptomics are needed to elucidate how n-3 PUFAs regulate gut–liver axis metabolism, which could inform the optimization of poultry diets and improve the nutritional quality of poultry products.

Conclusion

This study systematically revealed the interaction between dietary n-3 PUFAs, liver lipid metabolism, and intestinal microbiota in ducks, directly addressing our objective of clarifying the nutritional regulation mechanism underlying the intestinal–liver axis. We demonstrated that reducing the dietary n-6/n-3 PUFAs ratio significantly enhanced hepatic n-3 PUFA deposition while inhibiting n-6 PUFA synthesis. Furthermore, dietary n-3 PUFA supplementation reshaped the intestinal microbiota, improved villus development and mucosal integrity, and markedly enriched beneficial taxa such as Bacteroides in the cecum and Campylobacter in the colon. Importantly, based on RF modeling and correlation analysis, we identified Caecibacterium sporoformans as a potential key mediator positively associated with multiple n-3 PUFAs across intestinal segments, suggesting its critical role in transmitting signals along the intestinal–liver metabolic axis. Collectively, these findings highlight a mechanistic link between dietary fatty acid composition, gut microbiota modulation, and hepatic lipid metabolism, providing both new biological insights and a theoretical basis for optimizing poultry diets and improving meat quality.
