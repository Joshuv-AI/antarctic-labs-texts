Caregiver burden in owners of dogs with chronic enteropathies—a survey-based assessment
Authors: Clara Stumpf, Constanze Pape, Yury Zablotski, Kathrin Busch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic enteropathy (CE) in dogs requires lifelong management and may cause caregiver burden (CB), potentially affecting owner well-being and, indirectly, veterinarians.Hypothesis/ObjectivesWe aimed to determine whether owners of dogs with CE experience CB compared to owners of healthy dogs and to assess emotional, social, and financial aspects, as well as the owner–dog relationship.AnimalsThe questionnaire was completed by 223 owners of dogs with CE and 447 owners of healthy dogs.MethodsCross-sectional study using an owner-reported questionnaire. Items were adapted from the Zarit Burden Interview and the Monash Dog Owner Relationship Scale. Disease severity was assessed using the canine inflammatory bowel disease activity index.ResultsOwners of dogs with CE reported substantial psychological burden and lower quality of life (QoL) for themselves compared with controls. More frequent episodes of severe clinical signs were associated with CB and decreased QoL. Despite this, the owner–dog relationship remained largely intact with most owners maintaining emotional closeness and attachment to the dog. Contributing factors to CB included veterinary visits, the perception of dogs with CE as time-consuming, and financial factors. However, concerns for the dog’s well-being—such as suffering, recurrence of severe clinical signs, or death—were ranked as the most important source of burden.Conclusion and clinical importanceChronic enteropathy can cause substantial CB and negatively affect owners’ QoL, while the emotional bond with the dog remains preserved. These findings highlight the importance of considering caregiver well-being as part of veterinary care.Keywords: dog–owner relationship, financial strain, quality of life, social burden, well-being

Introduction

Chronic enteropathy (CE) is characterized by persistent or intermittent clinical signs such as vomiting, diarrhea, borborygmi, hyporexia, abdominal pain, and weight loss, lasting longer than 3 weeks.1 Because CE is incurable, management generally requires lifelong administration of specific diets, nutritional supplements, and, if necessary, immunomodulatory medications. Despite intensive therapeutic measures, flare-ups or progressive deterioration still may occur.2,3

Caregiver burden (CB) lacks a universally accepted definition, with numerous perspectives regarding this concept.4 Broadly, it refers to the negative psychological impact experienced by individuals who provide care for a loved one or an animal in need.4 Caregiver burden can have multiple consequences on the mental health of the caregiver. Increasing evidence suggests that CB can lead to anxiety and even burnout.5 Several studies have reported CB in various chronic or terminal conditions, including chronic dermatologic, oncologic, and neurologic diseases.6–8 The impact of CE on owners’ quality of life (QoL) and the dog–owner relationship has already been examined.9,10

Caregiver burden may affect people who interact with the caregiver, such as the treating veterinarian. Some individuals have a tendency to externalize their stress, often by attributing blame to others or external circumstances. This phenomenon, known as burden transfer, represents a common coping mechanism for managing negative emotions.11 In these situations, the veterinarian may become the target of this transferred burden. In addition to their regular workload, veterinarians must also manage emotional demands of the owners, which requires strong communication skills. This dynamic can lead to unbillable veterinary services and additional hours worked, which, in turn, may negatively impact the veterinarian’s own well-being.12–14 Another consequence can be compassion fatigue.15 This term refers to the emotional exhaustion that arises from feeling deep empathy for the suffering of others and becoming overly involved in their situation.15 Compassion fatigue is a well-known concern among healthcare providers, particularly veterinarians, that can strongly affect their mental health.15,16

Although veterinarians primarily care for animals, they also bear a responsibility toward the owners. Whether by changing stressful aspects of care or shifting the perspective on them, veterinarians can address CB and decrease the owner’s burden. Doing so, in return, might strengthen the relationship between the owner and the veterinarian.

We hypothesize that CE in dogs is associated with CB in their owners. Our aim was to compare the emotional, social, and financial aspects of dog ownership as well as the owner–dog relationship between owners of dogs with CE and owners of healthy dogs, and to analyze whether CB is present and what aspects differ between the groups. Additionally, we sought to identify the most burdensome aspects of the disease that may contribute to CB, providing veterinarians with valuable insights on how to better support owners in managing CB.

Materials and methods

Study population

The study was approved in February 2024 by the ethics committee of the veterinary faculty of the Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität (LMU) München (File number 391-28-01-2024). This questionnaire study was a prospective, cross-sectional study, aimed at owners of dogs with CE and owners of healthy dogs. It was published via the survey portal SoSci and was available from February 7 to September 30, 2024.

Only fully completed questionnaires from participants over the age of 18 years were included in the analysis. Questionnaires submitted by owners of dogs with other chronic diseases were excluded. Participation was voluntary, and all owners agreed to the anonymous publication of their data.

The questionnaire consisted of 2 sections. The first part was addressed to all participants. At the end of this first part, the owners were asked to indicate whether the dog was healthy, was affected by CE or by another chronic disease. The health status was owner-reported and not confirmed by a veterinarian. For owners of healthy dogs as well as those whose dogs suffered from a chronic condition other than CE, the questionnaire ended at this point. Owners of dogs with CE were directed to the second part, which contained specific questions regarding different aspects of the disease.

Signalment

We collected demographic data from the owners (age, sex) and the dogs (age, sex, breed).

The canine inflammatory bowel disease activity index (CIBDAI) was used to assess the severity of the disease.17 For control purposes, the CIBDAI parameters also were assessed in the healthy dogs (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

The obtained data was statistically analyzed using SPSS 29.0 and R. Likert scale items are ordinal by definition; therefore, nonparametric tests were used for inferential analyses. Means and SDs additionally are reported as descriptive summaries consistent with established practice in Likert scale research.20,21 To assess normal distribution, the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were applied. Because all data were not normally-distributed, nonparametric tests were applied. Group comparisons were conducted using χ-squared test, Fisher’s exact test, and Mann–Whitney U test. Potential associations between variables were examined using Spearman’s correlation for relationships between numeric variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis Test to compare continuous variables across categorical groups. Ordinal ranking data (1-9) were analyzed using the Friedman test. A significance level of P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Evaluating caregiver burden

Emotional aspects of dog ownership

We identified several significant differences in responses regarding the emotional aspects of dog ownership. Owners of dogs with CE reported significantly more often than owners of healthy dogs that their pet was a psychological burden and that it negatively impacted their QoL (Figures 4 and 5). Additionally, these owners were less likely to consider getting a dog again and imagined having a dog would be more positive. Dog–owner relationship and social aspects

There were notable differences, particularly regarding the social aspects of dog ownership. Owners of dogs with CE tended to be more stressed when other people cared for their dog, were more likely to state that their dog is time-consuming and perceived visits to the veterinarian as more burdensome.

For questions regarding the dog-owner relationship, no significant differences were observed between the groups, with one exception: Owners of dogs with CE sought less comfort from their dog when feeling unwell compared with owners of healthy dogs (Table 11). Only 6.2% of owners of dogs with CE stated that their relationship to the dog deteriorated because of the disease (Figure 6).

Ranking of aspects regarding the extent of burden

In general, aspects related to the dog’s well-being and the severity of the disease are of highest importance to owners of dogs with CE. The concern that the dog suffers from the disease was ranked as one of the top 3 concerns by 83.5% of owners. It therefore represents the most relevant factor in CB. In contrast, financial and logistical issues tended to occupy middle to lower ranks. Nearly 1 in 5 owners did not include these aspects in their ranking (Figures 10 and 11, Table S2).

Discussion

Dogs in general can have a positive psychological impact on the well-being of their owners.22 As the social value of dogs continues to increase, understanding the impact of a dog’s disease on the owner, particularly in the case of CE, becomes increasingly important.

Regarding the signalment, we found that dogs with CE were significantly older, which was not unexpected. Because the disease usually develops gradually, it takes time for a diagnosis to be made. This factor explains the underrepresentation of dogs younger than one year of age in the CE group. The observed difference in age reflects a natural distribution in the population, because older dogs are more likely to develop chronic diseases whereas healthy dogs are typically younger. We also found that dogs with CE were significantly more frequently purebred compared with healthy dogs. However, other studies have not reported this finding.10 Although some breeds, such as Miniature Poodles, West Highland White, and Border Terriers, frequently represented in our study have been described as predisposed, a general overrepresentation of purebred dogs has not been previously reported.23

The CIBDAI indicated a difference between ill and healthy dogs, which is not surprising because it is a score used to classify CE. The CIBDAI was significantly higher in dogs with CE. Unexpectedly, 34 (7.5%) apparently healthy dogs had a CIBDAI between 4 and 7, indicating mild to moderate disease. This result may be linked to the occurrence of acute gastroenteritis at the time the owner completed the questionnaire. Alternatively, it raises the possibility that some owners may not recognize their dog’s chronic gastrointestinal clinical signs.

Non-clinically relevant scores in dogs with CE are anticipated, because the disease often manifests in episodes, reflecting the possibility of a stable condition at the time of assessment. However, this observation does not imply that there is no potential CB, because knowledge about the diagnosis and anticipation of future clinical signs may itself contribute to burden.

The most common instrument to assess burden in caregivers of human patients is the Zarit Burden Interview.19 This 29-item self-report questionnaire for caregivers includes statements that can be categorized in 5 areas: caregiver’s physical health, psychological and emotional well-being, the relationship between the caregiver and the impaired individual, social aspects, and finances.19 The answer to each statement contributes to a total score, which reflects the extent of burden experienced by the caregiver.

Although attempts have been made to adapt the Zarit burden score to veterinary medicine, we intentionally chose not to use validated questionnaires and scores.24 Instead, we based our statements on 4 categories used by the Zarit Burden interview to capture a broader and more nuanced picture of the various manifestations of CB in owners of dogs with CE. Caregiver physical health was not evaluated, because we assumed that although physical health may decline as a consequence of other issues, there is no primary physical burden associated with caring for a dog with CE.

Our goal was to encourage as many dog owners as possible to complete the questionnaire, which is why we kept the number of questions as low as possible. Validated scores in this context attempt to objectify subjective feelings. However, because this approach may result in the loss of information or a misrepresentation of the owner’s actual experience, we asked questions directly and purposefully.

We found that owners of dogs with CE perceived their dogs more likely as a psychological burden compared with owners of healthy dogs. This finding highlights the presence of a subjective CB. Various studies have shown that CB exists among owners of dogs with several chronic conditions such as skin disease, osteoarthritis, canine cognitive dysfunction, and cancer.6,7,25–28 In dogs with CE, owners’ reports of CB did not correlate with disease severity measured by CIBDAI. In contrast, a higher frequency of severe clinical signs was significantly associated with more frequent reports of psychological burden by these owners, whereas disease duration showed no correlation.

Our results show that owners of dogs with CE have a lower QoL because of the disease, which is consistent with previous studies.10,14 In addition, we found that more frequent episodes of severe clinical signs were associated with a larger perceived decrease in QoL.

Given these findings, it is not surprising that owners of healthy dogs perceived dog ownership more positively than did owners of dogs with CE. As the frequency of severe clinical signs increases, owners are more likely to agree with this sentiment. The owners are also less inclined to want another dog, and they are not happier since having a dog compared with owners of healthy dogs. The more frequently severe clinical signs occurred, the lower was the perceived happiness about having a dog. Nearly two-thirds of owners (65.5%) stated that their life would be easier with a healthy dog. Administering medications and supplements was a stressful aspect of the disease for only 17.1% of the owners of dogs with CE.

The dog–owner relationship did not appear to be significantly affected by the presence of disease in most cases. Owners in both groups expressed a desire to organize their day such that they can spend as much time as possible with their dogs, confide in their dogs in ways they might not with humans, and think about their dogs when they are apart to a similar extent. They talk about their dogs just as frequently in social settings and seek the same level of emotional attachment. The only notable difference was that owners of dogs with CE were less likely to seek comfort from their dogs when feeling unwell themselves. One possible explanation is that these owners perceive a responsibility to maintain strength within the relationship, rather than drawing additional emotional support from their dogs.

Social aspects differed between the groups. Owners of dogs with CE were more stressed when others care for their dogs, which may lead them to cancel plans so as to be able to provide care for the dogs, potentially decreasing social interactions. Additionally, owners of dogs with CE considered their pets more time-consuming than owners of healthy dogs. In some cases, treatment demands frequent feedings (even at night) to prevent vomiting or the dog requires multiple walks because of frequent defecation and diarrhea.14 Such demands can decrease social interaction, a well-established risk factor for the development of depression.29 Veterinary visits are another factor that contributes to the burden. These visits may be stressful because of the dogs’ discomfort in the clinic environment, the owners’ fear of receiving negative news, or the financial strain associated with repeated consultations. These factors also may explain why owners perceived their dogs as time consuming.

Owners in both groups were asked about financial aspects of dog ownership. As expected, owners of dogs with CE were significantly more likely to perceive their dogs’ expenses as excessive compared with owners of healthy dogs. Besides the costs for veterinary visits and medication, treatment requires special diets, which are considerably more expensive than standard commercial dog food.17 Although 17.4% of owners of dogs with CE spent more than 200€ per month on dog food, only 4.2% of owners with healthy dogs spent the same amount. A similar pattern was observed for veterinary and medication costs. Nearly half of the owners of dogs with CE reported annual expenses exceeding 1000€ compared with only 8.7% of owners of healthy dogs reaching that amount. It is therefore not surprising that owners of dogs with CE often stated that the costs are a financial burden to them compared with owners of healthy dogs. For many owners, these costs may create stress, potentially exacerbating the emotional burden of caring for a sick pet. Understanding the financial challenges faced by these caregivers is essential for providing appropriate support and ensuring that the owner’s well-being is considered alongside the dog’s health. Moreover, financial burden may directly affect owner compliance. Owners may report adherence to prescribed diets while not fully implementing them because to financial constraints, or they may be less likely to return for follow-up consultations because associated costs.

Although identifying factors contributing to CB in general is valuable, determining the most important ones provides practical guidance. The aspects most frequently ranked as most important by owners all were related to the dog’s well-being. Owners expressed concern that the dog might suffer, that episodes with severe clinical signs might occur, that treatment could fail, or that the dog could die. These concerns emphasize the deep emotional connection between owners and their pets, highlighting the importance of the dog to the owner’s mental well-being.30

Financial and logistical challenges appear less relevant. Approximately 1 in 5 owners did not mention factors such as high food costs, regular veterinary visits, or the time commitment required for the dog in the ranking, indicating that these issues are not seen as burdensome as others. The responses from other owners mostly were distributed in the middle to lower ranks. This finding is interesting, because the owners simultaneously reported being burdened by costs and veterinary visits. In addition, compared with owners of healthy dogs, they were significantly more likely to consider their dogs time-consuming. The ranking indicates that these stressors play a subordinate role relative to the health concerns owners have for their dogs. Nevertheless, they still represent burdensome aspects. It is also important to recognize that the outliers in the ranking represent dog owners and, by extension, veterinary clients. For some owners, costs and veterinary visits constitute the most burdensome aspects of the disease. Consequently good communication about different aspects of the disease is essential to address the needs of individual owners.

According to a previous study,31 several aspects are important regarding communication. First, all communication should educate the client. Caregivers of IBD patients emphasize the value of comprehensive information for better understanding the disease and improving their QoL.32 Likewise, dog owners may benefit from clear information, because it helps them understand available treatment alternatives should the current treatment lose its effectiveness, thereby minimizing related concerns. A written information sheet with a detailed explanation about the disease also might help the owner and decrease unbillable working hours for the veterinarian.14 Second, it is important to provide therapeutic choices and respect the owner’s decisions.31 In this way, owners can select the treatment option that best matches their desires, particularly regarding financial and logistical capacities. The dog-owner relationship also might influence therapeutic decision-making. Third, effective 2-way-communication is crucial.31 Cultural, religious, geographical, and many other factors can substantially influence the owner’s perception of CB. For example, the social role of companion animals, the insurance status of the animal, and the owner’s ability to work flexibly or bring the dog to work may lead to considerable variation in how different aspects of CB are experienced. Directly asking about emotional strain, as well as financial concerns and time constraints, and allowing the owner to discuss these issues, could help tailor therapeutic decisions to the owner’s situation and decrease CB.

Owners of dogs with CE experience CB, which makes it essential to address this issue in clinical consultations and potentially find strategies to lower the burden. Doing so may not only support the owner, but also lessen burden transfer and compassion fatigue, which may benefit the veterinarian’s mental health.11,33 Future studies should explore the use of digital health tools for both owners and veterinarians in this context. Furthermore, research should assess owners’ satisfaction with veterinary communication and explore their expectations and needs in this regard. In addition, it would be valuable to examine how veterinarians perceive and manage CB for owners of dogs with CE, and whether doing so has an impact on their own well-being.

Limitations

Our study had some limitations. The overrepresentation of female owners may have influenced our results. Because of the large discrepancy in the number of responses from each gender, no comparison was made between them. Additionally, the online questionnaire did not allow for verification of the animals’ health status. Instead, owners self-reported whether their dogs had a diagnosed CE, was healthy, or had another chronic condition. Another limitation of our study is that the significant age difference between healthy dogs and those with CE was not accounted for in the analysis.

Validated instruments for assessing CB in veterinary medicine are currently lacking. For this reason, no established CB scores were applied. Rather we adapted a questionnaire approach commonly used in human medicine to the veterinary context to bridge the gap between human and veterinary medicine. Although the lack of formal validation may limit generalizability, the results provide meaningful insight into CB and can inform future studies and questionnaire development.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight the important CB experienced by owners of dogs with CE. It impacts their QoL and mental health and is correlated with the number of episodes with severe clinical signs the dog suffers. Financial strain and time investment add to this burden, but owners remain primarily concerned with their dogs’ well-being. Despite these challenges, the dog–owner relationship mostly remains unchanged. Veterinarians should recognize and address CB in owners of dogs with CE during clinical consultations.
