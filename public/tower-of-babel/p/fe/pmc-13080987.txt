Investigation of Novel Antiproliferative Drugs Interaction with mPEG2k-PCLy Copolymers Using Molecular Dynamics Simulation Approach
Authors: Karina Solórzano-Acevedo, Carlos Zepactonal Gómez-Castro, Emma Martín Rodríguez, Álvaro Artiga, Rosa M. Quispe-Siccha, Mónica Corea, Itzia I. Padilla-Martínez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The therapeutic potential of many new drugs is limited by poor aqueous solubility. This work addresses the solubilization improvement of novel fluorescent dihydropyrazole-carbohydrazide derivatives (DPCH), with proven antiproliferative activity against human breast cancer, through encapsulation in three distinct methoxy poly­(ethylene glycol)–poly­(ε-caprolactone) (mPEG-PCL) diblock copolymers. All-atom molecular dynamics simulations (100 ns, CHARMM36 force field, NAMD) of 52 distinct configurations revealed favorable interactions between DPCHs and PCL residues, resulting in the formation of micellar supramolecular assemblies with PEG coronae that facilitate enhanced DPCH water solvation. Systematic evaluation of micelle size, composition (5, 14, and 21 copolymer strands; 20, 30, 40, 56, 84, 112, and 168 drug molecules), and hydrophobic chain length (PCL 1k, 2k, and 5k) through radial distribution functions, radius of gyration, solvent accessibility, RMSD analysis, and interaction energy calculations identified optimal encapsulation conditions. Regardless of the DPCH derivative tested, mPEG2k-PCL5k produced the most stable, monodisperse micelle populations with the highest loading efficiency. Molecular docking calculations further confirmed strong drug-polymer affinity. Experimental validation through nanoparticle synthesis and characterization via dynamic light scattering, zeta potential measurements, cryogenic transmission electron microscopy, and fluorescence microscopy confirmed successful self-assembly with entrapment efficiencies up to 97% and internalization of loaded micelles into cancer cells. These findings demonstrate that mPEG-PCL micelles are potential carriers for DPCH derivatives, as computational predictions closely align with experimental data.

Introduction

In recent years, the implementation of new compounds with antiproliferative action, derived either from natural extracts or by structural diversification of preexisting molecules with known activity, has been sought to overcome conventional cancer chemotherapy drawbacks. Some compounds and fluorescent probes based on a dihydropyrazole nucleus have shown anticancer properties, leading to the development of a wide variety of drugs. Recently, our research group carried out the synthesis of several 4,5-dihydropyrazole-carbohydrazide (DPCH) derivatives that demonstrated antiproliferative activity against human breast cancer cell lines due to their binding affinity with the catalytic domain 2 (DD2) of histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6), thereby enabling its inhibition. Moreover, their identified antioxidant properties enhance DPCH therapeutic efficacy, further supporting their anticancer potential, as both the overexpression of HDACs and oxidative stress are associated with cancer onset and progression.

Among a family of nine compounds tested on human breast cancer lines (MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231) and healthy cell lines (3T3/NIH and MCF-10A), compounds identified as 2a–d, Figure , showed the lowest cytotoxicity toward the noncancerous cells as well as a significant cell-death induction percentage in tumor cell lines, even in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) subtype. The entire family, at a concentration of 100 μM presented antioxidant activity above 95%. Compound 2c exhibited a 2,2-diphenyl-1-(2,4,6-trinitrophenyl)­hydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity, with IC50 value of 16 ± 4 μM comparable to ascorbic acid (13 ± 2 μM). Considering their cytotoxic effects against tumoral and nonmalignant cells alongside their antioxidant performance, compounds 2a and 2c provide the highest performance, whereas 2b showed the most exergonic value of binding free energy ΔG b (−7.86 kcal/mol) with the DD2-HDAC6, and proved its function as an HDAC inhibitor with an IC50 value of 12 ± 3 μM. Conversely, their application has several limitations derived from their expected low solubility in water, as evidenced by their reported physicochemical descriptors, in particular their computed logP values, which are close to 3.0. Hence, with the aim to overcome this constraint, DPCH encapsulation into micelles was performed. Micelles were obtained from the self-assembly of amphiphilic block copolymers constituted by poly­(ε-caprolactone) (PCL) and poly­(ethylene glycol) monomethyl ether (mPEG). PCL is a biodegradable (in the human body, it degrades to 6-hydroxycaproxycaproic acid), biocompatible, and bioresorbable polyester. − Whereas mPEG is a polymer that confers enhanced biocompatibility and improved biodistribution due to its hydrophilic nature, and inherent advantages of drug protection against the reticuloendothelial system.

5-(2-Hydroxyphenyl)-N′,1,3-triphenyl-4,5-dihydro-1H-pyrazole-4-carbohydrazide, R = H in 2a, R = Cl in 2b, R = OCH3 in 2c and R = Br in 2d.

Given the previously reported success of mPEG-PCL micelles as nanocarriers for different hydrophobic drugs, − in the present work, we investigated the interaction between the above-mentioned DPCH derivatives and three different mPEG2k-PCLy copolymers using molecular simulation and the nanoprecipitation method to obtain DPCH-loaded nanostructures.

Contrary to long-standing nanostructure-based drug carriers, self-assembled nanomedicines can offer significant enhancements for delivery and targeting in cancer chemotherapy. They overcome issues with elaborate preparations, reduced reproducibility, and large-scale production, given that the formation of noncovalent bonds is the underlying mechanism of self-assembly.

A thorough insight into the self-assembly of diblock copolymers and their application as nanocarriers can be acquired utilizing multiscale molecular theoretical frameworks that include atomistic simulations. All of them provide not only the outlook for the involved process but also the prediction of micellar morphology, stability under dilution (condition achieved when entering the bloodstream), and drug loading efficiency by offering insights into the factors influencing drug–polymer interactions and micelle generation, valuable details that orient a refined drug delivery nanosystems design. Of outmost importance is Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulation, a key tool that pairs macroscopic observations with theoretical atomic and molecular principles. MD grants a dynamic portrayal of the assembly course in a determined timespan, allows to achieve data acquisition at different spatial and temporal scales, and enables the description of multiple physicochemical characteristics, design assistance, and experimental optimization. ,

Structural conformation and functional attributes, of copolymers identified by all-atom MD simulations, contribute to determining their capability as drug delivery systems in diverse solvents and conditions. MD simulations allow the visualization and analysis in detail of the micelle self-assembly, the dissolution and encapsulation processes, as well as the appraisal of micellar structures stability and drug loading. MD simulations generally supply a feasible approach that facilitates the reduction of time and costs. −

Materials and Methods

Computational Details

MD simulations and molecular docking studies were conducted to depict and assess the nature of DPCH compounds and copolymer–compound interactions. The goal was to determine whether the process results in a self-assembled nanostructure and correlate the data with the experimental observations.

Models

2a–d Structures

Structures were constructed and optimized using deMon2k 5.0 employing a density functional theory-based method applying the PBE functional and the DFT-optimized double-ζ (DZVP) orbital basis set, with a GEN-A2* auxiliary basis set. Topology files were generated using the VMD Molefacture add-on and the CHARMM General Force Field (CGenFF) online server for automatic assignment of CHARMM36 force field parameters to perform the potential energy calculations. ,

mPEG2k-PCLy Structures

Structural models were based on the polymer composed by mPEG2k-PCLnk with 45 units (2k) of ethylene glycol and “y” units of caprolactone (y = 8 (1k), 17 (2k), 45 (5k)), Figure . The mPEG and PCL structural units were incorporated into the polymeric chain using the psfgen 2.0 plugin included with VMD using CGenFF force field modified topology definitions in a similar manner as described for compounds 2a–d. Once a prototype strand consisting of start, repeat, and end units for each mPEG2k-PCLnk copolymer was constructed, a preoptimization was performed by applying 3000 conjugated-gradient optimization steps, running the NAMD 2.14 software. This optimized structure was then used as a template to generate the micelle structures.

Molecular structure of mPEG2k-PCLnk, where x = 45 (2k) and y = 8 (n = 1), 17 (n = 2), 45 (n = 5).

Octanol–Water Partition Coefficients (Log P) Calculation

FEPT (Free-Energy Perturbation Theory) calculation approach was selected for the estimation of solvation energies needed to calculate the octane–water partition coefficients (log P ow) of the DPCH compounds according to the formula: log⁡Pow=(ΔGw−ΔGo)RTlog⁡e where ΔG w and ΔG o are the solvation free energies of the solute in water and n-octanol, respectively. R is the universal gas constant, T the absolute temperature, and e the base of the natural logarithm function.

The calculation consisted of “alchemical” MD simulations considering a “dual topology” approach, where a solute molecule immersed in a water or n-octanol solvent box vanished in a series of short simulations according to a perturbative parameter lambda (λ) ranging from 0 to 1, where λ = 0 represents the solvated solute and λ = 1 contains only solvent molecules with the solute entirely vanished. The contribution of all intermediate states is scaled according to the lambda parameter and optionally through some smoothing functions.

The alchemical simulations were performed using the program NAMD v.2.14, considering the CGenFF v.36 parameter set. The CGenFF program version 2.5 (https://cgenff.com/) parametrized the n-octanol molecule. The water box used in each simulation added a solvent pad of 15.0 Å to the solute forming cubic boxes with ∼2500 TIP3 water molecules. The n-octanol boxes were first built from an optimized n-octanol molecule using the program PACKMOL in a cube of 64.0 Å on each dimension and comprised of 600 solvent molecules that were then minimized and equilibrated at 298 K and 1 atm for 20 ns using NAMD. The solute molecules were then immersed in water or n-octanol boxes to perform an equilibration consisting of minimization, warming, and density-fitting simulations, with lengths of 3000 steps, 100 ps, and 200 ps, respectively, for water simulations, and lengths of 5000 steps, 600 ps, and 600 ps, for n-octanol simulations.

After equilibration of the systems, the alchemical production simulations consisted of “forward” (λ = 0 to 1) and “backward” (λ = 1 to 0) simulations, divided into 32 × 2 lambda windows of length 0.03125, so that each window considered a lambda value of 0, 0.03125, 0.0625, 0.09375, etc. Each λ window was run for 200 ps in water simulations and 400 ps in n-octanol. Each window was also considered a short equilibration of 20 and 40 ps before sampling for water and n-octanol, respectively. The free energies of the whole process were calculated using the Bennett Acceptance Ratio (BAR) estimator as implemented in the VMD program, with %SD ≤ 2. Supplementary Note 1 (SN1), in the Supporting Information, includes rigorous analysis of the accuracy and convergence metrics of these calculations and compares the results of other two configurations to evaluate the sensitivity of different sampling strategies on the log P estimations.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

Studied Experimental Setups

The initial arrangements were made using PACKMOL 20.14.1. The hydrophobic end of each polymer strand was confined within a sphere with a radius of 5 nm, leaving the hydrophilic portion beyond it. The first 36 systems, mPEG2k-PCL2k copolymer-based, were designed using q = 5, 14, and 21 copolymer strands (q­(mPEG2k-PCL2k)). In addition, all four 2a–d compound molecules were incorporated into the inner sphere at rates of 4, 6, or 8 molecules per strand.

The generated micelle structures were dispersed in water using the VMD Solvate plugin, with a solvent margin of 12 Å around the micelle structure. The explicit water model TIP3 featured in the CHARMM force field was used.

MD simulations were conducted initiating with a system equilibration performed according to the following protocol: the first part consisted of an energy minimization step to promote system relaxation using the conjugate gradient algorithm, followed by a heating step (0–298 K) for 50 ps, both steps restraining the polymer and solute structure to equilibrate the solvent structure. The second stage consisted of repeating the first two steps, freeing the polymer and solute structures from any restrictions, followed by a density adjustment step where the system is brought to the final simulation conditions (298 K, 1 atm) by using the NTP (isothermal–isobaric) ensemble for 50 ps to equilibrate the pressure.

Production run started from the last equilibration step using the same simulation conditions for 100 ns, utilizing an integration step of 2 fs per step, applying the SHAKE method to restrict the bond length to hydrogen atoms and enable multiple frequency of noncovalent interactions, calculating Lennard-Jones interactions every two fs and electrostatic interactions every four fs. Langevin molecular dynamics was applied to control the temperature, and the Nose-Hoover barostat was used to maintain the system pressure. , Noncovalent interactions were calculated using a 12 Å cutoff radius, applying an attenuation function at 10 Å and recording pair lists at a radius of 14 Å. Long-range electrostatic interactions under periodic conditions were calculated by using the particle-mesh Ewald (PME) method.

Once the results regarding DPCH association around the hydrophobic fraction were evaluated, the number of strands required for the mPEG2k-PCL1k and mPEG2k-PCL5k simulated systems was determined. These systems were composed of 14 and 21 copolymer strands in combination with 6 DPCH molecules per strand, adding up a total of 16 more systems, 8 composed of copolymer mPEG2k-PCL1k and 8 more based on copolymer with the longest chain length (mPEG2k-PCL5k). At the end, 52 systems were obtained, a schematic overview of all configurations is provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S1).

The free compounds were also analyzed under aqueous conditions, following the same procedures established for the copolymer–drug systems. In each case, a 30 Å per side cubic box comprising 50 DPCH molecules was used.

Evaluation of MD Simulation Findings

Produced trajectories were analyzed using built-in VMD tools. The behavior in the water of the free DPCH compounds was compared with that in the presence of amphiphilic copolymers. The evolution of systems over time, aggregation features, and molecular and energetic interactions were examined. Association thresholds were set according to a critical distance of 4 Å between the DPCH molecules, copolymer blocks, and water molecules. Aggregation percentage was defined as the fraction of DPCH molecules that were in contact with the copolymer at a certain simulation frame, according to the previous criterion. ADPCH, DPCH aggregation percentage at time t; N Aa, total number of DPCH atoms in contact with the copolymer at time t; N AM, number of atoms per molecule of DPCH; and N M, total number of DPCH molecules in the system.

The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and radius of gyration (R g) were estimated for DPCH in two different contexts to study their stability in water throughout time as free compounds and being copolymer-associated. The aggregation percentage of the compound to the copolymer was determined as a function of time. Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) was measured to compare the exposure of the DPCH to the solvent in the different setups as encapsulated compounds in contrast with the free molecules. The radial distribution function (RDF) was calculated to identify how the favored associations between the copolymer fractions and the DPCH molecules impact the loaded structure organization. The obtained values of the interaction energy for each system were compared to analyze the suitability of the DPCH-copolymer interaction.

The obtained results for each analyzed parameter across all 52 studied experimental setups were subjected to a systematic statistical assessment and significance tests to determine whether the amount and molecular weight of the different copolymers and the nature and quantity of the DPCH significantly affected the resulting clusters. All analyses were performed using the integrated statistical tools in MATLAB R2024a.

To establish the nature of the distribution of each data set, Lilliefors tests of normality were performed. In all cases, the null hypothesis was rejected. Therefore, the Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric technique was used for intergroup comparisons (the test statistic H, and the p-value were reported; the first one indicates if there are significant differences between the median values of the groups by being compared with the chi-square distribution with the number of groups–1 degrees of freedom, the existence of a larger H value indicates that the distribution of at least one group is different from the rest), followed by a post hoc multiple comparison test (using Dunn’s method and Sidák’s correction) to identify specific between-pair differences.

A global efficiency index was defined to identify the most suitable system for each DPCH; additional information can be found in Supplementary Note SN2 – Table SN2 lists the 52 simulated arrangements, ranked by their performance.

Molecular Docking

mPEG2k-PCLnk micelles were first modeled in an aqueous environment using molecular dynamics, and the resulting stable aggregates were retrieved for a broader investigation of PCL recognition for DPCHs, applying a molecular docking approach to calculate the binding free energy (ΔG b). Each DPCH molecule was used as a ligand, while a set of copolymer strands arranged in the precise spatial distribution as acquired from the MD served as a receptor. To consider the significance of cluster size on the energy values, seven different receptors with varying numbers of strands were tested. A blind docking search using AutoDock 4.2, yielded the most suitable PCL/DPCH complexes. VMD was used to analyze and obtain three-dimensional representations of such complexes.

The Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm (LGA) was used to conduct conformationally flexible ligand docking with seven torsional degrees of freedom to obtain the ΔG b values. The population count was configured to 150, the number of runs was set to 100, and the maximum number of generations was 2.7 × 104, with 2.5 × 106 being the highest possible number of energy evaluations. The grid was located at the receptor’s center of mass, and the grid size was set to 128 points along all dimensions with 0.375 Å spacing.

Experimental Section

Loaded micelles were prepared to provide experimental support. Analytical entrapment efficiency was compared with the molecular simulation observations. Additionally, the internalization of loaded carriers was assessed with fluorescence microscopy.

Materials

Polyethylene glycol monomethyl ether (mPEG, with a molecular weight of 2000 Da), ε-caprolactone (ε-CL, 98% purity), and stannous 2-ethylhexanoate (Sn­(Oct)2 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Before use, mPEG and dichloromethane (DCM) were dried, other reagents and solvents were from technical purity grades. DPCH derivatives 2a–d were synthesized as reported (Balbuena-Rebolledo et al., 2022). Human breast cancer cell lines MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection.

Synthesis and Characterization of Diblock Copolymers

Three copolymers of different molecular weights (mPEG2k-PCLnk where n = 1, 2, and 5) were synthesized by ring-opening polymerization using Sn­(Oct)2 as the catalyst, as previously reported. ,− mPEG2k (1 mmol), ε-caprolactone (9, 18, and 46 mmol), and Sn­(Oct)2 (10 μL) were added to a round-bottom flask and stirred under a nitrogen atmosphere at 140 °C. After 12 h, the resultant copolymer was dissolved in dichloromethane (DCM) and precipitated with cold diethyl ether; this process was repeated several times for further purification. The chemical structure and the number-average molecular weight (M n) of diblock copolymers were determined by spectroscopy techniques. IR spectra were acquired using a PerkinElmer Spectrum GX series with the attenuated total reflection (ATR) FTIR sampling technique. 1H NMR spectra were collected in a Varian Mercury 300 MHz in deuterated chloroform (CDCl3).

Preparation of DPCH-Loaded Micelles

Loaded micelles were prepared through the nanoprecipitation method incorporating the tip sonication, as introduced in earlier studies. ,, mPEG2k-PCLnk copolymers (20 mg) and DPCH derivatives (2 mg) were dissolved in acetone. This drug-polymer solution was afterward added dropwise to 20 mL of Milli-Q water. The mixture was homogenized with a CGoldenwall probe sonicator (300 W maximum power, 30% working amplitude) in 24 intervals of 25 s on and 5 s off and subsequently stirred at room temperature until acetone evaporation. To remove the unloaded drug, the resultant suspension was centrifuged several times at 14,000 rpm for 3 min each (Hettich Universal 320, Tuttlingen, Germany) and the supernatant was then filtered using a syringe filter (220 nm). The clear aqueous solution was finally stored at 4 °C. A portion was freeze-dried for additional characterization. Void micelles were obtained following the same procedure.

Characterization of the Micelles

Particle Size and Morphology

Measurements were performed by Cryo-TEM (JEM-2100, Japan) from direct observation of 10 μL droplets of 2a-loaded into mPEG2k-PCL2k micelle aqueous dispersion applied to a copper grid, subsequently treated at 21 °C in a constant humidity atmosphere using a Cryoplunge 3 system (Gatan Inc., Pleasanton, CA, USA) to allow its vitrification by plunging it liquid nitrogen. Grids were maintained in a cryo-stage at −170 °C. The microscope was operated at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV. Dispersions were used at a concentration of 1 mg of micelles per milliliter. Information regarding the diameter of each particle was extracted using ImageJ software. According to the distribution of the data, normality was rejected. Based on its robustness with data of this nature, optimal interval widths for each class in the histogram were calculated using the Freedman–Diaconis rule.

Zeta Potential and Particle Size Distribution

Zeta potential and particle size distribution were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZSP (Malvern Panalytical, UK). Measurements were acquired in triplicate at 25 °C, setting a 173° scattering angle. Disposable cuvettes were used for dynamic light scattering (DLS) assays. Folded capillary cells (DTS1070) were employed for laser Doppler velocimetry (LDV) analysis to determine zeta potential.

Entrapment Efficiency Determination

Drug content within the micelle core was spectrophotometrically determined using a PerkinElmer Lambda 35 spectrophotometer. Freeze-dried micelles (1 mg) were dissolved in 1 mL acetonitrile, and the absorbance was read at 350 nm. Entrapment efficiency (EE%) and drug-loading ratio (DL%) values were calculated as follows: EE%=WeightoftheentrappeddrugWeightofthedrugfedinitially×100 DL%=WeightoftheentrappeddrugTotalweightofthedrug−loadedmicelles×100

Fluorescence Microscopy

MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were cultured in a 24-well plate (1 × 104 cells per well). After 24 h of incubation, three different treatments were added: (a) free 2c dissolved in DMSO (final concentration of 0.1% of organic solvent); (b) 2c (10 μM) loaded onto mPEG2k-PCL5k micelles; and (c) mPEG2k-PCL5k void micelles. Prior to administration, all treatments were prepared in DMEM. The DPCHs were added to achieve a final concentration of 10 μM in the culture medium, either in their free form or encapsulated in micelles; while void nanocarriers were administered at the same micelle concentration as in the compound-loaded treatment to guarantee an equivalent copolymer amount. Samples were observed after 48 h incubation under cell culture conditions. Cells were fixed after being carefully washed thrice with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) to remove residual treatment dispersions and culture medium. After this, cells were incubated with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in DPBS for 20 min at 4 °C and then washed again three times with DPBS to eliminate the fixative. An inverted fluorescence microscope Eclipse Ti2–U (Nikon Instruments Inc., Japan) was used for imaging. Micrographs were acquired using 40× and 60× objectives (Nikon) with a high-gain CCD camera. Ultraviolet (UV) light source was used to excite 2c-treated samples along with a DAPI filter set (excitation centered at 350 nm, emission centered at 460 nm). Images were processed with ImageJ 1.54g software, and MATLAB. Fluorescence quantification involved automated image processing using segmentation based on Otsu’s adaptive threshold method. A morphological operation was applied to the generated binary image to minimize noise from artifacts smaller than 50 pixels. All pixels from the original image within the regions of interest (ROIs) were extracted based on the resulting mask. Relative fluorescence intensity was calculated as the sum of the intensity values of these pixels normalized by the segmented area. The average fluorescence intensity observed in the control group (cells without treatment) served as the baseline.

Given the inhibitory activity of DPCHs on HDAC6, which is located mainly in the cell cytoplasm, nuclear staining was used to verify the location of loaded micelles in such region. In pursuit of this, MDA-MB-231 cells were treated with a 10 μg/mL propidium iodide (PI) solution following the fixation process. Posterior to 15 min incubation at 37 °C, cells were washed once more.

Results and Discussion

Molecular Simulations

Hydrophobicity Assessment of DPCHs

The estimation of the octanol–water partition coefficients (log P) was performed using the Free-Energy Perturbation Theory (FEPT) calculation approach. The estimated values indicate a highly hydrophobic character for 2a–d.

As can be observed in Table , the log P values for 2a–d range from 7.4 to 9.9, indicating reduced aqueous solubility and, therefore, poor bioavailability. Compared to the first estimates of DPCHs log P based on their chemical structures, with values between 2.7 and 3.4, the current estimates are considerably higher. This discrepancy arises from the fact that the previous methodology did not incorporate quality control data, leading to less rigorous estimations. The FEPT methodology facilitated a more accurate description of the nature of the selected DPCHs, their three-dimensional structure, and specific interactions with solvent molecules that successfully enabled calculation of log P values for unreported compounds even in the absence of prior experimental data.

The obtained log P values support the application of the proposed amphiphilic material, as the PCL block ensures effective encapsulation while the PEG component facilitates transport throughout biological systems. PCL exhibits noteworthy compatibility with highly hydrophobic compounds, supporting its use for the transport of drugs with log P values greater than 2.5. Therefore, all 2a–d compounds would benefit from their loading into micelles, given their high hydrophobicity.

MD Simulations Insights

MD simulations provided molecular-level insights into supramolecular aggregate formation and copolymer–DPCH interactions. For the following comparative analyses, it should be noted that the results presented for a specific DPCH are referred to only one experimental configuration for each compound that demonstrated the best performance according to the E index (highlighted in the Table SN2 from Supplementary Note SN2). These cases correspond to q = 21 for 2a–c and q = 14 for 2d; all based on mPEG2k-PCL2K except for the one that encapsulated 2b, which is formed by mPEG2k-PCL5K strands. When needed, subscript notation differentiates between free DPCH derivatives (f) and encapsulated compounds (e), both simulated in aqueous solution. Moreover, the discussion drawn from the statistical analysis (H statistic and p-values) across the 52 systems are supported by boxplots that summarize the distributions of the evaluated parameters (Supporting Information, Figures S2–S7).

Stability of DPCH Molecules in Water and Encapsulated in Micelles

The trajectories obtained from the MD simulations visualized the movement and arrangement of free DPCH molecules in the presence of water. The snapshots depicted in Figure A correspond to the beginning of the production simulations and the conclusion at 100 ns.

Simulations revealed no stable dispersion of DPCH molecules in water at any stage. Considering that initially the DPCH molecules were randomly located inside a sphere with a radius of 50 Å within the water box, aggregation can be observed right from the start, resulting in even larger agglomerates spread throughout the box.

Unlike free DPCHs, conformational changes of the copolymer-DPCH assemblies during the MD simulation are evident as depicted in Figure B, resulting in all cases in more defined and structured aggregates. The PCL fraction bends to enclose the DPCH molecules within the core of the structure. In contrast, the PEG block stretches to interact with the surrounding water, providing an effective barrier against nonfavorable interactions. The root-mean-square deviations (RMSD) plots of DPCHs in water were measured to quantitatively compare their stabilization and aggregation during the MD simulations with those of the systems where a copolymer was added. The graph in Figure A shows the low stability of each derivative in the media since significant and constant fluctuations prevent RMSD values from reaching a plateau over time, thus indicating the continuous reorganization of the molecules in the solvent to minimize their contact energy. The RMSD probability distribution curves in Figure B show that 2b is located at the lowest values, implying that it undergoes fewer conformational transitions than the other compounds, contrary to 2a, which, apart from being located at high RMSD values, has two main wide peaks, suggesting that it is in constant change. However, the fact that every RMSD distribution curve has several peaks is indicative of the existence of numerous conformational states, and, therefore, of the lack of structural homogeneity in water.

As foreseen, the response of DPCH molecules differs depending on whether the copolymer is present. Conformational stability characterization of DPCHs when interacting with the copolymer strands through the RMSD calculation and its distribution, Figure , suggests that the retention of the compound inside the assembly contributes to lower RMSD fluctuations in contrast to those observed in free compounds. The presence of copolymer promotes RMSD convergence to stable plateaus (Figure C) and shifts the probability distributions toward lower values with fewer, sharper peaks (Figure D and Table ). These observations reveal a consistent trend: encapsulation within copolymer assemblies significantly reduces the structural fluctuations observed for free compounds in water. The system encapsulating compound 2b exhibits the lowest RMSD median and dispersion values, although this stabilization effect is consistently observed across all DPCHs.

Similarly, evaluation of the 52 systems assessed suggests in any case, regardless of the chain length of the hydrophobic moiety or the number of existing strands, that DPCH stabilization in water increases with the addition of the copolymer, given that the RMSD fluctuations are lower. Significant differences were found in the average RMSD values obtained from the last 20 ns of simulation depending on the number of interacting components in each system (H = 29.856, p = 3.3 × 10–7 considering the number of copolymer strands, and H = 24.286, p = 0.001 concerning the number of DPCH molecules). Systems with q = 5 showed higher RMSD values, suggesting a lower capacity for entrapment and stabilization of the compound than systems with 21 strands (p = 1.6 × 10–7), capable of adopting more restrictive structures. Systems with the highest number of DPCH molecules exhibit a significant decrease in RMSD compared to systems containing 20 to 56 molecules (0.004 ≤ p ≤ 0.048), indicating that saturation of the hydrophobic microenvironment of the formed core favors the stabilization of DPCH molecules.

Additionally, the RMSD evolution of all 52 setups (corresponding to the entire micellar aggregate) over the 100 ns production simulation is reported in Figure S8. It is worth mentioning that most systems exhibit a plateau-like behavior consistent with equilibration.

In addition to copolymer-DPCH association, general cluster formation, considering also association between DPCH molecules and between copolymer strands, was analyzed. Thus, all assemblies of either single or multiple copolymer strands with or without DPCH molecules that arose at some point and were maintained throughout the simulation time were considered clusters. A cutoff distance of 4 Å was defined to consider the association of two molecules; this distance corresponds to the shortest between any pair of atoms of two molecules.

In most systems, there is the existence of one to three clusters (87% cases), 80% of all available copolymer strands take part in a group (75% overall), from 60% to 100% of copolymer belongs to the main cluster (87% of the total), between 80% and 100% of the DPCH molecules is gathered within the largest cluster (for 81% of all systems) and a copolymer-DPCH association greater than 79% occurs (in 96% of the setups). Global Structure of Assemblies and Capacity for Solvent Protection

Conformational changes exhibited by compound molecules in water, either in their free form or being part of the structures that result from their association with copolymer strands, were analyzed by calculating the radius of gyration (R g), as a measure of the shape or compactness of the structure. Lower R g values indicate a compact or globular structure, whereas larger values are characteristic of more extended or elongated constructs.

Figure A shows the temporal evolution of R g for free DPCH molecules and carried by a copolymer. Free compounds exhibit significant fluctuations in R g over time without reaching a steady state, suggesting that in this scenario, molecules remain in a continuous process of reorganization. Conversely, the R g becomes more stable and exhibits minimal variability when the copolymer is part of the experimental setup, indicating its ability to constrain DPCH molecule mobility and promote their encapsulation inside a defined and stable aggregate.

The average R g of DPCHs, when interacting with the copolymer in the selected systems, decreased between 15.2 to 28.5% relative to free DPCH, Figure B. A clear reduction in the dispersion of values was also observed, thus, all DPCHs benefited from the encapsulation reaching a more compact and defined structure during the simulation. Although compound 2b showed the smallest reduction in R g, the presence of the copolymer improved its stability reaching the lowest average R g of 23.5 Å, as it exhibited the largest decrease in degree of fluctuation (82.9%), suggesting that, at the end, this cluster has a higher degree of order and fewer components undergoing changes.

Analysis of the 52 systems revealed that a greater number of strands significantly promotes more compact and stable aggregate structures, in terms of R g standard deviation, of the molecules when associated with the copolymer (H = 33.075, p = 6.6 × 10–8). Comparison between the 5-strand and 21-strand systems (Figure S3) showed the most notable difference (p = 2.7 × 10–8), indicating that a greater number of strands reinforces the stability of the aggregate.

Copolymer–DPCH Interaction and Structural Correlations within Clusters

The radial distribution functions (RDF), denoted as g(r), of the associations occurring in each system, were calculated to examine how the assemblies are organized in each experimental setup. Calculations were performed for three different interactions: DPCH–DPCH, copolymer–copolymer, and copolymer–DPCH. The first two are thoroughly discussed in the Supplementary Note SN4, while results for the latter case are summarized below and analyzed in detail in the Supplementary Note SN5. In order to further investigate the structure of the simulated micelles, an exploratory analysis of the radial distribution between the copolymer blocks and water was performed for four representative systems based on mPEG2k-PCL2k and an individual system for each DPCH type. This specific approach aimed to assess whether the final structures exhibit the expected core–corona organization. This is included in the Supporting Information (Supplementary Note SN6).

The g(r) obtained for the most favorable systems for each type of DPCH are shown in Figure , which displays the RDF for the overall interaction between the copolymer and DPCH, Figure A. Interactions between the PCL or the mPEG2k moiety and the DPCH molecules taken separately are depicted on the right side of this figure, demonstrating that most of the significant occurring interactions are the ones involving the hydrophobic fraction, Figure B, implying that the compound predominantly loads in the nucleus rather than in the hydrophilic region, as the RDF for this interaction presents low values, Figure C, suggesting a minor interaction. It is worth mentioning that although such graphs correspond to four punctual systems, similar patterns are observed in all cases across the other configurations. Such curves resemble those reported for doxorubicin-loaded micelles based on the same material.

RDF for (A) copolymer–DPCH, (B) PCL–DPCH, and (C) mPEG–DPCH interactions. Only the best encapsulation systems for each compound are displayed.

As described in Supplementary Note SN5, analysis of the 52 experimental setups provides insights into the spatial organization of the core of the studied aggregates. Remarkably, the analysis revealed that the spatial organization of DPCHs within micellar cores is governed by copolymer architecture and system composition (namely, copolymer type, number of strands, and DPCH load) rather than the specific DPCH structure. Copolymers with intermediate molecular weight maximized the distance where the highest density is found in the RDF due to optimal balance between encapsulation capability and chain flexibility. Additionally, increasing the number of copolymer strands or DPCH molecules resulted in more homogeneous cores with improved molecular dispersion (reduced agglomeration). These findings demonstrate that micellar core compactness and DPCH distribution can be tuned through systematic variation of copolymer molecular weight, strand number and drug loading.

Energy Evaluation of Copolymer–DPCH Interactions

Interaction Energy Analysis

Based on the temporal evolution of the van der Waals (vdW) component of the interaction energy, Figure , for each system having the best compound transport, a quantitative analysis of the copolymer-DPCH interaction energy for the 52 simulated systems was carried out, given that the compound becomes favorably embedded in the hydrophobic core of the micelle and does not involve covalent bonds. The appropriate time region was identified for comparative analysis as not all reached equilibrium in the total simulation time. For this purpose, 10-time windows were evaluated with a relative stability metric based on the coefficient of variation obtained through a linear least-squares fit of the slope and the average energy of the values found within the i-th analyzed time window. A period with the minimum value of this coefficient, common to all systems, was selected to guarantee that all shared a comparable phase of minor perturbations. Interaction energy analysis involved a span of 80 to 100 ns for each system.

Temporal evolution of the interaction energy (van der Waals contribution) between the DPCH molecules and the copolymer strands.

To assess encapsulation strength under different conditions, the relative stability index (RSI), calculated as the ratio between mean absolute energy and its standard deviation, was analyzed. Results showed no significant differences based on copolymer molecular weight (H = 0.440, p = 0.803) or DPCH type (H = 2.482, p = 0.479), as all components share similar physicochemical characteristics. However, significant differences were observed for the number of copolymer strands (H = 23.876, p = 6.5 × 10–6) and DPCH molecules (H = 25.176, p = 7.1 × 10–4), attributed to increased association sites that favor interaction energy and reduce fluctuations.

Van der Waals energy decreased progressively from initial values of approximately −30 kcal/mol (0–1 ns) to −2703 kcal/mol (final 20 ns), indicating favorable DPCH encapsulation. The energy change (ΔE vdW) significantly depends on copolymer molecular weight (H = 16.142, p = 3.1 × 10–4), number of strands (H = 27.908, p = 8.7 × 10–7), and DPCH loading (H = 28.073, p = 2.1 × 10–4), but not on DPCH type (H = 0.207, p = 0.976). Higher molecular weight copolymers provide greater effective interaction area (p ≤ 2.4 × 10–4), systems with q = 14 and 21 show more favorable interactions than q = 5 (p ≤ 0.003), and increased DPCH loading (from 20 or 40 to 126 or 168 molecules) enhances energy reduction (0.013 ≤ p ≤ 0.039).

The temporal evolution of total interaction energy (Figure S9) closely resembles the vdW energy profiles in Figure , indicating that hydrophobic interactions are the primary stabilizing force within the micellar core.

Agreement between Molecular Simulations Outcomes and Experimental Data

The obtention of drug-loaded micelles confirmed the predictions derived from theoretical analyses. A close correlation was found between the molecular simulation predictions and the experimental measurements. It is worth noting that both revealed no statistically significant differences depending on the type of encapsulated DPCH, suggesting that mPEG-PCL diblock copolymers are suitable materials for transporting any of these compounds in micellar structures.

Furthermore, both demonstrate marked differences in the nanocarrier behavior and efficiency depending on the molecular weight of the hydrophobic fraction. Although loaded micelles were achieved in all cases, their characterization supported molecular simulation predictions: the copolymer with the highest PCL content produces better encapsulation efficiency, colloidal stability, and size results.

A multivariate comparison was made, including parameters from both approaches, distinguishing between those that give insights into the arrangement and structural organization of the micelles and the ones that describe their effectiveness as carriers. Figure shows these comparisons based on the DPCH type and the copolymer used in each case. All the graphs in this figure present relative differences by illustrating general profiles that do not represent specific magnitudes or involve significant differences. The DM data used for comparison were those resulting from the examination of all simulated configurations. Conclusion

The assessment of the interactions between mPEG-PCL copolymers and DPCH loaded systems, via molecular simulation, has established the suitability of utilizing these materials as drug delivery vehicles. Initially, a favorable affinity was predicted between PCL and each of them as the calculated log P values of the DPCH derivatives ranged from 8.0 to 9.7. The predicted effective encapsulation was supported by several key factors: the inferred noncovalent interactions derived from van der Waals energy values (E vdW) that showed favorable values in all cases, the stability conferred by the copolymer to DPCH molecules indicated by root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) data, the expected degree of system packing based on the radius of gyration (R g), denoting cluster formation in the most cases, the observed reduction in solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) compared to free compound molecules, and the core spatial organization as determined by the radial distribution function (g(r)) calculations that confirms DPCH molecules preference for PCL fraction. Further experimental investigations, incorporating direct observation and measurement of properties such as particle morphology, size, colloidal stability, and encapsulation efficiency, validated the derived results, with both analyses demonstrating the superior performance of the copolymer with the highest molecular weight. Although no significant differences were found depending on the type of compound, clear trends suggested the existence of more favorable interactions of 2b and 2c with the copolymers. Upon confirming the endocytosis of loaded micelles based on the copolymer with the highest molecular weight, through fluorescence microscopy, it was noted that the fluorescence intensity increased significantly thanks to its encapsulation. This enhancement can be attributed to the protective capabilities and aqueous dispersion afforded by the carrier, in conjunction with the advantages associated with the reduced size of the micelles.

These results demonstrated the potential of the computational tools employed to analyze the use of an amphiphilic copolymer to encapsulate DPCHs, facilitating their subsequent application without the impediments associated with poor water solubility.
