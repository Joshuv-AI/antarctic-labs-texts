Pediatric non-rhabdomyosarcoma bladder tumors (NRBTs): a 20-year retrospective longitudinal cohort study at a tertiary referral center
Authors: Yangyue Huang, Yiwei Fang, Hongcheng Song, Weiping Zhang, Ning Sun, Wenwen Han, Guannan Wang, Chao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric non-rhabdomyosarcoma bladder tumors (NRBTs) encompass a diverse spectrum of tumors with low incidence rates. This study aims to improve understanding and contribute to the management of pediatric NRBTs.MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled patients with primary pediatric NRBTs diagnosed and treated at our center between 2003 and 2023. Clinicodemographic and clinicopathological data were collected. NRBTs were classified into four categories: urothelial tumors (UTs), mesenchymal tumors (MTs), neuroendocrine tumors (NTs), and rare types of bladder tumors (rBTs). Intergroup comparative analyses were conducted.ResultsWe identified 51 cases of NRBTs. Four categories encompassed: UTs (n=25, 49.0%), MTs (n=17, 33.3%) and NTs (n=5, 9.8%), and rBTs (n=4, 7.8%). No cases of UTs were identified below the age of 3 years. Comparative analyses demonstrated significant intergroup differences in preoperative anemia prevalence (P=0.02) and tumor size (P<0.001). Total 45 patients (88.2%) completed follow-up, with a median duration of 64 months [interquartile range (IQR), 28–113 months]. Local recurrences were identified in 5 cases (11.1%), among whom 1 case (2.0%) succumbed to local recurrence and disease progression.ConclusionsPediatric NRBTs demonstrate generally favorable clinical outcomes. Age under 3 years, preoperative anemia or a maximum tumor diameter exceeding 3 cm should raise a strong suspicion for non-UTs bladder tumors. Postoperative surveillance protocols can incorporate urinary ultrasonography (US) as a non-invasive monitoring tool.Keywords: Non-rhabdomyosarcoma bladder tumors (NRBTs), children, urothelial tumors (UTs), mesenchymal tumors (MTs), neuroendocrine tumors (NTs)

Introduction

Pediatric bladder tumors represent a clinicopathologically distinct entity from adult counterparts. While urothelial tumors (UTs) dominate among adults, the pediatric bladder tumor spectrum differs considerably, with rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) being the most common diagnosis in children. Population-based study reports an annual RMS incidence of only 4.71 per million among individuals under 20 years in the United States, with just 8% originating in the genitourinary tract (bladder/prostate) (1). Non-rhabdomyosarcoma bladder tumors (NRBTs) in children are even rarer; pediatric UTs, for instance, account for only 0.4% of all UTs (2). Incidence of other NRBT subtypes remains largely unreported. Compounding this rarity is the limited collective of clinical experiences, with only a few centers publishing their reports of diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for pediatric bladder tumors (3,4). Furthermore, NRBTs comprise a histologically heterogeneous group, including inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT), papillary urothelial neoplasm of low malignant potential (PUNLMP), paraganglioma (PGL), etc., which considerably complicates preoperative diagnosis.

Low incidence rate, histological heterogeneity and paucity of high-quality clinical evidence contribute to management challenges. Critical gaps persist in standardizing diagnostic modality, treatment protocols, and surveillance strategies. To address these gaps, we conducted a retrospective analysis of NRBTs cases spanning over 20 years at our tertiary referral center. By far, this is the largest pediatric NRBTs cohort, including first-hand reported cases. This study systematically reviewed the clinicopathological data of pediatric NRBTs in our center, aiming to establish diagnostic, therapeutic and surveillance strategies for clinical practice. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2025-1-963/rc).

Methods

Patient selection and data collection

We retrospectively reviewed electronic medical records of bladder tumors using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes combined with the histopathological diagnoses in our center between January 2003 and December 2023. The ICD-10 codes used to identify patients included: bladder tumor (N32.901), tumor at bladder neck (N32.902), malignant bladder tumor (C67.901), multiple malignant bladder tumor (C67.902), and benign bladder tumor (D30.301). Inclusion criteria were patients pathologically diagnosed with primary urinary bladder tumors at our center. Exclusion criteria included: (I) patients diagnosed with tumor-like lesions of urinary bladder such as polypoid cystitis, urothelial hyperplasia, fibroepithelial polyp, and nephrogenic adenoma; (II) patients diagnosed with RMS; (III) patients with underlying conditions, such as neurogenic bladder, history of bladder exstrophy, and history of bladder augmentation; (IV) patients not initially surgically treated at our center; (V) patients elder than 18 years.

Clinical characteristics, treatment, follow-up and outcomes were collected for each case. Tumor size was measured as the largest diameter of the bladder tumor with urinary ultrasonography (US). If chest, abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan or bone scintigraphy suggested possible distant metastatic lesions, positron emission tomography (PET)-CT examination was performed to identify metastatic lesions. The histopathology of NRBTs was reviewed by specialized pediatric genitourinary pathologists. Patients were recommended to choose a routine urine test and US as a regular surveillance tool every 3 months during the initial year and every year for the next 4 years. Lower urinary tract symptoms were also collected during follow-up to identify patients with lower urinary tract dysfunction. The ending of follow-up for recurrent cases was defined as radiographic detection of recurrent lesions. A diagnostic, therapeutic and surveillance algorithm for NRBTs was depicted based on our clinical experiences. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This work has been approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Children’s Hospital (No. [2025]-Y-031-E). Individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Statistical analyses

NRBTs were classified into four categories: (I) UTs; (II) mesenchymal tumors (MTs); (III) neuroendocrine tumors (NTs); and (IV) rare types of bladder tumors (rBTs). Statistical analysis was performed by using the IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while continuous variables with skewed distributions were presented as median [interquartile range (IQR)]. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Differences of continuous variables between categories were compared by using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables between categories. For continuous variables, if a statistically significant difference was identified among multiple groups, pairwise comparisons were performed with the Bonferroni correction; for categorical variables, Tamhane’s T2 correction was applied. The complete-case analysis was adopted to handle missing data in the subgroup comparative analyses. Patients who were lost to follow-up after a period of observation were treated as censored data. The event-free survival (EFS) of NRBTs, UTs, NTs, MTs and rBTs was calculated and plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

Results

Clinical characteristics and presentations

Between January 2003 and December 2023, 173 bladder tumors were initially treated at our center. Fifty-one NRBTs (29.5%) were included, comprising 34 males and 17 females (ratio 2:1) with a median age of 9.4 years (range, 0.9–17.8 years). Gross hematuria was the predominant symptom (n=35, 68.6%), followed by bladder irritation signs (n=13, 25.5%), dysuria (n=9, 17.6%), anemia (n=8, 15.7%), and hypertension (n=3, 5.9%). Histological distribution included 25 cases (49.0%) of UTs, 17 cases (33.3%) of MTs, 5 cases (9.8%) of NTs and 4 cases (7.8%) of rBTs. Clinical features were summarized in Table 1. The proportion of UTs, MTs and NTs accounting for pediatric bladder tumors were 14.5% (25/173), 9.8% (17/173) and 2.9% (5/173), respectively. All patients underwent US at diagnosis, with CE-CT and MRI performed in 49.0% and 15.7%, respectively. Axial CT images of UTs, MTs and NTs are presented in Figure 1A-1C. Significant differences were observed in tumor proportion, bladder irritation signs, anemia, hypertension, catecholamine-associated symptom (CAS) and tumor size (P<0.05). Pairwise comparisons revealed significant differences in tumor proportion between the UTs and MTs groups (P<0.025), and when comparing the UTs and MTs groups separately with the NTs group (both P<0.001). For bladder irritation signs and anemia, pairwise comparisons among the three groups showed significant differences (P<0.001). Regarding hypertension or CAS, significant differences were observed when comparing the UTs and MTs groups separately with the NTs group (both P<0.001). In terms of tumor size, pairwise comparisons indicated significant differences between the UTs and MTs groups (P=0.001) and between the UTs and NTs groups (P=0.02). However, no significant difference was found between the MTs and NTs groups (P>0.99). Histopathological features of UTs, MTs and NTs were demonstrated in Figure 2. During a median follow-up of 64 months (IQR, 28–113 months), local recurrence occurred in 5 cases (11.1%), among whom 1 case (2.0%) succumbed to disease progression. Details of local recurrence were presented in Tables 1,2. As all six children lost to follow-up in this study had no postoperative follow-up visits and were therefore excluded from the survival analyses. The 5-year EFS was 87.0% overall, and 89.7%, 75.0%, 91.7%, and 75.0% for UTs, MTs, NTs, and rBTs, respectively (Figure 3). Throughout the surveillance periods, no patients reported lower urinary tract symptoms or exhibited abnormalities on US. Urodynamic evaluation was reserved for symptomatic cases or those with sonographic abnormalities. A diagnostic, therapeutic and surveillance algorithm for NRBTs is depicted in Figure 4. For patients detected of bladder tumors, a comprehensive clinical evaluation is the essential first step. In cases suspected of paraganglioma of the urinary bladder (UBPGL), biochemical testing is mandatory. This screening allows for identification of UBPGL and enables necessary preoperative therapy to prevent severe intraoperative hemodynamic instability. Tumor biopsy can be obtained through cystoscopy and tumors confined to the mucosal layer with a small tumor size can be completely resected via cystoscopy. For larger tumors or those invading the muscular layer, open or minimally invasive surgery (MIS) is recommended to ensure complete resection. Patients should strictly follow the surveillance protocol after surgery. For PUNLMP and low-grade non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), urinalysis combined with US can be chosen as a first-line tool to monitor postoperative local recurrence. Cystoscopy is indicated if tumor recurrence is suspected. Cases of unresectable, rapid progression and metastatic UBPGL require systemic therapy. Genetic testing should also be conducted in these scenarios.

UTs

The cohort included 25 UTs: 19 urothelial papilloma (UP) (76.0%), 1 inverted papilloma (4.0%), and 5 PUNLMP (20.0%). No cases of low-grade (LG) or high-grade (HG) non-invasive papillary urothelial carcinoma were identified. Total 12 cases (48.0%) of UTs were aged under 10 years. Most UTs (96.0%) were smaller than 3 cm, with median sizes of 2.0 cm for UP and 2.3 cm for PUNLMP. Open resection was performed in 76.0% of patients, including one case with concomitant ureteral reimplantation for peri-orifice involvement. Transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURB) was used in five cases (four piecemeal, one en bloc), and a transvesicoscopic approach in one. All open and transvesicoscopic resections achieved excision with negative margins, and no patient required secondary management. Two PUNLMP cases received adjuvant intravesical pirarubicin; the others received no additional therapy. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining results showed that p53 was positive in four PUNLMP, and the median Ki-67 index was 5.0%. Surveillance US was performed in 21 patients (84.0%) who completed follow-up. During follow-up, two patients (one UP, one PUNLMP) developed local recurrence as PUNLMP at 12 and 26 months, both of whom were successfully treated with tumor resection and intravesical chemotherapy of pirarubicin.

MTs

Seventeen cases of MTs were histologically confirmed, comprising 14 IMTs (82.4%), 2 hemangiomas (11.8%), and 1 lymphangioma (5.9%). The median age at IMT diagnosis was 8.6 years (IQR, 5.9–10.6 years), with a 1:1 gender distribution. Common presentations included gross hematuria (57.1%), bladder irritation signs (64.3%), and anemia (28.6%). Among them, one was diagnosed with mild anemia, three with moderate anemia. Two cases with moderate anemia required preoperative blood transfusion. The median IMT size was 4.5 cm (IQR, 3.8–6.1 cm). Most MTs (76.5%) and IMTs (71.4%) were located in non-trigonal areas, and all were unifocal. All IMTs were managed via open surgery: five by biopsy, eight by partial cystectomy, and one with additional ureteral reimplantation. Hemangioma and lymphangioma cases also underwent partial cystectomy. Secondary management was performed in 5 (29.4%) biopsy-confirmed cases. All resections achieved R0 margins. Anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) IHC staining was positive in 92.3% (12/13) of IMT. With a median follow-up of 7.0 years (IQR, 3.9–9.3 years), no IMT cases developed recurrence or metastasis. One hemangioma recurred at 47 months and was successfully resected, with no further recurrence over 16.4 years.

NTs

All five NTs were UBPGL. Three cases (60.0%) had hypertension and catecholamine-associated symptoms, among them two with headaches and one with palpitations after micturition. They received preoperative biochemistry testing and α and β-adrenoceptor blockade for hemodynamic stabilization. Two asymptomatic patients underwent surgery without pretreatment; one developed intraoperative systolic hypertension over 180 mmHg. All patients underwent partial cystectomy, 2 (40.0%) with concomitant ureteral reimplantation. One case with iliac lymph node metastasis identified on preoperative PET-CT underwent en bloc dissection and tested positive for an SDHB germline mutation (chromosome 1p36.13). R0 resection was achieved in four patients; one had R1 resection. During follow-up, US suggested a local recurrence nodule in the patient with R1 resection at 6 months after surgery, along with possible enlargement and metastasis of pelvic lymph nodes. The patient was subsequently referred to another hospital for further treatment, including blood pressure control and targeted agents. The patient remains alive at the last follow-up. The remaining three showed no recurrence or metastasis at a median follow-up of 1.9 years (IQR, 1.3–9.7 years). One patient was lost to follow-up.

rBTs

The clinical features, management, and outcomes of the four rBTs, all malignant, are summarized in Table 2. The age distribution ranged from 0.9 to 12.5 years. Hematuria was the most common presentation (75.0%), and tumor size varied from 2.4 to 7.2 cm. Two patients underwent partial cystectomy, while the other two underwent initial biopsy due to being suspected of RMS, followed by definitive resection. During a follow-up ranging from 1 to 8.8 years, one patient died from disease progression, while the other three remained free from recurrence or metastasis.

Discussion

Pediatric NRBTs constitute a histologically heterogeneous group of rare tumors, with current evidence predominantly derived from small-scale, single-center series (2-5). This study presents the largest single-institutional experience of pediatric NRBTs cohort to date, comprising 51 cases diagnosed over two decades. Among them, both cases of alveolar soft part sarcoma (ASPS) and epithelioid sarcoma (ES) were reported as NRBTs for the first time. Clinical outcomes were favorable, with an 11.1% local recurrence rate and 97.8% overall survival at a median follow-up of 64 months (IQR, 28–113 months). Based on this cohort, we focus specifically on optimizing therapeutic management and surveillance protocols.

UTs

Even though UTs were the most prevalent NRBTs in our cohort, they only ranked as the second most frequent bladder tumors overall—a deviation from previous studies (3,4,6). This discrepancy may be attributed to our cohort’s age distribution, with 54.9% of patients under 10 years (6,7). Urinary cytology was not routinely included in our diagnostic protocol due to its limitations in detecting LG/G1 NMIBC lesions (8). Location of UTs demonstrated interstudy variability, with trigonal involvement predominating in our cohort (9). Given that complete resection (R0 resection) was prioritized and over 50% of NRBTs had a non-epithelial origin, TURB was not routinely performed. Discordant with existing literature, urothelial carcinoma was not identified in this study due to our cohort’s younger age profile (7,9). While the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines recommend cystoscopy for postoperative surveillance of NMIBC, US was used as the primary modality in our institution (8,9). However, the guidelines also indicate that for Ta LG/G1-2 urothelial carcinoma, urinary US could be an alternative to cystoscopy if that is not available during surveillance (8). Urinary US leverages its high sensitivity, non-radiation exposure, and absence of discomfort compared to cystoscopy, which requires general anesthesia for applying on children. This strategy aligns with accumulating evidence supporting US as a viable surveillance tool for low-risk NMIBC (8-11). All pediatric PUNLMP showed Ki-67 indices below 10%, distinct from the over 25% threshold correlated to recurrence and progression in adults (12). This observation suggested an indolent tumor biology specific to pediatric cases. Intravesical chemotherapy was rarely administered in our series (only 2 cases), consistent with systematic reviews reporting a utilization rate of 6%—a limitation that precluded meaningful assessment of its efficacy (9). Both of our cases were suspended of intravesical chemotherapy for severe bladder irritation signs. In our cohort, the local recurrence rate was 5% for UP and 20% for PUNLMP, with no disease progression to muscle-invasive bladder cancer, which is consistent with prior study (13). The EAU guidelines note substantially diminished recurrence risk after 5 recurrence-free years in low-risk tumors, and systematic reviews show that most recurrences in pediatric urothelial carcinoma occur within the first 36 months (8,9). Both recurrences in our cohort occurred within 36 months, underscoring the importance of complete follow-up during this period.

MTs

IMT of the bladder is an MT with local invasiveness and recurrence potential, but low metastatic risk. Our cohort’s median age and absence of gender predilection align with previous reports (14). Anemia occurred in 30% of cases (4/14), with 21% (3/14) requiring blood transfusion. Notably, all patients who required transfusion had tumors over 5 cm, underscoring the necessity for hematologic evaluation (14,15). Tumors were predominantly unifocal and located in non-trigonal regions, congruent with previous reports (15). Open resection was essential to achieve R0 margin, particularly given the high recurrence (14–33%) and residual disease rates (92%) associated with TURB (15,16). No recurrences occurred after R0 resection in our series or in a published review of 42 pediatric cases. Preoperative diagnostic biopsy was required in 36% (5/14) of patients due to radiographic similarity to RMS. ALK immunopositivity was observed in 92% of cases, exceeding the published 45–73% prevalence range, yet it showed no correlation with recurrence after a median 7-year follow-up in our cohort (16). Although Coffin et al. associated ALK overexpression with local recurrence, Casanova et al. found no correlation with EFS (17,18). Other systemic therapies, including chemotherapy, ALK inhibitors and anti-inflammatory drugs, can be applied for unresectable, recurrent and metastatic IMT, however, that was not identified in this cohort (17). Surveillance was performed using US as recommended in the previous review.

NTs

Pheochromocytomas and paragangliomas (PPGLs) are rare NTs with an estimated annual incidence of 0.6 per million. Pediatric cases demonstrate a predilection for extra-adrenal localization (PGL), constituting 30–60% of childhood cases (19). While UBPGL accounted for less than 0.05% of bladder tumors in adolescents and adults, their proportion in our series reached 2.9%, indicating that there is potential for under-recognition (20). Preoperative suspicion was prompted by hypertension or catecholamine-associated symptoms in 60% (3/5) of cases. Notably, the asymptomatic patient who did not receive preoperative therapy experienced intraoperative systolic hypertension exceeding 180 mmHg. Diagnostic challenges arise from the low prevalence of UBPGL, variable symptomatic presentation, and insufficient biochemical evaluation, collectively accounting for a 39–46% initial underdiagnosis rate (20-22). Preoperative evaluation should be prioritized in cases with tumors larger than 3 cm, multifocal disease, distant metastasis, or a family history of PPGL. UBPGL in our cohort predominantly localized to non-trigonal areas, with a median tumor size of 3.7 cm, concordant with prior literature (20,21,23). Partial cystectomy achieved superior complete resection rates versus TURB (97% vs. 46%), particularly for T3–4 tumors (22,23). Achieving R0 resection remains paramount, yet was accomplished in only 83% of UBPGL cases (24). Systemic therapies, including systemic chemotherapy, targeted agents and targeted radionuclide therapy, are indicated in patients with progressive PPGLs or with visceral tumor burden to prolong survival or as an adjunct to debulking surgery. Local recurrence or metastasis rates were 15–20% (20,23). In our cohort, the sole recurrence (20%) occurred in an R1 resection patient. Genetic testing was only performed in the patient with lymph node metastasis at initial diagnosis in our cohort and germline SDHB pathological variations were identified. Considering the prevalence of hereditary predisposition of PPGL is significantly high in the pediatric population (80%) and UBPGL (63%), genetic testing is recommended irrespective of metastatic status (24).

rBTs

Four malignant rBTs were identified in our cohort. Myoepithelial carcinoma (MEC), a rare aggressive tumor typically associated with salivary glands and characterized by infiltrative growth and high recurrence rates, was diagnosed in one pediatric case (25). This is the second reported pediatric bladder MEC, with the patient succumbing to local recurrence and disease progression at 12 months postoperatively. Up to 15% of yolk sac tumor (YST) arise in extra-gonadal sites, mostly in midline structures (26). Notably, primary bladder YST have been documented exclusively in adult populations, with only two cases reported to date (26). ASPS, a rare mesenchymal malignancy, most frequently occurs in extremities and is associated with 40–65% of synchronous distant metastasis (27). Our case represents the first documented occurrence of ASPS in the bladder, with fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) confirming the ASPSCR1-TFE3 fusion gene. Given the absence of established therapeutic guidelines for this entity, the patient received adjuvant anlotinib following R0 resection, achieving sustained disease-free survival at last follow-up. ES represents an exceptionally rare malignant MT, accounting for less than 1% of all soft tissue sarcomas and is associated with a poor prognosis. This study reported the first case of primary bladder ES, which was managed with complete surgical resection followed by four cycles of VCD (vincristine, cyclophosphamide, and doxorubicin) alternating with IE (ifosfamide and etoposide) chemotherapy. The patient remained disease-free without evidence of recurrence or metastasis at the last follow-up (28).

Diagnosis and management of NRBTs

Preoperative diagnosis of NRBTs remains challenging due to their rarity and histologic diversity. Nevertheless, certain clinical features may facilitate subtype prediction. No UTs cases were identified under the age of 3 years, align with prior studies demonstrating a predominant diagnosis of UTs beyond age of 3 years (7,9). Paner et al. further substantiated that only 1.9% of UTs cases occur in children younger than 5 years among those younger than 20 years (29). Preoperative anemia may indicate muscularis propria involvement and relatively large tumor size, which suggests non-UTs subtypes. Preoperative hypertension or catecholamine-associated symptoms should raise suspicion for UBPGL. In our cohort, 96.0% of UTs measured less than 3 cm, indicating tumors exceeding this threshold have a low probability of being UTs—a finding consistent with data of the EORTC trials (30). US serves as the primary preoperative imaging modality employed in 60% of pediatric urothelial carcinoma cases (9). Although the EAU guidelines recommend cystoscopy for UTs surveillance, 86% of pediatric cases are diagnosed with stage Ta, supporting a less intensive surveillance protocol (8). Moreover, US has demonstrated a favorable diagnostic accuracy, and when combined with microscopic hematuria, a non-inferior negative predictive value is demonstrated compared to cystoscopy in T1-LG NMIBC (11). US is also applicable as a surveillance imaging modality for IMT. For UBPGL, surveillance should integrate clinical, biochemical, and radiographic evaluation. Therefore, our center utilized US as the first-line imaging tool to monitor postoperative local recurrence.

Limitations and future directions

There are several limitations in this study, inherent to its retrospective design and the rarity of NRBTs, which resulted in a limited sample size and potential selection bias. The subgroup analyses may have been underpowered to detect anything but large effect sizes, which not only warrants caution in interpreting negative findings but also underscores the exploratory nature of the comparisons, given the small and imbalanced group sizes. The evolution of diagnostic and management strategies over the 20-year study period, particularly the emergence of MIS as an alternative surgical approach, introduced temporal heterogeneity. While these findings cannot establish causality and have limited generalizability, they offer valuable clinical insights. Future prospective, multi-center registries are needed to generate robust evidence for optimizing NRBT management.

Conclusions

Pediatric NRBTs demonstrate histopathological heterogeneity yet generally favorable clinical outcomes. The low prevalence of each subtype predisposes to preoperative diagnostic challenges. Age under 3 years, preoperative anemia or a maximum tumor diameter exceeding 3 cm should raise a strong suspicion for non-UTs bladder tumors. Postoperative surveillance protocols for local recurrence can incorporate urinary US as a non-invasive monitoring modality for NRBTs in children.
