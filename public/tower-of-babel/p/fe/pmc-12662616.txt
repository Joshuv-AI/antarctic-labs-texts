Assessment of knowledge, attitude, and practice regarding reporting of adverse events due to medical devices among healthcare workers in Gujarat
Authors: Jonsi Tavethia, Samidh Shah, Anshul Bhadania, Shubh Mehta, Prahar Himanshubhai Darji, Kavya Darji, Kinjal Mulubhai Solanki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVE:To assess the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) regarding reporting adverse events due to medical devices among healthcare workers in our hospital.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional, observational, and questionnaire (KAP) study was conducted among healthcare professionals working in the various departments of our hospital. Healthcare professionals from different specialties who volunteered to participate in the study were enrolled. A total of 15 questions were included: 8 based on knowledge (7 scored), 2 on attitude, and 5 on practice. Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel® worksheet, Chi-square, and unpaired t-test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.RESULTS:A total of 370 responses were received. The knowledge of healthcare workers was found to be 73.57%. The mean score (out of 7) for doctors and paramedical staff was 5.78 ± 1.21 and 3.76 ± 1.50, respectively, indicating a wide knowledge gap between them (P < 0.05). Most healthcare workers (63%) reported witnessing fewer than 5 MDAEs. The majority (37.30%) mentioned that the cause of underreporting was a lack of knowledge. Out of all healthcare workers, 85.13% responded positively and are willing to report MDAEs in future, and most of them considered it important to report MDAEs.CONCLUSION:Despite healthcare professionals having adequate knowledge and a positive attitude toward reporting, very poor reporting of MDAEs is observed. Lack of knowledge is a significant barrier leading to underreporting, and a substantial knowledge gap among healthcare professionals is evident.

Introduction

The term “vigilance” refers to the action of being watchful and able to monitor any potential adverse event. Pharmacovigilance relates to drug monitoring and includes the safe use of drugs and vaccines and reporting adverse events due to them. Similarly, materiovigilance deals with the coordinated system of identification, collection, reporting, and analysis of any Medical Device-associated Adverse Effects (MDAEs) and aims to protect patient health by preventing their recurrence.[12] The World Health Organization has defined a medical device as any “instrument, apparatus, implement, machine, appliance, implant, reagent for in vitro use, software, material, or other similar or related article, intended by the manufacturer to be used, alone or in combination, for human beings, for one or more specific medical purposes.”[3] There has been an increase in the number, diversity, and complexity of medical devices used. The Indian market for medical devices exceeds 1.3 billion dollars. Various case reports have highlighted that several unsafe medical devices were being sold.[45] Such unsafe devices have caused more than 1.7 million reported injuries worldwide, and over 83,000 deaths have been reported in the last decade as a result.[4] Despite this, there are insufficient measures to protect patients from the adverse occurrences associated with these devices.[5] For a long period, India lacked a proper regulatory system for reporting adverse reactions due to medical devices.[5] Therefore, it was crucial to address Medical Device-associated Adverse Events (MDAEs). To fulfill this purpose, the Materiovigilance Programme of India (MvPI) was launched on July 6, 2015, with the initiative to monitor MDAEs to generate the safety data, create awareness among various stakeholders, and prescribe best practices for patient safety.[6] It was launched with the objectives to create a nationwide plan for monitoring medical devices, examine the benefit-risk ratio, and collaborate with international health agencies for better information exchange.[7] For MvPI to flourish, participation at the grassroots level must be increased. Awareness among healthcare professionals and the public about MvPI must be amplified. Although it has been 9 years since MvPI was launched, very few studies assessing the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) of materiovigilance among healthcare workers have been conducted. Hence, this study was planned with the aim to assess the knowledge, awareness, and attitude regarding the reporting of medical device-associated adverse reactions at our institution. Our institution is a tertiary teaching hospital in suburban Gujarat with about 2000 outpatient department visits per day.

Materials and Methods

This cross-sectional, observational, and questionnaire-based study was conducted at a tertiary care teaching hospital in Gujarat, India, from August to October 2022. It is a 2000-bedded tertiary care teaching hospital. The study was initiated after obtaining approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee in July (Ref No. EC/Approval/63/2022/18/07/2022). This study included the participation of healthcare professionals working in various departments of the tertiary care teaching hospital. Healthcare professionals, including nursing staff, postgraduate students (resident doctors), biomedical engineers, interns, and medical faculty who were willing to participate were enrolled in the study. A hard copy of the questionnaire was distributed to various departments such as surgery, orthopedics, pathology, medicine, dermatology, gynecology, pediatrics, ohpthalmology, otolaryngology in the hospital. Above departments were chosen due to high use of medical devices in patient treatment and diagnosis. A questionnaire was made after thorough discussion with the pharmacology department and materiovigilance reporting center of the hospital. It was pretested among 40 participants belonging to different departments, where an equal proportion of nursing staff and doctors were maintained. Suitable modified version was finally administered to the willing respondents. A total of 370 responses were collected. The sample size was calculated with the help of a previous study conducted in our hospital about pharmacovigilance and causes of underreporting of drug-related adverse events.[8] This KAP questionnaire consisted of 8 knowledge questions (7 multiple-choice and 1 open-ended), 5 practice-based questions, and 2 attitude-based questions. Knowledge questions that were multiple-choice (7 questions) were scored with one point for correct answers and zero points for incorrect answers. The questions were designed to assess KAP regarding medical device reporting. The questionnaire was designed so that answers were not mutually exclusive. Some questions were open-ended to extract the opinions of the respondents. All those who signed consent were included in the study. In this study, healthcare workers were grouped into two categories: Doctors (including medical college professors, postgraduate students [resident doctors], and interns) and paramedical staff (including nursing staff and biomedical engineers). Data were entered into a Microsoft Excel sheet, and the frequency and percentage of responses were calculated. Data were analyzed using the unpaired t-test and Chi-square method. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall, 370 healthcare workers participated in this study. Of these, 46.21% (171) were resident doctors, 28.37% (105) were nursing staff, 17.57% (65) were interns, 5.14% (19) were medical teachers, and 2.70% (10) were biomedical engineers. Therefore, doctors, as mentioned in the grouping system above, comprised 68.91% (255), and paramedical staff comprised 31.08% (115). Seven closed-ended knowledge questions were assigned scores and were compulsory to attempt. One point was awarded for each correct answer and zero for incorrect answers. Out of the 370 participants, 78 (21.08%) scored seven points, 110 (29.72%) scored six, 80 (21.62%) scored five, 46 (12.43%) scored four, 31 (8.37%) scored three, 20 (5.41%) scored two, and 5 (1.35%) scored one point [Table 1]. The mean score for healthcare workers, the average score for doctors, and the average score for paramedical staff are shown in Figure 1. There was a statistically significant difference in scores obtained by doctors and paramedical staff (P < 0.05) [Figure 1].

Discussion

Although medical devices have been used for a long time and many adverse reactions are encountered, reporting these MDAEs remains a new concept for many healthcare workers, who often lack awareness of the process. Despite the establishment of 380 reporting centers, the reporting of device-related reactions remains significantly lower than their occurrence rate. While numerous KAP studies have been conducted on pharmacovigilance among healthcare professionals, very few have addressed materiovigilance.

Our study shows a good response rate among healthcare workers, with a large sample size of 370 collected within 2 months. The overall response rate from doctors was 68.91% (255), and from paramedical staff, it was 31.08% (115). Other studies in India, such as the one by Meher et al.,[9] reported a response rate of approximately 76% with 138 healthcare workers, including 105 (45 faculty and 60 residents) who returned completely answered questionnaires. Similarly, Panchal et al.[10] collected 156 responses from surgeons, with the majority (n = 54, 34.6%) being general surgeons, followed by orthopedics (n = 30, 19.2%).

In this study, the mean score of healthcare workers was 5.15 ± 1.50, indicating good knowledge about MvPI. The mean score for doctors was 5.7 ± 1.21, whereas the average score for paramedical staff was 3.76 ± 1.50, with a P < 0.05 indicating a significant difference in knowledge between the two groups. This highlights the need for various interventions, such as lectures, seminars, and group discussions on materiovigilance, to enhance the knowledge and potentially increase reporting rates.

Among the 370 participants, 75.68% were aware of the MvPI. Of the doctors, 81.96% answered correctly, while 61.74% of paramedical staff did so. The study by Panchal et al.[10] found that 71.8% of participants were aware of the reporting system, whereas Meher et al.[9] reported that 40% of paramedical staff believed not all healthcare practitioners could report MDAEs, which could act as a barrier to reporting. In addition, 33.79% of healthcare workers feared legal implications, with 21.96% of doctors and 60% of paramedical staff being unaware and thinking that reporting had legal consequences. Kurien et al.[11] and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) have noted that legal liability concerns are the significant barriers to reporting.[12] Similarly, Kingston et al. found that fear of litigation was a major barrier in Australia.[13] To address this, the concerned department should educate doctors about the program and clarify that no legal action can be taken based on these reports.

The majority (70.54%) of healthcare workers were aware of the apex center, the Shri Chitra Institute of Medical Science. In addition, 91.89% were aware that all device-related adverse reactions should be reported. However, only 25.10% of doctors and 12.20% of paramedical staff knew of at least one reporting center, which indicates a need for improved knowledge and awareness to encourage voluntary reporting of MDAEs.

Most healthcare workers (63%, or 234) encountered 0–5 MDAEs per month, and 64.32% reported witnessing at least one adverse event. Despite this, the reporting rate remains very low, consistent with the study by Panchal et al.,[10] where 51.9% of participants experienced adverse events due to medical devices but only 9% reported them. Similarly, Meher et al.[9] found that only 19% of participants reported adverse events. Under-reporting of medical device-induced adverse events is a common issue worldwide, with only 0.5% reported to the FDA.[12] The most common reason cited for underreporting was a lack of knowledge about how to report, mentioned by 37.30% of healthcare workers. Inman[14] summarized reasons for under-reporting as the “seven deadly sins,” and improving knowledge about the importance of reporting and the reporting system is essential to address these deficits.

Implementing educational interventions, providing acknowledgment, and offering feedback to reporters can help improve reporting practices. Gagliardi et al.[15] identified barriers to reporting, including a lack of a proper reporting system and an unfavorable environment. No significant influence of age or designation on responses was observed, suggesting that enhancing awareness through interventions could improve reporting culture among medical professionals regardless of age or position.

Regarding preferred reporting methods, 45% (167) of healthcare workers would directly email the filled form to mvpi@sctimst.ac.in, 32% (119) would fill out the MDAE report form directly, and 23% (84) would call the helpline number. To encourage reporting, various methods should be introduced, making the process more accessible and time-efficient. The MDAE reporting form is currently a lengthy, five-page document available only in English, which may hinder reporting. Therefore, forms in multiple languages should be introduced, and government measures to streamline reporting processes should be considered. Mobile applications for reporting MDAEs should be promoted and designed to be user-friendly.

A total of 85.13% of healthcare workers responded positively and are willing to report adverse reactions in the future, with 85.49% of doctors and 84.34% of paramedical staff indicating they would report adverse reactions in their practice. The Chi-square analysis revealed no significant difference between doctors and paramedical staff in their outlook on reporting MDAEs. A similar positive attitude was observed in studies by Panchal et al.,[10] Meher et al.,[9] and Kurien et al.[11] However, Gagliardi et al. found a negative attitude toward reporting medical device-induced adverse events, with participants considering reporting unnecessary.[15]

Conclusion

There were few limitations of this study. First, the study lacks equal distribution of both the group, i.e., the number of residents and nursing staff were not equal. Second, it is a single-center study. Multicentric study could provide better validation. Furthermore, the association of this hospital with a materiovigilance center could have possibly lead to more awareness among participants. Despite these limitations, this study does provide insight into the possible interventions that could be planned in the future. Study shows a wide gap between knowledge on this subject among doctors and paramedical staff. Adequate knowledge and a positive attitude toward reporting MDAEs are found in healthcare professionals, yet an attitude and practice of underreporting of MDAEs is observed.

These gaps can be overcome by conducting training programs such as continuous medical education and workshops by experts in the field.
