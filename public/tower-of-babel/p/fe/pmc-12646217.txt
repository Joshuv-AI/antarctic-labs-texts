Auxin transport positions stem cells in the vascular cambium during normal development and regeneration
Authors: Xixi Zhang, Xiaoyu Wang, Ondřej Smetana, Meeri Mäkelä, Riikka Mäkilä, Enrico Scarpella, Jing Zhang, Ari Pekka Mähönen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificancePlants are frequently subjected to injury, yet they possess remarkable ability to regenerate tissues or organs. Stem cells of vascular cambium produce xylem (wood) and phloem. When vascular cambium and the overlaying bark are removed because of injury, new vascular cambium and bark are regenerated from remaining trunk. To study this regeneration process in detail, we selectively eliminate various cell types in vascular tissues of Arabidopsis thaliana and show that when vascular cambial stem cells are lost, neighboring xylem cells can divide and replace the lost stem cells, ensuring regeneration. This regeneration process is associated with altered auxin signaling and requires auxin transport. This study deepens our knowledge of how vascular cambial stem cells are regenerated at the cellular level.

Postembryonic growth in plants relies on the activity of stem cells in apical and lateral meristems. Apical meristems are responsible for the elongation of roots and shoots, whereas lateral meristems, especially the vascular cambium (cambium hereafter), contribute to organ thickening (1). Primary vascular development in the root apical meristem (RAM) results in the formation of primary xylem and phloem cell files, with undifferentiated procambial cell files between them. All those cell types are surrounded by pericycle cells. After leaving the RAM, procambial and pericycle cells remain quiescent for 2 to 3 d before starting to divide periclinally—the hallmark of the activation of secondary growth (2, 3). Lineage tracing studies in Arabidopsis roots show that the cambium originates from xylem–pole pericycle (XPP) cells and primary xylem–connected procambial cells. This suggests that primary xylem provides positional cues that define stem cells in adjacent cells. During the active phase of secondary growth, the mature cambium of the Arabidopsis root consists of two to five layers of concentric undifferentiated meristematic cells. In each radial cell file, one of those undifferentiated meristematic cells functions as a bifacial stem cell, producing both secondary xylem and phloem: each stem cell division gives rise to another stem cell and a cell with either xylem or phloem identity (4).

Auxin is the main regulator of cambium development (4–10). Inhibition of auxin signaling reduces secondary growth in both tree stems and Arabidopsis roots (4, 7). Cell-resolution imaging shows that in Arabidopsis roots auxin signaling is highest in the xylem side of the cambium (4). Additionally, the width of the auxin gradient within the cambium regulates the fate of stem cell daughters (10). Furthermore, induction of an ectopic clone of high levels of auxin signaling in secondary tissues leads to differentiation of the clone as xylem vessel. Such differentiation is followed by activation of cambium-like cell divisions in cells adjacent to the clone (4). Induction of a clone expressing VASCULAR-RELATED NAC-DOMAIN 6 (VND6), a master regulator of terminal differentiation of vessels (11), also leads to differentiation of the clone as xylem vessel, but the VND6 clone fails to induce cambium-like cell divisions in adjacent cells (4). Those findings suggest that it is the local auxin maximum rather than the chemical or physical properties of terminally differentiated vessels that defines the cambial stem-cell organizer, which in turn positions stem cells in neighboring cells (4). However, it is unknown whether auxin is upstream of the signaling hierarchy also during normal development.

Microsurgical and laser-based cell elimination techniques have been used to study the overall relationship between different cell types in Arabidopsis apical meristems (12–15). However, physically ablating procambium and cambium cells is problematic because of their embedded location in plant tissues. Genetic ablation using toxic proteins, such as the diphtheria toxin A-chain (DT-A) (16) or the proapoptotic protein Bcl-2 Associated X-protein (Bax) (17) provides a potential tool to eliminate cells in the procambium and cambium. Promoters specific to stem cells, as well as those promoters associated with xylem and phloem identity, have been identified (4). However, constitutive expression of a toxic protein under those promoters would lead to complex phenotypes because ablation of one cell type often leads to respecification of the same cell type elsewhere in the meristem (13). As such, constitutive ablation would lead to undesired feed-forward ablation events.

Here, we investigated interactions between vascular cell types in Arabidopsis roots by ablation of targeted cell types through cell type–specific, transient induction (18) of mitochondrial-localized murine-Bax (mBax) (19). Our results show that ablation of cells with primary xylem identity (PXI) within the RAM leads to mispositioning of stem cells in a different region within the vascular bundle. Therefore, already during the primary development, these xylem identity cells act as stem-cell organizers, guiding the specification of adjacent cells as stem cells. Moreover, a combination of ablation and of chemical and genetic inhibition of auxin transport shows during regeneration as well as during primary and secondary development, the position of stem cells depends on auxin transport and its ability to dynamically establish an auxin maximum.

Results

Establishment of the mBax-Mediated Conditional Cell Ablation System.

To test whether plant cells can be conditionally ablated in a cell type–specific manner, we integrated mBax (19) into the 17-β-estradiol (EST)-inducible XVE vector system under a cell type–specific promoter (18, 20) (Fig. 1A). The ablation and subsequent regeneration of quiescent center (QC) cells in the RAM has been extensively studied using laser ablation (13, 15, 21). Therefore, to assess whether mBax expression can effectively eliminate targeted cells, we expressed mBax by the QC-specific, inducible promoter of WUSCHEL-RELATED HOMEOBOX 5 (WOX5) (18, 22) (Fig. 1B).

After a 10-h EST induction of pWOX5:XVE>>mBax-YFP; pWOX5:GFP seedlings, mBax-YFP was expressed in QC cells (Fig. 1B). After transferring seedlings to EST-free medium for a 1-d recovery, we observed QC cell death, as indicated by PI uptake (Fig. 1B). During a 2-d recovery period, pWOX5:GFP expression was initiated in former cortex/endodermis stem cells and then confined to former procambial cells, consistent with the effects induced by laser-assisted QC cell elimination (13, 15).

In conclusion, transient, cell type–specific induction of mBax-YFP is an effective tool for specifically ablating targeted cell types.

Ablation of Cells with PXI in RAMs Leads to Relocation of Stem Cells within the Vascular Bundle.

A subset of cells within the RAM possesses PXI (hereafter PXI cells, SI Appendix, Fig. S1A), before differentiating into primary xylem (i.e., cells within xylem axis). Our previous lineage tracing studies led us to hypothesize that PXI cells in the RAM act as stem-cell organizer, since cambial stem cells are located in those procambial and pericycle cells that are adjacent to primary xylem axis (4). If PXI cells function as stem-cell organizers, their ablation should significantly impact cambium organization. To test that, we leveraged the promoter of the ARABIDOPSIS THALIANA HOMEOBOX 8 (ATHB8) gene, which is specifically active in PXI cells (23) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B), to generate a pATHB8:XVE>>mBax-YFP line to ablate PXI cells in an inducible manner (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2).

Upon EST induction, mBax-YFP was expressed in the RAM, leading to the death of PXI cells, as indicated by PI uptake (Fig. 1C). To investigate the consequences of PXI cell ablation on vascular cell identities, we introduced the cambial stem cell reporter pAINTEGUMENTA (ANT):erRFP (10), the phloem identity reporter pPHLOEM-EARLY-DOF 1 (PEAR1):erRFP and xylem identity reporter pATHB8:erRFP (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B) into the pATHB8:XVE>>mBax-YFP line. Induction of mBax-YFP for 1 d (1Ind in the figure panels) and subsequent ablation of PXI cells triggered premature periclinal cell division in XPP cells (Fig. 2A, 1Ind) and premature onset of pANT:erRFP expression in procambial cells (Fig. 2B, 1Ind), occurring approximately 2 d earlier than in controls (Fig. 2B). After 1 d recovery (1Ind+1Rec in the figure panels), pericycle cells continued to divide, and procambial cells started to divide and to express pANT:erRFP and pATHB8:erRFP (Fig. 2 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2C, 1Ind+1Rec). Additionally, unlike in control roots, pPEAR1:erRFP expression appeared in the pericycle cell lineage (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D, 1Ind+1Rec). Three to five days after recovery, scattered, ectopic differentiated vessels emerged within vascular tissues (Fig. 2A, 1Ind+3Rec-5Rec), surrounded by pANT:erRFP (Fig. 2B, 1Ind+3Rec-5Rec) and pATHB8:erRFP (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C, 1Ind+3Rec-5Rec) expression. Furthermore, pPEAR1:erRFP expression broadened, and differentiated sieve elements became visible (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2D, 1Ind+3Rec-5Rec). As a result, at that stage, the root vasculature appeared to contain several small vascular bundles consisting of differentiated vessels, surrounded by cambial cells and phloem. By 12 d of recovery, the scattered vascular bundles had coalesced, and pANT:erRFP expression had become confined to a narrowed domain encircling the xylem (Fig. 2 A and C, 1Ind+12Rec), indicating the formation of a continuous cambium cylinder. These results demonstrate that ablation of PXI cells in the RAM leads to respecification of stem-cell identity elsewhere in the vascular bundle. Thus, these results support our previous lineage tracing studies (4) that PXI cells act as cambial stem-cell organizers, establishing stem cell identity in adjacent procambial cells already during primary growth.

Our previous study suggested that cells with secondary xylem identity (hereafter SXI cells) are stem-cell organizers in the mature cambium (4). To examine the role of SXI cells in cambium development, we induced mBax-YFP expression in the ATHB8 domain of 13-d-old roots, which contain a mature cambium with multiple layers of cambial cell files (SI Appendix, Figs. S1A and S3 A and B). Ablation events occurred only in a subset of the radial cell files due to the restricted expression of pATHB8:XVE>>mBax-YFP at that developmental stage (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C, 1Ind). We observed collapse of SXI cells and respecification of new SXI cells from the stem cells in the same radial cell files as the collapsed SXI cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C, 1Ind+3Rec).

Those results highlight the importance of xylem identity cells as cambial stem-cell organizers for the establishment and maintenance of cambial stem cells. Our results also suggest that if only a subset of xylem identity cells is ablated, the stem cell in the same radial cell file divides to form a new stem cell and stem-cell organizer pair to maintain radial growth. By contrast, when all the xylem identity cells are ablated, new stem cells and their organizer cells need to be established elsewhere in the vascular bundle.

Auxin Transport Drives the Formation and Regeneration of the Cambial Stem-Cell Organizer.

Auxin transport, mediated by PIN-FORMED (PIN) proteins, directs auxin from the shoot toward the root tip, creating a local auxin response maximum in the PXI cells within the RAM. This process is critical for primary xylem specification, differentiation, and for radial patterning of the primary vascular bundle (24). Because cell ablation experiments demonstrated the importance of PXI cells in cambial stem cell positioning, we next studied whether auxin transport and auxin accumulation in the PXI cells are required for organizer formation in its correct position.

Consistent with a role of auxin transport in defining the stem-cell organizer, during the secondary growth of hypocotyls, the high-order pin mutants pin1pin3pin4pin7 and pin1pin3pin4pin6pin7pin8 (25, 26) lacked a defined primary xylem axis and had abnormal vascular patterns, often forming small amphivasal vascular bundles with centrally located phloem, surrounded by dividing cambial cells and differentiated vessels (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). As expected, auxin application did not rescue this phenotype (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A).

Next, to examine whether canonical auxin signaling is required for the formation of the stem-cell organizer, we generated a transgenic line expressing an inducible auxin signaling repressor axr3-1 (27) in PXI cells (pATHB8:XVE>>axr3-1). Unlike in the control, after a 3-d induction of axr3-1, the cells in the primary xylem axis remained undifferentiated (Fig. 3B, 3Ind). Two days after recovery, cambial cell divisions occurred near phloem poles, whereas in control roots, division took place adjacent to the primary xylem axis (Fig. 3B). Those results indicate that when auxin signaling is inhibited in PXI cells, stem cells fail to establish in adjacent cells and instead emerge further away in the vasculature. Those findings underscore the crucial role of auxin transport and derived auxin accumulation in specifying PXI cells as cambial stem-cell organizers in the RAM, ensuring the proper positioning of stem cells in adjacent procambial cells.

These findings also led us to the hypothesis that ablation of PXI cells interrupts auxin transport-directed auxin accumulation in these cells and that the subsequent reorganization of auxin transport machinery leads to the gradual regeneration of cambium. To test that hypothesis, we investigated whether transient inhibition of auxin transport with NPA, followed by NPA washout, would mimic PXI cell ablation and subsequent recovery.

A 7-d NPA treatment led to the failure of primary xylem axis establishment, resulting in a phenotype similar to that of pin multiple mutants. After NPA washout, vessels gradually formed in a scattered pattern within vascular tissues. This was followed by periclinal cell divisions around vessels, indicating cambium establishment (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B–D). Therefore, transient NPA treatment resembled the effects of PXI cell ablation.

Next, we crossed DR5v2:erRFP (10) into the pATHB8:XVE>>mBax-YFP line to study auxin signaling dynamics during regeneration. One day after PXI cell ablation, DR5v2:erRFP expression shifted from protoxylem to procambial and pericycle cells (Fig. 3D, 1Ind), confirming an altered auxin response pattern. After a 1-d recovery on EST-free medium, DR5v2:erRFP expression was detected in dividing procambial cells (Fig. 3D, 1Ind+1Rec). Three to five days after recovery, DR5v2:erRFP signals appeared in a scattered pattern associated with differentiating xylem vessels, further supporting the correlation between auxin response maxima and vessel specification (Fig. 3D, 1Ind+3Rec-5Rec). Those results are consistent with our hypothesis that PXI cell ablation triggers rapid and pronounced changes in auxin response, with the formation of new auxin maxima coinciding with the emergence of new stem-cell organizers.

Finally, we tested whether auxin transport is required for cambium respecification following PXI cell ablation. After PXI cell ablation and recovery in the presence of the auxin transport inhibitor NPA for 5 d, DR5v2:erRFP expression was absent. Additionally, we observed no vessel formation or cambium-like cell division, indicating a failure in cambium regeneration (Fig. 3D). Those findings indicate that PIN-dependent auxin transport is essential for the specification of cambial stem cells and their organizers during regeneration.

In conclusion, auxin transport and derived auxin accumulation play a key role in both the initial specification and the regenerative respecification of the cambial stem-cell organizer.

Ablation of Phloem Identity Cells Induces the Respecification of Phloem in Adjacent Procambial Cells.

Sieve elements and their companion cells (together, conductive phloem) are responsible for the transport of sucrose, peptides, and hormones (28). To investigate the role of primary phloem-derived signals in cambium development, we eliminated cells with primary phloem identity by inducing mBax-YFP under the control of pPEAR1:XVE promoter (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). Ablation of these cells resulted in the narrowing of pANT:erRFP expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B) and in the ectopic expression of the conductive phloem-specific reporter pALTERED PHLOEM DEVELOPMENT (APL):erRFP (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C) in procambial cells that are located between phloem poles and the primary xylem axis (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B–D, 1Ind+1Rec and 3Rec). As a result, these cells and the adjacent procambial cells differentiated into sieve elements. Furthermore, unlike in control roots, cambial cell divisions were reduced in phloem-ablated roots, and 12 d after recovery, phloem-ablated roots displayed reduced secondary growth (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 D–F, 1Ind+12Rec).

To examine whether ablation of secondary phloem identity cells have similar consequences, we ablated secondary phloem identity cells by inducing pPEAR1:XVE>>mBax-YFP in the mature cambium (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). Two days after recovery, pAPL:erRFP expression was detected close to ablated cells, indicating respecification of phloem from adjacent cambial cells and phloem parenchyma cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 B and C). Therefore, unlike in the RAM, ablation of secondary phloem identity cells did not inhibit division in the neighboring cambial stem cells.

In conclusion, these findings suggest that both in the RAM and in the mature cambium, phloem ablation leads to rapid formation of new sieve elements to replace the function of the ablated phloem cells. In the presence of a single procambial cell between xylem and phloem, as in the RAM, ablation of phloem identity cell leads to differentiation of adjacent stem cells as sieve elements. However, if the radial cell file contains additional undifferentiated cambial cells, as in the mature cambium, phloem can be respecified from those cells, allowing stem cell activity to be preserved.

Undifferentiated Xylem Cells Function as a Reservoir for Stem Cells During Cambium Regeneration After Ablation of Stem Cells.

Stem cells in the cambium are responsible for producing secondary xylem cells inward and secondary phloem cells outward. To eliminate these cambial stem cells, we induced the expression of mBax-YFP under the control of pANT:XVE promoter in the mature cambium (Fig. 4 A–C and SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S7A). After 1 d of induction, mBax-YFP was detected in the cambial stem cells (Fig. 4B, 1Ind). Although we observed no obvious morphological changes in cambial stem cells through confocal microscopy or histological analysis after 1 d of induction (Fig. 4 B and D, 1Ind), we detected organelle degradation and cell death in these stem cells by transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 4C). We visualized the collapse of cambial stem cells 1 d after recovery using confocal microscopy (Fig. 4B, 1Ind+1Rec) and histological analysis (Fig. 4D, 1Ind+1Rec). In addition, SXI cells, which are normally quiescent and adjacent to the stem cells (4), began to divide periclinally as indicated by newly formed thin cell walls and S phase-specific 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining (29) (Fig. 4 B, D, and E). We detected expression of both the stem cell marker pANT:erRFP and the SXI cell marker pATHB8:erRFP (Fig. 4 A, F, and G, Mock) in cells undergoing regenerative cell division within the xylem domain (Fig. 4 F and G, 1Ind+1Rec). Two to three days after recovery, expression of ANT, ATHB8, and PEAR1 changed. pANT:erRFP expression expanded, correlating with increased cell proliferation on the xylem side of the cambium (Fig. 4 D and F, 1Ind+2Rec and 3Rec). pATHB8:erRFP signals shifted further into xylem parenchyma cells (Fig. 4G, 1Ind+2Rec and 3Rec), suggesting the reestablishment of a new stem-cell organizer in the xylem domain. Expression of the phloem identity cell marker pPEAR1:erRFP was reduced in phloem identity cells distal to the ablated stem cells 1 to 2 d after recovery (Fig. 4H). Simultaneous change in both xylem and phloem marker expression after ablation further supports the bifacial nature of the stem cell. Three days after recovery, new pPEAR1:erRFP signals emerged on the xylem side of the ablated stem cells (Fig. 4H, 1Ind+3Rec), followed by pAPL:erRFP expression and sieve element differentiation (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B, 1Ind+6Rec), demonstrating the production of new conductive phloem from the regenerated stem cells. By 8 to 9 d after recovery, regenerative cell divisions, marked by pANT:erRFP expression, along with xylem and phloem formation associated with their respective markers, became confined to a narrow domain, indicating the establishment of an organized cambium (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 C–E, 1Ind+8Rec-9Rec). These observations show that SXI cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B) serve as the main source for regeneration when cambial stem cells are ablated in the mature cambium.

To further investigate the role of undifferentiated xylem cells (i.e., PXI or SXI cells) in cambium regeneration, we ablated procambial cells just before cambium activation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A), when no undifferentiated xylem cells are present (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and B). After the successful ablation of procambial cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S8C, 1Ind), we observed no cambium regeneration in the remaining radial cell files (SI Appendix, Fig. S8D, 1Ind+4Rec). The remaining living cells consist of periderm, containing undifferentiated, dividing cells, yet we did not observe vascular cambium regeneration in these cells. These findings together with results of PXI cell ablations indicate that the presence of undifferentiated xylem cells or (pro)cambial cells is essential for regeneration of cambium. If one of the two cell types is ablated, the cambium can regenerate from the remaining cell type. If both cell types are absent, such as following the ablation of procambial cells just before cambium activation, the cambium is unable to regenerate despite the presence of neighboring dividing periderm cells.

Auxin Transport-Dependent Auxin Reallocation within Xylem Domain Guides the Restoration of Cambial Stem Cells After Ablation.

To monitor auxin dynamics during regeneration of cambial stem cells, we introduced the auxin response reporter DR5v2:erRFP into the pANT:XVE>>mBax-YFP line. In control roots, maxima of DR5v2:erRFP expression were observed in SXI cells adjacent to dividing cambial stem cells, consistent with previous findings (4, 10) (Fig. 5A). After ablation of cambial stem cells and recovery for 1 d, DR5v2:erRFP signals decreased in SXI cells that are adjacent to dividing cells (Fig. 5A, 1Ind+1Rec). After recovery for 2 to 3 d, the maximum of DR5v2:erRFP expression shifted deeper into xylem parenchyma, adjacent to the respecified cambial stem cells (Fig. 5A, 1Ind+2Rec, 1Ind+3Rec). In summary, the maximum of DR5v2:erRFP expression shifts centripetally during cambial stem cells regeneration, suggesting a role for auxin in cambial stem cell replenishment.

We then tested whether PIN-mediated auxin transport plays a role in cambial stem cell regeneration, similar to its role in regeneration of the stem-cell organizer (Fig. 3D). pANT:XVE>>mBax-YFP seedlings were transferred to medium supplemented with NPA after being treated with either mock or EST for 1 d. DR5v2:erRFP signals diminished after NPA treatment in both mock- and EST-treated roots (Fig. 5A, 1Ind+1Rec), indicating reduced auxin response. We found that the ratio of cambial stem cell regeneration was significantly reduced in roots treated with NPA (Fig. 5 A and B, 1Ind+1Rec-3Rec), suggesting that auxin transport is required for cambial stem cell regeneration. Next, we independently tested the role of auxin transport by visualizing cambial stem cell regeneration after ablation in the pin1-7 mutant background. Consistent with the results of NPA experiments, the ratio of cambial stem cell regeneration in pin1-7 mutant was reduced (Fig. 5 C and D, 1Ind+1Rec-3Rec). Auxin treatment failed to rescue this reduced regeneration in the pin1-7 mutant background, indicating the importance of auxin transport and its distribution, not just the overall levels, in the stem cell regeneration (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). In conclusion, our results demonstrate that PIN-mediated auxin transport is required for cambial stem cell regeneration.

Discussion

Our previous cell lineage-tracing experiments in Arabidopsis roots demonstrated that primary xylem-contacting procambial cells and XPPs formed the stem cells of cambium (4), suggesting a crucial role for primary xylem cells and their precursors, PXI cells, in cambium establishment. In this study, we demonstrated that mBax-mediated, targeted elimination of PXI cells resulted in the loss of cambial stem cell identity at their original positions and the subsequent reestablishment of the cambium from the remaining procambial cells. These findings highlighted the essential role of PXI cells as organizers, providing positional cue necessary for specifying stem cell identity in the adjacent procambial and pericycle cells. We further demonstrated that inhibition of auxin transport or repression of auxin signaling specifically in PXI cells prevented the formation of the primary xylem axis and the correct positioning of stem cells. These findings align with our previous findings that clones with high auxin signaling in ectopic location can specify the cambial stem-cell organizer (4). Taken together, our current and previous findings demonstrate that auxin transport–driven auxin maxima specify the position of the cambial stem cell organizer already during primary development, within the procambium of the RAM.

We also used the mBax-mediated ablation to study cambium regeneration in the mature cambium. During normal development at this stage, as the xylem identity cell (the organizer) differentiates, the adjacent stem cell daughter acquires organizer identity (4, 30). We found that upon ablation of cambial stem cells, undifferentiated xylem cells—but not undifferentiated phloem—underwent regenerative proliferation to restore stem cells. This demonstrates the role of undifferentiated xylem cells as stem cell reservoir. In return, ablation of SXI cells in the mature cambium led to the respecification of SXI cells from cambial stem cells located in the same radial cell files. These results show that xylem identity cells and stem cells are functionally interchangeable during regeneration, as the ablation of one prompts regenerative activity in the other. Thus, we propose that these two cell types collectively form a stem cell niche that coordinates radial growth in the Arabidopsis root during normal development. Our results also show that when both xylem identity cells and procambial cells are eliminated, cambium regeneration does not occur in the pericycle or periderm cells. Though the underlying reason remains unclear, we speculate that initiating regeneration in these outermost cell layers might disrupt the structural integrity or function of the protective periderm barrier.

In the mature cambium, we found that auxin transport-mediated auxin accumulation in undifferentiated xylem cells is also required for the resumption of cambial stem cells after injury (Fig. 5E), emphasizing that undifferentiated xylem cells with a high level of auxin function as an organizer in root cambium. Therefore, our results confirmed that xylem cell identity and auxin transport are required not only for cambium establishment and maintenance during normal development, but also for the regeneration following wounding. Similarly, in the RAM, inhibition of auxin transport after ablation of PXI cells suppressed respecification of cambium.

These observations place auxin at the top of the regulatory hierarchy that positions and maintains cambial stem cells during both normal development and regenerative responses. Nevertheless, our ablation experiments likely also disrupted auxin-independent cell-to-cell signaling pathways important for cambium maintenance or regeneration. In support of this, stem cell regeneration was not completely abolished in the pin1 mutant or under NPA treatment, indicating that auxin transport–independent mechanisms act in parallel to regulate normal development and promote regeneration. Nonetheless, the dominant role of auxin raises an important question: What are the downstream factors of auxin during regeneration? During normal development, HD-ZIP III transcription factors, WUSCHEL-RELATED HOMEOBOX 4 (WOX4) and the receptor kinase PHLOEM INTERCALATED WITH XYLEM (PXY) operate downstream of auxin (4). The ligand for PXY, TRACHEARY ELEMENT DIFFERENTIATION INHIBITORY FACTOR (TDIF), is derived from phloem-expressed genes CLAVATA3/ESR-RELATED (CLE) 41, and 44. Experiments and modeling have demonstrated that the balance between auxin and PXY on the xylem side and TDIF on the phloem side of cambium defines the position and cell fates of cambial stem cells (4, 31–33). Thus, it is likely that some of these factors have also important roles in cambium regeneration. Several factors have been identified to convey regeneration of different plant organs. While some of the factors are specific for regeneration in a given organ, other factors mediate regeneration in multiple organs. These include a number of transcription factors such as WOUND INDUCED DEDIFFERENTIATION (WIND) 1 (34), jasmonic acid induced ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR (ERF) 113/RAP2.6L (34, 35), ERF109, 114, and 115 (15, 36, 37) as well as NAC071 and 096 (35, 38). Future studies are needed to explore the roles of known cambial and regeneration factors, as well as to identify potential novel, cambium-specific regulators involved in cambium regeneration.

Vascular regeneration following nontargeted wounding has been intensively studied in trees (39). Studies in populus show that when periderm, phloem, and cambium are removed through bark girdling, differentiating xylem cells can regenerate the cambium (40–42). This is in line with our findings in Arabidopsis roots. However, vascular regeneration in trees has primarily been inspected at the tissue level (40, 41, 43). Additionally, because girdling removes all tissues except the xylem, other cell types are eliminated and therefore cannot potentially contribute to the regeneration process.

Although laser-assisted cell ablation has been successfully applied in apical meristems (12, 13, 15, 21), targeting vascular cells with this technique remains technically challenging due to their internal position within root or shoot tissues. Here, we show that the mBax-mediated genetic ablation system holds strong potential for broader application in other plant species, including trees and crops. Key challenges in woody plants include the identification of cell-type-specific promoters due to the broader and less-defined cambium domains and the establishment of efficient conditional inducible expression systems. Recent advances in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics (44, 45), combined with the successful use of an estradiol-inducible system in Populus (46), highlight the potential for adapting this approach to more complex plant systems.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material and Cloning.

The entry clone 221z-mBax(-)stop was generated by amplifying mBax encoding sequence from the plasmid YEp51-mBax (19) using the indicated primers in SI Appendix, Table S1. The purified PCR product was then recombined into the pDONR221 donor vector, which has zeocin resistance, by performing a Gateway BP (Invitrogen) reaction. Expression vectors were generated by performing a MultiSite LR (Invitrogen) reaction to combine the promoter, gene of interest, tag with terminator, and the destination vector. All entry clones that were used to generate expression vectors are listed in SI Appendix, Table S2 and all expression vectors generated in this study are listed in SI Appendix, Table S3.

The expression vectors were transformed into the Arabidopsis accession Col-0 following the floral dip method. Transgenic lines with single insertion were selected based on the Mendelian segregation of the plant selection markers on the expression vectors. Several independent transgenic lines were selected to observe the expression of mBax-YFP. Plant materials that were used in this study, including those generated in this study and those published in previous studies, are listed in SI Appendix, Table S4. The following Arabidopsis transgenic lines used in this study were previously published: pWOX5:GFP (22), pANT:erRFP (10), pATHB8:erRFP (4), pAPL:erRFP (10) DR5v2:erRFP (10), pin1-7 (47), pin3pin4pin7 (25), pin1pin3pin4pin7 (25), pin1pin3pin4pin6pin7pin8 (25).

Plant Growth Condition and Sampling Procedure.

Arabidopsis seeds were surface sterilized with 70% ethanol for 10 min and then washed with sterile water for three times or sterilized with Cl2 gas for 4 h. Sterilized Arabidopsis seeds were then plated on the plates with half-strength GM (1/2 GM, pH5.8) containing 0.5× Murashige and Skoog (MS) salts with vitamins (Duchefa, M 0222), 1% sucrose, 0.5 g/L MES (Duchefa, M1503), and 0.8% plant agar (Duchefa, P 1001). After stratification at 4 °C in darkness for 2 d, the seeds with plates were transferred to the growth cabinet maintained at 22 °C under long day condition (16-h light and 8-h dark) to let the seedlings grow vertically. The age of the seedlings was counted from the day the plates were placed in the growth cabinet for all experiments.

Chemical Treatments.

Estradiol induction.

For 17-β-Estradiol (EST) treatment, a 20 mM stock solution of EST (Sigma, 50-28-2) was prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma, 67-68-5). 5- or 13-d-old seedlings were transferred to 1/2 GM plates supplemented with 5 μM EST to induce the expression of mBax-YFP, while an equal amount of DMSO was added as the Mock control. After ablation, seedlings were transferred to the 1/2GM plates for recovery for indicated time. For primary xylem or phloem cell ablation experiments, seedlings were incubated on 1/2 GM plates supplemented with 5 μM EST or DMSO for 1 d, then transferred to the 1/2 GM plates for recovery. Dots were added with permanent marker on plates to mark the root tips immediately after transfer. Cross-sections were made at 1 to 2 mm above the marked position. For the ablation experiments performed just before cambium activation and in the mature cambium, cross-sections were made at 5 mm below the hypocotyl–root junction.

N-1-naphthylphthalamic acid (NPA) treatment.

For NPA treatment, a 20 mM stock solution of NPA (Sigma, 132-66-1) was prepared by dissolving it into DMSO. 13-d-old seedlings with indicated genotypes were transferred to 1/2GM plates supplemented with 5 μM EST or DMSO for 24 h, followed by transferring to 1/2GM with 5 μM NPA or DMSO to grow for indicated time. For long-term (7-d) NPA treatment experiment, the sterilized seeds were soaked in 20 μM NPA (Coolaber, 132-66-1) at 4 °C for 3 d. Seeds were then plated on 1/2GM plates containing 20 μM NPA for germination. After growing for 7 d on NPA-supplemented plates, seedlings were washed three times with liquid 1/2GM before being transferred to 1/2GM plates for recovery.

1-Naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) treatment.

For NAA treatment, a 1 mM NAA stock solution was prepared by dissolving it into DMSO. For 14-d NAA treatment, seeds of indicated genotypes were germinated and grown on 1/2GM plates containing 100 nM NAA or DMSO. For 1-d NAA treatment, 13-d-old seedlings with indicated genotypes were transferred to 1/2GM plates supplemented with 5 μM EST for 24 h, followed by transferring to 1/2GM plates containing 1 μM NAA or DMSO to recover for 1 d.

EdU staining.

A 10 mM stock solution of 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) (Thermo Fisher, 61135-33-9) in DMSO was prepared. 13-d-old seedlings of the indicated genotypes were transferred to 1/2 GM plates supplemented with 5 μM EST or DMSO for 1 d induction. The seedlings were then transferred to liquid 1/2 GM with 10 µM EdU for incorporation for 24 h.

Vibratome Sectioning, Fluorescent Reporter Analysis, and Fluorescent Staining Detection.

Vibratome sectioning was performed as previously described (4, 10) to analyze the expression of mBax-YFP and fluorescent reporters, EdU staining, and cell wall staining. Briefly, Arabidopsis roots were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Sigma, 30525-89-4) in 1× PBS under vacuum for 1 h, after washing with 1× PBS for three times, the roots were embedded in 4% agarose in 1× PBS and then sectioned into 200 μm slices using a vibratome. 0.1% SCRI renaissance 2200 (SR2200, Renaissance Chemicals) in 1× PBS was used to stain the cell walls as previously described (10). 0.2% Basic Fuchsin (Sigma, 569-61-9) in ClearSee solution was used to stain lignin of the developing or mature vessels, following the previous published protocol (48).

For PI staining, 4- to 6-d-old seedlings were stained with 10 mg/L PI (Sigma, 25535-16-4) for 10 min and then washed with water for a few seconds to remove the excess PI dye before imaging.

For EdU-stained nuclei detection, Click-iT™ EdU Imaging Kit with Alexa Fluor™ 594 Azide (Thermo fisher) was used. Fresh EdU detection cocktail was prepared following the previously described protocol (29). Agarose sections were transferred into the EdU detection cocktail and incubated for 1 h in the dark. The sections were then transferred into 0.1% SR2200 in 1× PBS to stain the cell walls.

All fluorescence imaging was performed using Leica Stellaris 8 or Leica SP5 Confocal Microscopes. Sequential scan was used to detect the multiple fluorophores in one sample. To ensure consistency, acquisition settings of the confocal microscope for different treatments or genotypes within the same experiment were kept identical. Each cell type specific ablation data (EST induced) was compared to the corresponding mock control. To avoid redundancy in displaying identical marker gene expression pattern, only one representative mock control is shown in the main figures. The vessels in Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C and D were identified based on bright-field channel images. To enhance clarity, we included a composite image combining the bright-field, mBax-YFP, and RFP channels from the same root cross-section in Fig. 2B (1Ind+3Rec) as an example, shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2B.

Microtome and Histological Analysis.

For histological analysis, plastic sections were prepared and imaged as described previously (2, 4, 49) with modification. In brief, root samples were vacuum infiltrated in fixation solution (1% glutaraldehyde, 4% formaldehyde, 0.05 M sodium phosphate) or FAA (50% ethanol, 5% acetic acid, 5% formaldehyde) for about 30 min and then fixed overnight at 4 °C. After dehydration through a graded ethanol series, the samples were infiltrated and embedded using Leica basic resin (Leica) or Technovit 7100 (Kulzer Technik, Germany). 5 μm cross-sections were obtained using a Leica microtome (Leica RM2245) at a desired position. The plastic sections were stained with toluidine blue and ruthenium red before imaging with a Leica 2500 or Leica DM750 microscope.

Transmission Electron Microscopy.

After mBax-YFP induction with 5 μM EST for 1 d in 13-d-old seedlings, the higher region of roots sampled from the 5 μM EST or DMSO plates were fixed in 3% (v/v) glutaraldehyde in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.8). The hypocotyl and the 5 mm region below the root–hypocotyl junction were then excised and postfixed in 1% OsO4 for 1 h on ice. After dehydration through a graded ethanol series and propylene oxide, the samples were embedded in Low viscosity embedding resin (TAAB, UK). Ultrathin sections were obtained and examined as described previously (50). In brief, Ultrathin sections were cut at 5 mm below the root–hypocotyl junction with Ultracut UC6a ultramicrotome (Leica Mikrosysteme GmbH) equipped with a diamond knife. The sections were checked using JEOL 1200 EX II transmission EM (JEOL Ltd., Japan) after double staining with 2% aqueous uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Imaging was performed with an Erlangshen ES5000W, model 782 side-mounted CCD-camera (Gatan Inc., USA).

General Methodology and Statistical Analysis.

All experiments were repeated at least three times. The number of individual roots that were analyzed is used to calculate the frequency in the figures, except for Figs. 4 F–H and 5 B and D and SI Appendix, Figs. S3C, S5C, S6, and S7, the number of radial cell files were used in these figures. For quantification of Fig. 5 B and D, one event of regeneration of the cambial stem cell was counted when the cell division on the xylem side was observed under the collapsed stem cell after recovery for 1, 2, and 3 d. The fraction in right bottom corners of some figures indicates the frequency of the observed phenotype.

For statistical analysis, multiple t tests using the Holm–Sidak method were applied for SI Appendix, Fig. S5E, while a two-tailed t test was used for SI Appendix, Fig. S5F. Two-way ANOVA with Šídák’s multiple comparisons test was used for Fig. 5 B and D.
