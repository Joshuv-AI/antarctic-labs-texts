Cholesterol‐Lowering Treatment Blocks Epithelial‐Mesenchymal Transition (EMT) Associated Invasiveness and Drug Resistance in Breast and Colorectal Adenocarcinoma Models
Authors: Shanen Perumal, Naaziyah Abdulla, Ruth Aronson, Mandeep Kaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEpithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a cellular process involved in the invasion and metastasis of cancer cells. Deregulated cellular cholesterol is associated with treatment resistance and metastatic potential in cancer cells; however, the link between EMT and cholesterol is unclear.MethodsWe tested the effect of cholesterol‐lowering on EMT using three cell line models and three EMT inducers. EMT was induced in NMuMG, MCF‐7 and HT‐29 cells to assess the effect of cholesterol‐modulatory compounds, MβCD, HPβCD and simvastatin. Free cholesterol, lipid droplets and lipid rafts in cellular membranes were evaluated using immunofluorescence microscopy. Expression of EMT‐associated genes was assessed using immunofluorescent staining, RT‐qPCR and Western blotting.ResultsFollowing cholesterol depletion (free cholesterol, lipid rafts and lipid droplets) with HPβCD, RT‐qPCR showed an increase in CDH1 expression (log2 fold change [log2FC] = 2.03; p < 0.001), and a decrease in Vim (log2FC = −1.01; p < 0.05) in NMuMG cells post EMT. Expression of cholesterol biosynthesis gene HMGCR (log2FC = −1.72; p < 0.01), and efflux genes ABCA1 (log2FC = −1.74; p < 0.01) and ABCG1 (log2FC = −4.81; p < 0.001) were reduced. A 68.4% (p < 0.001) reduction in relative invasion in NMuMG cells and a 41.3% (p < 0.01) reduction in multidrug resistance measurements in MCF‐7 cells were observed post‐EMT after HPβCD treatment.ConclusionCholesterol depletion reversed EMT‐associated expression patterns in all three cell models. Results from this study support cholesterol depletion as a potential therapeutic intervention for mitigating metastatic cancer progression.

Introduction

Cancer is a leading cause of death worldwide, with rising incidence and mortality rates that reflect the growth and ageing of the global population [1]. Metastasis accounts for approximately 60.1% of cancer‐related mortality, increasing to 66.7% when only considering solid tumours [2]. Epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a reversible physiological process that normally occurs during embryonic development, wound healing and tissue regeneration. EMT can also drive cancer progression by enhancing the migratory and invasive properties of cells, enabling malignancy at distant organs [3]. Several different signalling pathways are involved in EMT‐associated signalling, with considerable cross‐talk between signalling pathways, including the TGFβ signal transduction pathway, hypoxia‐induced signalling with the concomitant expression of HIF‐1α, and IL‐6/JAK2/STAT3 activation [4, 5, 6]. These signalling pathways upregulate the expression of EMT‐inducing transcription factors (EMT‐TFs), including SNAI1, ZEB1 and TWIST1 [4]. During the process of EMT, epithelial cells lose their cobblestone‐like morphology and apical‐basal polarity in favour of a spindle‐like morphology which is characteristic of mesenchymal cells [7]. This is accompanied by repression of epithelial markers, such as E‐cadherin, and the gain of mesenchymal markers, such as vimentin and N‐cadherin [8]. Due to its role in cancer metastasis and drug resistance, EMT is an attractive target for cancer treatment [9]. In order to target the EMT process in cancer, the cellular mechanisms altered by EMT must be further elucidated to discover potential targets that drive EMT progression and maintenance. In this light, the emerging role of altered lipid metabolism in facilitating metastatic dissemination of cancer cells seems to be a potentially attractive target [10].

Cellular cholesterol homeostasis is deregulated during cancer progression [11], but its role in the context of EMT is poorly understood at a molecular level. Cholesterol is an important structural component of cell membranes, making up approximately 30% of the lipid bilayer [12]. In addition to its structural role, cholesterol also plays a role in molecular and vesicular trafficking and in modulating the functions of receptor proteins [13]. It is also the precursor to steroid hormones, including oestrogen, androgen and glucocorticoids, which are involved in cell signalling [14]. Cholesterol homeostasis is tightly regulated by key proteins, such as sterol regulatory element binding protein 2 (SREBP2), which acts as a sensor for intracellular cholesterol levels. In response to reduced intracellular cholesterol, SREBP2 enters the nucleus and induces the expression of genes involved in cholesterol synthesis, such as 3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl‐coenzyme A (HMG‐CoA) reductase (HMGCR), and cholesterol uptake, such as low‐density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) [15]. Excess cellular cholesterol activates the liver X receptor (LXR), which halts cholesterol uptake by inducing the degradation of LDLR [16], and upregulates the expression of cholesterol efflux proteins ABCA1 and ABCG1 [15]. Based on the pleotropic role of cholesterol in the cell, it is not surprising that cancer cells have evolved several mechanisms to facilitate cholesterol dyshomeostasis, ultimately conferring cells with traits that facilitate metastasis and drug resistance. While the role of cholesterol in promoting the EMT process in cancer is not fully elucidated, its involvement in treatment resistance and metastasis suggests an association with EMT pathways. Thus, cholesterol should be explored as a target to potentially attenuate the aggressive traits acquired by cancer cells during the process of EMT. Cellular cholesterol content can be mediated in several ways. The therapeutic strategies that were employed in the current study included the administration of cholesterol synthesis inhibitors or cholesterol depletory agents. Inhibiting cholesterol synthesis through the use of statins is a common practice in the treatment of hypercholesterolemia [17]. Statins prevent the synthesis of cholesterol by competitively inhibiting HMGCR, an enzyme necessary for catalysing the rate‐limiting step of cholesterol biosynthesis through the mevalonate pathway [18]. Alternatively, β‐cyclodextrins can be used to deplete cellular cholesterol without inhibiting the mevalonate pathway. They act by encapsulating cholesterol within the hydrophobic interior, through homodimer formation, while the hydrophilic exterior allows for solubility in aqueous solution [19]. Methyl‐beta‐cyclodextrin (MβCD) and hydroxypropyl‐beta‐cyclodextrin (HPβCD) are both β‐cyclodextrins able to solubilise cholesterol. MβCD is frequently employed as a lipid raft disrupting agent due to its preference for membrane cholesterol [20] and demonstrates an ability to deplete cellular cholesterol and suppress tumour growth [21]. HPβCD possesses the ability to sequester excess intracellular cholesterol through pinocytosis [22]. The reduced capacity of HPβCD to deplete excess cholesterol relative to MβCD renders it more toxicologically benign [23]. A recently concluded clinical trial for HPβCD administration in Niemann‐Pick Type C Disease was conducted over a 48‐week period and displayed no treatment‐emergent adverse events in both paediatric, as well as adult patients, resulting in further testing in a Phase III clinical trial, which is currently underway [24]. The promising safety profile observed in these patients as well as previous in vitro and in vivo data generated in breast cancer [25], potentiates exploring cholesterol depletion from a metastatic standpoint. Consequently, the current study aimed to investigate the change in cholesterol‐related pathways caused by the induction of EMT in cancer cells, as well as to assess the potential utility of lowering cholesterol with MβCD, HPβCD and simvastatin in attenuating and reversing EMT‐associated signalling in cancer. While a substantial portion of our study relies on image‐based analyses, this approach offers critical spatial and contextual information that is not accessible through bulk molecular techniques alone. To ensure the rigour and reproducibility of our findings, image‐derived observations were independently validated using molecular techniques, including quantitative PCR (qPCR) and Western blotting. These complementary assays confirmed the transcriptional and protein‐level changes observed in our imaging experiments, thereby strengthening the validity of our conclusions. By integrating spatial, quantitative and molecular data, our study leverages the strengths of each method to provide robust mechanistic insights pre‐ and post‐EMT induction following cholesterol modulation.

Materials and Methods

Cell Lines and Cell Culture

Three cell lines were employed in the current study: the non‐transformed mouse mammary gland epithelial cell line (NMuMG; a gift from Theresa Vincent's lab at Uppsala University, Sweden), human breast adenocarcinoma cell line (MCF‐7; The European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (ECACC, UK)), and human colorectal adenocarcinoma (HT‐29; Cellonex, South Africa). NMuMG and MCF‐7 cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US), supplemented with 10% FBS (Celtic Molecular Diagnostics, South Africa) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US). The NMuMG cell line was further supplemented with 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco/Thermo Fisher Scientific, US), and 10 μg/mL insulin (Sigma‐Aldrich, UK). The HT‐29 cell line was cultured in McCoy's 5a (Modified) Medium (Sigma Aldrich, UK), supplemented with 10% FBS (Celtic Molecular Diagnostics, South Africa) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US) at 37°C and 5% carbon dioxide (CO2). The cells were used within 20 passages after revival from frozen stocks. Mycoplasma monitoring was conducted using the MycoFluor Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US).

EMT Induction and Cholesterol‐Lowering Treatment

NMuMG cells were treated with 10 ng/mL of recombinant human transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGFβ; R&D Systems, US) for 48 h. The NMuMG cell line was employed in this study as it is the most robust and well‐established system for studying TGF‐β‐induced EMT [26, 27]. A stock solution of TGFβ was prepared by reconstituting 2 μg of recombinant TGFβ at 20 μg/mL in sterile 4 mM HCl (Sigma Aldrich, UK) containing 1 mg/mL of bovine serum albumin (BSA) (VWR Life Sciences, US) diluted in water. MCF‐7 cells were treated with 150 μM CoCl2 (Sigma Aldrich, UK) for 48 h. A stock solution of 25 mM CoCl2 was prepared by dilution in sterile distilled water. HT‐29 cells were treated with 50 ng/mL recombinant human IL‐6 cytokine (PeproTech, USA) for 24 h. A stock solution of 1 μg/mL was prepared in 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 1× Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS) (Sigma Aldrich, UK). Cells pre‐ and post‐EMT induction were treated with cholesterol inhibitory/depletory agents. 10 mM stock solutions of MβCD (Sigma Aldrich, UK) and 100 mM stock solutions of HPβCD (Sigma Aldrich, UK) were prepared by dissolution in purified water. These were further diluted in the respective cell culture media to achieve a treatment dose of 1 mM MβCD and 10 mM HPβCD, respectively. 10 mM stock solution of simvastatin (Sigma Aldrich, UK) was prepared by dissolution in pure ethanol and subsequently activated by adding 1 N NaOH. The resulting solution was pH‐adjusted to 7.2 using 1 N HCl. The stock solution was further diluted in the respective cell culture media to achieve a treatment dose of 10 μM simvastatin, in the final 2 h of EMT induction. Treatment concentrations were determined in previously published studies [25, 28].

RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using the Direct‐zol RNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo Research, Inqaba Biotec, SA) with DNase treatment. Reverse transcription was performed using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US), as per the manufacturer's protocol. RT‐qPCR was performed using the SensiFAST SYBR No‐ROX kit (Bioline, Canada) in the CFX96 Touch Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad, US). Primer sequences are presented in Table S1. Fold change of target mRNA was calculated using GAPDH housekeeping control, and the threshold cycle (Ct) was used to calculate ∆∆CT: ∆CT = target gene Ct—control Ct; ∆∆Ct = ∆Ct control condition—∆Ct test condition. Log2 fold change (log2FC) was calculated on the fold change.

Immunofluorescent Staining

20,000 cells/well were seeded on glass cover slips in 24‐well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US) and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Following EMT induction and cholesterol‐lowering treatment, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma Aldrich, UK) for 10 min. Cells were permeabilized by incubating in 0.1% Triton‐X (Sigma Aldrich, UK) for 15 min. Thereafter, blocking was performed by incubating cells with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (VWR Life Sciences, US) in 0.1% PBS‐Tween 20 (PBST) for 1 h. Primary antibody was added and incubated at 4°C overnight. Antibodies used are listed in Tables S2 and S3. Cells were incubated with secondary antibody diluted 1:1000 in 1% BSA in PBST for 45 min at room temperature. Nuclei were stained using 0.1 μg/mL of DAPI (Sigma Aldrich, UK) in 1× PBS. Coverslips were mounted on microscope slides using Fluoromount Aqueous Mounting Medium (Sigma Aldrich, UK) and visualised by using the Floid Cell Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US). Fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ image processing software (Software Version 1.41) (NIH, US). Corrected total cell fluorescence (CTCF) was calculated using the formula: CTCF = integrated density – (area of selected cell × mean background fluorescence). For each condition, 5 images and 10 cells per image were analysed, from three independent repeats (n = 150).

Cholesterol Staining

20,000 cells/well were seeded on glass cover slips in 24‐well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US) and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Following EMT induction and cholesterol‐lowering treatment, the cholesterol profile of cells was analysed with fluorescent dyes following the manufacturer's instructions. Filipin (Sigma‐Aldrich, UK) was used for free cholesterol, BODIPY 493/503 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) for cholesteryl esters (CEs), and the Vybrant Alexa Fluor 594 Lipid Raft Labelling Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) for lipid rafts. Nuclei were stained with either DAPI (Sigma‐Aldrich, UK) or NucRed Dead 647 ReadyProbes Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK). Coverslips were mounted on microscope slides using Fluoromount Aqueous Mounting Medium (Sigma Aldrich, UK) and visualised by using the Floid Cell Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US). Fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ image processing software (Software Version 1.41; NIH, US). Corrected total cell fluorescence (CTCF) was calculated using the formula: CTCF = integrated density – (area of selected cell × mean background fluorescence). For each condition, 5 images and 10 cells per image were analysed from three independent repeats (n = 150).

Western Blotting

Cell lysates were prepared in 1× RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4; 300 mM NaCl; 1% Triton X‐100; 1% sodium deoxycholate; 10% SDS; 10 mM EDTA, made up in water). Protein content of lysates was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) (Sigma‐Aldrich, UK) assay. Lysates were denatured at 95°C for 5 min. Lysate volumes containing 2 μg of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulphate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE), transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US), and blocked with BSA blocking solution 3% BSA in 1× TBST (tris‐buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20) at room temperature for 1 h. Membranes were incubated in primary antibody diluted 1:1000 in 5% BSA blocking solution overnight at 4°C, washed with 1× TBST, and blotted with secondary antibody diluted 1:5000 in BSA blocking solution for 1 h at room temperature, followed by washing with 1× TBST. Blots were imaged by adding equal volumes of peroxidase solution and Luminol/enhancer solution from the Clarity Western ECL Substrates (Bio‐Rad, US) to the membrane and visualising with the ChemiDoc XRS+ System (Bio‐Rad, US). β‐tubulin was probed as the loading control. Antibodies used are listed in Table S2.

Transwell Invasion Assay

Invasive properties of cells were assessed using the Transwell Invasion Assay following EMT induction and cholesterol‐lowering treatment. Cells were seeded at 100,000 cells/mL (maintained in serum‐free media for 24 h at 37°C) in a 0.8 μm‐pore Transwell chamber (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) mixed with GelTrex (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK). The lower chamber contained 500 μL of media supplemented with 10% FBS (Celtic Molecular Diagnostics, South Africa) to set up a chemotactic gradient. Following a 24‐h incubation period, non‐invasive cells on the top chamber were removed using a cotton swab. Cells that had invaded the GelTrex (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US) and migrated to the underside of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma Aldrich, UK) for 10 min and stained using 0.1 μg/mL DAPI (Sigma Aldrich, UK) for 10 min at room temperature. The membrane was washed with 1× PBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US), removed from the Transwell chamber using a sterile blade and mounted to glass coverslips using Fluoromount Aqueous Mounting Medium (Sigma Aldrich, UK). Cells were counted to determine the average number of invasive cells. The result was presented as a percentage increase or decrease in the number of invasive cells relative to the control. For each condition, 5 images and 10 cells per image were analysed, from three independent repeats (n = 150).

Multidrug Resistance Assay

The Vybrant Multidrug Resistance (MDR) Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US) was used to assess the functionality of the key drug transporter MDR1 post‐EMT induction. 10,000 cells/well were seeded in a 96‐well plate and incubated for 24 h at 37°C. Cells post‐EMT induction were treated with cholesterol‐modulatory compounds. Cells were treated with 1 μM of Verapamil (a known MDR1 inhibitor) as a positive control. Following this, 50 μL of calcein‐AM (0.25 μM) was administered to each well and subsequently incubated at 37°C for a duration of 30 min, protected from light. MDR activity was consequently assessed based on calcein retention in cells. Subsequently, two washes using PBS were completed, followed by the administration of 200 μL of PBS to each well. In order to determine fluorescence intensity, the Victor Nivo multi‐mode microplate reader (Perkin Elmer, US) was used with excitation maximum at 494 nm and emission maximum at 517 nm. The percentage of calcein retention was calculated to assess efflux activity and infer drug‐resistant potential.

Statistical Analysis

All experimental data are represented by the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates. An unpaired, two‐tailed Student's t‐test was used to assess statistical difference between two groups of independent samples, with normality assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. The one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was used for comparison between more than two groups with a single independent variable. A two‐way ANOVA was used for comparison between more than two groups with two independent variables. For pairwise analysis, a Bonferroni post hoc test was performed. A p value ≤ 0.05 was statistically significant. p values < 0.05 are marked with 1 star (*), p values < 0.01 are marked with 2 stars (**), and p values < 0.001 are marked with 3 stars (***). p values > 0.05 were considered non‐significant and labelled non‐significant (ns). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism5 (GraphPad Software, US).

Results

Multimodal Molecular Mechanisms Driving EMT in Various In Vitro Cellular Models

To confirm EMT induction in vitro, cells were morphologically assessed with the aim of observing alterations in cell polarity (Figure S1). Following exposure to EMT inducing agents, namely NMuMG cells using TGFβ, MCF‐7 cells using CoCl2, and HT‐29 cells using IL‐6 treatment, it was noted that cell–cell contacts were reduced, and cells adopted a spindle‐shaped morphology, resembling a mesenchymal phenotype (Figure S1). As further validation the expression of epithelial marker E‐cadherin, mesenchymal marker Vimentin, and EMT transcription factors (EMT‐TFs) were evaluated before and after EMT induction, displaying reduced fluorescence intensity of labelled E‐cadherin (Figure 1a,c) and increased fluorescence intensity of Vimentin in all cellular models (Figure 1b,c). As a confirmation in NMuMG cells, qPCR analysis showed a significant reduction in CDH1 expression (log2FC = −0.97; p < 0.05) in response to EMT induction, and a significant increase in VIM (log2FC = 1.28; p < 0.05), SNAI1 (log2FC = 3.17; p < 0.01), TWIST1 (log2FC = 2.42; p < 0.05), ZEB1 (log2FC = 2.28; p < 0.05), SMAD4 (log2FC = 2.96; p < 0.01), and CTBP1 (log2FC = 1.76; p < 0.05) expression after EMT induction in NMuMG cells (Figure 1d). Western blotting showed reduced E‐cadherin (68.37%) and increased Vimentin (108.18%) and N‐cadherin (108.50%) protein abundance following EMT induction in NMuMG cells (Figure 3d and Figure S7). Immunofluorescent staining validated that CoCl2 induced EMT via the HIF‐1α signalling pathway in MCF‐7 cells, and IL‐6 exposure in HT‐29 cells activated downstream STAT‐3 signalling (Figure S2).

EMT Induction Modulates Cellular Cholesterol Profile In Vitro

Growing evidence indicates that malignant tumours accumulate cholesterol to facilitate cancer development [11]. To further explore the role of cholesterol in the context of EMT, cholesterol content was measured pre‐ and post‐EMT induction by fluorescently labelling free cholesterol (Figure 2a), cholesteryl esters (Figure 2b) and lipid rafts (Figure 2c). Our data indicate that EMT induction significantly alters cellular cholesterol content across all three models. Alterations to cellular cholesterol profiles were quantified using ImageJ to measure CTCF. The non‐cancerous NMuMG model displayed a reduction of stained free cholesterol (43%, p < 0.001), cholesteryl esters (72%, p < 0.001), and lipid raft cholesterol (37%, p < 0.01), following EMT induction (Figure 2d–f). In contrast, the MCF‐7 model displayed no change in free cholesterol fluorescent intensity, and an increase in cholesteryl esters (32%, p < 0.01) and lipid raft cholesterol (31%) content after EMT induction (Figure 2d–f). In the HT‐29 model, the fluorescent intensity of free cholesterol, cholesteryl esters and lipid rafts increased by 34% (p < 0.05), 29% (p < 0.05) and 35%, respectively, following EMT induction (Figure 2d–f). Importantly, all three models exposed to 1 mM MβCD and 10 mM HPβCD indicated consistent depletion of free cholesterol levels (Figure 2g–i and Figure S3), stored cholesterol (Figure 2j–l and Figure S4) and lipid raft cholesterol content (Figure 2m–o and Figure S5) pre‐ and post‐EMT induction.

Cholesterol Modulatory Agents Reverse EMT‐Induced Molecular Signatures

It has been established that cholesterol plays an important role in EMT induction. However, the effect of cholesterol in maintaining an EMT phenotype has not been fully elucidated. To assess this, our EMT models were exposed to cholesterol modulatory agents (1 mM MβCD, 10 mM HPβCD and 10 μM simvastatin) and the changes in EMT markers, including E‐cadherin and vimentin, were observed. Following treatment with these cholesterol modulatory agents, it was noted that the fluorescent intensity of E‐cadherin increased, while Vimentin expression decreased (Figure 3a and Figure S6). Changes in protein expression were quantified using ImageJ to measure CTCF, which indicated an initial 70% reduction in E‐cadherin average CTCF upon EMT induction (p < 0.01), followed by a 181% increase in fluorescence when treated with MβCD (p < 0.001), 233% with HPβCD (p < 0.01), and 141% with simvastatin (p < 0.01) after EMT induction in the NMuMG model (Figure 3b and Figure S6). Vimentin fluorescence increased by 450% upon EMT induction (p < 0.01) and decreased by 65% (p < 0.05), 62% (p < 0.05) and 60% (p < 0.05) after treatment with MβCD, HPβCD and simvastatin, respectively (Figure 3c and Figure S6). The same trend was observed in EMT‐induced MCF‐7 (p < 0.01) and HT‐29 models (p < 0.05), following MβCD and HPβCD treatments (Figure 3b,c).

The potential for cholesterol depletion to reverse the EMT phenotype was further investigated in the NMuMG model using RT‐qPCR and western blotting to assess changes in the expression of key genes and proteins in EMT signalling pathways. All three cholesterol‐modulating agents increased the expression of E‐cadherin and decreased vimentin and N‐cadherin, indicating reversal of the EMT phenotype (Figure 3d and Figure S7). Similarly, cholesterol depletion increased CDH1 expression, with log2FC of 1.41 following MβCD treatment (p < 0.01), 2.03 following HPβCD (p < 0.001), and 1.36 following simvastatin (p < 0.01), relative to the untreated post‐EMT control. Additionally, each treatment significantly reduced the expression of VIM with log2FC of −1.69 after MβCD treatment (p < 0.05), −1.01 after HPβCD (p < 0.05), and −1.44 after simvastatin (p < 0.05). MβCD, HPβCD and simvastatin treatment reduced the expression of TWIST1 (MβCD: log2FC = −1.13; p < 0.05, HPβCD: log2FC = −3.02; p < 0.01, and simvastatin: log2FC = −4.07; p < 0.05), ZEB1 (MβCD: log2FC = −2.28; p < 0.01, HPβCD: log2FC = −1.8; p < 0.05 and simvastatin: log2FC = −1.28; p < 0.05), SMAD4 (MβCD:log2FC = −4.01; p < 0.001, HPβCD: log2FC = −2.76; p < 0.01, and simvastatin: log2FC = −0.41; p < 0.05), and CTBP1 (MβCD: log2FC = −3.27; p < 0.05, HPβCD: log2FC = −0.64; p < 0.05, and simvastatin: log2FC = −1.01; p < 0.05). Additionally, the treatment groups did not significantly impact the expression of SNAI1 (Figure 3e).

EMT‐Induced Alterations in Cellular Behaviour Are Attenuated by Cholesterol Modulation

Building on the observation of EMT phenotype reversal, we further investigated changes in key EMT‐associated characteristics, specifically invasion and multidrug resistance. This analysis aimed to determine whether cholesterol depletion not only reverses EMT markers but also impacts the characteristics typically linked to EMT in cancer cells. To explore invasive potential, a Transwell invasion assay was employed, which measures the ability of cells to attach and invade the ECM and migrate towards a chemo‐attractant. Nuclei stained blue with DAPI indicate the number of invading NMuMG cells (Figure 4a). Percentage invasion was calculated relative to the untreated post‐EMT control (set to 100% invasion) to represent the population of invasive cells in a sample of cells undergoing EMT. As expected, the pre‐EMT cell population was 75.2% less invasive (p < 0.001) in the NMuMG model. Importantly, treatment of cells post‐EMT with MβCD, HPβCD and simvastatin each resulted in a significant reduction in invasion of 84.7% (p < 0.001), 68.4% (p < 0.001) and 74.4% (p < 0.001), respectively, which indicates comparable invasive potential as the pre‐EMT group (Figure 4b). The MCF‐7 cells displayed 84.1% (p < 0.001) less invasive potential, pre‐EMT, and in concordance with the NMuMG model, treatments with MβCD, HPβCD and simvastatin, post‐EMT, resulted in a significant reduction in invasion of 70.3% (p < 0.001), 66.9% (p < 0.001) and 80.2% (p < 0.001), post‐EMT, respectively (Figure 4c). Similarly, HT‐29 cells displayed 64.0% (p < 0.01) less invasive potential pre‐EMT, relative to the untreated post‐EMT control, and EMT‐induced cells exposed to MβCD displayed 43.0% (p < 0.05) reduced invasiveness and cells exposed to HPβCD showed a 47.8% (p < 0.05) reduction in invasive potential (Figure 4d and Figure S8).

It is well known that drug resistance remains one of the greatest obstacles in the treatment of metastatic cancer. To assess the functionality of the key drug transporter MDR1 following cholesterol synthesis inhibition/depletion, the intracellular retention of fluorescent calcein (metabolised MDR1 substrate) was measured in MCF‐7 cells (Figure 4e). Fluorescence intensity pertains to the amount of calcein dye retained intracellularly, indicating decreased MDR activity. Since MCF‐7 cells without hypoxic induction do not express MDR1 [29], intracellular retention of calcein in untreated pre‐EMT cells was set to 100% fluorescent intensity, indicating a baseline of no MDR activity. Hypoxia‐induced EMT led to a significant increase in MDR potential as evidenced by the 47.13% (p < 0.05) decrease in calcein retention. Treating post‐EMT cells with cholesterol‐targeting agents led to a significant decrease in MDR potential, as evidenced by the increase in calcein retention following treatment with 1 mM MβCD (45.01%; < 0.01), 10 mM HPβCD (41.27%; p < 0.01) and 10 μM simvastatin (35.31%; p < 0.01).

Mechanistic Implications of Cholesterol Modulation Following EMT Induction

To better understand the role of cholesterol in EMT, transcriptional changes in expression within cholesterol homeostasis pathways were investigated in NMuMG cells, as this represents the most robust model to study EMT [30]. Interestingly EMT induction resulted in increased transcriptional expression of cholesterol efflux genes: ABCA1 (log2FC = 0.92, p < 0.05), ABCG1 (log2FC = 4.54, p < 0.01), and LXR (log2FC = 0.89, p < 0.05); cholesterol biosynthesis genes: SREBF2 (log2FC = 1.09, p < 0.05) and HMGCR (log2FC = 1.43, p < 0.05); cholesterol uptake gene: LDLR (log2FC = 1.08. p < 0.05) and displayed decreased transcriptional activity of cholesterol metabolising gene: LCAT (log2FC = −0.79, p < 0.05). EMT induction did not significantly affect the expression of PCSK9 (Figure 5a). It is hypothesised that the increased cholesterol efflux, particularly through the transporter ABCG1, may serve as one of the mechanisms utilised by NMuMG cells to reduce cellular cholesterol following EMT induction.

Additionally, we further explored the transcriptional effect of cholesterol modulating agents on cholesterol homeostasis (Figure 5b). All three cholesterol‐modulating agents decreased the ABCA1 expression post‐EMT, with log2FC values of −0.98 after MβCD treatment (p < 0.05), −1.74 after HPβCD (p < 0.01), and −1.51 after simvastatin (p < 0.05). Similarly, ABCG1 expression was reduced after cholesterol‐depletion, with log2FC values of −2.62 after MβCD treatment (p < 0.01), −4.81 after HPβCD (p < 0.001) and −5.81 after simvastatin (p < 0.001). LXR expression was decreased following treatment with MβCD, with log2FC values of −2.42 (p < 0.01), and HPβCD treatment, with log2FC of −3.08 (p < 0.01), but no significant change was observed following simvastatin treatment. Expression of HMGCR was reduced after cholesterol‐lowering treatment with log2FC values of −1.86 after MβCD treatment (p < 0.05), −1.72 after HPβCD treatment (p < 0.01), and −0.94 after simvastatin treatment (p < 0.05). Finally, the expression levels of both LDLR and LCAT were reduced by MβCD treatment, with log2FC values of −1.38 (p < 0.05) and −1.1 (p < 0.05), respectively, and increased by simvastatin treatment, with log2FC values of 1.19 (p < 0.05) and 1.79 (p < 0.01), respectively. HPβCD treatment had no significant effect on the expression of LDLR or LCAT. Expression of SREBF2 or PCSK9 did not significantly change with cholesterol depletion. Concurrent with the gene expression analysis, post‐EMT induction, western blotting showed a slight reduction in ABCG1, HMGCR and LXR protein expression following treatment with cholesterol‐lowering agents (Figure 5c and Figure S9). At a protein level, nuclear SREBP2 levels were not significantly different in all three treatment groups post‐EMT (Figure 5c and Figure S9).

Discussion

Elevated cholesterol levels are associated with a higher risk of developing cancers of the prostate, breast, colon, rectum and testicles [11]. Further, dysregulation of the mevalonate pathway, through which cholesterol biosynthesis occurs, results in enhanced cell growth in both transformed and non‐transformed cells [31]. Recent studies have demonstrated the role of increased cholesterol biosynthesis and accumulation in cancer cell growth, proliferation and aggressiveness, as well as the potential role of cholesterol depletion in reducing tumour growth in vitro and in vivo [25, 32, 33]. Despite evidence of the role that cholesterol plays in tumorigenic signalling, there is limited evidence on the efficacy of cholesterol‐lowering compounds in reducing EMT‐associated characteristics in cancer cells. The present study is the first of its kind presenting a comprehensive molecular elucidation of altered cholesterol profiles in cells undergoing EMT, assessing altered cholesterol profiles in three different cell models using three different EMT inducers. On this basis, we elucidate the vulnerability of these cells when subjected to cholesterol‐lowering treatments, demonstrating attenuated EMT‐associated expression patterns and cell behaviour in multiple in vitro models of EMT.

EMT is associated with the loss of E‐cadherin expression, accompanied by the gain of N‐cadherin and Vimentin expression [34, 35]. Consistent with this, we observed a loss of E‐cadherin expression and a gain in Vimentin expression following EMT induction in the NMuMG, MCF‐7 and HT‐29 models, indicating the transition of cells to a mesenchymal‐like state (Figure 1). Additional analyses in the NMuMG model demonstrated an increase in N‐cadherin expression, as well as an increase in transcriptional expression of EMT‐TFs (Figure 1), which further confirms the activation of EMT pathways [36, 37, 38]. Induction of EMT in MCF‐7 cells with CoCl2, caused an increase in HIF‐1α expression (Figure S2a), which in turn upregulates the expression of EMT‐TFs (SNAIL, TWIST1 and ZEB), contributing to the EMT phenotype [39]. Similarly, EMT induction in HT‐29 cells using IL‐6 resulted in elevated STAT3 expression (Figure S2b), which promotes the EMT phenotype via activation of JAK/STAT signalling and upregulation of EMT‐TFs [4]. These findings confirm the activation of EMT pathways in the cell models used in this study.

Despite evidence indicating that cholesterol directly regulates signalling pathways related to EMT, no publication to date has attempted to delineate the effect of EMT induction on the cellular cholesterol profile in cancer. To further complicate matters, conflicting evidence exists in the literature regarding the impact of EMT on lipid metabolism. TGF‐β has been reported to be involved in the suppression of enzymes involved in lipid metabolism, which is validated in our NMuMG model [40, 41]. Additionally, inhibition of the cholesterol pathway was shown to induce TGFβ‐mediated EMT in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, leading to poor patient outcome [42]. This was mediated by the induction of SREBP1 transcription to activate autocrine TGFβ signalling and subsequent activation of the canonical SMAD2/3 effector cascade [42]. Moreover, CtBP1 and ZEB1 are both upregulated in NMuMG cells post‐EMT induction (Figure 1d). CtBP is shown to regulate cholesterol homeostasis by forming a complex with ZEB1 and repressing the transcription of SREBF2, consequently decreasing cholesterol biosynthesis [43]. Additional studies conducted in hepatocytes document TGFβ‐mediated regulation of crucial genes involved in cholesterol biosynthesis, metabolism, esterification and transport, which corresponded to a reduced cholesterol profile and led to enhancement of p‐Smad2/3 signalling [44]. This reduced cholesterol milieu proved critical to supporting TGFβ‐mediated induction of EMT and actin polymerisation [44]. This observation is further supported by the fact that high cholesterol promotes cholesterol‐mediated endocytosis and degradation of TGFβ receptors [45]. Furthermore, it is suggested that the suppression of lipogenesis decreases proliferative requirements, promoting cell invasion and metastasis [46]. This is evidenced by the observed increase in expression of EMT‐related transcription factors, Snail and Slug, which are associated with decreased cellular cholesterol and have been shown to regulate genes encoding ganglioside‐metabolising enzymes, leading to reduced ganglioside content and increased plasma membrane fluidity for cellular invasion [43].

On the contrary, cholesterol is shown to induce EMT in breast [47] as well as colorectal cancer [48]. This contrasting evidence on the effect of EMT induction on cholesterol levels is further reflected and validated by the increased cholesterol levels in both the MCF‐7 and the HT‐29 models (Figure 2). In the hypoxia‐induced MCF‐7 model of EMT, increased cholesterol levels may be due to the role of hypoxia in the regulation of SREBP2 transcriptional activity [49, 50] as well as cholesterol catabolism and reverse cholesterol transport [51]. In the IL‐6‐induced HT‐29 model, the increased cholesterol levels may be caused by increased inflammatory stress through IL‐6 signalling, which has been shown to increase cholesterol accumulation by enhancing expression of SREBP2, LDLR and HMGCR in several cell types, leading to cellular uptake of plasma LDL and further promoting de novo biosynthesis [52, 53, 54, 55]. Abnormal accumulation of lipid droplets, which subsequently act as reservoirs of cholesterol, enables membrane biogenesis and modifies lipid raft structure which ultimately enhances cancer aggressiveness [11]. In cells undergoing EMT, together with an increase in membrane cholesterol, a reduction in the ceramide pool synergistically contributes to enhanced membrane fluidity, which is emerging as a crucial requirement of cancer cells to facilitate metastatic dissemination [56]. Additionally, increased lipid raft cholesterol facilitates tumorigenic progression by modulating signal transduction [57]. This is evidenced by studies indicating that tumour promoting molecules such as osteopontin interact with integrin αVβ3 as well as CD44 receptors located in lipid raft domains to mediate activation of several signalling pathways, including MAPK, PI3K/Akt, as well as NFκβ to induce expression of EMT‐related genes [58]. Consequently, cholesterol is implicated in not only directly modulating EMT‐associated signalling intermediates but also modifying the physical properties of the membrane to support EMT. Importantly, the inherent variability of cellular cholesterol profiles observed in these different models is not well understood but is likely due to the mechanism of EMT induction and principal pathways that are activated in response to exogenous EMT‐inducing stimuli. This is well supported by the context‐dependent manifestation of EMT in various cancer types [59]. Furthermore, intratumoural as well as intertumoural metabolic heterogeneity should also be considered [60], consequently warranting further investigation in this field.

The role of cholesterol in cancer progression is complex, with cholesterol being an important part of cell membrane structure, as well as participating in signalling pathways. In the NMuMG model, EMT induction altered cholesterol signalling (Figure 5). Despite the decrease in cellular cholesterol observed following EMT induction, genes playing a role in cholesterol biosynthesis (SREBF2 and HMGCR) were upregulated after EMT induction (Figure 5). HMGCR expression is associated with EMT and metastasis, and its targeted inhibition is shown to attenuate TGFβ‐mediated tumour progression [61]. SREBP2 may also drive EMT and promote metastasis in cancer cells [62]. The overall decrease in cholesterol is likely driven by an upregulation of genes involved in cholesterol efflux, including ABCA1, ABCG1 and LXR (Figure 5). LXR binds to the promoters of both ABCA1 and ABCG1, increasing their expression [15]. The upregulation of ABCA1 observed after TGFβ exposure is consistent with studies showing its upregulation in breast cancer EMT [63]. It may also play a role in cancer drug resistance, as it is associated with acquired chemotherapy resistance in ovarian cancer [64]. ABCG1 is associated with a metastatic cancer phenotype and its inhibition triggers tumour regression in three‐dimensional colon cancer models [65]. Its upregulation, together with upregulated Wnt signalling, is associated with oxaliplatin resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma [66].

An important observation within our data was that the effect of cholesterol‐depleting cyclodextrins on the EMT process remained independent of the elevation of cholesterol levels across the three models. The expression patterns of EMT marker proteins E‐cadherin and Vimentin were consistently reversed across all models (Figure 3), suggesting that depleting cellular cholesterol may reverse EMT and cause mesenchymal‐like cells to revert to an epithelial state, which is well supported by other studies conducted in the field [67, 68, 69]. Modulation of MAPK‐dependent EMT induction via TGFβ is dependent on the integrity of lipid raft domains. Cholesterol depletion consequently has been shown to disrupt TGFβ activation of MAPK, resulting in the upregulation of E‐cadherin [70]. Moreover, studies conducted by Wu et al. postulate that cholesterol depletion through HPβCD administration negatively regulates TβR1 expression by influencing lipid raft distribution [69]. Wnt/β‐catenin is another EMT‐related signalling pathway that becomes disrupted by membrane cholesterol depletion [71]. Focusing on cholesterol synthesis inhibition, Simvastatin mediated suppression of Smad 2 and 3 phosphorylation attenuates TGF‐β1 induced EMT as well as PI3K/Akt/GK3β, Hippo/YAP/RhoA and MAPK/ERK signalling pathways, consequently impairing metastatic potential [72, 73, 74]. These findings highlight the importance of lipid raft integrity in maintaining an EMT phenotype. In this study, lipid raft cholesterol was reduced across all three cell lines after treatment with cholesterol‐lowering compounds (Figure 2m–o and Figure S5), which may have prevented EMT induction. In addition, cholesterol depletion with MβCD, HPβCD and simvastatin in NMuMG cells reversed the expression patterns of EMT‐TFs TWIST1, ZEB1, CTBP1 and SMAD4 (Figure 3e). These expression changes were corroborated by changes in EMT‐associated cellular characteristics (Figure 4).

The observed restoration of E‐cadherin and downregulation of vimentin, following cholesterol depletion in EMT induced cells, was consistent with the invasive potential of cells (Figure 4). An important property of mesenchymal cells is increased membrane fluidity, which modulates changes in cell adhesion, migration and differentiation [75]. Cholesterol depletion with β‐cyclodextrins increases membrane stiffness, which results in decreased cell migration, stem cell characteristics and has been shown to inhibit EMT induction in vitro [76, 77]. Furthermore, MβCD administration induces the shedding of the CD44 receptor which is overtly expressed in cancer cells conferring cells with an aggressive disease phenotype as it mediates cancer cell migration, invasion and metastasis [78]. Morphologically, this translates to a retraction of membrane protrusions, which is potentially dictated by the influence of MβCD on the non‐canonical Wnt pathway [79]. Additionally, disruption of lipid rafts with MβCD decreases the secretion of MMPs, which suppresses the invasiveness of cancer cells, through the cells' limited ability to degrade ECM components [80]. Notably, simvastatin has been documented to inhibit EMT by affecting the GSK3β/Akt as well as the p38 MAPK non‐canonical Smad signalling pathway [73, 81]. Interestingly, evidence also exists implicating simvastatin in modulating the expression of CD44 at a transcriptional level to suppress migration and invasion of breast cancer cells [82]. This may explain the observed decrease in invasive potential followed by cholesterol depletion in all three EMT models (Figure 4).

Cholesterol is largely implicated in acquired drug resistance in cancers, by modifying cholesterol‐related MDR signalling pathways and/or regulating the activity of multidrug transporters [39]. Membrane cholesterol depletion proved effective in reducing MDR (Figure 4e). This observation is validated by studies reporting that treatment with membrane cholesterol depletory agents abrogates expression of the MDR1 transporter, whereas treatment with statins affects the activity [83, 84]. Cholesterol can modulate transporter conformation by binding at MDR1 substrate sites, and therefore, regulates MDR1 catalytic activity and efflux [39]. Consequently, seeing that these transporters are housed in membrane lipid rafts, depleting lipid raft cholesterol is seen to impair the efflux activity of MDR1 [85] due to alterations in lipid‐raft membrane structure, which affects basal ATPase activity [86]. Interestingly, studies indicate that membrane cholesterol depletion results in translocation of MDR1 to detergent soluble fractions, leading to a loss of activity [87]. Independent from its ability to alter membrane fluidity, evidence exists implicating HPβCD in directly modulating ATPase activity of MDR1 [88] which could potentially contribute to the results obtained in this study. Importantly, cholesterol targeting agents such as simvastatin and MβCD can re‐sensitise cells to chemotherapies by disrupting the activity of drug resistance‐related pathways such as integrin β3/focal adhesion kinase (FAK)/ERK and PI3K/Akt signalling, highlighting the utility of this approach to combat drug resistance in EMT [89, 90].

The use of cyclodextrins presents an alternative approach to reducing cellular cholesterol levels in cancerous cells. Statin treatment is a common strategy used to target cellular cholesterol through cholesterol synthesis inhibition. Statins exhibit anti‐cancer properties in mesenchymal‐like cells and have been reported to suppress EMT by restoring E‐cadherin and decreasing vimentin expression [61, 91]. Cholesterol synthesis inhibition has been shown to be successful in a laboratory setting; however, the effects of statins extend past the lipid profile, causing metabolic disruptions in the production of steroid hormones, vitamin D and bile acids in normal cells [92]. Consequently, the severe side effects of statins have raised questions about their usefulness in a clinical setting.

This study has several limitations. One limitation is the use of in vitro models of EMT, which may not accurately represent the heterogeneity of tumours in the body. Secondly, inducing EMT in vitro relies on continuous exposure to an EMT‐inducing stimulus, such as TGFβ, which may interfere with cellular response to cholesterol depletion. Thirdly, while the NMuMG cell line reliably mimics EMT in vitro, the MCF‐7 and HT‐29 cell lines were not designed to assess EMT and therefore EMT induction may have only resulted in partial EMT, in which cells retain characteristics of epithelial cells. A further limitation is the use of imaging‐based methods to assess relative cholesterol distribution rather than absolute quantification, and imaging resolution constraints inherent to the available instrumentation. Future work will incorporate quantitative lipidomic approaches. While these limitations warrant caution in interpreting the results presented, a strength of this study is the use of various models of EMT, which ensures results are generalizable. The results of this study provide support for cyclodextrins as an alternate strategy to remove excess cholesterol in cancer cells, which may reduce EMT‐associated aggression and enhance the efficacy of current therapeutic options. Further validation in vivo is required to support these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have demonstrated that EMT induction is accompanied by changes in cellular cholesterol profile in the established NMuMG EMT model cell line. The MCF‐7 and HT‐29 cell lines were used to support these findings in a diverse set of cancer models. Treating cells undergoing EMT with cholesterol‐lowering agents, such as HPβCD or simvastatin, reverses expression patterns associated with EMT induction and attenuates EMT‐related characteristics of advanced cancers, such as invasive potential and drug resistance, across all models tested (Figure 6). The reversal of EMT‐associated expression patterns and characteristics supports the use of cholesterol‐lowering drugs such as HPβCD in the treatment of aggressive cancers with high risk of invasion or drug resistance. Although this study is limited to in vitro models, it provides preliminary evidence to justify further investigation of cholesterol‐lowering in combination with established therapeutic regimens to reduce the risk of metastasis and treatment resistance.
