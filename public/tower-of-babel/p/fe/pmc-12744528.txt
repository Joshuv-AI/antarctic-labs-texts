Prognostic impact of systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) on infarct size and clinical outcomes in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction
Authors: Ya-li Zhu, Lai Wei, Xu Wang, Yong Zhou, Jun Pu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic immune-inflammation index (SII), calculated as platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count, is a novel and easily accessible inflammatory marker. Its prognostic value in predicting infarct size and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) remains to be fully explored.MethodsWe analyzed 421 patients who underwent primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) within 12 h of symptom onset, enrolled in a prospective multicenter registry (NCT03768453).All patients received immediate admission blood tests for SII calculation (platelet × neutrophil/lymphocyte counts) and completed standardized CMR imaging within 10 days post-PCI.Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis identified the optimal SII cut-off value (914) to predict large infarct size (≥20 % of left ventricular mass). Patients were stratified into high (≥914) and low (<914) SII groups. The relationships between SII, infarct size, and MACE were analyzed using multivariate logistic and Cox regression models.ResultsPatients with high SII had significantly larger infarct size (median 29.0 % vs. 22.3 %, p < 0.001). SII ≥ 914 was independently associated with large infarct size (OR 1.889, 95 %CI: 1.100–3.242, p = 0.021) and higher incidence of MACE (HR 1.874, 95 % CI: 1.255–2.796, p = 0.002).ConclusionsElevated SII (≥914) independently associates with larger infarct size and increased MACE risk post-PCI, suggesting potential utility in risk stratification.Keywords: Systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR)

Introduction

ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) remains a leading cause of cardiovascular mortality worldwide [1,2]. Primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has been established as the preferred reperfusion strategy for STEMI patients within 12 h of symptom onset [3]. While timely PCI significantly reduces ischemic myocardial damage and mortality rates, patients continue to face substantial risks of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) following successful reperfusion [4].

The inflammatory response plays a crucial dual role in both the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis and subsequent myocardial remodeling following infarction [5]. Growing evidence has systematically evaluated associations between diverse inflammatory biomarkers and clinical outcomes in acute myocardial infarction (AMI) patients [[6], [7], [8]].

The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), calculated as platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count, provides a comprehensive assessment of systemic inflammatory status by integrating multiple immune pathways. Growing evidence indicates that SII serves as an independent predictor of adverse outcomes in STEMI, particularly for heart failure and stent thrombosis [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. However, the precise mechanisms linking SII levels to myocardial damage require further elucidation.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging with late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) has emerged as the reference standard for quantifying infarct size due to its superior spatial resolution and excellent reproducibility [14]. Multiple studies have confirmed that infarct size measured by CMR strongly correlates with long-term mortality and heart failure hospitalization rates [15]. We hypothesize that SII, as a sensitive marker of systemic inflammation, may contribute to more extensive myocardial necrosis, thereby increasing both infarct size measured by CMR and subsequent MACE.

Despite established associations between SII and clinical outcomes, the relationship between SII and CMR-derived infarct size in STEMI patients following PCI remains unexplored. This study was designed to: (1) evaluate the correlation between admission SII levels and infarct size quantified by CMR; and (2) determine whether SII independently predicts MACE in this patient population.

Methods

Study design and population

Study patient data were obtained from the prospective, multicenter EARLY-MYO-CMR(Early Assessment of Myocardial Tissue Characteristics by CMR in STEMI) registry (NCT03768453) [16,17], which enrolled consecutive ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) patients undergoing cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging for early myocardial tissue characterization. The registry was approved by an institutional review committee at each center following the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Between August 2013 and March 2021, 706 STEMI patients were prospectively enrolled. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) first-time STEMI diagnosis with typical ischemic symptoms and electrocardiographic ST-segment elevation (≥2 mm in ≥ 2 contiguous precordial leads or ≥1 mm in peripheral leads); (3) symptom-to-PCI time <12 h; (4) successful culprit lesion PCI; (5) complete admission blood tests; and (6) CMR performed within 10 days post-PCI. Key exclusion criteria included CMR contraindications (e.g., estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2), hemodynamic instability, pre-CMR mortality, or non-diagnostic image quality. After applying these selection criteria, 421 patients comprised the final analytic cohort. As detailed in Fig. 1 (Study flowchart).

Clinical data collection

We retrospectively collected baseline characteristics (age, sex, body mass index, medical history), ischemia–reperfusion time, and Killip classification from electronic medical records. Coronary angiography findings included infarct-related artery identification, multivessel involvement quantification, and TIMI flow grade assessment (pre- and post-reperfusion). Admission venous blood samples were analyzed using a Sysmex XN-4500 automated analyzer (Japan) for complete blood count (white blood cells, neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets) and derived indices (neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio [NLR], platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio [PLR], systemic immune-inflammation index [SII]), along with biochemical measurements (lipid profile, high-sensitivity troponin I, B-type natriuretic peptide).

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) protocol

CMR was performed at a median of 5 days (IQR: 3–6 days) post-PCI, as this time window is widely recognized as the optimal period for acute CMR assessment of infarct characteristics [18]. All CMR examinations were performed using a 3.0-Tesla scanner (Achieva TX; Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, Netherlands). The imaging protocol included (Fig. 2):

Cine imaging

Balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) sequences were acquired in 2-, 3-, and 4-chamber long-axis views, along with contiguous short-axis slices covering the left ventricle (LV) from base to apex. Imaging parameters: Field of view (FOV) = 300 × 300 mm2, voxel size = 1.5 × 1.5 × 6 mm3, repetition time/echo time (TR/TE) = 3.0/1.5 ms, flip angle = 45°

Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE)

To assess myocardial infarction (MI) size and microvascular obstruction (MVO), LGE imaging was initiated 10–15 min after intravenous administration of 0.15 mmol/kg gadopentetate dimeglumine (Bayer Healthcare, Canada). A phase-sensitive inversion recovery gradient-echo sequence (2D-PSIR) was used with the following parameters: TR/TE = 3.3/1.7 ms, flip angle = 25°, slice thickness = 10 mm

T2-weighted imaging

Short tau inversion recovery (STIR) T2-weighted spin-echo sequences were applied to evaluate intramyocardial hemorrhage (IMH). Parameters: FOV = 300 × 300 mm2, voxel size = 1.5 × 2 × 10 mm3, TR = 2 × RR interval, TE = 75 ms, flip angle = 90°

CMR image analysis

Post-processing was performed using Circle Cardiovascular Imaging® software (version 5.5.6.1). Key steps included:

Functional analysis

Endocardial and epicardial contours were manually traced in end-diastolic and end-systolic phases from short-axis cine images. LV volumes, ejection fraction (LVEF), and stroke volume were calculated, with papillary muscles included in myocardial mass.

Infarct quantification

On LGE images, MI was defined as myocardial signal intensity (SI) ≥ 5 standard deviations (SD) above remote normal myocardium. MVO was identified as hypointense regions within hyperenhanced myocardium. Results were expressed as % of LV volume.

Edema and hemorrhage assessment

On T2-weighted images, myocardial edema was defined as SI ≥ 2 SD above remote myocardium. IMH was characterized as hypointense regions within edematous myocardium [19]. Blood pool artifacts were excluded (Fig. 2).

Clinical outcomes

The primary endpoint was infarct size by CMR, while secondary endpoints included major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including all-cause death, nonfatal reinfarction, heart failure, and stroke during follow-up (median 3.8 years).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range). The strength and direction of associations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman's rank-order correlation. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies (percentages). The optimal cut-off value for the SII, as determined by maximizing Youden's index (sensitivity + specificity − 1), was applied to classify patients into two categories. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis determined optimal cutoff values for inflammatory indices. Multivariable regression models adjusted for clinically relevant covariates identified through univariate screening. Survival analysis employed Kaplan-Meier methods with log-rank testing. All statistical tests were two-tailed P value of <0.05 was considered nominally significant. A Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons across secondary outcomes. A two-sided P value of <0.01 (0.05/5) was considered statistically significant for these analyses. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Association between inflammatory indices and infarct size

Linear regression analysis demonstrated a significant association between systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) and infarct size (R2 = 0.034, p < 0.001), with stronger correlations than neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR; R2 = 0.023, p = 0.002) or platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR; R2 = 0.018, p = 0.006) (Fig. 3).

Scatterplot demonstrating linear relationship between SII and infarct size (IS). Abbreviations: IS, infarct size; SII, systemic immune-inflammation index.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis identified an optimal SII cutoff of 914 (AUC = 0.593, 95 % CI: 0.533–0.652) for predicting large infarcts (≥20 % left ventricular mass), with sensitivity of 62.6 % and specificity of 53.4 % (Fig. 4). This predictive value exceeded that of NLR (AUC = 0.583) and PLR (AUC = 0.527).

ROC curves comparing predictive value of SII, NLR and PLR for large myocardial infarction (≥20 % LV mass). Abbreviations: NLR, neutrophil to lymphocyte; PLR, platelet to lymphocyte; ROC, receiver-operator-characteristic; SII, systemic immune-inflammation index.

Baseline characteristics

The cohort (n = 421) was stratified by the SII cutoff into high (≥914, n = 242) and low (<914, n = 179) groups (Table 1). The high SII group showed significantly worse Pre-PCI TIMI flow grade (p = 0.008) and worse Post-PCI TIMI flow grade (p < 0.001), higher white blood cell counts (12.0 vs. 9.9 × 109/L, p < 0.001), platelet counts (254.5 vs. 196.0 × 109/L, p < 0.001), and Brain natriuretic peptide levels (230.0 vs. 145.0 pg/mL, p = 0.004), but lower triglyceride levels (1.6 vs. 1.7 mmol/L, p = 0.032).

During a median follow-up of 3.8 years (IQR: 2.2–6.8), 119 patients (28.3 %) experienced major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), comprising all-cause death (n = 32, 26.9 %), new-onset congestive heart failure (n = 62, 52.1 %), recurrent myocardial infarction (n = 24, 20.2 %), and stroke (n = 19, 16.0 %). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated significantly higher cumulative MACE incidence in patients with elevated systemic immune-inflammation index (SII ≥ 914) versus those with SII < 914 (79 events [32.6 %] vs. 40 events [22.3 %]; log-rank p < 0.001) (Figs. 5 and 6).

Cox regression analysis confirmed SII ≥ 914 as an independent predictor of MACE (HR 1.874, 95 % CI: 1.255–2.796, p = 0.002) after adjusting for clinical and imaging parameters. The higher risk of MACE in AMI patients with high SII was mainly driven by the higher rates of hard endpoints, including all-cause death (8.7 % vs. 6.1 %, HR 2.437, 95 % CI: 1.114–5.332, p = 0.026) and stroke (6.2 % vs. 2.2 %, HR 5.309, 95 % CI: 1.689–16.683, p = 0.004). Discussion

This study is the first to explore the relationship between the Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) and the infarct size measured by cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) and clinical prognosis in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Our main findings are as follows: (1) High SII (≥914) was significantly associated with the occurrence of large infarct size after PCI (OR 1.889, 95 %CI: 1.100–3.242, p = 0.021); (2) High-SII patients exhibited poorer baseline characteristics including lower pre-PCI and post-PCI TIMI flow grade, elevated inflammatory markers (leukocytosis, thrombocytosis), and higher B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP); (3) High SII was significantly associated with an increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) after PCI (HR 1.874, 95 % CI: 1.255–––2.796, p = 0.002), and it remained an independent predictor even after adjusting for confounding factors.

Atherosclerosis, the pathological foundation of coronary artery disease, progresses through chronic inflammation [20]. Plaque rupture involves complex innate and adaptive immune interactions [21]. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), integrating platelet, neutrophil, and lymphocyte counts, effectively reflects inflammatory and immune status. Previous studies have identified the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) as a robust predictor of adverse clinical outcomes in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) [9,10,11]. Our current findings significantly extend this evidence by demonstrating that elevated SII levels (≥914) exhibit an independent association with larger infarct size (quantified by delayed gadolinium-enhanced cardiac magnetic resonance imaging).

Neutrophils drive atherosclerotic progression via multiple mechanisms [22]. In acute myocardial infarction, activated neutrophils release reactive oxygen species (ROS), proteases (e.g. myeloperoxidase, elastase), and alarmins (S100A8/A9), destabilizing plaques [23]. ROS promote endothelial activation, matrix degradation, and oxidized LDL formation, exacerbating inflammation and plaque vulnerability [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Hypoxia-induced S100A8/A9 impairs cardiomyocyte mitochondrial function while activating Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) mediated cytokine release (such as interleukin (IL) − 6, tumor necrosis factor − α (TNF − α)) and granulopoiesis, perpetuating neutrophilia [28,29]. Myeloperoxidase-generated hypochlorous acid oxidizes LDL, accelerating foam cell formation [30,31]. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) activate NLRP3 inflammasomes, inducing IL-1β/IL-18 release, smooth muscle cell apoptosis, and fibrous cap thinning—key steps in plaque rupture [[32], [33], [34]].

Concurrently, stress-induced lymphopenia impairs immunoregulation, permitting uncontrolled inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and thrombosis [35,36]. Platelets amplify atherosclerosis through CD40L/P-selectin-mediated leukocyte recruitment and ROS-driven LDL oxidation, further destabilizing plaques [[37], [38], [39]]. These processes culminate in microthrombosis, vascular occlusion, and infarction [40]. The SII (platelets × neutrophils/lymphocytes) encapsulates these pathological changes. Elevated SII indicates pronounced inflammation, immunosuppression, and prothrombotic states, explaining its association with larger infarct size and worse outcomes [41].

CMR is the most accurate tool for visualizing and quantifying post-infarction parameters in AMI patients [42]. The infarct area measured by it is highly correlated with patient mortality and the hospitalization rate for heart failure [15]. This study is the first to link SII to the infarct area evaluated by CMR, confirming the significant correlation between high SII and large – area myocardial infarction, and further supporting SII as a reliable indicator for predicting the prognosis of STEMI patients.

Studies have shown that A high SII at admission in patients with acute myocardial infarction is associated with an increased risk of adverse events (such as arrhythmia, cardiogenic shock, and death) during hospitalization [43]. For example, in a study of 5602 CAD patients who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), Yang YL et al. demonstrated that SII is an independent predictor of all-cause mortality and non-fatal cardiovascular events [9]. And Li et al. confirmed that SII has a better predictive value for in-hospital MACE in patients with acute coronary syndrome than other inflammatory markers (such as NLR and PLR)[8].Our study also shows that a high SII is significantly correlated with the incidence of cardiovascular events within 7 years after myocardial infarction and can be used as an independent predictor of long-term prognosis.

Based on the fact that SII comprehensively reflects multiple pathophysiological processes such as inflammation, thrombosis, and immune stress. These factors jointly lead to a larger extent of myocardial necrosis and adverse remodeling, ultimately increasing the risk of MACE. This study confirmed that patients with SII ≥ 914 had a larger infarct area and a worse long-term prognosis, further supporting its value in risk stratification. Enabling early triage for intensive monitoring. This aligns with recent guidelines advocating inflammatory markers for STEMI prognostication [44].Compared with single inflammatory markers, SII provides a more dynamic and accurate assessment of inflammation, with higher sensitivity and predictive ability. As a simple, cost-effective, and easily accessible inflammatory marker, SII is suitable for the early risk stratification of STEMI patients in clinical practice, especially in resource-limited areas.

Conclusions

This study is the first to establish the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) as an independent predictor of both infarct size, as quantitatively assessed by cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), and long-term major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) patients following primary percutaneous coronary intervention (pPCI). A high SII was significantly correlated with larger myocardial infarct areas and an increased risk of hard clinical endpoints, independent of CMR examination timing and after rigorous adjustment for covariates and multiple comparisons. These findings underscore the potential of SII as an accessible biomarker for risk stratification in this patient population.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the exclusion of patients due to CMR contraindications, protocol non-adherence, or missing data may introduce selection bias, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings to hemodynamically stable STEMI patients eligible for CMR. Although standardized procedures were employed, potential measurement errors in CMR interpretation and clinical data collection cannot be entirely ruled out. Second, the predictive value of the SII was not correlated with established inflammatory biomarkers, such as C-reactive protein or interleukin-6; thus, we could not further validate its role as an inflammatory indicator. Future studies incorporating these biomarkers are warranted to corroborate our findings. Furthermore, the optimal SII cut-off value was derived from and specific to our cohort, and its discriminative power was modest. The generalizability and clinical utility of this threshold require validation in larger, prospective, and externally validated cohorts. Despite applying a conservative Bonferroni correction, our study may still be susceptible to inflation of type I error due to the exploratory nature of some analyses. The findings regarding secondary outcomes should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require validation in future studies. Finally, as our sample predominantly comprised an Asian population, the results may not be fully generalizable to other ethnicities or regions.
