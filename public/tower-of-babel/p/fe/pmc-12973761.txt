Vancomycin trough levels: it’s not one-size-fits-all
Authors: H. W. Zijlstra, F. Sombogaard, W-P. Manubulu-Choo
Published: 2026

To the editor

In a recent letter Movahedan et al. [1] commented on the recommended vancomycin trough levels mentioned in a review article on antibiotics in critical care patients [2]. In this brief report the authors highlighted that, based on recent pharmacokinetic studies, the traditional vancomycin trough levels of 15–20 mg/L in critically ill patients may lead to excessively high vancomycin exposure, with an area under the curve to minimum inhibitory concentration ratio (AUC/MIC) exceeding the recommended target range of 400–600 mg·h/L. We would like to address some important issues in this discussion.

Firstly, with the development of population pharmacokinetic (popPK) models and the use of Model Informed Precision Dosing (MIPD) software tools, the AUC/MIC can be estimated, using the vancomycin trough levels as a point of reference. In the Netherlands these models are based on a nationwide vancomycin database including critical care patients with altered drug pharmacokinetics. These models allow clinicians to directly target an AUC/MIC of 400–600 mg·h/L using trough levels, rather than aiming for a predefined trough level and assuming the corresponding AUC/MIC is appropriate. Using these models, the optimal trough levels are individualized and may vary between patients. When available, these models provide a more accurate and individualized approach to vancomycin dosing, and AUC/MIC guided therapy is recommended by American consensus guidelines [3] and might be associated with less nephrotoxicity.

More importantly, the vancomycin trough levels corresponding to an AUC/MIC of 400–600 mg·h/L is influenced by the interval of vancomycin administrations. As the therapeutic target is a specific 24-h AUC/MIC, more frequent administrations of an equal daily dose lead to lower peak levels and higher trough levels. This principle is illustrated in three hypothetical cases in which MIPD is applied to the same patient with normal renal function receiving vancomycin 30 mg/kg/d (Fig. 1). Firstly, 15 mg/kg is administered twice daily, resulting in an AUC of 415 mg·h/L with a trough level of 9.0 mg/L. When the same daily dose is administered four times daily (7.5 mg/kg), the same AUC of 415 mg·h/L is calculated, but this regimen results in a trough level of 11.7 mg/L. In contrast, continuous infusion of the daily dose results in a vancomycin level of 17.3 mg/L, which also corresponds with an AUC of 415 mg·h/L. During continuous vancomycin administration, a steady-state concentration of 16.7–25 mg/L corresponds with an 24-h AUC of 400–600 mg·h/L. The figure below shows the three cases.

Although the literature is variable on the factors associated with vancomycin nephrotoxicity [4], we believe mainly that limiting the peak levels could be beneficial, even though it results in higher trough levels, as long as the target AUC/MIC is reached. Continuous infusion of vancomycin is therefore generally preferred over intermittent infusion in many Dutch hospitals, particularly for critically care patients due to its more stable and consistent serum concentrations and lower risk of nephrotoxicity.

Finally, it is important to note that the relevant therapeutic target is an AUC above the MIC, which means that the trough level should be adjusted to the MIC. Depending on the causative bacteria and local antimicrobial resistance, the trough levels should be higher to reach therapeutic ranges. This is a generally accepted principle, however this nuance was lacking in the correspondence by Movahedan et al.

In conclusion, where targeting AUC/MIC is not feasible, the trough levels of vancomycin are not one-size-fits-all and are dependent on dosing frequency and MIC.
