The MERGE Dataset: A Harmonized Global Resource for Climate Disaster Impacts and Human Vulnerability
Authors: Debarati Guha-Sapir, Sarah Elizabeth Scales, Fabian Gans, Jemimah Ndugwa, Peter Huang, Álvaro José Guerrero Torres, Shannon Doocy, Markus Reichstein, Jeroen Smits, Andreas Forø Tollefsen, Henrik Urdal, Ben Zaitchik, Markus Enenkel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The MERGE Initiative is a global data infrastructure designed to address one of the most persistent challenges in climate disaster research and response: the fragmentation of data across incompatible systems, sectors, and formats. Despite a growing abundance of disaster-related data, the lack of harmonization and integration standards continues to hamper effective decision-making, risk modelling, and anticipatory action. MERGE brings together data holders and subject matter experts from leading scientific institutions, working collaboratively to curate, align, and integrate trustworthy data across disciplines at global scales. MERGE has, in its proof-of-concept phase, created a publicly available pilot dataset that integrates 250 + variables from 10 distinct databases. This MERGE pilot dataset is being used to develop cutting-edge data and methods to support researchers, governmental decision-makers, international financial institutions, local responders, and policymakers through access to timely and relevant data on climate-related disasters and human vulnerability. The MERGE platform is a public good and the proof-of-concept dataset is publicly available.

Background & Summary

As weather extremes and related disasters increase in both frequency and intensity, the global community faces a critical paradox. More data on disasters are being generated than ever before.

However, the ability to exploit the richness and added value of these data for timely, effective response remains insufficient, leading to an increase in data, but not in data-driven actionable knowledge. Inconsistent reporting and data harmonization standards, fragmented datasets, undocumented data gaps, and institutional silos leave decision-makers in many sectors without the coherent, high-resolution insights needed to protect vulnerable populations and mitigate cascading impacts. Despite decades of international calls for data harmonization, progress has been uneven and too slow, even in high-income countries1.

The MERGE initiative directly responds to this need through a collaborative, global data infrastructure within a single, free, publicly accessible platform. It focuses on one of the most significant barriers to effective data use – data integration across multiple disciplines. MERGE also builds on datasets curated by established scientific institutions, providing a guarantee of trustworthiness and quality, a major source of concern in recent years. All data are aligned with the FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) principles2 designed to simultaneously serve researchers and practitioners. Providing data that are truly “ready to use” is not merely a technical challenge. In a world where poorer communities are facing challenges related to the digital divide, it is also a scientific and moral imperative.

As the current climate change adaptation gap is widening3 and the humanitarian system is at a critical tipping point4, the cost of inaction is growing - both in terms of human lives and economic losses. Data relevant to human and economic disaster impacts are too often trapped in incompatible formats or scattered across disconnected domains: from emergency response documentation and demographic registries to climate reanalysis and geospatial data. In parallel, datasets like the International Disaster Database EM-DAT are often used as “ground truthing” or to calibrate financial instruments without considering data gaps or data quality. Recent research findings indicated significant levels of missingness in not only economic losses throughout various hydro-climatic disaster categories5, but also hazard-specific insights6. In the case of drought-related disasters, for instance, around two-thirds of all reported events include unclear or no statistics on human losses5.

Existing disaster datasets – such as the Dartmouth Flood Observatory, the United States Geological Survey, the Prompt Assessment of Global Earthquakes Response (PAGER), the European Joint Research Council’s Global Drought Observatory, and NASA’s Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS), among others – may include valuable information in isolation. However, without harmonized standards or metadata conventions, their collective potential is very limited. One dataset might record the spatial extent of a flood, while other tracks the affected population, and a third one estimates related economic losses. Different location codes, time frames, or naming conventions make it virtually impossible to compare or merge these datasets to get a holistic picture of a disaster’s impact and related historical trends. This fragmentation also hampers risk modelling efforts, which rely on high-quality, consistently validated data to produce accurate predictions, perform scenario analyses, or design parametric insurance triggers.

As a result, multiple institutions frequently duplicate efforts—independently cleaning, reformatting, and reconciling the same datasets—leading to inefficiencies and inconsistent outputs. The absence of shared standards or interoperable data structures not only slows down analysis but also increases the risk of analytical errors and missed insights. These challenges are particularly acute in settings where rapid, cross-sectoral understanding is needed to guide disaster preparedness and response7–13. Recognizing this, the MERGE initiative prioritizes systematic data integration from the outset, drawing on expert inputs including, but not limited to, authors of this paper.

Launched in 2023 at the Rockefeller Foundation’s Bellagio Center and then with initial start-up funding through the Foundation, the MERGE Initiative is a multi-stakeholder effort that establishes a scalable, open-access platform consolidating high-quality disaster data while preserving the disciplinary integrity of each component dataset. A unified data architecture that integrates diverse information streams and enables timely interpretation has the potential to transform fragmented inputs into actionable intelligence. MERGE complements existing and emerging decision-making tools by enabling local decision-makers, governments, and humanitarian actors to access integrated data, that can effectively support identification of vulnerable communities and risk factors to prioritize adaptation and mitigation action in disaster prone communities at high resolution all over the world. Drawing on the data legacy of participating Consortium members, MERGE unites climatological, social, economic, and geospatial datasets through a shared technical and ethical framework, supporting rapid analysis, policy formulation, and local adaptation strategies.

A core principle of the MERGE initiative is that datasets must be understood to be used responsibly. While Open Science principles and the revolution in public-facing dashboards and databases have enabled powerful integrated research efforts, they come with risks when it comes to high-stakes decision analyses, like those required for disaster preparedness and response. Misunderstanding of datasets can lead to misuse and unreliable conclusions. Recognizing this, MERGE was developed as a consortium initiative rather than as a single team project. Data Friendly Space (DFS), an informatic non-profit organisation, worked together with the Consortium members to apply data integration across the datasets. Involvement of PRIO as a long-term host for MERGE also brought in long-standing expertise in spatial data harmonization and interdisciplinary data infrastructure. It has a worldwide reputation in developing and maintaining large scale datasets including the PRIO-GRID framework. In this role, PRIO provides support to develop shared standards, transparent protocols, ensure interoperability across geographic, temporal, and thematic dimensions, and support construction of a scalable and analysis-ready data platform together with the specialised data suppliers. Surrounding this central team, the consortium includes experts involved in the production of all integrated datasets. No dataset is brought into MERGE without direct consultation with its primary producers or curators – whether government agencies, international organizations, academic centres, businesses, or non-profits. This approach of carefully documented direct exchange of expertise secures the reliability and replicability of the end product, necessary for both academic and non-academic user groups.

Methods

The MERGE method involves several crucial data processing steps (Fig. 1). It starts with an event-based database structure, where various climate variables, socioeconomic data, and other impact metrics are systematically layered onto specific events. For administrative boundaries, MERGE utilizes Global Administration Areas (GADM) as the foundational baseline, ensuring standardization and consistent alignment with primary ISO3 code standards across all datasets. Geospatial data undergo rigorous standardization, typically to a daily temporal resolution when hourly data is available. Cross-boundary calculations are handled through areal weighting, providing aggregated sums, means, maximums, and minimums. Areal statistics for administrative boundaries (e.g., GADM levels 0, 1, and 2) were calculated by clipping raster data to polygon geometries and computing area-weighted aggregations. A detailed description of these methods and code is available in the GitHub repository. All processing includes comprehensive quality control measures, including data schema validation, integrity checks, and systematic detection of duplicates.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of data processing steps.

Schematic diagram of data processing steps.

MERGE implements deterministic GLIDE-based deduplication. Accordingly, the pipeline inserted all EM-DAT. GLIDE code overlaps between EM-DAT and IDMC events were then checked. Matches updated the displacement field, and non-matches were inserted as new records. Standardization of location names to match GADM administrative boundaries was needed for both EM-DAT and IDMC data. The pipeline for processing and merging disaster event data from multiple sources into a unified PostgreSQL database is described in the README file. The pipeline standardizes event records, administrative locations, disaster classifications, and external identifiers to create a comprehensive events database for analysis. Event types are mapped and classified according to a peril classification and hazard glossary, consistent with taxonomies used by sources like EM-DAT. Basic unit conversions are consistently performed, including transforming all dates to ISO 8601 format (YYYY-MM-DD) and converting population figures and currencies to absolute numbers and USD, respectively. Data value extraction is achieved through direct value mapping for matched fields, simple handling of categorical data, and basic numerical value normalization. A hierarchical matching algorithm is then used to match GADM administrative boundaries. Mismatches between administrative boundaries were manually rectified. More information on Geospatial ISO/AdminName processing can be found in the README file within the GitHub repository.

Input data

MERGE draws on global data sources capable of assessing population vulnerability, displacement, socioeconomic exposure, and the biophysical dynamics of extreme climate events, while also offering strong potential for technical integration. Dataset selection was informed by the collective experience of the core MERGE team, which brought together decades of analysis and engagement in the disaster data ecosystem. The core members also have a long-standing working relationship, which is essential to sustainable co-operation in building a new initiative. The MERGE Initiative focused, in this phase specifically, on sources that employ publicly available and freely accessible data. In the proof-of-concept phase, preference was given to institutionalized datasets rather than those dependent on individual contributors, to ensure sustainability and reliability. The willingness of lead investigators to collaborate and contribute toward the creation of a global public data good was also a key criterion. Data sources used to build the PoC

We drew data from the data sources described in Table 1 to allow the PoC test for integration on a common frame. All institutions housing demographic, human geography, and health-related datasets collaborated closely with the MERGE team to allow the development of the PoC.

EM-DAT, the Emergency Events Database14–16 managed by the Centre for Research on the Epidemiology of Disasters (CRED), aggregates global disaster occurrence and impact data from 1900 to the present (https://public.emdat.be/). The database includes standardized metrics on mortality, injury, displacement, and economic loss. Sources include UN agencies, governments, NGOs, insurers, and academia, following a consistent inclusion methodology. EM-DAT became, over years, a key reference for disaster data scientists and has held this unique distinction for many years.

The Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre (IDMC)17,18 of the Norwegian Refugee Council serves as the global authority on internal displacement caused by conflict and disasters. IDMC’s Global Internal Displacement Database (GIDD) offers verified data at the first administrative level (Admin-1), supported by qualitative risk assessments. Displacement figures are compiled from government sources, humanitarian organizations, and media reports. Persistent challenges include limited data disaggregation, temporal resolution, and integration in fragile contexts. GIDD is available through the IDMC Data Portal (https://www.internal-displacement.org/database/displacement-data/).

The Global Data Lab19–25 (GDL, www.globaldatalab.org) at Radboud University has since the 1990s brought together over 600 household survey datasets for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). These datasets have been harmonized and combined in a huge data infrastructure, called Database Developing World (DDW), containing information on over 40 million persons from all regions of the developing world. Using this information, supplemented with data from other sources, indicators databases have been constructed for subnational areas across the globe. The Subnational Human Development Database20 contains subnational version of the UNDPs Human Development Index and Gender Development Index plus the underlying indices for education, health and standard of living for around 1800 regions in 180 + countries since 1990. The Subnational Corruption Database22 presents information on corruption in these regions since 2000. While SHDI and SCD contain yearly information for almost all countries of the world, the GDL Area Database23 and the Climate Change Vulnerability Database24,25 provide subnational development indicators only for LMICs. These databases contain 100 + indicators – including wealth, poverty, education, gender, demographics, health, infrastructure and vulnerability – for some 1500 regions within 130+ LMICs. Given that these indicators have been derived from household surveys, they are only available for survey years, which vary between 2 and 9 years in the 1990–2024 period. All indicators can be freely downloaded and used for non-commercial purposes in line with GDL’s terms of use (www.globaldatalab.org/termsofuse/) The GDL platform also supports custom mapping and data visualization.

The WorldPop26 database maps population and demographic data globally, with particular focus on LMICs. Since 2013, WorldPop has provided publicly available data, updatable maps, and metadata for 44,745 individual datasets (https://www.worldpop.org/).

MERGE also integrated publicly accessible and available physical science, earth observation, and environmental data. These sources include Copernicus’s European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) Reanalysis v5 (ERA-5)27 and European Space Agency Land Cover (ESA Land Cover)28,29 (https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/); the consortium of researchers that support the Global Fire Emissions Database (GFED)30 (https://www.globalfiredata.org/); the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations’s Global Livestock Environmental Assessment Model (GLEAM)31 (https://www.gleam.eu/#home); and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) Earth Science Data System’s (https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/) Modern-Era Retrospective Analysis for Research and Applications, version 2 (MERRA-2)32 (https://gmao.gsfc.nasa.gov/gmao-products/merra-2/) and MODerate-resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) Reflectance (MCD43C4)33,34 (https://ladsweb.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/missions-and-measurements/products/MCD43C4).

Complementary data distribution efforts

There are complementary data distribution efforts that informed the MERGE process and from which MERGE has benefited from subject matter expertise in principles of complex data integration. Data from these sources, however, were not integrated into the MERGE PoC dataset. These complementary data distribution efforts are described:

The Columbia University Center for Integrated Earth System Information (CIESIN) maintains a suite of population datasets using varied methodologies, including high-resolution spatial representations that allow real-time estimation of populations at risk of losing access to essential services during climate emergencies. These Socioeconomic Data and Applications Center (SEDAC) datasets are extensively used in spatial risk modelling and exposure analysis. Data previously managed by CIESEN-SEDAC is now accessible via NASA’s EarthData Search (https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/catalog).

MERGE was highly influenced by the The Max Planck Institute for Biogeochemistry (MPI-BGC) data harmonization effort of the Earth System Data Cube (ESDC). MPI-BGC serves as an example of expertise in data integration, particularly relevant for a range of spatial data. MPI-BGC supports the FLUXNET network, which produces datasets characterizing ecosystem responses to climate variability. Data are derived from Earth observation platforms and processed using machine learning to produce global estimates at resolutions of 5 km or finer. The institute also maintains a spatiotemporal catalog of extreme events based on FAIR and Analysis-Ready Data (ARD) principles. However, the databases that are sources into the FLUXNET (https://fluxnet.org/fluxnet-data-system/) network were used directly in the development of the MERGE proof-of-concept. Dataset selection was made with the MPI-BGC team with due diligence regarding the dataset quality, sustainability, and coverage. At this stage of project execution, the extraction of raw data was more efficient for integration. Beyond the proof-of-concept, the MERGE framework is readily extensible to established and emerging gridded flux and extremes datasets spanning complementary spatial scales and addressing ecosystems’ function and contribution to people. At the coarser end, the FLUXCOM ensemble provides global energy-flux products at 0.5° (RS + METEO) suitable for long-term, climate-forcing-consistent benchmarking and uncertainty propagation across methods and forcings35. Incorporating the new X-BASE (FLUXCOM-X) products adds ~5 km (0.05°), hourly estimates of CO₂ and water fluxes36, enabling cross-scale consistency checks, diurnal diagnostics, and improved evaluation of event-scale responses. Finally, the DeepExtremeCubes37 minicube collection offers even higher-resolution sub km scale, multi-modal samples targeted around compound heatwave-and-drought events; integrating these cubes would let us stress-test generalization under extremes, quantify scale breaks in drivers, and prototype explainable ML that links fine-scale impacts to coarser-scale flux anomalies. Together, these three sources—FLUXCOM (0.5°), X-BASE (~5 km), and DeepExtremes (sub-km minicubes)—outline a coherent path to multi-scale evaluation and upscaling within the same analytic pipeline.

Technical Validation

Several approaches were used for validation of ISO and administrative unit data. Match rate monitoring after each Extract, Transform, and Load (ETL) run was used to check for unmatched locations. For the GDL area datasets, the match rate was 85 – 95%, varying by country naming conventions. For GDL SHDI datasets, cleaner naming conventions resulted in a 90–98% match rate. WorldPop and IDMC had match rates of 100%, resulting from direct GADM GID usage and the exclusive use national-level data, respectively. Location matches were validated against expected country to verify ISO3 classification. UNIQUE constraints were used to prevent duplicates, and variables were converted to numeric types, with errors/missing values coerced to null. Population units were standardized to persons rather than millions or thousands of persons. The raw datasets provided population figures in different units. GDL Area data was in ‘millions of persons’ (e.g., a value of 39.5 representing 39.5 million) and GDL SHDI data was in ‘thousands of persons’ (e.g., 331.0 representing 331,000). We standardized all valid values to be absolute integer counts of individual persons (e.g., 39500000 or 331000) within the database. This ensures consistency across all population queries so that users do not need to check metadata to know if a column should be multiplied by 1,000 or 1,000,000. For GADM, the use of foreign key relationships enforced referential integrity (e.g., admin1 references admin0 via ISO3; admin2 references both admin0 via ISO3 and admin1 via GID_1).

Variable transformations from raw data imported for use in the MERGE dataset and subsequent validation are included in respective dataset folders within the GitHub repository. For events data, expected outputs were delineated, and verification code was generated to check against expected outputs. Quality control measures for Geospatial data included missing data tracking, data validation, and clear definitions of known issues with utilization of specific datasets. All scripts for geospatial processing calculate missing value percentages (e.g., 0% = full coverage; 100% = no data). MERGE database keys are derived from the GADM administrative boundaries, not the coverage of the input raster files. These are iterated through every global administrative unit and attempt to calculate statistics from the gridded data. If a specific raster dataset has no data for a given area (e.g., due to cloud cover, sensor limits, or being outside the dataset’s extent), MERGE still records that administrative unit in the database but flag it with a missing_value_percentage of 100% and NULL statistics. This allows users to explicitly query for ‘areas with no data’ rather than having them silently missing from the dataset. Automated validation checks included CRS conversion to EPSG4326, NoData value handling (specific to NetCDF), fill value masking (specific to NASA MCD43C4), and quality filtering (specific to NASA MCD43C4). MERGE is presented as a Proof of Concept (PoC), with purpose to demonstrate the feasibility and value of integrating heterogeneous disaster-related datasets into a harmonized structure. The limitations discussed below fall into two categories: (a) methodological limitations inherent to the data integration approach itself, and (b) items intentionally outside the PoC scope that would be addressed in a production deployment but are not relevant to evaluating the PoC’s contribution.

Geospatial processing limitations

All-touched clipping bias: Due to the computational constraints of the PoC environment, most raster processing uses all_touched clipping, which overestimates area for small polygons (up to + 44% error for 50 km regions with 0.25-degree cells). An area-weighting method is already implemented and available in the codebase for ERA5 and can be extended to other raster indicators in a production deployment (see Next Steps).

Relevant considerations outside of the PoC scope

The following are not limitations of the PoC methodology, but rather engineering and operational considerations that were intentionally deferred as they fall outside the scope of a Proof of Concept. The PoC’s objective is to demonstrate the feasibility and value of harmonized multi-source disaster data integration — not to deliver a production-ready platform. These items are noted here for transparency and to inform future development:Scalability: The current single PostgreSQL instance handles the PoC’s data volumes (tens of millions of rows) without issue. Scaling to sub-daily resolution or higher spatial granularity would require partitioning or distributed systems in a production setting.Deployment automation: Some scripts use interactive configuration for database credentials. Fully automated, containerized deployment would be standard in a production system.External data provider dependencies: Changes to upstream APIs, access policies, or data formats could affect individual ETL pipelines. Version-pinning and monitoring would be part of a production maintenance strategy.GADM version management: All spatial referencing is anchored to GADM v4.1. A production system would include a migration strategy for boundary version updates.
