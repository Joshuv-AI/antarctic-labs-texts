The incidence and risk factors of urinary tract infections in patients undergoing bladder tumor resection: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Siyu Lin, Yunlan Jiang, Ting Xu, Xinyao Zhou, Xiaodi Bai, Shulan Liu, Heyao Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimTo summarise the available evidence concerning the incidence and risk factors of UTIs in bladder cancer patients after surgery.MethodsSystematic searches were conducted on PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, the China National Knowledge Base Database (CNKI), Wanfang Database, Vips Database (VIP), and the China Biomedical Database (Sinomed). These searches encompassed literature from the inception of each database up to March 2025. This study adhered rigorously to the PRISMA guidelines. The quality of the studies included in the review was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Centre for Evidence-Based Health Care in Australia and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.ResultsA total of 19 original studies were included in this analysis, encompassing 5,905 patients. The meta-analysis results indicated that the incidence of UTIs in patients with bladder tumor resection was 22.3% [95% CI (17.7, 27.3)]. The identified risk factors for UTIs in patients with bladder cancer after surgery include diabetes, age, preoperative catheter indwelling, Use of antibiotics before surgery, and Operation time≥90 min.ConclusionsUTIs are higher in patients who have undergone bladder tumor resection. Clinical staff should prioritize preoperative assessment and risk stratification for UTIs. They must adhere to established guidelines and recommendations regarding the prophylactic use of antibiotics before surgery, maintain strict control of patients’ blood sugar levels, and manage catheters meticulously to minimize the risk of UTIs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-025-01751-5.

Introduction

Bladder cancer is a type of malignant tumor affecting the urinary system, characterized by a high incidence and fatality rate. It ranks as the tenth most common cancer globally, representing a significant threat to public health [1, 2]. A study conducted in 2022 indicated that the incidence and mortality rates of bladder cancer are expected to rise further over the next decade [3]. Approximately 430,000 new cases are diagnosed annually [4]. Treatment options for bladder cancer are primarily determined by the stage at which the disease is diagnosed. It usually involves transurethral resection of bladder cancer (TURBC), partial or radical cystectomy (RC), intravesical immunotherapy, chemotherapy, or systemic chemotherapy with or without radiation [5, 6]. Surgery can effectively reduce the recurrence of bladder cancer and delay tumor progression. However, it may also result in a range of potential infections. A urinary tract infection (UTI) is a common complication associated with urological surgery. Since surgical resection is invasive, surface pathogens can quickly enter the urinary system during surgery, significantly increasing the risk of postoperative infection [7]. In particular, surgical resection can damage the bladder wall and urethral mucosa, further compromising the defense mechanisms of the urethra and facilitating the retrograde movement of bacteria [8]. Therefore, UTIs have the highest incidence among various types of infections. This is not only a significant risk factor for poor postoperative outcomes in patients with bladder cancer, but it also impacts surgical effectiveness, prolongs hospital stays, and can lead to death in severe cases [9]. Around 150 million people worldwide die each year from UTIs and related complications [10].

However, the incidence survey across different regions is incomplete, with few comparative studies with other countries. Additionally, the relevant risk factors discussed have not been standardized, and there is insufficient research on individualized prevention strategies for various risk groups. Therefore, this study aims to fill these knowledge gaps through a domestic and international meta-analysis of the incidence and influencing factors of UTIs following bladder cancer surgery. The goal is to provide evidence-based recommendations for clinical treatment and patient care.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This study has been registered with PROSPERO under the registration number CRD42024569662. The systematic review used PRISMA (the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines.

Search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, the China National Knowledge Base Database (CNKI), Wanfang Database, Vips Database (VIP) and the China Biomedical Database (Sinomed) to collect current studies on the incidence of UTIs following the resection of bladder tumors. The search was conducted until March 2025. The search terms employed in this study encompassed (“Urinary Bladder Neoplasm*” OR “neoplasm urinary bladder” OR “bladder tumor*” OR “tumor* bladder” OR “neoplasms bladder” OR “bladder neoplasm*” OR “neoplasm bladder” OR “urinary bladder cancer” OR “cancer urinary bladder” OR “malignant tumor of urinary bladder” OR “cancer of the bladder” OR “bladder cancer*” OR “cancer bladder” OR “cancer of bladder”)AND (“Urinary Tract Infections” OR “infection* urinary tract” OR “tract infection* urinary” OR “urinary tract infection” ) AND (“risk factor*” OR “relevant factor*” OR “root cause analysis” OR “influence factor*” ). The search strategy employed a combination of subject vocabulary and free terms.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria included (1) cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, and case-control studies; (2) Participants were patients after bladder tumor resection; (3) The outcome indexes were the incidence and risk factors of UTIs after bladder tumor resection.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) narrative reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, and conference abstracts; (2) literature that did not report or from which the prevalence of UTIs could not be extracted; (3) literature for which the full text was unavailable; and (4) duplicate publications.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently screened the literature by reviewing the title, abstract, and full text to determine study eligibility. Data extraction tables were utilized to collect the following information: first author, year of publication, study area, type of study design, sample size, surgical modalities, and risk factors. Two independent researchers conducted separate data extractions and quality assessments for all included studies.

Assessment of methodological quality

The quality of cross-sectional studies was evaluated using the quality assessment tool for cross-sectional studies developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Centre for Evidence-Based Health Care in Australia [11]. This tool comprises eight items, with a maximum score of eight; a higher total score indicates better methodological quality of the literature. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was employed to assess the risk of bias in case-control and cohort studies [12]. A total score ranging from 0 to 9 was assigned, with scores of 0–3 categorized as low quality, 4–6 as moderate quality, and 7–9 as high quality. Each evaluator independently completes the literature quality assessment. After completing the evaluation, the evaluators cross-check each other’s scores and flag articles with discrepancies. Both parties then re-examine the relevant sections and reach a consensus based on the evaluation criteria. If the evaluators cannot agree through discussion, a third evaluator may be invited to arbitrate.

Statistical analysis

The analysis was conducted using Stata 15.0 software and Review Manager Software 5.3. The I² statistic and Q test were used to assess the heterogeneity among the included studies. When P>0.1 and I² < 50%, indicating low heterogeneity. Conversely, a random effects model was selected when P≤0.1 and I²≥50%, indicating high heterogeneity. Factors such as diabetes, preoperative catheter indwelling, type of operation, and study designs were analyzed to explore the sources of heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis was used to evaluate the stability of the research findings. Odds ratios (OR) and associated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used in Review Manager Software 5.3 to assess the risk variables for UTIs in patients undergoing bladder tumor resection. A funnel plot and Begg’s and Egger’s tests were used to evaluate publication bias. The significance level for the meta-analysis was set at α=0.05.

Results

Search results

After the retrieval process, 1,376 articles were obtained. After reviewing the titles and abstracts, we removed 283 duplicate articles and excluded 1,042 with inconsistent content. Additionally, there were 32 articles from which data could not be extracted. After careful screening, 19 articles were included in the final analysis [13–31]. (Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics of the included studies

A total of 19 articles were included in this study, encompassing 5,905 patients. The papers were published between 2016 and 2025 and comprised 4 cross-sectional studies and 15 case-control studies (Table 1). Among the 19 studies analyzed, 1 originated from South Korea, 1 from India, and 2 from the United States, while the remaining studies were all conducted in China. The range of NOS was 6–8, while the range of JBI was 6–8. The incidence of UTIs in patients undergoing bladder tumor resection ranges from 10 to 66%.

Incidence of utis

We quantitatively pooled 19 studies and found significant heterogeneity among them (I² = 94.57%, P<0.001). Analysis using a random effects model indicated that the incidence of UTIs in patients undergoing bladder tumor resection was 22.3% [95% CI (17.7, 27.3)]. (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis

The high level of heterogeneity observed in this study necessitated the categorization of groups according to the type of surgery, study design and region. Despite this categorization, the heterogeneity remained high, so the random effects model was chosen. The results are as follows. (1) Type of operation: The incidence of UTIs was higher with TURBC (24.8%) than with RC (20.0%); (2) Study designs: The incidence of UTIs in cross-sectional studies and case-control studies was 21% and 22.9%, respectively. (3) Region: The incidence of UTIs among patients undergoing bladder tumor resection is more outstanding in Asia (23.0%) compared to non-Asian regions (15.6%). (Table 2)

Risk factors of utis in patients undergoing bladder tumor resection

Our study identified 11 associated risk factors, with data pooled for five. Only qualitative descriptions were provided for the remaining factors due to the limited number of studies or the inability to combine data. By extraction and analysis of OR values for corrected confounders from the original study, we identified five risk factors (diabetes, age, preoperative catheter indwelling, Use of antibiotics before surgery, and Operation time≥90 min.) associated with UTIs in patients undergoing bladder tumor resection(Table 3). Age was mentioned in eight studies, but only six were included in the analysis due to differing age criteria. Two studies used a cutoff of ≥60 years, both showing statistically significant results (OR 3.27, 95% CI 1.199–8.924; OR 2.49, 95% CI 1.13–5.49) [19, 30]. Two studies identified bladder irrigation≥2 days as a significant risk factor (OR 8.16, 95% CI 3.61–18.42; OR 4.00, 95% CI 1.32–5.51) [13, 15]. Multiple tumors were analyzed in two studies (OR 4.18, 95% CI 2.50–6.98; OR 4.12, 95% CI 1.56–5.54) [13, 19], and preoperative positive urine culture was reported in two studies (OR 8.26, 95% CI 2.73–24.95; OR 7.06, 95% CI 2.13–23.27) [18, 26], both with statistically significant results. Long-term bed rest was analyzed in three studies, all showing significance (OR 2.53, 95% CI 1.44–2.36; OR 6.62, 95% CI 2.05–21.34; OR 2.77, 95% CI 1.27–6.06) [17, 22, 30], but due to unclear criteria, these results were not combined. Ureteral stenosis was reported in two studies (OR 5.93, 95% CI 1.28–27.52; OR 17.9, 95% CI 3.95–81.18) [23, 24], both statistically significant. CCI was analyzed in two studies. One study divided CCI into three levels, while the other did not specify criteria and concluded that CCI was a risk factor [23, 28].

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

No significant outcome changes were observed through the sequential elimination of individual studies, indicating a robust meta-analysis (Fig. 3). The funnel plot reveals an asymmetric distribution of study points across the axis and outliers, indicating potential publication bias. This was further supported by Egger’s regression test, which showed significant publication bias (t=3.62, P=0.002), likely due to small sample sizes and methodological limitations in some included studies. We applied the trim-and-fill method to assess further publication bias, which iteratively imputed missing studies and recalculated the effect size. The adjusted effect size showed minimal change compared to the original, indicating that publication bias had a limited impact on the results (Fig. 4).

Meta-regression test

Given that the overall prevalence derived from meta-analysis is influenced by various factors, including sample size, study year, and age of participants, these factors contribute to increased heterogeneity and reduced accuracy of the results. To address this, meta-regression analysis was employed to investigate the relationship between the incidence of UTIs following bladder tumor resection and these potential influencing factors. However, due to the lack of precise age data in most of the included studies, the meta-regression analysis was limited to examining the effects of two variables: sample size and study year. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the incidence of UTIs exhibited a statistically significant decrease with increasing sample size (P<0.05). Furthermore, Fig. 6 demonstrates that the study year did not significantly impact the incidence of UTIs.

Discussion

UTIs can lead to a range of adverse clinical outcomes. The emergence of multidrug-resistant bacteria may lead to complex UTIs. Sepsis following UTIs, often caused by complex postoperative infections, has a mortality rate of 13%, increasing to 20% in men over 65 years of age [32]. Survival analysis from another study revealed that patients developing a UTI 31–90 days post-discharge had poorer overall survival [33]. In addition, another study found that postoperative UTIs were significantly associated with the risk of tumor recurrence (OR, 3.331; p=0.001) [7]. Bacillus Calmette-Guerin (BCG) immunotherapy is a successful adjuvant therapy that reduces bladder cancer’s recurrence and progression rate. However, adverse reactions can destroy the patient’s treatment compliance and clinical effect. An RCT study showed that 23% of patients receiving BCG maintenance therapy developed bacterial cystitis [34]. 70–80% of bladder cancer patients treated with BCG develop lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) within 48 h [35]. Due to the severity of LUTS, 7-20% of patients are forced to stop BCG treatment, increasing the risk of cancer progression and reducing survival [36]. Therefore, it is imperative to summarise and analyze the risk factors of UTIs to help medical workers better identify them. Our study examined the incidence and risk factors of UTIs in patients undergoing bladder tumor resection, suggesting the importance of controlling UTIs for medical workers and accurately identifying their risk.

The included studies did not explicitly report detailed information on UTI definitions, urine sampling methods, or isolated microorganisms. Our study provides only a qualitative description of these aspects. A total of 19 studies were included in this research, of which 6 defined UTIs as the presence of gram-positive bacteria at ≥104 CFU/mL and gram-negative bacteria at ≥105 CFU/mL [13, 15, 19, 26, 29, 31]. The criteria for defining UTIs were not reported in 8 studies. In one paper, the leukocyte count exceeded 10 cells per high-power field (HPF) [30]. Two studies reported colonies at ≥105 CFU/mL [23, 28], while two others reported colonies at ≥104 CFU/mL [24, 27]. Direct analysis of midstream urine was documented in 7 studies [13, 14, 18, 23, 24, 26, 31], while analysis of clean urine collected in a urine bag was reported in 2 studies [15, 29]. The remaining studies did not provide clear information on the subject. Escherichia coli was the most commonly isolated microbe in the majority of studies [13, 14, 16, 18–20, 25, 28, 31], followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae [13, 14, 19, 20, 31], which was also frequently reported. Additionally, Pseudomonas aeruginosa [16, 31], Acinetobacter baumannii [16], Enterococcus [23, 24, 27], and Proteus mirabilis have been identified in several studies [26].

Our data showed that 22.3% of patients having bladder tumors removed had UTIs. According to earlier research, the incidence of UTIs ranges from 7–30% [37], where our meta-analysis is located. By contrast, a meta-analysis conducted in Iran on UTIs in kidney transplant recipients found that the incidence was 35% [38]. This figure is notably higher than our study. The reason may be attributed to the extensive use of immunosuppressants and antibiotics in kidney transplantation, which increases the body’s susceptibility to various infections [39, 40].

Subgroup analysis results indicated that Asian patients undergoing bladder tumor surgery experienced a higher incidence of UTIs compared to non-Asian patients. A study of the global burden of UTIs shows that East and South Asia are already the regions with the highest incidence, prevalence and mortality in 2021 [41]. This phenomenon may be attributed to several factors. First, as of 2022, Chinese adults with diabetes represent 18% of the global adult diabetes population, ranking second worldwide [42]. The risk of postoperative UTIs is significantly elevated among diabetic patients. Second, antibiotic resistance is more prevalent in Asia than Europe and the Americas [43]. This increase in resistance complicates infection treatment, potentially leading to a higher incidence of postoperative UTIs. The type of surgery also influences UTIs in people who have had bladder tumors removed. One study revealed that UTIs following TURBT were 42%, ranking third in frequency, surpassed only by hematuria and acute urinary retention [44]. TURBT procedures are conducted via the urethra, which inevitably inflicts some degree of trauma to the urethral mucosa. The function of the mucosal barrier is compromised by this injury, which reduces the resistance to bacterial colonization [45]. Our study revealed that the incidence of UTIs was marginally higher in the case-control studies compared to the cross-sectional studies. We think that this discrepancy may be attributed to several factors, including sample size, geographical variations, and differences in statistical methodologies. Firstly, our study revealed a significant imbalance in study designs, with case-control studies outnumbering cross-sectional studies by a ratio of three to one. Additionally, the sample sizes exhibited considerable variability across the different study types. The minimum sample size of the four cross-sectional studies included was 114, while the maximum reached 1,133. In contrast, the sample sizes for the case-control studies ranged from 76 to 1,248. These findings indicate a pressing need for more high-quality research in the future to provide accurate and reliable estimates of the incidence of UTIs in patients following bladder tumor resection across various study designs.

In our study, five risk factors were found to have a statistically significant association with UTIs in patients undergoing bladder tumor resection. These factors include diabetes, age, preoperative catheter indwelling, Use of antibiotics before surgery, and Operation time≥90 min. Diabetes emerges as a significant risk factor for UTIs in this patient population, corroborating findings reported by Fu [46]. A meta-analysis demonstrated that the prevalence of UTIs in patients with type 2 diabetes was 11.5% [47], which was significantly higher than that in non-diabetic individuals. In addition, The risk of UTI development was found to increase with age. For instance, in Carrondo’s study, the incidence of UTIs was 9% in individuals aged 18–64, compared to 27.5% in those over 85 years [48]. Another epidemiological study showed geographical differences in diabetic UTIs, with the highest incidence in developing countries (24%) [49]. The correlation between age and the incidence of UTIs can be attributed to the degeneration of the kidneys and the mucosal membranes of the urinary tract, which increases the likelihood of bacterial invasion from urinary fluid, thereby facilitating the development of UTIs [50]. Furthermore, studies have reported that the incidence of UTIs in individuals over the age of 60 is three to five times higher than in those aged 50 to 60 years [51]. Deaths from UTIs occur primarily in people over the age of 80 [41]. Preoperative indwelling catheters are recognized as a significant risk factor for UTIs, corroborated by previous research [52]. Of hospital-acquired UTIs, 75% are classified as catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs) [53]. Another study showed that longer catheter retention times can increase infection rates by up to 10% per day [54]. Among hospitalized patients with indwelling catheters for 2–10 days, 26% developed bacteriuria, 24% had UTIs, and 3.6% experienced bacteremia of urinary origin [55]. Our study further identified that the Use of antibiotics before surgery and Operation time≥90 min were significantly correlated with UTIs in patients undergoing bladder tumor resection. An extended operative time increases the duration of exposure between the surgical wound and pathogenic bacteria, thereby elevating the likelihood of various infections [56].

Previous meta-analyses identified age, preoperative pelvic radiotherapy, hospital stay, surgery duration, tumor size, and pyuria as potential UTI risk factors [57], with three overlapping factors in this study. The incidence of UTI was only 3.2%, which was significantly different from the 22.3% in this study. In addition, our study explored risk factors more deeply compared to previous systematic reviews that focused more on antimicrobial use. However, this meta-analysis only descriptively examined three studies without quantitative results. It included seven reports, three from conferences, and lacked rigorous quality assessment. Our study addresses these limitations by expanding the number of included studies, performing quantitative risk factor analysis, and utilizing meta-regression to investigate sources of heterogeneity further.

Based on the findings of this study and evidence-based practices, clinicians should assess and stratify patients’ risk before surgery [58]. They should adhere to guidelines for the prophylactic use of antibiotics, select the most effective against various pathogens, and utilize antibiotics with a narrow spectrum of activity to minimize collateral damage [59]. A multidisciplinary management policy is implemented for perioperative diabetic patients to flexibly control blood glucose levels based on the varying underlying conditions of each patient. Additionally, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists may offer benefits for immediate perioperative blood glucose control [60]. UTI monitoring should be conducted based on a facility’s risk assessment and/or local regulatory requirements. The patient population at risk of UTI can be identified by reviewing the frequency of catheter use and other potential risk factors [61].

Limitations of the study

Most of the literature included in this study consists of case-control studies, and retrospective studies may be more susceptible to recall bias. The reviewed literature lacked comprehensive details on prophylactic antibiotic use, aseptic surgical procedures, and nosocomial infection control measures. Additionally, few studies clearly defined UTIs or detailed pathogen information from urine samples. The absence of aggregated analysis on these aspects further limits the findings. Many studies tend to have small sample sizes, resulting in insufficient test efficiency and representation, and a large amount of literature from Asia affects the reliability of the results.

To enhance the accuracy and reliability of future studies, we recommend conducting multi-center, large-sample research incorporating diverse study designs and geographic regions. This approach will improve the representativeness and dependability of the results. Furthermore, it is essential to harmonize the diagnostic criteria for UTIs and establish standardized prophylactic antibiotic protocols to minimize heterogeneity.

Conclusion

Postoperative UTIs represent a prevalent complication in patients undergoing bladder tumor resection. Both prior research and our study corroborate the association of numerous risk factors with the incidence and progression of this complication, which may subsequently contribute to elevated mortality rates among patients. Identifying individuals at high risk for UTIs allows for more targeted treatment and care strategies, thereby enhancing patient safety. Future research should focus more on preventing and managing UTIs preoperatively and postoperatively.
