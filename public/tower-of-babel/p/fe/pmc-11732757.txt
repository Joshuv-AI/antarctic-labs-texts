Homogenization-circulating ultrasound in combination with aqueous enzymatic pretreatment for microwave-assisted extraction of kernel oil and essential oil from the fruit of Litsea cubeba
Authors: Xinyu Yang, Yang Yang, Ke Zhang, Ru Zhao, Hao Tian, Lei Yang, Xiuhua Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Homogenization-circulating ultrasound and enzymatic pretreatment was used.•Kernel oil and essential oil were obtained simultaneously from Litsea cubeba fruits.•Ultrasound treatment resulted in increased kernel and essential oil yield.•D-limonene, trans-citral, and cis-citral are the main component of essential oil.•C10 and C12 medium-chain fatty acids are the main components of the kernel oil.

Introduction

Plants produce volatile secondary metabolites that combine to form essential oils. Essential oils are composed primarily of monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, and some low-molecular-weight aromatic compounds. Owing to their unique chemical composition and biological activities, essential oils have garnered widespread attention. They are known for their significant antioxidant [1], [2], anti-inflammatory [3], and antifungal properties [4], [5]. As a global plant-based product, the value and impact of essential oils should not be underestimated. They hold great potential in the medical field and possess broad commercial value. Their applications have a substantial impact on commerce in a variety of industries, including agriculture, food and beverage, pharmaceutical, nutritional supplement, perfume, and personal care items [6]. The use of essential oils is particularly important for aromatherapy and wellness. Unlike standard plant extracts, essential oils involve simple distillation and separation processes. These methods are not typically aimed at isolating single components but rather are used in their entirety [7]. The extensive industry application of essential oils provides substantial economic benefits to the market.

Vegetable oils are natural fats extracted from seeds, fruits, or other parts of plants, and they are widely used in the food, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, and chemical industries. The chemical composition of vegetable oils is diverse and primarily consists of fatty acids, tocopherols, phytosterols and so on [8], [9]. These components contribute to various health benefits, such as antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, and prevent cardiovascular diseases. In recent years, as awareness of health and environmental sustainability has increased, the demand for vegetable oils has increased. The sustainable production and use of vegetable oils have become important research areas, particularly for reducing greenhouse gas emissions and protecting biodiversity. Additionally, vegetable oils show potential in industrial applications such as biodiesel, metal working fluids, coatings, and lubricants. Medium-chain fatty acids (MCFAs) are a unique type of fatty acid found in natural oils and are characterized by a chain length of 6 to 12 carbon atoms. They are primarily present in a few vegetable oils, such as coconut oil [10], palm kernel oil [11], and babassu oil [12]. MCFAs have gained attention because of their distinctive metabolic pathways and health benefits. They are easily digested and absorbed in the body and quickly converted into energy rather than stored in fat tissue, making them potentially beneficial for controlling obesity and improving metabolism. Furthermore, they are being studied for their role in managing conditions such as diabetes and hypertension. Another important application of MCFAs is in the food industry, where they are employed as stabilizers to improve the flavor and texture of food products [13].

Owing to the significant differences in chemical composition and molecular structure between essential oils and kernel oils, essential oils consist of volatile small molecules with low boiling points. In contrast, kernel oils are primarily composed of larger, nonvolatile fatty acids with higher boiling points. These differences in physicochemical properties make simultaneous extraction of both essential oils and kernel oils from plants challenging. Regardless of the extraction method used, overcoming the barrier that prevents these oils from escaping—the plant cell wall—is necessary. The aqueous enzymatic method involves the hydrolysis of specific chemical bonds in cell wall components, such as β-1,4-glycosidic bonds [14], and disrupts the ultrastructure of oleosomes, allowing the release of essential and kernel oils. However, aqueous enzymatic methods have the drawback of lower extraction efficiency than traditional methods do. Ultrasound irradiation technology offers a rapid and efficient method for breaking down cell wall structures with low energy consumption [15]. Partial collapse or breakage of the cell wall can reduce the mass transfer barrier for enzyme solutions or extraction solvents [16]. As ultrasound waves propagate through a liquid medium, they create rapid compression, forming and collapsing tiny bubbles in the liquid, which generates shear forces [17], [18]. In conventional ultrasound devices, ultrasound waves are often confined to a fixed space, with plant materials relatively stationary, limiting the cavitation effect on the material. However, in circulating ultrasound systems, a rotary controller can promote the movement of materials within the ultrasound field, thereby increasing the efficiency of the ultrasound process [16], [19], [20].

Litsea cubeba is a shallow-rooted, high-fruiting deciduous small tree in the Lauraceae family. Native to the southern and southeastern regions of China, it is now widely cultivated in areas south of the Huai River. It is also found in South Asia and Southeast Asia. This plant has a wide range of uses and is an important economic species. The wood of L. cubeba is resistant to moisture and insect infestation, making it suitable for furniture and construction materials. The flowers, leaves, and fruits all contain essential oils, which are used in pharmaceuticals and fragrance formulations. Kernels contain fatty acids, particularly MCFAs, which are known to improve metabolism [21], support cardiovascular health [22], [23], treat insulin resistance [24], and protect the nervous system [25].

The purpose of this study was to develop a homogenization-circulating ultrasound combined with aqueous enzymatic pretreatment and a microwave-assisted extraction technique (HCUEPM) to obtain high-yield kernel oil and essential oil from L. cubeba fruits, which has significant economic value. After the effects of enzyme type and concentration were investigated, a Plackett–Burman design (PBD) was used to identify significant factors among parameters that could influence the yields of kernel oil and essential oil. A Box–Behnken design (BBD) was subsequently employed to further optimize three statistically significant factors to determine the optimal extraction conditions. The components of the obtained kernel oils and essential oils were analyzed via GC–MS, and the results were compared with the products obtained via traditional homogenization-assisted hydrodistillation (HMHD) and solvent reflux extraction-hydrodistillation (SEHD) methods.

Experimental

Materials and chemicals

In August 2022, L. cubeba fruits were collected from Gulika village in Nongzhang town (Yingjiang, China). The fruits were naturally air-dried and stored in a dry, dark environment. To ensure consistency and prevent variability in the experimental results, all the experiments used the same batch of collected fruits. The enzymes used were purchased from Yuanye (Shanghai, China) and included cellulase (containing endoglucanase EC 3.2.1.4, exoglucanase EC 3.2.1.176 and EC 3.2.1.91, and β-glucosidase EC 3.2.1.21, 400 U/mg), hemicellulase (EC 3.1.73, 20 U/mg), pectinase (EC 3.2.1.15, 500 U/mg), neutral protease (EC 3.4.24.4, 100 U/mg), acid protease (EC 3.4.23.6, 50 U/mg), alkaline protease (EC 3.4.21–24, 200 U/mg), and α-amylase (EC 3.2.1.1, 100 U/mg).

Simultaneous extraction of kernel oil and essential oil from L. cubeba fruits

A TGCXN-2B laboratory-scale ultrasonic circulating extraction device (Beijing Hongxianglong Biotechnology Co., Beijing, China) (UCE, working volume of 2 L) and a WS-003 homogenizer (Zhongshan Jincai Electronics Co., Ltd., Zhongshan, China) were used for homogenization-circulating ultrasound in combination with aqueous enzymatic pretreatment. An appropriate amount of L. cubeba fruit was accurately measured, and different types of enzyme solutions were added and adjusted to a suitable pH according to various liquid–to–solid ratios. The mixture was then placed in a homogenization-circulating ultrasound system and incubated under various conditions, including temperature, time, liquid–solid ratio, pH, stirring speed, homogenization time, duty cycle, and ultrasound irradiation power. In accordance with the experimental design, three parallel experiments were conducted under each set of conditions.

After incubation, each sample was separated from the circulating ultrasound system and centrifuged at 8000 r/min for 30 min. The centrifuged mixture was separated into an oil layer, an emulsion layer, a water layer, and a plant residue. The oil layer at the top was collected and retained. The emulsion layer was collected and frozen at −20 °C overnight, thawed in a 30 °C water bath and left to stand for half an hour to collect the oil layer, which was then combined with the previously collected oil layer.

The combined oil layers of each sample were supplemented with an equivalent volume of deionized water, and hydrodistillation was performed via a microwave-assisted hydrodistillation apparatus (Midea M1-L213B microwave oven (Foshan, China)). The essential oil was collected from a Clevenger. To separate the water–oil mixture, it was moved from the bottom flask to a separatory funnel, and the upper layer of kernel oil was collected. The volumes of the obtained essential oils and kernel oils were measured, and the yields were calculated. Screening of enzymes

Different enzymes hydrolyze specific chemical bonds in cell wall components, making the type of enzyme a critical factor to evaluate. The choice of enzyme depends on the structure of the plant material and the composition of its cell walls. Essential oils are typically found in specialized cells or glands and can vary by plant species. Kernel oils are primarily stored in the endosperm or cotyledons of the fruit, which are surrounded by a protein layer. Dense starch granules may also interfere with mechanical or solvent extraction processes, reducing efficiency. Therefore, this study screened various enzymes, including cellulase, hemicellulase, pectinase, α-amylase, proteases, and a mixed enzyme (hemicellulose:pectinase:protease at a 1:1:1 ratio, w/w/w). Another crucial factor influencing yield is the enzyme concentration. To optimize the yield of L. cubeba kernel oil and essential oil, different concentrations were tested: 0, 0.5 %, 1.0 %, 1.5 %, 2.0 %, 2.5 %, 3.0 %, 3.5 %, and 4.0 % (w/v), with the aim of identifying the optimal enzyme concentration.

Box–Behnken design (BBD)

After screening with the PBD, the significant factors were further optimized via a BBD to predict the optimal yield of L. cubeba kernel oil and essential oil through HCUEPM. To achieve higher yields, the three key independent variables identified through the PBD model were further studied via a BBD model with three factors at three levels.

The selected factor ranges were X1: 40–60 °C, X2: 6–8 mL/g, and X3: 1–3 h. The BBD was fitted via a second-order polynomial model, as shown in Equation (4):(4)Y=β0+∑i=13βiXi+∑i=13βiiXi2+∑i=12∑j=i+13βijXiXjwhere Y is the predicted response value; Xi and Xj are the coded independent variables; and β0, βi, βii, and βij are the regression coefficients for the intercept, linear terms, quadratic terms, and interaction terms, respectively.

Comparison with conventional methods

To further discuss the impact of different methods on the yield of kernel oil and essential oil, we compared the HCUEPM with conventional homogenization-microwave-assisted hydrodistillation and solvent reflux extraction-hydrodistillation methods.

Homogenization-microwave-assisted hydrodistillation (HMHD)

One hundred grams of L. cubeba fruit sample was weighed into a homogenizer, 600 mL of deionized water was added, and the homogenizer was set to 10,000 r/min. After homogenization for 5 min, the mixture was transferred to a flask, which was placed in a microwave oven, and the Clevenger hydrodistillation apparatus was connected. Under a microwave power of 540 W, microwave-assisted hydrodistillation was performed for 40 min. After the reaction was complete, the volume of essential oil was measured, the yield was calculated, and the essential oil was collected after cooling. The collected essential oils were stored in a refrigerator at 4 ± 2 °C in the dark.

Solvent reflux extraction-hydrodistillation method (SREHD)

One hundred grams of each L. cubeba fruit sample was weighed, ground into a fine powder via a grinder, and placed into a round-bottom flask. Next, 500 mL of n-hexane was added, the flask containing the material was placed in a heating mantle, and a reflux condenser was connected. After reacting at 85 °C for 240 min, the mixture was removed, and the mixture was cooled to room temperature. The mixture was filtered, the filter cake was transferred back into a round-bottom flask, another 500 mL of n-hexane was added, and the reflux extraction was repeated under the same conditions for 60 min. After cooling and filtering, the n-hexane layers obtained from both extractions were combined. Using a rotary evaporator, the n-hexane was recovered under reduced pressure at 60 °C and −0.09 MPa until a small amount of liquid remained in the flask. The water bath temperature of the rotary evaporator was increased to 95 °C, and evaporation was continued at −0.09 MPa for 30 min to obtain a mixture of L. cubeba kernel oil and essential oil. Then, the prepared mixture of L. cubeba kernel oil and essential oil along with 200 mL of deionized water was added to a flask, which was placed in a heating mantle, and the Clevenger apparatus was connected. Under a heating power of 450 W, hydrodistillation was performed for 120 min. After the reaction was complete, the volume of essential oil was measured, the yield was calculated, and the essential oil was collected after cooling. The collected essential oils were stored in a refrigerator at 4 ± 2 °C in the dark. The remaining liquid in the round-bottom flask was transferred to a separatory funnel, allowed to settle and separate, and then the layers were separated. The upper layer contains L. cubeba kernel oil. The volume of kernel oil was measured, the yield was calculated, and the mixture was stored in a refrigerator at 4 ± 2 °C protected from light.

GC–MS analysis of the main components of the kernel and essential oils

The composition of L. cubeba essential oil

The chemical composition of the obtained essential oil was analyzed via GC–MS. The analysis was conducted with an Agilent gas chromatograph (model 7890 A) − mass spectrometer (model 7000B) equipped with an Rtx–5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). The detection conditions are based on the method proposed by Wang et al. [26]. Before injection, the essential oil samples were diluted 100 times with n-hexane, dried with an appropriate amount of anhydrous sodium sulfate for 24 h, and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter membrane. The GC–MS testing conditions were as follows: injection volume of 1 µL; nitrogen carrier gas flow rate of 1.0 mL/min; and injector temperature of 250 °C. The initial column temperature was maintained at 50 °C for 5 min, then increased at a rate of 5 °C/min to 230 °C and held for 5 min, followed by an increase at 10 °C/min to 250 °C. The electron ionization energy was set at 70 eV, and the mass range scanned was 50–500 m/z. The results were compared with data from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) library and identified via the Kovats index.

The composition of L. cubeba kernel oil

Before the GC–MS analysis of L. cubeba kernel oil, a methylation process was required to improve its chromatographic behavior. The following procedure was followed: 200 mg of kernel oil was accurately weighed and transferred to a round-bottom flask. A 0.6 mol/L sodium hydroxide-methanol solution was prepared, 5 mL of this mixture was mixed with the kernel oil, and the mixture was heated in a 60 °C water bath for 30 min. After the reaction, the mixture was allowed to cool, 5 mL of n-hexane was added, and the mixture was mixed thoroughly. The mixture was allowed to stand for 5 min, 10 mL of deionized water was added, and the mixture was transferred to a separatory funnel. The mixture was allowed to stand for 10 min to achieve phase separation. The upper layer was retained, dried with an appropriate amount of anhydrous sodium sulfate for 24 h, and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter membrane before GC–MS analysis. The detection conditions were based on the method proposed by Zhao et al. [27]. The same instrument used for essential oil analysis was used, with the carrier gas helium (He) set to a flow rate of 1 mL/min and a split ratio of 10:1. The initial column temperature was set at 210 °C, held for 1 min, and then increased at a rate of 5 °C/min to 230 °C. The mass spectrometer interface delay was set to 4.5 min. The mass spectrometry conditions were as follows: interface temperature of 250 °C, electron impact ion source with an ion source temperature of 230 °C, electron energy of 70 eV, and scanning mass range of 30–450 amu.

Results and discussion

Effect of enzyme type and concentration

As shown in Fig. 2a, among the eight enzymes tested, hemicellulase and pectinase were more effective at increasing the yield of kernel oil from L. cubeba fruit samples than the other enzymes were. The effectiveness of cellulase treatment was relatively modest. Unlike the cell walls of other plant parts, such as stems and leaves, the cell walls of fruits have relatively lower cellulose contents. Fruits serve as storage sites for nutrients that support seed germination. During germination, the seeds swell, and pectin and hemicellulose provide structural support for this expansion. Thus, the pectin content in fruit cell walls is relatively high. The presence of pectin helps maintain the rigidity and stability of the cell wall, increasing the difficulty of accessing the cellular contents. Pectin forms a gel-like matrix that encapsulates intracellular components, acting as a physical barrier to oil extraction. Furthermore, pectin interacts with other cell wall components, such as hemicellulose, to form a complex network that can further hinder the release of oils. Treatment with pectinase can weaken this pectin network, which is crucial for extracting oils from L. cubeba fruits. As depicted in Fig. 2a, the yield of kernel oil was significantly greater when pectinase and hemicellulase were used than when other enzymes were used. Pectinase was slightly more effective than hemicellulase. The kernel oil yield clearly increased noticeably as the enzyme concentration increased from 0 to 3.0 %. However, further increases in the enzyme concentration did not result in a significant improvement in oil yield. This may be because the substrate is gradually depleted during the enzymatic reaction. Therefore, a pectinase concentration of 3 % is sufficient to achieve satisfactory kernel oil yield.Fig. 2Effects of enzyme type and concentration on yields of Litsea cubeba kernel oil (a) and essential oil (b). As shown in Fig. 2b, similar to the results in Fig. 2a, the use of hemicellulase and pectinase yielded more essential oils than other types of enzymes did. This occurred because pectinase and hemicellulase effectively loosen the matrix of the cell walls in L. cubeba fruits, thereby enhancing the permeability of the cell wall structure. As the enzyme concentration increased from 0 to 3.0 %, the yield of essential oil gradually increased. However, further increases in enzyme concentration did not lead to a significant increase in oil yield. Unlike the impact of different enzymes on kernel oil yield, the essential oil yield remained similar across all eight enzyme systems when the enzyme concentration increased from 3.0 % to 4.0 %. This may be because the essential oil in L. cubeba fruits was already thoroughly released at lower enzyme concentrations. In general, mixed enzyme systems are more effective at extracting essential and kernel oils from plant cells [16]. However, in this study, the effectiveness of the mixed enzyme treatment was not as pronounced. This may be due to the specific structure of the cell walls in L. cubeba fruits, which have relatively high proportions of pectin and hemicellulose. The mixed enzyme system had a relatively low concentration of the more effective pectinase, which was insufficient to break down the cell wall structure effectively, making the cellular contents less accessible. Additionally, the preparation of mixed enzymes can be complex and less effective, so pectinase was chosen for subsequent use in the HCUEPM method. To avoid resource waste and ensure a high yield of kernel oil and essential oil, a 3 % enzyme concentration was selected as the optimal concentration. Under these conditions, the yield of kernel oil from L. cubeba fruits was 204.23 ± 8.78 mL/kg DW, and the yield of essential oil was 58.19 ± 2.79 mL/kg DW.

The experimental results indicate that pectinase has high activity within a suitable pH range. Increasing the stirring speed can increase the collision frequency between the enzyme mixture and the particles, effectively breaking down the cell walls and releasing more oil. A longer homogenization time allows the enzymes to act more fully on the cell structures, but excessively long homogenization increases energy consumption. Adjusting the duty cycle appropriately can affect the duration of cavitation; however, further increases beyond a certain point do not significantly improve the yields of either oil. Ultrasound irradiation power generally enhances the extraction yield because its cavitation effect can better breakdown cell walls and improve mass transfer [28].

In the process of simultaneously extracting kernel oil and essential oil from L. cubeba fruits, it is important to identify factors that significantly affect the yields of both oils. On the basis of the results of the enzyme screening process, PBD experiment, and the need for low energy consumption, the following conditions were selected: 3 % pectinase concentration, a stirring speed of 1000 r/min, pH of 5, homogenization time of 4 min, duty cycle of 20 %, and ultrasound irradiation power of 400 W. The factors identified as having the most significant impact—incubation temperature, incubation time, and liquid–solid ratio—will be further optimized via a BBD.

Analysis of the response contours

Three-dimensional response surface plots can visualize complex data, enhancing our understanding of the interactions between variables and clearly illustrating how two independent variables interact to influence a dependent variable. This makes it easier to analyze and determine the optimal conditions. Therefore, we selected the significant interaction terms—incubation temperature (X1) and incubation time (X3)—as the x- and y-axes, with the liquid-to-solid ratio fixed at 8 mL/g. The yields of kernel oil and essential oil were plotted on the z-axis, as shown in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Effects of incubation temperature and incubation time on yields of Litsea cubeba kernel oil (a) and essential oil (b).

Effects of incubation temperature and incubation time on yields of Litsea cubeba kernel oil (a) and essential oil (b).

The interaction effect of incubation temperature and incubation time on the yield of kernel oil is depicted in Fig. 4a. As the incubation temperature increased, the kernel oil yield initially increased but then decreased. This is because when the temperature exceeds the optimal level for the enzyme, the hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds that maintain the enzyme's three-dimensional structure are disrupted, leading to changes in the protein structure and partial loss of enzyme function [29]. At lower temperatures, molecular movement slows, and the formation rate of the enzyme-substrate complex decreases, resulting in a slower enzymatic reaction [30] and thus a lower kernel oil yield. As the incubation time increased, the yield of kernel oil gradually increased and then plateaued. This trend occurred because the HCUEPM requires a certain amount of time to allow the enzymes to act on the substrate, fully breaking down the cell wall structure and releasing more kernel oil.

The interaction effect of the incubation temperature and incubation time on the yield of essential oil is shown in Fig. 4b. Unlike the kernel oil yield, the essential oil yield increased as the incubation temperature increased and then slightly decreased after it stabilized. This difference may be because kernel oil is stored mainly in the endosperm or cotyledons of the fruit, where more pectin is present. Pectinase is more effective in breaking down the plant cell structure in the endosperm; therefore, when the incubation temperature becomes too high and the enzyme activity decreases, it has a greater effect on the yield of kernel oil.

As the incubation time increased, the yield of essential oil also gradually increased, followed by stabilization. The optimal conditions predicted by the BBD response surface are an incubation temperature of 52.78 °C, a liquid-to-solid ratio of 9.31 mL/g, and an incubation time of 2.53 h. Under these conditions, the predicted yields are 241.68 mL/kg DW for kernel oil and 65.80 mL/kg DW for essential oil.

Adequacy analysis of the model

The adequacy of the model can be diagnosed by evaluating the predictive accuracy and analyzing the residuals. Fig. 5 presents the adequacy analysis of the models for kernel oil and essential oil. Fig. 5a and d show the normal probability plots of the residuals. The residuals for both the kernel oil and essential oil models roughly align along a straight line, indicating a normal distribution. This suggests that the model's assumptions are valid and that there are no significant outliers, demonstrating that the model effectively explains all the data points. As shown in Fig. 5b and e, the actual values for both the kernel oil and essential oil are mostly evenly distributed around the straight line, indicating that the model can accurately predict responses under new experimental conditions within the studied range. The model's predictions are thus accurate. The residual plots by data order, shown in Fig. 5c and f, indicate that all the data points fall within the ± 3 range, with no apparent patterns or trends, indicating a random distribution. This randomness suggests that the model accounts for most of the variability in the data. Model validation

Through the screening of enzyme systems, PBD model selection, and BBD model optimization, the optimal conditions for the HCUEPM of kernel oil and essential oil from L. cubeba fruits were determined. These optimal conditions include the use of 3 % pectinase, a stirring speed of 1000 r/min, a pH of 5, a time of 4 min, a duty cycle of 20 %, an ultrasonication power of 400 W, an incubation temperature of 52.78 °C, a ratio of 9.31 mL/g, and an incubation time of 2.53 h. To verify the effectiveness of the optimized conditions, three repeated experiments were conducted under slightly modified conditions for practicality: 3 % pectinase, a stirring speed of 1000 r/min, a pH of 5, a homogenization time of 4 min, a duty cycle of 20 %, an ultrasonication power of 400 W, an incubation temperature of 53 °C, a liquid–solid ratio of 9.3 mL/g, and an incubation time of 2.5 h. The actual yield of kernel oil obtained was 240.56 ± 11.07 mL/kg DW, and the actual yield of essential oil was 64.89 ± 3.1 mL/kg DW. The theoretical predicted yields were 241.68 mL/kg DW for kernel oil and 65.80 mL/kg DW for essential oil. The close match between the actual yields and the predicted yields further confirms the reliability of the model.

Comparison of different methods

The yields of kernel oil and essential oil obtained via the HCUEPM were compared with those obtained via traditional HMHD and SREHD, as shown in Table 5. The results indicate that the essential oil yield from HMHD was 65.63 ± 3.20 mL/kg DW, which was slightly greater than the yield from HCUEPM, but the difference was not statistically significant according to one-way analysis of variance. The HCUEPM method inevitably produces an oil–water emulsion, leading to some losses even after demulsification. HMHD can increase the essential oil extraction yield because microwave energy rapidly affects polar molecules, such as water molecules inside plant cell walls, resulting in quick vaporization [31]. However, HMHD cannot simultaneously obtain kernel oil. The essential oil yield obtained via SREHD was 62.36 ± 3.0 mL/kg DW, and the kernel oil yield was 241.48 ± 10.6 mL/kg DW, which was not significantly different from that of the HCUEPM method in terms of the kernel oil yield. The slightly lower essential oil yield in SREHD than in HCUEPM may be due to losses during solvent recovery, which requires high-temperature vacuum distillation. Although the SREHD method can simultaneously extract both kernel oil and essential oil, it is time-consuming and energy-intensive. Conclusions

Homogenization-circulating ultrasound combined with aqueous enzymatic pretreatment and microwave-assisted extraction (HCUEPM) was successfully applied to extract kernel oil and essential oil from L. cubeba fruits. After various enzymes and concentrations, including cellulase, hemicellulase, pectinase, neutral protease, acid protease, alkaline protease, and α-amylase, a 3 % pectinase aqueous solution was selected as the extraction solvent. Using PBD, significant factors affecting the yields of kernel oil and essential oil—such as the incubation temperature, incubation time, and liquid-to-solid ratio—were identified among many variables, including pH, stirring speed, homogenization time, duty cycle, and ultrasound irradiation power. These significant factors were further optimized via a BBD. The optimal conditions were a stirring speed of 1000 r/min, a pH of 5, a homogenization time of 4 min, a duty cycle of 20 %, an ultrasound irradiation power of 400 W, an incubation temperature of 52.78 °C, a liquid–solid ratio of 9.31 mL/g, and an incubation time of 2.53 h. Under these optimized conditions, the HCUEPM method achieved a yield of 240.56 ± 11.07 mL/kg DW for kernel oil and 64.89 ± 3.10 mL/kg DW for essential oil. Compared with other methods, such as HMHD and SREHD, the HCUEPM method in this study efficiently produced greater yields of kernel oil and essential oil from L. cubeba fruits in a shorter time. GC–MS analysis of the kernel oil and essential oil obtained via different methods revealed that their compositions were similar across the methods. The main components of the essential oil were D-limonene, trans-citral, cis-citral, and citronellal. The primary components of the kernel oil were MCFAs, specifically C10 and C12. These findings lay a foundation for the potential future application of L. cubeba kernel oil and essential oils in the field of human health.
