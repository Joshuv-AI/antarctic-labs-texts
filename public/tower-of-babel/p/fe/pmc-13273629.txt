“Doctor, if it were you, which would you choose?”: Navigating personal preference questions in anti‐amyloid immunotherapy selection
Authors: Eric Dinnerstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe approval of lecanemab and donanemab represents a watershed moment in Alzheimer Disease (AD) treatment, yet clinicians face a novel challenge: guiding patients through treatment selection when head‐to‐head comparative data are absent. When confronted with the question “What would you do if you were me?”, clinical providers must balance evidence‐based medicine with the therapeutic alliance. This perspective examines the ethical, practical, and relational dimensions of responding to personal preference queries in the context of anti‐amyloid immunotherapy selection, offering a framework for authentic engagement that honors both professional boundaries and patient‐centered care.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease (AD), anti‐amyloid immunotherapy, clinical equipoise, donanemab, lecanemab, medical ethics, patient‐centered care, physician‐patient relationship, shared decision‐making, treatment selection

BACKGROUND

The landscape of AD treatment fundamentally shifted with the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approvals of lecanemab (July 2023) and donanemab (July 2024), marking the first disease‐modifying therapies targeting a core pathological feature of AD. 1 , 2 For the first time, geriatricians, neurologists, geriatric psychiatrists, and other dementia specialists can offer patients more than symptomatic management—yet this therapeutic advancement introduces a paradox of choice. After a comprehensive evaluation confirms candidacy for anti‐amyloid immunotherapy, patients and families frequently arrive at an impasse: lecanemab or donanemab?

The question “Doctor, if it were you, which would you choose?” emerges predictably in this decision‐making crucible. This deceptively simple query reflects the patient's desire for authentic guidance, yet it challenges fundamental principles of medical decision‐making. How should clinicians respond when evidence‐based medicine cannot definitively answer which therapy is “better,” institutional factors may limit options, and personal values inevitably color clinical judgment?

This perspective explores the multifaceted dimensions of this question, drawing from clinical ethics, shared decision‐making literature, and practical experience in memory clinics navigating this new therapeutic era.

THE CLINICAL CONTEXT: WHY THE QUESTION ARISES

Absence of direct comparative data

The CLARITY‐AD and TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ 2 trials, while both demonstrating efficacy on the Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB), enrolled different populations and employed distinct protocols. 3 , 4 CLARITY‐AD showed 27% slowing of clinical decline over 18 months with lecanemab, while TRAILBLAZER demonstrated 35% slowing at 18 months with donanemab. However, these percentages are not directly comparable: differences in placebo group decline rates, baseline characteristics, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 carrier proportions, and outcome measurement timing preclude definitive superiority claims for either agent.

Practical differences that matter to patients

Despite similar mechanisms of action, meaningful differences exist:

Administration schedules: Lecanemab requires biweekly infusions indefinitely, though the 2025 introduction of subcutaneous dosing options has significantly improved convenience for many patients; donanemab involves monthly infusions with a finite treatment duration based on amyloid clearance (median ∼18 months).

ARIA rates: Amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities with edema or effusion (ARIA‐E) occurred in 12.6% with lecanemab versus 24.0% with donanemab. Symptomatic ARIA‐E rates were 2.8% versus 6.1%, respectively. 3 , 4 ARIA‐H (microhemorrhages/hemosiderosis) demonstrated similar differential patterns.

Treatment discontinuation strategy: Donanemab uniquely incorporates a stopping rule based on achieving a low amyloid burden on PET imaging. Recent 3‐year data suggest that for many patients, discontinuation at 18 months remains viable without immediate rebound of amyloid accumulation, though long‐term monitoring remains essential. In contrast, lecanemab treatment duration remains less defined in standard protocols.

Real‐world availability: Insurance coverage, infusion center capacity, and institutional formulary decisions may effectively remove choice from many patients.

The uncertainty patients cannot resolve alone

After clinicians present comparative data—efficacy metrics, safety profiles, and logistical considerations—many patients remain unable to choose. It is important to acknowledge that while the choice between agents is difficult, the initial decision of whether to initiate anti‐amyloid immunotherapy at all—particularly for patients who are homozygous for APOE ε4, over age 80, or have significant co‐pathologies—is often far more complex. The medical facts do not answer the deeper question: “What should I do?” This uncertainty stems from the values‐laden nature of the decision. Risk tolerance, treatment burden acceptance, and personal experiences all influence this “to treat or not” framework in ways that transcend trial data.

Understanding the question subtext enables more responsive engagement.

Common pitfalls: The “data‐only” response

A common pitfall in responding to personal preference queries is the “data‐only” deflection. In this scenario, a clinician responds to the “What would you do?” question by simply reiterating clinical trial percentages or safety statistics. While intended to maintain professional neutrality and preserve autonomy, this approach often inadvertently leads to patient abandonment at a moment of high decisional conflict. When patients seek an expert's gestalt or relational validation, a purely informational response can leave them feeling unheard and medically isolated in their vulnerability.

Precedents from other specialties

The “what would you do?” question pervades medicine. In obstetrics, patients facing decisions about prenatal testing or delivery mode frequently pose this query. 5 In oncology, treatment pathway decisions routinely prompt patients to seek their oncologist's personal choice. 6 In psychiatry, medication selection among multiple evidence‐based options generates similar requests. 7

Research across specialties reveals a consistent pattern: physicians often deflect these questions by reiterating medical information, providing additional data, or redirecting focus to patient values exploration. 5 , 8 While these approaches have merit, they may leave patients feeling abandoned at the moment they most seek guidance. Conversely, some physicians readily share personal preferences, potentially inappropriately influencing patients whose values differ from their own. 9

Ethical considerations

Several ethical principles come into tension:

Autonomy: Patients have the right to make decisions aligned with their values, free from undue influence—yet autonomy is exercised more meaningfully with trusted guidance than in informational isolation.

Beneficence: The physician's duty to act in the patient's best interest might argue for either sharing or withholding personal preferences, depending on individual circumstances.

Non‐maleficence: Could physician disclosure cause harm by overshadowing patient values? Alternatively, does refusing to answer abandon patients in vulnerable moments?

Authenticity and trust: The therapeutic alliance depends on authentic engagement. Scripted deflections may undermine the relational foundation essential for longitudinal Alzheimer care.

FRAMEWORK FOR RESPONSE: ENGAGING AUTHENTICALLY

Rather than prescribing a single “correct” response, neurologists can develop a flexible framework adaptable to individual patients and circumstances.

Clarify the question

Before answering, ensure understanding of what is being asked:

“Let me clarify your question. Are you asking which treatment is better for you specifically? or which treatment would I choose for myself? You understand that I cannot definitively answer which treatment is better for you—we are different people with different values and circumstances. But I can share how I think about the decision, if that would be helpful.”

This clarification accomplishes several goals: it acknowledges the question's legitimacy, explains why a simple answer may not exist, and offers to engage authentically while maintaining appropriate boundaries.

Acknowledge legitimate equipoise

When uncertainty exists, name it:

“These are both effective, scientifically validated treatments. I do not have a clear medical recommendation that one is definitively better than the other for you. That is actually why your values and preferences are so important in this decision.”

Acknowledging equipoise validates the patient's struggle and frames the decision appropriately as values‐based rather than purely medical.

Share decision‐making frameworks, not just decisions

Rather than stating a preference, explain how you approach such decisions:

“When I make important medical decisions—for myself or my family—I focus on a few key questions: How much does treatment burden matter relative to potential benefit? How do I feel about calculated risks versus conservative approaches? What does my day‐to‐day life look like, and how would treatment fit into that? Your answers to these questions might differ from mine, and that is completely appropriate.”

Offer preference‐sensitive considerations with personal context

This approach involves sharing personal perspectives on specific factors while explicitly noting these as individual preferences:

On treatment frequency and duration: “Personally, I do not mind needles or frequent medical appointments—that is just how I am wired. For me, the difference between every 2 weeks versus every month would not be a major factor. But I know many people feel very differently about this. Some patients have told me they appreciate the finite treatment course with donanemab because there is an endpoint. Others prefer the established routine of continuing lecanemab. Both perspectives make sense.”

On risk–benefit trade‐offs: “There are two reasonable schools of thought here. One is to be more conservative, choosing a treatment that may be slightly less potent but may carry a somewhat lower risk of ARIA. The other is to accept a calculated risk for potentially greater efficacy. Personally, I tend to be [more/less] conservative with medical decisions for myself and my family. But that is my personality, shaped by my experiences. Your calculus might reasonably differ.”

On novelty versus established use: “Lecanemab has been in clinical use since 2023; donanemab since 2024. Personally, having an extra year of real‐world safety data would/would not be an important factor for me in this decision. Some people feel more comfortable with the earliest available option; others prefer waiting for additional experience. Both instincts are valid.”

On decision‐making process: “When facing complex medical decisions, I gather multiple trusted opinions, read the primary sources, and synthesize different perspectives. I am a data‐driven person. But I also know people who trust their gut instinct or defer to a single expert they trust completely. If you are asking how I would approach the decision process itself, I would probably [describe your genuine approach]—but you might make excellent decisions very differently.”

Distinguish professional recommendations from personal preferences

When you do have a clear medical recommendation based on individual patient factors, state it directly:

“Based on your particular situation—especially your [APOE ε4 status, prior microhemorrhages, schedule constraints, etc.]—I actually do have a medical recommendation, not just a personal preference. I think [option] makes more medical sense for you specifically because [evidence‐based reasoning]. This is different from what I might choose for myself if our situations were different.”

This distinction honors both evidence‐based practice and personal authenticity.

Validate multiple approaches to the decision

Acknowledge that reasonable people—including reasonable physicians—might choose differently:

“I have had colleagues choose differently than I might, for good reasons. Dr. [X] prioritizes minimizing ARIA risk above all else. Dr. [Y] values the finite treatment course highly. We are all competent neurologists looking at the same data and arriving at different personal preferences based on our values. That is actually reassuring—it means there is not a wrong answer here, just different right answers for different people.”

Affirm the patient decisional authority

Conclude by empowering the patient:

“Ultimately, you are the expert on your life, your values, and what matters most to you. I can provide medical information and share how I think about these issues, but you are the one who has to live with the treatment. Whatever you decide, I will support that decision and help make it work as well as possible.”

SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS IN AD

Cognitive impairment and shared decision‐making

Early‐stage AD presents unique challenges. While patients typically retain decisional capacity for treatment choices at the stage where they qualify for anti‐amyloid therapy, mild cognitive impairment may affect their ability to weigh complex information or manage decisional uncertainty. 10 The “what would you do?” question may partly represent cognitive offloading—seeking to reduce the cognitive burden of decision‐making.

Neurologists should:

Assess decision‐making capacity explicitly

Involve care partners while preserving patient autonomy where capacity exists

Recognize when the question reflects decision fatigue rather than information needs

Consider whether breaking the decision into smaller components might help

The longitudinal relationship

Unlike one‐time procedural decisions, Alzheimer treatment involves an extended relationship. The neurologist will monitor for ARIA, adjust infusion schedules, manage adverse effects, and potentially transition to other therapies as the disease progresses. This longitudinal engagement strengthens the case for authentic relational engagement when patients ask personal questions. The therapeutic alliance built through honest, boundary based disclosure may enhance provider adherence, monitoring compliance, and trust during inevitable treatment challenges.

Family dynamics and surrogate decision‐making

Often, the question comes from a care partner rather than the patient: “If this were your mother, what would you choose?” This variant introduces additional complexity:

“I appreciate you asking, and I understand you are trying to do right by your [family member]. If this were my mother, I would want to know what mattered most to her—her tolerance for medical interventions, her feelings about risk, her priorities for these years. I would try hard to choose what she would want, not what I would want for myself. Can we talk about what you know of her values and preferences?”

This response models surrogate decision‐making best practices while acknowledging the emotional weight of the question.

Authentic engagement does not mean unlimited disclosure. Neurologists should consider withholding personal preferences when:

Strong institutional or practice constraints exist: If your center only offers one therapy, sharing that you would personally prefer the unavailable option serves no constructive purpose and may generate frustration or distrust.

PRACTICAL IMPLEMENTATION

Preparing for the question

Memory clinic teams can prepare by:

Team discussion: Ensure consistency in approach across providers while allowing for individual authentic expression. Discuss as a group how to handle the question.

Personal reflection: Before the question arises, clarify your own thinking. What would you choose for yourself or a family member? Why? What values drive that preference?

Decision aids: Develop structured tools that make values clarification explicit, potentially reducing the frequency of the “what would you do?” question by helping patients answer it themselves. 11

Training scenarios: Role‐play responses to the question in different clinical contexts during team meetings.

Documentation considerations

When you share personal preferences with patients, consider documenting:

“Discussed comparison of lecanemab and donanemab, including efficacy data, ARIA rates, and logistical differences. Patient inquired about the provider's preference. Explained that both treatments are medically appropriate and that personal values should guide selection. Shared that I personally tend toward [more/less] conservative medical approaches, but emphasized this reflects individual values and that either choice is medically sound for this patient situation. Patient will consider options and inform us of the decision at the next visit.”

This documentation demonstrates thoughtful shared decision‐making while creating a record of what was discussed.

INSTITUTIONAL AND SYSTEMIC FACTORS

When choice is illusory

Many patients face constrained choices despite two FDA‐approved options:

‐ Insurance coverage: Some plans cover only one agent or impose differential cost‐sharing

‐ Institutional formularies: Health systems may contract with only one manufacturer

‐ Infusion capacity: Limited infusion suite availability may restrict options

‐ Geographic access: Rural or community practices may not have infrastructure for either or both therapies

When systemic factors eliminate choice, the conversation shifts:

“I understand you are asking what I would choose, but in our health system, we currently only offer lecanemab/donanemab. While I can discuss the theoretical comparison, the practical question is whether you want to pursue treatment with the available option. I can share my thoughts on lecanemab/donanemab specifically and whether I would recommend it given your situation.”

Neurologists can simultaneously work to expand access while helping patients navigate current realities.

Advocating for patient choice

The profession should advocate for:

‐ Insurance coverage of both therapies without preferential positioning

‐ Institutional formularies that include both agents when feasible

‐ Infrastructure development to support both infusion protocols

‐ Head‐to‐head comparative trials to inform future decision‐making

EMERGING EVIDENCE AND FUTURE CONSIDERATIONS

As real‐world evidence accumulates and longer‐term outcomes data emerge, the landscape will evolve. Neurologists should:

‐ Update their own knowledge regularly and acknowledge when new information changes previous thinking

‐ Be transparent with patients when evolving data shifts recommendations

‐ Distinguish between current evidence and anticipated future data

‐ Avoid anchoring to initial preferences when evidence changes

Registry data from the Alzheimer Clinical Trials Consortium and other real‐world evidence sources will likely inform comparative effectiveness assessments that may eventually provide clearer guidance. 12 Until then, navigating uncertainty with authenticity and humility serves patients well.

CONCLUSION

The question “Doctor, if it were you, which would you choose?” represents a pivotal moment in the clinician–patient relationship. Rather than viewing it as an uncomfortable intrusion, geriatricians, neurologists, and other dementia specialists can embrace it as an opportunity for deeper engagement. While the choice between agents is a common focal point, we must recognize that the broader decision of whether to pursue these therapies at all is frequently the more challenging clinical juncture. By applying a shared decision‐making framework to the entire treatment trajectory—acknowledging legitimate uncertainty and offering boundaried personal perspectives—we can honor both patient autonomy and the therapeutic alliance.

In this new era of disease‐modifying Alzheimer therapy, we walk alongside our patients not as omniscient authorities with definitive answers but as expert guides navigating uncertainty together. When we respond to “What would you do?” with authentic, thoughtful engagement—sharing how we think while preserving space for patients to choose according to their own values—we strengthen the collaborative partnership essential for the long journey ahead.

The choice between lecanemab and donanemab may not have a single “right” answer. But how we help patients navigate that choice—with honesty, humility, and authentic presence—can make all the difference.
