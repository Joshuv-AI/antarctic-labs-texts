Long-term survival in severe epidermolysis bullosa simplex with the potentially fatal KRT5 p.Glu477Lys variant
Authors: Tuntas Rayinda, Paranita Ferronika, Amelia Pungky, Nadia Nur Asifa, Retno Danarti
Published: 2026

Introduction

Epidermolysis bullosa (EB) comprises a heterogeneous group of inherited mechanobullous disorders caused by pathogenic variants in genes encoding structural proteins of the epidermis and the dermal-epidermal junction. Epidermolysis bullosa simplex (EBS), the most common subtype, is characterized by intraepidermal blistering resulting from basal keratinocyte fragility.1 Pathogenic variants in KRT5 and KRT14, encoding keratins 5 and 14 respectively, account for the majority of EBS cases.1 Clinical severity ranges from mild, localized blistering to severe, generalized disease presenting at birth, which may be life-threatening.1 Severe phenotypes are frequently associated with mutations affecting highly conserved regions of the keratin rod domain, particularly the helix initiation and termination peptides.2 We report a case of severe generalized EBS caused by a pathogenic KRT5 variant in an underrepresented population, with long-term survival and characteristic pigmentary changes.

Case report

An 18-year-old Javanese male presented with recurrent, lifelong blistering since birth, predominantly in areas subject to minor trauma. During the neonatal period, he experienced generalized severe blistering requiring intensive care but subsequently exhibited a normal growth trajectory. There was no family history of similar conditions, nor any history of consanguinity. On examination, numerous tense bullae and erosions were present over trauma-prone sites. Reticular pigmentation was found primarily on the trunk. Palmar and plantar hyperkeratosis was also observed. Nail dystrophy and contractures involving multiple finger and toe joints were observed (Fig 1).

Routine laboratory investigations were unremarkable. A skin biopsy taken from a lower extremity blister showed an intraepidermal cleft in the basal cell layers, with vacuolar degeneration of basal cells, and varying degrees of pigmentation in the basal layers. Moderate inflammatory infiltration, predominantly composed of lymphocytes, and melanin incontinence were found in the superficial dermis. These findings support the diagnosis of EB simplex (Fig 2).

Following informed consent, whole-exome sequencing was performed on DNA from the patient’s peripheral blood. The result of whole-exome sequencing showed a heterozygous pathogenic variant in KRT5 (NM_000424.4:c.1429G>A, NP_000,415.2:p.Glu477Lys), confirming diagnosis of epidermolysis bullosa simplex (EBS).

Discussion

EBS results from pathogenic variants that compromise the structural integrity of keratin intermediate filaments within basal keratinocytes, rendering the epidermis susceptible to mechanical stress. The clinical spectrum of EBS is broad, ranging from localized blistering limited to hands and feet to severe generalized forms associated with extensive neonatal blistering, mucosal involvement, palmoplantar keratoderma, nail dystrophy, and secondary pigmentary abnormalities.

The identified underlying variant in this case is a guanine-to-adenine substitution at nucleotide 1429 of the KRT5 coding sequence, located within the highly conserved H1 head-tail linker domain. This change results in a p.Glu477Lys amino acid substitution, which has been linked to severe, often lethal, generalized forms of EBS, with reported deaths occurring as early as 8-170 days of life (Table I).2, 3, 4, 5, 6 The glutamic acid at position 477 lies at the end of the helix termination peptide (amino acids 469-477) within the keratin 5 rod domain, a region that is highly conserved across species and important for the proper assembly of keratin intermediate filaments.5,7 Mutations affecting this region, or the corresponding helix initiation peptide in the 1A domain, are responsible for severe EBS.5 Even subtle changes in these short peptide segments can disrupt the coiled-coil structure of keratin dimers, leading to filament instability and skin fragility.5 Furthermore, structural modelling predicts that the p.Glu477Lys substitution induces structural changes and alters the surface charge of the keratin 5/14 heterodimer, thereby severely compromising filament assembly and epidermal resilience.5

Genotype-phenotype correlations for this variant have been reported, showing marked variability in fatality rates among individuals with the p.Glu477Lys variant.2,4,5 In a cohort of 8 affected individuals, death was reported in 5 cases,2 whereas other case series documented only 1 fatality among 6 patients.5 Despite this variation, all neonates in the latter series required intensive care due to severe blistering and aplasia cutis.5 Given the potential severity of this variant, clinicians should remain vigilant and proactively manage risks such as recurrent infections and gastrointestinal or respiratory complications.5 Survivors typically showed gradual improvement in blister frequency with age and developed a characteristic reticulated or mottled pigmentation pattern, often accompanied by palmoplantar hyperkeratosis and nail dystrophy.5 The clinical course of our patient mirrors this trajectory, demonstrating severe disease in early life, stabilization in adolescence, and emergence of pigmentary changes consistent with previous reports.5

The differential diagnosis in this case includes epidermolysis bullosa simplex with mottled pigmentationsuggested by the presence of reticular pigmentation. Epidermolysis bullosa simplex with mottled pigmentation features reticulate macular pigmentation on the neck, upper trunk, and acral sites, often with punctate keratoderma and occasional nail dystrophy, and mild blistering.1 Meanwhile, our patient had predominantly truncal pigmentation and severe neonatal blistering. Epidermolysis bullosa simplex with mottled pigmentation is most often associated with KRT5 variants in the V1 domain, particularly p.Pro25Leu, with some reports involving KRT14.1 Another differential diagnosis for this case is Kindler EB, caused by pathogenic variants in FERMT1, which presents with acral blistering, photosensitivity, and progressive poikiloderma.1 Although poikiloderma may resemble mottled pigmentation seen in this case, true poikiloderma in Kindler EB is defined by hypo- and hyperpigmentation, epidermal atrophy, and telangiectasia.8 Moreover, the presence of multilevel epidermal cleavage on histopathology helps distinguish Kindler EB from other forms of EBS.8

In summary, we report a rare case of severe generalized EBS in an underrepresented population carrying the KRT5 p.Glu477Lys variant. This case is notable for survival into late adolescence, comprehensive long-term phenotypic documentation, and reticulated pigmentation associated with a helix-termination peptide mutation. This case also illustrates how advances in next-generation sequencing have transformed the diagnostic approach to EB, enabling precise molecular classification, clearer genotype-phenotype correlations, and improved prognostication during the critical early period of life.9 Molecular confirmation not only informs anticipatory management and multidisciplinary care planning but is also essential for counselling families regarding recurrence risk and assessing eligibility for emerging molecular therapies, including gene replacement therapy, gene editing, exon skipping, and protein therapy.10 These insights broaden the recognized clinical spectrum of this rare and often lethal variant and underscore the role of molecular diagnostics in guiding diagnosis, prognosis, and long-term management in EB.
