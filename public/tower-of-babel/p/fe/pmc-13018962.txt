Non-coding RNAs in neurodegeneration: an axis-based, evidence-tiered mechanistic synthesis
Authors: Sina Salem Ahim, Mehrdad Bandegi, Erfan Ghanbarzadeh, Roozbeh Heidarzadehpilehrood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Evidence supporting the involvement of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) in neuronal plasticity and susceptibility has been fragmented across different diseases and ncRNA subtypes. We propose an evidence-tiered, axis-based framework integrating microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs) across major neurodegenerative disorders. We have focused on studies that have attempted to establish links between ncRNAs and disease pathogenesis through functional perturbation, validation, and/or rescue in relevant disease models. To facilitate cross-disease comparison, we have categorized these studies along four axes of proteostasis/protein aggregation, neuroinflammation, synaptic/circuit dysfunction, and mitochondrial/autophagy dysfunction. Overall, these ncRNAs have been reported to function as modulators and potential indicators of bioenergetic stress, influencing glial cell function, neuronal survival, metabolic vulnerability, and network plasticity. We have highlighted common regulatory modules, including the role of miRNAs in modulating autophagy and inflammatory-metabolic coupling, lncRNAs in regulating amyloid/synuclein-related processes, and circRNA-mediated miRNA/RBP modules in regulating processes involving mitochondria, while also pointing out discrepancies. We have also discussed the possibility of ncRNA panels and therapeutics, facilitated by advances in delivery strategies, for the discovery of biomarkers and drug targets, while also pointing out some of the challenges that need to be addressed, including heterogeneity of evidence, cell-type specificity, blood–brain barrier constraints, and off-target effects.

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases are increasingly recognized as major contributors to cognitive and motor disability. Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Huntington's disease (HD), and multiple sclerosis (MS) all have different disease patterns and specific pathological markers, yet they also have fundamental biological “axes” [1]. Proteostasis dysfunction is a shared feature across these disorders, resulting in abnormal protein folding, aggregation, and lysosomal accumulation of disease-triggering proteins. These proteins may also exhibit associated proliferation and strain characteristics. [2]. Neuroinflammation and immune imbalance also drive disease courses with intricate glia-neuronal interactions and disease-specific microglial activation profiles [[3], [4], [5]]. Simultaneously, dysfunction and disconnection at the level of synapses have emerged as fundamental and early disease-relevant events, very tightly associated with cognitive and motor dysfunction [6]. Oxidative stress and mitochondrial malfunction are closely associated with energy failure, redox disturbances, and faulty organelle homeostasis [7,8]. These pathological axes are highly interconnected. The autophagy-lysosome and degradative pathways cross-talk these axes as they control aggregate removal, turnover, and inflammation [9,10].

Within this multi-axis paradigm, ncRNAs have increasingly been recognized as regulatory mediators, with particular focus on miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs [11]. These molecules exert regulatory effects through diverse mechanisms, including post-transcriptional repression, chromatin modulation, and molecular scaffolding. MiRNAs, on the other hand, are species of small RNAs with an average length of ∼22 nucleotides [12]. These molecules possess an inhibitory regulatory role on targeted messenger RNAs at a post-transcription stage. The total impact of miRNAs influences a large set of gene expression networks. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have varied nuclear and cytoplasmic localizations. The main functions include transcription regulation and chromatin organization and RNA and protein processing. In addition, they interact with DNA, RNA, and proteins through modular interactions [13,14]. Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are generated from back-splicing events and include covalently closed RNAs that are cell-type and tissue-specific and are involved in RNA-protein interactions, miRNA sequestration, transcription regulation, and translation [15]. At a conceptual level, these three types of ncRNAs can be seen as offering complementary modes of regulation: they fine-tune gene expression, nucleate regulatory complexes, and coordinate interaction networks, and offer stable modules of highly specific regulatory functions, respectively [12,13].

First, an important translationally relevant implication is that ncRNAs can interact on common disease pathways with different entry points. An ncRNA-mediated modulation of ubiquitin-proteasome and autophagy-lysosome pathway components might affect aggregate accumulation and protein synthesis; ncRNA regulation of cytokine pathways and microglia might alter neuroinflammatory set-points; ncRNA modulation of synaptic gene functions might affect plasticity and excitability; and ncRNA modulation of mitochon-drial and antioxidant pathway functions might affect bioenergetic and redox stability [5,10]. Since these processes appear conserved across different disorders, cross-disorder synthesis might be useful for examining recurrent ncRNA 'hubs' and combining disease-specific with common ncRNA signatures [16]. At the same time, there has been interest in ncRNAs as biofluid biomarkers because they have regulatory selectivity and because it would be feasible to develop multiplex biosensors [2,3,5].

Unlike prior reviews that examine individual ncRNA classes or single neurodegenerative disorders in isolation, this review integrates miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs across major pathogenic axes shared by Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and multiple sclerosis. Importantly, we introduce an evidence-tier framework to distinguish correlative expression changes from mechanistic and in vivo functional validation, thereby differentiating putative biomarkers from plausible pathogenic drivers.

This cross-disease, cross-RNA-class, and mechanistically graded synthesis provides a structured foundation for prioritizing ncRNA targets for translational development. Literature basis and synthesis approach

This review is a narrative, mechanism-centered synthesis focused on experimentally supported ncRNA interactions reported in AD, PD, ALS, HD, and MS. We organized the discussion around four disease-relevant axes (proteostasis/protein aggregation; neuroinflammation; synaptic/circuit dysfunction; mitochondrial/autophagy impairment) to enable cross-disorder comparison and to highlight convergent regulatory hubs and recurring therapeutic constraints. Where available, priority was given to studies providing functional perturbation and mechanistic linkage (e.g., loss/gain-of-function with downstream target validation), while expression-only observations were interpreted cautiously.

Evidence grading framework

To enhance interpretability and maintain consistency with our stated prioritization of mechanistic studies, we implemented a qualitative evidence-tier framework to categorize the strength of experimental support underlying each ncRNA–axis association. Studies were classified as follows: Tier 1: In vivo perturbation with phenotypic rescue and direct target validation. Tier 2: In vitro perturbation with mechanistic validation (e.g., luciferase reporter assays, RNA pull-down, RIP, or functional rescue). Tier 3: Expression profiling with validated direct target interaction but without functional perturbation. Tier 4: Expression-only association studies without mechanistic validation. This framework was applied to synthesize the literature while acknowledging that evidence strength varies substantially across ncRNA classes and disease contexts.

Mechanisms of action and functions of ncRNA

miRNAs

miRNAs are small (∼22-nt) ncRNAs that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally and function as guides for Argonaute-proteins containing effector complexes targeting specific transcripts, thus influencing pathway-level outputs rather than single-gene modulation [12]. Noting that miRNA annotation and quantification rely on database releases as well as counts on either precursors or mature molecules, quantitative statements regarding miRNA numbers should therefore be interpreted in the context of specific database releases, with miRBase v22, for instance, annotating 1917 human hairpin precursors and 2654 mature sequences [17]. Canonical miRNA biogenesis begins with transcription, usually mediated by RNA Polymerase II, of primary miRNAs (pri-miRNAs). Subsequent nuclear processing via the Drosha-DGCR8 microprocessor produces precursor miRNAs (pre-miRNAs). The pre-miRNAs are then transported into the cytoplasm by Exportin-5 and processed into a miRNA duplex. Subsequently, a preferred strand of the miRNA duplex is loaded onto Argonaute to form an RNA-induced silencing complex, commonly referred to as miRISC [18]. miRISC usually uses seed targeting by nucleotides 2-8 with preferred binding to 3′ untranslated regions and contributes significantly to miRNA-directed translational repression and rapid target mRNA degradation mediated by deadenylation and subsequent decapping and 5' → 3′ exonucleolytic degradation [19,20].

From a neurobiology perspective, miRNAs are recognized as having cell- and region-specific expression and functions related to neuronal differentiation, synaptic plasticity, and activity-dependent gene regulation, making miRNA dysfunction uniquely interesting with regard to circuit resilience and repair across neurodegenerative disease [[21], [22]]. While 3′ UTR associations are most common, associations with 5′ UTRs and coding regions have also been shown with relevance to miRNA dysfunction [23]. A prime example here would be miR-10a, associating with 5′ UTRs of mRNAs encoding ribosomal proteins and shown capable of promoting translation under given conditions [24,25]. Aside from intracellular repression, it would appear that extracellular miRNAs have roles in intercellular communication as vesicle-associated or protein-bound molecules, consistent with a hormone-like role in certain contexts [26]. Additionally, some extracellular miRNAs have been shown to serve as ligands for innate immune receptors, including TLR7/8, thus underpinning a link between miRNA secretion and an inflammatory loop phenomenon [27,28]. Finally, non-canonical biogenesis routes-including intron-derived “mirtrons” and pathways that bypass Drosha or Dicer-have been described and should be considered when biogenesis perturbations are inferred from disease models [18]. An additional emerging concept highlighted in Fig. 1 is that pri-miRNA loci may encode short miRNA-encoded peptides (miPEPs). These were initially established in plants and are increasingly explored in animals; however, evidence for consistent functional feedback regulation in humans remains limited and appears context-dependent rather than generalizable, warranting cautious framing [29].

Collectively, these canonical and non-canonical properties (Fig. 1A–E) provide the mechanistic foundation for interpreting miRNA dysregulation across the shared neurodegeneration axes mapped in subsequent sections.

LncRNAs

LncRNAs are a heterogeneous class of molecules that were initially delineated from protein-coding genes based on transcript size, generally greater than 200 nucleotides, and the apparent absence of protein-coding potential [30]. Though such an operational definition has gained considerable traction, it has also become increasingly evident that such a definition does not capture the biological heterogeneity of lncRNAs adequately. In fact, new lines of evidence suggest that lncRNAs may also contain small open reading frames that are potentially capable of encoding functional micropeptides, thus obscuring the distinction between protein-coding and non-coding RNAs [14,30]. Therefore, such delineation of lncRNAs on the basis of transcript size or protein-coding potential is considered insufficient. A more biologically informative definition of lncRNAs would be as molecules that are generally considered to be regulatory in nature, whose functions are dependent on their genomic, transcriptional, processing, and kinetic characteristics, as well as their subcellular localization and selective interactions with DNA, RNA, and protein targets [13].

Within this framework, several distinct modes of action of lncRNAs have been identified. These include the ability of lncRNAs to act as guides to recruit regulatory factors to target chromatin regions or RNA molecules; to act as scaffolding molecules to facilitate the assembly of multi-component ribonucleoprotein complexes; to act as decoys to regulate cellular processes by sequestering transcription factors, RNA-binding proteins, and miRNAs; and to act as signaling molecules to report on cellular status and facilitate rapid and context-dependent reorganization of gene regulatory networks [13,14,31]. Given the common usage of the terms “cis-” and “trans-” to describe lncRNAs, it is important to note the distinction between transcript-dependent and locus-dependent effects. Elegant studies have shown that local regulation of certain loci may be mediated by the promoter, the process of transcription itself, and/or co-transcriptional splicing, rather than the lncRNA transcript itself-a point of interpretation that has important implications for the causal effects observed in knockdown and overexpression studies [32]. By contrast, trans-acting lncRNAs have also been shown to function over distance via RNA-protein interactions, nuclear and cytoplasmic localization, and effects on mRNA stability and translation in a manner consistent with regulatory wiring rather than single pathway modulation [13,14].

The biogenesis of lncRNAs is mediated by RNA polymerase II and is characterized by mRNA-like features. These include 5′ capping, splicing, and polyadenylation [33]. However, comparisons suggest that lncRNAs can be characterized by unique transcription dynamics and RNA processing features that might affect nuclear retention, turnover, and function [34]. The subcellular localization of lncRNA is another critical factor. While nuclear or chromatin-associated lncRNAs can be linked to transcription regulation and nuclear body biology, cytoplasmic lncRNAs can be linked to translation, signaling pathways, and RNA/RBP complex organization [35]. Recently, another dimension has emerged that is becoming increasingly important: the ability of RNA to organize biomolecular condensates and phase-separated structures. This might provide a mechanistic link between lncRNA, RBP, and state-dependent regulation that could be especially relevant in long-lived neuronal cells [[35], [36]]. These considerations are especially relevant in neurobiology and neurodegenerative diseases. The vulnerability of neurons is often linked to subtle and chronic disruption in RNA processing, RBP regulation, and glia-neuron communication [35,37]. Recent syntheses highlight that lncRNAs can intersect with protein synthesis and stress responses, neuroimmune programs, and synaptic regulation precisely the domains that underpin the four disease-relevant axes used throughout this review [38,39]. In parallel, work focusing on neuroinflammation has underscored how specific lncRNAs (including paraspeckle-linked transcripts) may modulate inflammatory set-points and cell-state transitions, reinforcing the need to weight claims by model system, perturbation design, and validation depth [40].

As summarized in Fig. 2A–E, lncRNAs exert regulatory effects through DNA/chromatin engagement, RNA-centric control of translation and stability, miRNA sequestration or blockade, protein scaffolding/guiding, and less commonly micropeptide production.

CircRNAs

CircRNAs are unique single-stranded RNA molecules that are characterized by a covalently joined circular loop. Their unique closed-loop structure makes it biochemically stable and distinctively regulated compared to linear RNAs. In addition, circRNAs do not have a cap structure and are less vulnerable to exonuclease digestion, making them more stable than linear RNAs. Many circRNAs are therefore significantly enriched in long-lived tissues such as those in the nervous system [15,41].

CircRNA biogenesis is primarily driven by back-splicing-a non-canonical splicing mechanism where an upstream 3′ splice acceptor is joined with a downstream 5′ splice donor. These factors include inverted repeats and complementary sequences within the intronic regions that enhance circularization. In parallel, RNA-binding proteins (RBP) associated with intron-flanking regions could regulate splice-site selection to modulate circular RNA biogenesis [42,43]. This framework accounts for cell- and context-specific production of multiple circRNA isoforms generated from a single genomic locus [44].

Structurally, circRNAs are commonly categorized into circular intronic RNAs (ciRNAs), exon-intron circRNAs (EIciRNAs), and exonic circRNAs (ecircRNAs), along with various unique subclasses, including tRNA intronic circRNAs (tricRNAs), during pre-tRNA splicing [44,45]. However, these circRNA specifications are functional and structural, and not simply taxonomic. In contrast, ecircRNAs are always discussed regarding their significance in post-transcriptional regulatory contexts and are enriched in the cytoplasm, while the nuclear presence of EIciRNAs and ciRNAs are widely discussed in their role in transcriptional modulation of their parental loci in specific systems. Published mechanistic claims of circRNAs are often one-sidedly described as regulatory hubs with varied levels of evidential depth across literature. A large portion of reports primarily rely on expression-based associations, whereas a comparatively smaller subset provides perturbation, binding, and rescue experiments that permit stronger causal inference. Within this constraint, quite a few recurring themes of circRNA functions can be drawn out [46].

A subset of circRNAs influence miRNA activity by having binding sites for specific miRNAs, which sequester the miRNAs and cause a reprogramming of the downstream mRNA repression. CDR1as/ciRS-7 is the archetypal example and has been reported to contain multiple miR-7 binding sites. CircRNAs can interact with RBPs, acting as scaffolds, adaptors, or decoys that regulate protein availability, complex assembly, or signaling cascades. Notably, RBPs may also function as upstream determinants of circularization, thereby coupling circRNA abundance to broader splicing and RNA-processing programs [43], In this context, RBPs act not only as downstream effectors but also as upstream determinants of circularization, coupling circRNA abundance to coordinated splicing and RNA-processing programs.

Hence, by integrating functions as miRNA/mRNA sponges and RBPs interactors, circRNAs can regulate networks of gene expression at different levels. Both the nuclear-enriched EIciRNAs and the ciRNAs have also been linked to transcriptional or promoter associated forms of regulation, unlike the mostly cytoplasmic ecircRNAs [47].

Translation from circRNAs has been shown for a subset of transcripts via cap-independent mechanisms, including IRES-driven or m6A-associated initiation routes [41,48]. Functional claims in this context require proteomic validation or better-controlled reporter assays rather than mere computational prediction. Translation competence of circRNAs should therefore be viewed as context-specific rather than universal. Reports of peptides derived from circRNAs that can alter cellular phenotypes, exemplified by circFGFR1p in oncological contexts, add weight to this notion but caution is warranted to avoid overstretching such findings across different tissues and diseases [49].

The invariant stability, tissue specificity, and detectability of circRNAs in biofluids have raised significant interest about their possible use as biomarkers. This must be interpreted with caution in neurological disorders. Tissue origin, cell-type composition, comorbid inflammatory states and analytical pipelines can substantially influence on apparent circRNA signatures. Autosomal circRNAs expression changes have been reported in patient cohorts and discussed with respect to disease activity in multiple sclerosis (MS), for instance; however, replication across cohorts and assay harmonization remain critical pre-requisites to clinical translation [50].

Non-coding RNAs in neurodegenerative disorders: an axis-based synthesis

There is a great deal of clinical heterogeneity in neurodegenerative diseases, but biologically they also converge again and again on a limited set of vulnerabilities that are common. These include breakdowns in protein and organelle quality control checkpoints, chronic neuroinflammatory signaling, vulnerability of synapses and neural circuits, and a slow cellular bioenergetic homeostasis derail [4]. There are important anatomical and symptomatic differences between individual disorders including Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Huntington's disease (HD), and multiple sclerosis (MS) but the molecular stresses underpinning neuronal dysfunction and loss significantly overlap. The increasing volume of non-coding RNA (ncRNA) literature is not organized along individual disorder lines; instead, we use a framework for ncRNA mechanisms in miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circular RNAs (circRNAs) within four applicable, recurring mechanistic classes that emerge. Axis I: proteostasis/homeostasis and pathological protein accumulation

Proteostasis collapse, characterized by maladaptive misfolded protein accumulation, inefficient clearing mechanisms and maladaptive stress responses is a defining feature across neurodegeneration with variable dominant proteins (Aβ/tau for Alzheimer's, α-synuclein in Parkinson's, TDP-43/Fused in sarcoma (FUS) in ALS/FTD, mutant huntingtin in Huntington's). Several miRNAs have been linked to amyloidogenic processing, tau phosphorylation cascades, and downstream stress signalling in AD. An often cited example is the miR-29 family, which has been associated with β-secretase (BACE1) regulation and amyloid burden in sporadic AD models. Mechanistic support comes from in vitro perturbation studies (Tier 2 evidence), highlighting its potential regulatory role while acknowledging that in vivo rescue data remain limited [53]. In parallel, inflammation-associated miRNAs (e.g., miR-146a) have been reported to correlate with signaling pathways intersecting tau pathology and neurofibrillary tangle formation (Tier 4 evidence), underscoring that proteostasis and immune activation may be intertwined in vivo, though functional perturbation data remain limited [54]. In ALS, impaired miRNA processing and selective depletion of miRNAs such as miR-218 have been observed (Tier 4 evidence) suggesting a potential vulnerability of motor neurons to combined proteostatic and RNA-metabolic stress, although direct functional perturbation data remain limited [55]. Importantly, ‘downregulation in disease’ requires weighting according to model type (human tissue vs cell line) and replication, with single-model assertions overstating biological generality (Fig. 4A).

lncRNAs provide an additional layer of regulation through transcriptional programs, RNA stability, and protein regulatory environments that can influence proteostasis. In AD, BACE1-AS has been highlighted as a regulatory lncRNA associated with BACE1 expression and Aβ production, supported by Tier 2–3 evidence from combined in vitro perturbation and expression studies [56,57]. Other AD-associated lncRNAs, such as NDM29 and 51 A, have also been proposed to modulate amyloidogenic pathways and APP processing, reflecting a broader role for lncRNAs as gene-program modulators (Tier 3 evidence from expression profiling studies [58,59]. In PD, lncRNAs such as HOTAIR have been implicated in α-synuclein aggregation–related phenotypes through ceRNA-like mechanisms, supported by Tier 2–3 evidence encompassing in vitro perturbation, target validation, and some in vivo replication across studies [60]. In ALS, emerging lncRNA studies increasingly converge on pathways involving ubiquitination, autophagy–lysosomal flux, and proteasome stress, consistent with proteostasis/homeostasis as a central axis. The mechanistic support ranges from Tier 2 perturbation studies in cell lines to Tier 3–4 expression-based analyses in patient tissues (Fig. 4B) [61,62].

CircRNAs have been reported to influence proteostasis, in part through proposed miRNA sequestration mechanisms, and by tuning stress-response nodes, often via ceRNA-like motifs, while mechanistic confidence depends on direct target engagement and functional rescue. In AD models, circRNAs such as circ-AXL and circ-LPAR1 have been reported to modulate inflammatory and amyloidogenic pathways in miRNA-linked axes (Tier 2), [63,64]. A complementary line of evidence links circRNA regulation to autophagy-related proteostasis: circBptf reverses memory and learning deficits in APP/PS1 mice via a miR-138-5p/p62 (SQSTM1) axis, providing Tier 1 mechanistic support for functional in vivo relevance [65]. These findings are conceptually compelling and should be carefully framed as model-dependent mechanistic studies rather than disease rules (Fig. 4C).

Brain-enriched miRNAs, such as miR-124 and miR-132, are consistently implicated in neuronal homeostasis (Tier 3-2 depending on functional validation), whereas immune-associated miRNAs, including miR-155 and miR-146a, repeatedly modulate microglial activation and cytokine signaling across neurodegenerative disorders (Tier 2–3), [51,88]. In PD, a similar pro-inflammatory profile has been associated with miRNA dysregulation and microglial activation, with STAT3-linked signaling changes in the let-7 family observed in Tier 2 mechanistic studies and Tier 3 expression-based analyses, although the directionality and magnitude of these effects vary depending on the specific dataset and biospecimen. [66,67]. In MS, several miRNAs-most notably miR-155 and miR-21-correlate with inflammatory markers in CSF and peripheral compartments. These observations come primarily from Tier 4 expression-only studies, highlighting their potential as disease activity readouts, but without clarifying whether they act as causal drivers or passive markers of immune activation (Fig. 5A) [68,69]. MiR-155 exhibits context-dependent dual roles: while it promotes pro-inflammatory microglial activation in MS and PD models (Tier 2–4 evidence), in certain neuronal contexts it may contribute to neuroprotective feedback loops, modulating cytokine responses and limiting excitotoxicity [89].

lncRNAs often act as ‘state regulators’ in inflammatory microenvironments due to their ability to assemble protein complexes, scaffold chromatin modifiers, and rewire transcriptional programs. In the AD context, MALAT1 has been implicated in both neurite integrity and inflammatory cytokine profiles, with the strongest support coming from Tier 2 perturbation studies in vitro and Tier 1 in vivo models; these effects are frequently discussed in combination with miRNA-linked modulation [70]. In PD models, NEAT1, SNHG14 and TUG1 have been repeatedly positioned within microglial activation and inflammatory milieus, often via miRNA-mediated nodal functions (e.g., sponge-like interactions) and downstream signalling, with support ranging from Tier 2 in vitro perturbation studies to Tier 1 in vivo validation, reflecting varying mechanistic confidence across reports [[71], [72], [73]]. In MS/EAE scenarios, the lncRNAs have been tied to astrocytic activation and T cell-linked cytokine programmes (e.g., IL-17/Th17 and IL-9/Th9 axes), advocating a non-isolated triggering of inflammatory circuitries by the lncRNAs (Fig. 5B), with mechanistic support spanning Tier 2 in vitro perturbation to Tier 1 in vivo validation [71,85].

CircRNAs implicated in neuroinflammation often converge on macrophage/microglia polarization and stress kinase pathways. In EAE, circ0000518 has been proposed to exacerbate disease severity by promoting M1 polarization through a FUS/CaMKKβ/AMPK axis, supported by Tier 1 in vivo perturbation data, directly aligning circRNA biology with glial-state control [76]. In PD-related models, circHIPK2 has been described as a miRNA sponge that influences receptor-linked stress responses, with Tier 2 mechanistic evidence from in vitro perturbation, illustrating how circRNA–miRNA coupling can act as a regulatory “gain control” on inflammatory signalling [75]. As with other axes, the field benefits from explicitly separating mechanistic studies (Tier 1–2) from expression-only associations (Tier 3–4), particularly when extrapolating from immortalised cell lines (Fig. 5C). However, effective miRNA sponging is strongly constrained by circRNA:miRNA stoichiometry and subcellular localization; only high-copy circRNAs such as CDR1 as can sequester multiple miR-7 molecules efficiently, whereas most ecircRNAs, including circBptf and circCwc27, likely act through combinatorial or indirect regulatory mechanisms rather than direct stoichiometric sponging [15,65].

Experimental studies have identified multiple brain-enriched miRNAs (miR-124, miR-132, miR-134, miR-9, miR-137) as regulators of synaptogenesis, dendritic spine morphology, and plasticity-related signalling, with mechanistic support ranging from Tier 2 (in vitro perturbation with functional assays) to Tier 1 (in vivo perturbation/rescue) evidence in select models [51,77,78]. In AD, miR-132 has emerged as a recurrent node in synaptic and neurogenic vulnerability, with Tier 1 and Tier 2 studies suggesting that miR-132 reductions plausibly couple synaptic dysfunction to downstream neurodegenerative cascades [79]. In PD and HD, altered miRNA profiles have also been observed in the context of neuronal differentiation and circuit stress, with Tier 3 expression-based evidence highlighting disease- and compartment-specific considerations (Fig. 6A), [86,87].

Some lncRNAs are highly enriched in the nervous system and contribute to synaptic structure and function. Among them, MALAT1 has been associated with synaptic plasticity–related regulatory programs. High-tier (Tier 1) evidence from in vitro systems and murine models of Parkinson's disease indicates that MALAT1 promotes neuronal apoptosis via miR-124 sponging [80]. In HD brain tissue and model systems, changes in MEG3, NEAT1, and XIST have been reported, with Tier 3 evidence suggesting that lncRNA dysregulation accompanies broader transcriptional re-patterning impacting neuronal network integrity [81,82]. These lncRNAs may be best framed as “reporters and modulators of neuronal state transitions” rather than single-cause drivers (Fig. 6B).

CircRNAs are enriched in neuronal tissue and can exhibit activity- and age-dependent regulation, making them plausible modulators of synaptic resilience. The neuronal-enriched circCwc27 has been reported to increase in AD patients and AD model mice, with Tier 1 perturbation and rescue studies showing that knockdown improves cognitive and behavioural outcomes in model systems [83]. Such observations are consistent with the broader view that circRNAs may participate in circuit-level vulnerability; however, the field would benefit from more systematic alignment of circRNA findings with synaptic phenotypes measured across comparable models and behavioural paradigms (Fig. 6C).

In PD, miR-124 has been mechanistically linked to autophagy regulation, suppression of neuroinflammation, and apoptosis control, reflecting a model in which neuronal survival depends on coordinated mitochondrial quality control and immune restraint (Tier 2-3 evidence) [84]. Additional PD-associated miRNAs, such as miR-195, which targets ROCK1 in microglial activation contexts, further illustrate how mitochondrial stress and inflammation can be mechanistically intertwined, with effects depending on the cellular compartment driving pathology (Tier 2 evidence) (Fig. 7A), [66].

In ALS, emerging lncRNA findings increasingly implicate autophagy–lysosome processes and cellular stress adaptation. For instance, lnc-HIBADH-4 has been supported by Tier 2–level mechanistic evidence linking lysosomal activity and autophagy dynamics through a miRNA/CTSD axis [61,62]. In addition, recent work has identified downregulation of lnc-ABCA12-3 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from ALS patients, correlating with faster disease progression and shorter survival, while in vitro modulation of this lncRNA alters apoptosis susceptibility and multiple protein homeostasis pathways, including ubiquitination, autophagy flux, mTOR signalling, and stress granule formation, through interaction with the ubiquitin shuttle protein UBQLN1 (Tier 2 mechanistic evidence involving stress and proteostasis programmes) [62]. In MS/EAE, lncRNAs tied to astrocyte activation and cytokine signalling also exhibit Tier 2–strength evidence for influencing metabolic and stress-response adaptations, suggesting that mitochondrial and immune axes may co-amplify rather than function independently (Fig. 7B), [71,74].

Multiple circRNAs have been linked, primarily in preclinical models, to mitochondrial homeostasis and mitophagy. In PD models, circEPS15 has been reported in experimental PD models to regulate PINK1–PRKN-dependent mitophagy via miR-24-3p sponging and thereby supporting mitochondrial quality control through a canonical neurodegeneration-relevant pathway [92]. Additional circRNA axes, such as Circ016719/miR-29c/Map2k6 in ischemia/reperfusion contexts, provide Tier 3–strength evidence for tuning stress survival programmes through miRNA-linked signalling cascades [93]. In ALS, circRNA interactions with RBPs such as FUS offer a mechanistic bridge between RNA metabolism and mitochondrial/proteostatic stress, although translational generalisation remains limited due to model-specific constraints (Fig. 7C), [15,94].

Cross-axis takeaways and evidence calibration

Across disorders, ncRNAs frequently appear to converge on a limited set of regulatory “junctions” where inflammation, proteostasis, synaptic stability, and mitochondrial quality control intersect. Three practical implications follow. First, disease specificity often lies less in whether an ncRNA is involved and more in which cellular compartment or regulatory axis predominates (e.g., microglia-led inflammation in MS/EAE versus neuron-led proteostasis stress in AD/HD). Second, mechanistic certainty varies widely; wherever possible, claims should be anchored to direct target validation and functional perturbation (ideally with rescue), while expression-only associations should be framed as hypothesis-generating rather than causal. Third, the same ncRNA can plausibly occupy different roles across axes (biomarker vs. driver), which argues for explicitly annotating evidence tiers and contextualizing mechanistic claims within defined experimental systems to avoid overgeneralization across heterogeneous neurodegenerative contexts [15,79].

Notably, several mechanistic claims across ncRNA classes remain model-dependent and are frequently derived from single-laboratory systems without independent replication. Differences in cell type composition, disease stage, and experimental perturbation design may substantially influence the apparent directionality and magnitude of ncRNA effects. Therefore, translational prioritization should be restricted to ncRNAs supported by multi-model validation and, ideally, in vivo rescue data.

Controversies and limitations

Despite the growing mechanistic insights into ncRNA functions in neurodegeneration, several important limitations and context-dependent controversies remain. First, certain miRNAs such as miR-155 exhibit dual and sometimes opposing roles in neuroinflammation versus neuroprotection, with effects highly dependent on cell type, disease stage, and experimental model [89]. Second, the proposed miRNA “sponging” function of circRNAs is frequently constrained by low circRNA-to-miRNA stoichiometry; only rare high-abundance examples (e.g., CDR1as) are likely to exert effective sequestration, emphasizing the need for stoichiometry-aware interpretation [15,44]. Third, ncRNA activity is strongly cell-type and region-specific, with many reported effects in neurons not necessarily generalizable to glia or other CNS cell types. More broadly, cross-study comparisons are limited by heterogeneity in experimental design, disease models, perturbation depth, and validation approaches, which may confound mechanistic conclusions. Together, these considerations underscore the necessity of careful contextualization of mechanistic claims, highlight the importance of multi-model and multi-level validation, and identify areas requiring further systematic investigation.

Future perspectives

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) including miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs are increasingly positioned at the interface of mechanistic neurobiology and clinical translation, particularly as (i) biofluid biomarkers and (ii) modulators that can be therapeutically targeted. The near-term opportunity is not simply to expand ncRNA catalogues, but to convert heterogeneous findings into replicable, model-aware, and clinically actionable evidence streams.

A priority direction is biomarker maturation. Circulating and extracellular vesicle (EV) associated ncRNAs are attractive because they can be repeatedly sampled and may reflect cell-state transitions in neurons and glia; however, diagnostic performance is sensitive to pre-analytical variables (sample handling), EV isolation methods, and platform effects. Reviews focusing on AD-related exosomal miRNAs consistently emphasize this methodological bottleneck and the need for assay harmonization before clinical deployment [95]. Within this space, several studies have reported discriminative serum exosomal miRNA panels in AD; for example, Duan et al. quantified differential expression of multiple exosomal miRNAs (including miR-125b-1-3p, miR-193a-5p, miR-378a-3p/miR-378i, and miR-451a), supporting the feasibility of multi-marker signatures rather than single-miRNA tests [96]. Nonetheless, robust clinical translation will likely require multi-modal frameworks (ncRNA panels integrated with neuroimaging, protein biomarkers, and cognitive/functional metrics), as well as longitudinal designs that distinguish state markers (disease activity) from trait markers (risk) [97].

A second priority is delivery and safety engineering for RNA therapeutics in the central nervous system (CNS). While miRNA-based therapies are conceptually appealing due to their capacity to modulate entire gene networks, this broad regulatory scope also increases the risk of unintended off-target effects and immune activation. In neurodegenerative disorders, for example, restoration of miR-132 in Alzheimer's disease models has shown neuroprotective potential; however, preclinical studies also highlight that delivery strategy, dosing, and immune compatibility critically influence therapeutic safety and efficacy. These observations underscore that, beyond target selection, vector design and CNS-specific pharmacodynamics remain central challenges in miRNA-based intervention [98]. In contrast, the recent regulatory approval of intrathecal antisense therapy for SOD1-ALS (tofersen) has demonstrated that CNS RNA drug delivery is achievable when dosing, pharmacodynamic readouts, and risk–benefit profiles are carefully managed. Although this model is mutation- and indication-specific and does not eliminate broader delivery challenges across NDDs [99].

An emerging translational avenue for durable gene modulation in neurodegenerative diseases is AAV-mediated delivery of therapeutic miRNAs. A leading example in this domain is AMT-130, an AAV5 vector encoding an engineered miRNA targeting the HTT transcript to lower mutant huntingtin levels in Huntington's disease [100]. Preclinical studies using AAV-delivered miHTT constructs have demonstrated broad distribution in the brain, significant lowering of HTT mRNA and protein, and prevention of neuropathological features in rodent and large-animal models, supporting its movement into early phase clinical trials (e.g., phase I/II) with a favorable safety profile and signals of slowed disease progression in high-dose cohorts [101]. These approaches illustrate a key translational trade-off: potentially long-lasting target engagement after a single administration, coupled with limited reversibility and an elevated requirement for long-term safety monitoring, especially given the permanent nature of vector DNA expression in non-dividing neurons.

Parallel efforts focused on enhancing brain targeting and BBB traversal increasingly emphasize extracellular vesicle (EV)-based delivery systems as promising alternatives to conventional vectors. EVs, including exosomes and small EVs, are naturally occurring nanoscale carriers capable of transporting RNA cargoes such as miRNAs across the BBB with minimal immunogenicity and enhanced biocompatibility, making them attractive for CNS applications where immune activation and delivery efficiency are critical concerns. Recent reviews highlight that engineered EVs loaded with miR-124 attenuated neuronal apoptosis and promoted neuroprotection in Parkinson's disease and Alzheimer's disease models [102]. Preclinical studies further support this translational potential. For example, modified EVs carrying miRNAs have shown the ability to modulate neuroinflammation and promote neurofunctional recovery after traumatic brain injury through targeted delivery to injured brain regions, illustrating practical CNS delivery pathways for miRNA therapeutics [103]. Despite these advances, major translational challenges remain. Standardised benchmarks for EV biodistribution, therapeutic cargo loading efficiency, and scalable manufacturing protocols are still under development, and rigorous comparative studies are needed to fully define their safety and efficacy profiles in human neurodegenerative disease contexts.

From a scientific perspective, the most productive next step for ncRNA neurodegeneration research is to move toward evidence-calibrated mechanistic pipelines: prioritizing ncRNAs supported by direct target binding and perturbation/rescue in disease-relevant systems, explicitly reporting model context (human tissue, iPSC-derived neurons/glia, in vivo models), and systematically documenting contradictory findings. This will help distinguish biomarker candidates from causal regulators and will better support rational therapeutic prioritization, including combinations (e.g., ncRNA modulation plus anti-inflammatory or proteostasis-directed interventions) aligned to dominant disease axes.

Conclusions

This narrative review synthesises evidence that ncRNAs—miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs—participate in central neurodegenerative mechanisms, including proteostasis and pathological protein accumulation, mitochondrial and oxidative injury, neuroinflammation and glia-neuron crosstalk, and synaptic/circuit vulnerability. Across AD, PD, ALS, HD, and MS, dysregulated ncRNA profiles are frequently reported in brain tissue and peripheral biofluids, supporting their relevance both as mechanistic regulators and as candidate biomarkers. Translational progress will therefore depend on more consistent evidence grading, replication across models and cohorts, and improved standardisation of EV/ncRNA measurement pipelines. Conceptually, ncRNAs may not function as isolated disease triggers, but rather as dynamic gain modulators operating at points of intersection between proteostasis, inflammation, synaptic plasticity, and mitochondrial stress. Therapeutically, recent advances in CNS oligonucleotide delivery provide a credible path for RNA-based interventions in selected settings, while experience from early miRNA mimic trials underscores that immunotoxicity and delivery design are central constraints. Taken together, the current review highlights the importance of ncRNAs as plausible bridges between molecular pathophysiology and clinical translation in neurodegenerative disorders, provided that future work remains anchored in rigorous, model-aware validation and clinically interoperable biomarker strategies.
