A breast cancer patient developed γδ T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia after chemotherapy: a case report with literary review
Authors: Xiaofeng Yang, Zhihua Zhang, Xue Wu, Xinhong Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gamma delta T-cell (γδ T-cell) acute lymphoblastic leukemia(γδ T-ALL) is rare and aggressive, but secondary γδ T-ALL following breast cancer is more rare. Our case presents a patient with breast cancer, received surgical treatment and chemotherapy consisting of epirubicin and cyclophosphamide. Three years later, the patient got secondary γδ T-ALL. The chemotherapeutic agents had a significant effect on the breast cancer patient, who has an increased risk of secondary γδ T-ALL.

Introduction

There are two classes of T-cells depending on the structure of the T-cell receptor (TCR), alpha-beta T-cells (αβ T-cells) and gamma delta T-cells (γδ T-cells) [1]. T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) expresses the αβ or γδ T-cell receptor (TCR) in less than 35% of the cases. T-ALL is uncommon with only 2% of all acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) cases showing expression of the γδ TCR [2]. Most cases of T-ALL develop from αβ T-cells. γδ T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia(γδ T-ALL) is a rare and aggressive subtype of T-lymphoid leukemia that accounts for 9–12% of all T-ALL cases and is correlated with elevated rates of induction failure, relapse, and increased mortality, although recent approaches with anti-γδ TCR chimeric antigen receptor T cells have demonstrated promising effect against γδ T cell malignancies [3].

Here we present a patient with γδ T-ALL after breast cancer treatment.

Case description

In February 2022, a 62-year- woman was diagnosed with Left breast invasive ductalcarcinoma and received surgical treatment. Pathological diagnosis: Infiltrating ductal.carcinoma of the left breast, grade II. The invasive carcinoma measured a maximum diameter of 2 cm without evidence of clear vascular invasion. The surrounding breast tissue showed hyperplastic changes. No cancer involvement was observed at the nipple, overlying skin, or basal margins. No metastatic cancer was detected in axillary lymph nodes (0/11). Immunohistochemistry: ER (+) (strong, positive rate approximately 80%), PR (+) (strong, positive rate approximately 80%), PS2 (-), AR (+) (moderate intensity, positive rate approximately 70%), HER2 (1+), P53 (-), EGFR (-), Ki-67 (proliferation index approximately 40%),CK5/6 (-). The patient did not test BRCA1-2 gene mutation. After the surgery the patient received eight cycles of chemotherapy consisting of 110 mg epirubicin and 80 mg cyclophosphamide.

In June 2025, the patient had been off chemotherapy for two years and was found to have elevated white blood cells for 2 months. CT showed multiple enlarged mediastinal lymph nodes. White blood cell(WBC) count was 112.20 × 109/L, hemoglobin(HB) was 138.00 g/L, platelet(PLT) count was 105.00 × 109/L. Peripheral blood smear showed 80% prolymphoblasts. Discussion with literature review

A unique feature of ALL is that the cell of origin is derived from immature cells of either the B or the T lineages (rarely from both), which are two separate arms of the immune system.The US clinical trials found that the majority of ALL patients are precursor B cell (BCP) (approximately 80% in children and 75% in adults). The remainder are T-ALL, which accounts for 25% and 15% of adult and pediatric. γδ.

T-ALL is a rare and aggressive subtype of T-ALL [4]. γδ T-ALL accounts for only 9–12% of all T-ALL cases.The outcome of T-ALL has improved with recent changes in therapeutic strategy. But γδ T-ALL is potentially associated with higher rates of refractory disease and poor prognosis.

Immunophenotypically, lymphoblasts in T-ALL are positive for cytoplasmic or surface CD3 by flow cytometry, and may show variable expression of CD2, CD4, CD5, CD7, CD8, TdT, CD34, and CD1a [5]. Cytogenetic abnormalities observed in 50–70% of T-ALL include rearrangements involving 14q11.2, and 7p14-15. Other translocations include t(10;11)(p13;q14). Van Dongen et al.described a small subset of normal γδ T-cells in the peripheral blood which was either CD4 or CD8 positive, in addition to the vast majority of CD4 and CD8 negative cells. T-ALL is stratified into pro-T, pre-T, cortical T, and medullary T subtypes based on progressive stages of differentiation [6].

Although a correlation has been made between ALL and environmental exposure to tobacco smoke, pesticides and traffic emissions [7], a negative association has been seen between incidence of ALL and socioeconomic status [8, 9]. Differences in incidence and outcomes may also suggest genetics, as a contributing factor. Recent studies have examined the genomic landscape of T-ALL, and defined several T-ALL subtypes arising from dysregulated oncogene expression. The abnormal expression of transcription factors (TAL1, LMO1/2, TLX1/3, LYL1, HOXA, MEF2C) and their developmental arrest at a specific stage of T-cell differentiation [5]. Molecular studies have also revealed signalling pathway deregulation in T-ALL occurs in the NOTCH, PI3K/AKT, JAK/STAT, RAS/MAPK, and ABL kinase pathways. The most common mutations involving the NOTCH1 pathway activation and a large number of tumor suppressors (e.g., CDKN2A/B, PHF6, PTEN, TP53, etc.). The latter undergo loss-offunction mutations as well as deletions. In addition, copy number variants (CNVs) have been observed in oncogene ectopic regulation mechanisms (e.g., C-MYC, PTEN, NUP214::ABL1, C-MYB, or SIL::TAL1). NOTCH1 pathway activation can be caused by activating mutations involving the extracellular eterodimerization domain and/or the terminal PEST domain of NOTCH1 (50% of cases) and/or by loss-of-function mutations in FBXW7, a negative regulator of NOTCH1 (30% of cases). Epigenetic dysregulation occurs via mutations in PHF6, SUZ12, EZH2, TET2, H3-3 A (H3F3A), KDM6A, EED, SETD2, and DNMT3A [6]. PHF6 mutation is often associated with.

NOTCH1 mutation, and has no significant effect on prognosis. NOTCH1 and/or FBXW7 mutations have been associated with better clinical outcomes. EP300 somatic mutations have been reported mainly in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), follicular lymphoma (FL) and T-ALL.

Within the category of secondary leukemias, secondary myeloid leukemia, specifically therapy-related myeloid neoplasms (t-MNs), is widely acknowledged and forms a distinct category within the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of myeloid neoplasms. In contrast to secondary myeloid leukemias, the data on secondary lymphoblastic leukemias(sALL) are scarce as the incidence is much lower than secondary myeloid neoplasms(sMN). sALL has two subtypes: individuals with a history of previous cancer, known as antecedent-malignancy ALL (am-ALL), and those resulting from exposure to genotoxic agents such as chemotherapy or radiation therapy, known as therapy-related ALL (tr-ALL)(accounts for only about 10–15% of therapyrelated leukemia) [10, 11]. Similar to t-MNs, the etiology of tr-ALL is likely related to the genotoxic effects of cytotoxic treatments on hematopoietic progenitor cells. However, the exact mechanism of tr-ALL is not fully understood. A large SEER analysis [10] found 6.6% newly diagnosed sALL in the United States. GIMEMA group reported a lower incidence of 2.3% [13], while Aldoss et al. [14] described an incidence of 9.3% at a single U.S. institution.

Breast cancer survival rates have improved due to better chemotherapy and radiation treatment options. Nevertheless, breast cancer patients are at an increased risk of developing a secondary nonbreast cancer. Breast cancer survivors exhibit a long-term increased risk of therapy-related acute leukemia(t-AL), most commonly and less frequently therapy-related acute lymphoblastic leukemia(t-ALL). It was reported that, patients who underwent chemotherapy for breast cancer had a 0.2% risk of AML. The ratio of T-ALL cases to therapy-related acute myeloid leukemia(t-AML) cases is 1/9 [12–14]. Translocation involving the MLL gene was frequently found in tAL caused by a topoisomerase inhibitor and was related to poor prognosis. Praga et al. found that the cumulative probability of developing t-AML/t-MDS was 0.37% in the patients treated with standard regimens and 4.97% in the patients treated with higher doses of topoisomerase inhibitors. The dosage of chemotherapeutic agents had a signifificant effect on the development of t-AL [15]. Yen-Hsun Chen, et al. [14]. report the case of a lady who developed to pro-B ALL with t(4;11)(q21;q23) 16 months after cyclophosphamide, epirubicin, and 5-fluorouracil (CEF) chemotherapy for her breast cancer. In 2017, Daiki Hori, et al. [17] reported the case of T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma (γδ T-LBL) arising from the mediastinum in a toddler, that demonstrated good clinical response to aggressive treatment and cord blood transplantation. In 2025, Xiaohua Luo, et al. [18] detailed a case of child with γδ T-ALL who underwent induction chemotherapy and intensification treatment, followed by haploidentical hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. The patient achieved a clinical complete remission and remains minimal residual disease negative with chidamide maintenance post-transplantation. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed a connection between histone HIST1 genes and γδ T-ALL and identified potential effector functions of γδ T cells in combating this leukemia. Highlighting the relationship between histone modification patterns and γδ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes in γδ T-ALL cells for developing novel therapeutic approaches.

Although intensive treatment protocols have markedly improved the survival rates of patients with T-ALL, cure rates remain below 60% for adults and 85% for children [19]. Patients with γδ T-ALL are more likely to have a poor response to treatment as reflected by the high minimal residual disease(MRD) levels during remission induction therapy. 67% of patients with γδ T-ALL had MRD levels ≥ 1% after 15–19 days of induction therapy and received additional chemotherapy. A third of the γδ T-ALL patients still had elevated MRD levels ≥ 1% at the end of induction therapy and became candidates for allogeneic stem cell transplant(HSCT) [20]. K imura S, et al. [21] studied 200 children with γδ T-ALL from 13 clinical study groups to understand the clinical and genetic features of this disease. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and RNA-sequencing data identified several genomic subtypes of γδ T-ALL. Patients with γδ T-ALL (age at diagnosis < 3 years) or MRD >0.1% at EOI showed dismal outcomes and should be classified as having high-risk disease. The STAG2/LMO2 subtype was enriched in this very young age group. STAG2 inactivation may perturb chromatin conformation and cell differentiation, and confer vulnerability to Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibition. The identification of γδ T-cell lineage-specific genetic alterations leading to the fusion transcripts, SET-NUP214 and CALMAF10, have been associated with chemotherapy resistance and poor prognosis [22, 23].

Conclusions

Patients with γδ T-ALL are more likely to have a poor response to treatment, and the incidence of breast cancer is increasing. γδ T-ALL lacks a biologically informed framework to guide its classification and treatment trategies. Comparably for patients with T-ALL, recent studies have identified γδ T cell specific genetic alterations that is predictive of poorer response to therapy or outcomes. Understanding the mechanism driving leukemic transformation and identifying factors contributing to the poor treatment response may help to develop treatment strategies to improve clinical outcome of these patients. Further studies must investigate treatments for γδ T-ALL, a serious complication of breast cancer treatment.
