Rural targeted messaging, multiplex salivary measurement, and rural equity in SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing: Protocol overview of a SeroNet investigation
Authors: Leah Maschino, Kelly A Hirko, Christopher D Heaney, Douglas A Granger, Steve W Granger, Anurag Dawadi, Lindsey Rose, Monicia Summers, Nora Pisanic, Olivia Aspiras, Stefan MM Goetz, Todd Lucas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRural communities were disproportionately impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic. Current and future pandemic preparedness in rural areas could be aided by serological (antibody) testing. However, benefits to be gained from serosurveillance are challenged both by the accessibility of serological testing resources in hard-to-reach rural areas, and by likely hesitancy towards their use in many rural communities. Trials are needed to evaluate outreach and health communication strategies that can increase rural receptivity towards serosurveillance.MethodsWe conducted a two-arm randomized controlled trial that recruited N = 194 participants from rural northern Michigan. In a single online session lasting approximately 45 min, participants were randomly assigned to view either a general SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing didactic video, or a rural targeted didactic video co-created with a rural community advisory board and containing unique rural messaging. Primary self-report outcomes included receptivity to antibody testing, as well as activation of medical mistrust and concern for rural-discrimination in future SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing. We also developed and provided an opportunity to participate in a home-based salivary antibody screening program. In addition to assessing the feasibility and fidelity of this program, we assessed interest and full participation in the serosurveillance program as behavioral outcomes.DiscussionFindings from this study can guide the use of home-based screening programs and targeted health communication strategies to promote greater uptake of health resources in rural communities. In doing so, study results can also guide public health strategies to reduce pandemic-related and other rural health disparities.Keywords: SeroNet, Rural health disparities, Health communication, Antibody testing, COVID-19, Medical mistrust, SARS-CoV-2

Introduction

Nearly one in five Americans lives in a rural area [1]. Rural communities in the United States have been historically disproportionately impacted by a broad range of illnesses [2,3], and rural Americans today continue to face higher incidence and mortality rates for many preventable diseases [[4], [5], [6]]. The COVID-19 pandemic served as a stark reminder of rural health disparities, with many rural communities experiencing higher rates of infection and more severe disease outcomes, even as vaccination and other treatment options became increasingly viable [5,7,8]. Although due to numerous causes, rural pandemic disparities are attributable in part to lower rates of preventive care utilization [9]. In turn, lower utilization of COVID-19 resources can be attributed not only to difficulty accessing evidence-based preventive care in many rural areas, but also to hesitancy and feelings of medical mistrust toward their use in many rural communities. Indeed, the COVID-19 pandemic underscored that mistrust can be highly prevalent in rural areas, with rural residents more often reporting feeling overlooked by the government, and exhibiting lower levels of trust in media [10]. Taken together, the COVID-19 pandemic highlights an urgent need to implement outreach programs that can improve rural access to testing and treatment, and to develop and evaluate rural health education and communication strategies that can increase receptivity towards these resources [10,11].

Salivary serological testing as a strategy to increase testing access

One resource that could substantially aid current and future pandemic preparedness is serological (antibody) testing. Serological screening can serve important functions in assessing and managing infectious disease outbreaks, including supporting efforts to develop and deploy public health responses to viral epidemics [77]. In contrast to molecular and antigen-based diagnostics that identify active infections, antibody detection via serological testing can reveal evidence of past infection, and immune responses to vaccination. Other applications include measuring protective immunity levels [12], monitoring disease transmission patterns and population-level immunity [13], and supporting research into immunological mechanisms underlying disease progression and recovery [14]. Although serological testing offers significant value, realizing potential benefits depends on widespread participation in serosurveillance efforts, including among rural communities.

A promising development in response to the COVID-19 pandemic was the emergence of saliva-based SARS-CoV-2 antibody tests [13,15,16]. Lessons learned from COVID-19 revealed that benefits of serosurveillance may be limited by screening programs ill-suited for rural settings. Most serosurveillance relies on invasive, time-consuming methods that reduce scalability, accessibility, and acceptability in rural areas [17,18]. Saliva collection is an effective, non-invasive, and convenient method that can be implemented outside traditional healthcare environments, enabling more equitable access to serosurveillance activities among underserved populations, especially populations reluctant to engage with medical institutions or with limited healthcare access [19,20]. Salivary serosurveillance thus represents a promising strategy for promoting rural equity in antibody testing uptake while maintaining scientifically rigorous screening.

Rural targeted health communication as a strategy to increase testing uptake

Benefits to be gained from serosurveillance also depend on understanding and acceptance of serological testing in rural communities. Many rural communities are persistently challenged by accessing healthcare services [21,22], and this was further exacerbated during the COVID-19 pandemic [6,23]. These access barriers contributed to already heightened levels of mistrust and hesitancy towards health screenings and treatment in many rural populations [24,25]. Medical mistrust in rural communities has been linked to historical experiences of being underserved by healthcare systems, perceived stigmatization by urban medical providers, and feelings of being overlooked by public health initiatives that fail to address rural-specific concerns [26]. Given both pre-existing and amplified levels of hesitancy, there is an urgent need for innovative, community-informed health education tools and communication approaches for use with rural individuals and communities.

One promising, yet still underutilized approach involves the use of rural targeted didactics and health communication. Culturally targeted approaches involve crafting health information and messaging to reflect the shared values, experiences, and concerns of specific social groups [27]. In turn, rural targeted health communication encompasses the use of health information and messages that embody shared rural values and lived experiences (for review, [28]). Effective rural targeted messaging can include surface-structure rural adaptations (e.g., rural representative images and language), as well as deep-structure targeting that addresses core rural values and experiences [29]. Importantly, these rural targeted approaches may help reduce medical mistrust by acknowledging rural-specific health disparities and demonstrating that health interventions are designed with rural communities in mind rather than simply adapted from urban contexts. By explicitly recognizing rural experiences and values, targeted messaging can build trust and increase receptivity to health resources [30]. Informational and persuasive health communications that include rural targeted content have been shown to encourage preventive health behaviors, such as cancer screening (e.g., [31]). Yet, rural targeted approaches were scarce and slow to develop during the COVID-19 pandemic, highlighting a considerable opportunity to leverage such approaches in current and future pandemic preparedness [32].

The present study

This protocol outlines the development, implementation, and evaluation of a rural targeted messaging campaign supporting non-invasive, home-based saliva collection for SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing. Building on prior work [20,33], the study employs a novel multiplex assay that accurately detects antibodies in saliva and links antibody data with biological and behavioral variables. The accompanying health communication randomized controlled trial (RCT) assessed receptivity towards and uptake of salivary antibody testing among northern Michigan rural residents (see also, [34]). The overarching study aim was to examine how rural targeted health communication strategies and non-invasive, at-home salivary testing can increase uptake and reduce rural disparities in serological screening.

Specific aims

Aim 1: Main effects of rural targeted messaging. Our first aim was to develop and compare the effects of general (i.e., non-targeted) versus rural targeted SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing didactic videos on salivary antibody testing attitudes, intentions, and behavior among rural White Americans residing in Michigan. We hypothesized that receptivity and uptake of salivary antibody testing will be higher among rural residents with use of rural targeted didactics and communication, as compared to general didactics and messaging.

Aim 2: Reduced medical mistrust as a mediator of rural targeted messaging. Our second aim was to compare the effects of general versus rural targeted messaging on activation of medical mistrust among rural residents of northern Michigan when considering antibody testing. We hypothesized that rural targeted didactics and communication would reduce medical mistrust among rural residents, and that reduced mistrust would link rural targeted didactics to greater antibody testing interest and uptake.

Aim 3: Descriptive evaluation of home-based salivary antibody screening. Our third aim was to develop, implement, and evaluate the efficacy and fidelity of a protocol for home-based salivary collection and antibody testing in rural contexts. This included evaluation of sending, collecting, receiving and successfully assaying for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies using subsequently described salivary collection kits and assays. We assessed efficacy and fidelity of home-based rural salivary collection by measuring overall kit receipt and return rates, as well as rates of successful performing salivary antibody assays. Furthermore, we compared receipt, return and assay success rates within and across messaging conditions.

Methods

This study was conducted as part of the National Cancer Institute's Serological Sciences Network (SeroNet) initiative [14]. This study was pre-registered at Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06085547) and was an adaptation of a related and prior clinical trial that considered racial disparities in uptake of SARS-CoV-2 serological testing [34].

Participants

Following institutional review board approval by Michigan State University (00005016), participants were recruited from Munson Medical Center (MMC) in northern Michigan – a region that is classified as predominantly rural, including through designation in the updated 2023 Rural-Urban Continuum Codes (RUCC). MMC also conducted a human subjects review and provided approval. All MMC employees aged 18 years or older who identified as White/Caucasian were eligible, regardless of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination or infection history. The decision to restrict eligibility to White/Caucasian participants was made because our rural targeted messaging was developed specifically for White rural residents in northern Michigan, who comprise approximately 94% of the regional population [35]. Conducting this initial RCT in a relatively homogeneous population allowed us to isolate the effects of rural targeting without the complexity of race-by-rurality interactions. Future research should examine targeted messaging strategies for racially diverse rural communities and explore potential intersections between rural identity and racial/ethnic identity in shaping health communication receptivity. Recruitment occurred via a weekly advertisement in an employee e-newsletter that contained a pre-enrollment survey link to assess eligibility. As seen in Fig. 1, initial recruitment included 264 eligible individuals. Of these, 50 opted out and 214 completed an IRB-approved consent. Four participants were excluded due to another household member who was already enrolled. Following established procedures for online research quality control [36], 3 participants were excluded for inattentiveness detected by subsequently described attention checks, and 13 were excluded based on a true-false knowledge retention quiz used for quality control. This resulted in a final sample size of 194 participants. All participants were enrolled between May and November 2023. Table 1 presents sociodemographic characteristics of study participants. All participants received $50 financial remuneration for participating in the online portion of the study, which lasted 45-120 min (M = 119.35). Participants who opted to participate in the subsequently described in-person saliva collection received an additional $50 remuneration.

Study design and procedure

This study was conducted as a single factor, two-group (general versus rural targeted didactic video) randomized control trial. Participants were randomly assigned with equal probability to either a generally prepared didactic video, or to a rural targeted didactic video. Randomization was stratified by gender in order to ensure balanced numbers of women and men in both arms. General and rural targeted antibody video tutorials were professionally created using Vyond animation software. As seen in Table 2, this study was conducted over four phases that consisted of 1) an initial online prescreen; 2) an online didactic video viewing and immediate outcome measurement session; 3) a home-based saliva collection for those participants who elected to participate in the available no-cost SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing program; 4) a results reporting session, in which participants who provided saliva samples received their personal SARS-CoV-2 antibody test results. Participants who completed the in-person saliva collection completed a brief survey during the collection appointment, and also at the time antibody results were made available. All self-report and online data were collected using Qualtrics.

Online prescreen and didactic video session. Participants began by completing a series of questions to reconfirm study eligibility according to inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible participants then completed a digital informed consent and were directed to a brief prescreen questionnaire, which included individual differences measures of sociodemographic, psychological and behavioral characteristics that could potentially act as covariates or moderators of receptivity to rural messaging (Table 2). Following prescreen, participants viewed a series of animated SARS-CoV-2 didactic videos that sought to provide information about antibodies and serological testing both generally, and with particular focus on SARS-CoV-2 serology (see also, [34]). The didactic video also included an overview on use of saliva to conduct SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing. The didactic portion concluded with randomization to the subsequently described rural targeted adjunct for half of study participants. Finally, all participants completed an identical set of outcome measures related to impressions of and willingness to participate in the subsequently described no-cost SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing program.

Content and chronology of SARS-CoV-2 didactic videos. The brief, animated SARS-CoV-2 didactic video was built to accessibly educate individuals about serology both generally, and with a particular focus on SARS-CoV-2. The didactic video also included an overview use of saliva to enable SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing. Videos were professionally prepared, and an animated modality was selected both for its effectiveness in increasing patient knowledge [37,38] and for its potential to enable rapid revision and scaling [39]. The structure was modeled after a serological testing didactic that was prepared by members of the research team for use in a related consideration of racial disparities in serological testing [34]. The didactic video used a chapter-by-chapter format beginning with a brief introduction, after which a brief overview of antibodies and the immune system were provided, with special emphasis on COVID-19 and the SARS-CoV-2 virus. Subsequently presented chapters introduced SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing and reviewed appropriate (e.g., public health surveillance) and inappropriate (e.g., personal immunity) uses of information provided by antibody testing. Finally, participants viewed a video chapter in which they learned about an opportunity to participate in a salivary antibody testing study.

Community-partnered creation of didactic videos and rural targeted messaging. Didactic video content and rural targeted messaging were developed in collaboration with the Michigan State University SeroNet Rural Advisory Board. Board members were recruited by a research team member based in northern Michigan where study participants were recruited. The board consisted of six members who lived and worked in northern Michigan, including members with backgrounds in healthcare, and served as key advisors in the design of video content and rural messaging. Board engagement included an initial semi-structured virtual 90-min discussion session during the planning phase. Following video and study material development, the research team maintained ongoing email correspondence with board members to facilitate continuous review and feedback opportunities. Members provided guidance on visual imagery, language choices for rural targeted content, and study recruitment strategies. Board contributions ensured materials were culturally resonant and logistically feasible for rural audiences. The board also identified and suggested potential solutions to participation barriers for rural residents, including logistical challenges with home-based salivary test kits. One board member tested the sample shipping process to ensure a seamless participation experience. Two board members served as video narrators, further grounding the content in local community context. All board members received a modest remuneration in exchange for their collaboration.

Content and randomization of rural targeted videos adaptations. Participants were randomly assigned to view either the general didactic video, or the rural targeted version. Video versions were identical, with the exception that the rural targeted version included a two and a half-minute animated messaging adjunct that was viewed after the didactic portion. Fig. 2 shows screenshots from both video versions. Rural-targeted messaging was adapted from a culturally targeted messaging framework developed by members of the research team for use in racial disparities research [34]. Rural targeted messaging made use of both surface-structure and deep-structure targeting strategies [27]. Participants viewed a title slide entitled, “Antibody Testing and You,” and were then presented with information about ongoing health disparities in rural communities. This overview highlighted ongoing COVD-19 injustices in rural communities, while emphasizing the potential to overcome some aspects through antibody testing, specifically noting that benefits require rural residents to take personal control over testing use [40]. Visually, the rural targeted message featured an animated balance scale showing equilibrium during the personal control component of messaging, based on research showing balance scales can effectively induce thinking about justice [41,42]. All videos featured narration by a White American male physician with a corresponding animated character for consistency across conditions. Following presentation, participants were required to indicate they had viewed and understood the rural targeted health message.

General and rural-targeted didactic video samples.

Attentiveness and fidelity checks. To ensure attentive viewing, each video module was followed by low-difficulty true-false knowledge retention questions covering the preceding segment. Participants were required to correctly answer at least 21 out of 23 true-false items (>90%) for responses to be considered attentive (M = 22.47, SD = 0.677). Additionally, three attention check items were embedded throughout the survey, with participants automatically discontinued upon failing a second attention check.

Home-based saliva collection

All participants who completed the full online survey could participate in voluntary home-based, non-invasive salivary antibody testing. Collection kits were mailed to the participants’ preferred address. Collection of samples was aided by scheduling one-on-one virtual appointments with a member of the research team, who provided instructions for collecting, packaging, and mailing collected samples.

Appointment intake. Each appointment began with the researcher verifying the participant's name and date of birth. Participants answered brief health questions, including dental history and current medications, as these factors can potentially affect salivary composition and antibody detection accuracy. The researcher confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection and vaccination history when applicable. Participants were asked to display vaccination cards when available via webcam to accurately record vaccine dates and types.

Oral fluid collections. Two sample types were collected: whole saliva and gingival crevicular fluid (GCF). GCF, obtained from the gum line using a mild abrasive, is particularly suitable for antibody detection [20]. Participants could consent to banking remaining saliva for future research, though this was optional.

Whole saliva collection. Whole saliva was collected using a passive drool method. Participants provided saliva samples through a collection straw inserted into a 2-mL cryovial until approximately 1.0-1.8 mL was collected. Flow rate was monitored in real time at 2-min intervals, with participants holding the cryovial up to the camera for volume assessment.

Gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) collection. GCF was collected following the whole saliva procedure. Participants gently brushed along their gum line using an Oracol S14 saliva collection device (Malvern Medical Developments, UK) for 1-2 min until the sponge was saturated. This method collects GCF enriched with immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies from serum [43]. The saturated sponge was inserted into its storage tube and securely capped. To maintain assay validity, samples showing visible blood contamination were noted, as blood contains significantly higher antibody concentration than saliva and can compromise the accuracy of salivary antibody measurements [20].

Saliva storage and shipment. Upon collection, samples were immediately placed in an activated NanoCool shipping device to maintain cold-chain conditions for transport to the Johns Hopkins University (JHU) Environmental Health Microbiology and Immunology Laboratory (EHMIL) in Baltimore, Maryland. The FedEx NanoCool system uses evaporative cooling to sustain 2-8 °C for up to 96 h [44]. Participants were encouraged to ship completed samples the same day, or store samples in a home refrigerator and ship within 72 h. All samples were shipped overnight to the JHU lab. Upon arrival, whole saliva samples were weighed and stored at −80 °C. GCF samples were separated from Oracol S14 swabs by centrifugation (10 min at 1500×g) and transferred to 2-mL cryovials. GCF samples were stored at −80 °C until assays were performed. Frozen whole saliva samples were later shipped on dry ice overnight to Salimetrics (Carlsbad, California).

Antibody results reporting

Antibody Screening Results. Antibody tests were conducted by a CLIA-certified laboratory at Johns Hopkins University. Results were typically available within 6-8 weeks after sample shipment. Participants received written email reports indicating positive, negative, or inconclusive SARS-CoV-2 antibody results along with brief interpretations. Positive results indicated antibodies were detected, suggesting prior SARS-CoV-2 infection, vaccination response, or both. Negative results indicated little, or no antibodies were detected, suggesting no recent infection and/or vaccination. Inconclusive results indicated the test did not perform as expected, most commonly due to insufficient sample quantity or quality. The results letters also encouraged participants to contact their primary care provider with any questions or concerns about their test results. Additional resources provided in these letters included contact information for the research team, local health department, Michigan Department of Health and Human Services (MDHHS), and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

Post Antibody Results Evaluation. All participants completing antibody testing were invited to complete a brief post-antibody results evaluation survey (Table 2). This included items confirming their test results and feedback on the testing experience. Participants rated perceived test accuracy and indicated whether they continued to view antibody testing favorably. The survey also assessed future COVID-19 vaccination intentions, whether participants believed rural discrimination impacted their experience, and general vaccination attitudes.

Self-report and behavioral measures and outcomes

Prescreen measures. Prior to the didactic video, participants completed a prescreen battery to reconfirm study eligibility and collect health history, including SARS-CoV-2 infection and vaccination status and self-reported physical health status. The prescreen also gathered sociodemographic data and administered psychosocial individual differences measures (Table 2).

Primary self-report outcomes. Primary outcome measures were collected following video viewing. Most measures were administered before the final video chapter introducing the home-based salivary antibody screening program. Primary outcomes related to salivary antibody screening receptivity and uptake were assessed after viewing this final chapter.

General receptivity to SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing. Overall receptivity to antibody testing was assessed immediately following the videos using a Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) framework by examining attitudes, normative beliefs, perceived behavioral control, and intentions. Measures were adapted from prior TPB applications in health screening contexts [40,45]; and followed recommended guidelines to ensure validity and appropriate behavioral specificity [46]. All items used 7-point Likert scales ranging from 1 (strongly agree) to 7 (strongly disagree). Attitudes were assessed with four items (e.g., “Obtaining SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing would be good for me”), normative beliefs with five items (e.g., “Most people who are important to me think I should obtain SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing”), perceived behavioral control with three items (e.g., “I am confident that I can obtain SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing if I wanted to”), and intentions with three items (e.g., “I expect to obtain SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing”). Composite scores were computed by averaging items within each subscale.

Receptivity to salivary SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing. A saliva-specific TPB-based measure was developed by modifying items from the general antibody testing measure. Attitudes, normative beliefs, perceived behavioral control, and intentions to be screened were evaluated using adapted items with instructions to consider salivary SARS-CoV-2 antibody collection specifically. For example, the item “I am confident that I can obtain SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing if I wanted to” was revised to “I am confident that I can obtain salivary SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing if I wanted to.”

Activation of rural discrimination-related cognitions. A rural-adapted measure of anticipated rural discrimination [40] assessed activation of rural discrimination-related concerns specific to SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing. Anticipated rural discrimination, while related to medical mistrust, represents a distinct construct capturing momentary, context-activated expectations of experiencing discrimination in this particular health decision, rather than general discrimination experiences. This brief measure [40] included three items about rural discrimination's impact on SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing benefits, value, and accuracy. Three items were then presented: “In some way, my obtaining a SARS-CoV-2 antibody test would be impacted by rural discrimination,” “Rural discrimination would undermine the value of SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing for me,” and “Rural discrimination would negatively impact the accuracy of my SARS-CoV-2 antibody test”. Responses used 7-point Likert scales ranging from 1 (strongly agree) to 7 (strongly disagree), with composite scores calculated by averaging across the three items. While this measure was originally developed to assess anticipatory racism in African American populations [40], we adapted it to assess rural discrimination by substituting ‘rural discrimination’ for ‘racism’ throughout the items. We use ‘discrimination’ rather than ‘racism’ because our sample consists of White rural Americans, and discrimination more broadly encompasses unfair treatment based on various social identities, including rural identity and its associated stereotypes and structural disadvantages. We will assess the adapted measure's psychometric properties, including internal consistency reliability, in this White rural sample and report these findings in a subsequent manuscript reporting the trial results.

Behavioral outcome measures. Two behavioral measures served as primary outcomes: acceptance of the no-cost, home-based salivary antibody testing program (yes/no) and full participation among those who opted in (i.e. attendance at virtual appointment and test kit completion). Participants who opted in were contacted within one week to schedule a virtual appointment. After three unsuccessful contact attempts, participants were classified as nonresponsive.

Salivary antibody and inflammatory profile outcome measures

SARS-CoV-2 antibodies were measured in GCF and whole saliva using multiplex assays. GCF is well-suited for serological testing due to its serum-like composition enriched with antibodies [43,47]. Primary analyses focused on GCF antibody measurement, with whole saliva assays serving as exploratory comparisons.

GCF Multiplex SARS-CoV-2 Assay. SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in GCF were measured using a novel multiplex assay developed by the JHU-EHMIL lab. Full details are provided elsewhere [34,48]. The assay utilizes Luminex xMAP technology to detect IgG antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 antigens, including spike protein (S), nucleocapsid protein (N), and receptor binding domain (RBD) [48].

Whole Saliva Multiplex SARS-CoV-2 Assay. Whole saliva samples were analyzed using the Salimetrics SARS-CoV-2 IgG Antibody 3-Plex Assay at SalivaLab. Full details of this assay are also provided elsewhere [34]. In brief, this electrochemiluminescence-based assay detects IgG antibodies against three SARS-CoV-2 antigens: nucleocapsid protein (N), spike 1 protein (S), and spike 1 receptor binding domain (RBD). Each 25 μL sample was tested in duplicate with an average < 15% coefficient of variation. The assay demonstrates a 96.5% clinical sensitivity and 98.8% specificity.

Salivary Cytokine Testing. Whole saliva samples were analyzed for a 12–cytokine panel at Salimetrics SalivaLab. The primary focus was a 4-plex panel including interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin-8 (IL-8). Eight additional cytokines were measured: interferon gamma (IFN- g), interleukin-2 (IL-2), interleukin-5 (IL-5), interleukin-7 (IL-7), interleukin-10 (IL-10), interleukin-12p70 (IL12p70), interleukin-13 (IL-13), and interleukin-17A (IL-17A). All cytokines were assayed in duplicate using proprietary electrochemiluminesence methods developed and validated for saliva by Salimetrics, with <15% coefficient of variation. Data analysis plan

Our first aim is to compare general versus rural targeted didactic videos on receptivity to and uptake of home-based salivary antibody testing. Primary analysis will assess mean differences between rural residents assigned to the general video (group 1) or the rural targeted video (group 2). For continuous self-report outcomes, including Theory of Planned Behavior and rural-adapted anticipatory racism measures, independent samples t-tests will be conducted with Cohen's d effect sizes to assess practical significance. Subsequent analyses will explore sociodemographic variables and individual differences (e.g., gender, rural identity) as potential moderators of group differences using hierarchical moderated multiple regression. For dichotomous antibody test kit acceptance and completion outcomes, chi-square tests of independence will be performed. Our second aim is to assess activation of rural discrimination-related cognitions in response to standard and rural targeted messaging. Accordingly, analyses will evaluate whether rural discrimination mediates differences in testing receptivity and behavior between study arms using linear multiple regression and established mediation procedures [49]. Multiple mediator models will explore additional pathways, with structural equation modeling used for complex models [50]. Our third aim is to evaluate the efficacy and fidelity of home-based salivary antibody testing in rural contexts, from kit distribution through sample collection, return, and successful laboratory analysis. The study will assess feasibility, efficacy, and fidelity by measuring kit receipt, return, and successful completion rates, comparing those within and across video conditions, and examining how rural identity and attitudes may moderate these outcomes.

Discussion

This RCT contains several innovations that can promote better understanding and addressing rural health behavior disparities. Key among them is that we will consider whether a non-invasive, home-based screening method can be coupled with rural targeted education and communication to potentially address a pernicious rural health disparity that characterized the COVID-19 pandemic. Home-based screening modalities are thought to play a pivotal role in rural healthcare disparities by making testing and treatments more accessible. Home-based screening also carries a potential to alleviate the deleterious influences of hesitancy and mistrust in health resource uptake among rural communities. Yet, home-based screening programs remain relatively underutilized. Our trial will consider whether home-based screening for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies is acceptable and feasible, and ultimately practical with an eye towards current and future pandemic preparedness. Equally important, this trial will consider whether rural targeted communication can augment receptivity and uptake of home-based COVID-19 screening in a defined rural community. Without intentional equity-focused implementation, serology testing runs the risks of exacerbating rather than reducing health disparities in underserved populations [51]. Rural targeted communication is a low cost and highly actionable strategy that can be readily coupled with many health outreach programs. Along these lines, the present trial will explicitly test whether ‘overcoming injustice’ messaging that has been used in other culturally targeted messaging programs may be adapted to rural healthcare contexts. Such messaging may be especially well suited to rural contexts because it emphasizes on personal control and community benefit, rather than relying primarily on appeals to trust medical authorities or comply with public health directives. Thus, the current trial will explicitly consider whether acknowledging legitimate concerns about institutional failures while empowering rural communities to take actions serving their own interests is an effective messaging approach. Finally, the proposed trial will consider whether activation of medical mistrust and rural discrimination thoughts and feelings function as mechanisms that link didactic interventions to subsequent health resource uptake, and whether these mechanisms are mutable to rural targeted messaging. Attending to mechanistic functions of hesitancy and mistrust carries a potential to inform a broad range of rural health interventions, particularly as declining public confidence in U.S. health institutions may raise new questions about who bears the cost and who benefits from participation in home-based testing programs.

This trial has several potential limitations that suggest both a cautious interpretation of eventual findings and potential future directions. First, the proposed trial is focused on a single rural area in northern Michigan. Although rural communities share many commonalities, they are also highly diverse across a range of sociodemographic and cultural characteristics [52]. The generalizability of our findings to other rural regions and communities is likely to be limited. Second, the proposed trial is focused on a single antibody measurement occasion that was taken at a particular point in time during the COVID-19 pandemic. Future studies should consider sustained participation in serological testing programs, including in non-pandemic times, which may inform population-level serosurveillance goals and objectives that are likely important to future pandemic preparedness. Future implementation of home-based antibody testing programs should also include greater integration with primary care providers to facilitate appropriate follow-up and integration into clinical care pathways. Third, this study only examines a single rural targeted messaging strategy, and it does not consider individually tailored communication strategies, which can complement group targeted approaches [29,41]. Although the present evaluation is a useful starting point, future research should consider alternative rural targeted messaging, and also evaluate whether layering multiple approaches further enhances antibody testing participation in rural communities. Fourth, this study uses an adapted measure of rural discrimination based on a scale originally developed and validated to assess anticipatory racism in African American populations [40]. We will assess the psychometric properties of this adapted measure in our rural sample and report these findings in a subsequent manuscript reporting the trial results, although future research should further develop and validate measures specifically designed to assess rural discrimination and rural-related medical mistrust. Limitations acknowledged, this trial carries implications for policy and practice. Results will help illuminate whether rural targeted communication and home-based salivary screening can be coupled to promote serosurveillance efforts in rural communities that are often hesitant and hard-to-reach. The proposed trial will also help illuminate whether mistrust and discrimination-oriented cognitions are important and amenable to rural communication interventions to promote healthy behavior. Finally, although the proposed trial is focused on SARS-CoV-2 serosurveillance, results can help inform the use of other home-based screening program in rural communities. This includes programs for serosurveillance of other illnesses that rural communities may be especially vulnerable to, and also non-communicable illnesses and preventive screening, including those related to cardiovascular and cancer screening.
