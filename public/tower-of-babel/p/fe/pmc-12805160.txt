Isradipine enhancement of virtual reality cue exposure therapy is effective for individuals with higher baseline cue-induced craving
Authors: Santiago Papini, Cara C Young, Richard A Brown, Haruka Minami, Hitoshi Morikawa, Michael W Otto, John D Roache, Jasper AJ Smits
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEvidence based treatments for smoking cessation have high recurrence rates. Targeting cue-induced craving, a strong predictor of smoking recurrence, may be critical to promoting sustained abstinence. We previously found that isradipine, an FDA-approved antihypertensive, enhanced the effect of virtual reality cue exposure therapy (VR-CET) on cue-induced craving. In this secondary analysis we tested whether this augmentation strategy was more beneficial for participants with high (relative to low) baseline cue-induced craving.MethodsAfter a 24-h abstinence challenge, participants (N = 78) completed a single session of VR-CET with isradipine or placebo, and returned for a 24-h follow-up to repeat the procedure in a medication-free state. We conducted a moderator analysis to test the hypothesis that the effect of isradipine on cue-induced craving at follow-up would be larger among participants with higher (relative to lower) baseline cue-reactivity.ResultsIn the model of cue-induced craving at follow-up, the Group × Baseline cue-reactivity interaction was significant, p = .045. Among participants with higher baseline cue-induced craving, isradipine resulted in a large, significant reduction in mean craving in the follow-up session (M difference = −18.17, 95 % CI [−31.38, −4.95], p = .01, d = −1.46). Among participants with lower baseline cue-induced craving, results were not significantly different across groups (M difference = 1.38, 95 % CI [−12.98, 15.75], p = .85, d = 0.11).ConclusionsResults suggest isradipine enhances VR-CET, particularly for individuals with higher baseline levels of cue-induced craving. Future studies testing prevention strategies that target higher cue-induced craving with isradipine to reduce rates of smoking recurrence are warranted.Keywords: Cue exposure therapy, Cue-induced craving, Cue reactivity, Virtual reality, Precision medicine

Introduction

Cigarette smoking is the leading cause of preventable death in the United States, accounting for nearly half a million deaths each year (Le et al., 2024; National Center for Chronic Disease Prevention and Health Promotion (US) Office on Smoking and Health, 2014). While strides have been made in reducing rates of tobacco use with evidence-based treatments, even those that are highly effective for achieving abstinence have recurrence rates over 70 % (Healton and Fiore, 2008). There is a critical need to develop strategies that prevent smoking recurrence and promote sustained abstinence (Piper et al., 2020).

Conditioning theories emphasize the central role of environmental cues in the activation of powerful cravings to smoke even after periods of abstinence (Conklin and Tiffany, 2002). Cue-induced craving, or the activation of an urge to smoke upon encountering a smoking-related stimulus, is a robust predictor of smoking recurrence (Vafaie and Kober, 2022). Cue exposure therapy (CET), which is designed to extinguish cravings in response to smoking-related cues, targets cue-reactivity directly, yet results have been mixed, spurring research on pharmacologic approaches to enhance extinction (Luo et al., 2023). L-type calcium channels (LTCC) are a promising pharmacologic target because they regulate synaptic plasticity in the brain, which is a key neurobiological mechanism involved in the acquisition and modification of drug-cue associations (Degoulet et al., 2016). A promising approach to targeting LTCCs involves isradipine (ISR), which has facilitated long-term extinction of cue-drug associations in rodent experiments (Addy et al., 2018, Degoulet et al., 2016), and is already FDA-approved to treat hypertension in humans.

Recent findings suggest the promise of an isradipine-augmented CET protocol for reducing cigarette craving among adults who smoke (Young et al., 2024). Specifically, we conducted a triple-blind, randomized placebo-controlled trial that combined isradipine with virtual reality cue-exposure therapy (VR-CET) targeting cue-induced craving. Adults that reported smoking (N = 78) were randomized to receive VR-CET + isradipine (n = 40) or VR-CET + placebo (n = 38). Rather than having an acute effect on craving levels, isradipine was hypothesized to facilitate the consolidation of extinction of cue-elicited craving. This hypothesis was tested by measuring craving levels in a medication-free follow-up conducted 24-hours after the treatment visit, where the isradipine (vs. placebo) group had significantly lower mean craving across cue-exposure trials (d = −0.42, p = 0.046).

An important aim of treatment development for this novel combination intervention is to identify moderators (Kraemer et al., 2002). Previous research has shown that (1) the slope of cue-induced craving across six CET sessions is steeper among adults that smoke who present with higher cue-induced craving as compared to those with lower cue-induced craving (Unrod et al., 2014) and (2) medications used in conjunction with treatments that target extinction consolidation can offer more benefit for patients who have more room for improvement (Kleine et al., 2012, Otto et al., 2019, Siegmund et al., 2011). This empirical evidence is consistent with the clinical intuition that the more severe the target problem is, the more potent the treatment needs to be in order to address it. Therefore, we tested the moderator hypothesis that isradipine enhancement of VR-CET would be greater for participants with higher baseline cue-induced craving relative to participants with lower baseline cue-induced craving.

Methods

Overview

We conducted a secondary analysis of data from a triple-blind randomized controlled trial that tested pharmacologic augmentation of virtual reality cue exposure therapy for tobacco craving. Here we summarize the methods that are described in detail in the protocol and primary outcome results publications (Papini et al., 2020, Young et al., 2024). All procedures were approved by The University of Texas at Austin’s Institutional Review Board and the trial was registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03083353).

Participants

Participants were recruited from January 2020 to July 2023 from the Austin, TX, USA area. Key inclusion criteria were at least one year of daily cigarette use, current use of 5 cigarettes per day, and successful completion of a 24-h abstinence challenge prior to randomization. Exclusion criteria focused on contraindicated conditions for isradipine, concurrent treatment for smoking cessation, and severe psychiatric conditions. A detailed list of eligibility criteria is provided in the primary outcomes publication (Young et al., 2024).

Procedures

Since the treatment is designed to be delivered in the early stages of abstinence, eligible participants completed a treatment visit after completing a 24-h biochemically verified abstinence challenge, and were instructed to remain abstinent through the study period. During the treatment visit they were randomized to take either 15 mg immediate release isradipine or placebo. After a 75-m waiting period, participants began VR-CET delivered through an Oculus Go VR headset. Participants saw written descriptions of three immersive 360° video environments and were instructed to select the one most likely to activate the strongest cravings. Each scene presented a variety of dynamic smoking-related cues (e.g., individuals actively smoking in a bar) for 3 min during which participants also handled a pack of cigarettes. At the end of the scene, participants provided a rating of subjective craving, and the scene was repeated for a total of 10 trials. The VR-CET procedure was repeated in a medication-free state during a 24-h follow-up visit.

Measures

Throughout the study, self-reported craving was measured on a scale from 0 (no craving) to 100 (strong craving). This method for measuring craving was selected because meta-analyses of cue-reactivity research suggest self-reported craving is more a sensitive and robust index of craving than physiological responses (Carter and Tiffany, 1999) and self-reported craving (including cue-induced craving) during the early stages of abstinence is a robust predictor of smoking recurrence (Vafaie and Kober, 2022).

In the current secondary analysis, baseline cue-induced craving is the craving rating after the first cue-exposure trial in the treatment visit. This rating captures participants’ first exposure to the VR environment, and our primary outcome analyses showed robust activation of craving relative to a pre-cue-exposure baseline. Although the average rating was high across groups (isradipine M = 82.53 SD = 18.49; placebo M = 80.50, SD = 22.64), the left-skewed distribution of ratings had a median of 90. Thus, for the current analysis, baseline cue-induced craving was dichotomized using a median split, with ratings at or above 90 classified as higher (n = 42; 53.8 %) and ratings below 90 classified as lower (n = 36, 46.2 %). Of note, participants classified as “lower” still had relatively high baseline cue-induced craving (M = 63.66, median = 70, range: 15–85). During the follow-up visit, participants repeated the VR-CET procedure in a medication-free state; the 10 craving ratings served as the outcome of the current analysis.

Data analysis

We followed the same pre-registered analytic plan reported in the primary outcomes publication and added baseline cue-induced craving as a moderator in the analysis. The effects of Group (isradipine vs. placebo), Trial (1–10; linear and quadratic term), Baseline Cue-induced Craving (Higher vs. Lower), and Group × Baseline Cue-induced Craving were estimated in a mixed model with a random intercept and pre-specified covariates (sex and baseline non-cue-induced craving during the follow-up visit). Non-cue-induced craving was measured prior to exposure to any cue; adjusting for this in the analyses isolates the residual craving that is reported after exposure to a cue (i.e., cue-induced craving). Group differences in non-cue-induced cravings were non-significant (p = .41), which suggests that the effect of isradipine is specific to cue-induced craving.

The primary outcome analysis found a main effect of Group such that on average and across trials, Isradipine had significantly lower craving than Placebo. In the current analysis, the effect of interest is the Group × Baseline Cue-induced Craving interaction, which can indicate whether isradipine augmentation is more effective at high (relative to low) levels of baseline cue-induced craving. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.2) and followed intention-to-treat principles by including all randomized participants and using multiple imputation to account for missing follow-up data. Estimated mean differences are reported with 95 % CIs and Cohen’s d effect sizes, with a significance threshold of p < .05.

Results

Consistent with the expectation that isradipine does not have an acute effect on craving, groups did not significantly differ in the proportion of participants with baseline cue-induced craving that was either higher (isradipine n = 20, placebo n = 22) or lower (isradipine n = 20, placebo n = 15), X2 (1, N = 77) = 0.36, p = .57.

In the mixed model of craving outcomes at follow-up, the Group × Baseline Cue-induced Craving interaction was significant, B = -19.55, 95 % CI [−38.67, −0.43], p = .045. Fig. 1 shows the model-estimated group differences in craving across the 10 trials of follow-up split by level of baseline cue-induced craving. Among participants with higher levels of baseline cue-induced craving, isradipine resulted in a large, significant reduction in mean craving across the 10 trials (isradipine M = 37.16, SE = 5.01 vs placebo M = 55.32, SE = 4.60; M difference = −18.17, 95 % CI [−31.38, −4.95], p = .01, d = −1.46). Conversely, differences among participants with lower levels of baseline cue-induced craving were not significantly different across groups (isradipine M = 41.45, SE = 4.83 vs placebo M = 40.07, SE = 5.66; M difference = 1.38, 95 % CI [−12.98, 15.75], p = .85, d = 0.11).

Discussion

We previously found that a single dose of isradipine enhanced VR-CET’s efficacy for reducing cue-induced craving in the initial stages of abstinence. In this secondary analysis, we tested the putative moderating effects of baseline cue-induced craving. Consistent with the hypothesis, higher baseline cue-induced craving was associated with a large effect of isradipine. Conversely, isradipine did not significantly impact craving reduction among participants with lower baseline cue-induced craving.

This treatment development research is guided by strong evidence suggesting cue-induced craving is an important target for preventing smoking recurrence (Vafaie and Kober, 2022) and that CET can engage this target (Conklin and Tiffany, 2002). Keeping in mind public health significance, it is prudent to develop an intervention that is efficacious, brief and judicious. Growing research suggests that VR may increase CET efficacy (Malbos et al., 2023, Schröder et al., 2024). Cognitive enhancers that improve the consolidation of extinction have the potential to reduce the number of CET sessions required to achieve the desired outcome. The findings from this secondary analysis suggest that baseline cue-induced craving could identify individuals who would benefit from a pharmacological adjunct to VR-CET; future research is needed to determine whether this targeted approach would translate to lower rates of smoking recurrence.

We note several limitations. The moderator effects should be replicated in larger samples, which would allow for testing a wider range of craving levels and identifying an optimal threshold for isradipine, which may be different than the 90 level used in the median split analysis. Larger samples would also provide the opportunity to identify predictors of baseline cue-induced craving, including sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. Although meta-analysis indicates self-reported craving is a robust predictor of smoking recurrence (Vafaie and Kober, 2022), there are no established guidelines for what constitutes a clinically meaningful reduction in craving; thus, additional research is needed to determine whether the large effect sizes observed in our analyses translate to reduced rates of recurrence.

Consistent with the aforementioned limitations, we have several recommendations for future research, including: 1) fine-tune the intervention parameters (e.g., number of VR-CET trials, isradipine dosing); 2) determine the optimal timing of the intervention relative to the quit attempt (e.g., before quit, 24-hr after quit); and 3) examine effects in conjunction with additional smoking cessation strategies (e.g., nicotine replacement therapy). Should the findings observed in this study be replicated and extended in that work, developing easy-to-implement methods for identifying patients with higher cue-induced craving will facilitate the clinical significance of this research.

In sum, isradipine is a promising pharmacological adjunct to VR-CET among individuals with high baseline cue-induced craving. Given that cue-induced craving is associated with increased risk for smoking recurrence during initial abstinence, future studies testing strategies that target higher cue-induced craving with isradipine and examine the impact on recurrence are warranted.
