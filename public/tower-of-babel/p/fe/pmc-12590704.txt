A randomized control trial comparing Falls Reduction for Elderly Emergency Department (FREED) interventions and usual care
Authors: Jiraporn Sri-on, Kanokporn Pongvirat, Sukkhum Rujichanantakul, Adisak Nithimathachoke, Thanathip Pholphijit, Yupadee Fusakul, Shan Woo Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study evaluated the effectiveness of a Falls Reduction for Elderly Emergency Department (FREED) intervention in reducing recurrent falls among older adults presenting to the emergency department (ED) after a fall at 6 months.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial conducted in an ED in Bangkok, Thailand, included patients aged ≥ 60 years who had experienced a fall in the previous 7 days. The patients were randomized to receive the FREED intervention or usual care, including a systematic fall risk assessment, medication review, vitamin D supplementation, physical therapy referrals, and home environment assessment. The primary outcomes analysis included the intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analyses.ResultsAfter excluding 1,026 ineligible individuals, 216 patients (median age: 75 [interquartile range: 69–81] years were enrolled, 108 in the FREED intervention and 108 in the usual care arm. Six-month follow-up data were available for 97% of the participants. The intergroup difference in repeated falls (primary result) was not significant (intervention group: 20.4%, control group: 25.0%; the observed absolute risk reduction of 4.6% was not statistically significant (95% confidence interval: -15.8% to 6.5%, p = 0.42). At 6 months, activities of daily living (ADL) scores decreased very slightly in both groups, with no between-group differences.ConclusionThe FREED intervention was feasible and acceptable among older ED patients who had fallen but did not significantly reduce repeated falls at 6 months. Fall prevention in the ED can be challenging, and multicenter studies with longer follow-up periods are required to further explore the impact of ED-initiated fall prevention interventions.Clinical trial numberTCTR20180522002.Trial registrationThe trial was registered in the Thai Clinical Trial Register on 22 May 2018.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-025-01383-w.Keywords: Fall, Emergency department, Older adult

Background

Falls are a major public health concern in older adults. Every year, one in three community-dwelling older individuals (aged >65 years) falls in the United States (US) [1–4]. Falls can have various effects, ranging from minor to lethal, on older adults. There is well-documented evidence of health reduction, social isolation, higher chance of nursing home admission, restricted activity, and confidence loss after the first fall [1, 2, 5–8]. Major injuries occur in 10% of falls [9]. Furthermore, more than 10,000 deaths and 2.6 million nonfatal injuries occur from falls among older adults annually [10]. Moreover, falling is a major risk factor for future falls [11–13] and contributes to overall morbidity.

Because fall-related emergencies will likely increase with population aging, the emergency department (ED) is potentially an important setting for intervention as it optimizes teachable moments for patients [14, 15]. Implementing fall reduction strategies in older patients could affect downstream care, prevent future ED visits, and ultimately, be cost-effective [16–18]. A fall necessitating an ED visit is a sentinel event requiring a sentinel response. The ED typically concentrates on injury management and does not systematically examine patients’ multifactorial fall risk [17, 19, 20] or ascertain injury root causes. Consequently, it is often isolated from the fall management continuum and misses key opportunities to prevent future falls [20–22]. Patients with falls are ideal candidates for innovative fall prevention interventions. According to studies, 14–48% of elderly patients discharged from the ED after a fall experience another fall within 6 months [17, 23, 24]. A meta-analysis showed multifactorial interventions significantly reduced fall rates (rate ratio [RaR]: 0.76, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.67 to 0.86; 9,503 participants, 19 trials) [25], although the interventions were not conducted in the ED. The Geriatric Emergency Department Guidelines outline how to conduct multifactorial evaluations in elderly patients with falls [26]. However, despite well-established fall evaluation guidelines, they are variably implemented in the ED [27]. In some cases, ED clinicians do not know how to intervene after a fall assessment. Others have found that the interventions are time-consuming and too impractical for a busy ED [17, 28, 29]. Successful implementation requires a new approach involving greater coordination across settings and providers. The Geriatric Acute and Post-acute Fall Prevention Intervention (GAPcare) [30], The Sentinel fall presenting to the emergency department (SeFallED) protocol [31], and Block the Fall, a wearable device-based fall detection system [32], are examples of tools for identifying and preventing falls among older adults in the ED. Despite promising protocols, effective fall prevention strategies in EDs lack consistent, scalable implementation. Most interventions remain in early evaluation stages, with limited evidence on real-world feasibility or integration into routine workflows. This study addresses these gaps to improve fall prevention and downstream care for older ED patients. We propose taking advantage of the unique opportunity of the ED to reduce falls and impact downstream care among older patients who have experienced falls.

We conducted an efficacy study using a randomized controlled trial design to explore whether a checklist-based novel multifactorial ED fall intervention could reduce recurrent falls among older ED patients who have experienced falls.

Methods

Study design

This was an efficacy randomized controlled trial comparatively assessing a Falls Reduction for Elderly Emergency Department (FREED) intervention and usual care for older ED patients who have experienced falls. The hospitals averages 52,000 annual ED visits. This study was approved by the hospital’s Institutional Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects. The trial was registered in the Thai Clinical Trial Registry (TCTR identification number: TCTR20180522002). Our study adheres to CONSORT guidelines.

Selection of participants

Adults aged 60 years [33] and older who had experienced a fall in the previous 7 days and had a hospital beneficiary registration were recruited from an academic ED in Bangkok, Thailand. The patients were recruited on weekdays (from 0800 to 1600 h) from May 1, 2019, to April 30, 2022. Patients were excluded if they had acute severe conditions, such as acute myocardial infarction, acute stroke, or acute pulmonary embolism; had severe trauma, including lower extremity or spinal fractures; had moderate-to-severe head injury (defined as a Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS] score of < 14); had severe cognitive impairment (defined as a score of >12 on the 6-Item Cognitive Impairment Test [6CIT]); or did not have a hospital beneficiary registration. The trial registry stated a planned recruitment of 400 participants and a 12-month follow-up period; however, due to recruitment challenges during the COVID-19 pandemic, the study enrolled 216 participants with a primary endpoint at 6 months.

Study protocol

Intervention

The study aimed to determine whether the FREED intervention, implemented by research staff, decreases the proportion of 6-month recurrent fallers compared with patients who receive usual care among older patients who present to the ED after a fall (Table 1). The FREED checklist was adapted from the intervention developed by Tinetti et al. and the STEADI (Stopping Elderly Accidents, Deaths, and Injuries) initiative [34–37] and developed based on evidence-based principles, ED practices, and general guidelines [35, 37–45]. It was created by a multidisciplinary team that included input from geriatricians, ED physicians and researchers, primary care physicians, and physiatrists and considers the unique ED environment. This efficient checklist intervention covers four important aspects of fall risk and management (medications, orthostatic hypotension, gait/strength/balance, and home safety) and uses the ED to connect older patients to the necessary multiple care settings for successful fall prevention using the most practical high-yield elements (Table 1).

Usual care in the ED

Usual care in our ED included established fast-track protocols for acute conditions such as stroke, myocardial infarction, and syncope, which could indirectly address some fall-related causes. However, no standardized protocol existed for fall evaluation in older adults; assessments were instead performed at the discretion of the attending physician.

Two research assistants (RAs) enrolled the patients and performed screening daily. The RAs were trained before the study.

RA training process

The RAs underwent 6 h (h) of intensive training, which included 1 h of study using the RA manual before class. The Principal Investigator (PI) delivered a 2-h lecture on fall risk assessment. Thereafter, the RAs spent 1 h using the fall assessment manual under the PI’s guidance. The RAs then practiced assessments through simulated patient scenarios for 1 h. Finally, the PI observed the RAs as they performed fall risk assessments for ED patients.

Recruitment process

The screening process was conducted after emergency physicians (EPs) determined patient disposition and care plans but before the patients were discharged from the ED. Trained RAs screened the ED patient population for any community-dwelling older adults who complained of falls by asking clinicians if there were any such older patients. The RAs then conducted the 6-CIT and determined whether the patient could consent or if a family member needed to provide consent. The RAs then conducted a comprehensive fall evaluation adapted from the American Geriatric Society (AGS)/Geriatric ED guidelines and the STEADI program [26, 27, 34]. For those in the intervention arm, the RAs implemented the FREED intervention. Accordingly, they communicated with the primary physician regarding medications and referred patients to evidence-based outpatient physical therapy (PT) programs and community-based case management involving home safety evaluation by visiting nurses, depending on risk factors.

Randomization and marking

The patients were randomized into two groups for the fall reduction program by using a web-based independent randomization application to ensure allocation concealment. The RAs opened the group assignment envelope after the patient signed an informed consent form and assigned the patient to their group. The primary physician communicated with Group 1 regarding medications. Furthermore, this group received referrals to evidence-based outpatient PT programs and community-based case management involving home safety evaluation by visiting nurses. Group 2 received standard care in the ED.

Sample size calculation

This sample size was calculated to compare the proportion of the sample size, where, n refers to the number of samples in each group. The statistical significance level (α) was set at 0.05, so Z₁-α/₂ = 1.96. The test power was set at 80%. In this case, the ratio was set as 1. The proportions of samples in both groups were based on a previous study [35], which found that the incidence rates of repeated falls after 1 year in the experimental and control groups were 32% and 46%, respectively. These values were used to calculate the sample size. The calculated sample size was 190 participants per group.

Outcome measurements

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients with one or more recurrent falls during the 6-month period between the group that received the novel FREED intervention and the group that received usual care in the ED. Patients were followed up in hospital at 1 month to evaluate treatment adherence, recurrent fall rate, activities of daily living (ADL), and the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test. Further assessments were conducted via telephone interviews by RAs at 3 and 6 months. To minimize recall bias, a structured questionnaire was used during these regular calls. During scheduled follow-up calls, patients were systematically assessed for activities of daily living (ADL), occurrence of recurrent falls, omission of medications identified as potentially high-risk, continued use of calcium and vitamin D supplements, attendance of visiting nurse services, and ongoing participation in prescribed rehabilitation sessions.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as medians (interquartile range [IQR]) and categorical variables as frequencies with percentages. Differences in continuous and categorical variables between the two groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, respectively. The primary analysis of the primary outcome was an intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis. The difference in the proportion of recurrent fallers between the groups was assessed with a chi-square test, with the risk difference and 95% confidence interval (CI) reported. For the 7 patients lost to follow-up in the ITT analysis, the outcome was defined as “no fall,” a conservative approach for handling missing data. A per-protocol (PP) analysis was also conducted, excluding patients who were lost to follow-up. A secondary analysis also examined fall recurrence based on the number of intervention components received. The proportion of differences in fall recurrence between the intervention and control groups was calculated using the chi-square test, and 95% CIs were calculated. All p-values reported are two-sided. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Stata version 18 (Stata Corp., College Station, Texas), USA) was used for all analyses.

Results

Characteristic of study subjects

Over the 3-year period, 1,026 patients had fallen within seven days and visited the ED. The reasons for exclusion is presented in Fig. 1 Finally, 216 patients were eligible to participate in the study. In total, 108 patients were included in each group. Seven patients in the intervention group did not finish their 6-month follow-up: six (5.56%) were lost to follow-up and one (0.93%) died. In the usual-care group, three patients (2.78%) did not complete their 6-month follow-up: one (0.92%) were lost to follow-up and two (1.85%) died.

Recruitment of subjects

The median patient age was 75 years (IQR: 69–81 years); 65% were women. The baseline characteristics were well-matched between the groups. Both groups had the following comorbidity burden and functional status: a median Charlson Comorbidity Index of 4 and median baseline ADL score of 70. Prior to the index fall, 51.4% of participants reported an additional fall within the past year. Notably, the only significant baseline disparity was in the scores on cognitive screening tests; the intervention arm had slightly better cognition (median 6-CIT score 6 vs. 8 in controls, p = 0.003), although the number of patients with moderate cognitive impairment was small. Overall, the study population consisted of high-risk older adults with multiple fall risk factors (such as gait instability, polypharmacy, and a history of falls), with no significant differences observed between groups (Table 2).

Implementation of the intervention and proportion of recurrent falls at 6 months

All 108 patients in the intervention arm underwent an initial risk factor evaluation and focused interventions per the checklist. The most common risk factors identified were the use of inappropriate drugs (defined as psychoactive/sleep-aiding medications) and lack of vitamin D supplementation. Specifically, 27 (25%) patients were taking psychoactive or sedative drugs that prompted medication adjustment recommendations from the primary physician, and of those not already taking vitamin D (n = 62), 37 (59.7%) were recommended 1000 IU of vitamin D daily. Orthostatic hypotension was identified in 12 patients (11%) upon ED presentation; these patients were counseled regarding fluid intake and compression stockings. Nearly all intervention patients accepted referrals for further fall prevention interventions: 82 patients (75.9%) were referred to an outpatient PT program for gait/balance training, and 103 (95.4%) were referred for home safety assessment by a visiting nurse and educational handouts on fall-proofing the home. Intervention patients were also interviewed at 1-, 3-, and 6-months after the ED visit by the RAs to remind them of these interventions and inquire about any barriers. The control group received routine ED discharge care without anticipatory fall-related interventions. No adverse events related to the study interventions were reported.

Analysis of the recurrent fall rate at 6 months via the ITT analysis revealed that 22 patients in the intervention group (20.4%) had experienced at least one fall, whereas 27 patients in the control group (25.0%) had experienced falls. This equated to the observed absolute risk reduction of 4.6% was not statistically significant (95% confidence interval: -15.8% to 6.5%, favoring the intervention but not significantly (p = 0.42). On per-protocol analysis (excluding seven patients with unknown outcomes), the same was observed: 21.6% of the patients in the intervention group versus 25.2% in the control group experienced recurrent falls (difference, 3.7%; 95% CI, 15.1% to 7.8%; p = 0.53) (Table 3). For patients using psychoactive or sedative medications or requiring calcium supplementation, we specifically inquired whether high-risk medications had been discontinued and whether calcium or vitamin D supplements were continued. All patients reported ongoing use of calcium and vitamin D supplements and appropriate discontinuation of high-risk medications. Regarding rehabilitation, patients attended an initial session, and 70% reported continued adherence to prescribed exercises, with non-adherence primarily attributed to subsequent falls or intercurrent medical conditions. Thus, the FREED intervention did not result in a striking variation in the percentage of recurrent falls in older adults at 6 months compared with those who received usual ED care. A multifactorial FREED intervention rollout was achieved with an extensive uptake of the recommended components. Despite high compliance with the intervention protocol, the recurrent fall rates were not significantly different between the two groups (Table 4). At 6 months, activities of daily living (ADL) scores decreased very slightly in both groups, with no between-group differences (Additional file 1).

Discussion

In this randomized trial, the FREED intervention was successfully implemented in a busy ED setting but did not lead to a statistically significant reduction in the proportion of older adults experiencing recurrent falls at 6 months compared to usual care. This result is contrary to our original hypothesis and the findings from non-ED environments, where multifactorial interventions for falls significantly reduced fall rates [25].

Falls are among the main healthcare issues faced by older adults. Prior studies have shown that 14–48% of older individuals discharged from an ED after experiencing a fall may experience another fall within 6 months [16]. Our recurrence rates (23% all-cause) fell midway in this range, suggesting that the control group rate was as expected for a high-risk group. Multifactorial community and primary care programs for fall prevention reduce falls by 25–30% [46–48]. However, these interventions are typically linked to longitudinal follow-ups and coordinated care, which may explain why our single-encounter ED intervention failed to have a similar effect. Although the trial was initially planned to recruit 400 participants with a 12-month follow-up, only 216 participants were enrolled due to COVID-19–related recruitment challenges, with the primary endpoint assessed at 6 months. The FREED intervention, adapted for the emergency care setting, aligns theoretically with established multifactorial fall-prevention models such as STEADI [32]. While STEADI implementation in outpatient clinics has significantly reduced fall-related hospital admissions [47], differences in our findings may reflect the emergency care context, smaller sample size, and shorter or less intensive follow-up. The FREED intervention was an attempt to bridge this gap by utilizing research-trained personnel to conduct interventions and follow-up, which added feasibility. Indeed, the high intervention uptake rate in this study means that an ED fall prevention program can be implemented without workflow interruption, a conclusion consistent with the GAPcare pilot study in the US, in which the inclusion of a pharmacist and PT consultation in the ED did not add to the length of ED stay and was acceptable to both patients and clinicians [30] The FREED intervention builds on this concept by targeting a set of risk factors using a single checklist. However, unlike a primary care model in which professionals iteratively monitor and update a fall prevention plan, our ED-based intervention was largely a one-time assessment with referrals. The observed reduction in falls suggests that ED intervention alone may have been too subtle to counteract the multifactorial determinants of fall risk over time. Of note, nearly all patients who received the intervention complied with at least some suggested referrals (as evidenced by 96% completion of home visits); however, this did not yield a significant outcome difference. The inability to detect a significant gain from the FREED intervention program is not indicative of ineffective fall prevention but of the difficulty of reducing falls in the acute care environment. One comforting result was that the fall rate was slightly lower in absolute terms in the intervention group, indicating a potential benefit that could be revealed in a larger study.

Limitations

Our study had several limitations that may explain the absence of an apparent intervention effect, which should be considered when interpreting the results. The trial was conducted at a single academic medical center in Bangkok during the daytime on weekdays. This may limit the generalizability to other settings or rural hospitals and may have led to a selection bias because of the exclusion of patients who presented on weekends or nights (who might have differed in terms of social support or injury severity). Data collection was limited to weekends, reflecting the availability of research assistants, who were essential for participant recruitment.

Slightly more than 21% of those screened were enrolled in the study. Many individuals declined to participate (31% of those who were eligible), possibly because of a lack of perceived need or reluctance to engage in follow-up interventions, and may further limit generalizability. The reasons for refusal were not systematically collected.

This trial was likely underpowered to have picked up a small-to-moderate effect size, having recruited more than 200 patients. A small absolute risk reduction (~ 4–5%) would have required a much larger sample to be significant; thus, our negative result may be a Type II error (false negative) if there is indeed a true effect. Furthermore, it is a positive sign that 75% of patients were referred to PT; however, the adherence to PT remains unclear after PT first visit.

The FREED intervention was pragmatically tailored to focus on modifiable fall risk factors that could be feasibly addressed in the ED without disrupting acute care workflows. While this design ensured applicability in a high-throughput clinical environment, it did not comprehensively address all potential multifactorial contributors to falls, such as cardiovascular and neurological conditions, which were excluded in this study due to their acute and life-threatening nature. This pragmatic tailoring, while necessary for feasibility, may limit the intervention’s breadth compared with guideline-based multifactorial strategies, and should be considered when interpreting the findings. The FREED intervention addressed four key areas of fall risk; however, there are others (such as vision, footwear, and vitamin B12 levels) that are outside its scope, and we did not perform an analysis adjusted for the baseline imbalance in cognitive scores. In addition, some patients in the intervention arm had only one identified risk factor category (8% received a single intervention); thus, their falls could have been due to causes not readily modifiable by our protocol. Home safety evaluations in this study were conducted by visiting nurses rather than occupational therapists. Previous research suggests that home safety interventions may be more effective when delivered by occupational therapists, and this difference in provider expertise may have limited the potential impact of the intervention on reducing fall risk.

Many patients may not have continued therapy after the ED visit, despite our follow-up calls. Moreover, the outcome measurement depended on the self-reporting of falls during follow-up phone calls, which is susceptible to recall bias or underreporting (although we attempted to minimize this by making regular check-in calls). Future studies might benefit from more frequent contacts.

Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable data regarding the feasibility of implementing fall prevention programs in EDs. Our findings identify several areas for future research and fall prevention strategy improvements in ED patients. Larger multicenter trials are needed to determine whether the modest reduction in falls achieved with the FREED intervention can reach significance with adequate power.

Conclusion

In this pilot randomized controlled trial, we evaluated the efficacy of a FREED intervention in a Thai ED setting, as reflected by high patient acceptance of referrals, but did not lead to a significant reduction in the 6-month recurrent fall rates compared with usual care. The findings highlight the challenges of initiating fall prevention in the acute care setting and underscore the need for larger, multicenter trials to determine if a modest benefit exists and to explore ways to better integrate ED-based efforts with longitudinal community care.
