Association between Precipitation Events, Drought, and Animal Operations with Campylobacter Infections in the Southwest United States, 2009–2021
Authors: Erika Austhof, Heidi E Brown, Alice E White, Rachel H Jervis, Joli Weiss, Sarah Shrum Davis, Delaney Moore, Kristen Pogreba-Brown
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Weather variability is associated with enteric infections in people through a complex interaction of human, animal, and environmental factors. Although Campylobacter infections have been previously associated with precipitation and temperature, the association between precipitation and drought on campylobacteriosis has not been studied.Objective:Using data from Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico, and counties in Utah, this ecological study aimed to assess the association between precipitation and the incidence of campylobacteriosis by county from 2009 to 2021 and to determine how this association is modified by prior drought level and animal operations.Methods:We merged 38,782 cases of campylobacteriosis reported in 127 counties with total precipitation (in inches), temperature (in average degrees Fahrenheit), Palmer Drought Severity Index (PDSI, category), and animal census data (presence, density per square mile) by week from 2009 to 2021. Negative binomial generalized estimating equations adjusted for temperature with a 3-wk lag were used to explore the association between precipitation on campylobacteriosis with resulting incidence rate ratios (IRRs). Stratified analyses explored the association with precipitation following antecedent drought, presence of farm operations, and animal density.Results:A 1-in (25.4 mm) increase in precipitation was associated with a 3% increase in campylobacteriosis reported 3 wks later (IRR=1.03; 95% CI: 1.02, 1.04) after adjusting for average temperature and PDSI. Compared with normal conditions, there were significantly more cases when precipitation followed antecedent extremely wet (IRR=1.15; 95% CI: 1.04, 1.26), very wet (IRR=1.09; 95% CI: 1.01, 1.18), moderately wet (IRR=1.06; 95% CI: 1.01, 1.12), moderate drought (IRR=1.11; 95% CI: 1.07, 1.16), and severe drought (IRR=1.06; 95% CI: 1.02, 1.11) conditions, whereas there were significantly fewer cases (IRR=0.89; 95% CI: 0.85, 0.94) for antecedent extreme drought. Compared to counties with no animal operations, counties with animal operations had significantly more cases following precipitation for every PDSI category except extreme drought. Counties with a higher density of beef cattle, goats for meat, chicken broilers, and chicken layers had significantly higher rates of campylobacteriosis following precipitation than those with no such operations, whereas those with dairy cattle and goats for milk, did not.Discussion:In this majority arid and semiarid environment, precipitation following prior wet conditions and moderate and severe drought were significantly associated with increased rates of campylobacteriosis, and only in prior extreme drought did rates decrease. Where the precipitation fell made a difference; after precipitation, counties with farm operations had significantly more cases compared to counties without farm operations. Further work should assess individual-level risk factors within environmental exposure pathways for Campylobacter. https://doi.org/10.1289/EHP14693

Introduction

In the United States, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimate Campylobacter, a bacteria, causes 1.5 million infections each year.1 The majority of Campylobacter infections (90%) are due to one species, C. jejuni. Although Campylobacter infections are thought to be predominantly due to foodborne exposures, some have estimated that 30%–50% of C. jejuni infections may be a result of infection through environmental exposure pathways.2 The bacteria is well adapted to survive between 37°C and 42°C, but it is extremely sensitive to dry weather and ultraviolet radiation.3 The incidence of Campylobacter typically peaks in late spring and early summer; however, the reasons behind this variation are multifaceted and not well understood.4 Previous studies in the United States5 and internationally6–8 have shown an increased risk of Campylobacter following extreme precipitation (risk estimates ranging from 1.11 to 1.52), whereas others have found no association.9,10 Further, Austhof et al. summarized what is known about the weather and Campylobacter association in a systematic review, finding precipitation, temperature, relative humidity, and sunshine were correlated with Campylobacter infections.11 However, research presenting estimates of risk on the association between weather events and Campylobacter infections in different areas of the world are still needed.

Wet weather conditions can promote Campylobacter survival, and the clay soils typically found in arid environments increase the risk of the bacterial spread during surface water flow events because of their small pore structure and low water infiltration.12 Four states in the Southwest United States (Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico, and Utah) comprise a majority arid and semiarid climate. These states have also experienced a long-term drought,13 which is expected to increase in its duration and intensity due to climate change in the Southwest.14,15

Drought has been linked to numerous health effects, including decreased access to safe food and water.16 Intensification of drought can lead to shortages of clean drinking water, causing a shift to water sources not suitable for human consumption, and reuse of water sources for landscaping (reclaimed water), agriculture (recharged ground water), and consumption by animals.16–18 Decreased water availability and water quality emerge during drought, which can result in the accumulation and concentration of pathogens in both ground and surface water sources. For instance, in the United States drought is a concern for private well owners because there is already a noted association with the prevalence of wells and increased rates of campylobacteriosis.19 However, the specific mechanisms of how drought could lead to enteric illnesses in humans remains elusive owing to its indirect and prolonged nature.

The association between drought and diarrheal illnesses has not been explored fully in the research literature. A systematic review by Levy et al.18 found only three articles, with disparate results including a dose–response relationship with drought-related water restrictions in Wales,20 an outbreak of E. coli O157:H7 in Africa following rainfall preceded by drought,21 and a study in Africa with no correlation between diarrhea and drought.22 In a more recent study using data from developing countries, Wang et al.17 reported stronger positive associations following heavy precipitation events preceded by dry conditions compared with wet conditions in the Malnutrition and Enteric Disease Study, although pathogen-specific estimates were not explored.

A leading hypothesis in this area, the concentration–dilution hypothesis, may explain this variation in estimates.23 The concentration–dilution hypothesis posits that “rainfall following dry periods can flush pathogens into surface water, increasing diarrhea incidence, whereas rainfall following wet periods can dilute pathogen concentrations in surface water, thereby decreasing diarrhea incidence.”23 Although studies have examined temperature and precipitation variability on Campylobacter incidence, to our knowledge, no studies have explored the association between antecedent drought preceding extreme precipitation events on campylobacteriosis.24

Natural reservoirs for Campylobacter include wild animals (birds, boars, and wild pigs), farm animals (poultry, cattle, goats, sheep, and pigs), and domesticated animals (cats and dogs).25,26 Flies are also important carriers for Campylobacter given that they are able to contaminate both humans and animals and are especially active during the summer months when their larvae grow and mature.27 Numerous articles have shown that the presence and density of chicken and poultry operations, ruminant operations, and the presence of slaughterhouse(s) are associated with increased Campylobacter cases in studies, as well as three outbreaks of Campylobacter.11 The majority of agricultural land in the Southwest United States is used for grazing livestock, and there are numerous large animal operations across the region.

The objective of this study was to assess the association between precipitation and drought on the incidence of Campylobacter in the Southwestern United States from 2009 to 2021. We hypothesized that precipitation would be associated with an increase in Campylobacter incidence and that this relationship would be modified by antecedent drought level. Further, we aimed to explore whether the presence or density of animal operations was associated with Campylobacter infections following precipitation while accounting for antecedent drought level.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a county-level ecological analysis of public health surveillance data in four US states: Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico, and Utah (Figure 1). These states are large in land area of 683,615 km2 (total 424,779 mi2)28 with a total estimated human population of >18.3 million.29 From 2009 to 2019, Campylobacter cases generally increased in these four states (as seen in Figure S1), with fewer cases reported in 2020, likely due to the COVID-19 pandemic.30 Although drought intensified in the region over the study period,14,15 precipitation varied significantly within each state due to elevation and topographical features.31,32 Compared with other regions in the United States, the Southwest has not experienced an increase in total precipitation nor in extreme precipitation events.31,32 However, when extreme precipitation events do occur, they can cause catastrophic damage due to high surface water flow over poor-absorbing soils.31 Although all four states have majority arid or semiarid climate zones, Colorado, New Mexico, and Utah also have continental climate zones.33

Data Sources

Public health surveillance.

In the United States, Campylobacter infections are reported by health care providers to local, state, and territorial public health departments when a patient receives a positive laboratory test for Campylobacter spp. from a clinical specimen, usually stool. Passive surveillance for Campylobacter has been ongoing since 1993,34 and active surveillance began in 2015 when it became a nationally notifiable disease.35 In some states, local health departments receive case reports directly, whereas in other states, all cases are reported to the state health department and then shared with local health departments for follow-up.36

States provided de-identified daily reports of all confirmed, probable, and suspect Campylobacter spp. cases aggregated by county: Arizona, 15/15 counties in 2009–2020; Colorado, 64/64 counties in 2009–2021; New Mexico, 33/33 counties in 2009–2021; and Utah, 15/29 counties in 2012–2021. Owing to data suppression requirements in Utah, only cases reported in counties with >20,000 in population were included. Data on case demographics were not provided, thus demographics were not possible to explore. Case definitions vary between states, Arizona, Colorado, and Utah use the CDC and Council for State and Territorial Epidemiologists case definition, and New Mexico uses the FoodNet case definition (Table S1).35,37,38 We did not exclude outbreak-associated cases or those with recent travel because doing so would have resulted in a loss of data by restricting the analysis to those with a case investigation. Including these cases would likely increase confidence intervals (CIs) around estimates but not change them significantly. Reports of cases that were missing county information (n=4 for Colorado only) were dropped from the analysis. Daily data were aggregated to week of the year (starting on Sunday) for a total of 52 or 53 wk, depending on leap years, using the lubridate39 week() function in R Studio (version 4.2.2; Posit PBC). Data were declared as a time series in Stata (version 17; StataCorp) using tsset prior to analyses and using a 3-wk lag from exposure to report to the health department to explore the acute association between precipitation and infections. Based on stakeholder feedback from health department coauthors, we chose a 3-wk lag owing to the pathogen’s incubation period and estimated times to test and report results to the local health departments.

Weather and climate.

We obtained weather data from the Parameter-elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes Model (PRISM),40 including daily county-centroid (county center grid cell) minimum, maximum, and average precipitation (in inches) and minimum, maximum, and average temperature (in degrees Fahrenheit). PRISM is grid based and interpolates weather station data within an area, so in larger counties this may capture a more complete picture of precipitation and temperature on a given day owing to its use of both satellite and weather station data. To aggregate weekly data, average precipitation values for all days in a week were summed to determine the total precipitation in a week, and the average temperature values for all days in a week were averaged. The weekly minimum or maximum was determined by imputing the smallest and largest values in a week. We defined three extreme precipitation exposure events: a) heavy precipitation (defined as total precipitation in the week exceeding the 30-y PRISM average from 1990 to 2020), b) total weekly precipitation above the 95th percentile for the study period, and c) total weekly precipitation above the 99th percentile for the study period.

We obtained Palmer Drought Severity Index (PDSI) by month for each county in a climate division from the National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) via the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA).41 PDSI estimates the relative soil moisture conditions in the current month and in the previous nine months on a rolling basis using water balance equations that account for precipitation, evapotranspiration, water runoff, and available soil water capacity of the soil layer, resulting in an index (categories: extremely wet, ≥4; very wet, 3–3.9; moderately wet, 2–2.9; normal, −1.9 to 1.9; moderate drought, −2 to −2.9; severe drought, −3 to −3.9; and extreme drought, ≤−4).42 The magnitude of the index value in a given month indicates the extent of departure from average conditions of the previous nine months. We categorized the continuous PDSI value provided by NOAA, into the above categories using the category “normal” as reference for analyses.

Animal data.

We obtained the number of farm operations with sales in a county and density of animals from the US Department of Agriculture (USDA) Farm 2012 and 2017 censuses using USDA’s Quick Stats 2.0.43 For presence, we used the number of farm operations with sales defined as a farm operation that produced and sold at least $1,000 of agricultural products during a given year. We then categorized whether a county had those animal operations or not. Owing to differences in estimates between presence data (e.g., when comparing “goats and sheep” to “sheep only”), we explored density of animal types. For density, we extracted the number of livestock reported for poultry (broilers, layers) sheep including lambs, goats (for meat, dairy), and cattle (beef, dairy, total with calves). For density, animal count data were kept continuous and were transformed to the number of animals per square mile of the county to allow for comparisons across animal types. A list of data sources and metadata are available in Table S2.

Variables

Our exposure of interest was total precipitation (continuous) in a county in a week. Our outcome was the total count of confirmed, probable, or suspect Campylobacter infections reported in a county in a week. We considered temperature to be a confounder given previous research and included average temperature as a covariate to adjust for seasonality. We hypothesized that antecedent drought via the PDSI and presence of farm operations were both effect modifiers given the concentration–dilution hypothesis for drought and prior research for chicken and cattle operations.

Statistical Analysis

We used negative binomial generalized estimating equations (NBGEEs)44,45 to investigate the relationship between precipitation and counts of reported Campylobacter cases using an autoregressive correlation structure. We tested inclusion of population in our model in numerous ways, all of which led to nonconvergence and unstable estimates; we therefore chose to not include population in the model given these findings. We tested for spatial autocorrelation using Moran’s I for a subsample of years within each state and found no spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I=−0.04 to 0.05; p<0.00). The results of different model testing using Pan’s Quasi likelihood information criterion and Moran’s I statistics are shown in Tables S3 and S4.

The NBGEE model yields incidence rate ratios (IRRs). Thus the final model can be interpreted as a percentage increase or decrease in the number of human Campylobacter spp. case events following a 1-in (25.4 mm) increase in total weekly precipitation 3 wk prior, adjusted for average temperature 3 wk prior.

We conducted stratified analyses to explore effect modification by antecedent drought level, presence of animal operations, and density of animals. For PDSI, we estimated the association between precipitation and cases, stratified by each PDSI category using the normal category as the reference category. For presence of animal operations, we estimated the association between precipitation and cases, comparing counties without those operations to counties with those operations. For animal density, we estimated the association between precipitation and cases for a 1-unit increase in density of each type of livestock.

Datasets were merged and transformed in R (version 4.2.2; R Development Core Team) using the following packages: readxl,46 dplyr,47 purrr,48 readr,49 lubridate,39 data.table,50 stringr,51 or tidyr.52 Data were analyzed using Stata (version 17; StataCorp).

Sensitivity Analyses

We conducted numerous sensitivity analyses, chosen a priori, including exploring the association at different spatial scales (state, climate zone, rural vs. urban classifications), using a different drought index [the US Drought Severity Monitor (USDM)53], adjusting for the Southern Oscillation Index (SOI), and restricting analyses to cases reported prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. In addition, to explore the association with extreme precipitation events, not just total precipitation, we explored heavy precipitation above a 30-y average (relative for each county), total weekly precipitation above the 95th percentile (>1.19 in, 30.2 mm), and above the 99th percentile (>2.1 in, 53.3 mm) calculated across all states for the time period of interest. We estimated the association with cases comparing counties that experienced these precipitation events compared with counties that did not. Results for all models and these sensitivity analyses are available in Excel Table S1.

IRB and Data Availability

This work was reviewed and deemed exempt, minimal risk human subjects research by the University of Arizona Institutional Review Board (#00001514). Data and code are available via the research compendium on GitHub: https://github.com/austhofe/RainDroughtCampy/.

The greatest number of farm operations by type were in the following states: cattle and sheep in New Mexico, poultry in Colorado, and goats and sheep in Arizona (maps available in Figures S2–S5).

A 1-in (25.4 mm) increase in precipitation was associated with a small, yet significant, 3% increase in all Campylobacter cases 3 wk later (IRR=1.03; 95% CI: 1.02, 1.04) after adjusting for average temperature (lag 3 wk) and PDSI in the current and previous nine months (Figure 2, Table 2). When examining total precipitation above the 99th percentile, we found a greater risk of Campylobacter cases 3 wk later compared with counties without these events, although the results were not statistically significant. We found no statistically significant differences for heavy precipitation (>30-y average) and total precipitation exceeding the 95th percentile. In a post hoc analysis of only laboratory-confirmed Campylobacter cases, we found that a 1-in (25.4 mm) increase in precipitation was associated with a 7% increase in Campylobacter cases (IRR=1.07; 95% CI: 1.05, 1.10; Table S1).

Antecedent conditions were an effect modifier of the relationship between precipitation and Campylobacter cases. Compared with normal conditions, a 1-in (25.4 mm) increase in precipitation in counties experiencing antecedent (nine months prior) extremely wet (IRR=1.15; 95% CI: 1.04, 1.26), very wet (IRR=1.09; 95% CI: 1.01, 1.18), and moderately wet (IRR=1.06; 95% CI: 1.01, 1.12) conditions was associated with significantly increased rates of Campylobacter cases (lag 3 wk). Following precipitation, counties experiencing antecedent moderate drought (IRR=1.11; 95% CI: 1.07, 1.16) and severe drought (IRR=1.06; 95% CI: 1.02, 1.11) had a higher rate of Campylobacter cases, whereas in the most extreme drought conditions, rates were significantly lower (IRR=0.89; 95% CI: 0.85, 0.94; Figure 2).

Although not completely linear, as the PDSI category moved from extremely wet to extreme drought, IRRs also decreased. This association (higher rates following wet conditions, which decreased with increasing drought) remained consistent regardless of the spatial scale we explored, although most results were not statistically significant [state (Arizona, Colorado), climate zone, urban vs. rural (US Census), and urban–rural scheme (NCHS); Table S1]. State-level analyses showed significantly increased associations (compared to the crude analysis) for a 1-in (25.4 mm) increase in precipitation for cases in Utah counties with >20,000 people, with the greatest change in risk observed following severe drought (IRR=1.47; 95% CI: 1.30, 1.66) and decreased associations for rates in New Mexico although not all were significant (IRR crude= 0.99; 95% CI: 0.95, 1.04; Table S1). The results for sensitivity analyses restricting analysis to the years prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, adjusting for SOI, and using a different drought index (USDM) remained similar (Table S1). For the USDM analysis, following precipitation, rates increased with antecedent abnormally dry and moderate drought and were significantly decreased in antecedent severe to exceptional USDM drought categories (Table S1).

Presence of farm operations was a significant effect modifier of the relationship between precipitation and Campylobacter cases. Compared with counties with no such operations, counties with cattle, chicken and poultry, and sheep only had significantly more Campylobacter cases following a 1-in (25.4 mm) increase in precipitation, whereas counties with goats and sheep operations had significantly fewer cases (Table 3). The effect of drought on this relationship was consistent with our primary analysis; compared with counties without these operations, counties with these farm operations had higher IRRs than the crude IRR in all PDSI categories except for extreme drought (Table 3). For example, following precipitation, counties with chicken and poultry operations had 2.33 times the rate of counties with no chicken and poultry operations (IRR=2.33; 95% CI: 2.22, 2.46), with the greatest difference in risk observed when precipitation fell during extremely wet conditions (IRR=2.70; 95% CI: 2.44, 2.99; Table 3).

Next we explored the association for a 1-in (25.4 mm) increase in precipitation accounting for the density of different types of animals. We found that counties with animals used for meat production had higher IRRs than counties with animals for dairy and egg production (Figure 3). Density of beef cattle in a county was positively associated with cases following precipitation in each of the PDSI categories except extreme drought, whereas density of dairy cattle was not associated with greater risk. Density of goats for meat in a county was positively associated with case rates following precipitation in each of the PDSI categories, whereas density of dairy goats was associated with lower risk. Both density of broilers and density of layers within a county were associated with a significantly higher risk of campylobacteriosis; however, density of chicken layers (egg production) had a slightly smaller estimate than did density of chicken broilers (meat production).

Discussion

This analysis explored the association between precipitation and Campylobacter infections in four states in the Southwest United States from 2009 to 2021. We found that a 1-in (25.4 mm) increase in weekly precipitation resulted in a small, but significant increase in the rate of campylobacteriosis while adjusting for temperature and drought. Further, this association was modified by antecedent drought level; precipitation following wet conditions was associated with more cases (6%–15%), whereas precipitation following extreme drought was associated with fewer cases by 11%. There are several likely explanations for these results. Precipitation events, especially following wet conditions, may result in more rapid surface water transport of pathogens in the environment.12 The results for moderate and severe drought categories may partially support the concentration–dilution hypothesis23 or be explained by precipitation events (e.g., first flush, monsoons) in the early summer months delivering a concentrated dose following initial stages of dry conditions. For extreme drought, higher temperatures combined with low relative humidity creates conditions where Campylobacter is less likely to survive in the environment and therefore may not be transmitted as readily.3,54 Associated differences in cases may also be explained by unmeasured factors resulting in behavior differences, such as outdoor recreation or meals in fair weather,55 and socioeconomic or demographic factors, which were not explored here.56

These results are the first, to our knowledge, reporting the association between precipitation and drought on Campylobacter infections. However, our results can be compared with one other drought-related study. Although Wang et al.17 explored the drought-related diarrheal incidence using a longer-term drought index (6- to 24-months), their 6-month estimate of diarrheal risk for children <5 years of age in a global cohort study were similar to our results found here. Specifically, they found that diarrheal risk following a 6-month mild drought was 1.07 (95% CI: 1.05, 1.09) and for a 6-month severe drought was 1.11 (95% CI: 1.08, 1.14). We found similar results using PDSI (typically considered drought in the previous 9-months). However, our model explored total precipitation during different drought periods and for the total population (not just children), which may explain some of the variation in estimates.

We explored how the association between precipitation and case rates might differ in counties depending on animal presence and density, finding a statistically greater increase in counties with farm operations, compared to counties without. Notably, counties with chicken operations (broilers and layers), beef cattle, and goat meat operations were associated with significantly increased case numbers following precipitation events. Our results for presence and density of chicken operation analyses are similar to those of Poulsen et al.5 who found that as the number of extreme precipitation events increased, the odds of Campylobacter infections were 1.52 times higher in the highest quartile of poultry operation activity (number operations, distance to patient residence, number of poultry) compared with the lowest quartile (95% CI: 1.11, 2.09). Campylobacter contamination to the wider environment from chicken and poultry farms is most likely to come from other domesticated animals living around the farm, their feces, and human movement on the farm via transportation and contact with poultry.57 Our analysis did not explore transmission routes, so whether the Campylobacter risk to humans came from improper handling, undercooked poultry at consumption, or via contamination from environmental exposure pathways is unclear.

Similarly, our results for cattle and goat operations are consistent with those of Arsenault et al.,6 who found higher Campylobacter incidence in areas with a high density of ruminants (≥20 per km2) for most age groups. For the ruminant operations we explored, beef cattle, total cattle (including calves), and goat meat animal density, all were associated with more campylobacteriosis cases following precipitation, all with large effect sizes. We did not see the same association for milk ruminants (dairy cattle and goat milk), a finding that is a novel contribution to the research literature. These results demonstrate that there might be something specific to meat ruminant operations vs. dairy ruminant operations that may drive environment–exposure pathways or increase pathogen load in ruminants. Campylobacter prevalence in cattle feces is high for both dairy (29%; 95% CI: 23%, 26%)58 and beef cattle (5%–61.3%)59–64 depending on herd size, husbandry practices, and type. Observed variation in Campylobacter prevalence among operation type may be due to geographic location,60 presence and contact with wild birds,65–67 or increased density with combining individual livestock from multiple herds contributing to transmission potential during transport, finishing, and slaughter.61,68 In addition, Arsenault et al. also cautioned against interpreting estimates for ruminant density at a scale larger than census tract, as is the case here, owing to potential collinearity in poultry and ruminant operations.6 In our data we found a large overlap, with 54% and 98% of counties with chicken operations also having cattle or goat operations, respectively. Thus it is unclear whether the results reported here are a cumulative effect from both operations or not.

One of the strengths of this study is its contribution to our knowledge of the association between precipitation and campylobacteriosis while accounting for antecedent drought. Previous studies in the extant literature have noted this as a limitation. Our results do not support the concentration–dilution hypothesis.23 Rather, our results showed higher risk during wet, moderate, and severe drought conditions, and only in the most extreme drought conditions was risk lower. Our results are in alignment with the stratified analysis of Kraay et al., which showed that bacterial diarrhea was more common during rainy seasons when pooling data from studies in a meta-analysis (IRR=2.70; 95% CI: 1.60, 4.40).23 This difference in findings surrounding a dilution effect may speak to the importance of developing pathogen-specific estimates in understanding the risk posed by weather on different enteric illnesses. We found consistent results for both the PDSI and the USDM; however, there are many ways to measure drought and dry conditions, which should be explored further.69 The PDSI is good for integrating conditions over seasonal and longer-term timescales (∼9 months) and provides a relative estimate as a deviation from normal conditions. However, monitoring drought in the Southwest United States is difficult owing to the region’s low annual precipitation overall. The variation in estimates reported here may also highlight the importance of regional differences because the climate and topology of the Southwest United States is atypical; wet conditions and surface water flow events look very different in an arid and semiarid climate compared with a temperate, continental, or tropical climate. Finally, the attenuation of estimates at the extreme drought stage may speak to overall human behavior changes during these weather conditions that typically coincide with extreme heat, dry conditions, and fire restrictions. These behavior changes include less exposure to the outdoor environment, as well as potential changes to foods and diet.

Although our study has many strengths, there are limitations. First, this was an ecologic study and we run the risk of falsely attributing what is occurring at a county level to the individuals within the county. Data were not available at a finer spatial resolution for this initial analysis. County-level analyses are beneficial and useful for public health in determining if there is any association, and whether to conduct further analyses to identify populations at greater risk of enteric illness. Next, the weather data from PRISM is not county based but, rather, grid based, so it incorporates station data from the surrounding area, regardless of county boundaries. These stations are weighted by their physiographic similarity to the grid cell being modeled.40 We chose PRISM for this region because it provides a better estimate of the weather occurring in the county than if we had used one weather station (which can be biased by location within a county). However, there could be potential exposure misclassification as a result of using this data given the variability in county sizes across the states and attributing precipitation values from the county-centroid to the whole county. Future research could use subcounty-level surveillance data to match the PRISM grid to identify areas of particularly high vulnerability. Another limitation is the absence of potential demographic characteristics of cases and county-level socioeconomic indicators. We also used report date instead of onset date for this analysis given data confidentiality restraints. This allowed us to use a larger dataset, given that onset date is usually collected only during case interviews and interview rates vary by state. We accounted for the delays with typical reporting in this region by using a 3-wk lag, but future research could also explore different time lags or monthly level analyses. In addition, we also did not exclude outbreak-associated cases or cases with recent travel owing to data restraints.

On the other hand, the model we chose to use for this analysis is a great strength because it takes into account the nonindependence of our outcome data, as well as the correlation between our weather variables. However, we were unable to account for population using this model owing to nonconvergence and unstable estimates. This may be because the timescale was too short, or the scale of some of the counties was too large, particularly for Arizona, given that there are only 15 counties that divide the nation’s sixth largest state by area. Future studies could explore different time and spatial scales with population included. Further, model results for Utah should be interpreted with caution given that half of the counties were included in the analysis owing to data suppression requirements and thus the findings may not be generalizable to the whole state. Finally, the USDA Animal Census occurs every 4–5 y, and not every state in this analysis reported counts each year. This may have resulted in an underestimation in counties without data and an overestimation in counties with some but not all data. Future research could consider using alternate sources for animal data, although at the time of this writing, few are available at the spatial scale needed to pair with public health surveillance data.

Conclusion

In this study we found that Campylobacter infection incidence was higher following a 1-in (25.4 mm) increase in weekly precipitation while adjusting for temperature and drought. We also found that this relationship was modified by antecedent conditions, with the greatest incidence for precipitation following extremely wet conditions. We showed that where the precipitation fell also matters; counties with farm operations had significantly more cases following heavy precipitation compared with counties without, and as the density of animals used for meat within a county increased, so did the number of Campylobacter infections following precipitation. These results contribute to our knowledge about the many ways that weather variability affects our health. With projected climate change, as drought becomes more severe and extreme weather events become more common, counties may see increases in infections with precipitation following wet periods and a decrease in infections during extreme drought conditions.
