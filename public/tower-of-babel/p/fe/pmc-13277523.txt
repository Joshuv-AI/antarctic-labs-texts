Characteristics and Risk Factors of Infections among Patients with Autoimmune Bullous Diseases: A Retrospective Single-Center Study in China
Authors: Feiran Zhou, Xiaoping Zheng, Yixuan Yang, Qingluan Yang, Jingyu Zhou, Haoxin Xu, Xiaoqun Luo, Lingyun Shao, Wenhong Zhang, Zhonghua Li, Qiaoling Ruan, Haijuan Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Since infections can lead to adverse outcomes in autoimmune bullous diseases (AIBD), this study aimed to analyze infection characteristics in AIBD, predict infection risk factors and provide probable suggestions to infection prevention, which will spot a light on early discovery and prevention of infections in AIBD patients.Methods:This is a retrospective study of the medical records of inpatients diagnosed with AIBD at the Department of Infectious Diseases and Dermatology, Huashan Hospital from January 2017 to December 2021. We collected patients’ clinical manifestations and laboratory examination results. Risk factors for infections were evaluated using multivariate logistic regression.Results:A total of 263 AIBD patients were included in the study. The overall incidence of infection was 42.2% (111/263). Pemphigus had a higher infection rate (74/142, 52.1%) than bullous pemphigoid (37/121, 30.6%). Among the patients with infections, 49.5% (55/111) had bacterial infections, 13.5% (15/111) had fungal infections, 2.7% (3/111) had virus infections, and 34.2% (38/111) had mixed infections. Staphylococcus aureus and Candida were the most common pathogens in localized infections, while Cryptococcus and Candida were predominant in systemic infections. In the multivariate logistic model, pemphigus (odds ratio [OR] = 2.56, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.41–4.63), hypoalbuminemia (OR = 3.78, 95% CI 1.68–8.50), and systemic glucocorticoid treatment (OR = 4.67, 95% CI 2.51–8.68) were independent risk factors for infection in AIBD patients. Bullous pemphigoid (OR = 3.99, 95% CI 1.03–15.45), high-dose (average > 24 mg/day) compared to low-dose (average ≤ 6 mg/day) and medium-dose (average 6–24 mg/day) systemic glucocorticoid treatment (OR = 19.31, 95% CI 3.32–112.28, and OR = 5.71, 95% CI 1.37–23.79) were associated with increased risk factors of systemic infection in AIBD patients with systemic glucocorticoid treatment. A methylprednisolone dose averaging 14 mg/day over the past three months had a 75.0% positive predictive value for infections in AIBD patients.Conclusions:The incidence of infection in AIBD patients is high, with a broad spectrum of pathogens and infection locations. AIBD patients undergoing systemic glucocorticoid treatment, especially those receiving high doses in the previous three months, should be cautious about infection.Keywords: Pemphigus, Bullous pemphigoid, Glucocorticoids, Infections, Retrospective studies

1. Introduction

Autoimmune bullous diseases (AIBD) are rare cutaneous diseases induced by autoantibodies, causing vesicles and bullae on the skin and mucosal surface, of which the most common types are pemphigus and bullous pemphigoid. The incidence of pemphigus is 0.76–16.1 cases per million.[1] Pemphigus is induced by autoantibodies against intercellular desmosome antigens desmoglein 3 and desmoglein 1. Bullous pemphigoid has an incidence of 6–13 cases per million and is most common in adults over 60 years of age.[2] Bullous pemphigoid is caused by autoantibodies against basement membrane zone hemidesmosome bullous pemphigoid antigens 1 (BPAG1 or BP230) and bullous pemphigoid 2 (BPAG2 or BP180).[2,3]

Infection is an important complication of pemphigus and bullous pemphigoid, often leading to severe adverse outcomes and even mortality.[4,5 and 6] AIBD patients are particularly vulnerable to infection due to three primary mechanisms.[7,8] First, the skin and mucosa are first-line barriers against pathogens. The rupture of blisters provides a direct pathway for pathogens to invade the skin and mucosal surfaces, while blister exudate nourishes microorganisms to colonize the surface. Second, as autoimmune diseases, pemphigus and bullous pemphigoid cause immune system disorders, increasing susceptibility to infections. Third, treatment with glucocorticoids and other immunosuppressive agents may further compromise immune function, heightening the risk of infection.

Infection not only worsens the prognosis of AIBD patients, but also necessitates increased antibiotic use. Therefore, exploring effective strategies for early detection and even infection prevention among these patients is important. Previous studies[1,9,10,11,12,13 and 14] analyzing risk factors of infection in pemphigus and bullous pemphigoid were limited by either small sample sizes or insufficient comparison of infection types and have paid less attention to the risk factors, especially the classification of glucocorticoid dosage. This study addressed these gaps by analyzing the characteristics of infections in AIBD patients, including the pathogen spectrum and the location of infections, and by identifying predictive factors for infection risk factors.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Ethics approval

This retrospective, single-institution study was approved by the ethics committee of Huashan Hospital (KY2024-1021) and was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of Huashan Hospital because of the retrospective nature of the study. The data of each patient were handled anonymously. All human data of patients’ records were confirmed for collection in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

2.2. Study population and definitions

This study retrospectively enrolled inpatients diagnosed with pemphigus or bullous pemphigoid from January 2017 to December 2021 at the Department of Infectious Diseases and Dermatology, Huashan Hospital, Shanghai, China. Pemphigus and bullous pemphigoid are diagnosed by clinical manifestations, supportive histopathology, positive direct immunofluorescence microscopy, indirect immunofluorescence microscopy, or serological reactivity with desmoglein 1, desmoglein 3, BP180, or BP230. Infections were diagnosed based on clinical manifestations and laboratory examinations, including radiology, pathogen culture of blister fluid, blood, urine, sputum, or cerebrospinal fluid, and molecular biological tests. Localized infections mainly involved surface wounds, skin and soft tissue, oral cavity, and nails. In contrast, systemic infections mainly include respiratory tract infections and pneumonia, central nervous system infections, bloodstream infections, and urinary tract infections.

2.3. Data collection

The demographic and clinical data were collected from patient medical records, including gender, age, body mass index (BMI), disease diagnosis, infection condition (infection or non-infection, infectious pathogens, localized or systemic infection), complications (including diabetes mellitus, hypoalbuminemia), glucocorticoid treatment, other immunosuppressive agent treatment, and outcome. The infection location and pathogen spectrum were counted by cases of each specific category, such as respiratory tract infection or Staphylococcus aureus infection. Symptoms were recorded at the time of infection. Laboratory results were obtained from the day of infection diagnosis. When data were unavailable for the same day, laboratory data within three days were used.

The treatment history of glucocorticoids was of particular concern. The collected data included glucocorticoid type, dosage, and time duration. The cumulative dosage of glucocorticoids three months before infection diagnosis was collected as an index of glucocorticoid usage. Glucocorticoid doses were converted to methylprednisolone equivalents. According to the European League Against Rheumatism’s standard for glucocorticoid dosage, ≤540 mg cumulative dose of methylprednisolone in the previous three months (average ≤6 mg/day) was considered as low dose, 540–2160 mg (average 6–24 mg/day) was a medium dose, and >2160 mg (average >24 mg/day) was a high dose.[15]

2.4. Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using the Statistical Program for the Social Sciences (SPSS) (version 26.0, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.3.1) software. Comparisons based on infections were evaluated using Student’s t-test, Wilcoxson Mann-Whitney test for continuous variables, chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Risk factors for infections and systemic infections were evaluated using multivariate logistic regression. The cutoff value for glucocorticoid dose and positive predictive value (PPV) were evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. P-values were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 263 AIBD inpatients were enrolled: 142 with pemphigus and 121 with bullous pemphigoid. Among them, 47.1% (124/263) were female and 52.9% (139/263) were male. Overall, 42.2% (111/263) of patients developed infections after diagnosis, with 66.7% (74/111) in pemphigus patients and 33.3% (37/111) in bullous pemphigoid patients. Moreover, 17.1% (45/263) patients had localized infections, and 24.7% (65/263) patients had systemic infections, while one patient’s infection location remained unclear and recovered after anti-infection treatments. Among these 111 infected patients, one patient could have multiple localized infections or systemic infections. Localized infections accounted for 46.5% (72/155) cases, specifically wound surface, skin and soft tissue infection of 39.4% (61/155), oral infection of 3.2% (5/155) and nail infection of 1.9% (3/155). In comparison, systemic infections accounted for 52.9% (82/155) cases, respiratory tract infection and pneumonia accounted for 34.8% (54/155), brain infection accounted for 6.5% (10/155), bloodstream infection accounted for 6.5% (10/155), and urinary tract infection accounted for 3.9% (6/155). One hundred fifty-six patients were treated with systemic glucocorticoids, with an infection incidence of 57.1% (89/156). Forty-nine (31.8%) patients used an equivalent ≤540 mg (average ≤6 mg/day) cumulative dose of methylprednisolone in the previous three months, 65 (42.2%) patients used 540–2160 mg (average 6–24 mg/day) and 34 (22.1%) patients used >2160 mg (average > 24 mg/day). Data on cumulative doses of methylprednisolone was missing for eight patients.

3.2. Pathogen spectrum of infections

Of the 111 patients with infections, 15 (13.5%) had fungal infections, 55 (49.5%) had bacterial infections, three (2.7%) had virus infections, and 38 (34.2%) had mixed infections, as shown in Figure 1A. Among these 111 infected patients, one patient could have multiple infection pathogens and locations, causing a total of 202 bacterial, virus and fungal infection cases occurred. Taken different infection locations into consideration, 224 localized and systemic cases of bacterial, virus and fungal infection occurred. The pathogen spectrum indicates that fungi accounted for 30.2% (61/202) of the infections, bacteria accounted for 63.4% (128/202), and virus infection accounted for 6.4% (13/202), as shown in Figure 1B. Specific pathogens are shown in Figure 1C. The most frequent pathogens among all patients were Staphylococcus aureus (29/224, 12.9%), Candida (24/224, 10.7%), Cryptococcus (13/224, 5.8%), Enterococcus faecalis (9/224, 4.0%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (9/224, 4.0%). For localized infections, the most common pathogens were Staphylococcus aureus, Candida, Enterococcus faecalis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Corynebacterium. The most frequent bacteria were Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus faecalis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Corynebacterium, and Escherichia coli. The most common pathogens for systemic infection were Cryptococcus, Candida, Pneumocystis, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Cytomegalovirus. The most frequent bacteria were Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Staphylococcus aureus, Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, and Nocardia.

3.3. Risk factors for infection in AIBD

The characteristics of patients with or without infectious complications are summarized in Table 1. In the univariant analysis, there were no significant differences in the gender distribution, age, and BMI between the two groups. Pemphigus patients exhibited a higher incidence of infectious complications than bullous pemphigoid patients (P < 0.001). Infection group patients were more commonly with diabetes mellitus (P = 0.007), hypoalbuminemia (P < 0.001), and systemic glucocorticoid treatments (P < 0.001). The equivalent cumulative dose of methylprednisolone in the previous months (P = 0.002) between the groups was significantly different. 4. Discussion

Our study analyzed the characteristics of infections in AIBD patients, identified infection risk factors, and focused on the relationship between infection risk and glucocorticoid dosage. AIBD inpatients exhibited a notably high infection incidence (42.2%), with pemphigus (52.1%) showing a higher incidence compared to bullous pemphigoid (30.6%). Systemic glucocorticoid treatment was associated with an even higher incidence of infection, reaching 57.1%. The pathogen spectrum was broad, covering bacteria, fungi, and viruses. Staphylococcus aureus emerged as the most frequent pathogen. Bacterial infections were more frequent in localized infections, while fungal and viral infections were more prevalent in systemic infections. Skin and soft tissue and respiratory tract infections, including pneumonia, were most common in localized infections and systemic infections, respectively. Pemphigus diagnosis, hypoalbuminemia, and systemic glucocorticoid treatment were considered risk factors for infection. Notably, when the equivalent average dose of methylprednisolone reached 14 mg/day, the risk of infection rose to 75.0%. Using high doses of glucocorticoids (equivalent methylprednisolone >24 mg/day) significantly increased the risk of systemic infection to 88.8%.

In this study, the infection rates for localized and systemic infections were 17.1% and 24.7%, respectively. This systemic infection rate was lower than in a study with 50.4% serious infection in pemphigus and 54.6% in bullous pemphigoid.[11] This may be related to the narrow spectrum of infections. Consistent with previous findings, cutaneous and respiratory tract infections were the most common types of localized and systemic infections, respectively. A case-control study of 11 patients with pemphigus and 54 patients with bullous pemphigoid showed that respiratory tract infections, including pneumonia and upper respiratory tract infections, and urinary tract infections were the most common systemic infections.[9] Similarly, a cohort study of 252 bullous pemphigoid patients identified mucocutaneous infection (17.5%), respiratory system infection (12.7%), and blood infection (4.0%) as the most frequent types of infection.[9]

The pathogen spectrum showed bacteria (63.4%) as the most common, followed by fungi (30.2%) and viruses (6.4%). This result was consistent with a previous case-control study of 141 patients with pemphigus with a bacterial infection in 52% of cases, fungal in 50%, herpetic in 19%, and parasitic in 1.5%.[16] Several studies have also shown Staphylococcus aureus as a common pathogen in patients with pemphigus[1] and bullous pemphigoid.[6,11,12,17] In systemic infection, Cryptococcus, Candida, Pneumocystis, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Cytomegalovirus, Staphylococcus aureus, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Klebsiella pneumoniae were the most common, as shown in previous studies.[9,12] It may guide empiric antibiotic coverage before pathogenic evidence.

Pemphigus exhibited a higher susceptibility to infection than bullous pemphigoid, as evidenced by the infection rates observed in this study. This finding is consistent with previous research findings. In a retrospective case-control study involving 141 pemphigus patients, a high infection rate of 68% was reported.[16] Conversely, in studies focusing on bullous pemphigoid, infection rates ranged from 32.1% (81/252) to 56% (54/97) in retrospective cohort studies involving 252 and 97 patients, respectively.[10,12] This discrepancy in infection rates between pemphigus and bullous pemphigoid may be attributed to differences in the underlying mechanisms of disease induction. Specifically, the disruption of desmosomes in the stratum spinosum, which plays a crucial role in maintaining cutaneous integrity, may render pemphigus patients more susceptible to pathogen erosion than the disruption of bullous pemphigoid antigen in the stratum basal. Bullous pemphigoid has been shown to be more vulnerable to systemic infections than pemphigus, especially pneumonia, septicemia, and urinary tract infections.[9,11] Previous study on bullous pemphigoid with urinary tract infections[9,18] suggested inflammation and being vulnerable to infection may be related to the cross-reactivity of bullous pemphigoid antibodies with organ epithelium such as bladder.

In previous studies, old age,[9,11] impaired general condition,[6,10] hypoalbuminemia,[12,14] diabetes,[6,11,12] dementia,[10] severe degree of disease,[6] and systemic glucocorticoids treatment[4,9,12] were shown to be risk factors for infection in patients with pemphigus or bullous pemphigoid. This study included both pemphigus and bullous pemphigoid, and localized and systemic infections when analyzing risk factors, probably being more comprehensive. In this study, hypoalbuminemia and systemic glucocorticoid treatment were shown to be risk factors for infections. Additionally, high doses of systemic glucocorticoids compared to low or medium doses were risk factors for systemic infection after systemic glucocorticoid treatment.

Since the daily dose of glucocorticoids did not show a predictive effect for infection,[19] we considered the cumulative dose of glucocorticoids in the previous three months as an infection predictor for pemphigus and bullous pemphigoid patients using systemic glucocorticoid treatment. In the ROC curve analysis, when an equivalent cumulative dose of methylprednisolone in the previous three months went up to 1,284 mg (average 14 mg/day), infection risk increased to 75.0%. Using a high dose of glucocorticoids (equivalent methylprednisolone >24 mg/day), systemic infection risk increased to 88.8%. According to the Chinese Expert Proposal on the Diagnosis and Management of Pemphigus Vulgaris (2020)[20] and the British Association of Dermatologists’ Guideline for Management of Pemphigus Vulgaris 2017,[21] patients usually require glucocorticoid treatment for over three years. The equivalent initial methylprednisolone dose ranges from 16 to 72 mg/d based on disease severity. After controlling the disease for over two weeks, glucocorticoids will decrease to a maintenance dose of 8 mg/d. Therefore, infections should be screened before starting glucocorticoid treatment[22,23] and be closely monitored at the start of treatment. For immunocompromised patients like complicated with diabetes, the dosage of glucocorticoids should be evaluated with caution. Preventive therapy is not necessary[24] and requires further research.

Hypoalbuminemia may suggest a severe degree of disease, as it may be caused by blister fluid effusion.[25] Hypoalbuminemia may also relate to malnutrition, immune function impairment, and immune system disorders, which are vulnerable to infection. Immunosuppression of systemic glucocorticoid treatment causes a risk of infection. The use of systemic glucocorticoids may also suggest a more severe illness. Using a multivariate logistic regression model, this study showed that other immunosuppressive agent treatments, including azathioprine, methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, cyclophosphamide, ciclosporin, and rituximab, will not increase infection risk. Previous studies have also shown glucocorticoids with adjuvant immunosuppressive agents like rituximab did not increase infections in pemphigus and bullous pemphigoid patients, and even improved the disease remission.[26,27,28,29 and 30] However, because this study was limited to the patient number, immunosuppressive agents were not analyzed separately; therefore, the conclusion requires further confirmation.

There are some limitations to this study. Due to missing medical records, the severity of the diseases was not included in the analysis of infections. The influence of the severity of diseases on infections still needs further research. Because the study was limited to missing values, it did not include immune or inflammatory indices, which showed differences in characteristics in the multivariate logistic regression model for systemic infections. CD4 T cells, B cells, and IgG are essential in immune responses against pathogens. Studies have shown an imbalance between autoreactive T helper type 1, type 2, and B cells in pemphigus[31] and bullous pemphigoid[32] patients. Considering these, the immune cell count, and immunoglobulin level may also indicate systemic infections and need further support.

5. Conclusions

Infection is common in pemphigus and bullous pemphigoid, especially in patients treated with systemic glucocorticoids. The pathogen spectrum and location of infection varied widely. Pemphigus patients are at a higher risk than those with bullous pemphigoid. Systemic glucocorticoid treatment and hypoalbuminemia are risk factors for infection in patients with AIBD. AIBD patients undergoing high doses (averaging > 24 mg/day) of systemic glucocorticoid treatment in the previous three months should especially be cautious about infection.
