RANKL and CSF-1 are elevated in periovulatory follicular fluid of BRCA1 mutation carriers and increase proinflammatory signaling in fallopian tube epithelial cells
Authors: Alexandra Kollara, Vidushi Madaan, Jianhong Zhang, Ellen M. Greenblatt, Theodore J. Brown
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•BRCA1 mutation status influences cytokine levels in periovulatory follicular fluid.•RANKL and CSF-1 levels are higher in follicular fluid from BRCA1 mutation carriers.•RANKL and CSF-1 increase NFκB signaling in fallopian tube epithelial cells.•RANKL and CSF-1 increase levels of interferon-stimulated genes.•CSF-1 and RANKL effects in fallopian tube epithelial cells require NFκB signaling.

Introduction

High-grade serous tubo-ovarian cancer (HGSTOC) is the most commonly diagnosed ovarian cancer in western countries and is associated with a poor prognosis. This cancer is thought to originate from epithelial cells within the fallopian tube where precursor lesions have been identified [1]. Following malignant transformation and acquisition of an invasive phenotype, these cells can simultaneously seed the ovary and peritoneum [1,2]. Consequently, >80 % of patients diagnosed with this cancer present with disseminated disease, accounting for a median survival of only 44-months following initial diagnosis [[3], [4], [5]].

Among identified risk factors, heritable BRCA1 mutations pose the greatest risk, with studies reporting up to a 44 % lifetime risk of developing HGSTOC among mutation carriers as compared to a 1.4 % lifetime risk for the general female population [[6], [7], [8]]. Loss of function mutations within BRCA2 and other DNA homologous repair genes have also been associated with an increased risk for this cancer; however, BRCA1 gene mutations convey a substantially greater relative risk and an earlier age of onset [9,10]. Additional risk factors identified for HGSTOC are consistent with a role for increased number of lifetime ovulatory events [[11], [12], [13]]. Ovulation is an acute inflammatory process whereby the cumulus-oocyte complex and follicular fluid are expelled from the dominant ovarian follicle and taken up by the fimbria into the fallopian tube. As a result, fallopian tube epithelial (FTE) cells are exposed to follicular fluid [14] which contains proinflammatory cytokines, steroids, and other factors in a cyclic manner throughout the reproductive lifespan.

Inflammation is known to initiate and promote multiple cancers [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. For example, reactive oxygen species formation is increased with inflammatory signaling and can result in DNA adduct formation that can create mutations leading to malignant transformation [12]. Our lab has shown through transcriptome studies that non-malignant FTE cells obtained from BRCA1 mutation carriers during the post-ovulatory luteal phase more closely resemble HGSTOC cells, including a proinflammatory signature, than those obtained from control patients [19,20]. Further studies profiling primary FTE cells derived from control patients and BRCA1 mutation carriers and exposed to periovulatory follicular fluid in vitro revealed that BRCA1 deficiency associated with increased NFκB and EGFR signaling. In vitro follicular fluid exposure further increased proinflammatory signaling, including transcripts of type I interferon-stimulated genes [21].

Impaired homologous DNA repair resulting from a deficiency of BRCA1 activity undoubtedly features prominently in the increased risk for HGSTOC due to BRCA1 mutation. However, increased proinflammatory signaling resulting from BRCA1 deficiency likely also plays a significant role. Pregnancy and oral contraceptive use, which reduce the number of lifetime ovulatory events, reduces risk for HGSTOC among BRCA1 mutation carriers as well as non-mutation carriers [13,[22], [23], [24]]. Follicular fluid, which is largely generated by granulosa cells, contains a myriad of cytokines, growth factors and steroid hormones. Thus, the contents of periovulatory follicular fluid may differentially impact FTE cell signaling. Studies in mice indicate BRCA1 haploinsufficiency impacts granulosa cell function, raising the possibility that contents of periovulatory follicular fluid derived from BRCA1 mutation carriers may differ from that derived from non-mutation carriers. To test this possibility, we collected periovulatory follicular fluid from patients undergoing follicle stimulation and oocyte freezing as part of a fertility preservation procedure prior to their initiating treatment for breast cancer. Levels of selected cytokines and growth factors were measured by a proximity extension assay and compared between confirmed BRCA1 mutation carriers and confirmed non-BRCA1 mutation carriers. The potential impact of identified differential proteins present in follicular fluid from these patient populations on FTE cells was determined in vitro using an immortalized human FTE cell line.

Materials and methods

Follicular fluid collection and processing

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of Sinai Health (study number 19-0050-E) and all study subjects provided written informed consent. Eligible subjects were patients at Mt. Sinai Fertility, between the ages of 18- and 42-years who were diagnosed with primary breast cancer and undergoing an oncofertility preservation cycle prior to initiating their radiation or chemotherapy treatment. To be eligible, patients were also undergoing genetic testing for pathogenic mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2, with many patients undergoing multipanel gene testing, which included genes associated with Fanconi Anemia and Lynch Syndrome [25]. Patients received standard ovarian stimulation protocols consisting of controlled ovarian stimulation with gonadotropins and LH surge suppression with gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) antagonist. Human choriogonadotropin (hCG) and/or GnRH agonist trigger was administered 36 h prior to oocyte retrieval. All but three patients were simultaneously receiving letrozole (Femara), an aromatase inhibitor. A total of 59 patient samples collected were used in the study.

At the time of oocyte retrieval, the leading follicle was aspirated from each ovary, the oocyte was removed and the undiluted follicular fluid was collected into sterile 15 ml culture tubes. Following oocyte removal, the follicular fluid was centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min to remove cells and then at 2,000 g for 10 min to remove cellular debris. The supernatant was collected and aliquots were stored in cryovials at −80 °C.

Cytokine measurement and data analysis

Cytokine levels in follicular fluid samples were measured by Olink proximity extension assay (Olink; Boston, MA) performed by an Olink certified lab (Hamilton, ON) using a 96-panel inflammation antibody array. This panel measures relative levels of 92 proteins involved in immune response or immune-oncology diseases (www.olink.com). Data were intensity-normalized to the overall median value of all samples on the plate to provide Normalized Protein eXpressions (NPX) values and were assessed for quality control based upon the standard deviation of 4 internal controls. NPX values are of an arbitrary unit on a log2 scale. Intraassay coefficient of variance was 5 %. All data analyses were conducted using R version 4.1 (RStudio) with proteins below detection in 75 % or more of the samples excluded from analysis. Of the 92 proteins detected in the array, 82 were detected in 75 % or more of the samples.

Partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), well-established for feature extraction and discriminant analysis [26], was applied as a supervised form of Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to compare mutation status. Log2-transformed NPX values for the 82 proteins from the Olink assay were used as input for mixOmics (v6.16) (https://mixomics.org) package-based component variable selection and calculation. To visualize the relationship and similarity between samples, 2D graphs were generated using latent variables produced from the multivariate models using all 59 samples. To visualize the relationship of the 82 proteins, a correlation analysis was performed using the heat map (v1.3) package (https://cran.r-project.org). Comparison of protein levels between BRCA1 mutation (mtBRCA1) and non-BRCA1 mutation (non-mtBRCA1) groups was determined using the dplyr(1.0) package (https://cran.r-project.org) with p-values generated by Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)

The levels of progesterone in follicular fluid samples were measured using LC-MS. 17β-Estradiol levels were not measured as patients were treated with an aromatase inhibitor. Progesterone-d9 (Cayman Chemical; Cedarlane, Burlington, ON), was dissolved in LC/MS grade methanol (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA), and added to a pooled sample of follicular fluids to generate a standard curve. Individual follicular fluid samples (50 μl) were clarified by centrifugation at 14,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C, transferred onto centrifugal 10 K pore size filters (VWR, Mississauga, ON), and centrifuged at 14,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The samples were then concentrated using a SpeedVac vacuum concentrator. LC-MS was then performed by the Mass Spectrometry and Metabolomics facility at the Department of Chemical Engineering and Applied Biochemistry at the University of Toronto.

Cell culture and reagents

Human immortalized FTE cells were obtained from Dr. Ronny Drapkin (FTE194 and FTE246 cells; University of Pennsylvania, Pennsylvania) and Dr. William S.B. Yeung, (OE-E6/7 cells; University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong). All cell lines were grown in DMEM medium containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (all from Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) and were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5 % CO2. Cells were confirmed as to female derivation by short tandem repeat analysis and were tested for mycoplasma contamination using a Mycoplasma PCR Detection Kit (ABM; Cedarlane). Cells were used within 25 passages.

Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor Kappa Beta Ligand (RANKL) and Colony Stimulating Factor-1 (CSF-1), purchased from PeproTech (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Mississauga, ON), were dissolved in PBS containing 0.1 % BSA (100μg/ml) and stored at −20 °C. BAY11-7082 (Selleckchem; Cedarlane) was dissolved in DMSO (10 mM stock solution) and stored at −80 °C. 2-Amino-6-[2-(cyclopropylmethoxy)-6-hydroxyphenyl]−4-(4-piperidinyl)-3-pyridinecarbonitrile (ACHP; Tocris Bioscience; Cedarlane) was dissolved in DMSO (20 mM stock solution) and stored at −20 °C. Stock solutions were diluted with culture medium at the time of use with control treatments consisting of medium with an equivalent amount of DMSO.

Western blot analysis

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 120,000 cells per well and grown to 80 % confluency. Cells were placed in culture medium supplemented with 0.2 % FBS for 24 h prior to experimental treatment. At the time indicated for each experiment, total cell lysates were generated using RIPA lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES pH 7.25, 1 % Triton X-100, 0.1 % SDS, 1 % sodium deoxycholate) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche Diagnostics; MilliporeSigma, Oakville, ON).

Cell lysates (4 to 12 μg) were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (both from Bio-Rad, Mississauga, ON). Membranes were then incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. A list of all antibodies used is provided in Supplementary Table 1. The membranes were washed with PBS-T (Phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1 % tween 20) or TBS-T (TRIS-buffered saline containing 0.1 % tween 20) and incubated with either anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were then washed, and immunoreactivity was detected using chemiluminescence (Bio-Rad) and visualized with Chemidoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Image lab software (Bio-Rad) was used for densitometric analysis.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Cells were seeded into 24-well plates and grown to 80 % confluency. Cells were placed in culture medium supplemented with 0.2 % FBS for 24 h prior to experimental treatment. Total RNA was collected 24 h after treatment using a RNeasy plus universal kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) and cDNA synthesis was performed using SuperScripttm VILOtm (Invitrogen) as per manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR master mix (Bio-Rad) with cycling conditions of 95 °C for 10 sec, 95 °C for 15 sec and 60 °C for 60 sec for a total of 40 cycles. To confirm the amplification of a single product a dissociation curve was performed at the end of the cycle. Primer sets used are listed in Supplementary Table 2. Data were analysed using the ΔCCT method and the target Ct value was normalized to either B2M and/or TBP as the housekeeping gene.

XTT Assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2,500 cells per well. Cells were treated 24 h later with RANKL (500 ng/ml) and/or CSF-1 (400 ng/ml) in medium containing 0.2 % FBS. Relative cell number was determined 72 h after treatment using a XTT assay. XTT (Invitrogen) was added to each well to a final concentration of 50 μg/ml. The cells were further incubated for 3 h at 37 °C and absorbance was measured at 450 nM.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM of a minimum of 3 independent experiments. Data were analysed using two-way ANOVA followed by Holm-Sidak multiple comparison test. Graph Pad Prism (version 10.4.1 for macOS) was used to analyse the data and the statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05.

Protein levels between mtBRCA1 and non-mtBRCA1 samples were compared using a Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test. Our analysis indicated that 7 proteins were significantly different between the two groups (Supplementary Table 3). RANKL and CSF-1 were among those proteins with higher expression levels in mtBRCA1 samples. CSF-1 enhances RANKL activity by increasing expression of its receptor RANK [27]; therefore, we focused further experiments on these two cytokines.

RANKL and CSF-1 increase NFκB activation and interferon-stimulated proteins in FTE cells

Three immortalized FTE cell lines (FTE246, FTE194 and OE-E6/7) were screened for RANK expression. FTE194 and OE-E6/7 cells express high transcript levels of RANK whereas FTE246 cells have undetectable RANK transcript levels (Supplementary Fig 1A). To determine if RANKL and CSF-1 impact proinflammatory signalling in these two cell lines and to determine the optimal dose, FTE194 and OE-E6/7 cells were treated with different concentrations of RANKL or CSF-1 and TNFα transcript levels were assessed 24 h later by qPCR. RANKL and CSF-1 increased TNFα transcript levels in a dose-dependent manner in FTE194 cells. In comparison, RANKL-induced a lower fold-change in TNFα transcript levels in OE-E6/7 cells with little or no response observed with CSF-1 treatment (Supplementary Fig 1B and C). Based on these results, FTE194 cells were selected for subsequent studies.

RANKL has been shown to increase transcript and protein levels of the interferon-responsive gene ISG15 in mouse macrophage-like cells [28]. Therefore, we examined whether RANKL and CSF-1 affect protein levels of ISG15 and USP18, an interferon-inducible ISG15 deconjugase, in FTE194 cells. Cells were harvested 48 h following treatment with 500 ng/ml RANKL with or without 400 ng/ml CSF-1. RANKL or CSF-1 treatment increased ISG15 and USP18 protein levels approximately 3-fold and 7-fold respectively, with no further increase observed when treatments were combined (Fig. 2A). To determine the effect of RANKL and CSF-1 on activation of RELA, FTE194 cells were treated with 500 ng/ml RANKL with or without 400 ng/ml CSF-1. Levels of phosphorylated RELA (p-RELA) were determined 1 h after treatment. The effect of RANKL and CSF-1 on cell culture growth was examined in FTE194 cells. Cells were treated with RANKL, CSF-1 or a combination of RANKL and CSF-1 for 72 h. As expected, RANKL and CSF-1 independently increased cell number. However, the combination of RANKL with CSF-1 had no additional effect on culture growth (Fig. 2C). Altogether, these findings indicate that CSF-1 and RANKL exert similar effects on FTE194 cells and that CSF-1 does not enhance the effect of RANKL activity in these cells.

RANKL- and CSF-1-induced increased ISG15 levels were decreased by pre-treatment with BAY11-7082, an NFκB inhibitor that inhibits IκBα phosphorylation; however, this did not reach statistical significance. Both RANKL and CSF-1-induced increased USP18 levels were completely blocked by pre-treatment with BAY11-7082 (Fig. 3). Since our preliminary studies indicated pronounced cell death effects with BAY11-7082 at concentrations greater than 2 μΜ, we repeated these experiments using ACHP, a selective IκB kinase inhibitor. Treatment of FTE194 cells with 5 μM ACHP, a dose that did not cause cell death, 1 h prior to treatment with RANKL or CSF-1 completely blocked the increase in ISG15 and USP18 (Fig. 4A and B). Discussion

The results of this study indicate that cytokine profiles within periovulatory follicular fluid from BRCA1 mutation carriers differ from that collected from patients confirmed not to have a germline BRCA1 mutation. This latter group includes patients bearing germline mutations in other genes involved in homologous recombination DNA repair. While this could indicate the impact of BRCA1 deficiency on follicular fluid constituents is not a direct consequence of impaired homologous recombination repair, it must be noted that the pathological consequence of many of the identified mutations in these other genes is unknown and may not impact protein function. Correlation analysis of cytokine levels demonstrated a higher correlation of inflammatory proteins in samples from BRCA1 mutation carriers compared to those of non-BRCA1 mutated samples. This is consistent with the idea that BRCA1 loss leads to a concerted inflammatory response.

All patients, including non-mutation carriers, participating in this study were undergoing oocyte cryopreservation prior to gonadotoxic therapy for treatment of primary breast cancer. Follicular fluid samples were collected by follicle aspiration 36 h after hCG and/or GnRH agonist treatment simulating natural LH surge, as part of a controlled ovarian stimulation protocol. This design presents limitations that could impact generalizability, such as the use of exogenous hormones to stimulate follicle maturation rather than a natural cycle and treatment with letrozole (Femara) for breast cancer, which prevented the comparison of estrogen levels. However, this design ensured all patients were treated as similarly as possible and enabled us to collect follicular fluid just prior to when the follicle would normally rupture. Thus, the follicular fluid samples would be reflective of that which would enter the fallopian tube.

Among those cytokines found to be increased in follicular fluid samples of BRCA1 mutation carriers were RANKL and CSF-1. RANKL binds its cell surface receptor RANK and activates proinflammatory NFκB signaling in various cell types including mammary epithelial cells [29]. While CSF-1 also activates NFκB, its effects have predominately been observed in immune cells [30]. Treatment of FTE cells with either RANKL or CSF-1 increased proinflammatory gene transcript levels, including type I interferon-stimulated genes ISG15 and USP18. These effects were blocked by pre-treatment with ACHP, an IKK inhibitor, verifying the involvement of NFκB signaling. We previously identified an association of BRCA1 deficiency in primary FTE cells with increased IFN-inducible ISG15 and ISGylation pathway members, including USP18, following follicular fluid exposure [21]. ISG15 and ISGylation pathway members are downstream targets of both NFκB and type I IFN proinflammatory signaling [21,31,32]. ISGylation is a protein-modification pathway where ISG15, a ubiquitin-like protein, is covalently attached to a target protein through a three-step enzymatic process similar to ubiquitination. USP18, also encoded by an interferon-stimulated gene, serves as a deconjugase to remove ISG15 but has additional actions attributed to its functioning as a scaffolding protein. ISGylation has been shown to enhance innate immune signaling and we have recently shown that it enhances proinflammatory NFκB and type I IFN signaling induced by cytosolic dsRNA in FTE cells [33].

Prolonged chronic unresolved proinflammatory signaling can induce genetic and epigenetic changes that result in acquisition of cancer stem cell properties, and their expansion and survival [34]. TNFα, a direct target of NFκB, is expressed by ovarian cancer cells [35], and promotes detachment and motility [36]. Type I interferons, which are generally considered to have an anti-tumor function, can also confer a pro-tumorigenic role [37]. Type I IFN signaling can epigenetically rewire breast cancer cells towards a stem cell state that results in increased resistance to chemo-and immuno-therapy [38]. Consistent with this, direct association of increased expression of stem cell markers and EMT-related genes with RANKL and CSF-1 have also been reported in many tumor types [[39], [40], [41]]. In addition to promoting stem cell–like characteristics, elevated NFκB and type I IFN signaling can confer a survival advantage to cells under stress conditions. ISG15 is a key component of the Interferon-Related DNA Damage Resistance Signature (IRDS). Its expression has been linked to anti-apoptotic signaling and resistance to DNA-damaging therapies [42,43]. Like ISG15, sustained activation of IRF7-mediated type I interferon signaling has also been shown to promote resistance against chemotherapeutic drugs in breast cancer cells [44].

In addition to conferring a survival advantage, interferon-stimulated genes such as ISG15 and USP18 can also promote pro-tumorigenic effects of proinflammatory signaling through the establishment of positive feedback loops. We recently demonstrated that ISG15, through its conjugation pathway (ISGylation), amplifies proinflammatory responses triggered by intracellular dsRNA in immortalized non-malignant FTE cells [33]. Consistent with this, ISGylation has been shown to induce malignant transition of intestinal epithelial cells by enhancing production of proinflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species in macrophages [45]. While USP18, a major deconjugase of ISGylation pathway, can negatively regulate type I IFN [46], it also enhances IRF3- and NFκB-mediated proinflammatory signaling by recruiting key molecules that stabilize DNA damage response pathways through its scaffolding activity [47].

Increased proinflammatory signaling including ISGylation observed following exposure to follicular fluid could result from inflammatory factors released into the fluid by components of the periovulatory follicle. Follicular fluid is formed from a plasma exudate and factors largely produced by granulosa and thecal cells. As the follicle develops, the endothelial cells migrate to the thecal layer and the vascularization peaks at the antral stage when blood capillaries form a wreath around this thecal layer [48]. This increases the permeability of these follicles to plasma proteins. As a result, most blood plasma proteins accumulate in the follicular fluid, rendering it similar to serum as demonstrated by many studies [[49], [50], [51], [52]]. In response to the LH surge, granulosa and theca cells produce many cytokines and chemokines that recruit immune cells into the follicle [14]. These immune cells in turn contribute additional cytokines and chemokines. As a result, inflammatory factors accumulate in the periovulatory follicular fluid and play a crucial role in ovulation and luteinization [53]. CSF-1 is secreted by granulosa cells [54], and binds to its cell surface receptor on adjacent granulosa cells to promote cell proliferation and maturation [55,56]. RANKL transcripts have been detected in cumulus granulosa cells and RANKL is thought to play a role in the maturation of the oocyte-cumulus complex [57]; however, the precise role of RANKL in the female reproductive system, particularly during ovulation, remains to be elucidated.

BRCA1 deficiency has been associated with aberrant RANK/RANKL signaling in breast and ovarian cancer (reviewed in [58,59]). In ovarian cancer, aberrantly high levels of RANKL have been reported in BRCA1/2-mutated tumors compared to wild-type tumors. This aberrant increase of RANKL has been associated with reduced progression-free and overall survival, regardless of BRCA1 status [59]. In the breast, progesterone acts upon luminal epithelial cells to produce and secrete RANKL, which acts upon adjacent RANK-expressing mammary stem cells to induce proliferation and differentiation via the IKKα-NFκB-cyclin D1 pathway [29,60]. Schramek et al. demonstrated that deletion of RANK in mouse mammary epithelium impaired aberrant cell proliferation and expansion of the stem cell population induced by in vivo progestin treatment. Moreover, they found that deletion of RANK decreased the ability of progestin treatment to promote initiation of breast cancer [61]. High progesterone levels have been associated with BRCA1 deficiency [[62], [63], [64], [65]] and are considered to be potential risk factors for BRCA1-mutation associated breast cancer [66,67].

Preclinical studies provide evidence that blocking RANK/RANKL signaling might be an effective target to prevent early-stage breast cancer in BRCA1 mutation carriers [68,69]. Genetic inactivation of RANK or pharmacological inhibition of RANKL in BRCA1-and p53-mutated mice reduced mammary progenitor cell population, which is considered to be precursors of BRCA1 mutation-driven breast cancer [69]. This inhibition of RANK/RANKL signaling also reduced tumor development in mice. Similar inhibitory effects of RANK/RANKL were also demonstrated in organoid models generated from breast tissue derived from BRCA1-mutation carriers, where RANKL blockade attenuated progesterone-induced proliferation markers in the pre-neoplastic tissue [68]. These studies and the finding of dysregulated RANKL in plasma of BRCA1 mutation carriers [70,71] have led to speculation that denusamab, antiRANKL monoclonal antibody used to mitigate bone loss in osteoporosis patients, might be effective for prevention of breast cancer [72]. Consistent with this, our finding of elevated levels of RANKL in follicular fluid of BRCA1 mutation carriers in the present study, raise the possibility that this strategy might also be effective for prevention of HGSTOC.

CSF-1 has been suggested to play a pro-tumorigenic role in various cancers, including epithelial ovarian cancer [[73], [74], [75]]. High levels of CSF-1 and its receptor, CSF-1R, have been associated with poor prognosis [75]. Inhibition of CSF-1R with CSF-1R small molecule inhibitor, PLX3397, decreases proliferation of ovarian cancer cells [76]. This is consistent with our finding of increased cell number following either treatment with RANKL or CSF-1.

In addition to RANKL and CSF-1, osteoprotegerin (OPG/TNFRSF11B) was elevated in follicular fluid from BRCA1 mutation carriers. OPG is an endogenous decoy receptor for RANKL and thereby limits its availability to activate RANK signaling; thus, a high RANKL/OPG ratio is primarily considered to be pro-tumorigenic [72]. The Olink proximity extension assay used in this study is highly sensitive, specific and technically robust [77]; however, the protein levels generated signify relative measures rather than absolute concentration of the protein in the sample. Therefore, it was not possible to generate meaningful RANKL/OPG ratios from our data. In addition, OPG has RANKL-independent effects that can impact tumorigenesis. OPG also serves as a decoy receptor for TRAIL, which it binds with similar affinity as with RANKL [78], and thereby inhibits TRAIL-mediated apoptosis of ovarian cancer cells to promote their survival [79]. The antagonistic roles of RANKL and OPG should therefore be interpreted with caution as the interplay between RANKL and OPG can be complex in vivo due to the simultaneous presence of TRAIL. Additionally, OPG has been shown to interact with GRP78/BiP to promote the survival of inflammatory breast cancer cells [80].

As expected, high levels of progesterone were detected in the follicular fluid; however, no differences in levels between BRCA1 mutation carriers and non-BRCA1 mutation carriers were observed. A limitation of the study is that we could not compare estradiol or cortisol levels between BRCA1 mutation carriers and non-BRCA1 mutation carriers. Patients included in the study were taking the aromatase inhibitor letrozole (Femara) because of a prior diagnosis of breast cancer, precluding our ability to detect the hormone in follicular fluid. We and others have previously found high levels of cortisol in periovulatory follicular fluid [81,82]; however, we were not able to reliably detect and quantitate levels by mass spectrometry. There is limited evidence on how steroid hormone levels in follicular fluid affect FTE cells. Circulating estradiol promotes cell growth and ciliogenesis of FTE cells, whereas progesterone causes epithelial thinning and loss of cilia; with both hormones enhancing stem cell-like properties in these cells [83,84]. Early HGSTOC precursor lesions show increased estrogen receptor activity due to loss of PAX2, and progesterone-induced RANKL has been implicated in the initiation of breast cancer, suggesting that aberrant estrogen and/or progesterone signaling could be involved in early changes of HGSTOC development [60,69,85]. Further studies examining the impact of the hormonal milieu on RANKL and CSF-1 signaling in FTE cells are needed.

In summary, our data indicate that cytokine levels in follicular fluid differs between BRCA1 mutation carriers and non-mutation carriers, with increased levels of RANKL and CSF-1 found in fluids collected from BRCA1 mutation carriers. Our data further demonstrate that NFκB signaling is activated in FTE cells following exposure to RANKL and CSF-1. These findings raise the possibility that increased levels of these proinflammatory factors in follicular fluid could contribute to the predisposition toward malignant transformation of FTE cells in BRCA1 mutation carriers through continuous repetitive exposure over a course of decades. Further investigation of the effects of RANKL in HGSTOC is warranted to determine if targeting this signaling pathway could mitigate the risk for ovarian cancer in BRCA1 mutation carriers.
