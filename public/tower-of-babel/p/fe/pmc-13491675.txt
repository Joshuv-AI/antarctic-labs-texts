Autologous concentrated growth factors promote M2 macrophage polarization to accelerate wound healing after anal fistula surgery by activating the AKT/mTOR pathway
Authors: Xiufeng Zhang, Mengjie Zhong, Feifei Zhang, Miaoshan Lim, Jiazi Yu, Rongchao He, Jianming Qiu, Yuemin Yuan, Zhenfeng Lu, Houdong Wang, Zhong Shen, Jun He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDelayed healing of post-operative anal fistula wounds is driven by a hostile inflammatory microenvironment. While autologous concentrated growth factors (ACGF) show regenerative potential, the underlying immunomolecular mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigates if ACGF accelerates contaminated wound healing by modulating macrophage polarization via the AKT/mTOR pathway.MethodsIn vitro, RAW264.7 macrophages were treated with ACGF to assess phenotypic switching and AKT/mTOR activation. The paracrine effects of ACGF-primed macrophages on fibroblasts were evaluated using a co-culture system with AKT-siRNA validation. In vivo, a rat fecal-contaminated wound model was established. Therapeutic efficacy, with or without the AKT inhibitor Triciribine, was assessed via laser speckle imaging, histology, and immunofluorescence.ResultsACGF effectively reprogrammed macrophages from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward a reparative M2 phenotype, significantly upregulating CD206 and Arg-1 expression while activating AKT/mTOR signaling. Mechanistically, ACGF-primed macrophages significantly boosted RSF proliferation, migration, and collagen synthesis, effects that were substantially abrogated by AKT silencing. In vivo, ACGF treatment markedly accelerated wound closure, enhanced microvascular perfusion, and increased the density of CD31 + and Ki67 + cells. These regenerative benefits were accompanied by a significant shift toward M2 infiltration and a reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokines, all of which were reversed by pharmacological inhibition of AKT.ConclusionACGF promotes the healing of contaminated wounds by orchestrating an AKT/mTOR-dependent macrophage M2 polarization. This shift initiates a beneficial paracrine relay that enhances fibroblast activity and neoangiogenesis, effectively restoring the regenerative niche. Our findings establish ACGF as a potent immunomodulatory biomaterial and offer a promising therapeutic strategy for complex perianal wounds and chronic recalcitrant defects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-05063-2.

Introduction

Wound healing is a complex and highly orchestrated biological process encompassing several overlapping phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [1, 2]. Dysregulation at any stage can lead to delayed healing or the development of chronic recalcitrant wounds [3, 4]. In clinical practice, particularly within colorectal surgery, post-operative wound healing for anal fistula presents significant challenges. These wounds are typically categorized as open and contaminated, as they are chronically exposed to enteric bacteria, digestive enzymes, and fecal matter, which frequently trigger persistent infection and inflammatory responses [5]. Furthermore, the unique anatomical structure of the perianal region—characterized by frequent sphincter movement and distinct local microcirculation—often results in localized ischemia, hypoxia, and impaired re-epithelialization [6]. Clinical data indicate that the overall failure rate of anal fistula surgery exceeds 20%, with many patients suffering from prolonged pain and a diminished quality of life due to non-healing wounds [7]. Consequently, identifying a biotherapeutic strategy that can actively modulate the local immune microenvironment and accelerate the healing of infected wounds without compromising sphincter function has become a critical research priority.

In recent years, platelet concentrates have been extensively investigated in regenerative medicine due to their abundant bioactive components, including growth factors, cytokines, and fibrin [8–11]. Advances in preparation techniques have led to the evolution of these concentrates from first-generation Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) and second-generation Platelet-Rich Fibrin (PRF) to the third-generation Autologous Concentrated Growth Factor (ACGF) [9, 11, 12]. Developed by Sacco et al. in 2006, ACGF features a denser and more robust growth factor-fibrin matrix compared to its predecessors. Its unique three-dimensional fibrin network traps growth factors more effectively, facilitating a sustained release profile that is conducive to prolonged tissue regeneration [13]. Accumulating evidence suggests that ACGF significantly accelerates angiogenesis and soft tissue repair. For instance, Calabriso et al. demonstrated that ACGF promotes endothelial angiogenesis through both its soluble and cellular components, acting as a potent pro-angiogenic biomaterial [14]. Similarly, clinical findings by Kao revealed that ACGF accelerates soft tissue regeneration and peripheral re-epithelialization in chronic skin wounds, such as pressure ulcers and stasis dermatitis [15].

Our team’s previous research confirmed that ACGF enhances fibroblast function in vitro and promotes wound repair in an in vivo anal fistula model [16, 17]. Notably, we observed a significant increase in macrophage infiltration within contaminated wound tissues treated with ACGF [16]. Wound healing requires a sophisticated interplay between various cell types (e.g., platelets, neutrophils, macrophages, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells), the extracellular matrix (ECM), and signaling cytokines [18, 19]. Among these, macrophages serve as central regulators; they not only clear pathogens and necrotic debris but also secrete essential cytokines to coordinate the activities of fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and keratinocytes [19–21]. In addition, recent studies have emphasized that macrophage phenotypic remodeling is highly dependent on the local tissue microenvironment and plays a decisive role in shaping inflammatory and reparative responses [22]. However, the precise mechanisms underlying ACGF-mediated macrophage modulation remain poorly understood. Therefore, the present study employs both in vitro and in vivo experiments to investigate the relationship between ACGF, macrophage polarization, and wound healing, with a focus on identifying the underlying molecular pathways.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

Twenty-four healthy male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (SPF grade, 6–8 weeks old, weighing 200 ± 20 g) were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Beijing, China). Animals were housed at a controlled temperature (22ཞ24℃) and relative humidity (40%ཞ60%), with a standard 12-hour light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. The animal experiment protocol was approved by laboratory animal management and ethics committee of Hunter Biolnsight Technology Co.,Ltd.(Approval number: IACUC/HTYJ-11746-27).

Preparation of ACGF

ACGF were prepared from rat blood collected via the orbital venous plexus. Blood samples were processed using a Medifuge MF200 centrifuge (Silfradent Srl, Italy) with a standardized programmed cycle: 30 s acceleration, 2 min at 2700 rpm, 4 min at 2400 rpm, 4 min at 2700 rpm, 3 min at 3000 rpm, and 36 s deceleration to a full stop. After centrifugation, the blood was separated into three layers: the top platelet-poor plasma (PPP) layer, the bottom red blood cell (RBC) layer, and the middle ACGF fibrin gel. After the ACGF layer was separated, the concentrated ACGF solution was diluted with PBS at a volume ratio to reduce its concentration to 20% ACGF.

Cell culture and experimental grouping

Non-co-culture assay

The RAW 264.7 mouse macrophage cell line was purchased from iCell Bioscience (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Genomcell, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Opcel, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37℃ with 5% CO₂ upon reaching 75% confluence, cells were divided into: (1) Control group: cultured in 10% FBS-DMEM; (2) ACGF group: treated with 20% ACGF16 in 10% FBS-DMEM for 8 h prior to downstream analysis.

Co-culture assay and AKT pathway inhibition

A Transwell co-culture system with a 0.4 μm pore-size membrane in 6-well plates was used to evaluate the paracrine effects of RAW264.7 macrophages on RSFs. RSFs were seeded in the lower chamber at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/well. The experimental groups were defined as follows: (1) Control group: RSFs were cultured alone in the lower chamber, and the upper chamber contained culture medium only, without RAW264.7 macrophages; (2) RAW264.7 co-culture group: RSFs in the lower chamber were co-cultured with untreated RAW264.7 macrophages seeded in the upper chamber at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/well; and (3) RAW264.7 + 20% ACGF co-culture group: RSFs in the lower chamber were co-cultured with RAW264.7 macrophages exposed to 20% ACGF-containing medium in the upper chamber. The co-culture system was maintained in 10% FBS-DMEM for 36 h before downstream analyses [23, 24].

To investigate the AKT pathway, RAW 264.7 cells were transfected with siRNA-NC or siRNA-AKT (GenePharma, China) for 24 h prior to co-culture. The following groups were established: (1) 20% ACGF, (2) RAW 264.7 + 20% ACGF + siRNA-NC, and (3) RAW 264.7 + 20% ACGF + siRNA-AKT. After co-culture, cells from both chambers were collected.

Animal model and treatment

Modeling and grouping

Rats were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group): Control, Model, 20%.

ACGF, and 20% ACGF + Triciribine (TCBN). After inhalation anesthesia with isoflurane, full-thickness skin defects measuring 1.8 cm in diameter and 0.3 cm in depth were created near the spine in all rats. In the Control group, sterile saline was applied to the wound surface after wound creation. In the Model, 20% ACGF, and 20% ACGF + TCBN groups, 1 mL of fecal slurry, prepared by homogenizing rat feces in sterile saline, was applied to the wound surface to establish a fecal-contaminated wound model. Successful modeling was confirmed by the presence of purulent discharge after 48 h.

Intervention protocol

For all groups, the wound was gently cleansed with sterile saline to remove exudate prior to each dressing change. No sharp debridement was performed. The Control and Model groups received sterile saline dressings twice daily for 14 days. The 20% ACGF and 20% ACGF + TCBN groups were treated with 50 µL 20% ACGF gel twice a day for 14 days. Additionally, the 20% ACGF + TCBN group received daily oral gavage of the Akt inhibitor TCBN (1 mg/kg; MedChemExpress, USA) for 14 days [25].

Sample collection

After 14 days, rats were euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation, and wound tissues were harvested. Samples were either processed into single-cell suspensions for flow cytometry, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histology (H&E, Masson) and immunostaining (IHC, IF, TUNEL), or flash-frozen at -80℃ for ELISA, qRT-PCR, and Western Blotting.

Flow cytometry analysis

Macrophage phenotyping

Cultured RAW 264.7 cells were digested and incubated with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD16, CD86, CD206, and Arg-1 (Supplementary Table 1) at 4℃ for 30 min. For tissue samples, wounds were enzymatically digested to obtain single-cell suspensions. Cells were stained with anti-F4/80 followed by secondary antibodies, or directly labeled with anti-CD206. Data were acquired using a NovoCyte flow cytometer (Agilent, USA).

Phagocytosis assay

RAW 264.7 cells were treated with 20% ACGF for 24 h, followed by incubation with an IgG-FITC Phagocytosis Assay Kit (Cayman Chemical, USA) for 4 h. Extracellular fluorescence was quenched with 0.2% Trypan Blue. The percentage of phagocytic cells was analyzed via flow cytometry.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the EZ-10 Total RNA Extraction Mini Kit (BBI, China) and reverse-transcribed using the TRUEscript RT MasterMix (Aidlab, China). PCR was performed on a LightCycler® 96 System (Roche, Switzerland) using SYBR Green qPCR Mix. Relative gene expression was calculated via the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method, with GAPDH as the internal control. Primers are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

RSF proliferation assays

EdU Assay: Cell proliferation was assessed using the Beyoclick™ EdU-594 Kit (Beyotime, China). EdU-positive cells were quantified using ImageJ.

CCK-8 Assay: RSFs were seeded in 96-well plates (1.5 × 10⁴ cells/well). Following treatment for 24, 48, or 72 h, CCK-8 reagent was added, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured.

Wound healing evaluation

Wounds were photographed at day 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14. The wound closure rate was calculated as: [Initial Area - Current Area/ Initial Area] ×100%. Local blood flow was quantified using a Laser Speckle Imaging System (RFLSI III, RWD, China). Edema was scored on a 4-point scale (0: none to 3: severe).

ELISA

The levels of Collagen Type I (Col I) in cell supernatants, and Angiotensin I (Ang-I), Angiotensin II (Ang-II), VEGF, IL-1β, TNF-α, TGF-β1, IL-6, and IL-10 in wound tissues were measured using commercial ELISA kits (Supplementary Table 3) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, samples and standards were added to pre-coated microplates, followed by sequential incubation with detection antibodies and chromogenic substrates. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and concentrations were calculated based on the standard curves. All samples were analyzed in duplicate, and the experiments were independently repeated six times.

Western blotting

For tissue samples: Rat wound tissues were minced and homogenized in ice-cold RIPA Lysis Buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) using a tissue homogenizer, followed by lysis on ice. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation.

For cell samples: Cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and lysed in RIPA buffer on ice. After centrifugation, the supernatant was recovered for analysis.

Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. Protein samples were mixed with loading buffer and denatured by boiling at 100 °C for 5 min. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes using a wet transfer system. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 2 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with appropriately diluted primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 4) at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-linked anti-rabbit IgG antibody (7074, Cell Signaling, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Following thorough washing, protein bands were visualized using ECL chemiluminescence reagents and captured with a ChemiScope 6000exp imaging system (Clinx Science, China). The gray value of the target bands was analyzed using ImageJ software. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Evaluation of wound healing

Wound closure was monitored by capturing digital photographs alongside a standard ruler on days 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14 post-intervention. The wound area was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). The wound healing rate was calculated according to the following formula: Wound Healing Rate (%) = (Initial Area - Area at time )/Initial Area × 100%.

To assess microvascular perfusion, a Laser Speckle Imaging System (RFLSI III, RWD, Shenzhen, China) was employed on days 3, 7, and 14. Blood flow values were recorded for 30 s at four distinct quadrants of the wound bed, and the mean value of these four regions was designated as the perfusion level for each time point. Additionally, wound edema was clinically scored using a 4-point scale: 0 (none), 1 (mild), 2 (moderate), and 3 (severe).

Histological and immunofluorescence analysis

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and masson’s trichrome staining

H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining were performed using commercial kits (G1005 and G1006, Servicebio, China) to evaluate histological morphology and collagen deposition. Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Following differentiation and counterstaining, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted for observation under a light microscope.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval using Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). After blocking with normal goat serum to minimize non-specific binding, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Ki67 (1:300; AF0198, Affinity Biosciences, China) or CD31 (1:300; AF6191, Affinity Biosciences, China). Subsequently, the sections were incubated with an Alexa Fluor® 647-conjugated secondary antibody (Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG, 1:500; ab150079, Abcam, UK) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained and mounted using DAPI mounting medium (ab104139, Abcam, UK). Digital images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, where positive signals for Ki67 and CD31 appeared red, and nuclei appeared blue.

TUNEL assay

Cell apoptosis in wound tissues was detected using a One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (C1088, Beyotime, China). Briefly, sections were treated with Proteinase K (ST532, Beyotime, China) for antigen retrieval and membrane permeabilization. The tissues were then incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture for 2 h at 37 °C in a humidified dark chamber. After counterstaining nuclei with DAPI, the slides were mounted and observed under a fluorescence microscope. TUNEL-positive apoptotic nuclei exhibited green fluorescence, while all nuclei were visualized via blue DAPI staining.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Quantitative data were presented as the mean ± standard. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using the independent-samples t-test. For multiple group comparisons, the normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. If the data met both criteria, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was employed for inter-group comparisons. In cases where the data were normally distributed but exhibited unequal variances, Dunnett’s T3 test was applied. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis H test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ACGF Induces M2 polarization and enhances phagocytosis in RAW264.7 macrophages

RAW264.7 macrophages were exposed to 20% ACGF and subsequently evaluated for phenotypic polarization, cytokine expression, and phagocytic activity.Flow cytometry demonstrated a significant shift toward the M2 phenotype after ACGF treatment. The proportions of CD206⁺ and Arg-1⁺ cells were markedly increased, whereas CD86⁺ and CD16⁺ populations were significantly decreased compared with the control group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1A, B). Consistent with the polarization results, qRT-PCR analysis showed reduced mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-6 (p < 0.01). In contrast, anti-inflammatory and pro-repair factors IL-10 and TGF-β were significantly upregulated (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) (Fig. 1C). In addition, flow cytometry-based phagocytosis assays revealed a significant increase in the phagocytic rate of RAW264.7 cells following ACGF treatment (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1D and E).

ACGF enhances proliferation, migration, and collagen production of RSF in macrophage co-culture

A RAW264.7–RSF Transwell co-culture system was established to evaluate fibroblast responses. EdU and CCK-8 assays showed that macrophage co-culture significantly increased RSF proliferation compared with the control group (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01). The addition of 20% ACGF further enhanced proliferative activity (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) (Fig. 2A–C). Scratch assays demonstrated increased RSF migration in the co-culture group (p < 0.01), which was further augmented by ACGF treatment (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2D, E). ELISA analysis revealed significantly elevated type I collagen (Col-I) secretion in co-cultured RSF compared with controls (p < 0.01), and ACGF treatment further increased collagen production (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2F).

AKT silencing attenuates macrophage-mediated effects on RSF

To determine the role of AKT signaling, macrophages were transfected with siRNA-AKT prior to co-culture. Under ACGF stimulation, AKT silencing significantly reduced RSF proliferation and migration compared with siRNA-NC macrophages (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3E–H). Western blot analysis of RSF functional proteins demonstrated decreased expression of PCNA, COL1A1, and TIMP-1 (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01), while MMP-3 expression was significantly increased (p < 0.01) after AKT knockdown (Fig. 3I–M).

AKT inhibition delayed ACGF-mediated macroscopic wound healing in fecal-contaminated rat wounds

Compared with the model group, topical application of 20% ACGF significantly accelerated wound closure, increased blood perfusion, and reduced edema scores (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4A–D). Treatment with the AKT inhibitor TCBN significantly weakened these effects (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01).

Discussion

In this study, we systematically elucidated the mechanisms by which ACGF accelerates the healing of contaminated wounds through a coordinated immune-stromal relay. Our findings demonstrate that ACGF is not merely a passive reservoir of growth factors but acts as a potent immunomodulatory switch that reprograms macrophages via the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway.

ACGF reverses the pathological inflammation through immune reprogramming

Traditional perspectives often view autologous platelet derivatives primarily as trophic supplements for resident cells. However, in the context of contaminated wounds—such as post-operative anal fistula wounds-the primary barrier to healing is often the persistent, pathogen-induced inflammatory microenvironment rather than a simple lack of growth signals5.

Our data reveal that ACGF’s primary therapeutic role lies in its ability to shift macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory M1 state to a pro-reparative M2 state. This aligns with and extends the findings of previous studies regarding macrophage plasticity26–28. While persistent M1-derived cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β) typically drive excessive ECM degradation via matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)19, ACGF effectively interrupts this pathological cycle. Most importantly, our co-culture results suggest that this immunological shift precedes and potentially dictates subsequent fibroblast behavior, positioning the macrophage as the “first responder” to ACGF therapy. Thus, ACGF functions as a bioactive regulator that restores the essential transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative phase.

The AKT/mTOR axis: the molecular switch for macrophage plasticity

A pivotal finding of this study is the identification of the AKT/mTOR axis as the molecular driver of ACGF-mediated reprogramming. The AKT/mTOR pathway is well-recognized as a central hub for regulating protein synthesis and metabolic adaptation during macrophage activation29–34. While prior research hinted at ACGF’s ability to activate AKT35, our study establishes a definitive functional link between this signaling event and the subsequent paracrine crosstalk.

We observed that pharmacological inhibition of AKT using Triciribine significantly attenuated M2 infiltration and delayed wound closure in our rat model. This suggests that AKT/mTOR activation is a requirement, not a coincidence, for ACGF’s efficacy. As the downstream effector, the mTORC1 complex likely coordinates the mitochondrial reprogramming and cytokine translation (such as IL-10 and TGF-β) necessary for the M2 phenotype36, 37. By engaging this metabolic checkpoint, ACGF provides the upstream cues necessary to “program” macrophages into a regenerative state.

The macrophage–fibroblast paracrine relay and tissue regeneration

Tissue repair relies on a sophisticated “paracrine relay” between immune cells and effector cells. Our study highlights that fibroblast activation—characterized by enhanced proliferation, migration, and collagen synthesis—is largely mediated through ACGF-primed macrophages rather than direct stimulation alone.

When macrophage signaling was disrupted via AKT silencing, the pro-repair effects on fibroblasts were effectively abrogated despite the presence of ACGF. This supports a model of immune-stromal coupling: ACGF-stimulated M2 macrophages secrete high levels of TGF-β1, which drives myofibroblast differentiation and ECM deposition18, 19. Furthermore, our in vivo observations of increased microvascular density and upregulated angiogenic factors (Ang-1, VEGF) corroborate the pro-angiogenic properties of CGFs reported by Calabriso et al.14. This multi-targeted approach—combining inflammatory resolution with stabilized angiogenesis—is particularly critical for the high-bacterial-load environment of perianal wounds.

Integrated mechanism and clinical implications

Based on our evidence, we propose a hierarchical mechanism for ACGF-mediated healing:Primary Event: ACGF activates the AKT/mTOR signaling axis in macrophages.Intermediate Event: Macrophages polarize toward a pro-reparative M2 phenotype, resolving chronic inflammation.Secondary Event: A paracrine relay triggers fibroblast activity and robust angiogenesis.Outcome: Restoration of a regenerative microenvironment and accelerated closure of contaminated wounds.

Limitations and future perspectives

Despite these insights, several limitations remain. First, the use of the RAW 264.7 cell line may not fully replicate the transcriptional complexity of primary human macrophages. Second, our rat model, while simulating fecal contamination, is an acute injury model that does not capture the chronic anatomical challenges (e.g., sphincter dynamics) of human complex anal fistulas. Third, the potential off-target effects of chemical inhibitors like Triciribine cannot be entirely excluded. Fourth, although the TCBN dose (1 mg/kg/day) was selected based on published literature demonstrating its safety and efficacy in rats, we did not perform systematic toxicity assessments in our own study (e.g., daily body weight curves, behavioral scoring, or comprehensive organ histology). Fifth, we did not include a control group receiving TCBN alone without ACGF, which would help rule out non-specific effects of the inhibitor on wound healing. Finally, as an autologous product, the growth factor concentration in ACGF may vary based on individual systemic health. Future studies involving primary human cells, large-animal fistula models, dedicated safety evaluations of TCBN, and appropriate control groups (e.g., TCBN alone) are essential to validate these findings for clinical translation.

Conclusion

Despite some limitations, this study demonstrates that ACGF promotes the healing of contaminated wounds through a “dual-action” strategy. Beyond providing growth factors, it actively drives macrophage M2 polarization via the AKT/mTOR pathway, initiating a signaling cascade that enhances matrix reconstruction and angiogenesis. These results provide a robust mechanistic foundation for the clinical application of ACGF in managing complex, recalcitrant perianal wounds.
