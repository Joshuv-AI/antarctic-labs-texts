Multi-dimensional evidence reveals genetic, demographic and ecological vulnerability in endangered Ormosia microphylla
Authors: Xiao-ming Tian, Bin Yuan, Cun Mou, Guang-feng Xiang, Lu Zhu, Gao-fei Li, Chao Liu, Xiang-peng Li, Fu-liang Hu, Hao Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndangered woody plants are threatened by habitat degradation, demographic instability, and genetic erosion. Ormosia microphylla (small-leaved Ormosia) is an endangered tree, but how genetic, demographic, and environmental factors jointly contribute to its decline remains insufficiently resolved.MethodsWe investigated the age structure of seven wild O. microphylla populations and resequenced 62 adult individuals to assess genetic variation, population structure, linkage disequilibrium, gene flow and demographic history. In three representative populations, we further quantified seed production, germination, logging traces, soil properties and bird diversity, and integrated these variables using exploratory Spearman correlations, standardized multi-factor indices and principal component analysis (PCA).ResultsOverall, 71% of the surveyed populations exhibited unstable age structures. All populations showed low genome-wide nucleotide diversity (π < 0.005) and were grouped into three geographically interpretable genetic clusters: GX, HG and HN. TreeMix detected limited gene flow among clusters, and smc + + inferred historical declines in effective population size, with a slower decline in HN than in GX and HG. Rapid LD decay indicated that low diversity was not accompanied by extensive genome-wide linkage. Selective sweep analyses identified cluster-specific enriched pathways, including cutin, suberin and wax biosynthesis in HG and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis in HN. Among representative populations, all produced viable seeds, but seed production declined annually and germination was constrained by the seed coat. Spearman correlations among bird diversity, stump counts, seed output and recruitment were weak and non-significant. PCA indicated that regeneration status, mature-tree availability and reproductive output defined the primary population-performance gradient, whereas disturbance pressure and biotic support formed a secondary external-context gradient.ConclusionsO. microphylla vulnerability is associated with interacting genetic risks, demographic imbalance, reproductive limitation and external pressures, supporting targeted in situ protection, within-cluster genetic management and cluster-representative ex situ conservation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09412-x.

Background

Biodiversity is the cornerstone of Earth’s life-support systems and establishes ecosystem functions through mechanisms such as functional complementarity and resilience stabilization [1]. Driven by habitat loss, overexploitation, and climate change, the escalating extinction crisis threatens this foundational framework [2]. According to the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List [3], 43% of plant taxa face the risk of extinction. This trend is particularly prominent in tropical and subtropical regions. The decrease in plant diversity disrupts carbon sequestration and nutrient cycling and destabilizes mutualistic networks, such as plant–pollinator interactions [4]. These changes, in turn, trigger cascading impacts on ecosystem services critical to human well-being, including food security and climate regulation [5]. Therefore, protecting plant diversity is important for global ecological conservation and sustainable development.

Ormosia plants have attracted considerable attention as an important group within Fabaceae because of their beautiful red seeds and high-quality wood [6]. This genus is widely distributed in tropical and subtropical regions and plays an important role in forest ecosystems [7]. Its seeds are often dispersed by birds and form a complex interaction network with animals and plants, which is important for maintaining biodiversity [8]. However, habitat loss, overexploitation, and climate change are increasingly affecting Ormosia habitats [9, 10]. For example, Ormosia microphylla (small-leaved Ormosia), a representative Ormosia taxon, has shown a sharp population decline and a reduced distribution range, and has been listed as Endangered by the IUCN [11]. The Endangered status of O. microphylla highlights the conservation challenges this species faces and provides a focused case for understanding the threats to rare Ormosia trees. Current research on the decline of O. microphylla populations has focused mainly on single factors. Systematic studies of the multiple threats these populations face and the mechanisms underlying interactions among them remain limited [12]. This hinders the development of holistic conservation strategies aligned with the 2030 targets of the Global Biodiversity Framework [13, 14].

To address this knowledge gap, we systematically analyzed internal (genetic and demographic) and external (ecological and anthropogenic) factors associated with O. microphylla endangerment through a sequential, complementary set of analyses. First, population surveys and age-structure analyses characterized the demographic status of seven populations through quadrat-based assessments of saplings, young trees, adult trees, and old trees. Second, population genomic analyses, including variant calling, population structure, linkage disequilibrium, gene flow, and demographic-history inference, assessed genetic health and clarified conservation-relevant genetic clusters. Third, selective sweep analysis using pi-ratio, FST, and XPCLR identified genomic regions and pathways that may reflect local adaptation. Finally, seed traits, soil properties, bird diversity, and logging traces provided ecological context for interpreting the genomic and demographic patterns. Together, these analyses were used to identify the main factors associated with population decline and to formulate evidence-based conservation recommendations.

Therefore, we aimed to identify the genetic, demographic, ecological, and anthropogenic factors associated with the decline of O. microphylla and to provide a scientific basis for targeted conservation strategies for this Endangered species [15].

Materials and methods

Study area overview

The study area is located in south-central and southwestern China (107°14′E-110°19′E, 25°7′N-26°28′N), including four sites in Hunan Province, that is, Chengbu (CB, 110°19′E, 26°22′N), Tongdao (TD, 109°56′E, 26°10′N), Jingzhou (JZ, 109°42′E, 26°28′N), and Tangbaocun (TBC, 110°02′E, 25°49′N); two sites in Guizhou Province: Liping (LP, 109°08′E, 26°12′N), Jianhe (JH, 108°36′E, 26°38′N); and one site in Guangxi: Nandan (ND, 107°14′E, 25°07′N). The altitudes of the study sites vary from 564 m to 970 m. The vegetation type is subtropical evergreen broad-leaved forest. The region has a typical warm, humid subtropical monsoon climate with abundant sunlight. The average annual temperature is 16–22 ℃, the average temperature of the warmest month (July) is 25–30 ℃, and the average temperature of the coldest month (January) is 5–12 ℃. The average annual precipitation is 1,200–1,800 mm, with approximately 85% concentrated between April and October. The average annual relative humidity is above 75%.

In this study, field surveys were conducted annually from April to July between 2019 and 2024. Across the seven sampling sites, we selected 62 healthy adult O. microphylla trees in total for genome resequencing. Plant materials were collected from wild populations in the above regions. The species was formally identified by Dr. Lihong Yan (anlh0424@163.com), and voucher specimens (Nos. OMi-2024-001 to OMi-2024-062) have been deposited at the Hunan Botanical Garden (Hunan Province, China). Within the scope of a central-government-funded conservation project approved by relevant forestry authorities, all collections and experimental research on plants complied with institutional, national, and local legislation on biodiversity conservation. Documentation can be provided to the editorial office upon request.

Quadrat and population surveys

Field surveys were conducted from April to July (2019–2024) to investigate the population structure of seven wild populations of O. microphylla in ND, JH, LP, TD, JZ, TBC, and CB. The distribution ranges of the seven wild populations were determined through field surveys combined with records from local foresters. To cover all O. microphylla individuals at the distribution sites, we referred to the method of Ohsawa [16] for the population structure survey and made some adjustments in light of the actual situation. We set 20 × 20 m to 30 × 30 m sample plots based on the distribution range of the natural population, and established 13 quadrats. The number and height of O. microphylla in each quadrat were recorded. Individuals were classified into four height categories: (1) 0 m< tree height ≤ 1.3 m; (2) 1.3 m< tree height ≤ 2.5 m; (3) 2.5 m< tree height ≤ 5 m; and (4) 5 m< tree height. These height categories were grouped into four age classes: sapling (tree height ≤ 1.3 m), young tree (1.3 m< tree height ≤ 2.5 m), adult tree (2.5 m< tree height ≤ 5 m), and old tree (tree height > 5 m).

In addition, we also recorded the number of remaining O. microphylla stumps within each quadrat as an indicator of anthropogenic disturbance. Field records of logging traces and remaining stumps were integrated into the population age-structure and demographic-status assessment. Because O. microphylla is a protected species and logging in natural forests is legally restricted, stump records were interpreted as evidence of unauthorized logging disturbance.

Population genomic analyses

At the seven sampling sites, we randomly selected healthy, growing adult O. microphylla trees to collect leaves for genome sequencing. A total of 62 trees were sampled (Table S2). Genomic DNA was extracted from 50 mg of leaves of each individual using the plant DNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentration was measured using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and DNA purity was assessed by OD₂₆₀/₂₈₀ ratio on a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples with OD₂₆₀/₂₈₀ between 1.8 and 2.0 and total DNA ≥ 1 µg were used for library preparation.

Illumina sequencing libraries were constructed for each sample. Based on these data, we performed variant detection, population structure analysis, linkage disequilibrium analysis, gene-flow inference and demographic-history inference.

The reference genome (Ormosia henryi) was 2.64 Gb. Raw reads were filtered to obtain clean reads, and per-sample sequencing quality was summarized using clean reads, clean bases, Q20, Q30 and GC content (Table S4). Across the 62 samples, mapping rates of the 62 samples ranged from 98.53% to 98.98%, reference -genome coverage at 1x ranged from 42.75% to 47.34%, and average sequencing depth ranged from 6.316x to 13.56x. (Table S5). Variants were called using GATK (version 4.6.0.0; HaplotypeCaller, CombineGVCFs and GenotypeGVCFs; https://github.com/broadinstitute/gatk). Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and indels were filtered separately using GATK VariantFiltration with hard-filtering criteria. The report-level filtered variant summary contained 63,597,066 SNPs and 11,340,745 INDELs, with Ti/Tv = 2.639 and 74,937,811 biallelic sites (Table S6). For downstream population-genomic analyses, we used the filtered SNP dataset All.filtered.maf0.05.miss0.2.biallelic.rm.indel.vcf, which retained biallelic SNPs after applying missing-rate and MAF filters and removing INDELs. Genome-wide nucleotide diversity (pi) was calculated using VCFtools (version 0.1.16) with the parameter -window-pi, a sliding window size of 100 kb and a step size of 10 kb. Mapping and sequencing depth summaries were used to assess the suitability and limitations of the resequencing dataset for downstream population genomic analyses.

Population genetic structure was determined using ADMIXTURE (version: 1.3.0; parameter: -cv inputFile K, https://dalexander.github.io/admixture/index.html), with K values ranging from 2 to 10.

Genome-wide linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis was performed using PopLDdecay (version: v3.41; parameters: default parameters, https://github.com/BGI-shenzhen/PopLDdecay).

TreeMix (https://bitbucket.org/nygcresearch/treemix/wiki/Home) was used to infer population differentiation and gene flow from a set of population histories. Moreover, TreeMix 1.3.1 was used to return a maximum likelihood (ML) tree for a population by obtaining allele frequencies from multiple populations and inferring possible hybridization events.

We used smc++ (version: latest; base mutation rate: 4e-9; years per generation: 50) to infer historical changes in effective population size (Ne) from whole-genome resequencing data.

Selective sweep analyses

To identify genomic regions potentially subject to selection, we compared the three genetic clusters defined from the resequencing data using pi-ratio, FST and XPCLR. Genomes were divided into sliding windows. VCFtools (version 0.1.16; parameters: -weir-fst-pop and -window-pi; https://vcftools.sourceforge.net/man_latest.html) was used to calculate FST and pi-ratio values between compared clusters, and XPCLR (version 3.42; https://github.com/hardingnj/xpclr) was used to calculate XPCLR values. Candidate regions were defined as the intersection of windows that ranked in the top 5% across all three indices. KEGG enrichment analysis was then performed for genes located in candidate regions.

Seed number determination

We collected fruits from adult and old O. microphylla trees in TBC, TD, and CB between October and November from 2019 to 2024 to determine seed numbers across populations. The plants for fruit collection were selected as described in Sect. 2.2 (Table S1). Five branches were randomly selected from each individual, and fruits from each branch were collected. After fruit picking, seeds were stripped in the laboratory, and the total number of seeds was recorded. Average seed number was expressed as the mean number of seeds per adult or old O. microphylla tree.

Seed viability

We conducted sowing tests from November 2019 to February 2021 at the Conservation Nursery of the Hunan Provincial Botanical Garden to assess the viability of O. microphylla seeds based on germination rate. Owing to the limited number of seeds collected each year, the sowing trials were conducted over two years.

For the population-level germination comparison, seeds collected in November 2019 were sown immediately after 3 days of cold-water soaking. Five replicate (n = 5, 30 seeds per replicate) seed groups were established for each population, and the germination rate of each replicate group was treated as one observation. Seeds were evenly sown on the planting bed, covered with 1 cm of soil and film, and watered with tap water [17]. Seed germination was measured in April 2020.

To compare germination under different pretreatment methods, we conducted a second sowing test in February 2021. Four pretreatments were applied before sowing: (1) no soaking (CK); (2) soaking in hot water at 45 °C for 6 h (T1); (3) seed-coat scarification (T2); and (4) soaking in concentrated sulfuric acid for 3 h (T3). 270 seeds were sown for each treatment when available. Nine replicate (n = 9) seed groups were established for each treatment, and the germination rate of each replicate group was treated as one observation. Seed germination was determined in April 2021.

Germination rate (%) = (number of normally germinated seeds / total number of treated seeds) × 100.

Soil composition testing

We conducted soil sampling in June 2024 at TBC, TD and CB following the Chinese forestry industry standard LY/T 1210–1999 (Field sampling and preparation of forest soil samples), with modifications for the present field conditions. Three standardized 20 × 20 m plots were randomly deployed at each of the three sites, for a total of nine plots. Within each plot, five soil cores (diameter: 5 cm, soil depth: 30–60 cm) were collected using the five-point mixing method and combined into a single composite sample. Nine soil samples were collected in total (three sites x three plots). The soil samples were placed in self-sealing bags, refrigerated for transport to the laboratory and sieved through a 2-mm sieve to remove stones and plant roots.

The soil pH, dry matter, organic matter, NO3-N, NH4-N, available phosphorous, total nitrogen, total carbon, total phosphorus, and humus, including humic acid, fulvic acid, and humin were measured to assess the soil conditions.

Soil pH was determined using the methods of Hu, et al. [18] with some adjustments. Briefly, soil sample solutions (1:2.5, ratio of soil sample to deionized water) were assessed using a potentiometric method with an acidimeter (Sartorius PB-10). The dry matter content of the soil was determined by drying the soil to a constant weight using the drying and weighing method in an electric blast-drying oven (Boante BAT240-LGF).

The organic matter content in the soil was determined by the potassium dichromate oxidation-external heating method (the ratio of the soil sample to the solution was 1:40), which was heated by an oil bath at 170–180 ℃, cooled, washed with water, and titrated with ferrous sulfate solution [19].

The NO3-N and NH4-N contents of the soil samples were determined using an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (Shanghai Mepuda UV-1800PC). Fresh soil samples were extracted with a 1 mol/L potassium chloride solution for NO3-N and 2 mol/L potassium chloride solution for NH4-N [20]. Soil samples were air-dried and sieved to extract available phosphorus using an ammonium fluoride–hydrochloric acid leachate (1:10 soil-to-leachate ratio). After shaking, centrifugation, and filtration, the available phosphorous content was determined using the molybdenum antimony colorimetric method [21].

The total carbon and nitrogen contents of the soil were determined by dry combustion using an elemental analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) [22].

The soil total phosphorus content was determined according to the methods of Pan, et al. [23] with some adjustments. The soil samples were decomposed using sodium hydroxide fusion (1:8 soil-to-sodium hydroxide solids ratio). Molybdenum antimony anticolorant was added under acidic conditions. The total phosphorus content of the soil samples was determined using an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (Shanghai Mepuda UV-1800PC).

The soil humus content, including humic acid, fulvic acid, and humin, was determined as described by Li, et al. [24] with some adjustments according to the actual situation [25]. Humic acid and fulvic acid in the soil samples were extracted using a 0.1 mol/L sodium pyrophosphate–sodium hydroxide mixture and the ratio of soil sample to mixture was 1:20. The humic and fulvic acid contents were determined by oxidation, precipitation, and filtration with potassium dichromate, and the humin content was determined using difference calculations.

Bird diversity indices

Between April 2022 and May 2023, a bird diversity survey was conducted at three sites: TD, TBC, and CB. The sample line method was used for bird observations, with 3–5 km-long sample lines set up at each site, for a total of 312 sample lines. Observations were performed under clear and windless weather at a walking speed of 1–2 km/h along the set sample lines, and each observation lasted from 07:00 to 09:00 or from 16:00 to 18:00. The names and numbers of birds that appeared and called within 30 m of the left and right sides of the sample line were recorded. Data analysis

Data analyses were organized by data type. Demographic structure was evaluated using field age-class counts from the seven wild populations. Genetic structure was assessed using PCA and ADMIXTURE, and the resulting genetic clusters were used as conservation-relevant grouping units for downstream interpretation. Linkage disequilibrium, gene flow, and effective population size history were analysed using PopLDdecay, TreeMix, and smc++, respectively. Soil properties, bird diversity indices, stump counts, fruiting, and germination variables were summarized by population or representative site. For variables compared among groups, one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test when applicable, was used, with p < 0.05 as the significance threshold. To explore factors potentially affecting reproductive performance, natural recruitment and age structure, we calculated site-level Spearman rank correlations across the three representative populations (TBC, TD and CB) for bird abundance, Shannon and Simpson diversity indices, stump counts, seeds per adult tree, annual seed number and age-structure variables. We further constructed standardized multi-factor indices using min-max scaling for disturbance pressure, biotic support, reproductive output, regeneration status and mature-tree availability. Composite external-stress, population-performance and vulnerability scores were calculated from these indices, and PCA was used to examine how disturbance pressure and biotic support align with the response dimensions of reproductive output, recruitment and mature-tree availability. Because only three overlapping sites were available, correlation, index and PCA results were treated as exploratory evidence integration for conservation planning rather than confirmatory causal tests.

PCA was used to summarize genome-wide genetic variation and bird species composition. Legacy population-genetic and descriptive ecological analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and OriginPro 2025 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA). The newly added site-level Spearman correlation analysis, standardized multi-factor index construction, PCA of the multi-factor indices and publication-ready Fig. S7 and Fig. S8 graphics were generated in R version 4.5.3 using the readxl, openxlsx, dplyr, tidyr, ggplot2, patchwork, ggrepel, svglite and ragg packages.

Results

Age structure of O. microphylla populations

We conducted population surveys of O. microphylla at four sites in Hunan, two sites in Guizhou, and one site in Guangxi; we found a total of 1,329 individuals of O. microphylla (Fig. 1A). Among them, Jianhe (JH) in Guizhou had the largest number of individuals, with 1,020 individuals found. Liping (LP) in Guizhou had the fewest individuals, with only one at a height of 25 m and a breast-height diameter of 155 cm (Fig. 1B, Table S1). In the other populations, the number of O. microphylla individuals did not exceed 100 per site. By combining the cluster analysis and survey results, the seven O. microphylla populations were classified into four categories (Figs. 1B and S1): Category 1 only included the populations from Chengbu (CB) and LP, wherein the individuals had heights greater than 5 m; Category 2 included the population from Tongdao (TD), wherein more than 60% of the individuals were young trees with heights ranging from 1.3 to 2.5 m; Category 3 included the populations from Jingzhou (JZ) and Tangbaocun (TBC), wherein more than 60% of the saplings had heights ranging from 0 to 1.3 m; and Category 4 included populations from Nandan (ND) and JH, wherein the age structure of the trees was suitable for sustainable development.

Population structure analyses

To clarify the genetic relationships among the seven populations, a total of 62 adult individuals were sampled for genome resequencing (Fig. 1C; Table S2). Principal component analysis (PCA) broadly separated individuals by geographic origin, and ADMIXTURE was evaluated for K = 2–10 (Table S3). Although the lowest cross-validation error occurred at K = 2, we retained K = 3 as a biologically interpretable working level because it resolved three geographically meaningful genetic clusters that were consistent with the PCA pattern and conservation-unit interpretation: GX, 26 individuals from Nandan (ND, Guangxi); HG, 26 individuals from Jianhe (JH), Liping (LP), Jingzhou (JZ), and Tangbaocun (TBC); and HN, 10 individuals from Tongdao (TD) and Chengbu (CB). We therefore interpret the three clusters as conservation-relevant genetic units rather than as the only statistically possible clustering scheme. TreeMix analysis did not detect strong gene flow among these working clusters under the parameterization used (Fig. S2). All clusters showed low nucleotide diversity (pi < 0.005; Fig. 1E).

Sequencing and variant-quality summaries were added to support the population-genomic interpretation (Tables S4-S6). Across the 62 resequenced individuals, the dataset contained 184,839,299 (126,581,212–277,720,166) clean reads and 27.31 Gb (18.78–40.11 Gb) clean bases per sample; Q20 averaged 97.17% (95.84–98.94%), Q30 averaged 91.00% (88.45–95.13%), and GC content averaged 36.22% (35.07–37.01%). Reads mapped to the reference genome at 98.77% (98.53–98.98%); mean sequencing depth was 9.203 × (6.316-13.560x), with 45.00% (42.75–47.34%), 30.33% (27.58–33.40%), and 22.80% (18.20-26.83%) of the reference covered at 1x, 5x, and 10x, respectively. Variant calling yielded 63,597,066 SNPs and 11,340,745 INDELs in the report-level filtered variant summary, with Ti/Tv = 2.639 and 74,937,811 biallelic sites. In the missingness/MAF distribution table, 84,634,226 of 84,689,289 sites (99.93%) had a missing rate < = 0.2, and 44,625,714 sites (52.69%) had MAF > 0.05. Because these values indicate low-depth population resequencing and because reads were aligned to the Ormosia henryi reference genome, the genomic results were interpreted primarily at the population or genetic-cluster level rather than at the fine-scale individual genotype level.

The low level of nucleotide diversity was further evaluated in the context of linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns and demographic history. LD decayed rapidly with increasing physical distance between SNPs, as reflected by the sharp decline in r² values across genetic clusters (Fig. 1F). The report-level decay distance values were 52 for ND, 52 for TBC and 62 for TD, with maximum r2 values of 0.4846, 0.4906 and 0.5558, respectively. This pattern suggests that the low nucleotide diversity was not accompanied by extensive genome-wide LD and is consistent with frequent recombination and a possible history of outcrossing. Together with the demographic inference, this result suggests that reduced genetic diversity may be more closely associated with population-size decline than with long-term accumulation of linkage caused by reduced recombination or mating-system constraints. Consistently, smc + + inferred declines in model-based effective population size (Ne) trajectories under the specified assumptions (Fig. 1G), which should be interpreted as historical demographic trends estimated by the model rather than direct census population sizes.

Selective sweep analysis

Integrated analysis of the population age structure and genomic population structure revealed that the GX population exhibited the most stable age structure compared to the HN (lacking young individuals) and HG (lacking mature trees or dominated by senescent individuals) populations. To investigate the potential contribution of genetic adaptation to biological differences between adult trees, we performed selective sweep analyses using GX as the reference population against HG and HN. Employing a combined threshold approach (top 5% of π, FST, and XPCLR values), we identified 282 genes under selection (Fig. 2A). Compared to those in GX, selected genes in HG exhibited FST>0.19, 0.29 < θπ < 1.49, and XPCLR ≥ 6.71, while selected genes in HN showed FST>0.21, 0.43 < θπ < 1.40, and XPCLR ≥ 8.03 (Fig. S3). Among the 165 selected genes in HG, significant enrichment was observed in the cutin, suberine, and wax biosynthesis (ko00073) pathways (p-adjust < 0.05) relative to those in GX. Additional pathways, including RNA polymerase (ko03020) and Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940), were also enriched (Fig. 2B a). For the 117 selected genes identified in HN, KEGG annotation revealed enrichment in pathways such as other glycan degradation (ko00511), phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940), and cutin, suberine, and wax biosynthesis (ko00073, Fig. 2Bb) relative to those in GX.

Similarly, we used HG and HN as reference populations and identified 176 and 150 selected genes in GX, respectively (Fig. 2C). Seed production and seed viability of representative populations

Based on the above findings, we subsequently focused on the CB, TBC, and TD populations because they belonged to different age groups. The TD and CB populations belonged to the HN genetic cluster, and the TBC population belonged to the HG genetic cluster.

In this study, we determined seed production and seed viability in three representative populations (TD, TBC, and CB). The results showed that populations from different genetic clusters could naturally produce fruit. However, the lack of adult trees in the populations or the decline in the reproductive capacity of older trees could reduce seed numbers. Seeds were collected from all three populations selected in this study. However, the annual mean seed number per reproductive adult or old tree varied among populations and declined over time(Fig. 3). The TBC population had the highest seed number each year from 2019 to 2024. The TD population had a significantly higher seed number than the CB population from 2019 to 2023, and the seed number in 2024 was not significantly different from that of the CB population. Meanwhile, the CB population annually produced only a small number of seeds (p < 0.05, Fig. 3A). The 2023 count showed higher numbers of adult and old trees in TD (9) and CB (10) than in TBC (5). The TD and CB populations had significantly lower individual fruiting rates in 2024 than did the TBC population (p < 0.05, Fig. S4).

Environmental and biotic factors affecting O. microphylla populations

Illegal logging

All three representative populations selected were subjected to varying degrees of logging, with the TD population being the most severely logged. Stumps of O. microphylla were observed within all three populations examined. The number of stumps in the TD population was significantly higher than that of the TBC and CB populations (p < 0.05, Fig. 4A). This also implies that the TD population may have experienced the greatest degree of anthropogenic disturbance among the populations studied. This result also indicated that all three groups had larger population sizes in the past.

Soil conditions

Twelve soil variables were measured, including pH. PCA showed that the soil conditions of the TD and TBC populations were similar and different from those of the CB population (Fig. 4B). The ANOVA significance analysis of the 12 indicators did not reveal significant differences in pH and total phosphorus among the three regions (p > 0.05, Fig. S5). Except for pH, NO3-N, and total phosphorus, the soil composition of CB was significantly higher than that of the TBC and TD populations (p < 0.05). This implies that the soils in the CB area were of higher quality than those in the other two sites. Meanwhile, the soil conditions in the TBC area were more suitable than those in the TD area because the contents of seven components, including nitrate nitrogen, humus, fulvic acid, and humin, were significantly higher in the TBC soil than in the TD soil (p < 0.05).

Bird diversity

In the bird observation experiment, 56 bird taxa were identified (Table S7). The total number of birds did not differ significantly among the three sites (p > 0.05; Fig. S6). PCA was performed to analyze the taxonomic composition of birds across the three sites (Fig. 4C, a). The PC1 loading was 50.11%, and the PC2 loading was 26.49%. Ellipse plotting with PC1 and PC2 as the axes showed that the bird species compositions of the TD and TBC areas were similar, although both differed significantly from that of the CB area. Further analysis of bird diversity at the three sites showed that the Shannon and Simpson indices of birds in the CB and TBC were significantly higher than those in the TD (p < 0.05, Fig. 4C b, c). In contrast, the Shannon and Simpson indices did not differ significantly between the birds in the CB and TBC. The bird diversity in the TD group was substantially lower than that in the CB and TBC groups.

Interrelationships among environmental, demographic and biotic factors

To identify factors potentially associated with population structure and to inform conservation planning, we integrated genetic, demographic, and ecological evidence from three representative populations of O. microphylla (Table S8). However, Spearman analyses among bird variables, stump counts, reproductive output, recruitment, and mature-tree availability detected only weak, non-significant rank associations (Fig. 5A and Table S9).

In this study, we standardized multi-factor indices for TBC, TD, and CB, and PCA indicated that plant reproductive output, recruitment, and age structure were the main conservation-relevant response dimensions (Fig. 5B). Specifically, TBC showed the strongest reproductive output and regeneration status, but low mature-tree availability; CB showed the highest disturbance pressure, no sapling or young-tree recruitment, and the highest composite vulnerability; TD showed intermediate vulnerability, with lower plant reproductive output than TBC but higher mature-tree availability than TBC (Table S10). PCA was then used to explore how external drivers align with these reproductive and demographic outcomes. The first axis (PC1, 62.0%) was dominated by regeneration status, mature-tree availability, and plant reproductive output, indicating that the current population condition is most directly expressed through recruitment, reproduction, and age structure (Fig. 5B and Table S11). The second axis (PC2, 38.0%) was dominated by biotic support and disturbance pressure, consistent with the hypothesis that bird-mediated seed dispersal and anthropogenic logging act as external contexts that may modulate reproductive performance, recruitment, and age-structure stability.

Discussion

O. microphylla currently faces a high extinction risk due to synergistic anthropogenic and genetic threats. Given its ecological significance in maintaining biodiversity and its economic value as timber, we conducted comprehensive population surveys across seven naturally occurring populations in three Chinese provinces where O. microphylla is almost exclusively distributed. All populations exhibited varying degrees of vulnerability, necessitating the urgent identification of extinction drivers to formulate science-based conservation strategies. In the present study, age structure refers to the age-class composition and age-structure stability of each wild population, whereas genetic structure refers to genetic groupings derived from genome-wide variation. Genetic clustering was primarily performed to identify distinct genetic management units for conservation planning. All subsequent results were analysed and presented consistently at both the individual-population and genetic-cluster levels.

Unstable age structure in most populations

Usually, plant populations in the field present a certain capacity for natural regeneration and a suitable age structure [27, 28]. However, field data showed that at more than 70% of the surveyed sites, the age structure of O. microphylla was not conducive to population expansion and stable reproduction. These populations were characterized by an over-representation (> 60%) of young trees and saplings with tree heights between 0 and 2.5 m, or of old trees with tree heights > 5 m. Given the overabundance of young trees and saplings in the population, Ormosia trees may be unable to obtain sufficient nutrients to compete interspecifically. Ormosia spp. are light-demanding plants, and their slow growth relative to co-occurring taxa may prevent individuals from accessing sufficient light to develop into large trees [29]. Moreover, when old trees are overrepresented in the population, plant productivity declines and seed production becomes insufficient to sustain the population [30, 31]. The results of the 6 consecutive years of seed number counts supported this hypothesis and suggested that sustained population decline is almost unavoidable. The results of many surveys of threatened plants show that these populations usually exhibit an unbalanced age structure, with an overabundance of old trees and a low number of young trees [32, 33]. This differs from the dynamics of rare Ormosia plants and thus implies that O. microphylla populations with sufficient young trees were still naturally regenerating at some point in the past [34].

Inconsistencies between the population genetic analyses and the field survey data

Population genetics techniques have been widely employed to assess genetic risks in endangered plants, including genetic diversity and adaptive potential [35]. Our analyses revealed low nucleotide diversity across O. microphylla genetic clusters (pi < 0.005; Fig. 1E), indicating a potential threat to species persistence. This low diversity, combined with restricted gene flow among genetic clusters and field evidence of demographic imbalance, may reduce adaptive potential and increase the risk of deleterious allele accumulation [36]. Population history analysis based on resequencing data suggests that this Ne decline may have occurred 10 years ago. However, the predicted population decline of many threatened plants occurred 1000 years ago, suggesting that O. microphylla populations have been strongly disturbed in recent years [37, 38]. Despite the relatively stable age structures of the ND population (GX cluster) and the JH population (HG cluster), their low nucleotide diversity raises concerns about long-term resilience. The admixture patterns observed between the HG and GX clusters suggest shared ancestry and possible historical introgression, but do not demonstrate ongoing contemporary gene flow. Gene flow detected by TreeMix is lacking between the different genetic clusters, which is not conducive to the genetic diversification of populations [39]. Habitat fragmentation also reduces the gene flow in populations within genetic clusters [40]. This low nucleotide diversity and limited gene flow within a population may cause the accumulation of deleterious alleles and reduce its adaptability to environmental stress [41].

Selective sweep analyses indicated positive selection in the GX cluster for genes enriched in linoleic acid metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis and metabolism, and other types of O-glycan biosynthesis pathways, potentially reflecting local adaptation [42, 43]. Enhanced fatty acid and linoleic acid metabolism may contribute to membrane structure, energy storage, and signaling, while linoleic acid may stabilize membranes and support stress tolerance under challenging conditions [44]. Elevated seed lipid biosynthesis may also enhance seed disperser attractiveness [45]. However, both the HN and HG clusters were enriched in the cutin, suberin, and wax biosynthesis pathways, suggesting enhanced seed coat constraints relative to the GX cluster, despite these pathways contributing to seed protection [46]. The HG cluster exhibited greater enrichment (p-adjust < 0.05) than HN (p-adjust = 0.46), as supported by the seed germination experiments; however, the differences were not significant. Dormancy imposed by hard seed coats in Ormosia typically requires scarification or animal digestion for germination, which was experimentally validated [47]. Although hard-seed dormancy in endangered plants can regulate germination timing and persistence, but intensified wax biosynthesis in the HN and HG populations may exacerbate physical dormancy, potentially limiting sapling recruitment.

Although population genetic insights help interpret the population declines and unstable age structures, discrepancies with empirical field data emerged. (1) Endangered plants usually undergo a process of population decline [48, 49]. The smc + + analysis inferred effective population size trajectories that do not directly match the contemporary census patterns observed in the field. (2) Selected genes in the HG cluster were enriched in cutin, suberin, and wax biosynthesis pathways, suggesting possible seed-coat constraints, yet several HG populations were dominated by saplings. These findings imply that external ecological and anthropogenic factors may interact with genomic background to shape the current demographic patterns of O. microphylla.

Multiple factors may explain the contradictory result

Anthropogenic logging and declining bird diversity may help explain these discrepancies [50]. Given the economic value of O. microphylla, overharvesting may be a key factor contributing to its population decline [51, 52]. Traces of logging were found in all three representative populations (TBC, TD, and CB), and individuals with tree heights of 2.5–5.0 m were lacking in all three populations. The TD area, which had the highest number of stumps, had only one adult tree. Stump number may underestimate past logging because stumps can erode over time due to rain, microorganisms, and wind [53, 54]. Nevertheless, the available evidence supports logging disturbance as a plausible contributor to the presence of many young trees but few adult trees in some populations. Strengthening logging regulation is therefore a necessary component of O. microphylla conservation [55].

Additionally, in the TD population, although a certain number of seeds were observed annually, the number of saplings was extremely limited. Seed germination rates were similar across different populations of O. microphylla, and were significantly higher after acid treatment of the seed coat or hot-water soaking. This is similar to the findings in other Ormosia plants, such as O. microphylla, which exhibits seed coat limitation [56]. Additionally, this implies that the TD population lacks suitable conditions for the germination of O. microphylla seeds. However, the soil conditions in TD were similar to those in TBC, suggesting that soil conditions may not be responsible for the lack of saplings; the effect of the soil microbiome should be considered in future studies. In addition, the sample size was limited in this study because most of the collected seeds were used for breeding.

Considering the bird-dependent seed dispersal of O. microphylla, we suggest that the significantly lower bird diversity in TD than in the other areas may be an important factor limiting population development, although the population can normally produce seeds [57, 58]. Similarly, the higher bird diversity in the TBC area (HG population) may explain why the strong seed coat restrictions did not result in a lack of saplings. These results support the hypothesis that bird diversity may affect the sustainability of O. microphylla populations by influencing sapling abundance. The decline in O. microphylla individuals was accompanied by reduced seed production, suggesting that birds feeding on O. microphylla seeds have difficulty obtaining sufficient food, further exacerbating the decline in bird diversity. These results emphasize the importance of biological interactions in conservation, and maintaining these interactions may yield greater conservation benefits.

However, the correlation analysis did not identify statistically significant single-factor relationships between the measured external factors and seed-output or recruitment variables. The multi-factor index and PCA analysis nevertheless showed a coherent outcome-oriented pattern: reproductive output, regeneration status and mature-tree availability defined the strongest population-performance gradient, whereas bird diversity/biotic support and stump-based disturbance defined a secondary external-context gradient. This supports a working interpretation in which bird-mediated dispersal and habitat context may influence seed dispersal and recruitment, while logging directly alters mature-tree availability and age structure. We therefore interpret the decline of O. microphylla as a multi-factor process involving genetic risk, reproductive limitation, recruitment failure, anthropogenic disturbance, and biotic/habitat context. Because the overlapping site-level dataset is small, this interpretation should be treated as an exploratory guide for conservation planning rather than a confirmatory causal ranking.

Conservation implications for O. microphylla

O. microphylla faces interacting internal and external threats, and conservation measures should therefore combine genetic management with habitat and biotic-interaction management. Based on the present data, we propose the following targeted recommendations: (1) systematic age-structure surveys should be conducted across all known populations and should be integrated with information from forestry and biodiversity-management departments to evaluate logging disturbance, habitat condition, and bird diversity [59, 60]; (2) relatively stable populations, especially ND in the GX cluster and JH in the HG cluster, should receive strict in situ protection to maintain their current age structure and prevent future demographic imbalance; (3) populations with active regeneration but insufficient mature-tree availability, such as TBC, JZ, and TD, should be managed to protect saplings and young trees until they reach reproductive maturity, while preventing further loss of adult trees [61–63]; (4) populations with poor recruitment or old-tree-dominated structures, such as CB and LP, should be targeted for habitat restoration, bird-disperser maintenance, and, where necessary, carefully designed ex situ propagation or reinforcement [64]; and (5) assisted pollination and population reinforcement should be applied only within the same genetic cluster and under conservation-genetic guidance, while separate ex situ cultivation lines should be maintained for the GX, HG, and HN clusters.

Implementing these measures may help reduce survival pressures on O. microphylla populations. More broadly, the study shows that conservation planning for threatened woody plants can benefit from jointly considering demographic structure, genetic risk, environmental conditions, and biotic interactions, while keeping management recommendations bounded by the strength of available evidence.

Conclusion

This study evaluated O. microphylla through field surveys, population genomics, ecological measurements, exploratory correlation analysis, and multi-factor integration, providing a scientific basis for conservation planning. Most surveyed populations showed unbalanced age structures, and seed monitoring indicated declining seed production in representative populations. Population genomic analyses revealed low nucleotide diversity (pi < 0.005) and restricted gene flow among three working genetic clusters (GX, HG, and HN), suggesting reduced adaptive potential. Correlation analysis did not support a single external factor as a sufficient explanation for variation in seed output or recruitment, whereas PCA based on multi-factor indices indicated that plant reproductive output, natural recruitment, and mature-tree availability are important population-performance dimensions. O. microphylla therefore faces interacting genetic, demographic, and environmental risks. Targeted conservation strategies should prioritize in situ protection of key populations, strengthen logging regulation to protect mature-tree and age-class structure, maintain bird diversity and habitat conditions that support seed dispersal and recruitment, apply assisted pollination only within genetic clusters under strict safeguards, and establish ex situ cultivation for each genetic cluster.
