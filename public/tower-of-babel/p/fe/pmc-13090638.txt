Spatiotemporal quantification of gingival crevicular fluid NLRP3 expression during non-surgical endodontic management of pulp necrosis with symptomatic apical periodontitis: A prospective case-control analysis
Authors: Karina Singal, Dax Abraham, Monika Tandan, Anjana Goyal, Ruchi Pandey, Rajeev Kumar Malhotra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) levels in pulp necrosis (PN) with symptomatic apical periodontitis (SAP) before and after non-surgical endodontic therapy (NSET) and compare them with periodontally healthy controls.MethodsGCF samples were collected from healthy controls (n = 30) and patients with PN and SAP (n = 30) at baseline and one-week following standardized two-visit NSET. Sampling was performed at three sites per patient: the involved tooth, contralateral and adjacent teeth. NLRP3 concentrations were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Data distribution and variance assumptions were verified using Anderson–Darling and Levene's tests. Between-group and between-site differences were evaluated using unpaired t-tests and analysis of covariance, with longitudinal and clustered observations modelled using linear mixed-effects analysis. Diagnostic performance was examined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.ResultsBaseline NLRP3 concentrations were significantly higher at involved sites than in controls (adjusted mean difference 3.97 ng/mL; 95% CI 3.82–4.12; p < 0.05), and were also elevated at adjacent sites (1.11 ng/mL; 95% CI 0.98–1.24; p < 0.05). One week after NSET, a significant reduction was observed at involved sites (0.32 ng/mL; 95% CI 0.17–0.47; p < 0.05). Significant differences were detected across the three sampled locations (adjusted mean differences 2.93, 4.01, and 1.08 ng/mL). ROC analysis indicated excellent discriminatory ability (AUC = 1.0), with an optimal baseline threshold >2 ng/mL.ConclusionsWithin the limitations of this exploratory clinical study, GCF NLRP3 levels were elevated in PN with SAP and decreased following NSET, supporting its potential role as a candidate inflammatory biomarker.

Introduction

Pulp necrosis (PN) and symptomatic apical periodontitis (SAP) constitute major challenges in endodontic practice and are underpinned by complex inflammatory pathways leading to progressive tissue destruction.1 The pathogenesis involves coordinated innate and adaptive immune responses in which numerous inflammatory mediators regulate both detrimental and reparative processes. Among these, the NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome has emerged as a pivotal regulator of inflammation in dental pulp disease.2

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a cytosolic multiprotein complex that acts as molecular platform for activation of inflammatory caspases, enabling processing of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18.3 IL-1β secretion is mediated through a two-step mechanism, requiring initial induction of pro–IL-1β followed by caspase-1-dependent cleavage after inflammasome assembly. NLRP3 recognizes a broad range of microbial and danger-associated stimuli, including muramyl dipeptide, lipopolysaccharide, and bacterial RNA, highlighting its central role in innate host defence. Increased NLRP3 expression has been implicated in several oral inflammatory conditions, including periodontitis as well as in inflamed pulp tissue and periapical lesions, supporting its relevance to endodontic pathosis.2,4 However, data remain limited on temporal dynamics of gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) NLRP3 levels during endodontic therapy of PN and SAP.

Although histopathological assessment remains the diagnostic gold standard for pulpal and periapical disease, its invasiveness precludes routine chairside implementation. Consequently, there is increasing interest in non-invasive molecular diagnostics using oral fluids, particularly GCF.5,6 GCF reflects site-specific inflammatory activity and may provide real-time insight into apical periodontitis (AP) pathophysiology, as inflammatory mediators and dentinal proteins can diffuse from periapical tissues through the periodontal ligament into the gingival sulcus during active disease.7 GCF sampling is reliable and cost-effective, making it a practical chairside medium reflecting local inflammatory status.8,9 Analysis of inflammasome-related GCF biomarkers may provide insights into endodontic disease pathogenesis and future diagnostic and prognostic applications.10

However, because NLRP3 has also been implicated in periodontal inflammation, careful methodological control is necessary to minimise periodontal confounding when evaluating its role in endodontic disease. In the present study, periodontal status was strictly controlled through clinical screening and exclusion criteria, including the use of Russell's periodontal index, probing depth assessment, and standardised oral hygiene measures prior to GCF sampling, in order to better isolate the contribution of pulpal–periapical inflammation to GCF NLRP3 levels.

Non-surgical endodontic treatment (NSET) is the standard of care for teeth with PN and SAP and demonstrates high clinical effectiveness.11 Nevertheless, molecular mechanisms associated with post-treatment resolution of inflammation, particularly those involving upstream mediators such as NLRP3, remain incompletely characterised. While NSET remains assessed primarily by clinical and radiographic criteria, molecular biomarkers in oral fluids including GCF are currently best regarded as adjunctive indicators of local inflammatory activity rather than direct measures of periapical healing.

Within this context, the present study aimed to evaluate longitudinal changes in GCF NLRP3 levels in teeth with PN and SAP following NSET, and to compare these levels with those of healthy controls, generating preliminary clinical evidence regarding treatment-associated modulation of this inflammasome-related mediator.

Based on these objectives, the null hypothesis of the present study was that there would be no significant difference in GCF NLRP3 levels between teeth with PN and SAP before and after NSET, nor between diseased and healthy control sites.

Methodology

This prospective case-control study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee (MRIIRS/MRDC/SDS/IEC/2024/11), and the study protocol was prospectively registered with the Clinical Trials Registry–India (CTRI/2024/05/066690). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. The study was reported in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1) and complied with International Committee of Medical Journal Editors recommendations and Good Clinical Practice standards.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was determined using a paired t-test design based on anticipated pre-post treatment changes in GCF NLRP3 levels. With a standard deviation of 1 unit, a clinically significant difference of 0.6, a two-sided type I error of 5%, and 80% power, the minimum sample size required was 24 subjects. Allowing 10% attrition, 27 subjects were required. Accordingly, 30 patients with PN and SAP and 30 systemically healthy controls were recruited.

Eligibility criteria

Patient demographics and general health

Systemically healthy adults aged 18–40 years (American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status I) were included to minimise confounding due to age-related variability in inflammatory responses. Participants were required to adhere to study procedures, including the 7-day follow-up.

Diagnostic and dental criteria

Control participants exhibited caries-free dentition and Russell's Periodontal Index <0.9. The experimental group comprised teeth diagnosed with PN and SAP according to American Association of Endodontists (AAE) criteria, with Russell's Index <0.9 to minimise potential periodontal inflammatory confounding. To ensure anatomical standardisation, GCF sampling in both groups was restricted to mandibular molars. Teeth with asymptomatic apical periodontitis, extensive coronal destruction, prior restorative or endodontic treatment, or those unsuitable for rubber dam isolation were excluded.

Collection parameters

Baseline GCF sampling was performed between 08:00–10:00 h to minimise circadian variation.8 All sampling procedures were conducted under strict aseptic conditions to preserve specimen integrity. All clinical and sampling procedures were performed by a single calibrated examiner to ensure procedural consistency.

Systemic exclusions

Individuals with systemic disease or chronic inflammatory conditions (ASA II–VI), pregnant or lactating, diabetes mellitus (HbA1c >7.0%), autoimmune disease, immunocompromised states, cardiovascular conditions requiring long-term anticoagulation, bleeding disorders, metabolic bone disease, or active infection were excluded.

Medication-related exclusions

Participants who had used corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, bisphosphonates, or systemic antibiotics within 4 weeks prior to enrolment were excluded.

Intra-oral and procedural exclusions

Participants with multiple active carious lesions, traumatic occlusion, probing pocket depth >3 mm, active periodontal disease beyond the index tooth, poor oral hygiene (Plaque Index >1.5), recent periodontal therapy (<3 months), or inability to comply with oral hygiene instructions were excluded.

Teeth with anatomical or structural complexities, including pulpal calcifications, root resorption, complex canal morphology, or crown/root fractures, or those anticipated to require surgical management were excluded to ensure standardised chemo-mechanical debridement and procedural consistency.

Behavioural and lifestyle exclusions

Current smokers (>10 cigarettes/day), alcoholics or substance abusers, and individuals with psychological conditions likely to impair adherence to study procedures were excluded.

Participant screening and enrolment

A total of 71 participants were screened at the institutional outpatient department between October 2024 and April 2025. 60 participants were enrolled, comprising 30 healthy controls and 30 patients with PN and SAP. Eleven individuals were excluded due to positive pulp sensibility testing, absence of percussion sensitivity, significant periapical swelling, probing pocket depth >3 mm, refusal of sampling, or specimen contamination.

Procedures employed

Following comprehensive clinical, radiologic, and medical/dental history assessments, eligible participants were allocated into two groups. Group 1 (n = 30) comprised controls with clinically normal pulpal status confirmed by positive thermal and electric pulp sensibility testing and absence of radiographic periapical pathology. Group 2 (n = 30) included teeth with PN and SAP according to AAE criteria, characterised by negative pulp sensibility responses, tenderness to percussion and/or mastication, and radiographic changes ranging from periodontal ligament widening to periapical radiolucency.

To standardize periodontal status and minimise plaque-associated inflammatory confounding, all participants received supragingival scaling and polishing 7 days prior to GCF collection.

Sample collection

GCF was collected via standardized microcapillary technique. Sampling sites were gently air-dried, supragingival biofilm was removed atraumatically, and cotton-roll isolation was used to reduce salivary contamination. Sterile DNase/RNase-free microcapillary tubes (1–5 μL; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. P0549) were used to collect a fixed volume of 1 μL GCF by capillary action from the mesial and distal aspects at the entrance of the gingival sulcus. Because a fixed-volume microcapillary technique was employed, biomarker concentrations were expressed per unit volume, and the time required to obtain the sample was not recorded. In SAP group, GCF was obtained from affected mandibular molars as well as adjacent and contralateral teeth. In controls, GCF was collected from clinically healthy mandibular molars.

All collections were performed between 08:00–10:00 h under controlled ambient conditions and, the entire sampling procedure for each site, including isolation and sample transfer, was completed within approximately 15 min. Samples exhibiting blood or saliva contamination were discarded and recollected after 10 min.12 Immediately after collection, samples were transferred into pre-labelled microcentrifuge tubes and stored at −80 °C within 2 min until analysis.13

Clinical procedure

Preoperative periapical radiographs were obtained using a digital imaging system (Carestream Dental RVG® 5200; Carestream Dental LLC, Atlanta, GA, USA). All procedures were performed under aseptic conditions with rubber dam isolation (Hygiene™, Coltene). Local anaesthesia was administered using 1.8 mL of 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine (Septanest®, Septodont) via inferior alveolar nerve block with supplemental 0.9 mL buccal infiltration to standardize operative conditions.

Endodontic access was established using #2 round diamond bur under water cooling. Working length was established with #10 K-file in conjunction with an electronic apex locator (Root ZX Mini; J Morita Corp., Kyoto, Japan), and the final working length was set 0.5 mm short of the radiographic apex.

A mechanical glide path was prepared, followed by canal shaping using ProTaper Gold® instruments operated at 300 rpm and 2.0 Ncm−1 torque. Mesial canals were prepared to F2 and distal to F3. Irrigation was performed throughout instrumentation using 3% sodium hypochlorite, with a total irrigant volume of approximately 20–25 mL per tooth. A final rinse with 5 mL of 17% EDTA was performed for 1 min. Irrigants were delivered using a 30G side-vented needle positioned 2 mm short of the working length, and canals were dried using sterile paper points.

Calcium hydroxide paste was placed as an intracanal medicament, and the access cavity was temporised with Cavit W® (3M ESPE). At 7-day recall, the provisional restoration and medicament were removed, followed by root canal obturation using continuous-wave warm vertical compaction technique with a bioceramic sealer (EndoSequence BC Sealer; Brasseler USA, Savannah, GA, USA). The access cavity was permanently restored with resin composite (Filtek™ Z250), followed by occlusal adjustment and finishing/polishing (3M™ Sof-Lex™ System). Immediate postoperative radiographs were obtained.

The second GCF sample was collected one-week post-endodontic treatment from identical sites to evaluate early post-operative changes in inflammatory mediator levels. Samples were analysed using a commercially available human NLRP3 sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (FineTest®, Wuhan Fine Biotech Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), according to manufacturer's instructions. All samples and standards were assayed in duplicate, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The assay detection range was 0.781–50 ng/mL with a sensitivity of 0.469 ng/mL. Mean duplicate values were used for statistical analysis. Control subjects required no intervention, and baseline values were maintained for follow-up analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS v20.0 under the supervision of an experienced bio-statistician (R.M.). Anderson-Darling and Levene's tests assessed the normality and homogeneity of variance. Inter-group comparisons for age and baseline NLRP3 employed unpaired t-tests, while gender distribution was analysed via chi-square. Analysis of covariance, adjusting for age, gender, and Russell's index, compared marginal means between cohorts. Intra-site differences were evaluated using linear mixed models with baseline adjustment, and optimal covariance structure was determined by Akaike's information criterion. Violin plots visualized NLRP3 distributions pre- and post-treatment. Bonferroni correction maintained a family-wise error rate of α = 0.05 for post hoc comparisons. Receiver operating characteristics (ROC) analysis assessed the discriminatory power of GCF NLRP3 between disease and control groups. Heat matrix visualization (Supplementary Figure 2) generated via R Shiny app (https://malcolmbarrett.shinyapps.io/metaconfoundr/) depicted confounding patterns with hierarchical clustering.

Discussion

Contemporary endodontic diagnosis relies primarily on clinical symptomatology and pulp sensibility testing; however, these approaches have limitations in accurately reflecting the underlying histopathological status of pulpal and periapical tissues. Quantification of inflammatory mediators in dentinal fluid, pulpal blood, and GCF represents a transition towards molecular diagnostics with improved granularity for characterising local inflammatory activity and monitoring disease dynamics.14

NLRP3 inflammasome activity has been reported in pulpal and periapical pathosis,2,15 and the present study evaluated modulatory effects of NSET on GCF NLRP3 levels in PN with SAP. At baseline, NLRP3 levels were significantly higher at SAP-affected sites compared with controls, with intermediate elevations at adjacent teeth, supporting local diffusion of inflammatory mediators rather than a systemic response while contralateral sites largely maintained control-range values. Following NSET, NLRP3 levels decreased significantly, indicating attenuation of local inflammation and suggesting GCF NLRP3 utility as a surrogate indicator of inflammatory status and treatment response. The 7-day evaluation in the present study represents an early post-operative phase and was intended to capture short-term modulation of inflammatory mediators rather than definitive radiographic periapical healing.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis further demonstrated excellent diagnostic discrimination between SAP and healthy controls at baseline, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 1.0 and an optimal cut-off value of >2 ng/ml based on the Youden index, yielding 100% sensitivity and specificity. Although an AUC of 1.0 indicates complete separation of NLRP3 concentrations between the two groups within the present dataset, such ideal discrimination is uncommon in clinical biomarker research, although it may occur when the measured biomarker exhibits a clear distinction between disease and control populations, particularly within exploratory cohorts with relatively small sample sizes. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

The present findings are consistent with previous evidence demonstrating upregulation of NLRP3 in AP lesions2 and inflamed endodontic tissues16, 17, 18. Mechanistically, NLRP3 activation promotes caspase-1–dependent maturation of IL-1β and IL-18, cytokines implicated in tissue destruction and osteoclast-mediated bone resorption in periapical lesions.18 Modulation of this inflammatory axis may therefore represent a rational adjunctive strategy in SAP management.19,20

The marked post-treatment reduction in NLRP3 suggests that effective endodontic intervention influences both microbial drivers of infection and host inflammatory response, in agreement with reports demonstrating immunomodulatory effects of root canal disinfection protocols.21 Nevertheless, observed inter-individual variability in NLRP3 levels suggests potential contribution from epigenetic and host immune factors, reinforcing interest in personalised inflammatory profiling incorporating NLRP3.22,23

The present study has several methodological strengths, including stringent eligibility criteria, standardised GCF sampling, and quantitative ELISA-based analysis of NLRP3 levels. These methodological considerations address common challenges in biomarker research and provide a foundation for future investigations of GCF-based molecular adjuncts in endodontic diagnosis.24 Furthermore, the study design incorporated site-specific sampling from involved, adjacent, and contralateral teeth along with statistical adjustment for potential confounders using ANCOVA, thereby strengthening the validity of the observed associations.

Despite these strengths, the present study has several limitations. It is limited by its single-centre design, modest sample size, short follow-up, and evaluation of a single mediator using ELISA, which does not capture gene expression or cellular origin. These factors may also limit the generalisability of the findings to broader patient populations and different clinical settings. Additionally, baseline pain intensity was not quantified using a standardised scale, which may have contributed to variability in GCF NLRP3 concentrations. Moreover, although statistical adjustment using ANCOVA was performed, differences in baseline characteristics such as age and Russell's periodontal index may still introduce residual confounding.

Future research should employ multiplex biomarker panels, complementary molecular techniques, and advanced approaches (transcriptomic and multi-omics) along with longitudinal, multi-centre studies with extended follow-up and more rigorous control of potential confounding variables to better elucidate the relationship between inflammasome activity and periapical healing. Inclusion of asymptomatic apical periodontitis cases may further determine whether NLRP3 changes reflect lesion presence, symptomatology, or disease stage. Overall, this investigation provides preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility of GCF-based NLRP3 assessment in endodontic diseases while underscoring the need for further comprehensive research.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this exploratory study, GCF NLRP3 levels were significantly elevated in PN with SAP compared with healthy controls and showed a significant reduction following NSET, approaching control values. These findings suggest modulation of local inflammatory activity following treatment. However, the results should be interpreted as preliminary, and larger multicentre studies incorporating multi-biomarker approaches are required before clinical application can be considered.
