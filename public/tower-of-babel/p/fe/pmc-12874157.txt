Polymerization-Assisted Signal Enhancement and Visual Readout Techniques in Bioassays: A Mini Review
Authors: Nayoung Son, Subhra Sulipta Jena, Seonki Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Polymerization-based strategies have emerged as powerful tools for enhancing sensitivity and enabling user-friendly visual outputs in bioassays. Unlike conventional assays that rely on catalyst- or enzyme-mediated accumulation of molecular products for signal amplification, polymerization reactions produce material-level, macroscopic, or supramolecular structuressuch as hydrogels, polymer films, or insoluble precipitates. This mini review highlights recent advances in polymerization-assisted signal amplification techniques, with a particular focus on detection strategies and polymerization chemistries. We first classify detection approaches according to their readout mechanisms, including direct visual detection and integration with electronic or optical transducers. We then examine representative polymerization reactions employed in bioassays, including enzyme-mediated hydrogelation, nucleic acid polymerization, conductive polymer formation, and controlled radical polymerization. Both enzyme-dependent and enzyme-free systems are discussed, reflecting the growing versatility of polymerization-based platforms for biosensor development.

Introduction

In recent years, bioassays have played a pivotal role across diverse fields, including clinical diagnostics, environmental monitoring, food safety, and molecular biology. In particular, the accurate and rapid detection of trace levels of biological targetssuch as biomarkers, pathogens, or nucleic acidsis critical for early diagnosis and informed therapeutic decision-making. , While the inherent specificity of bioreceptors, coupled with advancements in transducer technologies and nanomaterials featuring unique optical and electrical properties, has significantly enhanced detection capabilities, these methods alone often fall short when it comes to identifying low-abundance targets. As a result, signal amplification strategies have emerged as a vital component in modern bioassay design. , By boosting weak biological signals, these techniques are central to achieving the ultrasensitive detection performance demanded in contemporary analytical applications.

Conventional amplification methods typically rely on catalyst- and enzyme-mediated accumulation of molecular products. For example, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) utilize enzymes such as horseradish peroxidase (HRP) or alkaline phosphatase (ALP) to catalyze the generation of multiple detectable speciesranging from colorimetric to fluorescent or luminescent outputs. − In nucleic acid detection, polymerase-based amplification serves as a cornerstone technique, enabling exponential target replication. − Electrochemical systems often employ redox-active species like benzoquinone or ascorbic acid, which are catalytically generated and undergo redox cycling at electrode surfaces, leading to amplified current responses. Catalytic nanomaterials, such as nanozymes or metallic nanoparticles, have also been explored to enhance signal transduction and stability under diverse conditions. Despite their widespread utility, these approaches share a common paradigm: signal generation is achieved through the repeated catalytic transformation of molecular substrates into detectable products. This dependence on enzymatic turnover and molecular-level reactions imposes several limitations, including restricted amplification gain, sensitivity to environmental factors, background interference, and in some cases, the need for sophisticated instrumentation. Moreover, because the signal remains confined to the molecular scale, these systems can be less accessible or adaptable for use in point-of-care or field-deployable applications.

In contrast, polymerization-based amplification introduces a fundamentally different, material-centric approach to signal generation. While some of these methods may still utilize enzymes as initiators, their underlying mechanisms diverge significantly from traditional catalysis-based systems. Rather than producing small-molecule outputs, polymerization-based systems generate macroscopic or supramolecular polymer structuressuch as hydrogels, polymer films, or insoluble precipitatesin response to specific molecular recognition events. Here, the polymer itself serves as the signal, and its physical manifestationwhether turbidity, viscoelasticity, gelation, refractive index change, or entrapment of dyesprovides a direct visual, mechanical, or optical readout. These material-level signals often require no complex instrumentation, enabling user-friendly and intuitive detection. This unique amplification mechanism, which relies on the bulk accumulation of polymer rather than stoichiometric molecular conversion, opens new avenues for assay design. First, visible physical changes such as gelation or increased turbidity allow for instrument-free detection. Second, the bulk nature of polymer formation offers greater robustness and resistance to noise or degradation compared to molecular-scale amplification. Third, reagents for polymerization-based assays can often be preloaded and dried onto portable substratessuch as paper strips or microfluidic devicesmaking them particularly well-suited for point-of-care diagnostics and use in resource-limited settings. Another key feature is the decoupling of signal generation from enzymatic turnover or fragile molecular probes. In many cases, polymerization-driven amplification proceeds through chain-propagation mechanisms that extend beyond the initial target-triggered stepsuch as radical-mediated chain-growth polymerizationresulting in the formation of large, signal-rich polymer structures.

In essence, polymerization-based signal generation represents a paradigm shift in diagnostic assay designfrom detecting molecular-level events to visualizing material-level responses. This transition enables the development of highly sensitive, robust, and deployable detection systems that bridge the gap between laboratory precision and real-world usability. In this review, we provide a comprehensive analysis of two promising amplification strategies that exemplify this paradigm shift: enzyme-initiated and enzyme-free polymerization reactions. We highlight their mechanistic principles, design considerations, analytical performance, and practical applications. By comparing their strengths and limitations across a range of assay formats, we aim to offer insights that will guide the rational development and selection of next-generation signal amplification platforms for both centralized laboratories and field-deployable settings.

One of the most direct and intuitive detection methods involves monitoring the sol–gel transition, which typically results in a visible change in the transparency or mechanical properties of the sample, allowing qualitative readout by the naked eye. For more quantitative evaluation, changes in viscosity can be measured, as they influence molecular diffusion across membranes or affect the capillary rise of liquids. , In some assay formats, preformed hydrogels are designed to encapsulate dyes that are released upon enzymatic degradation or chemical disruption of the gel matrix, yielding time-resolved optical signals suitable for colorimetric or fluorometric detection.

Beyond the sol–gel transition itself, volumetric changes within the hydrogel matrix provide additional avenues for signal generation. Asymmetric hydrogels, for example, can undergo anisotropic swelling or shrinkage, resulting in observable bending or folding motions. In another strategy, hydrogels embedded with photonic crystals exhibit shifts in diffraction patterns in response to swelling or deswelling, enabling real-time and label-free optical sensing. ,

The polymeric products formed during these assays often feature high bulk density and large surface area, properties that facilitate efficient adsorption of fluorescent dyes. This characteristic proves particularly advantageous in lateral flow assay (LFA) formats, where the accumulation of dye-labeled polymers significantly enhances the visible signal. Furthermore, when polymerization is spatially confined, it can lead to the formation of discrete polymer dots, which are easily visualized and quantified using fluorescence microscopy.

Coupling with Transducers

In addition to direct visual and optical approaches, polymerization events can be coupled with a range of transducer technologies to generate quantifiable and highly sensitive signals. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) offers a robust, label-free detection method by monitoring refractive index changes at or near the sensor surface resulting from polymer accumulation or matrix formation. , Thin-film transistors (TFTs), which are highly responsive to changes in surface conductivity or local charge distribution, are capable of detecting redox-active products generated in situ during polymerization reactions. Similarly, electrochemical sensors can measure current or impedance changes associated with redox-active species or interfacial modifications. ,

Beyond these approaches, additional transduction mechanisms further expand the utility of polymerization-based bioassays. Quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) sensors detect minute mass changes on the sensor surfacedown to the nanogram scaleresulting from polymer deposition. Microcantilever sensors operate on the principle of mechanical deflection caused by surface stress or mass loading, translating these nanoscale changes into measurable outputs. Optical fiber-based sensors, such as fiber Bragg gratings, detect shifts in strain or refractive index induced by hydrogel swelling or contraction near the fiber surface, offering real-time monitoring. In specialized applications, magnetic sensors such as giant magnetoresistive (GMR) devices are used to detect the displacement or immobilization of magnetic particles within or around polymerized matrices.

Reactions for Polymerization

Enzyme-Mediated Hydrogelation

Enzyme-mediated hydrogelation offers a powerful and biocompatible strategy for polymer network formation, particularly well-suited for biosensing and biomedical applications. Operating under mild, physiologically relevant conditions, this approach avoids the need for toxic cross-linkers, extreme temperatures, or harsh solvents. Instead, enzymes act with remarkable specificity and spatiotemporal control, initiating gelation through a variety of mechanisms including ionic cross-linking, oxidative coupling, nucleic acid amplification, and redox-driven polymerization. This diversity of mechanisms enables the design of responsive materials that can react to enzymatic activity found in specific diseases (e.g., cancer- or inflammation-associated enzymes), metabolic pathways (e.g., kinase/phosphatase activities), or signaling cascades. Ca2+-Mediated Gelation

A prominent strategy utilizes enzyme-triggered proton (H+) generation upon target recognition, which in turn leads to the dissolution of calcium carbonate (CaCO3), releasing calcium ions (Ca2+). These ions then cross-link alginate chains, resulting in a visible sol–gel transition. Wang et al. and Xu et al. quantified the extent of gelation by measuring changes in the diffusion radius, indicative of increased viscosity, and reported enhanced sensitivity compared to traditional colorimetric assays. , Expanding on this principle, Wei et al. developed a magnetic relaxation switching (MRS) biosensor for rapid and sensitive pathogen detection (Figure ). This system leverages the fact that water molecules confined within the alginate “egg-box” structure reduce the transverse relaxation time (T 2) of protons, yielding a measurable magnetic signal. Compared to optical or electrochemical sensors, this method offers advantages such as a higher signal-to-noise ratio, faster analysis, and simpler operation.

In addition to promoting gelation, enzymes can also mediate the degradation of preformed Ca2+-cross-linked alginate hydrogels, providing an alternative signal output. For instance, target-specific reactions that generate calcium chelators like citrate can disrupt the hydrogel matrix, offering a visual cue of target presence. Furthermore, beyond interactions with CaCO3, enzymatically induced pH changes can be coupled with pH-responsive hydrogels, undergoing visible volume transitions as another mode of signal transduction.

Horseradish Peroxidase-Mediated Polymerization

One of the most widely used enzymes in hydrogel-based assays is horseradish peroxidase (HRP), which plays a key role in mediating polymerization reactionsfar beyond its conventional use in ELISA. For instance, Lee et al. employed HRP to catalyze the cross-linking of catechol-conjugated biopolymers in response to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) generated via enzymatic recognition of target molecules. Because gelation is selectively triggered by the target analyteeven in the presence of nontarget biomolecules within complex biological fluids such as human serumthe reaction can be considered bioorthogonal. This selectivity enables the assay to be performed in a single step by simply mixing the reagents with the clinical sample. Similarly, Liu et al. incorporated HRP-catalyzed cross-linking of alginate–tyramine (Alg–Tyr) conjugates into a LFA. In this system, HRP immobilized at the test line via antigen–antibody interactions catalyzes localized hydrogelation, which physically blocks further fluid flow beyond the test line. In another application, Kim et al. demonstrated that HRP could locally catalyze the formation of melanin-like, black-brown polycatecholic pigments, enabling distinct visual detection of biomarkers in immunoassays.

Beyond HRP, various other enzymes have also been employed to induce sol–gel transitions or volumetric changes in hydrogels for biosensing applications. For example, Tai et al. developed a system to detect alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity using a phosphorylated peptide precursor that, upon dephosphorylation by ALP, self-assembles into a supramolecular hydrogel. Similarly, protein kinase A (PKA) phosphorylates peptides embedded within a hydrogel matrix, leading to changes in refractive index and diffraction patterns that correlate with enzymatic activity. Importantly, enzyme-responsive hydrogels are not limited to in vitro applications. As reviewed by X. Wang and Q. Wang, these materials play key roles in regulating and monitoring molecular communication in in vivo biological systems, offering potential for real-time physiological sensing and therapeutic feedback.

Nucleic Acid Polymerization

Rolling Circle Amplification (RCA) is a robust nucleic acid amplification technique that generates long single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) moleculesup to 20,000 nucleotides in lengthwith repetitive sequences. RCA is highly compatible with various assay platforms, allowing for flexible and simplified testing formats. For instance, Lee et al. demonstrated the successful integration of RCA with LFA for the detection of the mecA gene, a marker for methicillin resistance in Staphylococcus aureus, achieving a detection limit of 1.3 fmol. In addition, Schmidt et al. immobilized RCA products onto metallic sensor surfaces via immunoassays, enabling signal transduction through surface plasmon-enhanced fluorescence. This approach significantly improved the sensitivity of detection, reaching the single-molecule level. Alternatively, Martín et al. functionalized RCA products with oligonucleotide-labeled magnetic nanoparticles, which induced a shift in the nanoparticle relaxation frequency measurable via AC susceptometry. This method allowed for the detection of as little as 0.75 fmol of a synthetic target within just 20 min of amplification. In another innovative approach, Chen et al. introduced a unique DNA hydrogel formation approach, in which primers released from aptamers upon target recognition initiated the synthesis of two types of RCA products. These products intertwined and self-assembled into a hydrogel matrix capable of entrapping nanoparticles and magnetic beadsenabling both magnetic purification and detection via surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS).

Building upon RCA, an advanced variant known as Circle-to-Circle Amplification (C2CA) has been developed to further enhance signal amplification. This involves monomerizing the initial RCA products and circularizing them into templates for a second RCA cycledramatically enhancing signal output. Notably, Soares et al. applied C2CA to detect synthetic Zika virus (ZIKV) ssRNA, achieving a 1000-fold improvement in the limit of detection (from 1 pM to 1 fM) compared to single-round RCA (Figure A). Their microfluidic platform, which enriched fluorescently labeled C2CA products, reached a detection limit of <1.7 × 103 copies/mL of ZIKV RNA. Similarly, Martín et al. utilized C2CA for the naked-eye detection of the sul1 antibiotic resistance gene. In this system, magnetic nanoparticles aggregated in the presence of C2CA products, allowing for visual detection down to 1 amol from samples containing multiresistance plasmids. To address the complexity of traditional C2CA workflows, Grasemann et al. later streamlined the method, transforming it into a one-pot, single-step, isothermal reaction operable between 25 and 37 °C (Figure B).

Besides RCA, another nucleic acid–based amplification method, loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), can be employed to generate polymeric DNA products. LAMP is performed under isothermal conditions (60–65 °C) using 4–6 specially designed primers that recognize 6–8 distinct regions of the target DNA sequence. As the reaction progresses, high-molecular-weight DNA concatemers consisting of repeated target sequences are synthesized, often forming looped or cauliflower-like structures. These amplicons can exceed 20 kb in length, but due to their heterogeneity, they typically appear as a ladder of bands of varying sizes on gel electrophoresis, which may eventually transition into a smear. Zhu et al. conducted LAMP within a hydrogel matrix and observed that higher gel concentrations restricted diffusion, resulting in smaller, localized amplicon dots. These discrete amplification sites enabled quantification by simply counting the dots, allowing detection of SARS-CoV-2 particles at concentrations as low as 93 copies/mL. The polymeric LAMP products can also be readily detected using lateral flow assays, achieving a detection limit as low as 50 copies/μL. Due to their high molecular weight, LAMP amplicons can incorporate more fluorescent dyes, resulting in stronger signal amplification in fluorescence-based detection assays. Furthermore, Wang et al. demonstrated that the presence of double-stranded DNA significantly enhances the singlet oxygen generation capacity of SYBR Green Iup to 70-foldand applied this principle to develop a highly sensitive colorimetric LAMP assay for hepatitis B virus, achieving a detection limit as low as 1 aM.

Conductive Polymer Generation

Polyaniline (PANI) is one of the most widely used conductive polymers in biosensing due to its simple polymerization chemistry, typically mediated by the widely employed enzyme HRP. Building on this principle, Chen et al. developed an ultrasensitive biosensor by assembling a DNA hexahedral nanostructure (DHN) in the presence of target DNA sequences, which endowed the structure with HRP-mimicking DNAzyme activity capable of catalyzing the polymerization of aniline. The resulting PANI was deposited onto the DHNs through electrostatic interactions and subsequently detected via electrochemical signal readout. In another study, nanozyme-catalyzed polymerization of PANI was integrated into a sandwich immunoassay format and coupled with surface plasmon resonance (SPR) sensing to monitor the resulting refractive index changes. This enabled the sensitive detection of human immunoglobulin G (HIgG), achieving a broad linear range of 0.005–1.0 μg/mL and a low detection limit of 0.106 ng/mL. Interestingly, German et al. compared the signal amplification performance of PANI and polypyrrole (Ppy) synthesized via the same enzymatic polymerization pathway, evaluated through electrochemical methods. Their findings showed that enzymatically synthesized Ppy outperformed PANI, providing 1.35 times higher sensitivity and a 2.57-fold lower limit of detection.

Enzyme-Free Hydrogel-Based Bioassays

While enzyme-mediated hydrogelation offers high catalytic efficiency and reaction specificity, it often requires stringent control over experimental conditions such as pH, temperature, and cofactor availability. Moreover, enzymes are prone to degradation, denaturation, and batch-to-batch variability, which can hinder the long-term stability, reproducibility, and scalability of diagnostic platforms. To address these challenges, enzyme-free hydrogel-based bioassays have emerged as robust alternatives, relying on intrinsic physicochemical interactions between synthetic or biological moleculeswithout the need for enzymatic catalysis.

These systems typically utilize mechanisms such as nucleic acid–based molecular recognition, noncovalent cross-linking, or stimuli-responsive self-assembly to drive hydrogel formation, degradation, or phase transitions. A major advantage of enzyme-free approaches lies in their high degree of programmability, particularly when DNA or RNA strands are employed as both structural scaffolds and functional units. Sequence-specific hybridization enables precise control over gelation kinetics, structural responsiveness, and signal generation, while also supporting complex molecular computations or logic gate operations. Nucleic Acid-Cross-Linked Hydrogels

Hydrogels can be formed through the hybridization of oligonucleotides grafted onto biopolymer backbones, where the oligonucleotides function as cross-linking elements. In these systems, specific molecular recognition by nucleic acids can induce changes in the hydrogel’s physical properties, such as degradation, swelling, or mechanical deformation. For example, Wang et al. developed a DNA-cross-linked polyacrylamide hydrogel that undergoes degradation when the DNA cross-linkers hybridize with target miRNAs, effectively breaking the gel network. Similarly, Li et al. engineered a hydrogel incorporating cocaine-specific aptamers as dynamic cross-linkers; upon binding to cocaine, the aptamers dissociate from the polymeric backbones, leading to gel disassembly. In another design, aptamers prehybridized with complementary DNA strands release their partners upon target binding, allowing these free strands to hybridize with DNA segments embedded in preformed hydrogels, causing volume expansion. Yin et al. further demonstrated that aptamer displacement upon target recognition could trigger the mechanical bending of a hydrogel film, showcasing the potential of nucleic acid-hydrogel systems in mechanoresponsive biosensing.

Target-specific degradation of nucleic acid-functionalized hydrogels can be conveniently monitored, for example, by visual observation in capillary tubes, , or through the release of encapsulated agents such as enzymes that initiate cascade reactions for colorimetric detection. Notably, Jang et al. demonstrated that embedding 2D photonic crystals (2DPCs) within aptamer-cross-linked hydrogels enables detection of target-induced swelling via shifts in Bragg diffraction signals. Likewise, Park et al. observed refractive index changes caused by structural transformations in hydrogels using surface plasmon resonance (SPR). An innovative design by Si et al. used a DNA-cross-linked hydrogel as a physical barrier between biotinylated nanoprobes and surface-immobilized streptavidin. In the presence of the target miRNA, catalytic deoxyribozymes (DNAzymes)were activated and cleaved the DNA-based hydrogel structure. This enzymatic degradation allowed the nanoprobes to access and bind to the streptavidin surface, with the resulting interactions detected via surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS), enabling sensitive and specific miRNA detection (Figure ).

Hybridization Chain Reaction

Hybridization chain reaction (HCR) is an enzyme-free, isothermal nucleic acid amplification mechanism first introduced by Dirks and Pierce, wherein metastable DNA hairpin structures undergo cascade hybridization events triggered by a target sequence, resulting in the formation of long, nicked double-stranded DNA polymers and/or DNA nanostructures. Su et al. developed a microRNA-triggered G-quadruplex (G4) catalytic network via HCR-mediated hydrogel assembly, which generated peroxidase-like activity for the colorimetric detection of miRNA-155, achieving a limit of detection of 138 pM. Similarly, Kim et al. demonstrated multiplexed detection of miRNAs using hydrogel-confined HCR, achieving up to 35-fold amplification and over an order-of-magnitude enhancement in detection sensitivity for two exosomal miRNA biomarkers, hsa-miR-6090 and hsa-miR-3665. Furthermore, Beard et al. enhanced HCR performance for point-of-care applications by simulating intracellular crowding using polyethylene glycol (PEG)-containing evaporation-assisted microdroplets, allowing subpicomolar nucleic acid detection on paper-based diagnostic devices. In another advancement, Pan et al. introduced an antidromic HCR (anti-HCR) strategy, wherein preassembled HCR nanostructures undergo target-induced degradation. This approach enabled ultrasensitive electrochemiluminescent detection of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) at concentrations as low as 2.74 aM by coupling with Al-doped CuS colloidal nanocrystals. Beyond nucleic acid targets, HCR has also been combined with molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs) to create robust, enzyme-free biosensors for protein detection under harsh conditions. For instance, hemoglobin was detected at concentrations as low as 0.006 mg/mL using this approach.

Interestingly, Chen et al. developed a DNA Framework Signal Amplification Platform (DSAP) that enabled surface-confined HCR reactions on immune cell membranes. This platform allowed direct signal amplification without washing steps and enabled immune cell phenotyping comparable to flow cytometry in blood samples from HIV patients (Figure ). Liu et al. achieved real-time visualization of APE1 activity in living cells via a fluorogenic aptamer-assisted HCR system, achieving minimal background interference and a detection limit of 0.1174 U/mL.

When initiators or catalysts for these polymerizations are coupled with target recognition elements, the resulting polymer growth can be directly proportional to the concentration of target molecules. For instance, Wang et al. designed a copper (Cu) catalyst system for ATRP in which the release of the catalyst was proportional to the concentration of the target. This enabled on-surface synthesis of electroactive polymers on an initiator-immobilized electrode, allowing electrochemical detection of the model biomarker CA19–9 with a detection limit of 39 μU mL–1. In another study, Wang et al. employed target-specific immobilization of ATRP initiators on electrode surfaces, leading to the selective grafting of ferrocene signal molecules. This approach allowed electrochemical detection of microRNAs (miRNAs) with an impressive detection limit of 3.23 aM with a linear range from 10 pM to 10 aM. Beyond traditional metal-based ATRP catalysts, metalloproteins such as such as hemoglobin (Hb), HRP, and laccase have also been shown to catalyze ATRP in aqueous environments, enabling more biocompatible, nontoxic, and environmentally friendly bioassays.

RAFT polymerization can similarly be employed for signal amplification when RAFT agents are incorporated in a target-specific manner. For example, Hu et al. reported surface-initiated RAFT polymerization of electroactive polymers on electrode surfaces, triggered by the immobilization of the RAFT agent 4-cyano-4-(phenylcarbonothioylthio)­pentanoic acid (CPAD) in response to thrombin cleavage of its substrate peptide, which was preimmobilized on the electrode. Under optimal conditions, the resulting biosensor achieved a detection limit as low as 2.7 μU mL–1 (∼0.062 pM), with a linear response ranging from 10 to 250 μU mL–1 (R 2 = 0.997). In further studies by the same group, the electroreduction of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) was used to initiate RAFT polymerization following target-specific surface immobilization of RAFT agents. This strategy enabled RAFT polymerization at room temperature, offering a milder and more biocompatible alternative to conventional RAFT reactions, which typically require elevated temperatures (50–70 °C). ,

Conclusions and Outlook

This mini review has examined recent advances in polymerization-assisted bioassays, emphasizing how reactions such as enzyme-mediated hydrogelation, nucleic acid amplification, and controlled radical polymerization can be leveraged to enhance biosensing technologies. These strategies enable the formation of bulk polymeric productssuch as hydrogels, films, or precipitatesthat effectively convert molecular recognition events into macroscopic and often visually detectable outputs. Detection modalities range from direct visual inspection to integration with electronic and optical transducers, offering broad versatility across biosensing applications.

Among the reported approaches, enzyme-mediated hydrogelation (Section ) remains the most widely adopted. Enzymes have long been integral to traditional bioassays due to their high catalytic efficiency and molecular specificity, which enable precise detection of biomarkers in complex biological fluids. However, the repertoire of naturally available enzymatic substrates is relatively narrowtypically limited to small metabolites such as glucose and other simple molecules. In polymerization-based systems, enzymes can act as biocompatible initiators or catalysts, directly triggering polymer formation in situ. This dual role not only accelerates assay kinetics but also provides intrinsic signal amplification. Nevertheless, their high cost, sensitivity to environmental conditions (e.g., temperature and pH), and dependence on limited substrate pools restrict broader implementation.

Although these systems are still under development and validated for only a limited range of targets, two emerging strategies show great promise. The first is nucleic acid–based, enzyme-free polymerization platforms (Section ), which have emerged as highly programmable and structurally versatile alternatives. These systems exploit the precise hybridization and self-assembly properties of DNA to mediate signal transduction, allowing modular construction of nanostructured sensing interfaces. Despite their design flexibility and high biocompatibility, diffusion-limited hybridization kinetics often render these systems slower than enzyme-driven counterparts. The second emerging approach involves controlled radical polymerization (Section ). Techniques such as ATRP and RAFT have been successfully adapted for in situ polymer growth, enabling the generation of conductive polymers for electrochemical detection. The major strength of these lies in its precise control over polymer chain growth, which can reduce structural heterogeneity and improve batch-to-batch reproducibility. While conventional methods typically rely on metal catalysts or specialized RAFT agentsfactors that limit biocompatibilityrecent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of metalloprotein-mediated ATRP, in which natural enzymes such as hemoglobin or HRP catalyze polymerization in aqueous environments. Although further optimization and validation are required, these biomimetic catalytic approaches may pave the way for fully biocompatible and tunable systems capable of tailoring polymer architectures and enhancing signal output for diverse biosensing applications.

Direct comparison of the limits of detection among the discussed strategies remains challenging due to significant differences in analyte type (e.g., proteins, nucleic acids, small molecules), transduction mechanisms, and amplification designs. Nonetheless, it is noteworthy that electroactive polymer generation (Section ) and RCA-assisted hydrogelation (Section ) consistently demonstrate some of the lowest detection limits reported to date, underscoring their potential as leading candidates for ultrasensitive biosensing.

Many existing studies remain at the laboratory or proof-of-concept stage, with limited evaluation under clinically or operationally relevant conditions. Furthermore, most platforms are currently limited to single-analyte detection, underscoring the need for scalable multiplexing strategies. Looking forward, future efforts should focus on enhancing the stability and reproducibility of these systems while advancing their validation in clinically relevant environments. The development of multiplexed platforms and standardized protocols will be key to translating these promising approaches into robust, real-world diagnostic tools.
