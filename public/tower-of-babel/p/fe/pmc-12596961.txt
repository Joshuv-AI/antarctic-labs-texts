EGFL6 is a novel HER3 ligand, inducing HER3/integrin heterodimers to induce pERK centrosomal deposition and therapeutic resistance
Authors: Shoumei Bai, Navneet Gupta, Victoria Liu, Adetunji Fayomi, Stacy McGonigal, Ronald J Buckanovich
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEGF-like domain multiple-6 (EGFL6) is a secreted tumor growth/migration factor linked with poor outcomes in many tumor types. While EGFL6 is known to signal, in part, via its integrin-binding RGD domain, little else is known about EGFL6 receptors. We evaluated putative EGFL6 receptors and found that EGFL6 treatment of ovarian cancer cells leads to both transient phosphorylation of EGFR and prolonged phosphorylation of HER2 and HER3 and subsequent phosphorylation of ERK (pERK). We found that EGFL6 directly binds HER3. However, EGFL6-driven prolonged activation of HER3 is dependent on an intact EGFL6 integrin-binding RGD domain. Immunoprecipitation and proximity ligation assays confirmed that EGFL6 treatment of cancer cells induces HER2/3-integrin-β3 heterocomplexes. Suggesting EGFL6 could play a role in resistance to HER targeting therapies, EGFL6 is upregulated in EGFR/HER receptor inhibitor-resistant cells, and EGFL6 treatment increases resistance to EGFR/HER inhibitors in vitro. Interestingly, we found that, in EGFL6-treated ovarian cancer cells undergoing mitosis, pERK localizes to the centrosome. Both EGFL6-neutralizing antibodies and HER protein-targeted inhibitors resulted in aberrant pERK centrosomal localization with associated altered mitotic spindle alignment and mitotic catastrophe. Furthermore, combination anti-EGFL6 therapy with the pan-EGFR receptor inhibitor neratinib, compared to either therapy alone, led to an increase in aberrant pERK localization and cancer cell death in vitro and significant restricted tumor growth in vivo. Combined, our data suggests that EGFL6 is a new ligand for HER3 and that dual targeting of the EGFL6/HER signaling axis, via altered pERK localization, may be an effective therapeutic strategy in ovarian cancer.SignificanceThis work reveals that EGFL6 is a previously unrecognized ligand for HER3 which can increase resistance to HER family-targeted therapy. We also reveal a novel function of pERK downstream of pHER3 at the centrosome in mitosis. Importantly, we show that EGFL6 is an important therapeutic target to enhance the efficacy of EGFR/HER-targeted therapy.Keywords: EGFL6, HER3-ligand, Integrin-β3, ERK, Ovarian cancer, Therapeutic resistance

Introduction

EGF-like domain multiple-6 is a secreted tumor growth factor that is expressed in both tumor cells and tumor vasculature [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Our group and others have shown that EGFL6 plays a critical role in angiogenesis, ovarian cancer cell proliferation, and migration and metastasis [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Furthermore, high EGFL6 expression is associated with poor survival of multiple cancer types, including cancers of the breast [6], colon [[7], [8], [9], [10]], stomach [11], lung [12,13], and ovary [1,3,14]. Consistent with a role for EGFL6 in driving cancer growth and metastasis, antibodies which neutralize EGFL6 function have shown significant therapeutic benefit in breast and ovarian cancer tumor models [1,3,15].

EGFL6 signaling has primarily been linked to its integrin-binding RGD domain and activation of ERK/AKT pathway phosphorylation, but, EGFL6 has also been reported to activate WNT/β-catenin and TGFB/SMAD signaling [1,8,9,15,16]. While integrin binding is important for EGFL6 ERK activation [1,9,[17], [18], [19], [20]], the presence of EGF domains suggests the potential to bind to the EGFR/HER family of receptors [9,20]. EGFR/HER ligand-receptor interaction results in various receptor dimerization strengths, with distinct impacts on activation strength and duration [21,22]. For example, EGF, a strong EGFR ligand, induces strong but transient EGFR activation that results in cellular proliferation. In contrast, Epiregulin, a weak EGFR ligand, induces weak, more sustained signaling to promote differentiation.

Here, we show that EGFL6 can induce HER3/integrin-β3 heterodimers to induce phosphorylation of EGFR/HER receptors. EGFL6 treatment of ovarian cancer cells led to rapid and transient phosphorylation of EGFR while inducing prolonged activation of both HER2 and HER3. Prolonged activation of HER2/3 depends on EGFL6 integrin binding and the formation of HER2/3-integrin-β3 heterocomplexes. Suggesting a potential role for EGFL6 in therapeutic resistance to EGFR/HER targeted therapies, EGFL6 is upregulated in cell lines resistant to EGFR/HER family-targeted therapeutics, and EGFL6 treatment in vitro increased resistance to these therapies. EGFL6 knockout reduced HER3 phosphorylation and ovarian cancer cell proliferation and increased response to HER family receptor-targeted therapies. Consistent with this, combination EGFL6-neutralizing antibody therapy with the pan-EGFR receptor inhibitor neratinib, compared to either therapy alone, led to an increase in cancer cell death in vitro. Interestingly, we found that, in ovarian cancer cells undergoing mitosis, phosphorylated ERK (pERK) is localized to the centrosome. However, with anti-EGFL6 or neratinib therapy, pERK was aberrantly localized, resulting in altered mitotic spindle alignment and an increased number of cells undergoing mitotic catastrophe. Consistent with these findings, dual anti-EGFL6 and neratinib therapy significantly restricted tumor growth in vivo, resulting in increased tumor cell death and a reduction in angiogenesis.

Results

EGFL6 induces EGFR/HER receptor phosphorylation

Given that EGFL6 has multiple EGF domains, we hypothesized that EGFL6 could induce phosphorylation of the EGF (HER) receptor family. We therefore treated NIHOVCAR3 cells (a CCNE1-amplified HGSC line) with recombinant EGFL6 and evaluated phosphorylation of EGFR, HER2, and HER3. EGFL6 treatment resulted in activation of all three receptors evaluated (Fig. 1A-C). The fold activation and percentage decay of these receptors after activation varied (Fig. 1A-C). Phosphorylated EGFR reached peak phosphorylation (4-fold above baseline) 15 minutes after EGFL6 exposure and returned to baseline by 30 minutes. HER2 reached peak activation between 30 and 60 minutes (∼2.4-fold over baseline) and approached basal levels after 60 minutes. HER3 activation was the most robust, with peak phosphorylation, ∼9-fold above basal levels, occurring at 30 minutes and increased phosphorylation persisting beyond 60 minutes. EGFL6 similarly induced activation /phosphorylation of HER3 in SKOV3 and CAOV3 cells (supplemental Fig. 1). Consistent with HER receptor activation following EGFL6 treatment, we observed increased ERK phosphorylation. Phosphorylated ERK (pERK) was prominent within 7.5 minutes of EGFL6 treatment, peaked at 15 minutes, and persisted for over 60 minutes (Fig. 1A, B). pERK levels most closely mirrored pHER3 levels.

Sustained activation on HER signaling depends on EGFL6 RGD motif

Previously, we showed that EGFL6 activated integrin signaling by binding through an RGD motif [1]. We tested whether EGFL6 activation of HER signaling is independent of RGD binding. We purified both EGFL6 wild type (EGFL6_WT) and RGD mutant (EGFL6_RGE) proteins and stimulated cells with these proteins as above. As expected, EGFL6_WT induced robust and sustained phosphorylation of HER2 and HER3 (Fig. 1D, left panels, Fig. 1E). In contrast, the EGFL6_RGE mutant induced limited and transient phosphorylation of both HER2 and EGFR, peaking at 15 minutes and reduced to baseline by 30 minutes (Fig. 1D, right panels, Fig. 1E). EGFL6 directly binds to HER3 and induces HER2/3-integrin hetrocomplexes

We used co-immunoprecipitation assay to test whether EGFL6 directly binds to HER3 and whether the RGE mutant affects binding. Flag antibody and HER3 antibody were used to pull down the EGFL6 (Flag-tagged) and HER3, respectively, in cell lines expressing EGFL6 with doxycycline-inducible HER3 expression. Anti-Flag immunoprecipitation of EGFL6 pulled down both WT and RGE mutant (Fig. 2A, middle panels). Both EGFL6_WT and EGFL6_RGE mutant proteins co-immunoprecipitated HER3 at similar levels, suggesting that binding to HER3 is independent of the RGD domain. As previously reported, EGFL6_WT immunoprecipitated integrin-β3 (ITGB3), while the EGFL6_RGE mutant showed limited ITGB3 binding (Fig. 2A) [1]. Reciprocally, immunoprecipitation of HER3 co-immunoprecipitated both EGFL6_WT and EGFL6_RGE mutant proteins. As expected, HER3 also co-immunoprecipitated HER2. Interestingly, binding of HER3 to HER2 is reduced when co-expressed with EGFL6_RGE (Fig. 2A, right panels).

To confirm that EGFL6 induces cross talk between HER and integrin proteins, we used proximity ligation to directly visualize receptor complex formation of endogenous HER2/3 and ITGB3 upon addition of EGFL6_WT or EGFL6_RGE. We serum-starved NIHOVCAR3 cells for 3 hours, washed them to eliminate any endogenous EGFL6, and then stimulated them with either EGFL6-WT or EGFL6-RGE mutant for 15 min (when peak activation of HER proteins was observed (Fig. 1, Fig. 2)). We then performed Duolink proximity ligation, using either (i) EGFL6/HER3 antibodies, (ii) HER3/ITGB3 antibodies, or (iii) pHER2/ITGB3 antibodies. In the absence of EGFL6 stimulation, there was little or no association of HER3 or pHER2 with ITGB3. The addition of EGFL6_WT to cells induced a significant number of areas with EGFL6-HER3 co-localization (red dots), and the number of these co-localized complexes was markedly reduced with RGE mutant stimuli (Fig. 2B). The addition of EGFL6_WT similarly resulted in the formation of a robust number of HER3-ITGB3 proximal associations/complexes, which were significantly reduced with the EGFL6_RGE mutant (Fig. 2C). Similarly, phosphorylated HER2 (pHER2), the active HER3 signaling partner, showed a strong co-localization with ITGB3 in the presence of EGFL6_WT protein, and this was greatly reduced in the presence of EGFL6_RGE (Fig. 2D). Combined, these data indicate that EGFL6 binds to and induces a pHER2/HER3-ITGB3 complex.

EGFL6 and therapeutic resistance

We next used CRISPR-Cas to knock out EGFL6 (EGFL6-KO) in NIHOVCAR3 cells, a cell line with high EGFL6 expression [1]. We generated EGFL6-KO via guide DNA targeting EGFL6 exon 3. DNA sequencing of isolated clone F2 confirmed a 38-bp deletion and a 6-bp insertion at the same region of exon 3 (Supplemental Fig. 2A). Down-regulation of EGFL6 protein expression was confirmed via immune-cytofluorescence (Supplemental Fig. 2B). EGFL6-KO cells showed a similar pHER3 response to both EGFL6 wild type and EGFL6_RGE protein treatment with EGFL6 triggering prolonged pHER3 increases and EGFL6_RGE inducing a lower level and less sustained response (Supplemental Fig. 3A). We used these EGFL6-KO cells to evaluate the impact of EGFL6 loss on HER signaling in both 2D and 3D (tumor sphere) culture. EGFL6 knockout resulted in a significant reduction in pHER3 in both 2D and 3D cultures (Fig. 3A, B).

Given the data above, indicating that EGFL6 can activate the HER family of proteins, with sustained increases in pHER3, we next assessed whether EGFL6 could be associated with resistance to therapeutics targeting the EGFR/HER family receptors. In silico analysis indicated that, indeed, EGFL6 was upregulated in: (i) SKOV3 in vivo xenograft tumors that progressed on treatment of trastuzumab or pertuzumab, alone or combined (Fig. 3C) (GEO dataset GDS4814, [23]) and (ii) in the lapatinib-resistant BT474 breast cancer cell line compared to lapatinib-sensitive controls (GDS4083, Fig. 3D). Indeed, using the EGFL6 low Kuramochi ovarian cancer cell line, we found with 4 days of treatment with lapatinib, EGFL6 expression increased ∼2-fold (Fig. 3E). Western blot analysis of wild type and EGFL6-KO cells treated low dose lapatinib, indicated that loss of EGFL6 was associated with decreased pHER3 in response to lapatinib therapy (Supplemental Fig 3B).

To directly test whether EGFL6 could increase resistance to EGFR/HER-targeting tyrosine kinase inhibitors, we treated NIHOVCAR3 with increasing concentrations of gefitinib (EGFR inhibitor) or lapatinib (EGFR and HER2 inhibitor), alone or in the presence of EGFL6. Consistent with a role in resistance, the addition of EGFL6 to NIHOVCAR3 resulted in significantly higher cell viability in the presence of either gefitinib, lapatinib or Neratinib (Fig. 3F-G, Supplemental Figure 3C).

EGFL6 inhibition sensitizes ovarian cancer cells to HER inhibitors

Given that EGFL6 can activate HER receptor signaling and increase resistance to EGFR/HER inhibitors, we evaluated the impact of dually targeting EGFL6 and HER inhibitors. Since EGFL6 induced broad HER family member phosphorylation, we tested the response of wildtype and EGFL6-KO ovarian cancer cells to the pan-HER inhibitors neratinib and lapatinib, which target EGFR, HER2, and/or HER4. In addition, since the greatest impact of EGFL6 was on HER3, we also tested a HER3-specific inhibitor (HER3I, TX1-85-1) [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. At baseline, compared to wildtype, EGFL6-KO NIHOVCAR3 showed significantly slower growth, as monitored by live cell growth (Fig. 5A, C, E). EGFL6-KO demonstrated a further reduction in growth in response to all three inhibitors. We similarly tested dose-response of HER inhibitors in WT and EGFL6-KO cells, using tumor sphere viability assay. EGFL6-KO cells showed significantly fewer viable tumor spheres in a dose-dependent manner, using pan-HER inhibitors and HER3I (Fig. 4B, D, F).

Next, we assessed dual therapy with either neratinib or HER3I and a previously validated humanized EGFL6-neutralizing antibody [15]. We assessed these reagents on EGFL6-expressing NIHOVCAR3 and primary ovarian cancer Pt366 cells. The results showed that, in both NIHOVCAR3 and Pt366 cells, dual treatment significantly inhibited tumor sphere growth as compared to single-agent therapy (Fig. 4G, H).

Inhibiting EGFL6 and HER signaling results in aberrant pERK cellular localization at centrosome during mitosis

ERK phosphorylation downstream of HER2 activation is well established and known to drive cellular proliferation [28,29]. However, the cellular localization of pERK can also impact its function. We therefore evaluated pERK localization in control cells and cells treated with the EGFL6-neutralizing antibody or neratinib, or with dual therapy. In control cells in interphase, as expected, pERK demonstrated a predominantly cytoplasmic localization. Surprisingly, in cells undergoing mitosis, we observed strong localized pERK staining at what appear to be centrosomes (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, pERK localized at the contractile ring/cleavage furrow during cytokinesis (Fig. 4A). In contrast, when cells were treated with EGFL6-neutralizing antibody during mitosis, pERK assumed a disorganized multifocal staining pattern and a concurrent increase in the number of multipolar metaphase spindles (Fig. 4B). Neratinib, similarly to anti-EGFL6 treatment, demonstrated numerous cells in mitosis with multipolar spindles/ectopic centrosomes of smaller size (Fig. 4B). Dual therapy with anti-EGFL6 and neratinib resulted in very low pERK levels. With the diminished intensity, little to no pERK localization was detectable at putative centrosome or contractile ring locales (Fig. 5B).

To confirm that, during mitosis, pERK is indeed localizing to the centrosome, we co-stained pERK with the centrosomal marker γ-tubulin. As expected, pERK and γ-tubulin were co-localized in mitotic phase in control cells (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, in anti-EGFL6-treated and neratinib-treated cells, there were an increased number of γ-tubulin foci, only some of which co-localized with pERK. In addition, there were pERK foci that were not localized with γ-tubulin in treated groups, indicating increased centrosome numbers and ectopic pERK localization outside of the centrosomes.

Quantification of aberrant pERK foci in mitosis revealed significantly higher numbers of aberrant pERK foci in all treatment groups at mitotic phase compared to control (Fig. 6A). Consistent with the immunofluorescent staining results, pERK expression, as revealed by Western blotting, showed moderate inhibition by EGFL6 Ab in both Pt366 and NIHOVCAR3 (Fig. 6B and Supplemental Fig. 4). Neratinib treatment reduced pERK more significantly, with 70-75% inhibition in both cell lines (Fig. 6B and Supplemental Fig.4). Reduced intensity and mislocalization of pERK by EGFL6 Ab or neratinib treatment during mitosis was further confirmed using the HGSC Kuramochi cell line (Supplemental Fig. 5A-B). A pERK intensity plot showed multiple peak signals that represented pERK foci in EGFL6 Ab, neratinib, and combined treatment groups (Supplemental Fig. 5B). Because localization of pERK is not commonly reported at centrosomes, altered localization of pERK during mitosis was further evaluated in the presence of MAPK inhibitors. pERK’s predominant localization to the mitotic spindle structures was lost by two separate MAPK inhibitors: mirdametinib and trametinib (Fig. 6C).

ERK mutations eliminate EGFL6 antibody inhibition on tumor cell growth

Activation of the ERK-MAPK signaling axis results in nuclear translocation of ERK and activation of ERK substates via phosphorylation [[30], [31], [32]]. ERK recruits DEF-containing substrates, or D domain-containing substrates, via DEF- or D domain-docking sites, respectively [33,34]. To evaluate whether ERK mutations alter EGFL6 therapeutic targeting, we introduced wildtype or mutant ERK into NIHOVCAR3 cells. Specifically, we assessed the DEF-docking mutation L232A, or D domain-docking site mutation D319N [33]. EGFP and wildtype ERK2 were used as controls. Transfected cells were treated with EGFL6-neutralizing Ab, neratinib, or both. As expected, EGFL6 Ab or neratinib treatment resulted in cell growth inhibition in EGFP-expressing NIHOVCAR3, and combined treatment further inhibited cell growth (Fig. 6D-I). ERK2_WT-expressing cells had a similar response as that of EGFP (Fig. 6D-II). In contrast, cells expressing either L232A or D319 were irresponsive to EGFL6 Ab treatment, suggesting a critical role for the DEF and D domain in EGFL6-driven ERK docking (Fig. 6D, III-IV). Interestingly, neratinib, activity was unaffected by either mutation (Fig. 6D, III-IV). Combined, these data indicate that EGFL6-driven phosphorylation of ERK is critical to EGFL6’s activity.

EGFL6 antibody and pan-HER inhibitor neratinib dual therapy significantly inhibit tumor growth of ovarian cancer PDX

Given the findings above, we evaluated the therapeutic benefit of an EGFL6-neutralizing antibody (anti-EGFL6), with and without neratinib, in an EGFL6-expressing patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model. EGFL6 antibody or neratinib alone reduced Pt366 PDX growth significantly compared to control (p < 0.0001, Fig. 7A). Tumors in the dual-treatment group demonstrated strong growth arrest for the last 40 days of treatment, plateauing at ∼40 mm3 (Fig. 7A). At the time of euthanasia, average tumor weight in this group was only 0.07 g compared to 0.28 g of the control (Fig. 7B). Evaluation of tumors confirmed that targeting either EGFL6 or HER alone significantly reduced tumor cell proliferation (as measured by Ki67 staining) and that combination resulted in significantly greater reduction in proliferation (Fig. 7C, D and Supplemental Fig. 6). Conversely, we observed increased cell apoptosis in treated cells (as measured by cleaved caspase-3 (Asp175) stain) in all treatment groups, with the greatest level seen with dual-therapy groups (Fig. 7E-F and Supplemental Fig. 7).

As EGFL6 is also linked with angiogenesis, we also assessed ovarian tumor vasculature. Immunofluorescent staining of tumor vessels with CD31, showed a statistical reduction in CD31-positive vessel numbers and total vasculature per high-power field in all treatment groups (Supplemental Fig. 8 A, B, C).

Finally, given the impact of EGFL6 on ERK phosphorylation in vitro, we assessed pERK in tumors. The level of apoptosis in the treated PDX tumors made it difficult to evaluate the level of pERK. Therefore, to assess changes in pERK in vivo, we generated NIHOVCAR3 tumors. When tumors reached an average of 64-92 mm3, we administrated EGFL6 Ab, neratinib, or combination therapy for 1 week, and then the animals were euthanized and tumors harvested. pERK expression was significantly reduced in all treatment groups compared to control. Combined treatment showed a further reduction trend compared to neratinib alone but was not significant (p = 0.2, Fig. 7G-H).

Discussion

The studies completed here demonstrate that EGFL6 is a novel ligand for the EGFR/HER family of receptors, creating a pHER2/HER3/ITGB3 signaling complex. Formation of this complex results in ERK phosphorylation and induction of cellular proliferation, driving resistance to HER2/HER3-targeted therapies. Interestingly, we find that pERK localizes to the centrosomes, and disruption of the EGFL6/HER signaling axis results in aberrant pERK localization and mitotic catastrophe. As such, dual anti-EGFL6- and HER2/3-targeted inhibitor therapy increased tumor response in vitro and in vivo.

EGFL6 as a driver of HER pathway activation. The EGFR/HER family of receptors are known to play a critical role in cancer growth in general and in ovarian cancer specifically. EGFR is overexpressed in most ovarian tumors [35] and has generally been linked with poor prognosis [36,37]. Despite this, efforts to target this pathway therapeutically in ovarian cancer have been unsuccessful. Specifically, agents such as gefitinib (an EGFR inhibitor) and lapatinib (an EGFR/HER inhibitor), while showing activity in other cancers, have had limited activity in ovarian cancer [38,39]. The reason for this resistance in ovarian cancer is unclear. One potential reason could be strong activation of HER3. HER3 activation is linked with resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors targeting EGFR/HER2 [40]. Here, we show that EGFL6 is a strong activator of HER3/HER2 and, as such, could play a role in driving resistance to EGFR/HER2-targeting therapies. Consistent with a role in activating HER3, we saw added activity of anti-EGFL6 therapy with neratinib, an EGFR/HER2/HER4 inhibitor.

EGFL6 as a HER3 ligand. Our results indicate that EGFL6 is a HER3 ligand. In the setting of HER3 induction, immunoprecipitation of EGFL6 co-immunoprecipitated HER3 but not HER2. We did not see significant immunoprecipitation of HER2. This could be because levels of HER2 were too low without induction, or because EGFL6 does not directly bind HER2. Reciprocally, immunoprecipitation of HER3 co-immunoprecipitated EGFL6 and HER2. This was significantly more robust with wildtype EGFL6 vs the RGE mutant, suggesting the HER/integrin complex is more stable. Proximity ligation assay confirmed that EGFL6 induced the co-localization of HER2/HER3/integrin-β3 in an RGD domain-enhancing manner. This work suggests that EGFL6 signals by bridging integrin and HER receptors. This is consistent with several studies that have reported on important cross talk between integrin and HER family members [[41], [42], [43], [44], [45], [46], [47]].

EGFL6 and pERK. ERK phosphorylation/activation is an essential step of MAPK signaling. Phosphorylated pERK translocates to the nucleus and controls gene expression by regulating core oncogenic transcription factors such as Myc, Fos, and Jun [48]. Here and in prior work, we have found that EGFL6 is linked to strong Erk activation. However, in this study, we uncovered a potentially new function of pERK during the mitotic phase. We showed that pERK localized at centrosomes, which are essential for mitotic spindle structure formation, a critical step ensuring accurate segregation of sister chromatids. We found, when ERK phosphorylation is impacted by either EGFL6 neutralization, neratinib, or MEK inhibition during mitosis, ERK localization becomes aberrant, and this is associated with an increase in the number of multipolar spindles and the possibility of mitotic crisis. In line with this, we found that anti-EGFL6 and neratinib therapy are associated with a significant increase in cancer cell apoptosis in vivo. Consistent with our findings of pERK at the centrosomes, Shapiro et al. reported that pERK interacts with the kinetochore. They observed that active ERK has enhanced staining (foci) in misplaced chromosomes that are not at the metaphase plate [49]. We also observed that pERK localized to the contractile ring, the last step of cell division. Additional studies will be necessary for understanding the role of pERK in the centrosome and contractile ring.

Interestingly, mutations to the DEF- or D domain-docking sites or ERK resulted in a lack of response to EGFL6-neutralizing therapy. Future work will be necessary to determine a putative role in regulating ERK docking functions.

EGFL6 and therapeutic resistance. As an activator of the HER/pERK signaling axis, we find EGFL6 can drive resistance to HER family-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Not only does the direct addition of EGFL6 to cells reduce their response to these drugs, but cells which develop resistance to these drugs upregulate EGFL6 in response. Importantly, dual therapy with the pan-HER inhibitor neratinib and EGFL6-neutralizing antibodies shows additive activity in vivo, with a significant impact on cell survival and proliferation.

In summary, we find that EGFL6 activates HER3/HER2 phosphorylation and downstream activation of ERK. We find that pERK normally localizes to the centrosome during mitosis; however, neutralizing EGFL6 antibodies and/or HER1/2/4 inhibition with neratinib resulted in aberrant pERK localization during mitosis and the formation of multipolar spindles. As such, combined therapy with anti-EGFL6 and neratinib resulted in significant restriction in tumor growth and cancer cell apoptosis.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents: The HGSC cell line NIHOVCAR3 was purchased from ATCC and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium, with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, at 37°C with 5% CO2. The HGSC cell line Kuramochi was a gift from Deborah Marsh of the University of Technology, Sydney (New South Wales, Australia) and was cultured under the same conditions. Pt366 primary HGSC was derived from Pt366 ascites as described [15], cultured with RPMI:MEBM (with supplements) [50] at 1:1 and 10% FBS. Passage 3-14 of all cell lines were used. Authentication of cell lines was done every year. Neratinib and lapatinib were purchased from Selleck Chemicals. TX1-85-1 was purchased from Cayman Chemical. EGFL6 was purchased from Sino Biological.

CRISPR-mediated knockout of EGFL6: EGFL6 exon 3 was targeted with guide DNA GTAAGTTTGGTGAGTGCGTGG (Exon3), using pSpCas9(BB)-2A-GFP (PX458) (PMID: 24157548). Individual clones were isolated, and EGFL6 knockout was confirmed by sequencing.

IncuCyte live-cell imaging and cell viability analysis: Cell seeding, treatments, and analysis were as described [51]. Briefly, 3,000 cells/well were treated with vehicle or EGFL6 antibody (10-20 μg/ml), neratinib (0.2 μM), lapatinib (0.2 μM), or HER3I (TX1-85-1, 2.5 μM), followed by IncuCyte live-cell imaging and cell proliferation. Four images/well were taken every 4 hours, and cell confluence was recorded. CellTiter-Glo (Promega) was used for cell viability analysis.

Tumor sphere assay: Tumor cells, 500/well, were plated in 96-well low-attachment plates with serum-free MEBM supplemented with 20 ng/ml EGF, 5 μg/ml insulin, and 10 ng/ml β-FGF. After 3 days, tumor spheres were counted. CellTiter-Glo (Promega) was used for cell viability analysis. For tumor sphere cell counts, tumor cells, 5,000/well, were plated in 24-well low-attachment plates. After 3 days, tumor spheres were counted.

Immunofluorescence and Western blotting: Tumor paraffin slides were deparaffinized, antigen retrieved in citrate buffer (10 mM sodium citrate, pH 6.0), then incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4°C or 1 hour at room temperature, followed by fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. DAPI was used for nuclear staining. For immunofluorescence assay with cultured cells, cells were grown on cover glass, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, followed by immunofluorescent analysis. All antibodies used are listed in Supplemental Table 1. Images were acquired using Leica Application Suite X on Leica DM4B or Leica Stellaris confocal microscopes.

EGFL6 wildtype and RGE-mutant protein purification: EGFL6 WT or RGE mutant, established in p3xflag vector, was transfected into 293T cells. Expression of the proteins were confirmed using Western blot with both flag and EGFL6 antibodies. The proteins were purified using anti-Flag M2-affinity gel binding followed by 50 mM glycine (pH 2.5) elution.

Co-immunoprecipitation: Vector control, EGFL6 WT, or RGE mutant construct was co-transfected with doxycycline (dox)-inducible HER3 vector (pInducer-HER3) into 293T cells in two sets. 200 ng/ml dox was used to induce HER3 expression in one set. Flag antibody was used to pull down Flag-tagged WT or RGE mutant. HER3 antibody was used to pull down HER3. Western blot was used to confirm precipitated proteins.

Duolink proximity ligation assay (PLA) for protein complex formation in situ: NIHOVCAR3 cells were plated on cover glass in 12-well plates. Cells were treated with purified EGFL6 WT or RGE mutant for 15 min after 3 hours serum starvation. The cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by proximal labeling assay, according to the Duolink PLA Fluorescence Protocol (Sigma, DUO92101). Antibodies used are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

ERK2 wildtype and mutant expression: NIHOVCAR3 cells, 2 × 106, were seeded in a T75 flask overnight, followed by transfection, with pcDNA3-EGFP alone (10 μg) or co-transfection of pcDNA3-EGFP (5 μg), with ERK2_WT (5 μg, Addgene #8974) or ERK2_L232A (5 μg, Addgene #8974) or ERK2_D319N(5 μg, Addgene #8979), respectively, using FuGENE 6 (Promega) reagent. Successful transfection was confirmed by GFP expression, using an immunofluorescence microscope.

In vivo Ovarian tumor PDX and treatment: All studies were performed in mice and were conducted according to protocols approved by the University of Pittsburgh IACUC. Pt366, a patient-derived xenograft (PDX) was established with Pt366 ascites, 2 × 106, injected subcutaneously. When tumors reached endpoint, the tumor cells were isolated and passaged with 2 × 106 viable cells. For EGFL6 antibody and neratinib single and dual therapies, second passage Pt366 PDX, 2 × 106 viable cells were injected. After 5 days, mice were divided into four groups (N = 10 tumors/group): Control, EGFL6 antibody, neratinib, and dual-treatment groups. Humanized EGFL6 antibody or human IgG (10 mg/Kg) was administered I.P. twice weekly. Neratinib (40 mg/Kg) was administrated orally 5 days per week as described [52]. Tumor growth was monitored twice weekly. For NIHOVCAR3 tumors, 1.5 × 106 cells were injected subcutaneously. When tumors reached an average of 64-92 mm3, 6 tumors/group were administrated control IgG, EGFL6 Ab, neratinib, or combination therapy for 1 week, and tumors were harvested.
