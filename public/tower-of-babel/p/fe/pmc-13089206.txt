Elevated plasma klotho levels attenuate Alzheimer's disease pathologies and cognitive decline in APOE ε4 carriers
Authors: Jie Yang, Jinghua Wang, Wenhui Chai, Laihong Zhang, Anqi Li, Guoyu Lan, Mingxing Jiang, Xiang Fan, Hongjie Yang, Jun Li, Yalin Zhu, Yue Cai, Pan Sun, Zhengbo He, Xin Zhou, Zhen Liu, Qingyong Wang, Xuhui Chen, Guanxun Cheng, Lu Wang, Mengjie Dong, Ying Han, Tengfei Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONKlotho is a longevity‐associated protein with established neuroprotective properties. However, it is unclear how plasma klotho levels relate to Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathologies and cognitive performance.METHODSIn this study, we examined the associations between plasma klotho levels and plasma biomarkers, as well as amyloid beta (Aβ) positron emission tomography (PET), tau PET, neurodegeneration, and cognition, in 354 older adults. Stratified association, interaction, and mediation analyses were conducted to elucidate apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4–dependent relationships and potential underlying pathways.RESULTSHigher plasma klotho levels were associated with lower AD‐related biomarkers and cognitive decline in APOE ε4 carriers. Plasma klotho and APOE ε4 exhibited significant or marginal interactions with less abnormal changes in plasma phosphorylated tau217, glial fibrillary acidic protein, neurofilament light chain, Aβ PET, and cognition. These AD‐related biomarkers mediated the protective effect of plasma klotho on cognitive function in APOE ε4 carriers.DISCUSSIONThis study suggests that plasma klotho is an APOE ε4–dependent protective factor, which may attenuate AD‐related pathology and improve cognitive performance.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is pathologically defined by the accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau hyperphosphorylation, which can be quantified using positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and emerging plasma biomarkers, at least 15 years before the onset of obvious clinical symptoms. 1 , 2 According to the revised Alzheimer's Association framework, 3 early changes in Core 1 AD biomarkers, including Aβ PET, 4 approved CSF biomarkers, 5 and validated plasma biomarkers, 6 , 7 map onto either the Aβ or the AD tauopathy pathway. Later changes in Core 2 biomarkers, such as biofluid measures 8 and tau PET, 9 provide additional pathological information. In addition to the AD core biomarkers, several markers reflect non‐specific but AD‐relevant pathophysiological processes. Neurofilament light chain (NfL), growth‐associated protein 43 (GAP‐43), and structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are commonly used to assess neurodegeneration across the disease continuum. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and soluble triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (sTREM2), which reflect glial activation, are also closely associated with AD progression. 15 , 16 , 17 Additionally, non‐AD co‐pathologies, such as vascular brain injury and α‐synuclein pathology, are common in the aging population and contribute to cognitive decline. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Together, it is likely that, in addition to Aβ and tau, other pathologies may affect AD pathophysiology and progression. Revealing the roles of extensive co‐pathologies in AD is critical for us to understand the progression pattern of the disease and achieve precise diagnosis and intervention.

α‐Klotho, a longevity‐related protein with both transmembrane and soluble circulating forms, has emerged as a modulator of neurodegeneration. 22 , 23 The transmembrane type is mainly expressed in the kidney and choroid plexus, while the cleaved soluble form circulates as a hormone‐like factor, exerting effects in distal tissues and regions with low α‐klotho expression. 24 The function of α‐klotho in the central nervous system includes enhancing synaptic integrity, 25 promoting neuronal signaling, 26 , 27 suppressing oxidative and inflammatory stress, 28 , 29 and maintaining cognitive function. 30 Importantly, evidence from human studies suggests that apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele status may modify klotho's protective effects. 31 , 32 Several studies have shown that higher circulating klotho levels or the KL‐VS heterozygous genotype are associated with reduced AD risk and better cognitive performance, and these protective associations may be amplified in APOE ε4 carriers. 33 , 34 However, whether APOE ε4 status modifies the association between plasma klotho levels and AD‐related biomarkers, as well as cognitive function, remains unclear. Given klotho's potential neuroprotective functions, its relevance becomes critical in the current therapeutic landscape. Although anti‐amyloid therapies have demonstrated effects in slowing cognitive decline, their limited efficacy underscores the need for additional biological targets. 35

The primary aim of this study was to comprehensively assess the relationship between plasma klotho levels and AD‐related pathologies and cognitive outcomes across different APOE ε4 statuses, thereby evaluating its potential as a therapeutic target. To achieve this, we first evaluated the associations of plasma klotho levels with AD‐related pathologies and cognitive outcomes in APOE ε4 carriers and non‐carriers. Subsequently, we assessed whether APOE ε4 status significantly interacts with plasma klotho levels to influence AD‐related biomarkers and cognitive decline. Finally, we explored the potential mediating effects of AD‐related pathologies in the relationship between plasma klotho levels and cognitive performance.

METHODS

Participants

In this study, we included 354 participants from the Greater‐Bay‐Area Healthy Aging Brain Study (GHABS) cohort (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06183658), a project approved by the Shenzhen Bay Ethics Committee and initiated in May 2021. The GHABS study aims to monitor the pathological features and progression of AD and to identify novel targets for early diagnosis and prevention. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Detailed recruitment criteria and participant characteristics have been reported previously. 36

Plasma collection and analyses

Blood samples were collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes after an overnight fast of at least 6 hours. Plasma klotho levels were measured by the commercially available human soluble α‐Klotho Assay enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Immuno‐Biological Laboratories Co Ltd). Plasma samples were diluted 1:4 and tested according to the manufacturer's protocol. The concentrations of Aβ40, Aβ42, NfL, GFAP, and phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) 217 were detected using commercial Simoa NEUROLOGY 4‐PLEX E (N4PE, cat: 103670), and p‐Tau217 kit (cat: 104570) in Simoa HD‐X Analyzer (Quanterix Corp.). The concentrations of sTREM2 were measured using the Meso Scale Discovery platform with an electro‐chemiluminescence assay developed by the Haass group. 37 , 38 The concentration of GAP‐43 was measured using a commercial human ELISA kit (cat. no. abx250779, Abbexa LTD).

APOE genotyping

APOE genotyping was performed using TaqMan single‐nucleotide polymorphism assays for rs429358 and rs7412, which define the ε2, ε3, and ε4 alleles. Genomic DNA was isolated from EDTA blood samples using a standard DNA Isolation Kit after centrifugation.

MRI and PET imaging processing

Detailed MRI and PET imaging procedures have been described previously. 36 T1‐weighted structural MRI scans were acquired to estimate hippocampal volume (HCV) and cortical thickness via FreeSurfer (v7.2.0). The cortical thickness of the AD‐signature temporal meta‐region of interest (ROI) was derived by combining the entorhinal cortex, fusiform gyrus, inferior temporal gyrus, and middle temporal gyrus. 39 HCV was adjusted for estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) using the method described by Jack et al. 40 Fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) scans were collected to determine white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volume using an in‐house method reported previously. 41

Aβ PET scans were obtained in the GHABS cohort using 18F‐florbetapir (AV45) or deuterated AV‐45 (D3FSP) tracers. Aβ PET standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) was calculated using the brainstem as the reference region for D3FSP 42 and the whole cerebellum for AV45. 43 Cortical Aβ deposition was assessed using a composite Aβ PET SUVR derived from AD‐signature cortical regions, including the bilateral frontal, cingulate, parietal, and temporal lobes. Aβ positive was defined using composite SUVR thresholds of florbetaben ≥ 1.11, 44 D3FSP ≥ 0.78. 42 For participants (n = 47) who underwent two Aβ PET scans using different tracers, Aβ status was determined based on the scan closest in time to plasma collection. 18F‐Flortaucipir (FTP) SUVR was calculated using the inferior cerebellum as the reference region. 45 Tau burden was quantified using the SUVR in the entorhinal cortex and the temporal meta‐ROI, which was defined as a composite region encompassing the entorhinal cortex, parahippocampal gyrus, amygdala, fusiform gyrus, inferior temporal gyrus, and middle temporal gyrus. 46

Cognitive assessments

Global cognitive function was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and the Preclinical Alzheimer Cognitive Composite (PACC) scores. PACC incorporated Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale Cognitive subscale (ADAS‐Cog), Logical Memory Delayed Recall, Verbal Fluency Test (VFT), and Part B of the Shape Trail Test (STT‐B). 47 Memory function was evaluated using the Auditory Verbal Learning Test‐Delayed Recall (AVLT‐N5), verbal ability was assessed with the VFT, and executive function was measured by STT‐B duration. Diagnosis in this study was based on a multimodal approach that combined neuropsychological testing as previously described. 36

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). APOE ε4 carriers were defined as individuals with APOE ε3/ε4 or ε4/ε4 genotypes, whereas APOE ε4 non‐carriers were defined as those with APOE ε3/ε3 or ε2/ε3 genotypes. Additionally, we divided all participants into groups with 0 (ε2/ε3 or ε3/ε3), 1 (ε3/ε4), and 2 (ε4/ε4) copies of APOE ε4 to determine the APOE ε4 allele dosage effect. Participants with the APOE ε2/ε4 genotype (n = 8) were excluded from the study. Data were presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) or number (%). The demographic and clinical characteristics between the APOE ε4 carrier and non‐carrier groups were compared using two‐tailed Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi‐squared tests for categorical variables, with a significance level of p < 0.05 unless otherwise noted. The Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test was used to compare differences among APOE genotypes (ε2/ε3, ε3/ε3, ε3/ε4, and ε4/ε4). Plasma p‐tau217, GFAP, sTREM2, GAP‐43, and NfL were log10‐transformed to achieve a normal distribution. The total WMH volume was first corrected by eTIV and then log10‐transformed.

Linear regression was used to assess the relationship between age and plasma klotho levels. Independent‐samples t tests were conducted to compare plasma klotho concentrations across sex, APOE status, high versus low education attainment (dichotomized at the median of 13 years), and Aβ PET status. Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to compare plasma klotho levels across diagnostic groups.

Generalized linear models (GLMs), adjusting for age and sex, were used to analyze the associations between plasma klotho and other plasma biomarkers, neuroimaging markers, and cognitive scores. This analysis was performed separately within the APOE ε4 carrier and non‐carrier groups to capture group‐specific effects. Furthermore, the interaction effect (klotho × APOE status) was tested in the full cohort to statistically evaluate differences between the two groups. In addition, sex‐stratified analyses were performed to explore the influence of sex on this interaction. To assess ε4 allele dosage effects, we examined the interaction between APOE ε4 allele dosage (0, 1, or 2 copies) and plasma klotho on AD biomarkers and cognitive scores.

Mediation analysis was then performed using the structural equation modeling (SEM) framework (via the lavaan package) to examine whether the standardized effects of plasma klotho levels on cognitive performance could be explained by AD‐related biomarkers. All variables were initially z score standardized. Following adjustment for the aforementioned covariates, the total, direct, and indirect effects of the mediation model were robustly estimated and assessed for significance via a 5000‐iteration bootstrapping procedure.

RESULTS

Demographics of participants

The demographic characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1. Among 354 participants, 114 (32.2%) were APOE ε4 carriers. Specifically, participants were distributed as follows: APOE ε2/ε3 (n = 38), APOE ε3/ε3 (n = 202), APOE ε3/ε4 (n = 104), and APOE ε4/ε4 (n = 10). The APOE ε4 carrier group had a higher proportion of cognitively impaired diagnoses than the non‐carrier group (χ 2 = 8.118, p = 0.017). They also exhibited poorer cognitive performance, as indicated by lower scores on the PACC (p = 0.034), AVLT‐N5 (p = 0.041), and VFT (p = 0.001). In addition, APOE ε4 carriers exhibited higher Aβ (p < 0.001) and tau (p = 0.022) PET SUVR values compared to non‐carriers.

Plasma klotho levels across demographics, Aβ status, and clinical diagnoses

As shown in Figure S1 in supporting information, plasma klotho levels were not significantly associated with age (β std = 0.076 [95% confidence interval (CI): −0.029, 0.180], p = 0.155; Figure S1A). However, higher plasma Klotho levels were observed in females (standardized regression coefficient [β std] = 0.293 [95% CI: 0.084, 0.502], p = 0.006; Figure S1B). APOE ε4 carriers showed a trend toward higher klotho levels compared to non‐carriers, reaching marginal significance (β std = 0.209 [95% CI: –0.013, 0.431], p = 0.065; Figure S1C). Additionally, no significant differences were observed across education levels (β std = −0.082 [95% CI: −0.291, 0.127], p = 0.442), Aβ status (β std = −0.028 [95% CI: −0.259, 0.202], p = 0.809), or diagnostic groups (χ2 = 5.620, p = 0.334; Figure S1D–F).

Association of APOE status, plasma klotho, AD biomarkers, and cognition

In APOE ε4 carriers, plasma klotho levels were negatively associated with plasma p‐tau217 (β std = −0.195 [95% CI: −0.364, −0.026], p = 0.024), GFAP (β std = −0.209 [95% CI: −0.375, −0.043], p = 0.013), NfL (β std = −0.228 [95% CI: −0.388, −0.068], p = 0.005), and composite AV45 SUVR (β std = −0.273 [95% CI: −0.526, −0.020], p = 0.035; Figure 1A and Figure 2A–D). Higher klotho levels were also associated with better cognitive performance, as reflected by higher MoCA (β std = 0.203 [95% CI: 0.030, 0.375], p = 0.021), PACC (β std = 0.295 [95% CI: 0.104, 0.486], p = 0.002), AVLT‐N5 (β std = 0.292 [95% CI: 0.116, 0.468], p = 0.001), VFT (β std = 0.203 [95% CI: 0.016, 0.391], p = 0.033) scores, and shorter STT‐B durations (β std = −0.323 [95% CI: −0.491, −0.155], p < 0.001) in APOE ε4 carriers (Figure 1A and Figure 3A–E). In APOE ε4 non‐carriers, plasma klotho was positively associated with plasma GFAP (β std = 0.134 [95% CI: 0.016, 0.253], p = 0.027; Figure 1B and Figure 2B), while no other associations were observed (Figure 1B, Figure 2A,C,D, and Figure 3A–E). Interaction analyses revealed that APOE ε4 status significantly moderated the associations between plasma klotho and several AD biomarkers and cognitive scores, including plasma GFAP and NfL, composite AV45 SUVR, PACC, AVLT‐N5, VFT, and STT‐B (all p < 0.05; Figures 2B–D and 3B–E). Marginally significant interactions were observed for plasma p‐tau217 (p = 0.057; Figure 2A) and MoCA scores (p = 0.088; Figure 3A). These patterns suggest that the associations between plasma klotho levels and AD pathologies and cognition depend on APOE ε4 carrier status, highlighting the importance of genetic context in understanding the neuroprotective effects of plasma klotho. Full statistics for all interactions are provided in Table S1 in supporting information. Among APOE ε4 carriers, significant APOE ε4 status × klotho interactions were observed for plasma GFAP (β std = 0.643 [95% CI: 0.324, 0.961], p < 0.001), temporal meta‐ROI tau PET SUVR (β std = 0.623 [95% CI: 0.100, 1.146], p = 0.020), MoCA (β std = −0.417 [95% CI: −0.772, −0.062], p = 0.021), and VFT scores (β std = −0.603 [95% CI: −0.955, −0.251], P < 0.001) in males (Figure S2A,C,E,G in supporting information) but not in females (Figure S2B,D,F,H).

AD pathologies mediate the positive association between plasma klotho and cognitive function in APOE ε4 carriers

To investigate potential mechanistic pathways, mediation analyses were conducted in APOE ε4 carriers. Specifically, Aβ PET (composite AV45) SUVR (β std = 0.188 [95% CI: 0.018, 0.397], p = 0.050), plasma p‐tau217 (β std = 0.131 [95% CI: 0.027, 0.248], p = 0.018), GFAP (β std = 0.106 [95% CI: 0.020, 0.206], p = 0.025), and NfL (β std = 0.103 [95% CI: 0.025, 0.197], p = 0.020) mediated 74.6%, 66.5%, 53.8%, and 52.3%, respectively, of plasma klotho‐associated less cognitive decline in MoCA (Figure 5A–D). Similarly, Aβ PET (composite AV45) SUVR (β std = 0.147 [95% CI: 0.017, 0.290], p = 0.036), plasma p‐tau217 (β std = 0.096 [95% CI: 0.012, 0.193], p = 0.039), GFAP (β std = 0.088 [95% CI: 0.013, 0.178], p = 0.036), and NfL (β std = 0.095 [95% CI: 0.011, 0.200], p = 0.055) mediated 51.4%, 48.5%, 44.4%, and 48.0%, respectively, of the relation between plasma klotho and VFT (Figure 5E–H). In contrast, for PACC (Figure S3A–D in supporting information), AVLT‐N5 scores (Figure S3E–H), and STT‐B durations (Figure S3I–L), the association between plasma klotho and cognitive performance was partially mediated by several AD biomarkers. Detailed statistics for all mediation analyses are provided in Table S3 in supporting information.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated the associations among plasma klotho levels, AD‐related biomarkers, and cognitive performance in individuals with different APOE ε4 statuses and further explored potential mediating pathways. This study yields three key findings. First, plasma klotho levels were significantly associated with AD‐related biomarkers, including composite Aβ PET SUVR, plasma p‐tau217, GFAP, and NfL in APOE ε4 carriers, whereas these protective relationships were absent in APOE ε4 non‐carriers. Second, higher plasma klotho levels were associated with better cognitive performance in APOE ε4 carriers, as reflected in global cognition and specific domains. Third, mediation analyses demonstrated that the relationships between plasma klotho and cognitive performance were partially explained by AD pathological markers, suggesting both direct and indirect neuroprotective pathways. Taken together, these findings suggest a potential protective effect of plasma klotho on cognitive performance in older adults with the APOE ε4 allele.

The present study extends prior evidence of klotho's neuroprotective role in AD by showing that plasma klotho associations with AD‐related biomarkers are strongly modified by APOE ε4 status. Previous studies have shown that KL‐VS heterozygosity is associated with higher serum klotho levels and beneficial effects on cognition and brain structure. 48 In the context of AD, KL‐VS heterozygosity has been associated with lower levels of Aβ and tau pathology, as well as a slower rate of cognitive decline, particularly among APOE ε4 carriers. 31 , 32 , 33 Consistent with this, studies examining CSF klotho have reported that lower CSF klotho levels were linked to more severe AD pathologies and poorer cognitive performance. 34 , 49 In contrast, findings regarding plasma klotho have been less consistent, with some studies reporting no significant associations with AD‐related pathologies, 34 , 49 , 50 while others have observed inverse associations with CSF Aβ42 in small sample size. 51 In the present study, we focused on AD‐related biomarkers and cognitive performance across different APOE ε4 status and examined the interaction effects between plasma klotho and APOE ε4 status. Aligning with prior genetic and CSF‐based studies, the associations between plasma klotho and markers of Aβ pathology, tau phosphorylation, astroglia activation, axonal injury, and cognition outcomes were consistently stronger in APOE ε4 carriers than in non‐carriers, with several interaction terms reaching statistical significance. Notably, plasma GFAP exhibited divergent associations with plasma klotho across different APOE ε4 statuses. A negative correlation was observed in APOE ε4 carriers, whereas a positive correlation was found in non‐carriers. Additionally, we observed a marginal increase in plasma klotho levels in APOE ε4 carriers, suggesting a limited compensatory response. Given the reported anti‐inflammatory properties of soluble klotho, 52 , 53 the opposing relationships with plasma GFAP may reflect an adaptive defense mechanism in non‐carriers and a failure of homeostatic resilience in carriers. In summary, these results suggest that the biological role of plasma klotho may vary according to genetic risk background defined by APOE ε4.

Sex‐stratified analyses indicated that the APOE ε4–dependent association with plasma klotho was more pronounced in males than in females. These findings point to potential sex‐specific modulation of genetic risk and resilience pathways, consistent with prior evidence that sex modifies APOE‐related vulnerability and downstream AD pathology. 54 , 55 Additionally, previous studies have reported that klotho levels are associated with sex hormones 56 , 57 and exhibit sex‐specific relationships with various conditions, including diabetes, 58 atherosclerosis, 59 depression, 60 and sleep disturbances. 61 Further studies are needed to elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying these differences.

In addition to interaction effects, mediation analyses revealed that plasma klotho's association with cognition was partially through reductions in AD‐related pathologies in APOE ε4 carriers. Specifically, composite Aβ PET SUVR, plasma p‐tau217, GFAP, and NfL mediated associations with global cognitive score and language fluency, suggesting that klotho may preserve cognitive performance by attenuating Aβ, tau pathology, glial activation, and neuronal injury. However, for cognitive composites, memory scores, and executive function, direct effects remained significant after accounting for the levels of these biomarkers, indicating that klotho may also promote resilience through additional mechanisms independent of core AD pathology. These distinct pathways suggest that klotho functions as a multi‐target resilience factor supporting cognitive function. These mediation patterns further help explain the domain‐specific effects of plasma klotho on cognition. Klotho's protective effects on global cognition and language performance, as measured by MoCA and VFT, were primarily mediated by attenuation of AD‐related pathologies. In contrast, our findings suggest that executive function may be preserved by higher plasma klotho levels, independent of AD‐related pathological markers. Executive functions are critically supported by the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), and prior evidence shows that individuals carrying the KL‐VS heterozygous variant exhibit greater DLPFC volume and slower age‐related atrophy. 62 Moreover, higher systemic klotho levels have been associated with stronger intrinsic connectivity within key cognitive networks, including the default mode network and the frontoparietal control network, 63 both of which are essential for executive function. In addition, the delayed recall component of the AVLT reflects both attentional and memory processes. 64 While this measure is closely related to medial temporal lobe structures that are vulnerable to early AD pathology, memory performance also depends on large‐scale network integrity and prefrontal–hippocampal interactions. 65 Enhanced network connectivity and preserved prefrontal structure may therefore contribute to memory performance in a manner that is not entirely dependent on core AD pathological burden. Together, these observations support the independent or partially independent associations between plasma klotho levels and executive and memory functions observed in the present study.

Our findings suggest that the associations and mediation effects linking plasma klotho to AD‐related biomarkers and cognitive performance are dependent on APOE ε4 status, highlighting plasma klotho as a potential modulator of AD‐related pathologies and cognitive resilience, particularly among individuals carrying the APOE ε4 allele. In line with our findings, one recent study found that higher serum klotho levels mitigate the deleterious association between brain atrophy and cognitive function in older adults, with effects more pronounced in APOE ε4 carriers. 66 This genotype‐specific pattern may reflect a unique interplay between the neuroprotective properties of soluble klotho and APOE ε4–related pathophysiological processes. 67 While the precise molecular pathways underlying the interaction between APOE ε4 and klotho remain incompletely understood, APOE ε4 carriers exhibit more severe AD pathology, characterized by accelerated Aβ accumulation, severe neuroinflammation, impaired mitochondrial function, and increased synaptic vulnerability, which together create an intensified neurodegenerative environment. 68 Within this setting, klotho's functions may exert more detectable effects by enhancing Aβ clearance, reducing neuroinflammation, improving mitochondrial function, and supporting synaptic health, 23 thereby strengthening the negative associations between plasma klotho levels, downstream biomarkers, and cognitive decline in APOE ε4 carriers.

The major strength of this study lies in the comprehensive assessment of plasma klotho in a well‐defined, community‐based Chinese aging cohort. By integrating multimodal biomarker and cognitive data, we systematically evaluated the associations, interactions, and potential mediating relationships between plasma klotho levels and AD pathology, as well as cognitive function, across different APOE ε4 statuses. This approach allows us to delineate APOE ε4–dependent associations and explore potential biomarker pathways linking plasma klotho to better cognitive outcomes, thereby extending prior work that has focused primarily on genetic variants. Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, cross‐sectional analyses limit causal inference, and mediation models provide valuable mechanistic insights but cannot establish directional causality. Second, although we adjusted for key demographic factors, unmeasured confounders, such as lifestyle and comorbidities, may influence both klotho levels and AD outcomes. Finally, given the small sample size (n = 10) of ε4 homozygotes, these findings of ε4 allele dosage should be interpreted with caution and warrant replication in larger samples in the future.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that plasma klotho levels are associated with AD‐related pathologies and cognitive performance in an APOE ε4–dependent manner. These results underscore the importance of accounting for genetic risk in studies of protective factors and suggest that plasma klotho may be a potential target for early intervention in APOE ε4 carriers, who are at higher risk of AD. Future longitudinal studies and replication in additional independent cohorts are needed to validate the protective effects of plasma klotho in APOE ε4 carriers.
