How do the core symptoms of nomophobia differ between males and females: a network analysis
Authors: Yingdi Wang, Fang Luo, Yanan Liu, Tao Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile previous research has established gender differences in nomophobia prevalence and smartphone use motivation, few studies have explored whether the core symptoms of nomophobia differ between males and females.MethodsUsing network analysis, the present study investigated 2,055 smartphone users (1,060 females; 995 males) aged between 17 and 59 (mean age = 27.2 ± 7.3) using the Nomophobia Questionnaire (NMP-Q), to identify gender differences in the core symptoms (i.e., symptoms with high strength centrality) of nomophobia and network structure (i.e., partial correlation among symptoms).ResultsThe core symptoms of nomophobia among males were: (a) could not know if someone had tried to get a hold of me, (b) could not check messages on WeChat, QQ or emails, and (c) afraid of getting stranded. The core symptoms of nomophobia among females were: (a) could not check messages on WeChat, QQ or emails, and (b) disconnected from my online identity. Node centrality and network structure differed significantly between males and females.ConclusionsThese findings illustrate the importance of a loss of online connection in nomophobia and the different network structure of nomophobia between males and females, which may inform component-based preventive programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04460-2.

Introduction

Smartphones have become deeply embedded in individuals’ daily lives. According to the latest national statistics, nearly 1.105 billion people use smartphones to access the internet, accounting for 99.7% of all internet users [1]. As the number of activities that depend on smartphone use is increasing, some unexpected use patterns are appearing, including compulsive smartphone checking [2], smartphone overuse [3], and smartphone addiction [4]. Nomophobia, which is an abbreviation of “no mobile phone phobia”, represents one such problematic pattern of smartphone use [5–7]. Nomophobia was introduced by the UK-based research organization YouGov in 2008 and has already attracted the attention of many researchers.

Nomophobia refers to fear, anxiety, and discomfort when one lacks access to a smartphone and connection to the digital world [8, 9]. According to a systematic review, more than 70% of the population demonstrate an average level of nomophobia that is moderate to severe [10]. This high prevalence suggests that nomophobia has become a common psychological experience with widespread use of smartphones. Previous research suggests that different patterns of smartphone use and psychological needs may be related to distinct symptoms of nomophobia. From the perspective of the theory of extended self, smartphones store personal information and support identity expression, and losing access to them may therefore evoke feelings of self-loss, accompanied by discomfort and a strong urge to regain access [11, 12]. Consistent with this view, prior research has suggested that nomophobia may involve concerns related to losing access to personally meaningful information or feeling disconnected from one’s digital identity [9]. According to the Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution (I-PACE) model, individuals who rely on smartphone applications for gratification or emotion regulation, such as playing games or watching videos, may experience heightened nomophobia-related symptoms when access is disrupted, such as irritability and anxiety [13, 14]. In addition, fear of missing out (FOMO) theory suggests that when individuals are unable to use their smartphones, such as due to battery depletion, anxiety may arise from not knowing what others are doing [15, 16]. Empirical studies have similarly shown that adolescents often perceive smartphones as essential tools for communication and social connection, and report negative emotions such as anxiety and sadness when they cannot access them [17]. Taken together, these findings suggest that nomophobia may consist of heterogeneous symptoms and be driven by different psychological mechanisms rather than representing a single uniform experience. Therefore, studying nomophobia at the symptom level may provide a more nuanced understanding of its psychological processes.

Symptoms of nomophobia commonly include, but are not limited to, compulsive checking habits, feeling annoyed when certain capabilities cannot be used, and experiencing anxiety when unable to communicate through smartphones. Researchers have developed items to capture these typical behavioral and emotional symptoms and then used these items to form a scale for measuring nomophobia. For example, in the Nomophobia Questionnaire (NMP-Q) [18], which is the most widely used scale, 20 items were developed to identify symptoms from four dimensions: (a) not being able to communicate, (b) losing connectedness, (c) not being able to access information, and (d) giving up convenience. However, empirical studies have found inconsistent factor structures across different populations, such as four-factor [19, 20] and three-factor models [21], which suggests that the relationships between these symptoms may be more dynamic and sample-specific than a fixed latent structure can capture. Therefore, an increasing number of researchers have conceptualized disorders as a system to estimate the relationships between all symptoms [22, 23].

According to the I-PACE model, an individual’s core characteristics (e.g., gender) are likely to affect their phone use [24]. On this basis, many prior studies have explored the effect of gender on nomophobia. While moderate to severe levels of nomophobia have been observed in both males and females [25–27], prior studies have also reported significant gender differences in specific symptom dimensions. For instance, Buctot et al. [28] found that girls scored higher than boys on all four dimensions of nomophobia, and Mousoulidou et al. [29] also found significant gender differences in nomophobia among adults. Specifically, one study found that females scored significantly higher on the “inability to communicate”, while males scored significantly higher on the “lack of convenience” [25]. These differences are consistent with gender-specific patterns of smartphone use. Research suggests that females are more likely to use smartphones primarily for social communication and relationship maintenance through social networking services, whereas males are more likely to use smartphones for instrumental purposes, such as gaming and information access [28, 30, 31]. Therefore, given these gender-specific usage patterns, it is important to explore gender differences at the symptom level to gain a more nuanced understanding of how nomophobia presents across genders.

When analyzing relationships between multiple variables, traditional analytical frameworks, such as regression-based approaches, typically adopt a directional structure by assigning distinct roles to variables (e.g., independent vs. dependent variables) [32]. However, such approaches may overlook the complex interdependencies among symptoms. In contrast, network analysis offers a powerful framework to address this limitation by conceptualizing nomophobia symptoms as a system of mutually interacting components with equal theoretical status [23]. The reciprocal connections within the network not only enable us to better understand the interrelationships among symptoms, but also allow us to identify the core symptoms in the network, which may represent the influential factors in the development or maintenance of nomophobia [22, 23, 33]. For multivariate data, the Gaussian Graphical Model (GGM), which estimates a network of partial correlations, is particularly effective at visualizing the relationships between nodes [34]. In GGM, nodes represent the observed variables, and the edge between nodes represents their partial correlation (the correlation after controlling for all other nodes in the network). Node roles are typically indexed according to strength centrality, betweenness centrality, and closeness centrality [35]. In particular, strength centrality, which represents the sum of the absolute edge weights connected to a node, indicates node influence [36], whereby a node with high strength centrality is considered highly influential (i.e., in the present study, a core symptom). Interventions targeting core symptoms will help inform the prevention and treatment of disorders [37].

Moreover, the network comparison test (NCT) can be used to compare the networks of multiple groups [38]. Specifically, NCT is a resampling-based permutation test of invariance in network structure, edge strength, and global strength between groups. Many studies of nomophobia have applied network analysis and NCT to identify core symptoms. For example, Ren et al. [39] applied NCT to compare the network structures of nomophobia and mental health symptoms between middle school and college students and found significant differences in specific edges. Hong et al. [40] used network analysis and NCT to identify the central symptoms of nomophobia and examine differences in symptom networks across genders among Chinese adolescents. To date, however, the symptomatic architecture of the broader adult population remains significantly under-researched. Compared to adolescents, adults integrate smartphones more deeply into their functional autonomy, occupational roles, and social responsibilities. These differences in life context may influence how nomophobia symptoms are structured and interact. Therefore, building upon the methodological groundwork of prior studies, the present research employs NCT to explore gender-specific symptom networks in adults.

While prior research has shed light on the differences in nomophobia prevalence and smartphone use motivation between genders, few studies have focused on the core symptoms of nomophobia and how these might also differ between genders. Understanding the core symptoms in each subgroup can help target prevention and treatment. Hence, the current study aimed to explore: (a) the core symptoms of nomophobia for males and females, separately; and (b) differences in the nomophobia network structure and the role of individual symptoms between genders.

Method

Participants and procedure

A total of 2,068 Chinese smartphone users responded to the study questionnaires. Questionnaires were hosted on the Wenjuanxing platform and distributed via Credamo, a professional online data collection platform. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the Collaborative Innovation Center of Assessment toward Basic Education Quality at Beijing Normal University (approval number: 2026 − 178). Respondents accessed the survey through an online link and received monetary compensation after completing the questionnaires anonymously. All the respondents were asked to complete all the questions before submitting the results. Thirteen respondents were excluded because they failed an inattentiveness check. Accordingly, the final sample was comprised of 2,055 participants, of whom 1,060 (51.58%) were female. Participants’ average age was 27.2 years (SD = 7.3; range = 17–59).

Measures

The NMP-Q [9] was used to assess nomophobia. Previous studies have shown that the Chinese version of the NMP-Q has good reliability and validity [19, 41, 42]. The NMP-Q of the Chinese version is comprised of 16 items, which measure four dimensions: “not being able to reach information” (4 items), “losing connectedness” (4 items), “not being able to communicate” (4 items), and “giving up convenience” (4 items) [19]. Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Network analysis

Item selection

Research suggests that, when applying network analysis to the results of psychological scales, redundancy analysis should be conducted to ensure that individual components are unique [43]. Therefore, in the present study, redundancy analysis was performed using the R package networktools [44] to compare the correlation pattern of each pair of nodes. The results showed that fewer than 25% of the correlations are significantly different for pairs (2 and 4), (9 and 11), (13 and 15), suggesting redundancy [44]. Regarding item selection, we primarily considered the semantic breadth and conceptual clarity of items. Specifically, item 4 was retained over item 2 as it expresses a broader range of smartphone capabilities, item 9 was retained over item 11 to capture the specific anxiety of unreachability, and item 13 was retained over item 15 as it reflects the core state of disconnection rather than a specific behavioral consequence. Therefore, only one node from these pairs was retained (item 4, 9, and 13), and the final network included 13 items.

Network estimation

The R package qgraph (version 1.9.8) [45] was used to construct the GGM for male and female participants, respectively. Applying the Glasso algorithm, small partial correlations were shrunk to zero, inducing a sparse matrix. An Extended Bayesian Information Criterion with the default hyperparameter (gamma = 0.5) was utilized to select the optimal shrinkage degree. Finally, strength centrality measures were calculated to identify core nomophobia symptoms.

Network stability

To test network robustness, the R package bootnet (version 1.6) [46] was applied to evaluate the accuracy of edge weights and the stability of node centralities. Confidence intervals (CIs) of edge weights were estimated using 1,000 non-parametric bootstrap samples. Narrower intervals indicated higher accuracy. The correlation-stability (CS) coefficients of the centrality indices of nodes were calculated using the case-dropping bootstrapping method. According to Epskamp et al. [47], the CS-coefficient should not be below 0.25, and values above 0.50 are preferable for interpreting centrality differences.

Network comparison across gender

Network differences between genders were tested using the R package NetworkComparisonTest (version 2.2.2) [48], which compared male and female participants with respect to global network strength (i.e., the weighted absolute sum of all edges). The difference in network structure (i.e., the absolute maximum difference among all edge strengths) was also calculated. To ensure the stability of the estimated p-values, 2,000 permutations were performed with a fixed random seed. Furthermore, differences in node strength centrality between genders were tested. A p-value < 0.05 indicated a significant difference.

Results

Glasso network of nomophobia

Figure 1 displays the nomophobia networks generated for male (a) and female (b) participants. The female network was sparser than that of males. Of the possible 78 edges in each network, 49 edges (62.8%) in the male network and 45 edges (57.7%) in the female network had a value other than zero (see Supplementary Tables T1–T2).

The partial correlations of nodes belonging to the same dimension were typically greater than those of nodes belonging to different dimensions. Non-zero edge weights for nodes in the same dimension varied from 0.08 to 0.32 for males and 0.10–0.35 for females; in contrast, non-zero edge weights for nodes in different dimensions ranged from 0.05 to 0.21 for males and 0.06–0.20 for females. The strongest edge weights were between N10 (could not receive messages and calls) and N12 (could not know if someone had tried to get a hold of me) for males (weight = 0.32); and between N5 (running out of battery) and N6 (hit monthly data limit) for females (weight = 0.35).

The 95% bootstrap CIs of the edge weights were relatively narrow, suggesting good precision in the estimation (see Supplementary Figures F1–F2). In addition, bootstrap difference tests indicated that many edge weights differed significantly from one another (see Supplementary Figures F3–F4).

Core symptoms of nomophobia

Figure 2 shows the raw and standardized strength centralities of symptoms in male and female participants’ nomophobia networks, respectively. In the male network, N12 (could not know if someone had tried to get a hold of me), N16 (could not check messages on WeChat, QQ or emails), and N8 (afraid of getting stranded) demonstrated the strongest centrality, and therefore the greatest influence within the network. More specifically, the bootstrapped difference test showed that the strengths of N16 and N8 were significantly greater than those of more than half of the other network nodes (see Supplementary Figure F5). In the female network, N16 (could not check messages on WeChat, QQ or emails) and N13 (disconnected from my online identity) emerged as the nodes with the strongest centrality. The bootstrapped difference test showed that the strengths of N13 and N16 were significantly greater than those of one-third of the remaining network nodes (see Supplementary Figure F6). For both the male and the female networks, the CS coefficients of strength centrality were 0.36 (> 0.25), indicating an adequately stable estimation (see Supplementary Figures F7–F8).

As shown in Fig. 2, the raw strength centralities of nodes pertaining to a loss of connection in the female network were greater than those of the equivalent nodes in the male network. In contrast, the raw strength centralities of nodes pertaining to an inability to communicate, a loss of convenience, and an inability to access information were smaller in the female network, relative to the male network.

Network comparison

The NCT showed a significant gender difference in network structure (p = 0.031). Therefore, edge weight differences between genders were tested further. The results showed significant gender differences in edge weights between N3 (unable to get the news) and N5 (running out of battery), N5 (running out of battery) and N6 (hit monthly data limit), and N7 (did not have a data signal or could not connect to Wi-Fi) and N12 (could not know if someone had tried to get a hold of me) (p = 0.025, p = 0.001, and p = 0.019, respectively). Additionally, the global strength of the male nomophobia network (6.174) was significantly different from that of the female network (6.064, p < 0.05). Finally, differences in node strength centrality between genders were tested, with significant differences emerging for N4 (could not use smartphones and/or its capabilities) (p = 0.02).

Discussion

The present study used network analysis to explore gender-specific core symptoms of nomophobia within an adult population. The results suggest that the core symptoms of nomophobia differ according to gender, likely reflecting the different functional and social demands faced in adulthood. The network analysis sheds light on the differences in nomophobia between genders on a component level, to inform the development of preventive and intervention programs.

The finding that “could not check messages on WeChat, QQ or emails” (N16) emerged as a core symptom in both male and female networks partially supports our hypotheses and also reflects an evolving pattern of nomophobia among adults in the digital era. This result can be explained from two complementary perspectives. First, social media applications are major drivers of smartphone usage [49]. For both genders, interactions and self-presentation on social media influence the formation and development of self-identity [50]. Accordingly, when they are unable to use a smartphone to send and receive messages on social media, they may face threats to self-identity, resulting in anxiety or depression [51]. Second, social media platforms such as WeChat increasingly function as multifunctional infrastructures integrating social communication with task coordination and information seeking [52]. In such contexts, checking messages may also serve practical purposes, such as coordinating responsibilities or accessing important information related to work. Thus, individuals may experience concern about missing important information or responsibilities when unable to read or respond to messages promptly [53, 54]. Notably, although N16 is categorized within the losing connectedness dimension, these contextual features suggest that concerns about connectedness in adulthood may be closely intertwined with everyday role demands. Therefore, compared with earlier findings that social media was primarily associated with female communicative preferences [28, 30, 31], the present study found that N16 is the core symptom in both male and female networks. Furthermore, compared with adolescents, whose core symptoms focus on “staying up-to-date” or “keeping in touch with family” [40], which more directly index connectedness concerns, the core symptom of N16 in the adult network may indicate a shift in how connectedness concerns are expressed in adulthood.

Specifically, in the male network, “could not know if someone had tried to get a hold of me” (N12) and “afraid of getting stranded” (N8) emerged as core symptoms, whereas in the female network, “disconnected from my online identity” (N13) emerged as a core symptom. These gender-specific patterns can be interpreted through Social Role Theory (SRT), which posits that societal and cultural expectations shape distinct behavioral patterns for men and women [55]. In Chinese society, which emphasizes interpersonal harmony and social connection, females are often socialized to prioritize relational maintenance more than males [56]. This may explain why “disconnection from my online identity” (N13) emerged as a core symptom specifically for females, as their self-identity is more tightly interwoven with their social networks [57]. Meanwhile, the male core symptom of “could not know if someone had tried to get a hold of me” (N12) may reflect their role as primary breadwinners within the family under traditional gender expectations [58], where missing critical communications could potentially impact professional opportunities and economic security [59]. Another core symptom in the male nomophobia network was “afraid of getting stranded” (N8), related to a loss of convenience. This finding is consistent with previous research suggesting that men rely on mobile phone technology to enhance their independence and efficiency, while women use smartphones mainly for communication and social networking [60]. In China, smartphone connectivity is deeply intertwined with essential functions such as identity verification, navigation, mobile payments, and social participation. Under such conditions, men may become more reliant on the convenience afforded by smartphones, which may explain why N8 emerged as a core symptom in the male network.

Taken together, the sets of core symptoms in the male (N16, N12, and N8) and female (N16 and N13) networks may reflect different smartphone usage patterns across genders. This result may appear inconsistent with some research findings, which have shown that males primarily use their smartphones for work and entertainment [61], while females primarily use their smartphones for social interaction [62]. However, a closer examination of gender-specific patterns of social media use shows that women tend to engage in positive interactions on social media to maintain extensive interpersonal networks [63], while men tend to use social media more for information access and task-oriented activities [64]. Therefore, these patterns might theoretically suggest that the male core symptoms revolve more around an instrumental pattern of smartphone usage, while the female core symptoms appear to focus more on relational connectedness.

Research implications and limitations

The present results have some implications. First, for both males and females, the centralities of symptoms related to a loss of connection and an inability to communicate were higher than the centralities of symptoms related to a loss of convenience and a lack of access to information. This indicates that online communication may be the main motivation for smartphone use [49], and dependence on smartphones may stem more from a desire to engage in interpersonal activities than from practical needs [65]. Indeed, individuals obtain self-identity and belongingness from both in-person and virtual socialization [50, 66]. Thus, the relationship between nomophobia and psychological factors merits greater research attention. Second, for males, there were various core symptoms of nomophobia; while for females, the core symptoms of nomophobia were more focused. These differences suggest that potential interventions may need to consider gender-specific symptoms, as men may exhibit a structural pattern of multiple core symptoms, while women’s nomophobia might be driven by a more concentrated set of core symptoms. Third, our application of network analysis provides significant theoretical advantages over traditional approaches. This method allows us to conceptualize nomophobia not as a latent trait, but as a system of interacting symptoms, offering unique insights into the complex structure of this phenomenon. By identifying the most central symptoms–the ones with the strongest connections to others in the network–we reveal potential intervention targets that could disrupt the entire network of nomophobia. For instance, targeting “unable to check messages on social media” (N16), which was central for both genders, might produce cascading effects throughout the symptom network. This approach moves beyond simply assessing severity to understanding the functional relationships between different aspects of nomophobia, representing a significant methodological advancement in the field.

The present study also suffered from some limitations. First, while some studies have proposed severity classifications based on questionnaire scores, nomophobia has not been formally recognized in the DSM-5 or ICD-11. Consequently, no clinically validated diagnostic cut-off for nomophobia has been established. Future studies may benefit from determining a cut-off point based on large data sets and selecting only clinical subjects; this may contribute to further clarifying the core symptoms of nomophobia. Second, the network analysis only showed the core symptoms of nomophobia, and not how they interact. Thus, future research should use directed graphs or causal inference to explore the interaction between nomophobia symptoms. Third, the present study measured nomophobia through questionnaires, which may have introduced self-report bias and social desirability. An alternative data collection method may rely on the built-in usage reports of smartphones and ecological momentary assessment. These time series data, analyzed using a dynamic network approach, may allow for a more in-depth exploration of the behavior and psychological states of smartphone users.

Conclusions

The core symptoms of nomophobia among males were: (a) could not know if someone had tried to get a hold of me, (b) could not check messages on WeChat, QQ or emails, and (c) afraid of getting stranded. The core symptoms of nomophobia among females were: (a) could not check messages on WeChat, QQ or emails, and (b) disconnected from my online identity. Node centrality and network structure differed significantly between males and females. These findings demonstrate the significance of a loss of online connectivity in nomophobia and the distinct network structure of nomophobia between males and females, which may guide component-based preventive initiatives.
