Early-stage ocular toxocariasis: Progression and histopathologic analysis
Authors: Alexander M Melendez, Guillermo A Puebla Robles, Maria J Marcos Martinez, Victor M Villegas, Arun D Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report progression of early-stage ocular toxocariasis that evolved from retinal granuloma to tractional retinal detachment.MethodsSerial ophthalmic examination, multimodal imaging, and histopathological analysis were undertaken.CaseA 5-year-old girl without past medical history presented with left eye vision loss of one-week duration. Ophthalmoscopic examination revealed multiple circumscribed retinal granuloma-like lesions affecting the peripheral retina and macula associated with a rhegmatogenous retinal detachment. There was no evidence of vitreoretinal fibrosis/strands at initial examination.ResultsInitial 2-week treatment with oral albendazole 15mg/kg/daily lacked response, therefore pars plana vitrectomy, excisional biopsy, laser photocoagulation, silicone oil tamponade and sub-tenon triamcinolone injection was performed for management of retinal detachment and macular lesions. Histopathologic analysis showed a retinal granuloma. At the one-month postoperative evaluation, evidence of disease progression including vitreous strands and tractional retinal changes were present. Positive Toxocara IgG titers and peripheral eosinophilia were present. Histopathological analysis demonstrated a retinal granuloma with mixed plasma cells and eosinophils.ConclusionsWe present a unique case of early-stage ocular toxocariasis in which progression was documented by serial multimodal imaging. The diagnosis was supported by serology and histopathologic analysis.Keywords: Ocular toxocariasis, Retinal biopsy, Retinal detachment, Ocular oncology, Retinal tumor

Introduction

Parasitic infections affecting the eye contribute significantly to global blindness.1 Human toxocariasis, caused by the nematodes Toxocara canis and Toxocara cati, are among the most prevalent zoonotic nematode infections, particularly notable in industrialized nations.2,3 These parasitic roundworms primarily infect dogs and cats, with ocular toxocariasis being a relatively rare manifestation worldwide.1,4 The involvement of nematodes in eye diseases was first recognized by Wilder in 1950, and subsequent documentation of the association of Toxocara species with human disease was reported by Beaver et al., in 1952.5,6 Typically, toxocariasis affects young children (mean age of 7.5 years) when exposed to contaminated soil. Although systemic manifestations are more common than ocular ones,7 the clinical spectrum of ocular manifestations is essentially that of active chorioretinal inflammation (granulomas, neuroretinitis) or its sequelae (chorioretinal scars, tractional retinal detachments, and rhegmatogenous retinal detachments due to retinal tears secondary to traction).

The classical clinical presentation includes unilateral posterior pole or peripheral retinal granuloma with vitreous strands that extend to the optic disc.8,9 Bilateral involvement is uncommon.8,10,11 Early stage disease without vitreous strands has non-specific appearance and therefore can be a diagnostic dilemma.

In this report we describe a young female patient who presents with clinical manifestations of early onset retinal granulomas formation and rhegmatogenous retinal detachment without evidence of vitreous strands. Histopathology and serology although suggestive were not diagnostic until the progression to retinal strands and tractional retinal detachment.

Case report

A 5-year-old healthy girl without significant prior medical history was referred due to a rhegmatogenous retinal detachment in the left eye. No family history of inherited retinal disease or eye cancer was reported. The patient's chief complaint was painless, progressive visual loss of one week's duration. Best corrected visual acuity was 20/20 in the right eye and 20/80 in the left eye with normotensive intraocular pressure in both. Extraocular muscle evaluation showed full ductions and anterior segment examination was unremarkable without evidence of intraocular inflammation in either eye. Pupils were equally round and equally reactive to light without evidence of afferent pupillary defect. A dilated fundoscopy exam was undertaken with the right eye having an unremarkable examination, however, the left eye demonstrated a clear fundus with a posterior vitreous detachment, two small (less than 1.50 mm) hypopigmented round epiretinal masses, and a rhegmatogenous retinal detachment involving the macula (Fig. 1A). Macular SD-OCT a localized retinal tear situated at the base of one of the macular epiretinal lesions with macular detachment (Fig. 1B). On B-scan echography, both epiretinal masses were hyperechoic without evidence of calcification (Fig. 1C), and by fluorescein angiography there was late staining of the nasal epiretinal mass and diffuse temporal leakage (Fig. 1D).

At this time, a presumptive diagnosis of ocular toxocariasis was made. Comprehensive serology and systemic imaging (Brain MRI and chest CT) were ordered and oral albendazole 15mg/kg/day was prescribed. During the first week, clinical findings were unchanged and systemic imaging was negative. At two weeks there was lack of improvement for which patient underwent surgery for management of the acute rhegmatogenous retinal detachment. Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) with excisional biopsy of the macular lesion, laser photocoagulation of the nasal lesion, silicone oil tamponade under steroid cover (sub-Tenon triamcinolone acetonide was undertaken (Fig. 2A). Over subsequent 4 weeks, there was onset of retinal bands, typical of ocular toxocariasis. Positive Toxocara IgG titers and peripheral eosinophilia, supported the diagnosis (Fig. 2B). Histopathologic analysis of the epiretinal lesion revealed a fibrous nodule best characterized as non-necrotizing giant cell granuloma with mixed inflammation (plasma cells and eosinophils). The sample was negative for nematodes and microorganisms such as fungi and mycobacteria (Fig. 3).

Fundus appearance one day after excisional biopsy of macular epiretinal lesion, laser photocoagulation of the peripheral lesion, and pars plana vitrectomy with silicon oil tamponade (A). Note retinal haemorrhage at the biopsy site and resolution of retinal detachment. 4 weeks following surgical intervention, there was formation of vitreous strands extending from sites of the granuloma to the optic disc tractional retinal detachment (B).

Discussion

Visual loss in ocular toxocariasis is common with 80–90 % of cases having worse than 20/40 central visual acuity. Vitritis, cystoid macular edema (CME), and tractional retinal detachment are the most common mechanisms of visual decline.7,8 The development of retinal tears or breaks is believed to be secondary to tractional forces exerted by vitreous strands or membranes created by the inflammatory process, leading to tractional or rhegmatogenous retinal detachments.12, 13, 14 Similar to our case, Wilkinson and Welch (1971) described a case in which a patient with a posterior granuloma, without vitritis, developed a retinal tear secondary to traction, resulting in a rhegmatogenous retinal detachment.10 However, they did not describe follow-up or disease progression with vitreoretinal bands formation. Early stage toxocariasis may simulate retinoblastoma and may predispose patients to rhegmatogenous retinal detachment even without clinically evident vitreoretinal strands or inflammation.

Albendazole is often chosen as the primary anthelmintic medication due to its ability to penetrate the blood-brain barrier and target the larval stages of Toxocara species, particularly in cases with suspected neurologic involvement.11,15 The use of anthelmintic therapy typically ranges from 2 to 4 weeks when Ocular toxocariasis is suspected.15,20 Albendazole was given to our patient to ensure that possible inflammatory process did not worsen without treatment. However, anthelmintic medication can have the potential to trigger a severe inflammatory reaction caused by dying larvae.9 Steroids play a crucial role in managing intraocular inflammation, thereby preventing inflammation-induced tissue injury and the formation of vitreous membranes, which can lead to severe vision loss.7,9,11,15 Furthermore, the role of intravitreal steroid therapy has been described in prior cases characterized by severe, vision-limiting vitritis and peripheral granulomas sparing the macula; however, this approach was not pursued in our patient given the absence of vitritis.16 Although both steroid and albendazole therapies are indicated for managing sight-threatening ocular inflammation, there is currently no consensus on the optimal medical management approach.15,17 Oral Prednisone therapy was not initiated in our case due to a broad differential diagnosis that included infectious etiologies and a suspected neoplastic process, such as atypical Retinoblastoma. Furthermore, the absence of significant vitritis or anterior chamber inflammation did not warrant corticosteroid therapy. Follow-up examinations demonstrated stable retinal lesions, which helped rule out a possible neoplastic process or a worsening inflammatory response. In our case, only oral albendazole was administered initially due to suspected ocular toxocariasis, without change in clinical findings after two-weeks of treatment. Furthermore, pars plana vitrectomy with use of steroid (sub-Tenon triamcinolone acetonide injection) did not prevent disease progression and formation of vitreous strands.

Histopathologic confirmation is rarely pursued in the surgical management of ocular toxocariasis, with diagnosis primarily relying on clinical assessment of characteristic signs, symptoms, and the presence of serum antibodies to Toxocara spp.9,18 Pathological reports in ocular toxocariasis have arisen from enucleated eyes, typically prompted by suspicions of retinoblastoma or other ocular complications.5 Histopathological examinations have typically revealed total retinal detachment, granulomatous inflammation, and inflammatory granulation tissue infiltrated by eosinophils, lymphocytes, and plasma cells. Reports on histopathology have occasionally detailed the presence of developed larvae, degenerated larval remnants, and hyaline capsules.4,9,19 In our case, histopathological analysis of the macular granuloma did not reveal evidence of larval species. However, a mixed inflammatory granulomatous reaction, including plasma cells and eosinophils, consistent with ocular toxocariasis was observed. Additionally, confirmation of previous Toxocara infection was established through positive serum IgG titers, which although clinically relevant for ocular toxocariasis diagnosis, are not essential for disease confirmation.7,9,18 It is important to note that the development of Proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR) could have contributed to the formation of vitreous strands and traction during the postoperative period in this young patient, given the prior history of Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) and retinal surgery. However, the observed pattern of vitreoretinal strands originating from the granulomas and extending toward the Optic nerve is highly characteristic of the progression of Ocular toxocariasis.Our case highlights that early granulomas in ocular toxocariasis may not show any parasites, and antiparasitic therapies may not have any effect in intraocular disease progression.

Conclusions

In this well documented case of early-stage ocular toxocariasis, a young female patient presented with small retinal granulomas and rhegmatogenous retinal detachment. Despite initial absence of clinically evident vitreous inflammation, there was progression to late inflammatory sequelae without response to steroids and anthelmintic medication.
