Caveolin‐1 Is a Central Mediator of Acute Lung Injury in an Animal Model of Sickle Cell Disease
Authors: Clare C Prohaska, Justin R Sysol, Nicole Jones, Robert W Wysocki, Suellen D Oliveira, Richard D Minshall, Roberto F Machado
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute chest syndrome in sickle cell disease (SCD) carries high morbidity and mortality, with up to 15% of patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation. However, the rates and mechanisms of ventilator‐induced lung injury (VILI) in SCD remain poorly understood. We hypothesized individuals with SCD are protected from VILI in a caveolin‐1 (Cav‐1) dependent manner. SCD mice and control littermates underwent either spontaneous tidal breathing or high tidal volume mechanical ventilation for 4 h (VILI model), or received intratracheal Escherichia coli‐derived lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or sterile phosphate‐buffered saline and recovered for 16 h (LPS model). Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) samples were analyzed for inflammatory cytokine profiles and lung tissues were used for histology and Western blot. SCD mice were protected from VILI but were more susceptible to LPS‐induced lung injury, as evidenced by higher BAL fluid total protein concentrations, polymorphonuclear cell infiltration and total cell count. Inflammatory cytokine profiles differed significantly in BAL fluid: IL‐6, KC and MIP‐2 levels were attenuated in the SCD‐VILI model, while TNF‐α levels were significantly increased after LPS exposure. Cav‐1 expression was reduced at baseline in SCD mice and further decreased after exposure to VILI when compared to control animals. Phosphorylated Cav‐1 expression increased, leading to depletion of total Cav‐1 in the SCD‐VILI model. These data suggest SCD mice are protected from VILI, but not LPS‐induced lung injury. These differences appear to be mediated by distinct inflammatory cytokine profiles and expression of Cav‐1. Further studies are needed to explore differences in lung injury patterns in patients with SCD.Keywords: acute lung injury, caveolin‐1, sickle cell disease

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by a point mutation in the beta globin gene, leading to abnormal hemoglobin folding and oxygen carrying ability. When deoxygenated, red blood cells take on a classic sickle shape, which can lead to recurrent vaso‐occlusive episodes including acute pain crises or acute chest syndrome (ACS) [1, 2]. ACS is defined as the presence of fever and/or new respiratory symptoms accompanying a new pulmonary infiltrate on chest x‐ray and is the second most common reason for hospital admissions amongst the SCD population [2, 3]. ACS often occurs while patients are hospitalized, frequently following an acute pain crisis, but viral, bacterial or mixed infections account for approximately 30% of ACS episodes [4]. Regardless of the cause, vaso‐occlusion in the pulmonary vasculature results in the release of unbound heme or hemoglobin into the systemic circulation, causing further erythrocyte membrane damage and activating pro‐inflammatory signaling pathways in red blood cells, immune cells and endothelial cells [5, 6, 7]. Approximately 13% of patients progress to requiring invasive mechanical ventilation [4, 8]. ACS is associated with prolonged hospitalization, increased risk of developing chronic lung disease and decreased long‐term survival [2, 4, 9, 10, 11]. Over 10% of ACS cases are fatal, associated with adverse neurological events, or result in multiorgan failure [2].

Caveolae are 50–100 nm flask‐like invaginations that form in the cell membrane. Caveolae may be found in many different cell types, but their functions are most significant in endothelial cells [12, 13]. Caveolin‐1 (Cav‐1), a 22 kDa protein, is the major constituent of caveolae, regulating endothelial barrier function, endocytosis and transcytosis, short‐ and long‐term responses to mechanotransduction and mediating inflammatory signaling pathways [14, 15, 16]. Cav‐1 interacts with signaling molecules located within caveolae, such as G‐protein‐coupled receptors, receptor tyrosine kinases as well as endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), an enzyme critical to maintaining endothelial cell homeostasis via nitric oxide production [17, 18, 19].

Hemin, the oxidized prosthetic moiety of hemoglobin, acts as a danger‐associated molecular pattern molecule that is released during hemolysis during ACS in individuals with SCD. In endothelial cells, hemin can activate Toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling, leading to NF‐κB activation, leukocyte adhesion and microvascular vaso‐occlusion in SCD mouse models [20]. The signaling cascade between hemin and TLR4 contributes to endothelial barrier dysfunction during ACS, in part by causing necroptotic cell death [20, 21, 22]. Cav‐1 is also an important signal regulator during sepsis. Upon stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), TLR4 directly interacts with Cav‐1. Phosphorylation of Cav‐1 is required for sepsis‐induced lung inflammation. Inhibition of phosphorylation leads to inactivation of TLR4 signaling in pulmonary vascular endothelial cells, thereby reducing sepsis‐induced lung inflammation and injury [19, 23].

Described as early as 1966, our knowledge of ventilator‐induced lung injury (VILI) has expanded significantly over the past 60 years [24, 25]. VILI is caused by high tidal volumes and relatively low levels of positive end expiratory pressures (PEEP), leading to overdistention of the lung and surfactant depletion. This leads to infiltration of inflammatory cells, hyaline membrane formation, increased vascular permeability and pulmonary edema, the hallmarks of VILI [25, 26]. Although it had long been identified and described, the importance of VILI became paramount in 2000 when decreased mortality was identified among patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome ventilated with low tidal volumes compared to higher tidal volumes [27]. Having a high clinical suspicion for and managing VILI is thus extremely important in patients who are receiving invasive mechanical ventilation. Notably, previous studies have shown that Cav‐1 knockout mice are protected against VILI, with decreased inflammatory cytokine production and decreased neutrophilic infiltration that is restored upon rescue of Cav‐1 expression [16]. Thus, Cav‐1 plays an important role in the acute development and response to lung injury through endovascular leak [12, 16, 28]. However, no studies have investigated how Cav‐1 expression may change in the setting of ACS.

To our knowledge, no prior studies have evaluated the relationship between SCD and different forms of lung injury. In our present study, we hypothesized that animals with SCD develop less VILI in a Cav‐1 dependent manner. We tested this hypothesis by evaluating the relationship between SCD, VILI and LPS‐induced lung injury using a humanized SCD mouse model.

Methods

Animal Models and Genotyping

Animal studies were approved by the University of Illinois at Chicago and Indiana University Animal Care and Use Committees. Female and male humanized Townes sickle cell mice (Hbb hAγβS/hAγβS) and littermate‐matched controls (Hbb βA/βA) aged 8‐10 weeks were used for all in vivo experiments. Genotyping was completed using cellulose acetate hemoglobin (Hb) electrophoresis to separate HbS and HbA species from lysed red blood cell samples (see Supporting Information S1: Figure S1). Sickle cell mice (Hbb βS/βS) and littermate‐matched controls (Hbb βA/βA) were anesthetized and exposed to high tidal volume mechanical ventilation (Vt = 40 mL/kg) for 4 h, with spontaneously breathing (SB) mice used as controls. Alternatively, 8–10 week old sickle cell mice (Hb βS/βS) or littermate‐matched controls (Hbb βA/βA) were anesthetized and administered an Escherichia coli‐derived LPS solution (1 mg/kg) or sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS) intratracheally (IT) and allowed to recover for 16 h.

BAL Processing

At the end of each experiment, a bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed using 1 mL of PBS. The recovered volume was centrifuged and the supernatant was analyzed for total protein concentration using a BCA assay kit (Thermo). Total cell counts in BAL fluid samples were measured using an automated cell counter (Bio‐Rad TC10). Differential cell counts to quantify polymorphonuclear (PMN) cells in BAL fluid samples were completed following cytospin and eosin/methylene blue staining (Thermo, Kwik Diff kit).

Cytokine Assays

BAL fluid samples were used to measure the concentration of inflammatory cytokines before and after VILI and LPS‐induced lung injury. Quantification was completed using ELISA for TNF‐α, IL‐1β (BioLegend), KC/CXCL1 and MIP‐2/CXCL2 (Sigma).

Histological Analysis and Immunofluorescence Staining

Fixed, hematoxylin and eosin‐stained mouse lung tissues were used for histological analyses. Paraffin‐embedded lung tissue sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated. After antigen retrieval (Abcam), slides were blocked in PBS with 0.1% BSA and 0.3% TX‐100. After incubation with primary and fluorescent‐conjugated secondary antibodies, anti‐fade mounting media with DAPI (Life Science Inc.) was used to fix the coverslip to the slide. Slides were examined using a Nikon fluorescence microscope and images were processed using MetaMorph software. Fluorescence intensity and colocalization analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Western Blot

The right upper lung from each in vivo experiment was flash frozen. Tissue was homogenized in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer with added protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein were added to Laemmli buffer and samples were boiled for 7 min. Cooled samples were loaded into individual wells of a NuPAGE 4%–12% Bis‐Tris gels (Bio‐Rad). The SDS‐PAGE was transferred onto PVDF membranes and treated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After several washing steps, membranes were incubated for 1 h with the appropriate secondary antibody, detected (Supersignal, Pierce) and imaged using a Chemidoc (BioRad). Reblotting the membranes with β actin acted as a control for equal loading and transfer.

Antibodies

Antibodies against phosphorylated and total Caveolin‐1 and eNOS were obtained from Cell Signaling; β actin was obtained from Sigma Aldrich.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (San Diego, CA). Student's t‐test or ANOVA was used to compare differences between two or more groups, respectively. Data are expressed as individual values and mean ± SEM. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sickle Cell Mice Are Protected From VILI but Susceptible to LPS‐Induced Lung Injury

The schematic representation of the human β‐globin locus, mouse β‐globin locus and the recombinant knock‐in loci in sickle cell mice is displayed in Supporting Information S1: Figure S1A. Cellulose acetate hemoglobin (Hb) electrophoresis gels were used to separate HbS and HbA species in RBC lysates from mice and blood smears stained with Wright‐Giemsa demonstrate sickle‐shaped erythrocytes and reticulocytosis in sickle cell mice (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1B,C). Sickle cell mice display significant splenomegaly, as well as significant anemia compared to controls (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1D,E).

After sickle cell mice or littermate controls were exposed to high tidal volume mechanical ventilation for 4 h, BAL was performed. BAL fluid from sickle cell mice showed significantly less protein, fewer PMNs and lower total cell counts compared to control animals (Figure 1A). Control mice exposed to high tidal volume ventilation had a marked inflammatory infiltrate in the lungs, which was attenuated in sickle cell mice and not seen in spontaneously breathing mice (Figure 1B).

When exposed to 1 mg/kg IT LPS, sickle cell mice showed no significant difference in BAL protein or PMN percentage, but demonstrated a decreased total BAL cell count compared to littermate‐matched controls (Figure 1C). BAL protein level, the % PMN and total cell count were all significantly higher than those of PBS‐treated mice in both controls and sickle mice. Histologically, there was a significant inflammatory infiltrate seen both in sickle cell and control mice treated with LPS that was not observed in the vehicle control group. NSo sex‐based differences were observed (Figure 1D).

Differential Inflammatory Cytokine Profiles Depending on the Method of Lung Injury in Mice

BAL fluid inflammatory cytokine profiles from sickle cell mice and littermate controls were markedly different between VILI and LPS‐induced lung injury models. In the VILI model, there were significantly lower concentrations of IL‐6, KC and MMP‐2 when compared to controls (Figure 2A). In the LPS model, there were significantly higher concentrations of TNF‐α compared to controls. There was no significant difference in concentrations of IL‐6 or MMP‐2 in BAL fluid in sickle cell mice compared to controls when exposed to LPS, and no sex‐based differences were observed (Figure 2B).

(A) Concentrations of TNF‐α, IL‐6, KC (CXCL1), MIP‐2 (CXCL2), and IL‐1β cytokines in BAL fluid after 4 h of cyclic stretch, (B) 16 h after IT LPS administration. SB = spontaneous breathing, VILI = ventilator‐induced lung injury. Data represented as mean ± SEM; *p < 0.05.

Decreased Cav‐1 Expression in the Lung Tissue and Pulmonary Circulation of Sickle Cell Mice

Given previous findings demonstrating Cav‐1 ‐/‐ mice are protected from VILI [16], we hypothesized that the primary driver of changes in increased permeability and inflammation between our two models of acute lung injury was changes in Cav‐1 expression. To explore this hypothesis, we obtained tissue sections from mice treated with high tidal volume ventilation and LPS. We performed immunohistochemistry to qualitatively evaluate Cav‐1 and endothelial nitric oxide synthetase (eNOS) expression, which requires Cav‐1 for proper activation after endothelial cell stimulation [29]. Sections from sickle cell mice showed significantly less Cav‐1 compared to wild type (WT) controls at baseline. High tidal volume ventilation increased Cav‐1 in WT mice, a trend that was not observed in sickle cell mice. WT mice exposed to LPS showed a striking increase in eNOS compared to baseline, but there were no significant changes in sickle cell mice (Figure 3A). Fluorescence intensity confirmed lower expression of Cav‐1 in SCD animals compared to WT mice in all conditions, also corroborating higher eNOS expression in WT mice in response to LPS compared to SCD animals (Figure 3B). There was a strong correlation between colocalization of Cav‐1 and eNOS in WT animals at baseline that decreased in exposure to VILI or LPS. There was weaker correlation between Cav‐1 and eNOS in SCD animals at baseline that varied in response to VILI or LPS exposure (Figure 3C).

When exposed to high Vt (VILI model), sickle cell mice have decreased expression of Cav‐1 compared to WT. 16 h after IT LPS administration, sickle cell mice appear to have decreased Cav‐1 and eNOS expression compared to WT. (B) Quantitative fluorescence intensity of eNOS and Cav‐1, demonstrating lower Cav‐1 in sickle cell mice compared to WT mice in all treatment conditions. (C) Correlation coefficients between Cav‐1 and eNOS in WT mice (left panel) and sickle cell mice (right panel). IT = intratracheal; SCD = sickle cell disease; VILI = ventilator induced lung injury; WT = wild type.

Using Western blot, Cav‐1 expression was measured using whole lung homogenates from sickle cell mice exposed to VILI, LPS or control conditions. At baseline, sickle cell mice had significantly lower Cav‐1 expression. Sickle cell mice demonstrated further attenuation in total Cav‐1 expression when exposed to LPS and significantly lower expression when exposed to VILI compared to control mice (Figure 4A). Interestingly, sickle cell mice did not show a significant difference in Cav‐1 expression when exposed to high tidal volume ventilation compared to untreated controls. There was no significant difference in phosphorylated Cav‐1 (p‐Cav‐1) expression in WT, sickle cell mice or VILI‐exposed WT mice, but p‐Cav‐1 levels were significantly upregulated in sickle cell mice exposed to VILI (Figure 4B). Taken together, these data suggest that phosphorylation of Cav‐1 is the main factor driving the depletion of Cav‐1 expression in our SCD‐VILI model.

Treatment with LPS attenuates Cav‐1 expression in WT mice, with further decreased expression in SCD mice treated with LPS. VILI does not appear to affect Cav‐1 expression in WT mice but is lower in SCD mice exposed to VILI. (B) There is an increase in p‐Cav‐1 in sickle cell mouse lungs exposed to VILI that is not seen in other models. IT = intratracheal; SCD = sickle cell disease; VILI = ventilator induced lung injury; WT = wild type. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Discussion

In this study, data demonstrated that sickle cell mice are protected from the development of VILI but displayed an increased susceptibility to LPS‐induced lung injury. Cytokine profiles differ significantly between VILI and LPS models, but levels of IL‐6, KC and MIP‐2 are attenuated compared to control mice in our SCD‐VILI model. In our SCD‐VILI model, we found phosphorylation of Cav‐1 appears to be the primary mediator of Cav‐1 depletion. Taken together, these data suggest decreased Cav‐1 expression plays a protective role in SCD‐VILI, with proposed differences in injury models outlined in Figure 5.

Summary of endothelial cell responses to two different models of ALI in SCD animals. In WT, steady state environments there is an abundance of Cav‐1 in caveolae that is associated with eNOS, acting as its negative regulator. In SCD, there are fewer caveolae and lower concentrations of Cav‐1, with low levels of free heme contributing to smoldering inflammation in part through TLR4 signaling. When SCD endothelial cells are stimulated by LPS, both free heme and LPS stimulate TLR4 and pro‐inflammatory cytokine signaling, with significantly increased TNF‐α signaling and neutrophil infiltration compared to WT. To our knowledge, this is the first study to specifically evaluate the relationship between SCD, Cav‐1 and eNOS in the lung using two different methods of acute lung injury. eNOS is responsible for production of nitric oxide (NO), which regulates vasomotor tone through regulating expression of vasoconstrictor molecules and their receptors (i.e., endothelin‐1). NO acts as an antioxidant, specifically by scavenging superoxide and inducing heme oxygenase‐1, an enzyme capable of scavenging free heme, thereby preventing it from participating in other redox reactions. Due to its antiadhesive properties, NO decreases adhesion of circulating blood cells to the endothelial vascular wall and to each other by downregulating expression of cell adhesion molecules. Furthermore, NO has antithrombotic properties through its regulation of platelet activation and aggregation, the coagulation system and fibrinolysis [30].

The relationship between SCD and eNOS has been well described, with endothelial cell dysfunction leading to NO resistance in SCD. This is driven by scavenging of NO by cell‐free plasma hemoglobin that is released during steady state intravascular hemolysis as well as exuberant reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation that further reduces NO bioavailability. Hemolysis releases redox‐active free iron and heme from inside the erythrocyte to intra‐ and extravascular compartments where they can participate in oxidative reactions. Hemolysis rates change in response to vaso‐occlusive episodes; some patients have decreased red blood cell deformability during vaso‐occlusive crises, or hyperhemolytic episodes may occur during episodes of ACS and are associated with more severe hypoxemia and morbidity [30]. During hemolysis, cell‐free hemoglobin increases vasoconstrictor tone and blunts responses to NO‐dependent vasodilators. This leads to vascular NO deficiency, resulting in impaired vascular smooth muscle cell relaxation, increased adhesion molecule expression and increased platelet activation and aggregation, worsening hemolysis in a feed‐forward cycle [30].

Interestingly, data demonstrated that the only condition eNOS expression was more highly expressed than control was in SCD‐VILI animals, which also had the highest levels of phosphorylated Cav‐1. Cav‐1 has a baseline inhibitory function on eNOS, thus typically acts as a negative regulator of nitric oxide (NO) release [31, 32, 33]. Cav‐1 also plays a key role in the lung response to LPS. Through its inhibitory function on eNOS, NO has direct bactericidal and immunomodulating effects but also regulates NF‐κB, which is concentration‐dependent [31, 32, 33]. In a study performed by Garrean et al, after intraperitoneal injection of LPS, Cav‐1 ‐/‐ knockout mice showed decreased PMN sequestration, microvascular permeability and edema in comparison to wild‐type lungs, with decreased overall mortality. Cav‐1 ‐/‐ lungs showed suppression of NF‐κB activity, but after coadministration of LPS with a NO synthase inhibitor there was restoration of NF‐κB activity and restored PMN sequestration in the lung [34]. Subsequent studies demonstrated that phosphorylation of Cav‐1 at Tyr14 is necessary for Cav‐1 to interact with TLR4 leading to NF‐κB activation and pro‐inflammatory cytokine generation [23]. Another study demonstrated that chronic inflammation stimulated by LPS or TNF‐α led to depletion of Cav‐1 that was dependent on nitrosation, phosphorylation and ubiquitinylation [35]. More recently, Cav‐1 was recognized as a hub gene in ALI with the Cav‐1/NF‐κB axis associated with autophagy in ALI [36]. It is interesting to note that in our study, levels of total Cav‐1 expression were lowest in wild‐type and SCD animals treated with LPS when compared to baseline or a model of VILI, but there was no significant difference in Cav‐1 expression between control or sickle cell mice when treated with LPS.

It is well established that VILI leads to increased endothelial permeability in the lung as evidenced by increased total protein, cell count and PMNs in BAL fluid [14, 37], with previous studies specifically evaluating the role between Cav‐1, VILI and microvascular permeability. Using global Cav‐1 ‐/‐ mice, Maniatis et al showed that knockout mice had decreased microvascular permeability, inflammatory cytokine secretion and neutrophilic infiltration in response to VILI. After rescue of Cav‐1 expression, the injury response to VILI was reestablished, demonstrating the critical role that Cav‐1 plays in response to injurious stimuli in the lung [16], which is corroborated by our in vivo findings.

We found that in our animal model of SCD‐VILI, there was significant attenuation of IL‐6 and IL‐8 levels in BAL fluid compared to control animals. It has been well established that IL‐8 levels are increased among patients with SCD during vaso‐occlusive crises, as well as those presenting with leg ulcers [38, 39, 40]. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that elevated levels of IL‐6 and IL‐8 correlate with worse outcomes in individuals with SCD [38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43]. It is interesting to note that previous studies on VILI have found that hLMVEC produce IL‐8, leading to neutrophilic inflammation and worsening lung injury [41]. Another study using human dermal microvascular endothelial cells demonstrated IL‐8 preferentially recruited neutrophils to vessels with high levels of intercellular adhesion molecule‐1, with low levels of caveolae [44]. Given the role of IL‐8 as a chemoattractant for neutrophils, it is possible that the attenuation of IL‐8 levels in our SCD‐VILI mice may explain the relative decrease in neutrophilic inflammation seen in the lung.

There are a number of limitations to our study. While we assume that endothelial Cav‐1 is responsible for our findings, it is possible that our results were influenced by smooth muscle cell, fibroblast or platelet function, as all of these may play a role in cytokine production or Cav‐1 function. It is possible that our findings may be changed if looking at a different timepoint or if mice were exposed to a larger tidal volume. We used relatively young mice for our model, but it is possible that older sickle cell mice that have chronically been exposed to low‐grade hemin would differentially influence our results. Finally, we acknowledge the inherent limitations of animal models of disease which may limit the generalizability of our results to patient populations.

In conclusion, data indicated that Cav‐1 expression and phosphorylation are central regulators of VILI‐associated lung injury in an animal model of SCD. Future studies are warranted to further investigate how these findings may be harnessed to improve outcomes for individuals with SCD and ACS.
