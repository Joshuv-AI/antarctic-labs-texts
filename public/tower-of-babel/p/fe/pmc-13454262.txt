Autism spectrum disorder identification using machine learning models on MRI data
Authors: Milner Paul V, Caren Babu, Suni Jose, Shuma Adhikari, Loitongbam Surajkumar Singh, Jijo Francis, Adarsh V Parekkattil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition that affects sensory processing, speech, behavior and identifying the condition at an early stage is necessary to provide the treatment. The existing diagnostic methods are mainly based on personalized subjective and time consuming. This study introduces an objective diagnostic method by analyzing technical noise signature producing the Quality Vector which is extracted from multimodal MRI data. This work leverages Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) techniques on this Quality vector for improving ASD detection. The methodology leveraged technical Quality Assessment Protocol (QAP) metrics from the ABIDE II repository, integrating structural (sMRI), functional (fMRI), and diffusion (DTI) modalities. The methodology utilized Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN) for outlier removal and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction. A classification pipeline was implemented using a 1D-Residual Network (1D-ResNet), Convolutional Neural Network(CNN), SVM, kNN and a proposed Voting Ensemble (SVM, KNN, and XGBoost), validated through stratified 10-fold cross-validation. In this work, the proposed ensemble emerged as the superior model, achieving a diagnostic accuracy of 95.84%. These findings indicate that technical biomarkers derived from imaging quality metrics are highly useful for ASD detection. This research demonstrates that technical quality metrics, often dismissed as noise, contain significant diagnostic value. The Quality Vector framework provides a computationally efficient and objective tool for ASD identification. The high classification performance suggests that the biomarkers obtained from MRI have a potential future scope in research on early ASD diagnosis and detection.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder, Early detection, Machine learning, Magnetic resonance imaging, Convolutional neural network (CNN)Subject terms: Biomarkers, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Neuroscience

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder caused by genetic and environmental factors affecting how people communicate and interact socially and behave. It is associated with a spectrum of symptoms that vary widely in severity. An early diagnosis, followed by programs of behavioral therapy, speech therapy, or structured support, can greatly enhance the quality of life of the individual. According to the data available, more than 2 million individuals are affected with ASD in India; with percentage prevalence of 0.11 for age group (1–18 year) and 0.09 for age group (0–15 year) in the rural and urban areas1. The conventional diagnostic methods are mainly based on personalized evaluation and surveys, which take more time, making the symptoms more severe. This limitation stresses the importance of more advanced and precise methods that can enhance early diagnosis of ASD. There are research works that support early assessment and intervention but also challenges on the lack of empirically validated early interventions2. However, the study concludes that early intervention is more cost effective and time saving, than a “wait and see” approach. Compared to other countries, many children in India are diagnosed much later than in developed nations like the US, UK, or Australia, where universal early screening is common. The US and Europe use AI-powered screening tools, digital apps, and teletherapy, whereas India is still catching up in technology adoption for ASD. In Indian scenario, Indian Scale for Assessment of Autism (ISAA) tool was developed to help in disability certification under the RPWD Act, 2016, using Indian-specific behaviors and expressions rather than Western standards3. This throws an insight on the need for age-appropriate and multi-informant approaches to ensure accurate assessments and a more comprehensive approach to assessing ASD related disabilities in India.

In4 an approach to automatically characterize brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) images for detecting Autism Spectrum Disorders (ASD), by analyzing features from over 1,100 MRI scans of individuals with ASD, and identifies that there are structural differences in brain regions, such as the hippocampus, amygdala, and thalamus for individuals with ASD. These features serve as potential indicators of ASD and the framework shows promising accuracy, particularly in younger age groups, suggesting it could help with early ASD diagnosis. So, MRI (both structural MRI (sMRI) and functional MRI (fMRI)) helps identify ASD in early stages and provide elaborate information of the brain’s shape and connectivity, revealing ASD-related biomarkers such as cortical thickness and gray matter density. sMRI helps to detect anatomical differences in the brain of individuals with ASD and significant structural differences in their brain. sMRI can also detect abnormal brain growth patterns as early as 6–12 months of age. fMRI helps identify differences in brain activity and connectivity in ASD affected individuals, even before behavioral symptoms begin to appear.

Recently, machine learning (ML) has emerged as an effective method for ASD detection, collecting data from an extensive range of sources in addition to behavioral patterns, and brain imaging data5. The work in6 illustrates how ML is used for the identification of ASD using brain imaging and the methods for diagnosing ASD using functional and structural MRI data is available in7. The preprocessing stage uses fMRI and sMRI for feature extraction with tools like FreeSurfer for structural analysis. Various ML algorithms were used for classification, including Support Vector Machines (SVM) that work efficiently for datasets with higher dimensions and medium-sized datasets, which achieved an accuracy between 60% and 95%. In the work proposed by8, ASD is identified and predicted based on multi-site structural MRI with the help of machine learning. The two public datasets from the University of California, and Irvine (UCI) repository are used in9 combining features like age, gender, and diagnostic responses. The preprocessing includes handling missing values, one-hot encoding, and feature selection were included with the model. The Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE) dataset was utilized, which contains numerous and diverse collections of MRI scans and phenotypic data from people with ASD and neurotypical controls. The results of this study concluded with the effectiveness of AI-driven techniques in ASD diagnosis, providing better accuracy and efficiency as compared to traditional methods.

One of the drawbacks associated with machine learning models is that it often takes more time and effort to prepare features whereas the use of deep learning (DL) techniques is capable to diagnose ASD in children with high accuracy10,11. Deep learning (DL) addresses this issue by automating the feature extraction, with models such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), which are more effective in processing images12,13. The future research will explore advanced deep learning techniques, broader age groups, and enhanced interpretability methods to further refine ASD classification.

The main contributions and novelty of the research are the following. Firstly, new biological factor that can be identified as biomarker is proposed, demonstrating that the technical noise profile, encompassing signal stability, contrast-to-noise ratios, and motion artifacts. It serves as a high-accuracy proxy for ASD diagnosis, independent of traditional volumetric brain analysis. Secondly, we have shown a fusion mechanism that integrates technical metrics from sMRI, fMRI, and DTI into a unified ‘Quality Vector,’ which consists of 14 features, providing a multimodal perspective on neurodevelopmental stability. Thirdly, we introduce two novel engineered biomarkers: the Functional Stability Variance Index (FSVI) and the Movement Intensity Score (MIS).It was noted that these composite features effectively capture the behavioral and physiological phenotypes of ASD, significantly outperforming individual standard metrics in classification tasks.

Additionally, this paper provides a rigorous comparative benchmarking of classical ensemble methods against deep learning architectures like 1D-CNN and ResNet. The rest of this manuscript is organized to reflect these contributions: Sect. 2 details the Materials and Methods, including the ABIDE II dataset and model specifications; Sect. 3 presents the experimental Results and performance metrics; and offers a comprehensive Discussion of the biological plausibility and performance gaps observed; and finally, Sect. 4 summarizes the key findings and suggests future research directions.

Data sources

This study utilized phenotypic and imaging quality metrics from the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange II (ABIDE II) repository14. Specifically, we leveraged pre-calculated metrics from the Quality Assessment Protocol (QAP) subsets, which provide objective measures of image noise and signal stability for structural (sMRI), functional (fMRI), and diffusion (DTI) modalities. These metrics were generated by the Preprocessed Connectomes Project (PCP) team using the Configurable Pipeline for the Analysis of Connectomes (C-PAC)15 based on the ABIDE II dataset. The dataset consisted of 1,114 subjects (521ASD and 593Typically Developing) who possessed quality data across various imaging modalities of MRI. This study utilized phenotypic and imaging quality metrics from the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange II (ABIDE II) repository14. For analyzing, testing and training, we used the ABIDE II dataset, consisting of a vast and diverse collection of MRI scans and phenotypic data from people with ASD and neurotypical controls. The input data consists of various quality control (QC) metrics for three different MRI modalities such as sMRI, DTI, fMRI, each providing key aspects of image integrity, signal quality and motion-induced distortion while performing the scan.

A critical step in our data preparation involved the precise alignment of diagnostic labels with multimodal quality metrics to ensure the integrity of the ground-truth. The clinical labels (ASD vs. Typically Developing) were extracted from the ABIDE II phenotypic files. These labels were then merged with the technical quality records by matching the unique Subject ID (Sub_ID) which is present across three distinct Quality Assessment Protocol (QAP) subsets: anat_qap.csv for structural (sMRI) data, dti_qap.csv for diffusion data(DTI), and functional_qap.csv for functional (fMRI) data16. This merging ensured that each entry in our final integrated dataset of 463 records correctly reflects the participant’s clinical diagnosis alongside their specific imaging quality profile. By linking the phenotypic data directly to the QAP repositories via Subject IDs, we eliminated labeling ambiguity and established a reliable foundation for the subsequent machine learning pipeline. The final preprocessed multimodal MRI dataset consisted of 463 subjects, including 247 individuals with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and 216 Typically Developing (TD) controls. We removed labeling uncertainty and created a solid basis for the ensuing machine learning pipeline for autism detection using MRI by directly connecting the phenotypic data to the QAP repository via Subject IDs. A 14-dimensional multimodal feature vector was produced for each subject by disambiguating features with the same names across modalities (such as SNR, DVARS, and GCORR) using modality-specific suffixes (_anat, _dti, _func) to maintain their independent information content. This multimodal restriction guarantees that every record in our analysis provides thorough structural, diffusion, and functional neuroimaging information even though it decreased the sample size from the initial 1,114 participants. Comparable to well-known multimodal ASD neuroimaging categorization research is this sample size.

Figure 1 demonstrates data preprocessing with the first modality as Source 1, the second modality as Source 2 and the third modality as Source 3. The Source 1, Anatomical MRI dataset (anat_qap.csv), contains QC metrics specifically relevant to T1-weighted anatomical scans, analyzing features like structural clarity, contrast and sharpness. The dataset comprises a total of 12 features, describing the parameters like contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), entropy focus criterion (EFC), and cortical contrast. The Source 2, Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) dataset (dti_qap.csv), is a specialized MRI technique that maps the water movement within the brain tissue, that helps to study the white matter integrity and connectivity. This dataset contains 18 features that ensure quality validation for diffusion-weighted imaging, including measurements of SNR, motion distortion and the accuracy of tensor modeling, enhancing the precision of different mapping. The Source 3, Functional MRI dataset (functional_qap.csv), mainly focuses on the motion and signal-related QC metrics, examining variations in the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal, head motion effect and overall signal stability. The dataset contains 18 features that include quality metrics such as mean framewise displacement (mFD), percent of frames with excessive motion (PercentFD), and global correlation (GCORR). By integrating these datasets, the accuracy of MRI quality evaluation was enhanced, ensuring that only reliable data is used for advanced computational analysis like statistical modeling and sent to machine learning modules.

Data preprocessing.

Only subjects with complete, quality-approved data across all three modalities were retained for analysis. The intersection of subjects meeting quality criteria in anatomical MRI, DTI, and rs-fMRI yielded 284 unique subjects.

Data cleaning

The three raw datasets were preprocessed to refine, integrate and enhance the dataset by combining quality control metrics from all three modalities. Here two main steps were used- data cleaning and data merging. During data cleaning subjects with substantial missing data were eliminated to reduce the possible errors in the analysis. For accurate results, numerical variables with missing values median imputation were used to maintain the quality of the dataset while avoiding the risk of misleading patterns. Column names were normalized, and data types were aligned, enabling easy integration.

Data merging

For the integration process, a common identifier (E.g.,Sub_ID) was used to develop a well-organized dataset. Initially, standardizing identifiers was essential for maintaining uniformity in column names across the dataset, which in turn helped to eliminate mismatch during merging of the dataset. A suitable merging strategy was incorporated based on the subject availability in each dataset, using inner joins to ensure complete records and outer joins to retain all subjects, even if certain data points were missing. During the integration process, challenges like duplicate records were removed, while missing identifiers were either filled with available data or marked for further validation, and formatting irregularities were carefully resolved to avoid redundancy during the datasets merging. Finally, the preprocessed dataset contained 15 columns with 14 features, in which the first column represented subject identifier and the rest of the columns represented the numerical metrics such as SNR, CNR, and fMRI quality metrics like GCORR and PrecentFD.

Feature engineering

Feature engineering is a process of converting raw data into significant characteristics that can be used for subsequent analysis. It helps to identify crucial elements that emphasize the quality, stability and integrity of the MRI scans. Extracting features from the MRI data, helps to obtain the understanding about the various aspects of image quality, like signal deviations, motion distortions and functional coherence, which play a crucial role in enhancing the precision of the further analysis in machine learning and statistical modeling. In this study, three key features were extracted to enhance the MRI data quality analysis: SNR Difference (SNR_Difference), Functional -Structural Variability Index (FSVI) and Motion instability Score (MIS).These engineered features - SNR Difference, FSVI and MIS-play a vital role in detecting inconsistencies, motion-induced distortions and functional variations, providing a detailed understanding of the MRI data quality. The SNR Difference was evaluated by subtracting the SNR of DTI scans from that of anatomical MRI scans, identifying any variations in SNR between these two imaging types. The FSVI was calculated by averaging the DVARS(D referential RMS variation over temporal windows values) from anatomical and functional scans, capturing temporal fluctuations in signal strength to evaluate the stability of brain function during the scan. The Motion Instability Score (MIS) was computed by adding the percentage of frames in both Structural and functional scans with a framewise displacement (FD) greater than 0.20, indicating motion instability in the data. The engineered features, MIS and FSVI, were designed to capture technical variance that correlates with known behavioral phenotypes of ASD, such as scanner-related head motion and functional signal instability. The output of the feature extraction provides a strong foundation for the next stage, where machine learning and statistical techniques will be used for further analysis. These features enhancing dataset reliability and offer high-quality input for machine learning, lead to improved diagnostic accuracy .

Outlier detection and removal

Outlier detection and removal using Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN) is an effective tool technique for enhancing the data quality in subsequent analysis. DBSCAN is a widely used clustering technique in machine learning and data analysis which is effective at identifying clusters of irregular shapes and managing noise (outliers) within the dataset. DBSCAN groups the data points into clusters according to their Proximity to one another utilizing a fixed proximity parameter (epsilon).Data points with in dense clusters are assigned with distinct labels, where as those unable to meet the clustering criteria will be marked as -1 marking them as outliers. These outliers are removed to improve the dataset’s reliability for further analysis. In Fig. 5 the x-axis represents FSVI and y axis represents DVARS.

Outlier detection using DBSCAN.

The elimination of outliers is very important in order to enhance the model performance as these irregular data can distort patterns and reduce analytical reliability. DBSCAN efficiently eliminates these outliers by distinguishing between dense clustered and scattered, low density points, assuring that only relevant data is used for analysis purposes. Unlike traditional clustering methods, DBSCAN doesn’t require predefining the number of clusters, making it highly flexible to dataset with complex and irregular distribution.

Model Training and validation

The dataset was divided into a 4:1 ratio, in which 80% was used for training and 20% for testing. Three machine learning models were trained and evaluated: SVM, KNN, and the XGBoost Classifier.

Support Vector Machines (SVM)

SVMs are a type of supervised machine learning algorithm utilized for classification, where its main aim is to find a hyperplane that maximizes the margin between different classes. When data is not linearly separable, SVM uses kernel methods to map the data into a higher-dimensional space where a linear separation is possible17. The SVM was optimized using GridSearchCV, which calculates various combinations of hyper parameters such as regularization parameter C, kernel type (specifically the radial basis function or RBF), and the gamma value, to obtain the most suitable set of parameters for classification. Once the optimal parameters are identified, the SVM model is trained and used to predict class labels and probabilities on the dataset.

K-Nearest neighbors (KNN)

KNN is a simple, but effective, machine learning algorithm that classifies the data points based on the majority classes or average value of their K nearest neighbors. KNN utilizes a distance metric, usually Euclidean, to identify the closest neighbors and make predictions for classification18. Here a KNN model was set up with 5 neighbors, making it more suitable for obtaining complex relationships in data without assuming a specific data distribution. The KNN model was implemented on a clean dataset to make predictions, providing both class labels and class probabilities.

XGBoost (Extreme gradient boosting)

XGBoost is an advanced machine learning algorithm, which is based on the principle of gradient boosting framework, where decision trees are added sequentially to reduce errors from previous models19. It incorporates regularization techniques to prevent overfitting, handles missing values, and is optimized for speed and performance. The model training was done by using the XGBoost Classifier, with parameters specifically optimized for the classification process. During training, this ML algorithm progressively learns from the residual errors and improves its accuracy. The model was trained on both the cleaned and original datasets to assess the effectiveness of outlier removal.

Model validation

To ensure the robustness of the diagnostic model and mitigate the risks of overfitting associated with a single train-test split, we implemented a Stratified 10-fold Cross-Validation strategy. The ABIDE II dataset based preprocessed dataset (n = 463) with 232 records of TD and 231 records of ASD, was partitioned into ten equal folds, where each fold maintained the original ratio of ASD to Typically Developing (TD) subjects. In each of the ten iterations, nine folds were used for training the ensemble (SVM, KNN, and XGBoost), while the remaining fold served as the independent test set. This process was repeated ten times such that every subject was utilized for both training and testing exactly once.

Deep learning architectures: 1D-CNN and ResNet

For evaluating the performance of the proposed model, the datasets were used to implement two different DL models too, CNN and Residual Network (ResNet), architectures optimized for one-dimensional technical features.

To benchmark the proposed ensemble model against deep learning paradigms, we implemented Convolutional Neural Network (1D-CNN): The 1D-CNN was designed to capture local spatial correlations between MRI quality metrics20. The architecture consists of three convolutional layers with 32, 64, and 128 filters respectively, each followed by a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation and a 1D max-pooling layer (pool size = 2). A dropout layer (rate = 0.5) was integrated to prevent overfitting, leading to a final dense softmax layer for binary classification.

Residual Network (ResNet): To evaluate the efficacy of deep learning paradigms against classical ensemble methods, a one-dimensional (1D) variant of the Residual Network (ResNet) architecture was implemented. While standard deep networks often suffer from the vanishing gradient problem—where signal degradation occurs as network depth increases—the ResNet architecture21 mitigates this by introducing “skip connections” or shortcuts that enable the network to learn identity mappings.In this study, the 1D-ResNet was specifically adapted to process 1D technical MRI quality metrics rather than 2D images.

Unlike the standard ResNet-18, this custom architecture utilizes 1D-convolutional layers and residual blocks consisting of two convolutional layers followed by a shortcut connection that sums the original input directly into the block’s output. This design allows the model to extract deep hierarchical patterns from the 1D feature space while preventing gradient degradation.For model training, the Adam optimizer was employed for its adaptive learning rate capabilities, alongside a categorical cross-entropy loss function to oversee binary classification performance. To ensure convergence and prevent overfitting, the training was executed over 30 epochs, providing a robust benchmark to compare deep feature extraction against the engineered features used in the voting ensemble.

The primary novelty of this study lies in the strategic repurposing of Quality Control (QC) metrics, traditionally related to preliminary data screening as primary diagnostic features for ASD identification. While contemporary research, such as the deep learning frameworks proposed in6, focuses on extracting high-dimensional anatomical biomarkers from raw imaging data, our approach pivots toward the technical noise profile of the acquisition as a pragmatic and potent diagnostic proxy. Our methodology uses underlying neurobiological differences indirectly through data acquisition stability. It is well-documented that ASD subjects often exhibit unique physiological and motion-related signal variances compared to typically developing group. In classical neuroimaging pipelines, variances across sMRI, fMRI, and DTI modalities are often viewed as artifacts which are to be modified or given low importance. However, we demonstrate that by capturing these variances through engineered metrics such as FSVI (Functional Stability Variance Index), SNR Difference, and MIS (Motion Impact Score) can identify stable diagnostic signatures that raw anatomical data might overlook.

A critical aspect of this study is the biological grounding of the proposed technical metrics. Rather than treating imaging artifacts as secondary noise, the Motion Instability Score (MIS) and Functional Stability Variance Index (FSVI) serve as proxies for the behavioral and physiological phenotypes of Autism Spectrum Disorder. MIS, in particular, captures the increased head micro-movements frequently documented in ASD populations, which are often attributed to underlying challenges in motor control and neurological regulation. By quantifying these instabilities, the model effectively incorporates participant behavior within the scanner as a diagnostic indicator. Similarly, FSVI reflects fluctuations in functional connectivity and metabolic stability that distinguish the ASD brain from Typically Developing (TD) controls. This integration ensures that the model’s predictive power is not merely a technical artifact but is rooted in the clinical reality of the disorder.

Recent literature has seen a surge in deep learning applications for ASD identification; for instance, complex neural networks have been utilized to classify ASD by age and gender using raw structural MRI data. However, while such high-dimensional models provide significant depth, they often function as black boxes and require substantial computational resources. By integrating our feature engineering with DBSCAN-based outlier detection and an ensemble (SVM, KNN, and XGBoost), we provide a computationally efficient and highly efficient alternative. This is particularly valuable for resource-constrained clinical settings where deep learning models may struggle with the site-specific noise and heterogeneous protocols inherent in multi-center datasets like ABIDE II26. Our findings suggest that when features are precisely engineered to capture acquisition stability, classical machine learning can not only rival but outperform deep learning baselines such as CNN and ResNet.

Our study provides a distinct advance by demonstrating that the technical details of the image captured through metrics such as FSVI and SNR, contains sufficient discriminative signal to achieve superior performance (AUC = 0.9979). Unlike the methods discussed in27, which can be sensitive to site-specific acquisition protocols, our ensemble approach (SVM-KNN-XGBoost) utilizes QC parameters to effectively harmonize the multi-site variance inherent in the ABIDE II dataset. This repurposing of QC metrics from mere screening tools to primary diagnostic features represents a computationally efficient paradigm shift in neuroimaging pipelines.

While the 1D-CNN and ResNet architectures are highly effective for extracting spatial hierarchies from raw neuroimaging data, their relatively lower performance in this study compared to the classical ensemble (SVM-KNN-XGBoost) warrants analysis. This performance gap is likely attributable to three primary factors. First, feature dimensionality: deep architectures are typically optimized for high-dimensional grid data, whereas our study utilizes a refined vector of 14 technical Quality Assessment (QAP) metrics. Applying complex models to this small tabular feature set often leads to over-parameterization. Second, the inductive bias of tree-based models like XGBoost and kernel methods like SVM is naturally better suited for tabular data where feature relationships are non-linear but not spatially hierarchical. Finally, data volume plays a role; although our cohort is substantial for classical machine learning, it remains at the lower threshold for training deep residual networks from scratch, making the ensemble approach more robust for this specific feature space28.

While our study achieves high accuracy, we must acknowledge that the ABIDE II dataset is inherently diverse within itself. Because the data comes from multiple research sites using different scanner hardware, some of the patterns we observed might arise from the technical differences rather than biological ones. Additionally, although our model successfully distinguishes between two groups, ASD and TD. Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a broad spectrum; future research should move toward multi-class categories that better reflect this complexity. This study has several drawbacks even though it achieved good classification performance. For deep learning-based neuroimaging research, the final dataset’s 463 subjects might be regarded as a moderate sample size. The need for comprehensive and excellent multimodal imaging data spanning anatomical MRI, DTI, and functional MRI modalities restricted the sample size. Larger and more varied datasets could enhance the generalizability and robustness of the model, even if the dataset size is similar to previous ASD neuroimaging classification research using the ABIDE repository. Future research will concentrate on adding more datasets and multicenter data to improve the suggested framework’s scalability and clinical usability. Finally, while the “Quality Vector” approach is promising for ASD, testing it on other neurodevelopmental conditions will be essential to see if it can serve as a universal tool for brain-based diagnostics.

Conclusion

The early detection of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is essential as it allows for interventions that can substantially expand the potential for social and adaptive skills, which in turn enhances the developmental outcomes of the children. This work effectively processes the MRI data from three different modalities, extracts meaningful features and applies ML models to classify as ASD or non ASD (TD). The feature engineering techniques, such as, SNR Difference, FSVI and MIS helped to determine the key quality metrics, improving the dataset interpretability. The correlation and clustering analysis aided in identifying the potential ASD biomarkers. Additionally, the outlier detection and removal using DBSCAN, enhanced the dataset reliability and model performance. The three models- SVM, KNN and XGBoost were trained, tested and assessed based on the performance metrics. The work highlights relevance of data preprocessing and feature engineering in optimizing the ML models for medical diagnosis. In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the technical indicators of MRI quality often discarded as noise, carry significant diagnostic value for Autism Spectrum Disorder. By transitioning from high-dimensional raw imaging data to a refined 14-feature multimodal quality vector, we achieved a robust classification accuracy of 0.9584 and a ROC AUC of 0.9979 through Stratified 10-fold Cross-Validation.

This study contributes to the advancement of computational neuroimaging as it offers a framework for the automated MRI quality assessment and classification of ASD.Our research highlights a critical methodological insight: when combined with sophisticated feature engineering (such as FSVI and SNR Difference) and rigorous outlier detection, ensemble model can outperform complex deep-learning architectures. This approach offers a computationally lean and site-invariant alternative for multi-center clinical diagnostics. Ultimately, by proving that acquisition stability is a reliable proxy for neurobiological differences, this work establishes a new, interpretable benchmark for the future of automated ASD screening in heterogeneous clinical environments. The future work may focus on implementing the integration of deep-learning based approach and imaging modalities to further improve the diagnosis.
