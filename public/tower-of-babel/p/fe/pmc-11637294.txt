Associations between sex, race/ethnicity, and age and the initiation of chronic high‐risk medication in US older adults
Authors: Katharina Tabea Jungo, Niteesh K Choudhry, Alexander Chaitoff, Julie C Lauffenburger
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh‐risk medication use is associated with an increased risk of adverse events, but little is known about its chronic utilization by key demographic groups. We aimed to study the associations between age, sex, and race/ethnicity with new chronic use of high‐risk medications in older adults.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, we analyzed data from older adults aged ≥65 years enrolled in a national health insurer who started a high‐risk medication between 2017 and 2022 across 16 high‐risk medication classes. We used generalized estimating equations to estimate the associations between sociodemographic classifications and the onset of chronic high‐risk medication use after initiation (≥90 days' supply across ≥2 fills within 180 days). We adjusted the analyses for sociodemographic and clinical patient characteristics and added three‐way interaction terms for race/ethnicity, sex, and age to explore whether the outcome varied across different subgroups of race/ethnicity, age, and sex.ResultsAcross 2,751,069 patients (mean age: 74 years [SD = 7], 72% White, 60% Female), 406,075 (15%) became new chronic users of ≥1 high‐risk medication. Compared to White older adults, Asian (RR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.79–0.84), Black (RR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.90–0.94), and Hispanic (RR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.83–0.86) older adults had a lower risk of becoming new chronic users. Men had a higher risk compared to women (RR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.08–1.10). Age was not significantly associated with new chronic high‐risk medication use (≥75 years: RR = 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00–1.01). We observed differences across some medication classes, like benzodiazepines, first‐generation antihistamines, and antimuscarinics for which non‐White older adults were at a higher risk. The joint presence of specific age, sex, and race/ethnicity characteristics decreased the risk of becoming a new chronic user (e.g., Hispanic/Female/65–74 years: RR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99).ConclusionsNew chronic high‐risk medication use varied across older adults by sociodemographic characteristics, suggesting the need to individualize medication optimization approaches and better understand how systematic barriers in access to health care may influence differences in high‐risk medication use in older adults.Keywords: age, chronic medication use, gender, high‐risk medications, older adults, race/ethnicity, sex, sociodemographic characteristics

INTRODUCTION

More than 20% of older adults in the United States (US) regularly use at least one high‐risk medication, 1 , 2 with a prevalence that continues to increase. 3 , 4 Although the risks can vary by medication class, their use has been thought to lead to many different adverse outcomes, such as drug‐related hospital admissions and functional decline. 5 For example, sedative hypnotics and benzodiazepines have been shown to increase the risk of falling in older adults by 30%. 6 , 7 Although some medications should be avoided in older adults at all times, others become particularly problematic when being used for extended periods due to their long‐term consequences (e.g., benzodiazepines vs. proton‐pump inhibitors [PPIs]) and increased risks of adverse events over time (e.g., nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs]). 8 , 9 , 10

According to a recent systematic review, some of the most well‐known demographic and clinical factors among community‐dwelling US older adults associated with potentially inappropriate medication use include female sex, higher resource and medication use, and psychiatric comorbidities. 11 For other key patient factors, like age and race/ethnicity, associations with inappropriate medication use among older adults have varied. 11 , 12 , 13 Specifically, no specific race/ethnicity or age group has been consistently identified as being at greater risk of using high‐risk medications. These mixed associations across prior studies may be due to the heterogeneity of different medications in prior studies. Many prior studies have not included any race/ethnicity evaluations, which also limits the comparability. 11 , 12 Furthermore, most prior studies have also not specifically focused on chronic high‐risk medication use.

Therefore, we sought to study the association between age, sex, race/ethnicity, and chronic use of a wide array of high‐risk medications in older adults who newly started using such medications. We hypothesized that there could be some key differences across sociodemographic subgroups that may be important for clinical and policy interventions that seek to reduce the chronic use of these medications.

METHODS

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Brigham and Women's Hospital. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines. 14

Data sources

In this retrospective cohort study, we used administrative claims from Optum's de‐identified Clinformatics® Data Mart Database (CDM or Clinformatics®), which is derived from a database of administrative health claims for members of large commercial and Medicare Advantage health plans. Clinformatics® utilizes medical and pharmacy claims to derive patient‐level enrollment information, healthcare costs, and resource utilization information. The population is geographically diverse, spanning all 50 states and is statistically de‐identified under the Expert Determination method consistent with HIPAA and managed according to Optum® customer data use agreements. CDM administrative claims submitted for payment by providers and pharmacies are verified, adjudicated, and de‐identified before inclusion. Specifically, commercial private health insurance plans are offered by for‐profit and not‐for‐profit entities and are typically purchased by individuals or provided through employers. 15 Medicare Advantage, also known as Medicare Part C, is an alternative to traditional Medicare (Parts A and B) offered by private insurance companies approved by Medicare 16 and now provides coverage for over half of Medicare eligible beneficiaries. 16 , 17

Definition of high‐risk medications

Based on medications with a moderate or high quality of evidence and a strong level of recommendation for avoidance in older adults from the 2019 version of the American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria® and medications with strong anticholinergic properties from the widely used Medicines Scorecard of the Anticholinergic Burden Calculator (ACB calculator) (score of ≥2), 18 , 19 we picked 16 different medication classes for which potential harms are deemed to outweigh benefits in older adults (Table S1). We excluded medications that were mostly available over the counter (OTC) and required additional information (e.g., lab values, unavailable in the claims data). Because molecularly related medications are typically considered interchangeable (e.g., within benzodiazepines), we measured medication use at the class level.

Cohorts

The “cohort entry date” was the date on which a high‐risk medication from any of the classes was first dispensed between January 2017 and December 2022 (Figure 1). To be included, patients had to (i) start a medication in one of the 16 high‐risk medication classes, (ii) maintain continuous enrollment in the 365‐day period before the cohort entry date, (iii) be ≥65 years old at cohort entry, (iv) receive ≥1 medical service or have ≥1 medication dispensed in the 365 days before cohort entry, (v) have information on race/ethnicity, (vi) never have used a medication from the respective high‐risk medication class at any point previously (using all available data before 365 days before cohort entry), and (vii) have information on sex. We excluded individuals who did not start the follow‐up period (because they were censored before the start of the follow‐up period or had missing information on covariates) and those with negative co‐pays for medications during the baseline period (≤0.05% of sample). Patients could enter the overall cohort multiple times by being dispensed medications for each of the 16 classes (Figure S2). All cohorts were generated with the Aetion Evidence Generation Platform. 20

Visualization of inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Exposure

We measured age, sex, and race/ethnicity directly from enrollment files. Race/ethnicity information in the Clinformatics® dataset was derived using a proprietary algorithm that assigns a category based on geography and name. 21 For age, we stratified between 65 and 74 years and ≥75 years to distinguish by age group. Adults aged 65–74 years tend to have better health and face fewer health outcomes, require less support in activities of daily living, and often are still active on the job market. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25

Outcome

The primary outcome was onset of chronic high‐risk medication use after initiation (across any of the 16 high‐risk medication classes). Secondary outcomes were the onset of chronic high‐risk medication use after initiation by each of the 16 medication classes. Chronic medication use of high‐risk medications was defined as a days' supply of ≥90 days across ≥2 fills in the respective medication classes within 180 days of the start date (=cohort entry date), a conservative definition. 2 Reasons for censoring were the occurrence of the outcome, death, disenrollment, and end of available data.

Covariates

We measured patient characteristics in the 365 days before the cohort entry date, including: Combined Comorbidity Score and the 20 chronic conditions measured to assess this score, 26 , 27 number of unique medications, healthcare utilization (number of inpatient visits and number of outpatient/ambulatory visits), Frailty Index, 28 , 29 , 30 type of insurance (commercial/private vs. Medicare Advantage), low‐income subsidy, and co‐payments for all baseline medications. We also measured the co‐payment for the high‐risk medication on the index date.

Statistical analyses

We first described patient characteristics by race/ethnicity, sex, and age. For patients who entered the overall analytic cohort for multiple medication classes, we restricted descriptive statistics of baseline characteristics to their first cohort entry. For main analyses, we used generalized estimating equations (GEE) with a Poisson distribution with a log link function and robust standard errors to estimate relative risks for the association between race/ethnicity, sex, and age and new chronic use of high‐risk medications. 31 The models adjusted for measured patient characteristics and patient‐level clustering, because patients were able to enter the overall cohort multiple times for different medication classes (maximum once per class). We added three‐way interaction terms for race/ethnicity, sex, and age to the model (race/ethnicity # age category # sex) to explore whether the relationship between race/ethnicity, age, and sex and the outcome varies across different subgroups of these variables. In secondary analyses, we used generalized linear models (GLM) using a Poisson distribution, a log link function, and robust standard errors to examine the relationship between chronic race/ethnicity, sex, and age and new chronic use of high‐risk medications by medication class (Table S1), which by definition included each patient only once, and present the results by medication class in a heatmap.

In a subgroup analysis, we restricted analyses to the number of medications filled in the baseline period to investigate whether polypharmacy is an effect modifier (<5, ≥5, the most commonly used threshold 32 ).

Next, we conducted sensitivity analyses by (a) cohort entry year, (b) restricting to patients using medical services at baseline (≥1 ambulatory visit), (c) restricting to those with a complete 180‐day follow‐up, (d) by restricting to patients' first cohort entry (in case they entered the overall analytic cohort for multiple medication classes), and (e) focusing on American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria® only (the category “antimuscarinics” was dropped, the first‐generation antihistamine and skeletal muscle relaxants categories were newly analyzed after dropping the medications from the ACB calculator, and the remaining categories remained unchanged). Results from subgroup and sensitivity analyses are presented in a heatmap.

Finally, to explore a traditional approach to evaluating the sociodemographic characteristics and to explore the robustness of the main effects, we estimated the association between individual race/ethnicity, sex, and age categories and the new chronic use of high‐risk medications without the interaction terms using GEE with a Poisson distribution with a log link function and robust standard errors.

All analyses were conducted in Stata version 17. Forest plots were created in R (version 4.3.1). All p‐values were two‐sided, with p = 0.05.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

RESULTS

In the final cohort, there were 4,184,944 observations from 2,751,069 unique patients (each observation represents the initiation of a high‐risk medication). On average, patients entered the cohort for two different classes (median = 2 [interquartile range = 2]). Patients' mean age was 74 years (SD = 7), 72% were White (n = 1,978,958), and 60% were female (n = 1,649,246). Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics by age, sex, and racial/ethnic groups are displayed in Table 1 and Table S2. There were more Asian, Black, and Hispanic older adults with a low‐income subsidy, and the combined comorbidity score was lower in Asian older adults, but characteristics were otherwise similar across the race/ethnicity, age, and sex groups.

During follow‐up, 406,075 (15%) became new chronic users of at least one high‐risk medication (Figure 2). Across the 16 high‐risk medication classes, chronic use ranged from 2% for pain medications, 3% for barbiturates to 50% for long‐acting sulfonylureas (Table S3). Table S4 shows the reasons for censoring by race/ethnicity, sex, and age group, which were relatively similar across groups except for death by age group. Only 3.8% of older adults (n = 158,873) died during the follow‐up period. However, there was an age difference with 1.9% of adults aged 65–74 years and 6.5% of adults aged ≥75 years passing away during the follow‐up period.

Compared to White older adults, Asian (RR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.79–0.84), Black (RR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.90–0.94), and Hispanic (RR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.83–0.86) older adults had a lower risk of becoming chronic users. Men had a 9% higher risk compared to women (RR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.08–1.10) (Figure 3). In the main analyses, age was not significantly associated with new chronic high‐risk medication use (≥75 years: RR = 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00–1.01). As shown by the interaction terms, the joint presence of specific age, sex, and race/ethnicity characteristics decreased the risk of becoming a new chronic user (e.g., Hispanic/Female/65–74 years: RR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99, Black/Male/65–74 years: RR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.92–0.97) compared to White/Female/65–74 years older adults. A full list of relative risks from the main analysis is shown in Table S5. Other variables associated with the initiation of high‐risk medication were receiving a low‐income subsidy, co‐pay for medications, combined comorbidity score, several types of comorbidities, frailty index, number of medications filled, number of days hospitalized, and number of outpatient visits.

Like the overall results, similar trends across race/ethnicity, sex, and age subgroups were observed for most medication classes as can be seen in the heatmap (Figure S2). However, for some, we also found an opposite trend with some or all non‐White race/ethnic, sex, and age subgroups being at a higher risk of becoming new chronic users. For instance, for benzodiazepines, Hispanic older adults had a 9% higher risk of becoming chronic users (RR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.01–1.16). For first‐generation antihistamines, Hispanic older adults had a 11% higher risk of becoming chronic users (RR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.01–1.22). Similarly, Black older adults had a 10% higher risk of becoming chronic users of antimuscarinics (RR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.04–1.17).

Subgroup analyses by whether patients had <5 or ≥5 medications (those “with polypharmacy” 32 ) at baseline, showed similar results except for age which suggested some differences by age not evident in the primary analysis (Figure S4). Specifically, older adults aged ≥75 years with <5 medications had a higher risk of becoming new chronic users (RR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.15–1.19) than those 65–74 years of age, and older adults aged ≥75 years with ≥5 medications had a lower risk (RR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.96–0.97) compared to their counterparts aged 65–74 years. Subgroup analyses by cohort entry year also showed similar results for race/ethnicity and sex, but again differed for age. In 2021, older adults aged ≥75 years had a higher risk of becoming new chronic users (RR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.09–1.13), whereas in other years, they had a lower risk (2019: RR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.95–0.99; 2020: RR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.97; 2022: RR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.92–0.96).

All sensitivity analyses were like the main findings for race/ethnicity and sex, but for age, the results differed. For example, in patients with a complete 180‐day follow‐up period, patients aged ≥75 years had a 2% higher risk of becoming new chronic users (RR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.02–1.03) and in the analyses restricted to patients' first cohort entry older adults aged ≥75 years had a 2% higher risk of becoming new chronic users (RR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03). The results from the analyses restricted to American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria® were like the main results (Figure S4). In the main model, the only difference was a statistically significant age difference (≥75 years: OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.97–0.99). In the analysis by medication class, Hispanic older adults were no longer at a higher risk of becoming new chronic users of first‐generation antihistamines.

Models estimating associations of race/ethnicity, age, and sex without interaction terms showed similar results for race/ethnicity (Asian: RR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.75–0.77, Black: RR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.88–0.89, Hispanic: RR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.82–0.83) and sex (Male: RR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.09–1.10) (Table S6). However, these analyses indicated that older adults aged ≥75 years had a 1% higher risk of becoming new chronic users (RR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.01–1.02) than those 65–74 years of age.

DISCUSSION

In this nationwide retrospective cohort study, once older US adults started high‐risk medications, non‐White older adults had a lower risk of becoming new chronic users whereas men had a greater risk. Although the main results did not show any age difference, some secondary analyses found older adults aged ≥75 years had a lower or higher risk of becoming new chronic users of certain medications. The joint presence of specific race/ethnicity, sex, and age categories decreased the risk of becoming new chronic users, some of the most notable being female sex and age 65–74 years. We observed some differences across some medication classes, like benzodiazepines and first‐generation antihistamines.

Comparing against prior literature, 11 , 12 many previous studies analyzed “potentially inappropriate medications” as one group, not presenting by class, not focusing on new users; they have also often blurred the line between prescribing and actual medication use, and long‐term use, which are actually distinct behaviors and which limits the comparability. That women had a lower risk of becoming chronic users of high‐risk medications than their male counterparts warrants further consideration. Prior studies have found mixed associations between sex and high‐risk medication use. 11 , 12 , 33 One key reason for why our results may differ from previous studies 11 is that we focused on new chronic use, which conditions on having started the medication and may be more clinically meaningful. In other contexts, adherence and persistence to long‐term medication has consistently been shown to be lower for women than men due to a variety of barriers 34 , 35 including access and other systematic barriers to obtaining medication. The association between age and high‐risk medication use also has not been well characterized previously, which is in part due to murky outcome definitions (prescribing vs. medication use, new use vs. ongoing use), time windows, and age categories. Although some studies have found that younger age in older adults is associated with the high‐risk medication use, 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 other studies found the opposite, 2 , 41 , 42 and a third group did not observe any association. 43 Similarly, differences in secondary analyses and across calendar years could be due to underlying changes in healthcare utilization.

Considering known systematic barriers in access to healthcare related the social construct of race/ethnicity, that White older adults had the highest risk of becoming chronic users of high‐risk medication warrants further discussion. This finding aligns with a recent study of initial use in Medicare beneficiaries even though they did not specifically study new chronic use. 44 However, other prior studies have not consistently identified an association between race/ethnicity and use of high‐risk medications. 2 , 37 , 38 , 45 For example, several studies have found that Black older adults are at a higher risk, 46 , 47 whereas others found a lower risk, 48 and multiple studies identified Asian older adults as being at lowest risk. 36 , 46 Thus, racial/ethnic minorities may be at a lower risk of high‐risk chronic use, with some exceptions (e.g., benzodiazepines, first‐generation antihistamines, and antimuscarinics), perhaps in part because better healthcare access (and perhaps also less mistrust of healthcare providers) could be associated with more observed overtreatment in White older adults. 49 , 50 One study showed Hispanic older adults had higher risk of new benzodiazepine use, 48 but that finding was not replicated in subsequent research that found a lower risk of prescribing. 51 One explanation could be that despite their lower risk of being prescribed benzodiazepines, once starting, Hispanic older adults may be less likely to discontinue them due to worse access to other nonmedical treatments. 49 , 52 , 53 , 54 Socioeconomic factors may also play an important role, 55 , 56 related to our secondary finding that receiving a low‐income subsidy is also associated. Chronic high‐risk medication may also be more “dangerous” in certain subgroups due to structural/social factors. For example, a patient using a high‐risk medication (e.g., skeletal muscle relaxants) who has a spouse as a care partner or resources to hire a caregiver may be less likely to experience a fall than someone living alone without any support. 57

In recent years, there has been increasing interest in designing interventions to optimize medication use in all older adults, because access to high‐quality prescribing should be a priority for all older adults to prevent medication‐related harm. 58 However, previous medication optimization interventions have had often modest effectiveness. 58 , 59 , 60 One explanation for modest effectiveness is that most medication optimization interventions have considered older adults as a homogenous group, even though diverse populations of older adults may need different deprescribing targets. For instance, although we observed that racial/ethnic minorities have a lower risk of being a chronic user overall, there were some key differences by class, specifically a higher risk of long‐term use of benzodiazepines, antimuscarinics, and first‐generation antihistamines among non‐White older adults. Thus, focusing on individual sociodemographic groups who may be at the greatest risk of becoming long‐term users of high‐risk medication overall versus individual classes (e.g., benzodiazepines) may be one avenue to increase the effectiveness of deprescribing interventions. This might mean that some tailoring of deprescribing intervention delivery and content based on sociodemographic predictors may be warranted to meaningfully distinguish older adults who are at a substantially higher risk, although this requires future confirmatory research. Similarly, considering the large number of older adults using any high‐risk medication, 4 , 11 these findings might help tailor interventions to population groups at the highest risk, because simultaneously giving interventions to all other adults might not be possible at the population level. Finally, although medication reviews should be part of routine care of all older adults, racial/ethnic minorities are less likely to receive reviews in real‐world practice, for a variety of reasons, including for example fewer primary care provider visits. 61 , 62 , 63 Therefore, ensuring that interventions are as efficient as possible and focus on the most likely medication culprits may be necessary.

Finally, when thinking about the delivery of medication optimization interventions, we must better understand what role existing structural barriers and disparities play. As noted above, structural barriers and disparities in access to health care may have led to some of our findings. For instance, prior studies have noted lower adherence to medications, including high‐risk medications, in non‐White older adults. 64 , 65 , 66 Structural barriers also affect intervention delivery, like differences in number of healthcare encounters. 61 , 62 , 63 Because most deprescribing interventions have primarily been studied in White older adults and most studies did not report subgroup analyses by race/ethnicity, 67 , 68 , 69 , 70 better understanding the delivery of medication optimization interventions across sociodemographic groups merits further attention. This is especially important because, as shown in different contexts, interventions could contribute to widening disparities. For instance, an intervention designed to improve advanced care planning documentation in hospitalized patients unintendingly widened disparities across race/ethnicity with Latino and Black patients having lower rates of improvement than White patients. 71

This study has several limitations. First, the race/ethnicity information in the claims was derived using a proprietary algorithm that assigns a category based on geography and name, 21 introducing potential misclassification bias. 72 , 73 Further, the data source previously classified patients into racial/ethnic categories rather than separate categories. Second, these data contain older adults with private health insurance or Medicare Advantage with sufficient enrollment, limiting generalizability (e.g., covered individuals are more likely to be White). Third, claims data may not reflect actual medication‐taking, but we opted for a conservative and specific definition of chronic use that combines number of fills and days' supply, reducing outcome misclassification. Fourth, we excluded older adults with unknown race/ethnicity and sex. However, this only affected a small proportion of the analytic cohort (<0.01% for sex and 6.7% for race/ethnicity). Fifth, although we used the widely recognized American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria® to define high‐risk medications and focused on medications with the highest level of risk and evidence, the criteria used to define high‐risk medication are criterion rather than judgment‐based. Furthermore, the potential risks associated with the 16 different categories of high‐risk medications may differ (e.g., PPIs vs. central nervous system medications). Some high‐risk medications are also available OTC and were not captured in claims (e.g., NSAIDs, PPIs). Next, the competing risk of death could be a potential explanation for not detecting differences in new chronic use of high‐risk medication by age group. Finally, we also did not formally adjust for multiple testing, but the p‐values from our models were <0.001, which is below conservative Bonferroni thresholds for the number of adjustments.

CONCLUSIONS

Once older adults started high‐risk medications, White older adults and men had a higher risk of becoming chronic users, with differences that varied across medication classes. The results provide insights for designing and implementing medication optimization interventions for specific groups of older adults to reduce high‐risk medication use and emphasize the need to better understand how systematic barriers in access to health care may influence differences in high‐risk medication use in older adults.
