Bimodal ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene thermoplastic elastomers enabled with “half-sandwich” α-diimine nickel complexes featuring axial η6-phenyl coordination
Authors: Yunlong Gong, Xiaohua Wang, Lishuang Ma, Xuequan Zhang, Heng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bimodal ultra-high molecular weight polyolefin thermoplastic elastomers (UHMW-P-TPEs) represent an important class of materials that uniquely combine the exceptional mechanical strength of UHMW polymers with the high elasticity and improved processability imparted by a tailored low-molecular-weight fraction. Herein, a new family of half-sandwich α-diimine nickel complexes featuring a distinctive axial η6-phenyl coordination was synthesized and thoroughly characterized. During ethylene polymerization, such an unique structural motif not only provides highly effective suppression of chain transfer—enabling access to UHMW elastomeric architectures—but also introduces a dynamic metal–arene interaction that gives rise to two catalytically competent active species. As revealed by detailed experimental studies and DFT calculations, these dual active species promote two distinct monomer-enchainment pathways, i.e. upward and downward insertions, ultimately leading to the formation of bimodal polyethylene elastomers. Notably, the degree of bimodality can be modulated by using polymerization conditions such as temperature and ethylene pressure, which govern the balance between the tightly bound and weakly bound η6-phenyl coordination states. Overall, this work establishes an effective catalyst-design strategy for generating structurally tunable bimodal UHMW-P-TPEs and provides mechanistic insights that may guide future development of advanced polyolefin elastomers.

Introduction

Polyolefin-based thermoplastic elastomers (P-TPEs) derived from α-diimine Ni/Pd catalysts have witnessed tremendous growth over the past few decades.1–11 Unlike conventional approaches for synthesizing P-TPEs that primarily involve group IV catalysts for ethylene/α-olefin copolymerization or olefin homopolymerization, wherein hard and soft sequences are tuned by controlling comonomer incorporation or stereoregularity, the Ni/Pd-catalyzed P-TPEs represent a more conceptually simplified approach that relies solely on ethylene or propylene as the monomer feed and avoids the need for α-olefin comonomers and chain shuttling agents, which are required in the production of traditional P-TPEs, such as olefin block copolymers of INFUSE™. Additionally, by finely adjusting the extent of chain-walking, which affects the degree and distribution of branching, Ni/Pd-catalyzed P-TPE products with semi-crystalline or amorphous architectures can be conveniently obtained. Furthermore, for Ni/Pd-catalyzed systems, incorporation of sterically bulky ligands that effectively suppress the chain transfer reaction can enable the formation of ultra-high molecular weight P-TPEs (UHMW-P-TPEs) with significantly improved mechanical performance.12–21 However, similar to other UHMW polymers, UHMW-P-TPEs also suffer from poor processability due to their branched, highly entangled architectures in the melt state. One promising strategy to overcome this limitation involves introducing low-molecular weight counterparts to produce bimodal UHMW polymers, which can improve the entanglement dynamics and melt rheology while retaining the intrinsic mechanical properties and durability of the high-molecular-weight fraction.22–27 Nevertheless, to date, reports on bimodal UHMW-P-TPEs remain very scarce, although several examples of Ni/Pd-mediated bimodal P-TPEs with moderate molecular weights have been documented.28–36 As such, research studies in this field remain an active and evolving topic, with continued efforts focused on exploring novel catalytic systems, and/or developing effective polymerization techniques.

For α-diimine Ni/Pd complex mediated olefin polymerization, the key to producing UHMW polymers lies in the effective suppression of the chain transfer reaction. Besides increasing the axial bulkiness of the ligand (Chart 1(1)),33,37–66 introducing a secondary non-covalent interaction to occupy the axial site of the active species has recently emerged as an alternative and highly effective strategy for retarding chain transfer and thereby achieving products with exceptionally high molecular weight (Chart 1(2)).67–76 One prominent example is the α-diimine Ni/Pd system bearing axial pyridine donating ligands, with which products with molecular weight (Mw) up to 1.67 × 106 g mol−1 could be afforded.77 To date, such non-covalent interactions incorporated into the α-diimine Ni/Pd system have been primarily based on metal–heteroatom coordination. Although these interactions effectively suppress chain transfer, the heteroatom donors may also potentially induce partial catalyst deactivation by forming strong metal–heteroatom interactions—an effect particularly pronounced when considering the cationic nature of the active species. Therefore, incorporating heteroatom-free non-covalent interactions, such as metal-hydrogen or metal–π interactions that are often commonly observed in organo-Ni/Pd coordination complexes,78–83 presents an attractive alternative (Chart 1(3)).84,85 For instance, in our recent work, the incorporation of an anagostic Ni⋯H–C interaction into α-diimine NiBr2 complexes enabled the formation of active species exhibiting exceptional capability in suppressing associative chain transfer, thereby affording UHMW-P-TPEs with Mw values up to 7.24 × 106 g mol−1.86 Another notable example is the “sandwich” α-diimine Ni/Pd complex featuring metal–π arene interactions with η6 or η2-coordinated structures, which could also produce branched UHMW-P-TPEs with very narrow molecular weight distributions.87 These metal–arene interactions can effectively suppress chain transfer and simultaneously avoid catalyst deactivation. Nevertheless, they are theoretically much weaker than the aforementioned metal–heteroatom interactions, and therefore are anticipated to be possibly hemilabile in nature by occasional dissociation or partial dissociation to generate a different structured active species, thus providing a mechanistic foundation for the formation of bimodal polymer products as above desired.

α-Diimine Ni/Pd complexes bearing metal–heteroatom coordination interaction or metal-hydrogen or metal–π interactions (top); concept of constructing α-diimine Ni complexes bearing axial η6-phenyl coordination (bottom).

Building upon our previous research on α-diimine nickel complexes featuring an axial Ni⋯H–C anagostic interaction,86 we sought to further exploit such secondary non-covalent interactions to modulate polymerization behaviors. Inspired by the flippable nature of the central phenyl group in such a ligand, in this work, a bulkier α-diimine backbone, dibenzobarrelenyl, is introduced to induce a strong steric repulsion with the central phenyl ring, prompting it to flip over to construct an axial η6-phenyl coordinated structure (Chart 1 (bottom)). Mechanistic studies by DFT during ethylene polymerization (vide infra) reveal that such η6-phenyl coordination behaves as a partially dynamic interaction with the nickel metal center under elevated temperature conditions, thus creating two different Ni-based active species—one tightly coordinated with the phenyl ring and the other relatively weakly bound—that could successfully enable the generation of bimodal P-TPEs. Furthermore, the active species featuring tightly coordinated axial phenyl exhibit excellent suppression of the chain transfer reaction, thereby enabling the production of the desired bimodal UHMW-P-TPEs. Detailed studies will be discussed in the following section.

Treating the ligands meso-(S,R)-L1 and rac-(R,R-S,S)-L1 with a stoichiometric amount of DME·NiBr2 in CH2Cl2 could give the target nickel dibromide complexes meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and rac-(R,R-S,S)-Ni1. All the complexes were well identified by FT-IR, elemental analysis and mass spectra. In FT-IR, the stretching vibration peaks of the imine CN group exhibited clear redshifts from 1664 cm−1 to 1601 cm−1 after coordination. Nevertheless, perhaps due to the relatively weak interaction between nickel and the phenyl ring, these NiBr2 complexes are slowly decomposed when being stored at room temperature or rapidly decomposed under moisture conditions, which hampered the isolation of their single crystals after several attempts.

To provide more direct evidence for the desired Ni-phenyl η6-coordinated structure, meso-(S,R)-Ni1acac and rac-(R,R-S,S)-Ni1acac complexes, bearing cationic nickel metal centers, were alternatively prepared via treating the ligands with 1.0 equiv. Ni(acac)2 and [CPh3][B(C6F5)4], and subsequently thoroughly well characterized. For meso-(S,R)-Ni1acac and rac-(R,R-S,S)-Ni1acac, their 1H NMR spectra can be unambiguously assigned. As shown in Fig. S6 for meso-(S,R)-Ni1acac, the singlet at 5.26 ppm is assigned to the proton on the para-position of the tert-Bu group on the central phenyl ring. Its markedly upfield shift compared to typical aromatic protons is because such a hydrogen atom is located directly above one of the phenyl rings of the dibenzobarrelene skeleton (cf. the atom H1 in the single crystal structure of meso-(S,R)-Ni1acac), which causes strong shielding effects. The signal at 4.98 ppm is attributed to the protons on the CH linker attached to the central phenyl ring, and resonance peak at 4.58 ppm is ascribed to the methine proton on the dibenzobarrelene backbone. For the 1H NMR spectrum of rac-(R,R-S,S)-Ni1acac (Fig. S10), similar assignments were demonstrated. Layering diethyl ether over the saturated CH2Cl2 solution of the complexes could isolate the single crystal of meso-(S,R)-Ni1acac and (R,R)-Ni1acac. As the structures shown in Fig. 1(2 and 3) and data shown in Chart 2, such crystal structures reveal the following prominent characteristics: (1) the central phenyl rings (highlighted by red color) are nearly parallel with the coordination planes, giving very small dihedral angles of 3.21 and 5.93°, respectively; (2) the centroids of the central phenyl rings (defined as point A) lie almost directly above the nickel center, as evidenced by ∠Ni1-A-C1 angles approaching 90° (90.13° and 82.05°); (3) the distances between A points and the nickel atoms are 3.014 and 3.033 Å, falling within the expected range for nickel coordination bonds; (4) the distances between the nickel atom and six carbon atoms on the central phenyl rings are very similar, such as in meso-(S,R)-Ni1acac, such distances are 3.156, 3.193, 3.369, 3.567, 3.459 and 3.260 Å, respectively. All these data indicate the formation of half-sandwich type η6-coordinated structures in these two complexes.

To gain further insight into the character of the axial η6-phenyl coordination in nickel complexes meso-(S,R)-Ni1acac and (R,R)-Ni1acac, we carried out both visual and quantitative analyses of the non-covalent interactions within these complexes. The cation–π interactions between the central Ni and the phenyl ring, in the sense of the classical cation–π concept,88 are directly and graphically depicted in Chart 3 by means of the interaction region indicator (IRI) space function. In line with the structural trends, the IRI isosurfaces clearly show a more pronounced cation–π interaction in meso-(S,R)-Ni1acac than in (R,R)-Ni1acac.89,90 We then performed an energy decomposition analysis based on molecular forcefield (EDA-FF),91 in which the total interaction between Ni cations and the phenyl fragment is decomposed into electrostatic and van der Waals (vdW) contributions, with the latter further separated into repulsive and attractive dispersion terms (Chart 3). The EDA-FF results reveal total interaction energies of −12.11 and −7.52 kJ mol−1 for meso-(S,R)-Ni1acac and (R,R)-Ni1acac, respectively. In both cases the interaction is dominated by electrostatics (−11.18 kJ mol−1 for meso-(S,R)-Ni1acac and −6.62 kJ mol−1 for (R,R)-Ni1acac), whereas the vdW contributions are comparatively small. This behavior is consistent with the widely accepted view that cation–π interactions are noncovalent in nature and contain a substantial electrostatic component.88 Taken together, the IRI and EDA-FF analyses consistently show that the strength of the Ni⋯phenyl cation–π interaction decreases upon going from meso-(S,R)-Ni1acac to (R,R)-Ni1acac as a result of the steric modulation imposed by the ligands meso-(S,R)-L1 and rac-(R,R/S,S)-L1, which as discussed below, directly correlates with the different catalytic activities and stabilities of the two nickel catalysts.

Cation–π interaction region depicted by the IRI isosurface (left) where the isovalue is set to 1.0 a.u., and the interaction energy analysis obtained by the EDA-FF method (right, in kJ mol−1).

Ethylene polymerization

After their structures were well established, the nickel complexes Ni1 and Ni1acac were subsequently evaluated for ethylene polymerization under various conditions using diethylaluminum chloride (AlEt2Cl) as the cocatalyst. To further assess the influence of the axial η6-phenyl coordination structure on polymerization behavior, the catalytic performance of a dibenzobarrelene-derived α-diimine NiBr2 complex bearing two N-mesityl substituents (ref-Ni1) was also examined for comparison. As the results summarized in Table 1, under identical conditions, neutral dibromide nickel complexes meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1 exhibited significantly higher catalytic activities than their corresponding cationic acetylacetonate analogues meso-(S,R)-Ni1acac and rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1acac (1.66 × 106vs. 0.31 × 106 and 2.48 × 106vs. 0.97 × 106 g PE per (mol of Ni) per h respectively). Nonetheless, both of the present two complexes displayed notably lower activities than benchmark ref-Ni1. Such inferior performances may be attributed to the presence of an axial η6-phenyl coordination structure in Ni1 and Ni1acac, which likely induces a high steric congestion and thereby restricts ethylene access to the metal center. This assumption can be partially reflected from the buried volume (Vburs) differences around the metal center. As shown in Chart 2 (bottom), complexes meso-(S,R)-Ni1acac and the (R,R)-Ni1acac isomer in rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1acac exhibit Vburs of 63.5% and 62.3% respectively, which are substantially higher than the 48.1% in ref-Ni1, suggesting a much more sterically encumbered coordination environment in the former two complexes. Owing to the relatively higher activities of the dibromide nickel complexes, they were intentionally selected for subsequent optimization studies.

Upon increasing the Al/Ni ratio from 200 to 600, the catalytic activity of meso-(S,R)-Ni1 increased gradually from 1.19 × 106 to 1.66 × 106 g PE per (mol of Ni) per h, revealing an optimal Al/Ni ratio of 600. Likewise, increasing the ethylene pressure from 1 to 20 atm resulted in a pronounced enhancement of catalytic performance, with activities increasing from 0.08 × 106 to 1.81 × 106 g PE per (mol of Ni) per h for meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and from 0.08 × 106 to 4.44 × 106 g PE per (mol of Ni) per h for rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1, respectively (Fig. 2). These results are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that higher ethylene pressures generally promote higher catalytic activities.11 Moreover, under identical conditions, rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1 consistently revealed higher activity than meso-(S,R)-Ni1 (Fig. 2 (left)). To rationalize such stereochemistry-dependent differences in catalytic activity, the geometries of complex meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and two enantiomeric isomers of (S,S)-Ni1 and (R,R)-Ni1 in rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1 were optimized by DFT calculations. Based on the optimized geometries, a quadrant model describing the monomer approach to the nickel center was proposed. As illustrated in Fig. 3, for meso-(S,R)-Ni1, both the upper-right and upper-left quadrants in front of the nickel center are strongly sterically congested due to the two outward-protruding phenyl groups attached to the CH linker, whereas in (S,S)-Ni1 or (R,R)-Ni1, only one upper quadrant is strongly hindered, while the other remains semi-hindered owing to the inward orientation of one phenyl substituent. This asymmetric steric environment provides a more open coordination pocket in (S,S)-Ni1 and (R,R)-Ni1, thereby facilitating ethylene access to the metal center during chain propagation. Consequently, the racemic rac-(R,R-S,S)-Ni1 complex exhibits markedly higher catalytic activity than its meso-(S,R)-Ni1 counterpart. Such a mechanistic model was further supported by DFT calculations, which reveal that the meso-(S,R)-Ni1 – mediated polymerization pathway features comparatively higher energy barriers than the analogous process catalyzed by the (R,R)-Ni1 isomer in rac-(R,R-S,S)-Ni1 (vide infra), thereby accounting for the relatively lower reactivity observed in the former case.

Polymerization temperature also exerted a pronounced influence on the catalytic performances. As summarized in Table 2, increasing the temperature from −5 °C to 80 °C initially enhanced but subsequently reduced the polymer yields for both complexes meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1, with optimal activities of 1.66 × 106 and 2.70 × 106 g PE per (mol of Ni) per h observed at 20 °C and 40 °C, respectively. This trend stands in clear contrast to ref-Ni1, which exhibited a monotonic decrease in activity upon increasing the temperatures (Fig. 4 (top)). Another notable distinction is the response of activities to different temperature regimes. At low temperatures of −5 to 20 °C, ref-Ni1 significantly outperformed meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1. For instance, at −5 °C, the activity of ref-Ni1 is ca. 23- and 28- fold higher than those obtained from meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1. Nevertheless, at elevated temperatures of 40–80 °C, meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1 demonstrated excellent thermal robustness by maintaining appreciable catalytic activities. Specifically, meso-(S,R)-Ni1 retained an activity of 0.84 × 106 g PE per (mol of Ni) per h at 80 °C, corresponding to a catalyst decay percentage of 49.4% when compared with its optimal value at 20 °C, with a value of 1.66 × 106 g PE per (mol of Ni) per h. Similarly, rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1 delivered an activity of 1.04 × 106 g PE per (mol of Ni) per h at 80 °C, with a decay of 61.5% compared to its maximum at 40 °C, with a value of 2.70 × 106 g PE per (mol of Ni) per h. In striking contrast, ref-Ni1 suffered a sharp loss of activity above 40 °C, and at 80 °C, its activity dropped to only 0.26 × 106 g PE per (mol of Ni) per h, corresponding to a dramatic decay of 96.7% relative to its optimal value at −5 °C, with a value of 7.89 × 106 g PE per (mol of Ni) per h. All of these results indicate the markedly higher thermal stabilities of the active species in meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1, which were conferred by the axial η6-phenyl coordination.

13C NMR spectroscopy was performed to determine the chain structures of the obtained polyethylenes, and it was surprisingly found that only methyl branches were detected, while no long alkyl branches were observed. As shown in Fig. 5, for the representative sample obtained from Table 1, entry 14, a branching density of 70.3 branches per 1000C was determined, with methyl branches accounting for more than 99% of the total branches. In addition, no signals were detected in the range of 10.0–18.0 ppm, which is generally assigned to long-chain branches. A more detailed analysis of the methyl-branch distribution revealed the presence of 1,4-Me2, 1,6-Me2, and 1,x-Me2 units (x ≥ 8),47 whereas no 1,2-Me2 units, corresponding to two adjacent methyl branches, were detected. For the sample obtained from Table 1, entry 14, the contents of 1,4-Me2, 1,6-Me2, and 1,x-Me2 units were determined to be 29.2%, 30.7%, and 40.1%, respectively. These results indicate that the methyl branches are distributed with different methylene sequence lengths between neighboring branches. Furthermore, the methyl-branch distribution was found to be significantly influenced by the polymerization pressure and temperature. When a higher ethylene pressure of 10 atm was applied (Table 1, entry 3), the contents of 1,4-Me2 and 1,6-Me2 units decreased to 3.0% and 2.0%, respectively, while the content of 1,x-Me2 units increased to 95.0%. Similarly, when polymerization was conducted at a low temperature of −5 °C (Table 2, entries 1 and 7), the 1,4-Me2 and 1,6-Me2 units completely disappeared, and all methyl branches adopted the 1,x-Me2 distribution pattern, as shown in Fig. 5 (top). This result suggests that the methyl branches are isolated along the polyethylene backbone under these conditions. For these four samples, the high content of 1,x-Me2 units indicates the presence of long methylene sequences between neighboring methyl branches. This structural feature may explain why the obtained polyethylenes still exhibit relatively high melting temperatures despite their high branching densities. Fig. 5 (right) presents the DSC curve of the sample obtained from Table 1, entry 14. The polymer exhibits a very broad melting range, indicating the presence of polyethylene sequences with different lengths, which likely results from the heterogeneous distribution of methyl branches along the polyethylene backbone. This observation is consistent with the 13C NMR results. Such unevenly distributed polyethylene sequences can form crystalline domains with different lamellar thicknesses, thereby giving rise to broad melting regions. Meanwhile, the presence of sufficiently long polyethylene sequences accounts for the relatively high Tm of 112.9 °C.

13C NMR spectra of the obtained PEs (bottom) and the corresponding branch pattern and distribution (top).

Beyond catalytic activity, the molecular weights of the obtained PEs were also strongly influenced by the ethylene pressure, polymerization temperature, and ligand structure. For both meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1 systems, increasing the ethylene pressure led to a gradual increase in molecular weights (Mws) of the produced PEs from 111.77 × 104 to 183.09 × 104 g mol−1 and from 82.89 × 104 to 151.68 × 104 g mol−1, respectively, with meso-(S,R)-Ni1 giving PEs always exceeding the UHMW level across all tested pressures, and rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1 producing UHMWPEs only when the pressure was above 6 atm. Additionally, under identical conditions, meso-(S,R)-Ni1 consistently afforded PEs with higher Mw than rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1 (Fig. 2 (right)). This observation can be rationalized by the greater steric congestion around the nickel center in meso-(S,R)-Ni1, as illustrated in Fig. 3, which was able to effectively suppress the chain transfer reactions. When the polymerization temperature was increased from −5 °C to 80 °C, the resulting Mws of the obtained PEs decreased monotonically from 209.40 × 104 to 39.25 × 104 g mol−1 for meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and from 182.64 × 104 to 29.01 × 104 g mol−1 for rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1 respectively. This decline is attributed to accelerated chain-transfer processes at elevated temperatures, being consistent with many previous literature observations.11 Across the entire temperature range, meso-(S,R)-Ni1 continued to afford PEs with higher molecular weights than rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1 (Fig. 4 (right)), again corroborating the more effective suppression of chain transfer in the former due to the higher steric encumberment caused by the two outward-protruding phenyl moieties. Furthermore, at both low or high temperature, both meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1 produced markedly higher-molecular-weight PEs than ref-Ni1. For instance, at 60 °C, meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1 generated PEs with Mws of 97.15.40 × 104 and 95.93 × 104 g mol−1 respectively, both of which are ca. 9 times higher than that obtained from ref-Ni1. These findings highlight the governing role of the axial η6-phenyl coordination in effectively suppressing chain-transfer reactions, thereby enabling the synthesis of high-molecular-weight polymers, particularly under high-temperature conditions.

Besides the above higher molecular weight, another even more prominent feature for the obtained PEs is their bimodal characteristic that is strongly influenced by temperature and ethylene pressure. As shown in Fig. 6 (top), for both meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1 systems, elevating the polymerization temperature led to a pronounced broadening of the molecular weight distributions. For instance, in the case of meso-(S,R)-Ni1, the polydispersity index (Đ) increased from 2.72 at −5°C—corresponding to a nearly unimodal distribution—to 11.65 at 80 °C, clearly exhibiting a bimodal profile. This broadening was mainly attributed to the formation of new low-molecular-weight polymer fractions, whose proportion increased progressively with temperature. Similarly, for rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1, Đ increased from 2.47 to 16.70 over the same temperature range, indicating a comparable temperature-dependent evolution from unimodality toward bimodality by generating small-molecular-weight PE fractions. These results showed striking contrast to the ref-Ni1 – involved system, which consistently produced PEs with unimodal polydispersities (Đ = 2.06–2.98) across all tested temperatures. A similar dependence on ethylene pressure was also observed (Fig. 6 (bottom)). As the pressure decreased from 20 to 1 atm, the polydispersities gradually broadened, transitioning from unimodal to distinctly bimodal profiles. This broadening again originated from an increasing proportion of low-molecular-weight PEs. Specifically, for meso-(S,R)-Ni1, Đ increased from 6.15 to 31.75, accompanied by a gradual intensification of the low-molecular-weight peak; while for rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1, Đ similarly increased from 5.79 to 18.64 under identical conditions with a parallel enrichment of the low-molecular-weight component. These results collectively indicate that, at low temperatures or high ethylene pressures, both catalysts predominantly generate a single type of active species, which favors the formation of high-molecular-weight polymers. In contrast, elevated temperatures or reduced ethylene pressures promote the emergence of a second active species, characterized by a slower chain-growth rate and a tendency to produce lower-molecular-weight polymers. Consequently, as the temperature increases or the pressure decreases, the catalytic system transitions from a single-site to a dual-site mechanism, leading to the gradual evolution of the polymer molecular weight distribution from unimodal to bimodal.

Bimodal characteristics of the obtained PEs at different temperatures and pressures for meso-(S,R)-Ni1 and rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1 systems.

To obtain a clear and precise component of the composition of the bimodal polyethylene products, a representative sample (Table 2, entry 4) was subjected to Soxhlet extraction using various types of solvents, including tetrahydrofuran, n-heptane and toluene for 6 h. Among these, n-heptane was found to selectively dissolve the low-molecular-weight fraction. As shown by the GPC curves in Fig. 7, the n-heptane-soluble fraction exhibits a relatively low molecular weight (Mw) of 3.50 × 104 g mol−1 with a polydispersity of 1.92, whereas the insoluble residue retains a much higher molecular weight (Mw = 171.85 × 104 g mol−1) with a polydispersity of 3.29. Further analysis by 1H NMR revealed that both fractions possess comparable branching degrees of 33.3/1000C and 36.5/1000C, closely matching that of the pristine bimodal product (34.5/1000C), suggesting paralleling chain walking capabilities between the two possible catalytic activities.

In order to rationalize the temperature- and pressure-dependent bimodal characteristics observed in the molecular weight distributions, DFT calculations for the meso-(S,R)-Ni1 mediated ethylene polymerization process were performed (Fig. 8). The results reveal that the presence of an axial η6-phenyl moiety could impose different steric interactions with the incoming ethylene molecule, thereby making two alternative monomer enchainment pathways available. Taking the second monomer insertion step during the whole polymerization process as a representative example, the calculated Gibbs free energy profile (Fig. 8) shows that, at the end of the first ethylene insertion cycle, a β-agostic intermediate (Int_4Ni1) is formed, with a detailed coordination geometry depicted in Fig. 9 (top). In such a structure, the Ni–Cα–Cβ–Hβ agostic interaction plane is found to be nearly coplanar with the –CN–Ni–NC– coordination plane. During the subsequent insertion step, as ethylene approaches, cleavage of the Ni–Hβ interaction occurs, allowing the n-propyl–nickel species to rotate around the Ni–Cα bond towards two distinct directions. One is the clockwise rotation, in which the Ni–Cα bond is oriented above the –CN–Ni–NC– coordination plane while the incoming ethylene coordinates predominantly below it, giving intermediate Int_6Ni1 with a structure shown in Fig. 9 (left); the other one is the anticlockwise rotation that forms intermediate Int_5Ni1, wherein the Ni–Cα bond lies below the –CN–Ni–NC– plane and the coordinated ethylene is mainly present above it (Fig. 9 (right)). These anisotropic steric environments enable two distinct ethylene enchainment pathways in the ensuing insertion step. In the Int_5Ni1 → TS_2Ni1 pathway (Fig. 9 (right)), the ethylene molecule resides mainly above the –CN–Ni–NC– plane, experiencing pronounced steric repulsion from the axial η6-phenyl moiety. In contrast, in the Int_6Ni1 → TS_3Ni1 pathway (Fig. 9 (left)), the ethylene is located, on the other hand, below this plane, thereby encountering reduced steric congestion. Based on the spatial orientation of the inserting ethylene, these two chain-growth modes are herein denoted as the “upward” and “downward” enchainment pathways, respectively. The calculated Gibbs free energy profile reveals an obvious energetic difference between these two chain-growth pathways (Fig. 8), reflecting distinct thermodynamic preferences for each route. In the downward pathway, coordination of the ethylene molecule to Int_4Ni1 results in an energy increase of 2.2 kcal mol−1 to give Int_6Ni1, and the subsequent insertion step requires an energy barrier of 16.5 kcal mol−1 to be overcome viaTS_3Ni1; whereas for the upward manner, the significantly stronger steric repulsion between the axial η6-phenyl ring and the incoming ethylene molecule leads to higher energy requirements of 5.8 kcal mol−1 for coordination (Int_5Ni1) and 20.7 kcal mol−1 for insertion viaTS_2Ni1. These results clearly demonstrate that the downward enchainment pathway is energetically more favorable than the upward one, particularly under relatively low-temperature conditions, and thus represents the dominant active species in such environments to generate the high molecular weight products. At elevated temperatures, however, the moderate energy gap (ΔG = 4.2 kcal mol−1) between the two pathways can be thermally compensated, enabling the appearance of a second, less favorable active species corresponding to the upward enchainment mode, that gives products with relatively smaller molecular weights. This computational insight correlates well with the experimental observations, wherein the molecular weight distribution of the resultant polyethylenes is unimodal at low temperatures but gradually evolves toward a bimodal profile upon increasing the temperature.

For the ethylene polymerization process mediated by rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1, DFT calculations on the (R,R)-Ni1 isomer involved system similarly predict two distinct monomer enchainment pathways. As shown in Fig. S1, the downward and upward enchainment routes exhibit activation barriers of 17.3 and 18.9 kcal mol−1 respectively, also favoring the downward insertions. Additionally, these two insertion energy barriers are lower than those calculated for the corresponding insertion steps in meso-(S,R)-Ni1. This observation shows good consistency with the afore-mentioned experimental results, wherein the meso-(S,R)-Ni1 based system exhibited lower activities than rac-(S,S-R,R)-Ni1.

Additionally, comparison of the optimized structures of Int_4Ni1, TS_3Ni1 and TS_2Ni1 reveals clear differences in the strength of the axial metal–arene interaction. The Ni⋯phenyl distances in Int_4Ni1 and TS_3Ni1 are nearly identical (3.161 and 3.162 Å, respectively), indicating that the axial η6-phenyl coordination remains essentially intact along the downward enchainment pathway. In contrast, the corresponding Ni⋯phenyl separation in TS_2Ni1 is significantly elongated to 3.281 Å (Fig. 10, top), suggesting a partial weakening of the metal–arene interaction during the upward enchainment process. This structural trend is further corroborated by energy decomposition analysis (Fig. 10, bottom), which shows a noticeably reduced contribution from the Ni⋯phenyl cation–π interaction in TS_2Ni1 relative to Int_4Ni1 and TS_3Ni1. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the downward enchainment pathway is associated with a more tightly bound axial phenyl coordination, whereas the upward pathway involves a relatively weaker interaction.

The distance between nickel and the phenyl group (top) and interaction energy analysis (bottom) of DFT optimized structures of intermediate Int_4Ni1 and the transition states of TS_2Ni1 and TS_3Ni1.

The presence of a low-molecular-weight fraction in the resultant bimodal UHMPW-P-TPEs results in differences in their mechanical properties compared with the unimodal counterparts. Two presentative samples from Table 2, entries 1 and 4, were selected for tensile stress–strain tests, because the high-molecular-fraction of the bimodal sample (entry 4) closely parallels that of the unimodal polymer (entry 1) (Fig. 6). As the curve shows in Fig. 11(1), both materials exhibit comparable tensile strengths of 28.23 and 28.05 MPa, respectively. However, their elastic responses differ markedly: the bimodal sample displays a much higher elongation at break of 778.01% and a lower Young's modulus, whereas the unimodal material exhibits a significantly higher modulus of 256.01 MPa but fractures at a lower strain. These contrasting behaviors originate from both structural and componential reasons. On one hand, the unimodal polymer, prepared at low temperature, possesses a lower branching degree and thus higher crystallinity, resulting in a more rigid, plastic-like character. whereas the bimodal material, characterized by a higher branching density and reduced crystallinity, behaves more elastomerically. On the other hand, the presence of the low-molecular-weight fraction in the bimodal polymer acts as an internal lubricant, promoting chain-segment mobility and facilitating stress relaxation during deformation, and thus the bimodal sample exhibits a yield strength of 10.27 MPa, followed by a gradual increase in stress over a significantly extended strain range. Further evidence of this enhanced elasticity is provided by the hysteresis experiment, during which, upon stretching to 300% strain, the unimodal sample fractured during the first cycle, whereas the bimodal sample endured the deformation and exhibited a strain recovery (SR) of 33% after 10 cycles (Fig. 11(2)).92 All these findings clearly demonstrate that the unimodal sample (Table 2, entry 1) behaves more like a rigid plastic, while the bimodal counterpart (Table 2, entry 4) exhibits the flexibility, resilience, and recoverability characteristics of an elastomer.

Stress–strain curve (1), plot of the hysteresis experiment (2), and complex viscosity and storage modulus (3–4) for the unimodal and bimodal UHMW-P-TPEs obtained from Table 2, entries 1 and 4.

The complex viscosity and storage modulus of the above two samples were further examined by rotational rheometry in oscillatory mode over the temperature range of 80–180 °C, at a scan rate of 3 °C min−1 with an angular frequency of 1.0 Hz and a strain amplitude of 1%. As shown in Fig. 11(3 and 4), both the complex viscosity (η*) and storage modulus (G′) decreased progressively with increasing temperature, exhibiting a sharp decline near the respective melting temperatures. Notably, across the entire temperature range, the bimodal sample consistently displayed significantly lower complex viscosity and storage modulus than its unimodal counterpart, reflecting its markedly enhanced melt processability. Given that the high-molecular-weight fraction in the bimodal polymer closely matches that of the unimodal sample in molecular weight, this improvement in rheological behavior can be largely attributed to the presence of the low-molecular-weight fraction, which facilitates chain mobility and reduces melt entanglement density.

Conclusions

In summary, we have synthesized and comprehensively characterized a new class of α-diimine nickel complexes featuring a distinct axial η6-phenyl coordination. X-ray diffraction, NMR studies, and the interaction region indicator (IRI) space function analysis collectively confirmed the presence of such metal–arene interactions, with characteristic Ni⋯arene distances of ca. 3.0 Å, thus establishing a unique half-sandwich coordination motif within α-diimine nickel systems. In the presence of a cocatalyst, the obtained nickel complexes were able to afford UHMW-P-TPEs with molecular weights up to 209.40 × 104 g mol−1, owing to the strong suppression of chain transfer imparted by the axial Ni⋯arene interaction. Systematic polymerization studies revealed that both polymer yields and molecular weights increased monotonically with ethylene pressure, while higher temperatures reduce but do not eliminate activity even at a high temperature of 80 °C, highlighting the notable thermal robustness of the active species also imparted by the axial Ni⋯arene interaction. Most notably, the catalysts display a remarkable capability to generate bimodal molecular weight distributions. The extent of bimodality is also tunable at low temperature or high pressure, where the polymers remain predominantly unimodal, whereas increasing temperature or decreasing pressure progressively enhances the contribution of the low-molecular-weight component, leading to distinctly bimodal profiles.

DFT calculations provided a mechanistic rationale for the emergence of bimodality. During the ethylene enchainment procedure, the axial η6-phenyl group imposes an anisotropic steric environment around the metal center that enables two distinct “downward” and “upward” enchainment pathways. The downward pathway, with the monomer coordinated opposite the axial phenyl ring, features relatively lower activation barriers and is therefore favored at low temperature. In contrast, the upward pathway suffers from severe steric repulsion with the η6-arene ligand and displays obviously higher barriers, becoming accessible only at elevated temperature where thermal energy compensates for the penalty. The coexistence of these two energetically differentiated propagation channels directly accounts for the experimentally observed temperature- and pressure- dependent bimodal molecular weight distributions. Moreover, the thermally activated emergence of the higher barrier upward pathway at elevated temperatures directly accounts for the progressive formation of low molecular weight chains, leading to a broader molecular weight distribution and a decrease in the average Mw. Overall, this study identifies axial η6-phenyl coordination as an important design element for modulating the active site structure of α-diimine nickel catalysts. Further efforts toward extending this concept to other transition-metal platforms are underway in our laboratory.

Experimental section

Materials

Toluene was purified via an FLEANO XHFL-MD-3 Purification System. CH2Cl2 and n-hexane were distilled using CaH2 or the Na/K alloy under a nitrogen atmosphere and degassed by three freeze–pump–thaw cycles prior to use. Deuterated solvents used for NMR were dried and distilled prior to use. The chemicals, including anthracene, vinylene carbonate, triethylamine, p-toluenesulfonic acid, 2,4-dimethylaniline, 5-tert-butyl-m-phthalic acid, thionyl chloride, diethylaluminium chloride, etc. were purchased from J&K Scientific and Energy Chemical, and used without further purification. 9,10-Dihydro-9,10-ethanoanthracene-11,12-dione and 1NH2 were prepared according to previously reported methods.93,94

Instruments

All manipulations involving air- and moisture- sensitive compounds were performed under a dry and oxygen-free nitrogen atmosphere using standard Schlenk techniques or in a glove box. The NMR spectra of intermediates and ligands were recorded on a Bruker 400 MHz instrument at ambient temperature. The NMR spectra of polymers were obtained at 120 °C using 1,1,2,2-tetrachloroethane-d2 or o-dichlorobenzene-d4 as the solvent. FTIR spectra were recorded using a Bruker TENSOR 27 FTIR spectrometer. The molecular weight and molecular weight distribution (Mw/Mn) of polymers were measured using an Agilent 1260 infinity II HT high-temperature gel permeation chromatography system equipped with two PLgel 10 µm MIXED-B type columns at 150 °C with 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene as the eluent at a flow rate of 1.0 mL min−1. The obtained molecular weights were determined by using standard polystyrene calibration, and Mark–Houwink correction was also applied. The melting points (Tm) of polymers were obtained by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) on a Netzsch DSC 3500 Sirius instrument at a heating/cooling rate of 10 °C min−1 from 0 to 200 °C. The data were obtained from the second heating cycle. X-Ray diffraction data were collected on a Bruker SMART APEX diffractometer with a CCD area detector, using graphite-monochromated Mo-Kα (λ = 0.71073 Å) or Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.54178 Å). High-resolution mass spectra (HRMS) were obtained on a Waters XEVO G2 QT mass spectrometer.

Mechanical tests and the rheological experiment

Stress/strain experiments were performed at room temperature at 10 mm min−1 for quasi-static tests and 20 mm min−1 for cyclic tests (10 cycles to 300% strain) using a universal testing machine, Instron 5965 from Instron Corporation. At least five specimens of each polymer were tested. Each polymer was melt-pressed at 185 °C and 10 MPa for 5 min to obtain ASTM D638 Type V dumbbell specimens with the following dimensions: gauge length, 12.5 mm; width, 2 mm; thickness, 0.9 mm. Complex viscosity and storage modulus were determined by temperature-sweep experiments using an ARES-G2 rheometer (TA Instruments) with 25 mm diameter parallel plates under a nitrogen atmosphere. The temperature scan from 80 °C to 180 °C was conducted at 3 °C min−1, with frequency fixed at 1.0 Hz (6.28 rad s−1) and oscillatory strain maintained at 1% within the linear viscoelastic region.

Soxhlet extraction

A 0.5 g portion of the as-polymerized resin was wrapped in pre-extracted filter netting (pore size 20 µm), sealed with stainless-steel wire, and subjected to Soxhlet extraction with n-heptane (100 mL) at reflux for 6 h. The insoluble material remaining in the thimble was collected as the high-molecular-weight component. The extract was rotary-evaporated to dryness to afford the low-molecular-weight component. Both fractions were dried in vacuo (50 °C, 24 h) before gravimetric analysis and characterization.

Procedure for ethylene polymerization

A 100 mL stainless steel autoclave equipped with a mechanical stirrer and a temperature controller was heated in a vacuum at 120 °C for 2 h, recharged with ethylene gas three times, and then cooled to room temperature under an ethylene atmosphere. A CH2Cl2 solution (2 mL) of the precatalyst was transferred into the reactor. After the desired reaction temperature was reached, the required amount of the cocatalyst and toluene were added while maintaining the total volume at 50 mL, and then the autoclave was immediately pressurized to the desired pressure. The ethylene pressure was kept constant during the reaction. The reaction mixture was stirred at the desired temperature for 30 min. The polymerization was then quenched by injecting acidified ethanol containing HCl (5% in ethanol). The polymer was collected by filtration, washed with ethanol, and dried to a constant weight under vacuum at 50 °C.
