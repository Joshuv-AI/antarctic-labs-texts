Carboxylated Polystyrene Particles for Plastic Standards in Laser Ablation ICP–MS Trace Elements Analysis: Analytical Aspects and Environmental Relevance
Authors: Maud Gautier, Sandra Mounicou, Javier Jiménez-Lamana, Stéphanie Reynaud, Bruno Grassl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Reliable quantification of trace metals in polymers by direct solid-state analysis remains limited by the lack of suitable reference materials. In this study, we introduce a novel method for producing standardized plastic samples specifically designed for the calibration and analysis of trace metals by laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS). Carboxylated polystyrene (PS) nanoparticles, capable of adsorbing metal ions from solution, were synthesized and evaluated. The trace metal adsorption capabilities of these PS nanoparticles were assessed with adsorption isotherms fit well with the Freundlich model. The study focused cobalt (Co2+), copper (Cu2+), and lead (Pb2+) The adsorption constants were found to be 0.15 for Co2+, 0.48 for Cu2+, and 3.63 for Pb2+. The metal-sorbed PS nanoparticles were then processed into disc-shaped plastics via hot-press molding, and LA-ICP-MS analysis confirmed the homogeneous metal distribution both at the surface and in depth, with relative standard deviations (RSD) of less than 5% for all metals analyzed. Calibration curves for Pb2+ (1220–4400 mg kg–1), Cu2+ (210–600 mg kg–1), and Co2+ (67–220 mg kg–1) showed strong linear relationships, with R 2 values of 0.96 for Pb2+, 0.95 for Cu2+, and 0.94 for Co2+. The methodology limits of detection (LOD) were determined to be 113 mg kg–1 for Pb2+, 93 mg kg–1 for Cu2+, and 23 mg kg–1 for Co2+. The developed standards enabled the calibration of analytical instruments and thus improve the reliability of assessments concerning the contamination of environments with metal-laden microplastics.

Introduction

Plastic pollution is of growing concern, especially as smaller debris spreads throughout all environments and trophic compartments. Plastics consist of a polymer matrix, reactive residues, and chemical additives, with metals originating either from synthesis or as incorporated additives. Chromium and mercury can be used as polymer catalysts, copper as a biocide, and cobalt, lead, and manganese as plastic dyes. , Scientific studies should not only focus on the producer’s chemical formulation since plastics can sorb additional chemicals during their use or transport, described as the “Trojan horse effect”, particularly with regard to metals. ,

Holmes et al. reported that metal concentrations in environmental microplastics were comparable to or exceeded those found in sediments from the same site. They also found higher metal adsorption capacities for aged microplastics than for pristine ones. Another study showed that metal concentrations in microplastics increased over time, suggesting that the longer plastic debris remains in the environment, the more metals it may contain. While ingested, the contaminated plastics can transfer their sorbed pollutants as metals to organisms, leading to bioaccumulation. ,

Microplastics are thus pollutant carriers, and their characteristics must be known given the potential toxicity of the sorbed metals for living organisms. Furthermore, their interactions with biofilms can significantly influence their mobility, degradation, and contaminant-loading capacity. Recent studies have shown that biofilms forming on microplastic surfaces in freshwater and marine environments can modify their physicochemical properties, increase their adsorption potential for heavy metals and organic pollutants, and impact their transport in aquatic systems. This is particularly relevant in the context of microplastic-metal interactions, as biofilms can act as a vector for facilitating or inhibiting metal sorption and desorption, thereby affecting the overall fate and toxicity of microplastic-associated contaminants.

Analytical methods to study metals in plastics include bulk content analysis techniques such as inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP–MS), optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES), and atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS). These techniques require sample treatment via mineralization or digestion in acidic solutions and the use of commercial ionic metal standards. Solid sampling analysis techniques like laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS), X-ray fluorescence analysis (XRF), and laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) can also be used. , LA-ICP-MS differentiates the metals present as additives from those sorbed from the environment. However, despite its potential, the quantitative application of LA-ICP-MS remains limited by the very limited availability of reference materials specifically adapted to plastic matrices, particularly in solid form and suitable for direct laser ablation analysis (see Table S1 for an overview of available certified reference materials). Developing reliable, reproducible plastic reference materials is therefore essential to unlock the full analytical potential of LA-ICP-MS.

In the field of reference materials, several plastics are employed to calibrate and assess the presence of metallic contaminants in manufactured materials, in compliance with international guidelines and regulations. An example of such a regulation is the directive [2002/95/EC] on the restriction of the use of certain hazardous substances (RoHS), enacted by the European Union, which governs the use of specific hazardous substances in electrical and electronic equipment. These materials are certified by institutions such as the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), the Joint Research Centre (JRC), and the National Metrology Institute of Japan (NMIJ), and are available in various forms, such as polymer matrices with different concentration ranges of metals (see Table S1 in Supporting Information). Polyethylene (PE), typically in pellet form, encompasses a broad spectrum of chemical elements including aluminum (Al), arsenic (As), bromine (Br), cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), iron (Fe), mercury (Hg), lead (Pb), antimony (Sb), tin (Sn), zinc (Zn), and titanium (Ti), with concentrations ranging from 2 to 1000 mg kg–1 depending on the metals and the standards involved. Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) is also available in pellet form and is similar to polyethylene in terms of chemical elements including barium (Ba) and selenium (Se) with similar concentration levels. Metals such as cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), mercury (Hg), and lead (Pb) are incorporated into acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) matrices, available in pellets or discs. Polypropylene (PP) and polycarbonate (PC) are used in pellet form for concentrations below 1000 mg kg–1. These materials are also present in polyester, which are used in chip form with a unique concentration of 10 mg kg–1, lower than other polymers, and whose chemical composition is not known. The production process for these reference materials begins with the preparation of a master batch consisting of virgin polymer powder meticulously mixed with various organometallic compounds or multielement solutions before to be dried, and stored to ensure a homogeneous and stable batch. Subsequently, the latter is blended with a commercial polymer, which may include plasticizers and stabilizers. This composition is heated and mixed before being extruded to form pellets at a given concentration. The latter are then re-extruded to enhance the homogeneity of the final product, thereby ensuring that the final material meets the high standards required for reference applications as described in the literature. Sample characterization confirmed high homogeneity at both macroscopic and microscopic levels for all analytes. Previous research has demonstrated that the chemical nature of the metal specie plays a crucial role in achieving uniform distribution within polymer matrices. For example, studies have found that incorporating metals as organic salts resulted in superior homogeneity compared to metal oxides. , The findings indicated that organic salts are more uniformly distributed within the polymer matrix, leading to better overall material consistency. However, the use of organic salts limits the choice of metallic salts available, restricting the number of possible elements that can be used. Despite these advances, current polymer reference materials are certified for bulk elemental analysis (e.g., after acid digestion), but do not provide certified values or guarantees regarding surface homogeneity for direct solid sampling methods such as LA-ICP-MS.

There is therefore a critical analytical need for new reference materials specifically tailored to solid-state, spatially resolved techniques. Such materials must combine fine-scale homogeneity, stable and reproducible metal incorporation, and compatibility with direct ablation and quantitative calibration by LA-ICP-MS. Developing such standards is crucial to improve the precision, comparability, and reliability of trace metal analysis in polymer-based materials by LA-ICP-MS, and to support the broader implementation of this powerful technique in analytical laboratories.

In this context, our research aims to develop a novel method for synthesizing polymer-based reference materials specifically tailored for solid-state trace metal analysis by LA-ICP-MS. The strategy involves the sorption of metallic salts onto carboxylated polystyrene (PS) nanoplastics dispersed in aqueous media, followed by consolidation into homogeneous plastic discs through hot-press molding.

In this article, we build upon our previous works , to describe a strategy that exploits the ability of nanoplastics to absorb metals at their core by simply mixing aqueous dispersions of nanoplastics, obtained through a bottom-up process, with metallic salts. After drying, nanoplastics are processed into plastic discs via hot-press molding. Although, PS belongs to the big 6 i.e. the most commonly used and environmentally problematic plastics (PET, PE, PP, PS, and PVC); to the best of our knowledge, standard PS materials have yet to be developed. In order to fill this gap, PS was chosen as polymer matrix in this study.

The success of our strategy relies on the metal sorbing properties of the nanoplastics. Several polystyrene plastic standards have been obtained, doped with cobalt (Co2+), copper (Cu2+), and lead (Pb2+). The homogeneity of the metal distribution at the surface and inside the plastic standards was verified by LA-ICP-MS. Finally, the total metals concentrations were determined by ICP–MS and were used to calibrate the signal in LA-ICP-MS analysis. Therefore, LA-ICP MS calibration curves were plotted for the three metals and allowed metals quantification within a plastic. In this study, we selected Co2+, Cu2+, and Pb2+ as representative elements because they cover different chemical behaviors and environmental concerns: Pb2+ for its high environmental toxicity, Cu2+ for its essential role but potential toxicity at high concentrations, and Co2+ as an emerging contaminant linked to battery technologies.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Styrene (≥99%, CAS 100–42–5), acrylic acid (99%, CAS 79–10–7), and ammonium persulfate (98%, CAS 7727–54–0) were obtained from Aldrich. The aqueous solutions of metals were prepared using the corresponding solid salts: Cu­(NO3)2·6H2O (99.999%, Aldrich, CAS 13778–31–9), Pb­(NO3)2·6H2O (99.999%, Alfa, CAS 10099–74–8), and CoCl2·6H2O (≥98%, VWR, CAS 7646–79–9). Milli-Q water, with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm at 25 °C, was produced using a Millipore water purification system (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany).

Elaboration of Metal-Doped Plastic Standards

A protocol previously described by our group was followed to synthesize an aqueous dispersion of polystyrene (PS) nanoparticles with a surface functionalized by carboxylic groups mimicking aged plastic debris. Those PS particles, considered as nanoplastics model materials, were synthesized using a ratio of [styrene]/[acrylic acid + styrene] = 18 mol %. The resulting spherical particles had a diameter of d H = 320 nm, with a dispersity of 0.14 and a solid content of 6.7 wt %. The surface of these particles was functionalized with 45 carboxylic groups per nm. For the metal labeling, three separate aqueous solutions of metal salts were prepared: [Co2+] = 135 g L–1, [Cu2+] = 159 g L–1, and [Pb2+] = 203 g L–1. These solutions were then successively added in controlled volume ratios to individual vials containing 25 mL of nanoplastic dispersion, resulting in multielement metal-doped samples. The final volume of each vial was kept constant, and each contained a defined mixture of Co2+, Cu2+, and Pb2+ in varying total metal concentrations. This protocol ensured the preparation of multielement plastic standards, enabling the simultaneous incorporation and analysis of several trace metals. The vials remained on an oscillating Table (520 rpm, KS 130 basic, IKA) for 5 days. Subsequently, purification was achieved through three centrifugation cycles at 12,000 rpm for 30 min each. After each centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was redispersed in Milli-Q water through cycles of orbital shaking (10 min, 40 Hz, TopMix FB 15024, Fisher Scientific) and sonication (10 min, USC500TH, VWR). After the third centrifugation cycle, the purified sample was freeze-dried for 48 h (Crios-80, Cryotec), yielding dried powders of metal-doped nanoplastic model materials. Plastic standards were typically obtained as the following: 280 mg of dried powder of metal-doped nanoplastic model materials was introduced into a Teflon mold (diameter 17 mm, thickness 1.2 mm) before being placed on a heating press (MIE) at 180 °C for 10 min under a pressure of 30 bar. This protocol allowed the elaboration of plastic standards at different concentrations of each metal (n = 3).

Metal Analysis

The laser ablation system used for this study was an ESI NWR-213 equipped with a Nd:YAG laser source at 213 nm wavelength (ESI, Freemont, CA, USA) and a 2-volume (TV2) ablation cell. The ICP–MS detector was an Agilent 7700x (Agilent Technologies, Tokyo, Japan) mounted with Ni cones. Prior to coupling with the laser, the ICP–MS was tuned in liquid introduction mode using a 1 μg L–1 solution of Y, Li, Tl, Ce, and Ba in 2% HNO3 for mass calibration. The laser aerosol was transported to the ICP–MS using a He flow at 800 mL min–1. LA-ICP-MS conditions were optimized using NIST 612 glass material to maximize element signal intensity and achieve acceptable signal stability (RSD <10%). Carbon (13C) was used as an internal standard to correct for sample heterogeneity since all samples uniformly contained carbon. The laser parameters included a spot size of 30 μm, a fluency of 16 J cm–2 (65% energy delivery), a sample scan rate of 80 μm s–1, and a pulse frequency of 20 Hz. The analysis involved ablation along a line of 2 or 4 mm in length, with 5 or 15 laser scans per line. An offset from the sample surface was applied between each scan to ensure consistent ablation depth. 13C, 206Pb and 208Pb were monitored at 0.01 s as integration time, while 59Co, 63Cu and 65Cu at 0.1 s. A mixture of He (2 mL min–1) and H2 (3.5 mL min–1) was used in the collision cell of the mass spectrometer to remove possible interferences on Cu. Homogeneity curves were generated for the isotopes of interest using at least 15 laser scans for in-depth homogeneity and at least 5 laser scans for surface homogeneity, performed in four different locations. Each point on the calibration curves represents the average analytical signal recorded during the laser scans, normalized to the 13C signal. Ablated depth determination was conducted using an optical microscope (DM LM/P, Leica). For that purpose, plastic standards were sectioned perpendicularly to the ablation line with a razor blade (GEM) to observe their cross-section. Ablated depth measurements were performed three times using ImageJ software. For determining metal concentrations via ICP–MS analysis, the plastic standards (300 mg) were digested in triplicate using 5 mL of 70% HNO3 in a single reaction chamber microwave digestion system (Milestone SCI). The digestion followed a multistep temperature and pressure program: 10 min at 110 °C and 90 bar, followed by 10 min at 180 °C and 90 bar, then 10 min at 230 °C and 120 bar, and a final hold of 5 min at 230 °C and 120 bar, all steps at 1500 W. ICP–MS analyses were performed with an Agilent 7900 (Agilent Technologies) equipped with a collision cell. H2 gas was used at a flow rate of 5 mL min–1 to eliminate known interferences on Cu. The monitored isotopes included 59Co, 63Cu (H2), 65Cu (H2), 206Pb, and 208Pb. For LA-ICP-MS, reproducibility was assessed by analyzing between 4 and 15 ablation spots per sample. The relative standard deviation (RSD) for the calibration curves was calculated from the LA-ICP-MS signal normalized to carbon, based on five ablations in four different zones of a single disk sample. For total metal analysis by ICP–MS, each sample was digested in triplicate, followed by triplicate measurements of each digested solution.

Characterization of Nanoplastics Model Materials

Hydrodynamic diameters (d H) were determined using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) probe (VascoFlex, Cordouan Technologies). Each DLS analysis consisted of five averaged measurements, each lasting 60 s The average hydrodynamic diameter was determined by fitting a normal distribution to the raw data using the cumulants algorithm.

The surface functionality of carboxylic groups per nm2 at the nanoplastic surface was determined by titration with a Titrando 888 equipped with stirrer 728 and a combined pH glass electrode (pH: 0–14; temperature: 0–80 °C; [KCl] = 3 mol L–1). The pH of the dispersions was first increased to above 11 using a NaOH solution (0.1 mol L–1) before titration with an HCl solution (5 mmol L–1).

Metal Sorption

Adsorption experiments were carried out separately for each metal ion (Co2+, Cu2+, or Pb2+), using monoelement aqueous solutions adjusted to pH 5, as previously established in our earlier work. This pH was selected as it provides an optimal compromise between the deprotonation of carboxyl groups (favoring metal binding), the solubility of metal ions (preventing hydrolysis or precipitation), and the colloidal stability of the nanoplastic dispersion. The metal adsorption constants for nanoplastics model materials were calculated by fitting the experimental results with the Freundlich model. The Freundlich isotherm is represented by the following equation 1 Qe=Kads×Ceqn where Q e is the adsorbed metal concentration (mg g–1), K ads is the Freundlich adsorption constant at equilibrium, C eq is the concentration of metal at equilibrium (mg g–1), and n is the nonlinearity coefficient.

Results and Discussion

Trace Elements Enrichment of Carboxylated Polystyrene Particles

A previous study showed that metals in cationic forms interact with the negatively charged surface of nanoplastics. , To enable the adsorption of metals by the polystyrene nanoparticles, carboxylated nanoparticles containing a high amount of carboxylic groups at the surface were synthesized following the work of Pessoni et al. Carboxylated polystyrene nanoparticles containing 45 carboxylic groups per nm2 at the surface and having a size of 340 nm were obtained.

After purification by centrifugation/dispersion steps, the nanoplastics model materials were exposed to aqueous solutions of metals (Co2+, Cu2+, Pb2+) for 5 days. The nanoplastics were stable in the aqueous solution of metallic salts over the exposure period that was chosen to favor the surface adsorption of as much metal as possible. Indeed, a previous study that reported the sorption mechanisms of copper salt onto the same kind of nanoplastics model materials showed no significant variation of sorbed metal concentrations after 24 h of exposure. After 5 days of exposure, all batches were purified by centrifugation cycles and the samples were analyzed by ICP–MS to quantify the metal enrichment rate of the nanoplastics model materials.

Preparation and Analysis of Metal-Doped Plastics Using LA-ICP-MS

LA-ICP-MS Analysis Methodology

LA-ICP-MS analysis consisted of the ablation of a line with several laser scans at different depths to assess the metal distribution. During one laser scan, the signal can fluctuate due to the inhomogeneity of the ablated mass. This was overcome by normalizing the signal by 13C, i.e. the metal signal at t time is divided by 13C signal at the same time, as suggested in literature. One example of raw signals obtained on a plastic disc is reported in Figure . For data processing, the signal mean and standard deviation over one ablation line are calculated.

Example of raw signals from 13C (blue), 208Pb (red) and 208Pb/13C (green) obtained during LA-ICP-MS analysis on a plastic pellet. Signal of 13C was magnified by a factor of 10. The fluctuations in intensity correspond to different ablation spots on the sample.

To ascertain the in-depth homogeneity of our samples, it is imperative to determine the depth ablated during each ablation under our experimental conditions. After ablation, the plastic discs were cut perpendicular to the ablation line, and the section was imaged by microscopy. Images showing three plastic discs sorbed with Cu2+ and ablated 6, 12, and 18 times at the same location are provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S1). A mean real ablation depth of 4.3 μm per ablation was estimated by fitting the ablation depth measured by microscopy to the number of ablations. This measured ablation depth has been taken into account for the following analyses.

Figure A shows a constant 63Cu signal regardless of the number of ablations, i.e., at all depths. The results confirm the homogeneous dispersion of the metal in depth the plastic, with a relative standard deviation (RSD) of 4.9%.

To evaluate the surface homogeneity of the metal dispersion, four random locations were chosen on the same plastic disc and ablated by five laser scans. The results reveal a constant concentration of the metal at all sample locations, confirming the homogeneous dispersion of the metal across the surface of the plastic, with RSD values ranging from 1.3% to 4.4% and an overall RSD of 5% across the four locations. Similar results were obtained on three independent discs, and for other metals including cobalt (RSD range: 1.7–5.1%; overall RSD: 5.6%) and lead (RSD range: 2.1–4.8%; overall RSD: 5.2%).

In addition to demonstrating spatial homogeneity, the stability and reproducibility of the LA-ICP-MS signal also provide an indirect evaluation of the firmness of metal incorporation onto the nanoplastics. The observed low RSDs (<5%) for both surface and depth profiles indicate good metal attachment without significant desorption during analysis. In comparison to traditional CRMs, where metals are incorporated within the bulk polymer matrix via extrusion processes and often covalently or coordinatively bound, the present standards rely on physical adsorption onto the surface of the nanoplastics. This mechanism may lead to slightly lower resistance to aggressive chemical or aging conditions. Nevertheless, given that our standards are intended for direct solid-state analysis by LA-ICP-MS without any chemical digestion or harsh treatment, this difference does not compromise their analytical performance. In addition, the nanoplastics-based approach offers significant advantages, including finer microscale homogeneity, preservation of surface properties, and simplified fabrication processes compatible with solid-state calibration needs.

Calibration and Analysis of Pb2+, Cu2+, and Co2+ in Plastic Standards Using LA-ICP-MS

The calibration of LA-ICP-MS for the analysis of Pb2+, Cu2+, and Co2+ in plastic standards could provide significant insights into the distribution and concentration of these metals in environmental samples as demonstrated in our recent work.

The calibration curve for Pb2+ (Figure ) demonstrated a strong linear relationship across a concentration range from 1215 to 4402 mg kg–1, which extends beyond that of currently available Certified Reference Materials (CRMs) for the determination of metal concentrations in various polymers (see Table S1). The RSD values for Pb2+ ranged from 4.8% at the lowest concentration (1220 mg kg–1) to 4.4% at the highest concentration (4400 mg kg–1) (Table ), indicating excellent reproducibility. Plastic standards capable of measuring Pb2+ concentrations up to 4400 mg kg–1 are crucial for analyzing heavily polluted areas. This concentration range extends the applicability of the developed standards to real-world microplastic samples with elevated Pb levels, such as those reported by El Hadri et al. in marine debris and by Hahladakis et al. in plastic materials containing legacy Pb-based additives. The concentration range for Cu2+ spanned from 210 to 600 mg kg–1, with calibration curves (Figure ) showing a consistent linear response. Similarly to the results obtained for Pb2+, the RSD values remained low, with the highest at 5.1% for a Cu2+ concentration of 250 mg kg–1 and the lowest at 1.5% for 490 mg kg–1. Cobalt calibration exhibited comparable reliability, though with a narrower concentration range (67 to 220 mg kg–1). The method’s accuracy is underscored by the similarity in LA-ICP-MS signals for standards with closely matched cobalt concentrations (e.g., in Table : 83 and 86, 95, and 97 mg kg–1). The limit of quantification (LOQ) was determined from the calibration curves based on the standard deviation of the blank and the sensitivity of the method. Specifically, the LOQ was defined as ten times the standard deviation of multiple blank measurements, divided by the sensitivity of the calibration curve (i.e., its slope). The LOQs were set at 113 mg kg–1 for Pb2+, 93 mg kg–1 for Cu2+, and 23 mg kg–1 for Co2+. While no direct quantification was performed at concentrations near these LOQs, the strong linearity of the calibration curve supports the reliability of the method for quantification above this limit.

To further validate the accuracy of the LA-ICP-MS calibration approach, a leave-one-out cross-validation was performed. One standard (97 ± 4 mg·kg–1 Co, 490 ± 22 mg·kg–1 Cu, 2840 ± 190 mg·kg–1 Pb) was excluded from the calibration data set and treated as an unknown. Its metal concentrations were then predicted using the calibration curves built from the remaining standards. The comparison with the reference values obtained by acid digestion and solution ICP–MS showed good agreement, with relative deviations of −3.4% (Co), −5.1% (Cu), and +6.2% (Pb). These results support the quantitative potential and accuracy of the proposed standards for solid-state LA-ICP-MS analysis.

In this study, the metal content of the standards was primarily adjusted by varying the volume of metal salt solution introduced into the nanoplastic dispersion, under constant experimental conditions. While this method offers straightforward control over metal loading, other parameters may also influence adsorption efficiency and allow for broader calibration ranges. However, pH should be kept constant: values below pH 3 may compromise the colloidal stability of the nanoplastics, while values above pH 6 can promote metal hydrolysis or precipitation (especially for Pb2+), thereby reducing reproducibility.

A more robust strategy to predict and control metal loading involves first establishing adsorption isotherms, which provide a quantitative relationship between the concentration of dissolved metal and the amount adsorbed onto the particle surface. This approach can guide the preparation of standards with specific, target concentrations. Additionally, mixing metal-doped nanoplastics with nondoped particles offers a simple and reproducible way to prepare intermediate calibration levels without altering the adsorption protocol. This flexibility makes the method adaptable to different analytical requirements, including trace-level quantification or the simulation of highly contaminated materials.

While the developed multielemental loading strategy proved effective for producing functional calibration standards, it presents several limitations that must be considered for broader applicability. First, the coadsorption of multiple metal ions onto carboxylate-functionalized nanoplastics can lead to competition for binding sites. Differences in metal affinity may cause preferential sorption and limit the maximum achievable concentration for certain metals. Although reproducibility across replicates remained satisfactory, such interactions may restrict precise control over relative metal ratios in some cases.

The current methodology relies on carboxylated polystyrene nanoplastics synthesized via emulsion polymerization. This technique is difficult to apply to other widely used polymers such as polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP), primarily due to their poor compatibility with emulsion-based processes. These polymers are highly hydrophobic and challenging to disperse or functionalize in aqueous media under mild conditions. As recently shown by El Basset et al., the preparation of PE nanoplastics typically requires surfactant-free polymerization under high pressure, limiting the accessibility and versatility of the method in laboratories that are not specifically equipped for such synthetic procedures.

To extend the approach to other environmentally relevant or biosourced polymers, such as polylactic acid (PLA) or poly­(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) (PBAT), nanoprecipitation offers a promising alternative. This technique allows for better control over nanoparticle formation and surface chemistry, potentially enabling the preparation of doped standards from a wider range of polymer matrices.

Despite these constraints, the strategy presented here lays the groundwork for developing LA-ICP-MS calibration standards tailored to microplastic analysis, and opens perspectives for adapting the method to other plastics and contaminants of concern.

Environmental Impact and Significance of LA-ICP-MS

Environmental Implications

All three elements showed excellent reproducibility and reliability of the LA-ICP-MS analysis, suggests that these plastic standards can be effectively used for environmental monitoring and potentially for regulatory compliance testing. The calibration ranges established also align well with the globally reported concentrations in microplastics, highlighting their practical relevance in current environmental research scenarios. High calibration limits for lead are particularly relevant, given its significant toxicity and prevalence in industrial pollutants. These standards help in accurately assessing the risk and extent of lead contamination in aquatic and terrestrial environments. Given copper’s role in biological systems and its potential for toxicity at high concentrations, the dynamics of copper in microplastic pollution can now be studied more thoroughly. This is crucial for ecosystems where copper may accumulate, affecting biodiversity and water quality. , Although cobalt is typically found at lower concentrations in environmental samples than lead or copper, the precise measurement of cobalt is essential due to its emerging relevance in technological and industrial applications such as in lithium-ion batteries, which might increase its environmental footprint.

Importance of LA-ICP-MS in Environmental Studies

The combination of laser ablation with inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry has emerged as a powerful analytical technique for the quantitative analysis of metals in various matrices, including microplastics and nanoplastics. This method leverages the fine spatial resolution of laser ablation and the high sensitivity of ICP–MS.

Recent studies have demonstrated its effectiveness in environmental and biological contexts. El Hadri et al. employed LA-ICP-MS to map trace elements such as cadmium, lead, and copper in marine microplastics, revealing concerning levels of contamination. Similarly, Van Acker et al. showed that LA-ICP-MS enables nondestructive sampling of microplastics, providing precise results on particle size and composition. In plant biology, Wang et al. employed cryogenic LA-ICP-MS to track and image polystyrene nanoplastics in fresh cucumbers, finding significant accumulation in roots and associated decreases of trace elements abundance like zinc and copper. Pořízka et al. used LA-ICP-MS to identify and characterize aged microplastics with biofilm, highlighting detailed effects on particle surfaces. Brunnbauer et al. combined Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy (LIBS) with LA-ICP-MS for enhanced imaging and quantification of metals in microplastics, achieving very low detection limits for elements such as lead and tin (0.72 mg kg–1 for Pb and 9.5 mg kg–1for Sn).

These studies underscore the crucial role of LA-ICP-MS in providing high-resolution, quantitative insights into chemical elements distribution in environmental and biological samples. For similar studies, access to plastics standard is important to ensure the accuracy and comparability of the results.

Conclusion

This work presents a significant advancement in the development of analytical standards for the quantification of trace metals in polymer-based materials by LA-ICP-MS. By developing carboxylated polystyrene nanoparticles that can adsorb metallic salts from aqueous solutions and transform into homogeneously doped plastic discs, we have pioneered a novel approach to creating reference materials for metal analysis in microplastics using LA-ICP-MS.

The carboxylated polystyrene nanoparticles demonstrated exceptional adsorption capacities for trace metals such as cobalt, copper, and lead. This capability was thoroughly analyzed through adsorption isotherms that fit the Freundlich model, highlighting the effective and favorable interaction between the metals and the nanoplastic surfaces. After processing, plastic discs have been obtained not only allowing an accurate calibration of LA-ICP-MS but also providing a new standard based on homogeneously doped plastics.

These standards enable accurate calibration and reproducible quantification of trace metals within polymers without the need for destructive sample preparation, directly addressing a critical analytical gap. By providing a robust tool for LA-ICP-MS calibration, this work enhances the precision, comparability, and reliability of trace metal analysis in polymer matrices.

Beyond their immediate analytical applications, these standards establish a methodological foundation for future developments, including the extension to other types of polymers and a broader range of trace elements relevant to polymer contamination studies. Ultimately, this work strengthens the analytical capabilities necessary to support trace element analysis in solid polymer materials. Future extensions of this work will aim at incorporating a broader range of environmentally relevant elements, leveraging the versatility of the proposed method.
