Pseudomonas protegens as a biocontrol agent against phytopathogenic fungi - mini review
Authors: Jakub Dobrzyński, Zuzanna Jakubowska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPseudomonas protegens, a member of the P. fluorescens complex, is an important biocontrol agent with high potential in sustainable agriculture. It produces diverse secondary metabolites, including 2,4-diacetylphloroglucinol (DAPG), pyoluteorin (PLT), orfamides, and protegenins, as well as hydrolytic enzymes such as chitinases, which contribute to antifungal activity, fungal cell wall degradation, and induction of systemic resistance (ISR) in plants. Despite these insights, gaps remain in understanding the regulation of metabolite biosynthesis, variability of ISR across crops, and the field efficacy of P. protegens. Future research should employ omics approaches (metagenomics, transcriptomics) to optimize biocontrol strategies, explore natural inducers of metabolite production, and evaluate colonization efficiency under field conditions. This review synthesizes current knowledge on P. protegens, highlighting its importance, mechanisms of action, existing knowledge gaps, and directions for future research.Keywords: Biological control, Antagonistic bacteria, Plant–microbe interactions, Secondary metabolites, Rhizosphere colonization, Sustainable agriculture

Introduction

Pseudomonas is a genus within the class Gammaproteobacteria (Williams et al. 2010; Naziębło and Dobrzyński 2025). The bacterial genus Pseudomonas encompasses a diverse group of species found in various ecological niches, including soils, water environments, and plant and animal tissues (Hansen et al. 2022; Dobrzyński et al. 2024a, b; Camarillo et al. 2024; Naziębło and Dobrzyński 2025). A large number of Pseudomonas strains have been characterized as plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) (Raio and Puopolo 2021; Costa-Gutierrez et al. 2022; Dobrzyński et al. 2025). PGPB enhance plant health and productivity by improving nutrient availability and uptake through mechanisms such as nitrogen fixations, phosphate solubilization, or by synthesizing bioactive compounds like indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) that influence plant development (Ajijah et al. 2023; Dobrzyński and Kulkova 2025; Jakubowska et al. 2025). Additionally, PGPB can suppress phytopathogens and mitigate associated losses in plant productivity through mechanisms such as production of antimicrobial compounds, release of hydrolytic enzymes (including chitinases, glucaneses or cellulases), synthesis of HCN and ammonia, and elicitation of induced systemic resistance (ISR) (Morales-Cedeño et al. 2023; Serrão et al. 2024; Guardado-Fierros et al. 2025). In this review, we focus primarily on chitinases, orfamides, DAPG, and pyoluteorin, as they are among the most commonly detected secondary metabolites produced by Pseudomonas genus and play key roles in its biocontrol activity.

Among the most intriguing strains within the Pseudomonas genus is Pseudomonas protegens. P. protegens is part of the P. fluorescens complex, which includes over 50 recognized species (Mulet et al. 2010). Through multilocus sequence analysis and multiple whole-genome sequence-based studies, the P. fluorescens complex has been divided into nine or ten phylogenomic subgroups, with P. protegens and P. chlororaphis distinctly separated into two different groups (Garrido-Sanz et al., 2016). This separation is relevant because it underscores the unique genetic and functional characteristics of P. protegens, supporting our focus on this species. Recognizing its distinct phylogenetic position helps to contextualize its metabolite profile, biocontrol mechanisms, and potential applications in sustainable agriculture, differentiating it from closely related species such as P. chlororaphis.

While several previous reviews have addressed the direct plant growth promotion and biocontrol potential of Pseudomonas spp. or the P. fluorescens complex more broadly, they have not specifically focused on the fungistatic activity of P. protegens (Shahid et al. 2018; Alattas et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2025). Unlike these broader reviews, our mini-review systematically compiles and analyzes studies on the mechanisms, target fungal pathogens, and environmental factors influencing the fungistatic effects of P. protegens. By focusing on this species and its direct antifungal interactions, this mini-review provides a targeted synthesis that has not been previously available, summarizing the current knowledge on P. protegens, highlighting existing gaps, and proposing new directions for future research.

Production of chitinases

Chitinases belong to the hydrolase family (EC 3.2.1.14) and are classified as glycosidases. Depending on their structure and mechanism of action, they are divided into different families of glycosidases (GH, glycoside hydrolases) in the CAZy database (Carbohydrate-Active enZYmes database), mainly: GH18, GH19, GH23, GH48, GH144 (Bhattacharya et al. 2007; Kumar et al. 2022).

Chitinases are among the most important enzymes used in the biocontrol of fungal pathogens (Fig. 1). These enzymes break down chitin, the main component of fungal cell walls, leading to the inhibition of pathogen growth and development (Kumar et al. 2022). Bacteria of the genus Pseudomonas, including Pseudomonas protegens, are known for producing chitinases as well as other hydrolytic enzymes, such as proteases, cellulases, and beta-1,3-glucanases, which synergistically contribute to effective biocontrol. P. protegens not only directly degrades fungal structures, but also induces plant defense responses, further enhancing protection against infections (Takeuchi et al. 2023; Suganthi et al. 2024). Genes encoding chitinases and other hydrolytic enzymes have been detected in P. protegens in several works based on whole genome sequencing (Jousset et al. 2014; Camarillo et al. 2024).

Key biocontrol actions of P. protegens against fungal phytopathogens - include the production of chitinases and other hydrolytic enzymes, the synthesis of antibiotic substances (e.g. lipopeptides and polyketides), and the induction of ISR in plants

This section briefly discusses examples of studies in which chitinases produced by P. protegens have been used to suppress various fungal pathogens. For example, Ajijah et al. (2023) identified the chiC gene responsible for chitinase biosynthesis in P. protegens ML15. Previously, chitinolytically active P. protegens S4LiBe and S5LiBe were capable of inhibiting the mycelial growth of fungi such as Verticillium dahliae, Fusarium graminearum, Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus niger, and Botrytis cinerea (Bensidhoum et al. 2016). Additionally, in P. protegens E1BL2, the in vitro production of extracellular enzymes such as chitinases, proteases, and cellulases was detected (Camarillo et al. 2024).

There are also studies showing the possibility of increasing chitinase expression in strains intended for the biocontrol of fungi. Takeuchi et al. (2023), conducting an RNA-Seq analysis, observed that glutamate upregulated the expression of a chitinase gene cluster in P. protegens strain CHA0.

Although the role of chitinases of Pseudomonas in biocontrol is well documented, studies on the regulation of their expression in P. protegens are still limited. However, gene overexpression can be achieved not only through genetic engineering but also by using natural inducers - such as the previously mentioned glutamate (Takeuchi et al. 2023) - offering hope for more effective utilization of these enzymes in plant protection.

Role of antibiotic substances

Bacteria of the Pseudomonas genus, including P. protegens, produce a range of antibiotic substances capable of inhibiting the growth of fungal phytopathogens. These abilities may make them key agents in the biological control of phytopathogens (Lee et al. 2023; Ramette et al. 2011) (Fig. 1). Among the secondary metabolites synthesized by these bacteria, lipopeptides especially orfamides (Henkels et al. 2014; Ma et al. 2016; Alattas et al. 2024) as well as compounds such as 2,4-diacetylphloroglucinol (DAPG) (Patel and Archana 2018; Jing et al. 2020; Andreolli et al. 2019; Ajijah et al. 2023; Rai et al. 2017; Balthazar et al. 2022), pyoluteorin (PLT) (Ramette et al. 2011; Balthazar et al. 2022), pyrrolnitrin (PRN) (Ajijah et al. 2023; Cesa-Luna et al., 2020), and protegenins (Murata et al. 2021) are particularly noteworthy (Table 1.).

DAPG

DAPG is synthesized through the condensation of malonyl-CoA mediated by type III synthase (PhlD), followed by acetylation via the phlACB genes (Patel and Archana 2018). The antifungal activity of DAPG is associated with its ability to disrupt the integrity of fungal cell membranes, which in turn leads to the uncoupling of respiration from ATP synthesis and the subsequent collapse of cellular energy balance (Kwak et al. 2011; Stepanov et al. 2022). Moreover, DAPG can also act indirectly by triggering induced systemic resistance (ISR) in plants, enhancing their defense responses against a broad range of pathogens (Iavicoli et al. 2003). Its production has been detected in multiple strains of P. protegens, including Pf-5, MP12, ML15, RS-9, EMM-1, FD6, and WS5 (Andreolli et al. 2019; Cesa-Luna et al., 2020; Ajijah et al. 2023; Rai et al. 2017; Balthazar et al. 2022; Patel and Archana 2018). This compound exhibits activity against Rhizoctonia solani, Magnaporthe oryzae, Fusarium cerealis, Botrytis cinerea, Monilinia fructicola, and Ralstonia solanacearum (Jing et al. 2020; Ajijah et al. 2024; Balthazar et al. 2022; Rai et al. 2017).

Pyoluteorin

PLT is a chlorinated phenylpyrrole synthesized by strains such as Pf-5, RS-9, and ML15 (Balthazar et al. 2022; Ajijah et al. 2023). Although the specific molecular targets of PLT have not yet been fully characterized for all fungal pathogens, it is believed to inhibit growth by interfering with essential cellular functions and potentially damaging cellular structures such as membranes and cell walls (Pellicciaro et al. 2022; Balthazar et al. 2022).It demonstrates activity against numerous pathogens and acts synergistically with DAPG (Balthazar et al. 2022). Interestingly, its production can increase up to 100-fold in the presence of Pseudomonas capeferrum (Hansen et al. 2022).

Orfamides

Orfamides A, B, F, and G are cyclic lipopeptides synthesized by non-ribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPS), with their production regulated by the GacS/GacA pathway (Flury et al. 2017; Alattas et al. 2024). Orfamides act by disrupting the integrity of microbial cell membranes, leading to increased permeability, leakage of cellular contents, and ultimately inhibition of pathogens (Aiyar et al. 2017; Ma et al. 2016; Yang et al. 2025). Moreover, similar to DAPG, orfamides can also act indirectly by triggering ISR in plants (Ma et al. 2016). Among Pseudomonas species, above-mentioned orfamides are the best described (Ma et al. 2016). They have been shown to exhibit strong activity against several fungi including Rhizoctonia solani, Magnaporthe oryzae, Phytophthora, and Pythium (Flury et al. 2017; Ma et al. 2016).

Moreover P. protegens strains exhibit the ability to simultaneously produce multiple metabolites (Table 1). Strain ML15 synthesizes DAPG, PLT, PRN, orfamides, lipases, biosurfactants, and volatile organic compounds (VOC) (Ajijah et al. 2023). EMM-1 produces DAPG, PLT, and LlpA, inhibiting the growth of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria and fungi (Cesa-Luna et al., 2020). MP12 shows activity against grapevine pathogens (Andreolli et al. 2019), while RS-9 suppresses R. solanacearum in tomatoes (Rai et al. 2017).

Furthermore, genetic engineering and modulation of ecological interactions can boost antibiotic production. Deletion of the retS gene in Pf-5 increased DAPG synthesis (Jing et al. 2020), while introducing the DAPG biosynthetic pathway into WS5 resulted in a 6–7-fold production increase (Patel and Archana 2018). Sequential interactions with P. capeferrum induced elevated PLT levels while suppressing DAPG (Hansen et al. 2022).

Importantly, the effectiveness of P. protegens has been demonstrated not only in vitro but also in crops such as tomatoes, wheat, grapes, hemp, and cucumbers (Balthazar et al. 2022; Ajijah et al. 2024; Andreolli et al. 2019; Murata et al. 2021; Pellicciaro et al. 2021).

Elicitation of induced systemic resistance

ISR is a form of plant immunity against pathogens that can be triggered by interactions with microorganisms (Fig. 1). This type of plant immunity is regulated by the jasmonic acid (JA) and ethylene (ET) signaling pathways, in contrast to systemic acquired resistance (SAR), which primarily depends on salicylic acid (SA). Unlike SAR, which is typically activated after pathogen attack and involves widespread expression of defense genes, ISR prepares the plant in advance by priming it for faster and stronger responses upon subsequent pathogen exposure. Furthermore, ISR is mainly induced by beneficial microbes in the rhizosphere, highlighting its role in the interplay between plants and their associated microbiome (Choudhary et al. 2007; Kamle et al. 2020; Maithani et al. 2020).This mechanism enhances the plant’s ability to defend against a wide range of pathogens, including bacteria and fungi, by activating resistance-related genes and increasing the production of defense enzymes such as peroxidases (POD), polyphenol oxidases (PPO), and enzymes involved in phenolic metabolism (Huang et al. 2022; Dobrzyński and Nazieblo, 2024c).One of the key factors triggering induced systemic resistance is the production of secondary metabolites by microorganisms, including antibiotic compounds. These substances can act not only directly against pathogens, as mentioned above, but also stimulate plant immunity through ISR-related mechanisms (Balthazar et al. 2022).

The Pseudomonas genus has been extensively studied for its biocontrol potential through ISR induction. Among them, Pseudomonas protegens stands out as a particularly interesting model. Well-characterized strains include P. protegens CHA0 and Pf-5, both of which have been shown to induce ISR in various plant species (Verhagen et al. 2009; Balthazar et al. 2022). One of the most well-studied ISR inducers in P. protegens is the polyketide antibiotic DAPG, which effectively induced resistance in Arabidopsis thaliana against Hyaloperonospora arabidopsidis (formerly Peronospora parasitica) (Iavicoli et al. 2003).

P. protegens and related species can also trigger ISR through the production of orfamides. Ma et al. (2017) demonstrated that, in rice (Oryza sativa), orfamides act as ISR elicitors against the necrotrophic fungus Cochliobolus miyabeanus, the causal agent of brown spot disease, but show no activity against the blast fungus Magnaporthe oryzae. Additionally, ISR can be induced by other compounds; for example, nitric oxide (NO) produced by P. protegens CHA0 was shown to trigger ISR in bean plants against Rhizoctonia solani (Keshavarz-Tohid et al., 2016).

However, the effectiveness of ISR induced by P. protegens can vary and depends on the specific plant-pathogen-microorganism interaction. For example, while DAPG has been shown to effectively induce resistance in (A) thaliana against Hyaloperonospora arabidopsidis (Iavicoli et al. 2003), and in grapevine (Verhagen et al. 2009) and Cannabis sativa against (B) cinerea (Balthazar et al. 2022), it does not exhibit the same effect in other cases (Weller et al., 2012). A similar phenomenon was observed for orfamides - although they successfully activated ISR against (C) miyabeanus, they failed to induce resistance against M. oryzae (Ma et al. 2017). This suggests that the efficacy of ISR activation may depend on specific plant-microbe interactions and the presence of certain bacterial metabolites.

Moreover, Huang et al. (2022) demonstrated that P. protegens significantly enhanced the expression of defense-related genes in apple (Malus domestica), including β−1,3-glucanase (MdGLU), phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (MdPAL), peroxidase (MdPOD), and catalase (MdCAT). This upregulation contributed to reducing the pathogenicity of Botryosphaeria dothidea, a major causal agent of apple canker.

Studies on P. protegens CHA0 also indicate its role in enhancing the expression of resistance-related genes in wheat (Triticum aestivum), providing protection against Zymoseptoria tritici, the pathogen responsible for Septoria leaf blotch (Ashrafi et al. 2020). Notably, the interaction of P. protegens CHA0 with the endophytic fungus Serendipita indica also resulted in the increased expression of key defense genes, including phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), lipoxygenase (LOX), nonexpressor of pathogenesis-related genes 1 (NPR1), and pathogenesis-related protein 5 (PR5), further confirming the bacterium’s ability to induce systemic resistance (Ashrafi et al. 2020).

Additionally, in the early stage of infection, P. protegens significantly upregulated defense-related genes in peach (Prunus persica), including superoxide dismutase (sod), catalase (cat), PAL, polyphenol oxidase (ppo), chitinase (chi), and β−1,3-glucanases (glu). This response contributed to increased resistance against brown rot (Monilinia fructicola), a major postharvest disease affecting peach fruit (Shan et al., 2024). Similarly, in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), P. protegens ML15 has demonstrated strong biocontrol potential against B. cinerea, the pathogen responsible for gray mold, another significant postharvest disease affecting tomato fruit (Ajijah et al. 2023).

Furthermore, whole genome sequencing (WGS) of P. protegens E1BL2 allowed the identification of putative genes involved in metabolite synthesis, contributing to its beneficial effects on plant health. These include genes encoding inducers of ISR, such as flagellin A (flaA), flagellin G (flaG), O-antigen polymerase (rffA), and lipopolysaccharide heptosyltransferase (rfaP), which play crucial roles in triggering plant immune responses (Camarillo et al., 2024).

Despite significant progress in research on ISR induced by Pseudomonas protegens, there are still substantial knowledge gaps. It remains unclear why certain compounds, such as DAPG or orfamides, induce ISR in some cases but not in others. Additionally, most studies focus on model plants like Arabidopsis, while the potential of ISR in agricultural crops remains poorly explored. Further research is needed to better understand the molecular mechanisms underlying ISR and to develop strategies for utilizing P. protegens in agricultural practice.

Rhizosphere colonization and impact on native microbiota

The colonization of the rhizosphere by P. protegens bacteria directly impacts their effectiveness in controlling phytopathogens. The ability of these bacteria to colonize plant roots and the rhizosphere is crucial for their biocontrol functions, as it enables direct interaction with pathogens and the production of antimicrobial compounds at the site of infection.

Studies based on monitoring P. protegens colonization using quantitative PCR (qPCR) have provided valuable information on the dynamics of plant colonization. In research by Zhang et al. (2021), qPCR assays were developed based on the amplification of specific AFLP (amplified fragment length polymorphism) fragments for P. protegens FD6 and Bacillus subtilis NCD-2 strains. Genes specific to these strains were detected in the wheat rhizosphere, where P. protegens FD6 showed a greater colonization ability than B. subtilis NCD-2. In this study, the impact of bacteria on the Rhizoctonia solani AG-8 population was monitored, and it was shown that the presence of P. protegens reduced the pathogen number in the rhizosphere by 13 times. The same approach was applied in later research by Zhang et al. (2023), where a specific AFLP fragment was also amplified to monitor the colonization of the wheat rhizosphere by P. protegens FD6, but this time it was shown that the colonization by FD6 was up to 80 times greater than that of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens W10. Both inoculants reduced the population of Rhizoctonia cerealis by as much as 3 times. Castro Tapia et al. (2020) used a slightly different approach to quantifying bacterial populations via qPCR and monitoring P. protegens in the wheat rhizosphere by selecting the phlD + gene in the wheat rhizosphere. The results showed that seed inoculation with the P. protegens consortium increased the bacterial population by 73% in the first season and by 22% in the second season. At the same time, the intensity of infection by pathogens causing crown and root rot was reduced by an average of 16.8% compared to the control, which is associated with the production of DAPG. The phlD gene was also used for monitoring P. protegens by Quezada-D’Angelo et al. (2024). The results showed that the colonization of chicory roots by P. protegens strains ranged from 70% to 85%, which correlated with effective control of the pathogen Boeremia exigua.

The above studies confirm that qPCR is an effective tool for tracking the dynamics of colonization by P. protegens. In addition to molecular methods, P. protegens colonization can be monitored using microscopic techniques with the use of green fluorescent protein (GFP) markers. Jing et al. (2020) used confocal laser scanning microscopy to analyze plant colonization by genetically modified P. protegens Pf-5 strain tagged with the GFP fluorescent protein. This allowed precise determination of the bacterial location on the root surface and in the rhizosphere zone. These methods complement qPCR by providing information on the location and spatial distribution of bacteria in the rhizosphere. Recently, both of these techniques were applied in a single study. Zhao et al. (2024) measured the effectiveness and visualized the colonization of P. protegens Pf-5 and B. velenzis DMW-1 in tomato roots. The authors demonstrated that the pyoluteorin-deficient Pf-5 mutant not only cooperated better with DMW1 in root colonization but also in biofilm formation, metabolite production, and control of tomato bacterial wilt disease. Furthermore, the addition of P. protegens to the tomato rhizosphere microbiome significantly increased microbial diversity, and the absence of the pltB gene in the ΔpltB mutant resulted in even greater enrichment of the bacterial community, including a substantial proliferation of Bacillus spp. At higher taxonomic levels, in the presence of P. protegens or its mutant, an increase in the abundance of bacteria of phyla Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria was observed (Zhao et al. 2024).

Interestingly, Muzlera et al. (2024) investigated the role of orfamide (Ofa) production by P. protegens CHA0T in wheat root colonization and rhizosphere microbiome assembly. The authors demonstrated that orfamide production enhances the competitiveness of CHA0T for wheat rhizosphere colonization and influences the bacterial community structure, with the wild-type strain causing a distinctly different microbiome assemblage compared to the ΔOfa mutant.

In summary, root colonization by P. protegens is crucial for its effectiveness in biocontrol of phytopathogens, as confirmed by studies utilizing molecular techniques and microscopy. However, there are certain gaps in knowledge, such as the mechanisms of competition between P. protegens and other rhizosphere microorganisms, as well as the impact of environmental factors on colonization stability. The use of modern techniques, such as metagenomics and transcriptomics, could contribute to a better understanding of these phenomena (Vesga et al. 2020; Zhao et al., 2024). Furthermore, further research should focus on strategies to improve colonization efficiency under field conditions, which may enhance biocontrol effectiveness in sustainable agriculture.

Conclusions

P. protegens is a promising biocontrol microorganism due to its ability to produce diverse secondary metabolites, such as DAPG, pyoluteorin, orfamides, protegenin, and hydrolytic enzymes, which exhibit varied mechanisms of action. The biosynthesis of these compounds can be modulated through genetic engineering and natural environmental interactions, making this species an attractive tool for sustainable agriculture. Studies demonstrate that the production of key metabolites, such as DAPG, pyoluteorin, or chitinases, can be enhanced not only through genetic modifications, such as the inactivation of the retS gene in P. protegens Pf-5, but also via natural inducers, such as glutamate, which significantly stimulates chitinase gene expression in strain CHA0. Interspecies interactions, for instance with P. capeferrum, further influence metabolite production, highlighting the critical role of environmental context in biocontrol. Research by Muzlera et al. (2024) has shown that orfamide (Ofa) production by P. protegens CHA0T enhances its competitiveness for wheat rhizosphere colonization and shapes the bacterial community structure, with the wild-type strain inducing a distinct microbiome assemblage compared to the Ofa-deficient mutant. Furthermore, studies utilizing quantitative PCR (qPCR) and confocal laser scanning microscopy with GFP markers, such as those by Zhang et al. (2021, 2023), Castro Tapia et al. (2020), Quezada-D’Angelo et al. (2024), and Zhao et al. (2024), have confirmed that effective rhizosphere colonization by P. protegens is crucial for its biocontrol efficacy, significantly reducing pathogen populations (e.g., R, solani, R. cerealis, and B. exigua) and increasing microbial diversity in the rhizosphere, particularly of Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria.

Future perspectives

Despite progress in research on chitinases, orfamides, and ISR, the mechanisms regulating the expression of genes responsible for the biosynthesis of these compounds remain poorly understood. Current analyses primarily focus on gene identification and in vitro enzymatic activity, with a lack of comprehensive studies on environmental and molecular factors controlling overexpression. The efficacy of ISR induced by P. protegens metabolites, such as DAPG or orfamides, is variable and depends on plant species and biological conditions, with most studies focusing on model plants like Arabidopsis, limiting insights into applications for agricultural crops. Additionally, while molecular techniques like qPCR and GFP-based microscopy have advanced our understanding of P. protegens colonization dynamics, gaps remain in elucidating the competitive interactions with other rhizosphere microorganisms and the impact of environmental factors on colonization stability. Disruption of the native microbiota, for example through the suppression of key dominant bacterial populations, may lead to undesirable effects on plant health.

In the future, deeper investigations into the molecular mechanisms regulating secondary metabolite biosynthesis in P. protegens are essential to develop more effective biocontrol strategies. Identifying natural inducers and understanding interspecies interactions that enhance metabolite production without relying on genetic engineering will be critical. Expanding research to include crop plants, such as wheat and maize, will provide a better assessment of ISR potential and rhizosphere microbiome modulation in practical agricultural settings. The application of advanced techniques, such as metagenomics and transcriptomics could further elucidate the mechanisms of competition and colonization stability in the rhizosphere. Moreover, strategies to enhance colonization efficiency under field conditions could optimize the biocontrol potential of P. protegens. Integrating these discoveries with modern technologies could significantly improve the efficacy of P. protegens as a biocontrol agent and biofertilizer, supporting the development of more sustainable agricultural production systems.

In practice, P. protegens can be integrated with other biocontrol agents or used in combination with conventional fungicides to enhance disease control and reduce chemical inputs. Such integrated management strategies could improve the consistency and reliability of biocontrol under field conditions. Nevertheless, before large-scale commercialization, P. protegens strains should undergo thorough evaluation, preferably under field or semi-field conditions, to confirm their efficacy, stability, and safety across diverse environmental contexts. Integrating these discoveries with modern technologies could significantly improve the efficacy of P. protegens as a biocontrol agent and biofertilizer, supporting the development of more sustainable agricultural production systems.
