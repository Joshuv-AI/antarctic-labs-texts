Mechanically stable but biologically inactive Jones fracture nonunion managed without immediate revision surgery: a case report
Authors: Koji Nozaka, Tsuyoshi Shirahata, Yusuke Yuasa, Shuntaro Harata, Manami Nakanishi, Naohisa Miyakoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJones fractures are prone to delayed union and nonunion because of the vascular characteristics and mechanical loading environment of the fifth metatarsal metaphyseal–diaphyseal junction. Although nonunion is often attributed to mechanical insufficiency, host-related biological and functional factors may also be clinically relevant in selected cases. We report an illustrative case of Jones fracture nonunion with maintained implant integrity and radiographic stability that united without immediate revision surgery after integrated clinical assessment and management.Case presentationA 17-year-old male competitive basketball player sustained a zone 2 fifth metatarsal fracture and underwent acute intramedullary screw fixation. Computed tomography (CT) at injury demonstrated cortical thickening and intramedullary canal narrowing, suggesting a pre-existing stress reaction rather than a purely acute traumatic fracture. At 7 months after injury, he had persistent pain, a persistent fracture line on radiographs and CT, absence of bridging callus, and no hypertrophic response. Implant integrity and radiographic stability were maintained. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated 25-hydroxyvitamin D insufficiency (12.8 ng/mL) and elevated undercarboxylated osteocalcin (20.3 ng/mL), while bone mineral density was normal. Hip internal rotation was restricted to 5°. Immediate revision surgery was deferred after radiographic assessment of mechanical stability. Eldecalcitol, vitamin K2 supplementation, structured hip internal rotation stretching, continued low-intensity pulsed ultrasound, and activity modification were used as part of an integrated management approach. Radiographic bridging callus was observed 3 months after initiation of treatment at our institution, followed by staged return to jogging, basketball practice, and full unrestricted play after radiographic union. At 5 years, union was maintained without refracture, hip internal rotation remained 60°, and the patient was pain-free during occasional recreational basketball.ConclusionsThis single case illustrates that, in carefully selected Jones fracture nonunion with maintained implant integrity and radiographic stability, metabolic evaluation and kinetic-chain assessment may be considered as part of clinical assessment before immediate revision surgery. However, causality cannot be established, and the observed union cannot be attributed to any single intervention. Background

Jones fractures occur at the metaphyseal–diaphyseal junction of the fifth metatarsal, a vascular watershed region predisposed to delayed union, nonunion, and refracture [1–3]. Intramedullary screw fixation generally provides mechanical stability, but persistent delayed union, nonunion, and recurrent stress injury remain clinically relevant problems in competitive athletes [1–3].

Fracture nonunion is multifactorial. Mechanical insufficiency, implant failure, inadequate reduction, biological impairment, nutritional factors, endocrine abnormalities, and activity-related loading may each influence healing [4–6]. Brinker et al. described metabolic and endocrine abnormalities in patients with unexplained nonunion and proposed metabolic evaluation when no clear technical cause is identified [6]. This paradigm is relevant to clinical reasoning in selected nonunions; however, evidence supporting routine metabolic screening in all Jones fracture nonunions remains limited.

Vitamin D is important for bone mineralization. Low serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels have been associated with increased odds of zone 2/3 fifth metatarsal stress fractures [7], and recent reviews have discussed vitamin D deficiency as a potential factor in fracture healing [8, 9]. Nutritional indicators have also been highlighted in the assessment of nonunion [4]. Vitamin K is required for gamma-carboxylation of osteocalcin, and elevated undercarboxylated osteocalcin (ucOC) may reflect impaired vitamin K-dependent osteocalcin carboxylation [10, 11]. Nevertheless, direct evidence linking vitamin K deficiency or ucOC elevation specifically to Jones fracture nonunion is limited, although a previous case report has described fracture nonunion associated with vitamin D and vitamin K deficiencies [12].

Biomechanical influences across the kinetic chain may affect lateral column loading of the foot. Previous sports medicine studies reported that restriction of hip internal rotation was associated with the development of Jones fractures in football players [13, 14]. These studies address risk factors for fracture occurrence rather than treatment factors for established nonunion, and the two clinical scenarios should not be interpreted interchangeably.

We report a single illustrative case of a stress-related Jones fracture nonunion with maintained implant integrity and radiographic stability in which union was observed without immediate revision surgery after integrated clinical assessment and management. The purpose of this report is to describe the clinical reasoning process, not to establish an alternative treatment strategy.

Chronological clinical timeline and management of the present case

Abbreviations: CT, computed tomography; LIPUS, low-intensity pulsed ultrasound; ucOC, undercarboxylated osteocalcin

A 17-year-old male competitive basketball player resumed intensive training after a period of reduced activity and developed intermittent lateral right foot pain. At the time of injury (0 months), he sustained an inversion injury while landing from a jump. Radiographs demonstrated a zone 2 fifth metatarsal fracture. CT showed medial extension of the fracture line with cortical thickening and narrowing of the intramedullary canal, suggesting a pre-existing stress reaction. Therefore, although the patient presented after an acute inversion injury, the fracture was considered stress-related rather than purely acute traumatic (Fig. 1).

Initial radiographs and computed tomography at injury (0 months). a, b Anteroposterior and oblique radiographs demonstrate a zone 2 fifth metatarsal fracture. c, d, e Computed tomography images show medial extension of the fracture line with cortical thickening and intramedullary canal narrowing, suggesting a stress-related component

Intramedullary fixation was performed acutely using a self-compressing intramedullary screw. The fracture was fixed using a 4.5/4.7-mm tapered self-compression screw with a length of 50 mm. Postoperative radiographs confirmed appropriate alignment and screw positioning, with a nearly central screw trajectory. The estimated intramedullary canal occupancy was approximately 85% at the nonunion site and nearly 100% in the distal metatarsal canal (Fig. 2). Progressive weight bearing and low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) were introduced during postoperative rehabilitation.

Postoperative radiographs after intramedullary screw fixation. a, b Anteroposterior and oblique radiographs demonstrate appropriate alignment and screw positioning. The screw trajectory is nearly central, with adequate canal occupancy

At 7 months after injury, the patient had persistent local pain and tenderness. Serial radiographs and CT demonstrated a persistent fracture line without bridging callus or a hypertrophic healing response (Fig. 3). There was no screw breakage, loosening, migration, or loss of reduction. Because terminology varies in the literature, the diagnosis of nonunion was based not only on elapsed time but also on persistent symptoms, absence of progressive radiographic healing, CT confirmation of a persistent fracture line without bridging callus, and absence of a hypertrophic response despite maintained radiographic stability.

Immediate revision surgery was deferred after consideration of the maintained implant integrity and radiographic stability. Eldecalcitol (0.75 micrograms daily), vitamin K2 supplementation, and structured hip internal rotation stretching were initiated. Eldecalcitol was selected as a practical therapeutic option because standard calcium and vitamin D supplementation would have required multiple daily tablets, raising concerns regarding adherence in an adolescent athlete. This choice reflected an individual clinical decision and should not be interpreted as evidence that eldecalcitol is superior to conventional vitamin D supplementation for fracture healing.

During the nonunion period, the patient was instructed to avoid activities involving direct loading of the injured foot. Upper-body conditioning and lower-extremity exercises that did not impose direct weight-bearing stress on the fifth metatarsal were permitted. LIPUS and activity modification were continued during this period.

At approximately 10 months after injury (3 months after initiation of treatment at our institution), radiographs demonstrated bridging callus formation, and tenderness at the fracture site had resolved; therefore, light jogging was permitted. At 11 months after injury (4 months after initiation of treatment), the patient remained pain-free during jogging and radiographs demonstrated further progression of healing; participation in basketball practice was permitted, although competitive games remained prohibited. At approximately 12 months after injury (5 months after initiation of treatment), radiographs demonstrated union (Fig. 4), and the patient was pain-free during basketball practice; full unrestricted return to basketball, including competitive play, was permitted. The ucOC level decreased to 10.2 ng/mL, and hip internal rotation improved to 60°. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D was not reassessed.

Radiographs at 12 months after injury (5 months after initiation of treatment at our institution). Progressive callus formation and radiographic union are observed

Even after radiographic union, hip internal rotation stretching was continued because the fracture was considered stress-related. Metabolic therapy was continued until the patient retired from competitive basketball at university, with the intention of reducing the potential risk of recurrent stress injury. At the 5-year follow-up, radiographs demonstrated maintained fracture union, with findings essentially unchanged from those shown in Fig. 4. There was no screw breakage, implant loosening, or refracture. Metabolic therapy had been discontinued, but the patient continued hip internal rotation stretching, and hip internal rotation remained 60°. He had retired from competitive basketball and participated only occasionally in recreational basketball on weekends. He remained asymptomatic without recurrent stress injury or functional limitation. The American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society score was 100.

Discussion

This case describes a stress-related Jones fracture nonunion in an adolescent competitive basketball player in whom union was eventually observed without immediate revision surgery. The educational value of this case lies in the integration of mechanical assessment, metabolic evaluation, activity modification, and kinetic-chain assessment. However, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. This single case cannot establish causality or determine the independent contribution of any component of treatment.

Definitions of delayed union and nonunion in Jones fractures vary among published studies, and there is no universally accepted time threshold. In the present case, the diagnosis of nonunion at 7 months was based on a combination of clinical and imaging findings rather than time alone. Persistent symptoms, absence of progressive radiographic healing, CT confirmation of a persistent fracture line without bridging callus, and absence of a hypertrophic response despite maintained implant integrity were considered consistent with a biologically inactive nonunion rather than uncomplicated delayed union.

Mechanical stability was assessed radiographically rather than by formal biomechanical testing. The screw trajectory was nearly central, the estimated canal occupancy was approximately 85% at the nonunion site and nearly 100% distally, and there was no screw breakage, loosening, migration, or loss of reduction. These findings supported the decision to defer immediate revision surgery. Nevertheless, radiographic assessment cannot quantify construct stiffness, and the absence of objective biomechanical testing should be recognized as a limitation.

The Brinker metabolic screening paradigm informed our clinical reasoning because no clear technical failure was evident [6]. However, this paradigm should not be interpreted as an established standard of care for all Jones fracture nonunions. Evidence supporting routine metabolic screening in all patients with Jones fracture nonunion remains limited. In this case, metabolic evaluation was considered because fixation appeared radiographically stable and the nonunion pattern was biologically inactive. Future studies are needed to determine which patients with Jones fracture nonunion may benefit from metabolic assessment.

Vitamin D insufficiency was identified at baseline. This finding was consistent with reports linking low 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels with fifth metatarsal stress fracture risk [7] and with broader literature discussing vitamin D deficiency in fracture healing [8, 9–4, 15] . However, the degree of deficiency was modest, andpost-treatment 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels were not reassessed. Therefore, the effectiveness of vitamin D-related metabolic correction cannot be confirmed in this case. Because the patient received active vitaminD therapy, serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels would not necessarily reflect the biological effects oftreatment, but follow-up measurement would nevertheless have provided useful information.

The elevated ucOC level suggested impaired vitamin K-dependent osteocalcin carboxylation despite normal bone mineral density. However, direct evidence linking vitamin K deficiency or elevated ucOC specifically to Jones fracture nonunion remains limited. Therefore, ucOC elevation in this case should be interpreted as a potentially relevant biological finding rather than definitive evidence of causation. Vitamin K-related impairment of bone matrix quality remains biologically plausible, but its contribution to Jones fracture nonunion requires further investigation.

Biomechanical factors across the kinetic chain may influence lateral column loading of the foot. Previous studies reported an association between hip internal rotation restriction and the occurrence of Jones fractures in football players [13, 14]. These studies evaluated risk factors for fracture development, not treatment factors for established nonunion. Accordingly, hip internal rotation restriction in the present case should be regarded as a clinically relevant observation and a possible recurrence-related factor, rather than as proof that stretching promotes nonunion healing. The improvement in hip internal rotation from 5° to 60° preceded return to sport, but the relationship between this change and fracture union remains speculative.

Several alternative explanations for eventual union should be considered. The observed healing may have reflected delayed biological healing under stable fixation, independent of metabolic intervention. In addition, LIPUS, activity modification, the natural healing process, reduced foot loading, and the patient's intrinsic healing capacity may all have contributed. Because eldecalcitol, vitamin K2 supplementation, hip internal rotation stretching, LIPUS, and activity modification were implemented concurrently, the independent contribution of each intervention cannot be determined. The present case therefore demonstrates a temporal association between an integrated management approach and eventual union, not a causal treatment effect.

The CT findings at injury suggested that the fracture was stress-related rather than purely acute traumatic. This interpretation influenced long-term management after union, including continued hip stretching and continuation of metabolic therapy until retirement from competitive basketball. The absence of refracture at 5 years is encouraging, but no conclusion can be drawn regarding the effectiveness of these recurrence-risk reduction measures.

Limitations

This report has several important limitations. First, it is a single case report, and causality cannot be established. Second, multiple interventions were introduced concurrently, including eldecalcitol, vitamin K2 supplementation, hip internal rotation stretching, LIPUS, and activity modification; therefore, the independent contribution of each intervention cannot be determined. Third, serial metabolic monitoring was incomplete. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D was measured only at baseline, and ucOC was assessed only at baseline and after fracture union. Consequently, temporal changes in metabolic status during the healing process could not be evaluated. Fourth, mechanical stability was assessed radiographically rather than by objective biomechanical testing. Fifth, detailed gait analysis and plantar pressure analysis were not performed, and the biomechanical mechanisms remain speculative. Finally, the findings may not be generalizable to all patients with Jones fracture nonunion and should not be interpreted as evidence that metabolic optimization can reliably replace revision surgery.

Conclusions

This single illustrative case suggests that, in carefully selected Jones fracture nonunion with maintained implant integrity and radiographic stability, metabolic evaluation and kinetic-chain assessment may be considered as part of the clinical assessment before immediate revision surgery. However, fracture union cannot be attributed to host optimization or to any individual intervention in this case. The proposed integrated approach is hypothesis-generating and should not be interpreted as an evidence-based alternative to revision surgery.
