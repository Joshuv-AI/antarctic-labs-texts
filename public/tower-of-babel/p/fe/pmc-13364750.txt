Ligelizumab Immunogenicity and Impact on Pharmacokinetics, Pharmacodynamics, Efficacy, and Safety in Patients With Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria
Authors: Yan Ji, Claudio Calonder, Tiina Kirsiläe, Alis Burciu, Nathalie Laurent, Eva Hua, Thomas Severin, Manmath Patekar, Ralph Woessner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLigelizumab is a highly potent, humanized IgG1 anti‐IgE monoclonal antibody that was developed to treat chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU). The immunogenicity of ligelizumab and its clinical impact were investigated in CSU patients following subcutaneous administration of 72 or 120 mg ligelizumab once every 4 weeks based on pooled data from two pivotal phase 3 clinical trials (PEARL‐1 and PEARL‐2). Across the 72 mg ligelizumab, 120 mg ligelizumab and placebo transitioned 120 mg ligelizumab arms, treatment‐emergent anti‐drug antibodies (TE‐ADAs) were observed in 20.0%–22.6% of patients, out of whom 78.0%–81.7% developed neutralizing antibodies. The majority (> 99%) of patients with TE‐ADAs had treatment‐induced ADAs and 74.0%–90.0% developed a persistent ADA response. ADA titers became detectable in Week 12 after start of ligelizumab treatment and reached plateau around Week 20, with the median ADA onset at 143–147 days. In the 72 and 120 mg ligelizumab arms, the geometric mean steady‐state trough concentration of ligelizumab was 52% and 41% lower, and the pharmacodynamic marker total IgE (% change from baseline) was 71% and 66% lower in ADA‐positive than in ADA‐negative patients, respectively. However, primary efficacy endpoint (change from baseline in Urticaria Activity Score 7) and key safety endpoints (incidence of treatment‐emergent hypersensitivity and injection‐site reactions) did not show clinically meaningful difference between ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative patients. This comprehensive analysis is the first report characterizing the immunogenicity profile of ligelizumab. The results indicate that ADAs reduced ligelizumab pharmacokinetic exposure and target engagement but had no clinically relevant impact on efficacy or safety in patients with CSU.

Introduction

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have revolutionized the management of a wide range of diseases due to their high specificity and affinity for target antigens [1]. One of the concerns associated with the development and clinical use of a therapeutic mAb is immunogenicity, which is the ability of a therapeutic protein to trigger an immune response in the body, resulting in the development of anti‐drug antibodies (ADAs). ADAs are produced when the human's immune system recognizes all or part of a therapeutic mAb as foreign and initiates an immune response to it [2, 3, 4]. Advances in biotechnology have led to the development of humanized and fully human mAbs which reduce immunogenicity and enhance safety compared to earlier mouse‐derived or chimeric antibodies [5], however, therapeutic mAbs can still elicit immune responses in human [4, 6].

ADAs can be categorized as (a) neutralizing antibodies (nAbs), which bind to the pharmacologically relevant antigen‐binding site of the therapeutic mAb and inhibit its interaction with the target, or (b) non‐neutralizing antibodies (non‐nAbs), which recognize and attach to other regions of the mAb without directly interfering with its bioactivity but potentially increasing its clearance and leading to reduced drug exposure [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. ADAs can impact safety and efficacy of the therapeutic mAb; therefore, understanding and monitoring immunogenicity is crucial in drug development [2, 3, 11]. Regulatory agencies require a systematic, risk‐based immunogenicity assessment, which should be science‐driven and tailored to the product considering factors such as molecular structure, manufacturing process, patient population, or intended clinical use [12, 13, 14].

Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is a skin disorder characterized by the development of itchy hives, angioedema, or both lasting for at least 6 weeks [15]. The underlying pathophysiology of CSU is not fully clear, but involves activation of high‐affinity immunoglobulin E (IgE) receptors (FcεRI) on cutaneous mast cells and basophils, autoimmune mechanisms that involve IgG autoantibodies directed to IgE or its receptor FcεRI, and auto‐allergic mechanisms that involve IgE‐mediated hypersensitivity reactions against self‐antigens [16, 17, 18].

Ligelizumab (also known as QGE031) is a highly potent, humanized IgG1 isotype mAb that binds to IgE and was developed as a next‐generation IgE antibody to treat CSU [19]. Compared to omalizumab, the only approved IgE antibody to treat CSU [20], ligelizumab has shown superior inhibition of IgE binding to FcεRI, basophil activation, B‐cell mediated IgE production, and reduced passive systemic anaphylaxis in a mouse model [19]. Ligelizumab binds to specific epitopes in the C3 region of IgE and thus blocks the interaction of free IgE with both high and low affinity IgE receptors FcεRI and FcεRII/CD23, resulting in dose‐ and time‐dependent neutralization of free IgE [21, 22]. Ligelizumab showed improvement of urticaria symptom control in adult and adolescent patients with CSU in Phase 2b studies [23, 24, 25]. More recently, ligelizumab improved urticaria symptom control and was safe in adolescent and adult patients with CSU in two Phase 3 pivotal trials (PEARL‐1 and PEARL‐2). Both trials met the primary efficacy endpoint of superiority versus placebo but not versus omalizumab [26]. In this report, we present a comprehensive assessment of the immunogenicity profile of ligelizumab in the PEARL‐1 and PEARL‐2 studies. The analysis characterized ligelizumab immunogenicity and its impact on the pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD), efficacy, and safety of ligelizumab in CSU patients.

Methods

Clinical Trial Design

PEARL‐1 (QGE031C2302, NCT03580369) and PEARL‐2 (QGE031C2303, NCT03580356) were identically designed, Phase 3, randomized, double‐blind, active and placebo‐controlled, parallel‐group studies to investigate the efficacy and safety of ligelizumab in adult and adolescent patients with CSU [26].

Briefly, patients aged 12 years or older with moderate‐to‐severe H1‐antihistamine (H1‐AH) refractory CSU were randomly allocated in a 3:3:3:1 ratio to 72 mg ligelizumab, 120 mg ligelizumab, 300 mg omalizumab, or placebo (denoted as placebo‐ligelizumab 120 mg transitioned, as patients transitioned from placebo to 120 mg ligelizumab from Week 24 onwards), and dosed subcutaneously every 4 weeks (Q4W). After completion of the 52‐week treatment period, patients entered a 12‐week post‐treatment follow‐up period. The primary objective was to assess whether 72 and 120 mg ligelizumab Q4W were superior to placebo and omalizumab (300 mg Q4W) for the primary endpoint, change‐from‐baseline (CFB) in weekly Urticaria Activity Score (UAS7) at Week 12. UAS7 was analyzed in all eligible patients according to the treatment assigned at random allocation. Safety was assessed in terms of adverse events, serious adverse events (SAEs), adverse events by severity, and changes in vital signs, hematology, and clinical chemistry parameters, and was monitored throughout the study in all patients who received at least one dose [26]. To assess PK, PD and immunogenicity of ligelizumab, pre‐dose samples were collected from each patient on Day 1 and on the first day of Weeks 4, 12, 20, 24, 28, 36, 44, 48, 52 during the treatment period and Week 64 during the post‐treatment follow‐up period to quantify serum concentrations of ligelizumab and total IgE and to detect the presence and titers of ADAs.

Immunogenicity Assays

ADAs were assessed using a validated bridging electrochemiluminescence immunoassay which included sample pre‐treatment to mitigate drug and target interference [27]. The methodology involved a 3‐tiered testing strategy consisting of screening, confirmation and titration. Screening employed a statistically derived cut‐point providing a 5% false‐positive rate, and samples at or above this threshold were confirmed using a confirmatory cut‐point which provided a 1% false‐positive rate. This classified each sample as ADA‐negative, ADA‐positive or ADA‐inconclusive (total IgE levels too high). ADA‐positive samples were titrated by serial two‐fold dilutions. ADA titers were determined as the reciprocal of the interpolated dilution at the titration cut point multiplied by the minimum required dilution of the assay. Assay sensitivity based on a monoclonal positive control antibody (PC) was 30 ng/mL and drug‐tolerance assessments demonstrated that 100 ng/mL of PC are still detectable in the presence of ≤ 80 μg/mL ligelizumab.

A validated competitive ligand‐binding assay was used to characterize ADA‐positive samples further and assess their potential to neutralize drug bioactivity. Following pre‐treatment steps to minimize target interference by depleting IgE using magnetic beads and drug interference by applying acid dissociation, biotinylated ligelizumab was added to each sample to form complexes with ADAs. The mixture was then transferred to a streptavidin plate after washing, digoxigenin‐labeled IgE was added, and if nAbs were present, binding of labeled IgE to ligelizumab was blocked. Detection was performed with a horseradish peroxidase‐labeled anti‐digoxigenin antibody Fab fragment using tetramethylbenzedene as chromogenic substrate.

Immunogenicity Assessment on Subject Level

ADA dynamics were explored on subject level [11, 28]. For ADA‐negative patients, all samples had to be ADA‐negative. For ADA‐positive patients, the ADA response was considered treatment‐induced if the pre‐treatment sample was ADA negative and at least one sample after drug treatment was classified as ADA positive; the ADA response was considered treatment‐boosted if the pre‐treatment sample was ADA positive (pre‐existing ADAs) and at least one sample of the treatment period had a titer which was at least 4‐fold higher than the pre‐treatment titer [11, 25]. Treatment‐emergent (TE) ADA positive patients are the sum of subjects with an induced or boosted ADA response. The temporal ADA trend was assessed by classifying the ADA response in a subject as persistent if either (a) at least two ADA‐positive values were identified in its profile within ≥ 16 weeks or (b) only one late sample was ADA‐positive with < 16 weeks before the last ADA sample; otherwise, the ADA response is transient. The ADA trend was also explored via evolution of the titer over time.

Pharmacokinetic and Pharmacodynamic Assessment

PK (ligelizumab) and PD (total IgE) were assessed based on the bioanalytical assays described previously [27]. Serum concentration of ligelizumab was quantified by a validated sequential, target‐based sandwich enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay with a lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) at 1.0 μg/mL. Serum concentration of total IgE (free IgE and IgE bound to ligelizumab) was quantified with the commercial ImmunoCAP total IgE sandwich immunoassay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA) with an LLOQ of 2.00 IU/mL.

Efficacy and Safety Assessment

The primary objective of the PEARL‐1 and PEARL‐2 studies was to assess if ligelizumab was superior to placebo and omalizumab in terms of CFB in UAS7 at Week 12. The CFB in UAS7 was also assessed over the entire 52‐week treatment period. The UAS assesses daily itch severity (ISS) and hives count (HSS). A weekly HSS score (HSS7, range of 0–21) and ISS score (ISS7), scale of 0 (none) to 3 (intense or severe) was derived by adding up the average daily scores of the 7 days preceding the visit. The UAS7 is the sum of the HSS7 and the ISS7 scores and ranges from 0 to 42, with higher scores thereby indicating higher severity. For safety, occurrence of treatment‐emerged adverse events and SAEs were assessed.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were descriptive and were performed using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute, NC) and R (v4.3.1).

Analysis Sets

The pooled dataset of PEARL‐1 and PEARL‐2 studies were used for the ADA analyses. ADA analysis was conducted using the immunogenicity analysis set, which consisted of all patients who received at least one dose of ligelizumab and had at least one immunogenicity assessment from PEARL‐1 and PEARL‐2. Ligelizumab and total IgE concentrations were analyzed using the PK/PD analysis set, which included all patients who received at least one dose of ligelizumab and had at least one PK/PD assessment. Efficacy endpoints were summarized for the full analysis set, which included all randomized patients who received at least one dose of ligelizumab. Safety outcomes were summarized for the safety set, comprising all patients who received at least one dose of ligelizumab, regardless of randomization status.

Immunogenicity Assessment

ADAs were determined for all patients in all ligelizumab treatment arms: 72 mg ligelizumab, 120 mg ligelizumab, and placebo‐ligelizumab 120 mg transitioned. No apparent difference in PK, IgE and ADA data was observed between the PEARL‐1 and PEARL‐2 studies; therefore, the data of the two studies were pooled. Similarly, adolescent and adult patients were pooled for this analysis based on no apparent difference in observed outcomes between the age groups, which was consistent with previous results [29].

Descriptive statistics (numbers and percentages) was used to (a) assess the prevalence of ADAs at baseline, (b) determine the incidence of patients with TE‐ADAs, (c) distinguish between treatment‐induced and treatment‐boosted immune response in TE‐ADA positive patients, (d) distinguish between persistent and transient ADAs in TE‐ADA positive patients, and (e) assess the incidence of nAbs in TE‐ADA positive patients. For each treatment group, ADA kinetics was examined through the titer distribution over time. Onset of ADAs was examined by measuring the actual time between initial administration of ligelizumab and the first detection of TE‐ADAs.

Assessment of ADA Impact on PK and PD

Ligelizumab trough concentration (Ctrough) approached steady state around Week 12; therefore, ligelizumab and total IgE concentrations collected between Weeks 12 and 52 (end of treatment) were averaged within each subject to calculate their steady‐state concentrations. The effect of ADAs on PK and PD was assessed by comparing steady‐state Ctrough of ligelizumab and steady‐state %change from baseline of total IgE concentrations, respectively, by ADA status for each treatment group.

Assessment of ADA Impact on Efficacy and Safety

The effect of ADA on efficacy was assessed by exploratory graphical analysis of UAS7 change from baseline by ADA status. The effect of ADA on safety was assessed by comparing the incidence of adverse events of special interest (AESI) for hypersensitivity and injection site reactions (ISRs) by ADA status.

Results

Immunogenicity Assessment

ADA Incidence

At baseline, ADAs were detected in 1.3%, 0.8%, and 1.9% of the patients in the ligelizumab 72 mg, 120 mg and placebo‐ligelizumab 120 mg transitioned groups, respectively. Out of the 17 patients with pre‐existing ADAs, approximately 75% were characterized as non‐neutralizing. A total of 22.6%, 20.0%, and 20.4% of the patients in the ligelizumab 72 mg, 120 mg and placebo‐ligelizumab 120 mg transitioned groups, respectively, developed TE‐ADAs, as they had at least one treatment‐induced or boosted ADA‐positive sample at any time during the treatment or post‐treatment follow‐up period (Table 1). NAbs were detected in 81.7%, 78.0%, and 80.0% of the TE‐ADA‐positive patients in the ligelizumab 72 mg, 120 mg and placebo‐ligelizumab 120 mg transitioned groups, respectively (Table 1).

Of the 142, 127 and 40 TE‐ADA‐positive patients in the ligelizumab 72 mg, 120 mg and placebo‐ligelizumab 120 mg transitioned groups, the pre‐existing ADA response was boosted by ligelizumab treatment in 2, 0 and 1 patients, respectively, while the vast majority of ADA‐positive patients developed treatment‐induced ADAs (Table 1). In the 22.6%, 20.0%, and 20.4% patients with TE‐ADAs, persistent ADAs were noted in 76.1%, 74.0%, and 90.0% of ADA‐positive patients in the ligelizumab 72 mg, 120 mg and placebo‐ligelizumab 120 mg transitioned groups, respectively, while a transient ADA response was observed in the remaining 23.9%, 26.0%, and 10.0% ADA‐positive patients, respectively (Table 1).

ADA Kinetics

For 72 mg ligelizumab, no samples were ADA positive at Week 4, 7.1% were ADA positive at Week 12, and the fraction increased to 12.6%–18.0% from Weeks 20 to 64 (Figure 1). Following Week 20, titer distribution was comparable between different timepoints, with median titers ranging from 652 to 1070 (Figure 1). Similarly, for ligelizumab 120 mg, no samples were ADA positive at Week 4, 5.4% were ADA‐positive at Week 12, and the fraction increased to 11.1%–14.2% from Weeks 20 to 64, with median titers ranging from 369 to 714 (Figure 1). The titer distribution in patients of the placebo‐ligelizumab 120 mg transitioned group is consistent with the ligelizumab 72 mg and 120 mg arms.

ADA Onset

In the ligelizumab 72 mg, 120 mg and placebo‐ligelizumab 120 mg transitioned groups, treatment‐emergent ADAs became detectable after a median duration of 147, 146, and 143 days post‐first dose of ligelizumab, respectively (Table 2). The onset of ADAs is variable between patients with a first quartile (Q1) as low as 88 days and a third quartile (Q3) as high as 200 days for the three groups. The overall trend is consistent with the ADA kinetics in all three ligelizumab treatment arms (Figure 1).

Impact of ADA on PK and PD

The steady‐state Ctrough in ADA‐positive patients was lower compared to ADA‐negative patients for both ligelizumab doses (Figure 2). In the ligelizumab 72 mg and 120 mg arms, the geometric mean (geometric CV%) steady‐state Ctrough of ligelizumab was 1.16 μg/mL (131.8%) and 2.02 μg/mL (116.6%) in ADA‐positive patients and 2.41 μg/mL (78.7%) and 3.42 μg/mL (68.9%) in ADA‐negative patients, respectively, showing an approximately 52% and 41% reduction in exposure due to ADAs, respectively (Table S1).

In line with the impact of ADAs on PK, the percentage change from baseline of total IgE concentrations was lower in ADA‐positive patients compared to ADA‐negative patients at both doses (Figure 2). In the ligelizumab 72 mg and 120 mg arms, the mean (CV%) of the percentage change from baseline in total IgE was 69.6% (177.3%) and 74.4% (141.6%) in ADA‐positive patients and 243% (145.8%) and 222% (106.7%) in ADA‐negative patients, respectively, indicating an approximately 71% and 66% reduction in target engagement due to ADAs, respectively (Table S2).

Impact of ADA on Efficacy

ADA positive samples were observed starting from Week 12 after commencing ligelizumab treatment. Between Week 12 and 52, ligelizumab efficacy (mean and range of CFB in UAS7 response) was comparable in the ADA‐negative and ADA‐positive patients (Figure 3). For the primary endpoint of UAS7 CFB at Week 12, the mean was −19.9 and −17.2 for ADA‐negative and ADA‐positive patients, respectively, at the dose of 72 mg, and −20.8 and −17.4 for ADA‐negative and ADA‐positive patients, respectively, at the dose of 120 mg (Table S3). In the placebo arm, the mean CFB in UAS7 at Week 12 was −11.4 and −9.2 in PEARL‐1 and PEARL‐2, respectively [26]. Therefore, the mean CFB in UAS7 of both ADA‐negative and ADA‐positive patients treated with ligelizumab were substantially lower than that of patients from the placebo arm during the placebo control period. The observed effect on UAS7 was comparable between ADA‐negative and ADA‐positive patients, indicating there is no clinically relevant impact of ADAs on efficacy.

Discussion

Humanized antibodies are generally less immunogenic than mouse or human/mouse chimeric antibodies [5]. However, therapeutic antibodies can still result in both humoral and cellular immune response and thus the development of ADAs, which may alter PK and/or PD and hence impact the safety and/or efficacy of the drug [2, 3, 4, 6]. The likelihood of a therapeutic antibody to mount an immunogenic response is dependent on various factors including molecular properties, impurities, target, dose regimen, formulation, route of administration, and patient population. Immunogenicity assessment for a therapeutic antibody, comprising of ADA detection and characterization, allows to determine the clinical relevance of developed ADAs, guide dosing strategies of the antibody and inform benefit–risk evaluations for patients.

Effective immunogenicity assessment in clinical trials requires validated assays which tailor all aspects of interest in terms of immunogenicity risk and a thoughtful sampling scheme, and both are essential for a robust immunogenicity evaluation. In this study, sample pre‐treatment was included to limit interference from drug and target on ADA detection which, together with careful cut‐point settings, reduced the risk for classifying a sample as false‐negative or false‐positive. All ADA‐positive samples were then titrated to provide a semiquantitative assessment for ADA generation within and between patients over time. A dedicated assay for nAbs provided the incidence rate of neutralizing ADAs. The ADA sampling scheme included a pre‐treatment sample, with the purpose of determining the prevalence of pre‐existing ADAs, and also to distinguish between treatment‐boosted, −induced and ‐unaffected ADA responses. After start of drug treatment, the first ADA sample was taken at Week 4, followed by regular sampling time points with a 4‐ or 8‐week interval during treatment period and one sample during post‐treatment follow‐up. During the treatment phase, ADA sampling was performed at Ctrough before injecting the next dose to limit the amount of drug in the sample.

Following ligelizumab treatment in CSU patients, the incidence of ADAs and nAbs was consistent in the three ligelizumab treatment arms from both Phase 3 trials, and no apparent relationship with dose and treatment regimens was observed. Across the three groups, pre‐existing ADAs were observed in 0.8%–1.9% of the patients and approximately 75% thereof were non‐neutralizing ADAs. Following treatment with ligelizumab, 20.0%–22.6% of patients developed ADAs and 78.0%–81.7% thereof developed nAbs which were capable to neutralize bioactivity of the drug in vitro. In line with the low prevalence of pre‐existing ADAs at baseline, > 99% of the ADA‐positive patients developed a treatment‐induced ADA response.

The developed ADA response was persistent in 74.0%–90.0% of the TE‐ADA‐positive patients, indicating that the ADA response lasted longer than 16 weeks for the majority of ADA‐positive patients. The percentage of patients with persistent ADAs in the placebo‐ligelizumab 120 mg transitioned group was approximately 14% and 16% higher than in the ligelizumab 72 mg and 120 mg groups, respectively. This may be caused by the way persistent ADAs are defined (≥ 16 weeks between first and last ADA‐positive sample or ADAs being detected only at 1 late sampling time point with < 16 weeks before a last ADA sample), because the shorter observation period in the placebo‐ligelizumab 120 mg transitioned group increased the likelihood for having a patient with an ADA‐positive sample at one time point but no ADA result > 16 weeks later.

Following the start of ligelizumab dosing, all patients across the three treatment groups were classified as ADA negative at Week 4 post the first dose, and ADA titers were detectable for 5.4% to 7.5% of the patients at Week 12 post the first dose. From Week 20 onwards, the fraction of patients with an ADA titer reached an approximate plateau between 11.1% and 18.9%. This is in line with the median time of ADA onset, which was approximately 20 weeks post‐first dose of ligelizumab in all three ligelizumab treatment arms. However, additional immunogenicity sampling between Weeks 4 and 12 would have improved more accurate determination of ADA onset. Within each dose group, the median titer was largely consistent between Weeks 4 and 64, but titer variability increased, with very few patients developing high titers over time. The median titers in the 120 mg dose group were lower compared to the 72 mg group, but the higher drug levels in the former group tend to diminish titers.

Decreases in ligelizumab concentrations were observed in the presence of ADAs. The geometric mean steady‐state Ctrough was approximately 52% and 41% lower in ADA‐positive than in ADA‐negative patients from the ligelizumab 72 mg and 120 mg arms, respectively. Consistent with the observed data, population PK (popPK) modeling showed that ADA is a significant covariate for ligelizumab PK with an increase of apparent clearance (CL/F) by 30% for ADA‐positive patients compared to ADA‐negative patients [29]. PopPK simulations predicted that ADAs would reduce ligelizumab PK exposure by approximately 40% [29]. Upon binding of ligelizumab to its soluble target IgE, free IgE levels were suppressed and total IgE concentrations comprising of free IgE and IgE bound to drug were increased, reflecting ligelizumab target engagement. The mean %change (increase) from baseline for total IgE was approximately 71% and 66% lower in ADA‐positive than in ADA‐negative patients from the ligelizumab 72 mg and 120 mg arms, respectively. This is expected given the decrease in ligelizumab concentrations due to ADAs and the majority of total IgE being ligelizumab bound. Approximately 80% of the ADAs in ADA‐positive patients were nAbs, which may have specifically contributed to a decrease in change from baseline for total IgE.

For efficacy, the primary endpoint (UAS7 CFB at Week 12) of both 72 and 120 mg ligelizumab was superior to placebo (p < 0.0001) with no apparent difference between the two doses [26], suggesting that maximal efficacy was achieved at 72 mg ligelizumab. In the 72 and 120 mg ligelizumab arms, both ADA‐negative and ADA‐positive patients showed substantially lower UAS7 CFB than patients from the placebo arm during the placebo‐control period. Given ADA‐positive patients demonstrated efficacy comparable to ADA‐negative patients and superior to placebo patients, these data suggest that ADAs did not have a clinically relevant impact on efficacy. In addition, the range of mean UAS7 CFB across visits from Weeks 12 to 52 was comparable between ADA‐negative and ADA‐positive patients. At Week 12, the timepoint when the primary efficacy endpoint was assessed, the prevalence of ADA‐positive samples was between 5.4% and 7.0% and therefore lower than at later timepoints. However, UAS7 CFB was also assessed until Week 52 and the impact of ADAs on UAS7 CFB was comparable between Week 12 and later timepoints when the fraction of ADA‐positive subjects was larger and the ADA response had time to mature further. There was no pre‐specified statistical inference planned for efficacy data between the ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative subgroups, considering the relatively small sample size in the ADA‐positive subgroup and the limitations of the analysis such as ADA status being a post‐randomization and time‐varying covariate. Therefore, post‐hoc exploratory analyses were considered more appropriate to investigate potential impact of ADAs on efficacy. Even though ADAs lowered ligelizumab exposure and decreased %change of total IgE, our data suggest that sufficient drug concentration and target saturation to achieve efficacy were still maintained in the ADA‐positive patients. Furthermore, these results are consistent with model‐based analyses, which showed that ADAs had limited impact on the UAS7 response (~4% lower probability of achieving complete response in ADA‐positive patients) [29].

For safety, two AESIs (hypersensitivity and ISRs) that may occur in the presence of ADAs were evaluated. The data showed no clinically relevant difference in the proportion of patients with hypersensitivity events and ISRs between ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative patients. This data is consistent with model‐based analysis [29]. Similar as our findings, others also reported that even high incidence of ADAs/nAbs with reduction of drug exposure in ADA‐positive patients did not have clinically meaningful impact on efficacy or safety [30, 31, 32, 33, 34].

In conclusion, our analysis characterized the immunogenicity profile of ligelizumab and assessed its clinical implications based on data of two pivotal Phase 3 studies in CSU. This is the first comprehensive analysis of immunogenicity for ligelizumab. The data show that ADAs reduced ligelizumab PK concentrations and IgE target engagement but did not have a clinically relevant impact on efficacy or safety in CSU patients.
