Documentation and evaluation of care of dying patients
Authors: Maria de Freitas, Lucinda Roberts, Alexandra Cockroft, Graeme Duke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEvaluate the quality of documentation and delivery of EOLC in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) during the COVID-19 pandemic and compare with a pre-pandemic audit.DesignRetrospective clinical audit of medical records of patients who died in ICU during the COVID-19 pandemic, January 2021 to February 2022, using the Documentation and Evaluation of Care of Dying Equation (DECODE) survey tool.SettingThree metropolitan adult ICUs in Victoria, Australia.Main outcomesDECODE audit score, patient characteristics, demographics, end of life planning, quality of death indicators, management of dying.ResultsThere were 194 deaths over a 14-month period. 2 cases were excluded. Patients wishes were documented in 83 (43%) cases. A total of 175 patients (91%) were receiving active treatment 24 h before death. A total of 166 deaths (86%) were expected and occurred a mean of 4.5 (IQR 2-9) days from admission to ICU. A total of 52 (27%) had palliative or symptom control care plans. The median DECODE score was 14 (IQR 12-15) with statistical variation across the three sites (p=0.001). Compared to pre pandemic audits, the DECODE score was higher (p=0.001) despite pandemic restrictions.ConclusionEOLC in ICU remains challenging due to diagnostic dilemmas, prognostic uncertainty, and short time-frames. Assessment of quality of EoLC care helps assess and possibly improve provision of care. The DECODE questionnaire provides a semi-objective measure of quality of care provided to the dying patient in ICU.

Introduction

In Australia, most deaths occur within the hospital setting, with 24% in acute-care hospitals and only 28% in dedicated palliative care facilities.1 The delivery of optimal end-of-life care (EOLC) is frequently challenged by a range of factors, including the sudden onset of life-limiting illness, rapid deterioration despite active treatment, prognostic uncertainty, communication barriers, emotional distress, and a lack of clarity regarding patient preferences. These challenges are particularly evident in intensive care units (ICUs), where approximately one in twelve adult patients (8%) do not survive their admission.2,3 During the COVID-19 pandemic, strict visitor restrictions, limited access to specialist consultations, and disrupted communication all conspired to undermine optimal EOLC delivery in ICU.4

Whilst EOLC remains an integral component of hospital5 and ICU practice, current outcome metrics in critical care focus on survival6 rather than the quality of death and dying.[7], [8], [9] High-quality palliative care has been shown to reduce the economic impact on the health care system.10 Regular clinical review of EOLC is recommended, but not mandated, by the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC),11 and there remains a paucity of simple clinical indicators for the acute care setting. Existing audit tools focus on the hospice setting or retrospective family surveys limiting the applicability of real-time hospital practice. Current established measures such as the ACSQHC EOLC audit tool,12 are resource-intensive and often unfeasible in hospitals without a dedicated research infrastructure.[13], [14], [15], [16] A simple pragmatic audit tool, such as the “20 Processes of care” described by King et al.17 provide an alternative method for clinical services to track performance and improvement.

In a previous study,18 we developed the Documentation and Evaluation of Care of the Dying Equation (DECODE) audit tool: an unweighted binary questionnaire derived from international standards19,20 and the ACSQHC EOLC audit tool.12 In this study we present a DECODE score as a semi-objective screening tool to assess the quality of EOLC in the critical care environment. The secondary objective was to compare EOLC in ICU during the COVID-19 pandemic with pre-pandemic data.

Methods

Setting

The three participating centres are all general adult, public ICUs that collectively provide 30 staffed ICU beds, caring for over 2500 patients per annum (pa) (Table 2). Admission to ICU is based on clinical assessment of actual or potentially reversible life-threatening illness. Patients who are deemed too well or too unwell are triaged to alternative wards. Clinical management, including all diagnostic and therapeutic decisions, is conducted by the onsite intensive care medical and nursing teams under the supervision of the duty intensive care specialist.

All three ICUs maintained similar staffing models, with continuous 24 h onsite nursing and medical staffing. Each unit had daily access to palliative care consultation services and weekday access to allied health professionals, including social work, psychiatry, counselling, and spiritual care support. None of the study sites had dedicated on-site palliative care beds. The larger of the three hospitals had a fully integrated electronic medical record (EMR) system, while the other two sites utilised paper-based documentation, archived and accessed via secure electronic systems. During the study period (2021–2022), all three ICUs were subject to severe COVID-19 pandemic-related restrictions placed on staff and visitor access as well as communication, which severely constrained the provision of EOLC. The larger tertiary centre functioned as a designated COVID-19 hospital in Victoria, Australia. The Care Plan for the Dying Patient (Safer Care, Victoria) 21 was not employed, primarily due to duplication of local documentation procedures and the absence of digital integration.

Study population

All patients who died while in any of the three ICUs between January 2021 and February 2022 (including decedents referred for organ donation and/or to the state coroner) were eligible. Cases were excluded where access to full medical records was not possible. Treating teams were unaware of the study at the time of documentation. All cases were reviewed and audited retrospectively.

Data collection

The DECODE audit tool (Table 1) is an abbreviated and modified version of the locally validated ACSQHC EOLC audit tool12 and incorporates key elements from King et al.17 “20 Processes of Care”. It generates a numerical score from twenty binary (yes/no) components describing processes of care that are considered essential to high-quality EOLC19,20 yielding a composite score out of 20. DECODE was designed to be a simple data collection tool specific for use in the acute care setting by all clinical staff and incorporates important patient- and family-centred communication processes that are absent in current validated tools (#4, #9, #17, and #19, Table 1). These items were included to address important gaps in the evaluation of communication processes in EOLC, ensuring patients and families are involved in shared decision-making, care is consistent with patient values, family presence is supported, and treating specialist are appropriately notified. We conducted a retrospective chart review of stored EMRs and applied the DECODE scoring system to each death in ICU. All records were reviewed by qualified intensive care medical staff. Survey elements were deemed to be absent if documentation was unclear or ambiguous. Risk adjustment was not applied for case-mix and local resources.

In addition to the DECODE audit tool, we collected supplementary clinical and demographic patient data, including age, sex, comorbidities, length of stay, and admission diagnosis. Details of life-support treatment within the last 24 h of life were recorded and included invasive mechanical ventilation (MV), renal replacement therapy (RRT), vasopressor infusions, diagnostic imaging, and laboratory investigations. Re-direction of care was defined by withdrawal or withholding of curative therapy, and the date and time of the consensus decision were documented along with date and time of death. Finally, the assessor undertook a clinical judgement as to whether death was expected.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Eastern Health Research and Ethics Committee (Ref QA22-091-91151). The need for next-of-kin consent was waived due to the observational nature of the study and the absence of intervention. Patient identification was removed prior to data analysis. All study data were entered, encrypted, and stored using a purpose-written survey tool and REDCap™ software.22,23

Statistical analysis

Group data were reported as median (interquartile range; IQR) or mean and standard deviation for Poisson-distributed events. Results of this audit were compared to historical control groups from a similar audit undertaken in the two larger hospitals,18 using the same scoring system described above. Analysis of variance and the Kruskal–Wallis rank test were used to compare parametric and nonparametric data, respectively, between sites and epochs. Data were analysed with StataMP™ V16.1 (2019, College Station, TX) statistical software. STROBE guidelines were employed for this report.23

Results

Over the 14-month study period, there were 2657 ICU admissions and 287 (11%) deaths following an ICU admission, where the majority (194; 68%) were managed in the ICU. Two records were inaccessible due to medico-legal reasons, leaving 192 (99%) eligible records in the study population (Fig. 1). Apart from the differences in volumes of activity and services between the three hospital sites, there were no significant demographic differences between the study populations from each hospital (Table 2). Discussion

Summary of results

We conducted a retrospective clinical audit of EOLC in nearly 200 consecutive in-hospital deaths across three ICUs over a 14-month period during the COVID-19 pandemic. The DECODE audit tool (Table 1) was found to be a simple, pragmatic, screening tool based on core elements of high-quality EOLC(19). Whilst differences in raw (unadjusted) scores were present between sites, there was a significant temporal improvement in scores despite challenges faced in ICU and pandemic-related constraints on access, communication, and consultation. This improvement (Fig. 3) suggests that documentation standards, and possibly care delivery, were maintained, if not improved, during the pandemic.

Learning and implications

DECODE scores in this study (median = 14; IQR: 12–15) were lower than the “gold standard” score derived from a palliative care cohort (n = 297; median 16; IQR: 13–17) using a comparable 20-point survey tool.17 Key areas identified for improvement included documentation practices, access to spiritual and palliative care services, and provision of bereavement support.

Documentation of patient and family wishes was suboptimal, with only 43% of cases explicitly recording patient wishes or values. This may reflect infrequent enquiries by treating clinicians, a lack of patient decision-making capacity (e.g. due to delirium, coma, or advanced illness), or absent prior documentation such as an advance care directive. In addition, spiritual care services were documented only in 36% of cases. This may indicate a lack of engagement or awareness of patients’ broader social, cultural, and religious backgrounds. As highlighted by Bloomer,24 all healthcare professionals have a responsibility to explore cultural and spiritual backgrounds to ensure cultural safety and respectful care. Eliciting patient values is best practice,11 and goals-of-care discussions should be integrated into routine clinical decision-making to deliver high-quality patient-centred EOLC.25

In contrast to Meesters et al.,26 we observed consistent recognition and documentation of a dying patient. Nevertheless, a substantial number of patients continued to receive life-sustaining therapies despite documented treatment limitations. These findings are consistent with recent literature26,27 and may reflect clinical uncertainty regarding the appropriateness or timing of withdrawal of such therapies. Structured exploration and documentation of patient wishes may improve clarity and confidence in limiting or withdrawing potentially non-beneficial life-sustaining treatments.

DECODE revealed only 12% of patients were referred to specialist palliative care services. A recent systematic review28 supports improved safety and patient-centred outcomes with collaborative ICU palliative care approaches. Barriers to integration often include resource limitations, cultural attitudes within ICU, and the acuity of critical illness. In our cohort, the average ICU length of stay was four days, and many patients were recognised as dying only shortly before death, limiting the opportunity for specialist input. While predefined referral criteria may facilitate collaboration,28 feasibility remains a concern due to time constraints and resource limitations. Nonetheless, EOLC quality could be enhanced by structured frameworks addressing both medical and psychosocial needs, along with appropriate evaluation tools.

Proactive family communication and bereavement follow-up (BFU) have been shown to improve long-term grief outcomes[29], [30], [31] and are recognised by the Australia and New Zealand Intensive Care Society (ANZICS) as core components of intensive care practice.32 In our study, adherence to this domain was observed in only 56% of cases. Notably, when BFU was offered, 86% of families accepted, indicating strong receptivity to such interventions. Kentish–Barnes et al.29 demonstrated that a structured, three-step bereavement support strategy significantly reduced long-term grief symptoms. Similarly, McAdam et al.30 reported that follow-up phone calls reduced symptoms of prolonged grief and post-traumatic stress. Despite a growing body of evidence and formal recommendations, implementation remains limited, with less than half of Australian ICUs offering a formal BFU service,33 consistent with international trends.34,35 Encouragingly, a recent study36 has shown that BFU implementation is feasible in the Australian ICU setting and may yield promising outcomes, highlighting this as a key area for future service development.

Strengths

The methodology has several strengths. We audited consecutive in-hospital deaths across three ICUs over 14 months during the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, a period where EOLC provision was likely to have been challenging. The DECODE tool is derived from published literature and validated elements of EOLC. It is applicable across a variety of acute inpatient settings where EOLC is provided. The results offer valuable feedback for clinical staff, support quality assurance for hospital leadership, and offer reassurance to the community. Although resource-intensive, clinical audit remains feasible and aligned with national standards. Even where institutional resource constraints limit data collection, a smaller sample with as few as thirty cases is likely to provide meaningful insights.

Limitations

Our methodology carries several important limitations. DECODE is a screening tool, not a diagnostic test. While a low score may prompt further review or highlight specific process elements requiring attention, a high score does not necessarily confirm optimal care. Additionally, although all DECODE components (Table 1) represent best practice, not all elements are feasible or clinically appropriate for every patient. This inherent variability contributes to score distributions below the maximum. Even dedicated palliative care patients do not have a maximum possible score.12,17 An increase in DECODE score (Fig. 3) does not necessarily indicate improved quality of care. Potential contributing factors include variation in case mix, enhanced documentation practices, inter-rater variability, and differences in case selection. These limitations should be considered when interpreting scores across sites or over time.

Providing high-quality EOLC in ICU remains a critical yet challenging task. In our cohort, the median time to death was 108 h following hospital admission and 72 h post-ICU admission. These short timeframes limit the opportunity to confirm diagnoses, trial therapies, recognise EOLC transition, and adequately prepare and communicate with families. Compared to other audit tools,12,17 DECODE emphasises communication elements over medication use. While symptom control remains crucial, ICU settings generally offer more immediate access to medication and skilled staff than palliative or hospice environments.

Retrospective chart review is dependent on the quality of clinical documentation and may yield different results from prospective, real-time audits. While all DECODE items represent important aspects of EOLC, they do not hold equal value for patients and families. Risk-adjusted scoring was not explored; however, certain domains such as items #8 and #14 (Table 1) may warrant higher allocated scores than others, such as items #18 and #19. DECODE incorporates patient- and family-centred elements but does not formally assess their perspectives as validated tools for this already exist. Medical staff conducting the audit included clinicians who had been involved in the care of reviewed patients, introducing the potential for bias. However, this limitation permitted staff education by highlighting gaps in care using objective standards rather than subjective impressions. Lastly, we did not include a comparator cohort from a dedicated palliative care unit, limiting cross-setting comparison.

Conclusion

The expectation of high quality EOLC in ICU is widely accepted, but it is not routinely audited or reported. The incorporation of palliative care principles into routine intensive care practice is integral to ensuring patients and families experience high-quality EOLC. The DECODE audit tool offers a simple, cost-effective, and user-friendly screening tool for evaluating EOLC within the acute care setting. It is suitable for both prospective and retrospective reviews and yields objective data on the quality of documentation and potential gaps in clinical care. The results can serve as an internal quality benchmark and identify specific elements of care that may benefit from targeted improvement initiatives.
