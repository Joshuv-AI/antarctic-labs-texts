Structural characterization of a novel low-molecular-weight β-fructan from Panax quinquefolius and its effect on ameliorating hyperuricemia
Authors: Jinqian Yu, Chuangchuang Wang, Min Jiang, Tao Li, Yingjian Guo, Kai Zhong, Lei Zhao, Xiao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHyperuricemia (HUA), a widespread metabolic disorder, is closely associated with renal injury, tubulointerstitial fibrosis, and uric acid nephrolithiasis, posing a significant global health burden. In this study, we investigated the anti-hyperuricemic effects and underlying mechanisms of a novel low-molecular-weight β-fructan (AGP-W) derived from Panax quinquefolius L.MethodsAGP-W was extracted from P. quinquefolius using ultra-high pressure extraction. Its structural characteristics were determined by molecular weight analysis, monosaccharide composition, and glycosidic linkage assays. Its therapeutic effects were evaluated in hyperuricemic mice by assessing serum parameters, renal transporters, intestinal barrier integrity, and gut microbiota.ResultsAGP-W was characterized as a novel low-molecular-weight β-fructan. AGP-W administration significantly and dose-dependently reduced serum uric acid, creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen levels (P < 0.01), while ameliorating lipid metabolic disorders against the hyperuricemic mouse model. Mechanistically, AGP-W restored the expression of renal urate exporters ABCG2, OAT1/3, and OCT and suppressed the expression of URAT1, GLUT9. As for its effects on the intestines, it restored intestinal barrier integrity by upregulating tight junction proteins of ZO-1, Occludin, Claudin-1, reduced villus atrophy, and modulated gut microbiota by enriching Lactobacillus and suppressing HUA-associated bacteria.ConclusionAGP-W exerts potent anti-hyperuricemic effects by modulating renal urate transporters, and restoring intestinal barrier function with gut microbiota regulation. These findings highlight AGP-W as a promising natural candidate for hyperuricemia treatment.Keywords: Panax quinquefolius, Ultra-high pressure-assisted extraction, Low-molecular-weight β-fructan, Hyperuricemia, Gut microbiota

Introduction

Hyperuricemia (HUA), a prevalent metabolic disorder driven by socioeconomic development and modern lifestyle shifts, is defined by persistently elevated serum uric acid (UA) concentrations [1], which usually leads to a bidirectional pathological association with chronic kidney disease (CKD): hyperuricemia not only accelerates CKD progression via crystal-dependent and non-crystalline mechanisms (e.g., oxidative stress and inflammation) but also frequently arises secondary to renal dysfunction due to impaired urate excretion [2,3].

Conventional pharmacotherapies for HUA including xanthine oxidase inhibitors (e.g., allopurinol) and uricosuric agents (e.g., benzbromarone) are limited by hepatorenal toxicity and gastrointestinal intolerance [4]. Consequently, increasing research attention has shifted toward safer and more sustainable bioactive compounds, particularly dietary polysaccharides derived from natural food sources, for the prevention and management of HUA [5]. The gut microbiota predominantly metabolizes polysaccharides in the intestinal tract, with the resulting microbial compositional shifts and metabolic alterations contributing to hyperuricemia regulation, indicating a reciprocal relationship between gut microbiota and HUA [6,7]. Intestinal dysbiosis contributes to renal impairment through metabolic dysregulation, with disrupted gut homeostasis accelerating renal pathology progression [8]. Concurrently, HUA-induced nephropathy exacerbates gut microbial dysbiosis via enhanced enteric urate excretion [9]. Mechanistically, microbiota-derived pathological changes promote hyperuricemia progression through compromised intestinal epithelial integrity and impaired production of beneficial metabolites, particularly short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) [10]. These findings suggest that modulating gut microbial ecology and correcting dysregulated microbial-derived metabolites could offer a novel therapeutic approach for hyperuricemia intervention.

Panax quinquefolius L. (American ginseng), a perennial herb of the Araliaceae family, represents one of the most valuable medicinal plants in global traditional medicine systems. This botanical species is particularly rich in bioactive constituents, including ginsenosides, polysaccharides, peptides, polyacetylenes, and essential trace elements [11]. Among these components, polysaccharides are primarily composed of neutral and acidic heteropolysaccharides featuring galacturonic acid, arabinose, galactose, and rhamnose residues in their backbone structures [12]. Polysaccharides from P. quinquefolius demonstrate a broad spectrum of pharmacological properties, such as immunomodulatory, anti-hyperuricemic, neuroprotective, and anti-diabetic effects [13,14]. Notably, the anti-hyperuricemic activity of P. quinquefolius polysaccharides hold particular significance for addressing contemporary health challenges related to metabolic disorders, which has been shown to correlate with polysaccharide molecular weight, degree of branching, monosaccharide composition, and the spatial configuration of bioactive motifs [15].

Ultra-high pressure extraction (UHP, 100–600 MPa) represents an emerging non-thermal technology that preserves bioactive polysaccharide structures while achieving superior extraction efficiency compared to conventional methods, as well as the enhanced total sugar contents and porous nanostructures [16,17]. Our study demonstrated that UHP-extracted polysaccharides from P. quinquefolius exhibited significantly greater hypouricemic activity than hot water reflux-extracted ones by the xanthine oxidase (XOD)-induced HK-2 cells, maybe attributed to enhanced total sugar contents, decreased molecular weight and porous nanostructures, however, it's in vivo anti-hyperuricemic activity remain unknown. Herein, this study aimed to investigate the underlying mechanisms of polysaccharides from P. quinquefolius (AGPs) in regulating HUA. However, whether AGPs mediate urate metabolism modulation through the gut-kidney axis remains unclear. To address this, the dual regulatory effects of AGPs on urate metabolism in both the kidney (the primary target organ) and the intestine (a central metabolic hub) were systematically investigated using a hypoxanthine/potassium oxonate-induced hyperuricemia (HUA) mouse model. This study focused on three key aspects: gut microbiota remodeling, assessed via 16 S rRNA gene sequencing; temporal renal redox homeostasis, evaluated by antioxidant enzyme activities including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px); and regulation of urate transporter networks, determined by analysis of ABCG2, URAT1, and GLUT9. By integrating multi-omics and molecular biology approaches, a novel “gut microbiota-oxidative stress-urate efflux transporter” axis was uncovered, through which AGPs exerted anti-HUA effects. Finally, the novel β-fructan AGP-W, obtained from Panax quinquefolius via UHP-assisted extraction ameliorated HUA by simultaneously modulating the gut microbiota, restoring renal redox homeostasis, and regulating urate transporter expression, thereby exerting anti-HUA effects.

Materials and methods

Materials and regents

Panax quinquefolius L. was obtained from Wendeng Chuanfu Ginseng Industry Co., Ltd. (Weihai, China), with the roots were 5-year-old cultivated and dried at 40°C. Potassium oxonate (PO, 98%), hypoxanthine (HX, 98%), allopurinol (AP, 98%), and sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The 20% yeast feed was supplied by Jinan Xinjian Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Jinan, China). HPLC-grade reagents including formic acid, methanol, acetonitrile, and isopropanol were acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Analytical grade chloroform, n-butanol, and petroleum ether were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). For Western blot analysis, the following antibodies were used: Nrf2, Keap1, and ABCG2 antibodies from Cell Signaling Technology; iNOS, HO-1, NQO1, GAPDH, β-actin, URAT1, ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1 antibodies from Proteintech; and GLUT9, OAT1, OAT3 and OCT2 antibodies from Abcam.

UHP-extraction and purification of polysaccharides from Panax quinquefolius

The dried roots of Panax quinquefolius (3 kg) were pulverized and extracted with distilled water (1:40, w/v) using UHP processing (500 MPa, 6 min; KeFa High Pressure Technology Co.), followed by filtration, concentration, and ethanol precipitation (80% final concentration). The collected precipitate was centrifuged (8000 ×g, 15 min), sequentially treated with petroleum ether and Sevage reagent (CHCl3:BuOH = 4:1, v/v) for delipidation and deproteinization, then dialyzed (MWCO: 3.5 kDa), pre-frozen (−20°C, 6 h), and lyophilized to obtain deproteinized polysaccharides (AGP; 340.28 g). For purification, AGP (35.0312 g) in 50 mL distilled water was loaded onto a DEAE-52 cellulose column (6.5 × 80 cm) and eluted with distilled water followed by stepwise NaCl gradients (0.1, 0.2, and 2.0 mol/L at 15 mL/min), with polysaccharide fractions monitored by the phenol-sulfuric acid method and collected according to elution profiles.

Structural analysis of AGP-W

Monosaccharide composition analysis

The monosaccharide composition of AGP-W was determined by high-performance anion-exchange chromatography with pulsed amperometric detection (HPAEC-PAD) using an ICS 5000+ system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) as described previously [18].

Molecular weight determination

The molecular weight of AGP-W was tested using a gel permeation chromatography system coupled with refractive index detection and multi-angle laser light scattering (GPC-RI-MALLS) as described previously [18].

Infrared spectral analysis

FT-IR analysis was performed using a Nicolet™ iS50 spectrometer with ATR accessory as described previously [18].

Determination of glycosidic bonds

The glycosidic linkage pattern of AGP-W was determined by an improved methylation analysis method as described previously [18].

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

The structure of AGP-W was characterized by NMR spectroscopy using a Bruker 700 MHz spectrometer (Bruker, Rheinstetten, Germany) as described previously [18]. For SEM characterization, the polysaccharides were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold. Morphological analysis was performed using a scanning electron microscope (JSM-5600LV, Kevex, USA) under high vacuum, with an acceleration voltage set at 20 kV.

Experimental animals and treatments

A total of 48 male specific pathogen-free (SPF) Kunming mice (6-8 weeks old, 18-22 g body weight) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Animal license No. SCXK (Jing) 2021-0011). The experiment was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shandong Academy of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. SDZYY20230621004) in accordance with the national standards for laboratory animal welfare (GB/T 35892-2018).

Following a 7-day acclimatization period, 36 KM mice were randomly allocated into six experimental groups (n = 6 per group): normal control group (NC), HUA model group (MOD), allopurinol treatment group (AP, 5 mg/kg), and AGP-W treatment groups (AGP-W-L, 100 mg/kg; AGP-W-M, 200 mg/kg; AGP-W-H, 400 mg/kg), respectively. Mice were housed under controlled conditions: temperature 22 ± 2°C, humidity 50 ± 5%, and a 12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. The NC group was fed normal feed and given sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC, i.g, i.p) with equal doses to potassium oxyazinate and hypoxanthine for the MOD group. The experimental protocol was modified from established methods [18].

Preparation of specimens

Following the final administration, mice were euthanized under sodium pentobarbital anesthesia (40 mg/kg). Specimens for kidneys, liver, and ileum tissues were prepared according to previous methods, as well as pathological staining [18].

Biochemical and cytokine analysis

Quantification of serum biochemical parameters was performed using commercial assay kits according to manufacturers' protocols. UA, BUN, Cr, LDL-C, HDL-C were determined with kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute. Absorbance readings were acquired using a Spark® multimode microplate reader (Tecan China, Shanghai).

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis were carried out according to previous methods [18].

Gut microbiota analysis

Gut microbiota analysis were carried out according to previous methods [18].

Measurement of short-chain fatty acids

Measurement of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) were carried out according to previous methods [18].

Measurement of concentration-time profiles for AGP-W

AGP-W was labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) according to the previous methods [19] to obatin AGP-W-F, with the 1H NMR spectrum shown in the supplementary data Fig. S3. The rats were fasted for 12 h before the experiment, but they couldn't stop drinking. AGP-W-F was dissolved in normal saline and then administered orally. The animals were randomly divided into two groups (each group with six rats), in which the first group was given normal saline, the second group was given AGP-W-F solution by gavage with a dose of 200 mg/kg. Blood was collected at 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 6.0 and 8.0 h post-administration. About 300 μL blood from tail vein was centrifuged at 3500 r/min for 20 min to obtain plasma. Then 200 μL supernatant was diluted to 2.5 mL with PBS. Fluorescence intensity was measured (Ex = 495 nm, Em = 525 nm, slit width 2 nm).

Data analysis

GraphPad Prism 8.0 software was applied for statistical analysis, with data presented as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was followed by Tukey's post-hoc test for comparisons between the two compared groups with P ≤ 0.05 for statistical significance.

Results

UHP-extraction and isolation of polysaccharides from P. quinquefolius

The extraction conditions for UHP-assisted extraction of AGPs were systematically optimized using a Box-Behnken design (BBD)-based response surface methodology (RSM) (Table S1). The optimal UHP extraction conditions were determined to be a pressure of 400 MPa, a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:40 (w/v), and a holding time of 6 min. Under these conditions, the AGP extraction yield reached 18.5% ± 0.1%, which was notably higher than the 14.97% yield obtained through conventional reflux extraction methods reported in previous studies. These results suggested that the UHP extraction process developed in this study markedly enhances the extraction efficiency of AGPs. This enhancement could be attributed to the synergistic effects of UHP. It not only induced rapid cell wall disruption through high-pressure-induced permeabilization, facilitating the release of polysaccharides from the plant matrix [20], but also promoted mechanical stress that disrupts hydrogen bonds within cellulose–hemicellulose networks [21]. In addition, the pressure-driven infiltration of solvent into plant tissues improved mass transfer [22]. These factors collectively contributed to the higher extraction efficiency observed.

FT-IR spectroscopic analysis of AGP-W

The FT-IR spectrum of AGP-W (Fig. 2B) revealed characteristic absorption bands at 3385 cm−1 (broad peak, O-H stretching vibration of polysaccharides, indicating abundant hydroxyl groups responsible for hydrophilicity), 2933 cm−1 (C-H stretching vibration of pyranose rings), 1651 cm−1 (characteristic C=O stretching vibration of plant polysaccharides), and 1044 cm−1 (C-O stretching vibration confirming the presence of β/α-glycosidic linkages). All observed absorption bands were consistent with reported structural features of polysaccharides [21,22].

NMR structural elucidation of AGP-W

The comprehensive NMR analysis of AGP-W revealed a complex fructoglucan structure containing both fructofuranose and glucopyranose moieties. In the 13C NMR spectrum (Fig. 2C), three characteristic fructofuranose C-2 signals were observed at 103.66, 103.61, and 103.19 ppm, designated as fragments A, B, and C respectively. Through combined analysis of 1H NMR (Fig. 2D), COSY (Fig. 2E), HSQC (Fig. 2F), and HMBC (Fig. 2G) spectra, these fragments were identified as β-D-Fruf-(2→ (A) [24], →1)-β-D-Fruf-(2→ (B) [25], and →1,6)-β-D-Fruf-(2→ (C) [24], with complete 1H and 13C signal assignments for each position. Additionally, three glucopyranose residues (D, E, F) were detected through their distinct anomeric signals at δH/C 4.95/98.03, 4.67/95.85, and 5.41/92.12. Fragment E was confirmed as →4,6)-β-D-Glcp-(1→ [26] based on its characteristic β-anomeric proton (δH 4.67) and the downfield shifted C-4 (δC 75.92), while fragments D (→4)-α-D-Glcp-(1→) [27] and F (t-α-D-Glcp-(1→) [28] were identified as α-anomers through their higher anomeric proton chemical shifts (δH 4.97 and 5.42 respectively), specific data were shown in Table 2. The complete signal assignments for all fragments, including their ring proton and carbon resonances, provided strong evidence for the proposed structure, with all observed chemical shifts being consistent with literature values for similar fructoglucans. This detailed structural elucidation demonstrated AGP-W to be a branched heteropolysaccharide containing both (2 → 1)-linked β-fructofuranose chains and α/β-glucopyranose residues with varying linkage patterns ((Fig. 2H).

Structure change of AGP-W by UHP-extraction

The surface morphology and spatial conformation of P. quinquefolius polysaccharide were examined via scanning electron microscopy before and after UHP treatment, revealing notable structural alterations (Fig. S2). Prior to UHP extraction, the polysaccharide exhibited a relatively smooth, flat surface with a compact flaky architecture. Following treatment at 500 MPa, this sheet-like structure fragmented into smaller pieces. The disruption of intermolecular crosslinking led to partial decomposition of the network structure, accompanied by localized aggregation. These morphological changes may account for the observed reduction in the molecular weight of AGP-W.

Significant structural differences were observed between AGP-W and polysaccharides obtained by conventional hot-water decoction. UHP treatment yielded a β-fructan primarily consisting of repeating (→1)-β-D-Fruf-(2 → 1,6)-β-D-Fruf-(2→) units with lower molecular weight, while previously reported hot-water extracts were predominantly glucans composed of glucose, mannose, galactose, and arabinose, with α-1→4 glycosidic linkages forming the main chain with higher molecular weight [29]. These differences were attributable to the distinct physicochemical mechanisms inherent to the extraction methods. UHP processing generated mechanical stress capable of disrupting cell wall and membrane integrity, facilitating the release of cell wall-bound fructans [30]. In contrast, hot-water extraction relied on thermal solubilization, which more effectively extracts α-1→4-linked glucans embedded within the cell wall matrix [31]. Furthermore, fructans were typically stored within vacuoles, and UHP may compromise vacuolar membranes, facilitating the rapid and efficient exudation of β-(2 → 1)-linked fructans while simultaneously inducing the selective depolymerization of native high molecular weight fractions. This controlled scission, likely attributable to the forced disruption of hydrogen bonding and the alteration of chain conformation under extreme pressure, resulted in a more defined and lower molecular weight distribution. The acquisition of AGP-W was of paramount physiological significance, as the reduced molecular size and enhanced solubility are strongly correlated with improved bioavailability and a greater abundance of accessible terminal glycosidic bonds and reactive groups.

Overall, the observed compositional variation underscored the critical role of extraction methodology in determining the structural and functional properties of plant-derived polysaccharides.

Effects of AGP on body weight and serum biochemical parameters in HUA mice

The HUA mice model was successfully established using a 15-day induction protocol with PO, HX, and a 20% yeast extract diet. As shown in Fig. 3A, the experimental design included NC group, MOD group, AP group, and low-middle-high AGP-W treatment group. During the experimental period, compared to the NC group, mice in the MOD and AP groups exhibited a declining trend in body weight, whereas no significant difference was observed between the AGP-W group and the NC group (Fig. 3B). These results suggested that the hyperuricemia modeling procedure and positive drug intervention may induce physiological alterations in mice, while the AGP-W treatment regimen showed no significant weight suppression under the current experimental conditions. The MOD group showed significantly elevated serum UA levels compared to NC (Fig. 3C, P < 0.01), confirming successful HUA induction, while both AGP and allopurinol treatment effectively normalized UA levels. Furthermore, the MOD group exhibited increased serum BUN, Cre and ADA levels (Fig. 3D–G P < 0.01), suggesting HUA-induced renal dysfunction, which was ameliorated by all AGP doses (P < 0.01), demonstrating its renal protective potential. Analysis of serum lipid profiles revealed that the MOD group had significantly higher LDL-C (Fig. 3G, P < 0.01) and lower HDL-C levels (Fig. 3H, P < 0.01) compared to NC, with AGP-W treatment dose-dependently reversing these abnormalities, indicating its regulatory effect on lipid metabolism in HUA mice. These findings demonstrated that AGP effectively reduces serum UA and BUN levels while improving dyslipidemia in a dose-dependent manner, suggesting its therapeutic potential against hyperuricemia and associated metabolic disturbances.

AGP-W attenuates hyperuricemia-induced pathological damage in renal

Histopathological analysis via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed that mice in the MOD group exhibited characteristic hyperuricemia-associated renal injury, manifested by renal tubular dilation, epithelial cell desquamation, and interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 4A). In contrast, renal tissue architecture in the AGP-W treated group remained largely intact, with preserved tubular morphology and significantly reduced inflammatory infiltration, indicating a marked nephroprotective effect. Notably, the AP group failed to exhibit pathological improvements compared to MOD-treated mice, a finding consistent with previous reports on the limited organoprotective efficacy of chemically synthesized drugs [32]. Collectively, these data emphasized AGP-W's superior capacity to mitigate target organ damage while circumventing the multi-organ toxicities inherent to conventional therapeutic approaches, thereby positioning it as a promising candidate for gout-associated renal protection.

To uncover the molecular mechanism underlying AGP-W's regulation of uric acid metabolism, the expression levels of crucial urate transporters in the kidney were comprehensively evaluated. Western blot analysis (Fig. 4B–H) demonstrated that, when compared to the NC group, the MOD group exhibited a significant down-regulation of the renal urate efflux transporters ABCG2, OAT3, OCT2, and OAT1 (P < 0.01). Conversely, the reabsorption-related transporters GLUT9 and URAT1 were notably upregulated. These changes suggested an imbalance in the dynamic equilibrium of renal tubular urate excretion and reabsorption. Pharmacodynamic assessment indicated that high-dose AGP-W treatment effectively reversed the abnormal expression profiles of these transporters (P < 0.01). Remarkably, pretreatment with AGP-W led to a dose-dependent upregulation of ABCG2 and OATs, as well as a downregulation of GLUT9 and URAT1, which implied that AGP-W exerted a coordinated, multi-target regulatory influence to restablish renal urate metabolic homeostasis. These results provided molecular-level proof that the urate lowering effect of AGP-W was strongly correlated with its targeted regulation of urate transporter expression in renal tubular epithelial cells.

AGP-W protects intestinal barrier in hyperuricemia

The intestinal barrier, serving as a critical pathway for uric acid excretion, has been recognized as a key pathological feature in HUA [33]. H&E analysis (Fig. 5A) demonstrated that MOD group mice exhibited significant structural abnormalities in intestinal villi compared with NC group, characterized by local edema, collapsed atrophy, reduced villus height, and marked inflammatory cell infiltration. Following AGP-W intervention, these pathological alterations showed dose-dependent amelioration, with the high-dose AGP-W group (AGP-W-H) exhibiting nearly complete restoration of intestinal villus architecture. Regarding intestinal barrier function evaluation, protein expression levels of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1 in MOD group were significantly downregulated compared to NC group (P < 0.05), while AGP-W treatment dose-dependently upregulated these crucial tight junction proteins across all dosage groups (Fig. 5B–E). Notably, the abnormal elevation of intestinal permeability corresponded with these pathological manifestations. These findings collectively demonstrated that AGP-W effectively improves intestinal barrier function in HUA mice through dual mechanisms: restoring intestinal mucosal architecture and upregulating tight junction protein expression.

Effect of AGP-W on gut microbiota homeostasis in HUA mice

To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we performed 16 S rRNA sequencing analysis on fecal samples from AGP-W, NC, and MOD groups to evaluate the impact of AGP-W pretreatment on gut microbiota in HUA mice. Core/Pan species analysis (Fig. 6A–B) confirmed that the sequencing data volume and depth met the requirements for subsequent analysis. Venn diagram (Fig. 6C) revealed distinct operational taxonomic unit (OUT) distribution patterns among group of NC (1067 OTUs), MOD (825 OTUs), and AGP-W (1048 OTUs), which contained 298 (19.57%), 108 (7.09%), and 229 (15.04%) unique OTUs, respectively, suggesting AGP-W intervention significantly reshaped microbial composition. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distance (Fig. 6D) demonstrated clear separation trends among groups' microbial communities. α-Diversity analysis showed significant reductions in Ace, Chao, Shannon, and Sobs indices in MOD group compared to NC group (P < 0.01), while AGP-W treatment effectively restored species richness (Fig. 6E–H). Phylum-level analysis revealed that AGP-W group's microbiota structure closely resembled NC group, with MOD group exhibiting decreased Bacteroidota and increased Firmicutes abundances-both abnormalities were significantly reversed by AGP-W intervention (Fig. 6I). Genus-level analysis demonstrated that AGP-W group displayed significantly elevated Lactobacillus and Bacteroides abundances compared to MOD group, along with reduced norank-f-Muribaculaceae and norank-o-Clostridia-UCG-014 abundances (Fig. 6J). Heatmap analysis (Fig. 6K) further confirmed AGP-W group established a distinct microbial clustering pattern compared to MOD group, indicating its potential to ameliorate HUA progression through gut microbiota modulation.

To further explore the correlation between gut microbiota and HUA pathology, Spearman rank correlation analysis at the genus level was conducted (Fig. 6L), revealing that Lactobacillus had significant negative correlations with XOD, URAT1, and GLUT9 expressions (P < 0.01), while showing strong positive correlations with OAT1, ABCG2, and intestinal barrier proteins of ZO-1, Occludin, Claudin-1. In contrast, norank-f-Lachnospiraceae displayed opposing trends, positively associating with XOD, URAT1, and GLUT9 but negatively with OAT1, ABCG2, and barrier proteins. Observably, serum UA levels were significantly inversely correlated with the abundance of Bacteroides, Dubosiella, and Parabacteroides (P < 0.01). Experimental validation further demonstrated that AGP-W intervention significantly reduced UA levels compared to the MOD group (P < 0.01), concomitant with downregulated URAT1/GLUT9 expression and upregulated Occludin, ZO-1, and Claudin-1. These phenotypic alterations aligned with a marked increase in Lactobacillus abundance, suggesting its therapeutic potential in HUA via modulation of the URAT1/GLUT9 axis and intestinal barrier enhancement. Lactobacillus strains have been shown to significantly modulate the expression of key urate transporters involved in both reabsorption and excretion. It is reported that Lactiplantibacillus plantarum AR342 significantly reduces serum UA in HUA mice by modulating URAT1 and GLUT9 together with ABCG2 and OAT1 [34]. Similarly, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum WLPL04 reduces serum UA by upregulating ABCG2 while downregulating URAT1 and GLUT9 expression in a dose-dependent manner [35]. Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus Fmb14 reduces serum UA partly through promoting ABCG2 expression in the colon [36]. Moreover, GLUT9 is also expressed in enterocytes, where its deficiency leads to impaired uric acid transport, hyperuricemia, and metabolic syndrome in mice. These findings suggested that Lactobacillus enrichment may simultaneously modulate urate transporter networks in both renal and intestinal compartments to rebalance systemic UA homeostasis. As a probiotic genus, Lactobacillus may ameliorate HUA by restoring microbial homeostasis, optimizing nutrient metabolism, and attenuating gut inflammation.

AGP-W modulates SCFA profiles in hyperuricemic mice

SCFAs have been demonstrated to exert multiple beneficial effects on HUA and CKD [37]. To further investigate the impact of AGP-W pretreatment on SCFAs production, the contents of acetic acid, propanoic acid, isobutyric acid, butanoic acid, isovaleric acid, valeric acid, isohexanoic acid, and hexanoic acid in the fecal were measured (Fig. 7A). As shown in Fig. 7B, elevated UA levels significantly reduced total SCFAs content, while AGP-W intervention reversed this alteration (P < 0.05). Regarding specific SCFAs subtypes (Fig. 7C), compared with the NC group, HUA mice exhibited significantly decreased levels of acetic acid, butanoic acid, and propanoic acid (P < 0.01). Notably, high-dose AGP-W treatment contributed to the recovery of acetic acid levels.

Notably, the modulation of gut microbiota by AGP-W appeared to directly influence SCFAs production. As shown above, AGP-W pretreatment significantly increased the abundance of beneficial genera such as Lactobacillus and Bacteroides, both of which were well-documented SCFAs producers [38]. The elevated levels of acetic acid, butanoic acid, and propanoic acid observed in the AGP-W group may therefore be attributed to the proliferation of these taxa. These SCFAs are known to play pivotal roles in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, regulating immune responses, and modulating host metabolism—all of which are relevant to HUA pathophysiology [39]. Furthermore, Spearman correlation analysis supported this association, indicating that SCFAs-producing genera negatively correlated with serum uric acid levels and positively with intestinal barrier markers such as ZO-1 and Occludin [40]. Together, these findings suggested that AGP-W ameliorates hyperuricemia not only through direct regulation of urate transporters but also via reshaping microbial composition to favor SCFAs biosynthesis, thereby enhancing gut barrier function and mitigating systemic inflammation.

Discussion

HUA as a globally prevalent metabolic disorder with increasing incidence, has emerged as a significant public health challenge. In the present study, we systematically elucidated the structure, extraction efficiency, and multifunctional bioactivities of a novel lower molecular weight β-fructan fraction (AGP-W) obtained from Panax quinquefolius via UHP extraction. Our findings underscored the advantages of UHP technology in polysaccharide extraction, not only in terms of yield improvement but also in structural diversification, which underpins its potent bioactivities against HUA and associated metabolic disturbances.

UHP extraction significantly outperformed traditional reflux methods, achieving a polysaccharide yield of 18.5% under optimized conditions (400 MPa, 1:40 w/v, 6 min), compared to 14.97% reported previously under conventional extraction. This improvement was attributed to the UHP-induced mechanical disruption of plant cell walls and membranes, which facilitated the release of vacuolar-stored fructans and enhances solvent penetration [41]. This was consistent with prior studies reporting increased extraction yields of bioactive constituents under high hydrostatic pressure [42].

Comprehensive structural analysis revealed that AGP-W was a branched heteropolysaccharide composed predominantly of (→1)- and (→1,6)-linked β-D-fructofuranose residues with minor α/β-D-glucopyranose residues. Methylation and NMR spectroscopy confirmed its unique fructoglucan architecture, which differed markedly from previously reported hot-water-extracted polysaccharides. The hot-water-extracted polysaccharides from P. quinquefolius were reported as high-molecular-weight neutral and acidic heteropolysaccharides, composed primarily of glucose, mannose, and galactose with α-1,4 linkages. Their documented bioactivities focused primarily on immunomodulation, neuroprotection, anti-inflammatory activity, and so on. For example, AGC1, a 5.2 kDa neutral polysaccharide from P. quinquefolius by hot-water-extraction composed of 60% galactose with major linkages of 3-Galp and 3,6-Galp, showed immunomodulation activity via the NF-κB pathway [43]. In contrast, the UHP-derived AGP-W demonstrated targeted, potent anti-hyperuricemic and nephroprotective efficacy through the coordinated modulation of renal urate transporters, intestinal barrier restoration, and gut microbiota remodeling. The β-(2 → 1,6)-fructofuranosyl linkages in AGP-W enhanced its resistance to gastrointestinal degradation [44], facilitating its function as a prebiotic to enrich Lactobacillus and Bacteroides. Thus, the UHP extraction technology unlocked a structurally and functionally novel polysaccharide fraction from P. quinquefolius, expanding the medicinal application of this botanical from general wellness to precise metabolic intervention against HUA.

Functionally, AGP-W exhibited a robust anti-hyperuricemic effect in HUA mice, with efficacy comparable to that of allopurinol but without its associated weight loss or histopathological toxicity. AGP-W significantly reduced serum UA, BUN, creatinine, and ADA levels in a dose-dependent manner, indicating potent nephroprotective effects. These improvements were accompanied by restored renal histological architecture and normalization of lipid profiles, highlighting its systemic benefits in HUA pathology.

Mechanistic investigations revealed that AGP-W ameliorated HUA through modulation of key renal urate transporters. Specifically, AGP-W restored the expression of urate efflux transporters (ABCG2, OAT1/3, OCT2) and suppressed the reabsorption-related transporters (URAT1, GLUT9), thereby re-establishing renal urate excretion homeostasis. Notably, this coordinated multi-target modulation underlied the superior efficacy of AGP-W compared to monotherapeutic agents like allopurinol, which typically target xanthine oxidase activity alone.

In addition to its systemic effects, AGP-W preserved intestinal barrier integrity, as evidenced by histological improvement and upregulated expression of tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Occludin, Claudin-1). Given that approximately one-third of uric acid is excreted via the gastrointestinal tract, maintenance of intestinal barrier function is vital in preventing systemic urate accumulation and associated inflammation [45]. AGP-W's beneficial effects on the gut barrier therefore contributed substantially to its antihyperuricemic action. 16 S rRNA sequencing revealed that AGP-W treatment significantly restored gut microbial diversity and composition in HUA mice. The observed increase in Bacteroidota and decrease in Firmicutes reversed the dysbiotic trends seen in the HUA model, aligning with reports that microbial dysbiosis is tightly linked to metabolic and renal dysfunctions [46]. AGP-W notably enriched the abundance of Lactobacillus and Bacteroides—well-established probiotic genera—while reducing potentially harmful taxa such as Clostridia-UCG-014 and Muribaculaceae. These shifts contributed to reduced inflammation, improved nutrient metabolism, and enhanced SCFAs production. Indeed, fecal SCFAs analysis confirmed that AGP-W promoted the biosynthesis of key metabolites such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate. These SCFAs have been shown to strengthen the gut barrier, inhibit NF-κB signaling, and modulate host energy metabolism [47], making their restoration particularly relevant in HUA pathophysiology. The positive correlation between SCFA-producing genera and intestinal tight junction proteins supported the hypothesis that AGP-W exerted part of its therapeutic effect via microbiota-derived metabolite pathways [48]. Moreover, correlation analysis demonstrated that beneficial genera such as Lactobacillus negatively correlated with serum UA, URAT1, and GLUT9 expression while positively correlating with barrier markers and urate efflux transporters, establishing a gut–kidney axis mechanism for AGP-W's activity. This interplay further emphasizes the holistic therapeutic potential of polysaccharides as multitarget agents capable of modulating host-microbiota interactions, epithelial integrity, and molecular transport systems simultaneously [49]. In vivo absorption studies of AGP-W showed a peak blood concentration of 4.25 μg/mL at 1 h, which was similar to data reported for polysaccharides in the literature. This indicated that although AGP-W, as a low-molecular-weight β-fructan, possessed better transmembrane transport capacity compared to high-molecular-weight polysaccharides, however it still exhibited low oral bioavailability. This suggested that its primary target of action didn't direct entry into the bloodstream, but rather local regulation within the intestine, which is highly consistent with our previous findings that AGP-W significantly remodelled the gut microbiota, enriching Lactobacillus, increasing short-chain fatty acid production, and restoring intestinal barrier function. Therefore, the anti-hyperuricemic effect of AGP-W may rely more on indirect regulation via the gut-kidney axis than on direct absorption into the blood followed by action on distal organs.

In conclusion, our findings highlighted AGP-W as a structurally unique low molecular weight fructoglucan polysaccharide with multifaceted protective effects against hyperuricemia and its complications. Through a combination of urate transporter modulation, intestinal barrier protection, and gut microbiota remodeling, AGP-W demonstrated comprehensive efficacy in mitigating HUA pathology. Importantly, the distinct structural features conferred by UHP extraction played a pivotal role in elucidating its unique capability to generate structurally optimized, low molecular weight polysaccharides with amplified biofunctional potential, offering a promising platform for the development of high-value nutraceuticals and functional food ingredients.

While the present study compellingly demonstrated the multifaceted anti-hyperuricemic efficacy of AGP-W through the gut-kidney axis, certain limitations still exist, namely the absence of a comprehensive metabolomics study to trace the in vivo metabolic fate and biotransformation pathways of this specific polysaccharide, which remained unknown in understanding its ultimate bioactive forms. Furthermore, the precise structure-activity relationships, particularly how its unique UHP-derived β-(2 → 1,6)-fructofuranosyl linkages dictated its multi-target pharmacological effects, warranted deeper mechanistic decoding. Future research should not only integrate host and microbial metabolomics to elucidate the dynamic interplay between AGP-W, its microbial-derived metabolites, and systemic urate homeostasis, but also conduct long-term toxicological evaluations and human clinical trials to validate its safety and translational efficacy. The in vivo behavior of polysaccharides remained insufficiently understood, and further studies are needed to investigate their distribution in various tissues, among other aspects, so as to provide direction for subsequent in-depth research. Ultimately, leveraging the superior extraction efficiency and structural optimization conferred by UHP technology, AGP-W holds immense promise to be developed as a high-value, multi-target nutraceutical or functional food ingredient for the management of hyperuricemia and associated metabolic disorders.
