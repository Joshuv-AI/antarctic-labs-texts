The Effect of Maternal Pertussis Vaccination on Neonatal Health Outcomes in the Dutch Pregnancy Drug Register
Authors: Petra J Woestenberg, Veronique Y F Maas, Maud de Feijter, Leontine van Balveren, Maartje Conijn, Sanne Boetzkes, Anneke J L M Passier, Agnes C Kant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe currently high pertussis‐related morbidity and mortality among infants has led to increased international attention for pertussis vaccination during pregnancy. We studied the safety of maternal pertussis vaccination in the Netherlands with respect to neonatal health.MethodsData from the cohort the Dutch Pregnancy Drug Register were used. Pregnant participants self‐reported their pertussis vaccination and the health outcomes of their offspring. Participants with a singleton live birth ≥ 24 weeks gestation were included. Using log‐binomial regression analysis and correction for confounders, we studied the risk of maternal pertussis vaccination on various neonatal health outcomes. Moreover, we compared differences in health outcomes between second and third trimester vaccinations.ResultsThe study population included 10,280 participants (74.8% vaccinated against pertussis during pregnancy and 25.3% not). The adjusted risk ratio (RR) was: 1.01 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.82–1.23) for small for gestational age; 0.90 (95% CI: 0.73–1.11) for large for gestational age; 0.86 (95% CI: 0.59–1.25) for low birth weight; 1.06 (95% CI: 0.90–1.25) for neonatal health problems, and 0.68 (95% CI: 0.47–0.99) for infant hospitalization due to (suspicion of) infection. Also for the secondary outcomes neonatal death, low Apgar score, and neonatal intensive care unit admission, no increased risk after maternal pertussis vaccination was observed. There was no difference in neonatal health outcomes between second and third trimester pertussis vaccinations.ConclusionsThe maternal pertussis vaccination did not increase the risk of several adverse neonatal health outcomes, confirming the safety of pertussis vaccination during pregnancy with respect to neonatal health outcomes.Keywords: Boostrix, immunization, infant health, pregnancy, safety, Tdap, whooping cough

Introduction

Pertussis, also known as whooping cough, is a respiratory infection caused by the highly contagious bacteria Bordetella pertussis . Especially infants under 2 to 3 months of age are at high risk for severe disease, hospitalization, and even death due to pertussis (Jones and Heath 2014; Kandeil et al. 2020). The incidence of pertussis cases has been increasing globally. The currently high morbidity and mortality following pertussis infections among infants has led to increased international attention for pertussis vaccination during pregnancy (Khalil et al. 2024; Principi et al. 2024).

Maternal pertussis vaccination provides protection to the infant, following the transplacental transfer of maternal antibodies (Malek et al. 1996; Nguyen et al. 2022). Despite the proven high effectiveness of maternal pertussis vaccination against serious pertussis infections among infants in the first months after birth (Kandeil et al. 2020), in many countries the vaccination coverage remains limited (Khalil et al. 2024; Principi et al. 2024).

In the Netherlands, maternal pertussis vaccination has been in place since the end of 2019. Since there is no pertussis‐only vaccine, the tetanus, diphtheria, and acellular pertussis (Tdap) vaccine (without polio) is used. Next to pertussis toxoid, filamentous hemagglutinin, and pertactin, the Tdap vaccine also contains diphtheria and tetanus toxoid and the non‐medicinal ingredients aluminium (as aluminium salts), sodium chloride, and water for injection (GlaxoSmithKline Inc, n.d.). Vaccination is recommended from gestational week 22 onwards to also provide some protection to infants born prematurely. To maximize vaccine uptake, the vaccination can be given until the end of pregnancy (Fröberg et al. 2020). The maternal pertussis vaccination coverage in the Netherlands is around 60%–70% (van Lier et al. 2024).

Previous research has demonstrated no safety concerns for maternal pertussis vaccination (Clark and Johnson 2023), but the advised timing of vaccination during pregnancy highly differs between countries (De Weerdt et al. 2024). The majority of the previous research focused on third trimester vaccinations (De Weerdt et al. 2024; Vygen‐Bonnet et al. 2020), while in the Netherlands, maternal pertussis vaccination is recommended in the second trimester of pregnancy. The safety of the Dutch maternal pertussis vaccination program on infant health outcomes has not been studied extensively. In one previous study, adverse neonatal health outcomes among a limited number of vaccinated individuals were compared to a historical cohort from before the national introduction of the maternal pertussis vaccination program. In this study, no differences were found between small for gestational age (SGA) and a composite outcome of adverse pregnancy outcomes between vaccinated individuals and presumably unvaccinated individuals (Immink et al. 2023). Information addressing safety is important for pregnant individuals for their decision‐making on vaccination (Razai et al. 2024; Hansen et al. 2024).

In this paper, we studied the safety of maternal pertussis vaccination in the Netherlands with respect to neonatal health outcomes using data from the Dutch Pregnancy Drug Register (DPDR). The main aim was to study the risk of adverse neonatal health outcomes after pertussis vaccination during pregnancy compared to no pertussis vaccination during pregnancy. The secondary aim was to study the difference in risk of adverse neonatal health outcomes after pertussis vaccination in the second trimester compared to the third trimester of pregnancy.

Methods

Study Design

For the current research question, we used data from the DPDR. The DPDR is an ongoing register that is designed as a cohort where participants complete questionnaires at baseline and during follow‐up. The DPDR aims to study drug safety during pregnancy and breastfeeding. All pregnant individuals aged 18 years and older who master the Dutch language are eligible to participate. The study started in 2014. In 2021 it underwent a relaunch with a new Dutch name. Moreover, in 2021 the study changed to a different digital platform using a comparable study protocol but with slight adaptations in the questionnaire, resulting in a cohort before and after 2021. Details of the study protocol have been published previously (Vorstenbosch et al. 2019). Briefly: participants receive a maximum of three online questionnaires during pregnancy and three postpartum. The first questionnaire is administered at enrolment, the second at gestational week 17 (if applicable), the third at gestational week 34, and the last three at 2, 6, and 12 months after the due date. Topics of the questionnaires include: general health, lifestyle, drug and vaccine exposure, course of pregnancy, childbirth, and child health.

The DPDR follows ethical standards. Because the study collects data by means of questionnaires only, the Regional Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects Arnhem‐Nijmegen judged in 2013 that the study does not require specific ethical approval (protocol number 2013/259). In 2022, the Medical Ethical Committee Brabant judged this was still applicable (protocol number NW2022‐41). All participants provided informed consent digitally.

Study Population

For the current research question, we included all participants who participated in the DPDR with a due date before 01‐01‐2024 and who had a singleton birth after at least 24 gestational weeks. Using a Fisher exact test, we compared the proportion of stillbirths (including intrauterine fetal death) between participants who were vaccinated against pertussis during pregnancy and participants who were not. For further analyses, stillbirths were excluded because for these infants neonatal outcomes were missing. The due date was based on reported ultrasound results and was used to calculate the gestational week at any moment in time. We excluded participants for whom it was unknown if they were vaccinated against pertussis during pregnancy and participants for whom primary outcome variables were missing. For the secondary research question, we additionally excluded participants with an unknown moment of vaccination.

Exposure Definitions

A participant was considered exposed if a pertussis‐containing vaccination between gestational week 13 + 0 and the end of pregnancy was reported (Rijksinstituut voor volksgezondheid en milieu [RIVM], n.d.). The type of vaccine was not asked. The timing of vaccination could be reported as a date or a gestational week. A vaccination between gestational week 13 + 0 and 26 + 6 was defined as a second trimester vaccination. A vaccination from gestational week 27 + 0 onwards was defined as a third trimester vaccination. The questions used to define the exposures are listed in Table S1.

Primary Outcome Definitions

The following adverse neonatal health outcomes were included as primary outcomes: SGA at birth, large for gestational age (LGA) at birth, low birth weight, neonatal health problems, and infant hospitalization due to (suspicion of) infection. All outcomes were analyzed based on the information provided by the participants. The questions used to define the outcomes are listed in Table S1.

SGA and LGA at Birth

The outcomes SGA and LGA at birth were based on the reported date of birth, sex, and birth weight of the child and the Dutch reference birth weights (Perined, n.d.). SGA was defined as a birth weight below the 10th percentile and LGA was defined as a birth weight above the 90th percentile taking into account gestational age in days and sex of the child.

Low Birth Weight

A birth weight below 2500 g was defined as low birth weight.

Neonatal Health Problems

In the first postpartum questionnaire, participants were specifically asked if the following health conditions occurred shortly after birth: problems with breathing; problems with glucose levels; hypo‐ or hypertonia; infant jaundice requiring phototherapy; and/or (possible) infection. Infants were classified as having neonatal health problems if at least one of these pre‐defined health problems was reported by the participant.

Hospitalization due to Infection

A hospitalization due to infection was defined as a hospitalization of the infant due to an infection or a suspicion of an infection, within 1 month after birth.

Secondary Outcome Definitions

We also included the health outcomes neonatal death, low Apgar score, neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission, and major non‐genetic congenital anomalies. These outcomes were included as secondary/explorative outcomes because of low numbers (neonatal death), many missing values (low Apgar score), or because the outcome was unavailable for participants included before 2021 (NICU admission and major non‐genetic congenital anomalies). The questions used to define the secondary outcomes are listed in Table S1.

If the infant died within 28 days after birth this was classified as neonatal death. A low Apgar score was defined as an Apgar score of < 7 at 5 min after birth. NICU admission included hospitalizations of the infant at the NICU within 1 month after birth. A major non‐genetic congenital anomaly was defined as a major congenital anomaly with no known genetic basis, based on the European network of population‐based registries for the epidemiological surveillance of congenital anomalies (EUROCAT) classification reported in any of the postpartum questionnaires.

Covariables

Covariables that were included in the analyses were: maternal age at due date; paternal age at due date; maternal and paternal education level; maternal and paternal self‐reported ethnicity (included because of the association between birth weight and ethnic background (McCowan and Horgan 2009)); urbanity based on zip code (Centraal Bureau voor de Statistiek [Statistics Netherlands] (CBS) 2021); pre‐pregnancy body mass index (BMI); obstetric history of SGA or LGA; obstetric history of gestational diabetes or hypertension; alcohol use during pregnancy, smoking behavior during pregnancy, and illicit drug use in the 3 months before or during pregnancy; pregnancy duration; gestational diabetes or hypertension in the current pregnancy; and chronic comorbidity. Chronic comorbidity included epilepsy; diabetes mellitus; respiratory diseases; diseases of the thyroid gland; cardiac diseases; disorders of the digestive tract; kidney diseases; disorders of the genital organs; disorders of muscles, joints or bones; skin diseases, mental disorders; migraine; and/or cancer. Maternal influenza and COVID‐19 vaccination were also taken into account. All variables were self‐reported and involve the pregnant participant unless stated otherwise. Pregnancy duration was included as a continuous variable and the other variables were categorized.

Statistical Analyses

The prevalence of the different neonatal health outcomes was calculated by maternal pertussis vaccination status including the Wilson score 95% confidence interval (CI).

To study the risk of adverse neonatal health outcomes after maternal pertussis vaccination, we used log‐binomial regression analyses to calculate the risk ratio (RR), where we compared participants who were vaccinated against pertussis during pregnancy with participants who were not (reference). In model 0, we adjusted for the study cohort (up to 2021 or after 2021) only. This model 0 served as the crude model. In model 1, we also took the study cohort into account and additionally corrected for potential confounding using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores and stabilized weights (Austin 2011). Propensity scores were calculated using logistic regression analyses with maternal pertussis vaccination as an outcome and the potential confounders as independent variables. Potential confounders were selected a‐priori and included all variables described above except for influenza and COVID‐19 vaccination. A Standardize Mean Difference (SMD) of < 0.1 was used to define adequate balance, which was reached for all confounders after IPTW (Table S2). In model 2, we additionally adjusted for any influenza or COVID‐19 vaccination during pregnancy.

Missing values in the covariables were handled with multiple imputation (5 imputation sets and 20 iterations) using the MICE R package. We used predictive mean matching for the continuous variables and logistic regression for the binary variables (van Buuren and Groothuis‐Oudshoorn 2011).

To study the risk of adverse neonatal health outcomes by trimester of pertussis vaccination, we repeated the analyses where we compared participants who were vaccinated against pertussis during the second trimester of pregnancy with participants who were vaccinated against pertussis during the third trimester of pregnancy (reference).

To study the robustness of our findings, we performed six sensitivity analyses. In sensitivity analysis 1, we excluded preterm deliveries (< 37 weeks gestational age) because of their association with adverse neonatal health outcomes. In sensitivity analysis 2, we excluded neonatal deaths. In sensitivity analysis 3, we excluded participants who were vaccinated against influenza or COVID‐19 during pregnancy from the exposed and the reference group to solely study the effect of the pertussis vaccination during pregnancy. In sensitivity analysis 4, we only included participants included after 2021. In sensitivity analysis 5, we only included participants included before 2021. In sensitivity analysis 6, we changed the timeframe of the outcome infant hospitalization due to (suspicion of) infection to 1 year. In this analysis, we only included participants with complete follow‐up. Propensity scores and stabilized weights were recalculated and checked for balance using the SMD for all sensitivity analyses. We acknowledged that performing multiple statistical analyses increases the risk of a type I error. The primary outcomes were predefined and analyzed according to a prespecified statistical analysis plan. The sensitivity analyses were considered exploratory.

For most congenital anomalies, the first trimester is the most sensitive period for harmful exposure, which is before the exposure window for the maternal pertussis vaccination (generally after gestational week 22 + 0) (DeSilva et al. 2016). Major non‐genetic congenital anomalies were therefore not included in the log‐binomial regression analyses. Instead, we listed all major non‐genetic congenital anomalies reported in the DPDR cohort of participants included after 2021 using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD10) code. We included the assumed risk period for harmful exposure based on the literature, using the categories “1st trimester,”, “> 1st trimester,” and “depending on cause.” Moreover, we included the maternal pertussis vaccination status and the gestational week of vaccination. Participants who reported a genetic anomaly among their offspring were excluded.

All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.1 2021 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, www.R‐project.org) with a statistical significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Study Population

Of the 10,545 participants with a due date before 01‐01‐2024 and a reported singleton birth after 24 gestational weeks, 134 (1.3%) were excluded due to an unknown vaccination status (Figure 1). Moreover, 33 participants were excluded because of a stillbirth or because of missing data on life or stillbirth. The proportion of stillbirths did not differ between participants who were vaccinated against pertussis during pregnancy and participants who were not (p value Fisher exact test: 0.611). After excluding an additional 98 participants with missing primary outcome data, the final study population included 10,280 participants.

Selection of the study population.

The majority (n = 7687, 74.8%) of the study population was vaccinated against pertussis during pregnancy (Table 1). The median gestational week of vaccination was 24. Of the vaccinated participants, the majority (75.6%) were vaccinated in the second trimester of pregnancy. Of the participants vaccinated against pertussis during pregnancy, 10.8% and 63.9% were also vaccinated against influenza and COVID‐19 during pregnancy, respectively. In comparison, of the participants not vaccinated against pertussis during pregnancy, 3.2% and 3.6% were vaccinated against influenza and COVID‐19 during pregnancy, respectively. In the study cohort before 2021, the influenza and COVID‐19 vaccination rates were 3.8% and 1.5%, respectively, while in the study cohort after 2021 the influenza and COVID‐19 vaccination rates were 11.3% and 70.7%, respectively. Primary Outcomes

Overall, 9.4% of the participants gave birth to a child who was classified as SGA and 10.6% as LGA. Moreover, 3.7% reported a low birth weight, 14.9% reported neonatal health problems, and 3.1% reported infant hospitalization due to (suspicion of) infection. Most participants in our cohort did not specify the type of infection for the hospitalization. For the remaining, suspicion of infection, enterovirus, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and other respiratory infections were the most mentioned reasons for hospitalization. Pertussis was not mentioned explicitly as a reason for hospitalization. The prevalence of the primary outcomes by maternal pertussis vaccination status is presented in Figure 2.

Prevalence of the neonatal health outcomes by maternal pertussis vaccination status.

We did not observe an increased risk after maternal pertussis vaccination for any of the primary outcomes (Figure 3). Adjusting had a limited impact on the effect estimate. In model 2, the adjusted RR (aRR) was: 1.01 (95% CI: 0.82–1.23) for SGA; 0.90 (95% CI: 0.73–1.11) for LGA; 0.86 (95% CI: 0.59–1.25) for low birth weight; and 1.06 (95% CI: 0.90–1.25) for neonatal health problems (Figure 3). For hospitalization due to (suspicion of) infection, a significantly lower risk was observed after pertussis vaccination during pregnancy (aRR: 0.68; 95% CI: 0.47–0.99).

Secondary Outcomes

A total of 10 participants reported neonatal death. The risk of neonatal death was lower for participants who were vaccinated against pertussis during pregnancy compared to participants who were not (aRR: 0.13; 95% CI: 0.04–0.49). For 8255 participants the Apgar score at 5 min after birth was reported of which 141 (1.7%) were classified as low. There was no association between maternal pertussis vaccination and a low Apgar score (aRR: 0.77; 95% CI: 0.43–1.39). For 7002 participants it was known if the infant had been admitted to a NICU. Overall 1.6% reported a NICU admission, which did not differ by maternal pertussis vaccination status (aRR: 0.66; 95% CI: 0.24–1.81) (Table 2).

There were 6958 participants who were included after 2021 and who did not report a genetic congenital anomaly (6714 vaccinated against pertussis during pregnancy and 244 not vaccinated against pertussis during pregnancy). Of these, 143 participants reported at least one major non‐genetic congenital anomaly among their offspring (Table S3). Most anomalies had their origin in the first trimester of pregnancy and thus before the pertussis vaccination. We did not identify potential associations between the major congenital anomalies and (the gestational week of) pertussis vaccination.

Sensitivity Analyses

None of the sensitivity analyses showed an increased risk of the outcomes after pertussis vaccination during pregnancy (Figure S1).

Discussion

We studied the safety of the maternal pertussis vaccination in the Netherlands with respect to the self‐reported neonatal health outcomes: SGA at birth, LGA at birth, low birth weight, neonatal health problems, and infant hospitalization due to (suspicion of) infection. For none of these health outcomes, an increased risk was observed after pertussis vaccination during pregnancy. There was no difference in risks after second compared to third trimester pertussis vaccinations. Also for the secondary outcomes neonatal death, low Apgar score, and NICU admission, we found no increased risks.

The strength of our study is that we used a fairly large and well‐defined study population, including a control population of participants who were not vaccinated against pertussis during pregnancy. Moreover, we had many confounders available to correct for and we performed multiple sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. The wide study period had the advantage that it also included years prior to the implementation of the maternal pertussis vaccination program in the Netherlands. The group of unvaccinated participants therefore also contained participants who would have chosen to be vaccinated if they had the opportunity at the time, probably resulting in a more comparable control group. We do acknowledge some limitations. First, we solely used self‐reported data which is prone to recall bias and might have led to misclassification. However, previous research has shown that perinatal outcomes reported by participants through online questionnaires are reliable (van Gelder et al. 2017; Maas et al. 2024). Unfortunately, the type of pertussis containing vaccine was not asked. Therefore we were not able to perform analyses stratified by type of vaccine. It is likely that most participants were vaccinated with the Tdap vaccine, as this vaccine is used in the maternal vaccination program in the Netherlands. Second, despite our relatively large study population, some outcome measures are rare resulting in limited power. Especially for the secondary outcomes the power was low and results should be interpreted with caution. For the primary outcomes we had about 80% power to detect a RR of 1.2 for the outcomes SGA and LGA at birth (12% vs. 10% prevalence), a RR of 1.35 for the outcome low birth weight (5.4% vs. 4% prevalence), a RR of 1.16 for the outcome neonatal health problems (16.2% vs. 14% prevalence), and a RR of 1.4 for the outcome infant hospitalization due to (suspicion of) infection (4.2% vs. 3% prevalence). Third, the time period of this study was between 2014 and 2024 and covered the COVID‐19 pandemic, which may have influenced the background rate of adverse neonatal outcomes (Smith et al. 2023). In sensitivity analysis only including participants included before 2021 (predominantly pre‐COVID‐19 pandemic/vaccination), the results did not differ from the main analyses, indicating the COVID‐19 pandemic had no relevant influence on the results. Last, our study population is not representative of all pregnant individuals in the Netherlands, possibly impacting the generalizability of our results. For example, our study population is more often highly educated and perceived their ethnicity as Dutch (Vorstenbosch et al. 2019). We corrected for various potential confounders to limit potential bias. Behaviors after birth, like breastfeeding, were not taken into account.

Our results are in concordance with the literature where also no increased risks after pertussis vaccination during pregnancy were found for SGA, LGA, birth weight, neonatal death, Apgar score, NICU admission, respiratory disorders, and/or a variety of other neonatal complications (Clark and Johnson 2023; Vygen‐Bonnet et al. 2020; D'Heilly et al. 2019; Fakhraei et al. 2021; Tseng et al. 2022; Greenberg et al. 2023; Morgan et al. 2015; Kildegaard et al. 2025). Moreover, a comparison of our results with the background incidence of adverse neonatal outcomes in the Netherlands prior to the national implementation of maternal pertussis vaccination did not lead to safety concerns (Immink et al. 2021).

We observed a decreased risk of infant hospitalization due to (suspicion of) infection after pertussis vaccination during pregnancy compared to no pertussis vaccination during pregnancy. From the participants who mentioned the type of infection, respiratory infections were most often mentioned as the reason for hospitalization. Pertussis was not mentioned explicitly as a reason for hospitalization. In addition to the prevention of pertussis, prenatal exposure to pertussis vaccination might also decrease the risk of other infections. Some previous studies did not find an association between maternal pertussis vaccination and non‐pertussis infections in infants, but others did (Andersen et al. 2022). For example, Laverty et al. (2021) found that infants who were prenatally exposed to pertussis vaccination had less often respiratory infections or gastrointestinal infections within 6 years after birth. A possible explanation is that prenatal exposures could possibly modulate the general immune system (MacGillivray and Kollmann 2014). Another possible explanation is the healthy vaccine effect. It could be that participants who choose to be vaccinated are generally healthier and have other behaviors that are beneficial for the infant, like exclusive breastfeeding, which was not taken into account. We corrected for multiple behavioral and socioeconomic factors, but residual confounding is possible.

In our study, the effect estimates for infant hospitalization due to (suspicion of) infection and the other outcomes were comparable between model 2 without correction and model 3 with correction for maternal influenza and COVID‐19 vaccination. Moreover, the sensitivity analysis where we excluded participants who were vaccinated against influenza or COVID‐19 during pregnancy led to results comparable to the main analyses. Altogether, these results suggest that the effect of the maternal pertussis vaccination on infant hospitalization due to (suspicion of) infection and the other outcomes is not influenced by maternal influenza or COVID‐19 vaccination, which is in line with previous research (Sukumaran et al. 2015).

Internationally there is discussion about the optimum timing of maternal pertussis vaccination (De Weerdt et al. 2024; Olson‐Chen et al. 2024). Based on our findings, there is no difference between second and third trimester vaccination in terms of neonatal health outcomes. These results are in agreement with previous studies where there were no indications of safety concerns associated with the timing of pertussis vaccination in pregnancy (De Weerdt et al. 2024), suggesting that the discussion on the timing of vaccination can focus on topics like (cost‐) effectiveness, vaccine uptake, and feasibility of implementation.

Maternal vaccinations are upcoming. Currently, there are international recommendations for maternal vaccination against pertussis, influenza, COVID‐19, and RSV. Moreover, multiple other vaccines for use during pregnancy are under development (Rick and Beigi 2024). Most previous research has studied the safety of individual maternal vaccinations, while research studying multiple simultaneous or consecutive vaccinations during pregnancy is limited (Olson‐Chen et al. 2024; de Bruin et al. 2023). In the Netherlands, the pertussis and influenza vaccines are given simultaneously if applicable (Duvekot et al. 2023). Future studies should focus on the safety of multiple simultaneous or consecutive vaccinations during pregnancy on maternal, pregnancy, and neonatal outcomes including preterm birth and gestational age.

In conclusion, the maternal pertussis vaccination did not increase the risk of different adverse neonatal health outcomes. There was no difference in neonatal health outcomes by trimester of vaccination. This study confirms the safety of pertussis vaccination during pregnancy with respect to neonatal health also for the Dutch vaccination program.
