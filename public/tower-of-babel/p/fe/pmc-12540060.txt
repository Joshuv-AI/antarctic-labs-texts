Quantitative meta-analysis complementing systematic review evidence on yogic practices in hypertensive patients
Authors: G Tarakeshwari, Dapkupar Wankhar, Deenadayalan Boopalan, Maheshkumar Kuppusamy
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We read the recently published systematic review titled “Effects of yogic practices on physiological and biochemical parameters of hypertensive patients: A systematic review of clinical trials” [1] done by Tyagi and Jain with great interest. The systematic review examining the impacts of yogic practices on physiological and biochemical parameters in hypertensive patients. The authors done the comprehensive literature search in PubMed, Cochrane library till October 31, 2023 after removing duplicates and full text screening finally included 15 studies. Their comprehensive review of 15 studies (5 RCTs and 10 clinical trials) provides valuable qualitative insights into how various yoga interventions [[2], [3], [4]] positively influence autonomic nervous system activity [5], oxidative stress [6], and inflammatory markers [3] among hypertension patients.

Building upon their foundational work, we conducted a quantitative meta-analysis in R software (version 4.4.2) to provide statistical synthesis of the available evidence on specific biochemical parameters. A total of four included studies for triglycerides, total cholesterol, High-Density Lipoprotein, Low-Density Lipoprotein and six studies for systolic and diastolic blood pressure were pooled (Table 1). The forest plots were given in supplementary document.

The authors used PEDro scale to assess the quality of included studies. The scores are ranged between 1 and 7. Authors included the PEDro scores 4 or above only included in the final analysis.

Our analysis included 4–6 clinical trials within the published review encompassing 399–534 participants and examined standardized mean differences (SMD) for key outcomes including blood pressure and lipid profiles.

Our meta-analytical findings complement and extend Tyagi and Jain's qualitative observations. The pooled effects of four included studies [[2], [3], [4],7] with 399 participants assessing the role of yoga on triglyceride levels showed a non-significant SMD of −0.43 with a 95 % confidence interval (CI) ranging from −1.37 to 0.42 and 79 % heterogeneity. Similarly, the pooled analysis of four studies [[2], [3], [4],8] with 501 participants evaluating the effect of yoga on total cholesterol revealed non-significant reduction SMD of −0.26 (95 % CI: −0.84 to 0.32) with 77 % of heterogeneity. In terms of high-density lipoprotein (HDL), the pooled results from four studies [2,3,8,9] including 401 participants showed a positive effect with an SMD of 0.79, although the 95 % CI ranged from −0.54 to 2.11, and high heterogeneity 92 % indicating no positive results. Additionally, the effect of yoga on low-density lipoprotein (LDL), based on four studies with 401 participants [2,3,8,9] showed a small negative effect with an SMD of −0.11 (95 % CI: −0.38 to 0.15), with low heterogeneity which was also not statistically significant.

Furthermore, six included studies with 534 participants [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]] were pooled to assess the impact of yoga on systolic and diastolic blood pressure. The findings demonstrated a reduction in systolic blood pressure with an SMD of −0.82 (95 % CI: −1.74 to 0.09) and 85 % of heterogeneity, while the effect on diastolic blood pressure was minimal and not significant, with an SMD of −0.01 (95 % CI: −0.03 to 0.01) and low heterogeneity. These results suggest that while yoga may have a favourable trend in improving lipid profile and systolic blood pressure, the overall effects are statistically inconclusive based on the current pooled evidence.

Interestingly, our analysis revealed the most consistent findings for parameters showing minimal effects on LDL cholesterol and diastolic blood pressure demonstrated negligible but reliable effects with low heterogeneity (I2 < 25 %), suggesting these may be less responsive to yogic interventions.

While Tyagi and Jain's systematic review demonstrates the breadth of yoga's physiological benefits, our meta-analysis reveals the critical need for methodological standardization in yoga research. The high heterogeneity across studies indicates significant variations in intervention protocols, which limits our ability to draw definitive quantitative conclusions despite encouraging effect size trends.

We concur with the authors' call for investigating additional biomarkers such as vasopressin and Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid (GABA). Additionally, we propose that future research should prioritize standardizing yoga intervention protocols, duration, and outcome measurement methods to reduce heterogeneity and strengthen evidence quality. This would enable more robust meta-analyses and clearer clinical recommendations for integrating yogic practices into hypertension management. Our quantitative findings support Tyagi and Jain's conclusion that yoga shows promise as a complementary approach for hypertension management, while highlighting the methodological rigor needed to establish definitive evidence for clinical practice guidelines.
