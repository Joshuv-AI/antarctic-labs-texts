Fusing Structural Phenotypes with Functional Data for Early Prediction of Primary Angle-Closure Glaucoma Progression
Authors: Swati Sharma, Thanadet Chuangsuwanich, Royston K.Y. Tan, Shimna C. Prasad, Tin A. Tun, Shamira A. Perera, Martin L. Buist, Tin Aung, Monisha E. Nongpiur, Michaël J.A. Girard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo classify eyes as slow or fast glaucoma progressors in patients with primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) using an integrated approach combining optic nerve head (ONH) structural features and sector-based visual field (VF) functional parameters.DesignRetrospective longitudinal study.ParticipantsPatients with PACG from glaucoma clinics.MethodsPatients with PACG with ≥5 reliable VF tests over ≥5 years were included. Visual field progression was assessed using ZEISS FORUM Software, with the baseline VF test selected within 6 months of the OCT scan. Fast progression was defined as a VF index (VFI) decline of < –2.0% per year; slow progression as ≥ –2.0% per year. OCT volumes were processed using artificial intelligence-based segmentation networks to extract 31 ONH structural parameters. The Glaucoma Hemifield test defined 5 regions in each hemifield, reflecting retinal nerve fiber layer distribution. Mean raw sensitivity values were computed for each region and combined with the structural parameters to train machine learning (ML) classifiers for progression classification. Several ML models were evaluated. Shapley Additive Explanations identified the most influential predictors.Main Outcome MeasuresClassification of slow versus fast progressors using combined structural and functional data.ResultsA total of 250 eyes were analyzed after excluding severe glaucoma. The mean VFI progression rate was –0.80% per year; 82.4% showed slow progression, and 17.6% fast progression. The Random Forest multimodal model integrating structural and functional features achieved the highest classification accuracy with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.85 ± 0.02 over 2000 Monte Carlo cross-validation iterations, outperforming the conventional multimodal model based on routinely available clinical features (AUC, 0.77 ± 0.02), as well as structure-only (AUC, 0.79 ± 0.03) and function-only models (AUC, 0.75 ± 0.04). Adding clinical features to the proposed structure-function model did not significantly improve model performance (AUC, 0.86 ± 0.02; P = 0.29). Shapley Additive Explanation analysis identified key predictive features, which included inferior minimum rim width, retinal nerve fiber layer thicknesses, and average VF sensitivity in the superior-nasal sector.ConclusionsIntegration of ONH structural and VF functional parameters improves the classification of progression risk in patients with PACG beyond conventional clinical models. These findings support the utility of multimodal approaches for enhanced risk stratification.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.

Primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) is a major subtype of glaucoma characterized by a mechanical obstruction of the anterior chamber angle, resulting in impaired aqueous humor outflow and elevated intraocular pressure (IOP),1 which can progressively lead to raised IOP and subsequent optic nerve damage and irreversible vision loss.2 The chronic form of PACG may remain asymptomatic until the late stages, when significant visual field (VF) loss has already occurred, making early detection particularly challenging. Although PACG is less prevalent than primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), epidemiological studies have shown that it causes a disproportionately higher rate of blindness.1 This underscores the importance of timely identification of at-risk individuals and close monitoring of both structural and functional parameters. Early prediction of disease progression using biometric, structural, and clinical indicators could enable proactive management strategies. There is, thus, a pressing need for predictive models to assess progression risk in PACG, guiding clinicians in preserving visual function and optimizing patient outcomes.

Standard automated perimetry is the most widely used functional test for monitoring glaucoma progression and is considered the clinical gold standard for evaluating visual function. Numerous research efforts have focused on predicting future VF decline based on existing VF data.3,4 However, due to the subjective nature and significant variability of VF measurements, reliable predictions usually require a series of 6 to 8 tests.3 Consequently, it may take approximately 3 to 4 years after the initial diagnosis to determine the true rate of progression. In clinical practice, obtaining such a large number of VFs is often impractical, as treatment decisions frequently need to be made after only a few visits, making it challenging to identify fast progressors accurately and at an early stage. Further complicating the assessment, variability in VF outcomes may result from various factors, such as coexisting cataracts, advanced glaucoma with frequent fixation losses, learning effects during testing, or even patient distraction.

Glaucoma progression can be evaluated by analyzing both structural and functional parameters. In some cases, structural damage can be identified before functional impairment using current diagnostic tools, making it a potential predictor of future visual changes. OCT imaging of the optic nerve head (ONH) provides valuable structural information, including measurements related to the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) and the ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) complex. To address the variability of VF data and enhance the accuracy of glaucoma detection, several studies have integrated these structural data with functional metrics. For example, Garway-Heath et al5 demonstrated that combining VF and OCT data allowed for more precise estimation of glaucoma progression rates compared with using VF data alone. Similarly, Dixit et al6 showed that incorporating basic clinical data, such as cup-to-disc ratio (CDR), corneal thickness, and IOP, alongside VF data improved the model’s predictive performance. Despite achieving good accuracy, these studies faced limitations in explainability, as they were unable to identify the specific factors driving the predicted outcomes.

Recent machine learning (ML) approaches have advanced VF-based glaucoma analysis. For example, Moradi et al7 proposed PyGlaucoMetrics, a stacked meta-learning framework demonstrating improved performance using Humphrey Field Analyzer 24-2 data, whereas other studies have highlighted the advantages of nonlinear models for VF-based staging and alternative perimetric strategies.8 However, most existing approaches focus on diagnosis or staging and rely primarily on cross-sectional VF inputs. In this study, we focus on predicting disease progression, adopting the widely used Humphrey Field Analyzer 24-2 test pattern to ensure consistency with prior work and clinical practice while enabling extension toward multimodal frameworks that incorporate complementary structural information.9

This study aimed to integrate structural features of the ONH, derived from OCT images using artificial intelligence-based 3D structural analysis, with functional features from VF data to enable early prediction of glaucoma progression. The specific goals were as follows: (1) to extract structural parameters of the ONH from OCT images and combine them with VF data from 5 distinct sectors in superior (S) and inferior (I) hemifields, (2) to develop ML models to classify eyes as slow or fast progressors, and (3) to use Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAPs) to identify the most influential factors driving progression and understand their relationship with glaucoma progression.

Methods

Patient Recruitment and VF Progression

The retrospective study was conducted by reviewing the medical records of patients diagnosed with PACG who were seen at the glaucoma clinics of the Singapore National Eye Centre between 2005 and 2016. These patients had previously participated in a research study focused on identifying genetic factors associated with PACG.10,11 At the time of biological sample collection, written informed consent was obtained from all participants to access their clinical data. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the SingHealth Centralized Institutional Review Board and was conducted in adherence to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Primary angle-closure glaucoma was defined as the presence of angle closure (where ≥180° of the posterior pigmented trabecular meshwork was not visible on nonindentation gonioscopy in the primary gaze position) with glaucomatous optic neuropathy and compatible VF defect. Patients were excluded if they had any other ocular conditions in the study eye that could account for the VF loss other than glaucoma.10,11 Eyes were not excluded based on lens status, and VF examinations were not censored after cataract or glaucoma surgery. Surgical history (before baseline or during follow-up) was recorded and included in secondary analyses. Surgical categories were defined as no surgery, cataract extraction alone, and glaucoma surgery (trabeculectomy alone or combined with cataract extraction; eyes with prior cataract extraction followed by trabeculectomy were grouped with the combined surgery in the analysis).

Visual field testing was conducted using the 24-2 Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm (SITA) strategy (either SITA Standard or SITA-FAST) with the Humphrey Field Analyzer (Carl Zeiss Meditec) with appropriate refractive correction. The ZEISS FORUM platform was utilized to assess longitudinal VF changes, with the software allowing integration of SITA Standard and SITA-FAST tests to provide the overall slope of progression as a visual field index (VFI) percentage (%) per year, as well as the S and I slopes of progression. Only examinations accepted as reliable within the reliability assessment incorporated in the FORUM software workflow were included in the longitudinal analysis, and reliability filtering was therefore based on FORUM software-derived indices rather than investigator-defined numerical thresholds. To align VF progression analysis with structural imaging, the baseline VF test in the FORUM software was reselected to fall within 6 months of the OCT scan. Patients who had at least 5 or more reliable VFs and at least 5 years of follow-up were evaluated. Baseline VFs used for feature extraction were separated from the longitudinal series used for outcome estimation; VFI slopes were derived using regression across all follow-up tests within FORUM rather than from baseline-derived change. Visual field index was used as the functional progression metric due to reduced susceptibility to media opacity effects and suitability for longitudinal trend analysis. Fast progression was defined as a VFI decline of <–2.0% per year, whereas slow progression was defined as a decline of ≥–2.0% per year. Because no validated PACG-specific threshold for defining rapid progression exists, this cutoff was selected a priori as a pragmatic and conservative definition to provide clinically interpretable stratification (see Table S1 in supplemental material for more details, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Disease severity was classified based on the first VF test used in the FORUM software, categorized by mean deviation (MD) as mild (≥–6.0 dB), moderate (–6.01 to –12.0 dB), or severe (<–12.01 dB).11 Participants with severe VF loss were excluded, as glaucoma progression tends to be slower in such cases due to the floor effect in VF assessment,12 and because structural-functional correlations become less reliable in advanced disease stages. Raw point sensitivity values at individual test points were extracted from the baseline VF printouts obtained within 6 months of the OCT scan. These were selected to preserve spatially localized functional information for modeling, avoiding predefined sector-based aggregation inherent in total or pattern deviation summaries. To ensure spatial alignment, VF maps from left eyes were horizontally flipped to match the configuration of right eyes. For regional VF analysis, 5 distinct sectors corresponding to specific RNFL anatomical features were defined within each hemifield along the horizontal meridian. These sectors included the central, paracentral, nasal (N), arcuate 1, and arcuate 2 regions in both the S and I hemifields (Fig 1). Spectral-Domain OCT Imaging

Spectral-domain OCT imaging was conducted using the CIRRUS HD-OCT 6000 system (Carl Zeiss Meditec), employing its standard 6 × 6 mm2 scan protocol centered on the ONH. This protocol captures a data cube over a 6-mm square grid by acquiring 200 horizontal scan lines, each containing 200 A-scans. All scans were manually inspected using Zeiss CIRRUS HD-OCT Review Software 11.5.1. Scans of suboptimal quality, identified by segmentation errors, uneven signal intensity, excessively weak signal strength, or significant motion artifacts, were excluded from the analysis.

ONH Morphological Parameter Extraction

The Reflectivity software (Abyss Processing Pte Ltd) was used to segment the retinal and connective tissue layers of the ONH from each OCT volume (see Fig 1 and Fig 2). These layers included the RNFL, GCIPL, the remaining other retinal layers, the retinal pigment epithelium, the choroid, the visible part of the peripapillary sclera (including the scleral flange), and the lamina cribrosa (LC). The software was also used to extract 31 structural parameters, such as Bruch’s membrane opening area, LC curvature and depth, prelamina depth and thickness, scleral angle, and average thickness of the RNFL layer, GCIPL layer, and minimum rim width (MRW) across the S, superior-temporal, superior-nasal, N, temporal, inferior-nasal, inferior-temporal (IT), and I regions. More detailed information about the Reflectivity software and the definitions of the above parameters is provided in the supplemental material (see Fig S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).Figure 2A, A sample OCT volume of the optic nerve head. ML Approach for VF Progression Prediction

In addition to the 31 structural parameters derived from OCT images, 10 functional parameters extracted from VF maps, i.e., the mean retinal sensitivity values across the 10 VF sectors, were included as inputs for various ML models (Fig 1). These models were trained to distinguish between eyes with slow and fast progression. The ML models tested for this classification task included tree-based ML models, such as Random Forest, Decision Tree, and Extra Trees classifier; Gradient Boosting algorithms, such as XGBoost, CatBoost, and HistGradientBoosting classifier; and neural network-based multilayer perceptron classifier. These algorithms are widely recognized for their effectiveness in health care applications.13,14

We developed multiple models, including a proposed multimodal model integrating structural and functional parameters, as well as models based on routinely available clinical features and other feature sets. The clinical model included demographic variables (age and gender), surgical history (no surgery, phacoemulsification, and trabeculectomy), IOP fluctuation (defined as the standard deviation of IOP over the follow-up period), baseline functional parameters (MD and VFI), and baseline structural parameters (average RNFL and GCIPL measurements). Biometric parameters, including CDR, axial length, anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, and central corneal thickness, were available in a subset of 73 subjects; a separate model was constructed using these features. The performance of each model was then compared with that of the proposed structure-function multimodal model.

Due to the data set’s imbalance, with the slow progression class being approximately 6 times larger than the fast progression class, higher class weights were assigned to the fast progression group. The class weights were computed in such a way that the weight for each class was inversely proportional to its frequency. This adjustment mitigated the risk of model bias toward the slow progression class during training.

To assess model reliability, Monte Carlo cross-validation was employed.15 The data set was randomly partitioned into 80% training and 20% testing data over 2000 iterations. Splitting was conducted at the patient level to ensure that all data from a single subject remained within the same subset, thereby preventing data leakage between the training and testing sets. In each iteration, the ML models were trained afresh on the training data, and their performance was assessed on the test set. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) was calculated in each iteration to evaluate model accuracy. Additionally, precision, recall, and F1-score values averaged over 2000 Monte Carlo iterations were reported using the confusion matrix. Decision curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the clinical relevance of the model. Unlike conventional performance metrics, this approach assesses the clinical usefulness of the model by quantifying whether model-guided decisions provide greater benefit than treating all patients or treating none.

Explainability of ML Approach

We employed SHAP to interpret the model’s predictions and enhance explainability.16 SHAP offers a comprehensive framework for understanding ML model outputs by utilizing Shapley values from cooperative game theory. This method equitably distributes the prediction outcome among all features, ensuring that each feature's contribution is accurately assessed. By assigning each feature a SHAP value that reflects its influence on the model's output, the method provides consistent and interpretable explanations.

The “TreeExplainer” method from SHAP was utilized to identify key features in each iteration of the Monte Carlo cross-validation process. These feature importance scores were averaged across 2000 iterations to obtain the mean SHAP values and overall feature importance. This averaging technique enabled us to derive a stable estimate of feature significance throughout the repeated validation process.

Furthermore, to evaluate the stability of SHAP-based rankings across all 2000 Monte Carlo iterations, we quantified ranking stability by computing the proportion of iterations in which each feature appeared among the top 15. This frequency-based stability metric reflects how consistently a feature contributes to model predictions across different data resampling scenarios. Features with high inclusion frequencies indicate robust and reliable importance, whereas features with low frequencies suggest instability or sensitivity to sampling variability. This analysis ensured that the reported feature importance reflects reproducible patterns rather than iteration-specific fluctuations.

Results

A total of 250 subjects out of 323 were evaluated after excluding 73 subjects with severe glaucoma. Each subject contributed data from a single eye. The mean age at the time of OCT scan was 69.4 ± 7.6 years, and 49.2% were males. Of the 250 eyes, 171 (68.4%) had mild VF loss and 79 (31.6%) had moderate VF loss. The mean rate of VFI progression was –0.80 ± 1.1% per year; 206 (82.4%) eyes demonstrated slow progression, whereas 44 (17.6%) exhibited fast progression (see Table 1 for more details).

Our ML models, which integrated structural and functional parameters, demonstrated high accuracy in distinguishing between slow and fast-progressing eyes. Among all evaluated models, the Random Forest algorithm achieved the best performance on an independent test set, with an AUC of 0.85 ± 0.02 over 2000 Monte Carlo cross-validation iterations. Gradient Boosting models also delivered comparable results. A summary of performance metrics for all ML models is presented in Table 2. Decision curve analysis showed that the model consistently outperformed both treat-all and treat-none strategies, yielding the highest net benefit across threshold probabilities of 0.03–0.42 (Fig 3C). This demonstrates the model’s strong ability to correctly detect fast glaucoma progression while minimizing unnecessary treatment, reflecting a favorable balance between sensitivity and specificity. The narrow variability across Monte Carlo simulations further confirms the stability and reliability of the model. Together, these findings support its suitability as an effective screening tool, with diminishing advantage only at higher, rule-in thresholds.

The top 6 features contributing to the classification as identified by SHAP analysis were MRW in the I region, average RNFL thickness in the I region, nasal-temporal LC curvature, average VF sensitivity in the SN sector, GCIPL thickness in the I region, and RNFL thickness in the IT region. The top 15 most influential parameters based on SHAP values are shown in Figure 3D. We observed that the ranking of the influential parameters changed in each iteration (Fig S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). However, the feature ranking was stable after averaging over all iterations (Fig. 3D). The frequency-based stability metric demonstrated that the top 15 features appeared among the top-ranked features in >90% of the 2000 iterations, demonstrating high stability of feature importance (Fig S2 and Fig S3, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

The multimodal model with structural and functional parameters (AUC, 0.85 ± 0.02) outperformed all other models (Table 3). The model with routinely available clinical features demonstrated an AUC of 0.77 ± 0.02, whereas the reported AUCs of other models were 0.79 ± 0.03 (structural alone) and 0.75 ± 0.04 (functional alone). In contrast, the model based on limited biometric data demonstrated an AUC of 0.79 ± 0.02 (Fig 4 and Fig S4, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Shapley Additive Explanation analysis on structural-only and functional-only data revealed key features consistent with those identified in the combined data set. Dependency plots revealed strong correlations between input features and their SHAP values. Discussion

In this study, we integrated structural parameters of the ONH derived from OCT images with functional parameters from VF assessments to evaluate their combined effectiveness in classifying eyes as slow or fast progressors. The analysis was carried out in 2 stages. Using multiple ML approaches, the proposed structure-function multimodal model demonstrated strong and consistent performance in distinguishing progression profiles, achieving an AUC of 0.85. Shapley Additive Explanation analysis further identified key predictive features, highlighting the complementary contributions of structural and functional features in capturing disease dynamics in PACG.

We specifically evaluated whether the proposed model provides incremental benefits over a conventional multimodal model constructed using routinely available clinical, structural, and functional parameters. The conventional model showed lower predictive performance (AUC = 0.77). Similarly, models based on individual domains also demonstrated only moderate predictive ability. Notably, incorporating additional routinely available clinical features into the proposed model did not significantly improve performance (Table 3), suggesting that the integrated structural-functional feature set captures most of the relevant predictive information. Together, these findings support the added value of the proposed approach for risk stratification beyond current clinical models.

Several studies have investigated the mechanisms underlying glaucoma progression, primarily focusing on POAG due to its higher prevalence in studied populations.4,17,18 Although PACG is nearly 3 times less common than POAG, the number of individuals who go blind from PACG is comparable.19 This may be attributed to a limited understanding of PACG progression and a lack of optimized treatment strategies. PACG is particularly prevalent in Asian populations, accounting for approximately 86% of the global angle-closure glaucoma burden.20 Despite this, literature on PACG progression remains limited, with most studies emphasizing VF data.21, 22, 23 Recent research has begun to highlight the significance of RNFL and ganglion cell complex thickness measurements from OCT in tracking PACG progression.24,25 In this study, we designed a novel ML- and SHAP-based tool to predict PACG progression using only baseline data from one of the largest PACG cohorts to date. To our knowledge, this is the first approach to integrate functional VF data with structural ONH features from OCT for PACG progression prediction and to identify key features of disease progression. This framework also holds potential for application in POAG progression analysis.

Our proposed model demonstrated high accuracy in distinguishing slow-progressing eyes from fast-progressing ones with an AUC of 0.85 ± 0.02. Previous studies have highlighted the predictive value of VF maps and clinical parameters, such as CDR ratio, corneal thickness, and IOP in assessing glaucoma progression.3,5,6 However, these studies primarily focused on POAG, and it remains unclear whether these findings can be extrapolated to PACG, given the differences in clinical presentation, treatment approaches, and risk factors. Our study, focused exclusively on PACG, provides compelling evidence that integrating structural features from OCT images with functional insights from VF maps presents a promising new approach to tracking glaucoma progression.

Notably, structural features alone demonstrated slightly higher predictive performance than functional features, which may reflect the tendency for structural changes to precede measurable functional deterioration, particularly in earlier disease stages. OCT-derived measurements are also less influenced by patient-related variability and learning effects inherent to perimetric testing, potentially providing a more stable signal for modeling. In this cohort, where most eyes did not have advanced functional loss at baseline, structural parameters may capture early tissue susceptibility not yet manifested as sensitivity decline. These findings reinforce the complementary nature of multimodal integration, as combining structural and functional features yielded the best overall model performance.

Our SHAP-based analysis identified key ONH structural parameters, such as MRW, average RNFL, and GCIPL thickness in the I region, as well as RNFL thickness in the IT region, as critical predictors of disease progression (Fig 3C and Fig 6). The SHAP dependency plots revealed a negative correlation between SHAP values and these structural parameters, indicating that lower values of these parameters are associated with faster disease progression (Fig 6). However, in some cases, the relationship was found to be nonlinear (Fig 6F). These findings also build on our earlier cross-sectional observations in PACG, which showed that S hemifield defects were more pronounced than I ones.11 In the current study, we further demonstrate that these S defects are more closely associated with disease progression. This is anatomically consistent, as structural damage in the inferior ONH corresponds to S hemifield VF loss.

Our study demonstrated that the S hemifield sectors play a more significant role in predicting disease progression compared with the I ones (see Fig 3B). This observation aligns with several prior studies. For example, Verma et al21 reported that S sectors showed earlier progression in early-stage PACG, whereas multiple cross-sectional studies have also documented greater total deviation values in the S hemifield than in the I hemifield.26 Yousefi et al27 similarly observed more prominent VF damage in the S hemifield. These findings suggest that N and peripheral VF regions, particularly in the S hemifield, are more susceptible to early glaucomatous damage, which may later evolve into diffuse loss as the disease progresses.

Anatomically, this pattern is consistent with the known structural vulnerability of the I ONH, which has thinner RNFL bundles and is more prone to pressure-related damage.5 Given that PACG is a pressure-dependent form of glaucoma, chronic or subacute IOP elevation often results in focal or diffuse RNFL loss, particularly in the I retina, manifesting as S hemifield defects on VF testing.

Our SHAP-based analysis independently confirmed this structure-function relationship, highlighting that several S VF sectors, specifically the SN, central, and paracentral sectors, were among the most critical in predicting progression. Notably, the inferior-nasal sector also emerged as an important contributor, although superior hemifield regions were more prominently represented overall.

Our findings suggest that MRW may serve as a key structural marker for progression in PACG. Consistent with our results, several studies have reported similar observations. For instance, Chauhan et al28 first introduced MRW as a robust structural parameter aligned with the true anatomical path of axonal exit. We also observed that RNFL and GCIPL thinning may be a critical structural biomarker for PACG progression. Multiple studies have demonstrated that progressive RNFL thinning mirrors VF loss in PACG.29,30 In addition, our study identified LC curvature as a structural parameter associated with disease progression in PACG. A positive correlation was observed between N temporal LC curvature and SHAP values (Fig 5E), suggesting that increased LC curvature is linked to accelerated progression. Similar findings have been reported in POAG, where increased baseline LC curvature was associated with faster rates of disease progression.31,32 Our findings are consistent with these reports and further underscore the importance of ONH structural changes in predicting the progression of PACG.

We chose the VFI slope as the primary measure of VF progression, rather than the MD slope. Although both VFI and MD are widely used in the literature to assess glaucoma progression, several studies have highlighted that progression rates vary significantly among patients, with the rate of change being one of the most reliable predictors of future deterioration.29,33 Unlike MD, which can be confounded by media opacities, such as cataracts, VFI was specifically designed to mitigate these influences.11,33 As cataract severity increases, MD may inaccurately suggest accelerated VF loss, whereas postcataract surgery, MD values often improve, which complicates longitudinal assessments. In contrast, VFI is less sensitive to such changes and places greater emphasis on the central VF, aligning more closely with ganglion cell damage, a key factor in glaucoma.33 Moreover, the Humphrey Field Analyzer software incorporates the VFI slope to quantify the rate of progression, expressed as the percentage of VF loss per year. Given its robustness and clinical relevance, we used the VFI slope to classify eyes as slow or fast progressors. As no validated PACG-specific threshold exists, the –2% cutoff was pragmatically selected to identify clearly accelerated functional decline while maintaining stable cohort stratification (see supplemental material).

The inclusion of surgical variables did not change model performance, suggesting that surgical intervention alone did not account for progression classification. Although lens extraction may reduce IOP in PACG, evidence linking cataract extraction to VF progression remains limited, as most randomized studies in PACG34,35 have focused on IOP outcomes rather than functional deterioration.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it was a hospital-based study with participants primarily of Chinese ethnicity, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Second, patients with severe PACG were excluded from this analysis. Although this may seem to limit generalizability, previous studies suggest that such patients contribute minimally to measurable VF progression. This is likely due to a “ceiling effect,” in which advanced baseline VF damage restricts the ability to detect further functional decline.36 Third, the study used data from a single academic center and exclusively incorporated 24-2 Humphrey VF tests, which may restrict the applicability of the ML model to broader clinical settings in which different protocols are used. Fourth, the requirement for multiple longitudinal VF examinations to ensure stable slope estimation may introduce selection bias, potentially underrepresenting eyes with rapid deterioration that did not meet testing criteria. In addition, surgical interventions during follow-up may have confounded the estimation of VF progression rates. As postsurgical VF tests were included in some cases, the observed rates may have been attenuated; exclusion of these tests could potentially yield higher estimates of progression. Fifth, all OCT scans were acquired using a single imaging device. Given that image quality and segmentation accuracy can vary across machines, external validation using data from multiple devices is necessary to ensure robustness and generalizability of the findings. Finally, we utilized clinically well-established structural parameters of the ONH. In this context, deep learning-based methods could be employed to identify novel biomarkers, such as those proposed by Panda et al30 and Sharma et al,37 which may demonstrate stronger correlations with disease progression.

In summary, this study demonstrated that integrating structural parameters from the ONH with functional parameters improves the prediction of disease progression. The proposed framework identified key parameters associated with progression and their inter-relationships, offering potential utility for clinical risk assessment in PACG. Given that delayed awareness and underdiagnosis are important contributors to vision impairment and blindness in PACG, improved risk stratification and earlier identification enabled by this approach may facilitate timely intervention and optimised patient management.
