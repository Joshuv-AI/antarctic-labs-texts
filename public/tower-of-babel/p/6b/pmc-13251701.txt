Antiepileptic and antiepileptogenic effects of Lagenaria siceraria aqueous extract (Curcubitaceae) in PTZ-induced kindling in rats
Authors: King-Ghislain Kenne Gnoupa, Gwladys Temkou Ngoupaye, Aurelien Fossueh Foutsop, Bibiane Tatiana Diebo Kom, Stève Brunel Kenfack Ngoufack, Chirac Jospin Noubouwo, Ronel Bessam Diane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLagenaria siceraria (Curcubitaceae) is a plant traditionally used in Cameroon to treat epilepsy and other central nervous system disorders. The present study investigated the antiepileptogenic and antiepileptic effects of Lagenaria siceraria in PTZ-induced kindling in rats. Kindling was induced in male rats aged of 2–3 months using a challenging dose of PTZ (70 mg/kg, i.p.) followed by the administration of sub-convulsive doses of PTZ (35 mg/kg) every other day. The different treatments including the aqueous extract of Lagenaria siceraria (200 mg/kg and 400 mg/kg) were administered one hour before PTZ until the animals of negative group reached the kindled state. Seizure progression, latency of seizure and duration of seizure were assessed. After kindling was established, animals were sacrificed, hippocampus were collected to evaluate oxidative stress (malondialdehyde (MDA), reduced glutathione (GSH), catalase, nitric oxide (NO)), inhibitory neurotransmission (GABA, GABA-T), neuroinflammation (TNF-α, IL-1β), and blood-brain barrier integrity (MMP-9). The results showed that Lagenaria siceraria significantly increased the latency of seizure onset (***p < 0.001) and reduced seizure duration (***p < 0.001). Biochemically, Lagenaria siceraria significantly increased GABA and GSH levels and catalase activity (**p < 0.01), while GABA-T activity, TNF-α, IL-1β, MMP-9, MDA and NO levels were significantly reduced (*p < 0.05). Taken together, Lagenaria siceraria exhibited antiepileptic and antiepileptogenic effects by modulating GABAergic signaling, reducing oxidative stress and neuroinflammation. These findings support its traditional use and highlight its potential as an effective natural therapeutic agent for epilepsy.Keywords: Lagenaria siceraria, Epileptogenesis, Epilepsy, Neuroinflammation, Oxidative stress

Introduction

According to the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE, 2022), epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by a long-standing predisposition to generate epileptic seizures, accompanied by neurobiological, cognitive, psychological, and social consequences (Wirrell et al., 2022, Di Noia et al., 2024). Globally, epilepsy affects approximately 51.7 million people, making it one of the most prevalent neurological conditions (Feigin et al., 2025). It remains a significant public health concern in Cameroon, where the prevalence ranges from 35.4 to 134.5 per 1000 individuals, with a slight male predominance (Gams and Massi et al., 2019).

Epilepsy is therefore a significant burden, further exacerbated by discrimination and social stigmatization, particularly in regions with widespread misunderstanding, such as Africa, where the disease is often misinterpreted as witchcraft caused by bad spirits and is contagious, transmitted through saliva, air, or close contact with individuals with epilepsy (Njamnshi et al., 2009, Kirabira et al., 2020, Makasi et al., 2023).

Although epilepsy is often perceived as a mystical condition in certain cultures, science has clearly established that it arises from complex neurobiological mechanisms. Indeed, its pathophysiology is multifactorial and depends on the underlying etiology, which may include genetic mutations, infections, metabolic disturbances, drug-induced toxicity, and brain trauma (Sidhu et al., 2025). One of the first consequences of the primary seizure is the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which overwhelms antioxidant defense systems such as catalase and reduced glutathione (GSH). This imbalance leads to oxidative stress, as evidenced by elevated levels of lipid peroxidation markers like malondialdehyde (MDA). Oxidative stress not only damages neurons but also acts as a key activator of microglial cells, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system (Walker, 2023, Zhao et al., 2025).

Activated microglia subsequently release a range of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin−1 beta (IL−1β), which contribute to the inflammatory environment that characterizes epileptogenesis (Liddelow et al., 2017, PascualSanz et al., 2024, Zhao et al., 2025). IL−1β enhances neuronal excitability by modulating GABAergic transmission. For instance, by altering the sensitivity of GABA-A receptors which lead to a decrease in amplitude of GABA-A currents, weakening inhibitory synaptic control (Alfano et al., 2022). TNF-α, on the other hand, has been shown to upregulate glutamatergic activity by promoting the trafficking of AMPA receptors to the synapse, while simultaneously reducing GABA-A receptor expression, further tipping the balance toward excitation (Pribiag and Stellwagen, 2013, Chen et al., 2021).

In parallel, the inflammatory response triggers the upregulation of matrix metalloproteinase−9 (MMP−9), an enzyme that plays a central role in degrading extracellular matrix proteins and disrupting the structural integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) (Wang et al., 2024). The resulting BBB permeability allows peripheral inflammatory mediators and immune cells to enter the brain parenchyma, perpetuating the inflammatory response and enhancing neuronal vulnerability (Pijet et al., 2018, Han et al., 2024).

This vicious cycle from oxidative stress to microglial activation, to neuroinflammation, to BBB breakdown results in synaptic remodeling, loss of inhibitory synapses, increased excitatory drive, and long-term alterations in neuronal circuitry (Yu et al., 2024, Kim et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2025). These changes collectively promote a hyperexcitable neural environment and establish a chronic epileptogenic state, increasing susceptibility to recurrent seizures (Shi et al., 2025).

Epilepsy management encompasses a variety of therapeutic approaches. The most commonly employed are pharmacological treatments, which include antiepileptic drugs (AEDs). These medications such as sodium channel modulators (e.g., carbamazepine, sodium valproate and phenytoin) and GABAergic agents (e.g., and benzodiazepines) work by stabilizing neuronal activity (Witkin et al., 2022, Gunasekera et al., 2023, Yan et al., 2025). Despite their efficacy, AEDs are not without limitations, as they fail to control seizure up to 30% of patients with refractory epilepsy and are often associated with adverse effects such as liver toxicity, cognitive impairment, teratogenic effects, rashes and fatigue (Johannessen and Ben-Menachem, 2006, Fattorusso et al., 2021, Yan et al., 2025). For patients unresponsive to medication, surgical interventions may be considered. Focal resection and neuromodulation techniques like vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) or deep brain stimulation (DBS) are effective for specific cases, particularly focal epilepsy. However, these procedures are invasive, carry potential risks of neurological impairment such like dysphonia and are not universally applicable, particularly in case of generalized epilepsy (Cramer et al., 2023, Clifford et al., 2024).

Despite the variety of available options, epilepsy management remain fraught with challenges. Refractoriness to AEDs is a significant issue, leaving many patients without effective symptom control. Furthermore, the side effects associated with long-term drug use and the invasive nature of surgical procedures pose additional burdens (Clifford et al., 2024). Accessibility to advanced therapies like surgery or neuromodulation is often limited in resource-poor settings, where epilepsy is more prevalent (Caraballo et al., 2015). Additionally, many existing treatments focus on seizure control rather than addressing the underlying mechanisms of epileptogenesis, such as neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, or blood brain barrier dysfunction (Huang et al., 2025, Shi et al., 2025, Ventola, 2014). These challenges have led to a growing interest in phytotherapy as a compelling alternative, which offers significant advantages, particularly its lower side-effect profile. Lagenaria siceraria (Curcubitaceae), commonly known as bottle gourd or calabash, has long been used in traditional medicine across various regions of Africa and Asia. Its empirical applications are diverse and include treatments for hypertension, digestive disorders, fever, and neurological conditions such as epilepsy (Saeed et al., 2022). It is also traditionally employed in managing diabetes, ulcers, jaundice, cardiovascular diseases, hemorrhoids, and colitis (Shendge et al., 2021).

Beyond its traditional applications, several scientific studies have highlighted the antioxidant properties of this plant. In vivo experiments have shown that fruit extracts of Lagenaria siceraria reduce oxidative stress by enhancing catalase and glutathione activity, while decreasing levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and nitric oxide (NO) (Taha et al., 2024, Saeed et al., 2022). These antioxidant effects were further supported by a clinical study in dyslipidemic patients, where administration of Lagenaria siceraria extract significantly improved oxidative stress markers (Katare et al., 2014).

Although studies have explored the anticonvulsant potential of this plant, in particular its efficacy in maximal electroshock-induced seizure models (Tirumalasetti et al., 2017), no comprehensive scientific investigation has yet evaluated its antiepileptic properties in standardized experimental models.

This study was thus undertaken to evaluate the antiepileptogenic and anti-epileptic effects of Lagenaria siceraria (bottle gourd) in PTZ inducing Kindling model in rats.

Materials and methods

Drugs and chemicals

PTZ procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, USA) was used as an inducing agent and was prepared by dissolving 70 and 35 mg in 10 mL of distilled water to obtain the respective concentrations of 7 and 3.5 mg/mL. These solutions were administered by i.p injection at a volume of administration of 10 mL/kg, yielding administration doses of 70 mg/kg and 35 mg/kg, respectively (Ngoupaye et al., 2022). Sodium valproate (Sanofi-Aventis (Paris, France)) was used as positive control (Ngoupaye et al., 2022).

Plant material and extract preparation

The plant material used in this study consisted of the entire fruit of Lagenaria siceraria (Molina) Standl. encompassing the rind, pulpe, and seeds. The fruit were harvested in the west region of Cameroon, precisely in Bamboutos Division, Bamendjo locality, during the rainy season in August 2022. The fruits were dried away from direct sunlight and then ground into powder. A specimen of Lagenaria siceraria was authenticated at the National Herbarium of Cameroon by comparison with the reference specimen Nº42481/HNC collected by Westphal and recorded under Nº. 9359.

For the plant extract preparation and administration, a powder of Lagenaria siceraria weighing100 grammes was introduced in 1 L of distilled water, and the mixture was macerated for 5 h. The resulting extract was then filtered with a filter tissue, cotton, and filter paper n° 4 successively. The filtrate obtained was dried in an oven at 40°C. The extraction yield following extraction was 9.42%. The extract was administered p.o at a volume of 10 mL/kg body weight.

Animal material

Males Wistar rats (Rattus norvegicus), aged between 2 and 3 months, weighing between 150 and 200 g at the beginning of the study, were used for the antiepileptic and antiepileptogenic tests. The animals were bred and raised at the Animal Physiology and Phytopharmacology Research Unit of the University of Dschang. Animals were housed in cages (42.5 cm (length) × 26.5 cm (width) × 15 cm (height)) containing wood shavings bedding (3.5–4 cm thick), which was renewed every 2–3 days to absorb urine, limit ammonia buildup, and ensure animal comfort, under controlled conditions at 24 ± 1°C with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle (lights on at 07:00 AM and off at 07:00 PM). Three animals were housed per cage with ad libitum access to food and water. Animals were acclimatized to the experimental room for 7 days prior to the experiments and handled gently by the same experimenter throughout the study to minimize stress. Animals were treated following the guidelines of Cameroon’s Bioethics committee (reg N. FWA IRB00001954) and the international principles of laboratory animal protection (NIH Publication 8023, revised 1996) as well as Directive 2010/63/EU on animal experimentation. As such, efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of animals used in the experiment.

Animals treatments and kindling induction

A total of 35 male Wistar rats were distributed into 5 groups of 7 animals each and received treatments as indicated below:

● Group 1: received only distilled water (10 mL/kg) and served as vehicle); ● Group 2: received distilled water (10 mL/kg) + inducing agent (PTZ) and served as negative control; ● Group 3: received the plant extract at the dose of 200 mg/kg + inducing agent (PTZ); ● Group 4: received the plant extract at the dose of 400 mg/kg + inducing agent (PTZ); ● Group 5: received sodium valproate at the dose 300 mg/kg + inducing agent (PTZ) and served as the positive control.

All treatments were administered orally in accordance with the method described by Ngoupaye et al. (2022).

Experimental setup and observation chambers

All experimental procedures were performed in transparent Plexiglas observation chambers designed to allow continuous and unobstructed monitoring of animal behavior. Each chamber measured approximately 30 cm × 15 cm × 15 cm. During each PTZ administration session, animals were placed individually in the chambers and observed for 30 min to record the onset and progression of seizures. The use of transparent chambers allowed precise observation of behavioral and convulsive manifestations without disturbing the animals during the observation sessions.

Kindling induction

Kindling was initiated with an initial convulsive dose of PTZ (70 mg/kg). Forty-eight hours later, animals received subconvulsive doses of PTZ (35 mg/kg) and were observed for 30 min. One hour prior to each subconvulsive PTZ administration, animals received their respective treatments (distilled water, Lagenaria siceraria aqueous extract at different doses) and sodium valproate 300 mg/kg, 30 min before. Subconvulsive doses were repeated every other day for all treatment groups, except the vehicle group, until the occurrence of stage 4 seizures in two consecutive administrations (kindled state) according to Racine’s scale (1972), as follow:

•Stage 0: no response

Stage 0: no response

•Stage 1: ear, facial, or tail contraction

Stage 1: ear, facial, or tail contraction

•Stage 2: head nodding, myoclonic jerks of the head and body twtiches

Stage 2: head nodding, myoclonic jerks of the head and body twtiches

•Stage 3: unilateral forelimb clonus

Stage 3: unilateral forelimb clonus

•Stage 4: rearing with bilateral forelimb clonus

Stage 4: rearing with bilateral forelimb clonus

•Stage 5: generalized tonic-clonic seizures with loss of postural and global motor control

Stage 5: generalized tonic-clonic seizures with loss of postural and global motor control

All kindling induction, Racine scale scoring, and latency measurements were performed live, using three chronometers to accurately record observations in real time. In addition, video recordings were obtained to allow further verification and complementary offline analysis when necessary.

Seizure progression

Seizure progression was recorded by noting the appearance and development of convulsive stages 1–5 in each animal during each subconvulsive PTZ session. This parameter was measured to determine whether the treatments delayed or modified the progression of seizures toward the kindled state.

Latency of seizure appearance on the kindling day

Latency of seizure appearance on the kindling day was recorded in minutes, representing the interval between the administration of the subconvulsive PTZ dose and the onset of stage 4 seizure. This parameter was used to assess the antiepileptogenic effect of the treatments.

Number of seizure repetitions

The number of seizure repetitions was determined by counting how many times each animal repeated stage 4 on the day it was considered kindled. This parameter was used to evaluate the effect of the treatments on seizure recurrence following the establishment of the kindled state.

Seizure duration

Seizure duration was measured as the cumulative time (in minutes) each animal remained in a convulsive state after reaching the kindled state. This parameter was used to assess the effect of the treatments on seizure persistence.

Percentage of animals reaching stage 4

The percentage of animals reaching stage 4 was calculated for each group by dividing the number of animals that reached stage 4 by the total number of animals in the group, multiplied by 100. This parameter was used to evaluate the effect of the treatments on the development of the kindled state.

Biochemical analysis

Twenty-four hours (24 h) following the end of the kindling induction period, the animals were sacrificed by decapitation and immediately dissected for their hippocampi. These hippocampi were weighed and frozen at −20 °C to assay some elements of the GABAergic signalling pathway, such as GABA level and GABA transaminase (GABA-T) activity as well as some parameters of oxidative stress, reduced glutathione (GSH), Catalase activity (CAT), Nitric oxide (NO) and malondialdehyde (MDA), neuroinflammatory markers such likeTNF-α,IL−1β and MMP9 were also assayed. Hippocampi were homogenized (10% w/v) in a porcelain mortar using a 0.1 M phosphate buffer containing 1% Triton X−100 (pH 7.4). The homogenates were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at room temperature, and the supernatants were collected for subsequent assays (Ngoupaye et al., 2020).

Determination of GABA level

GABA levels were determined according to the method described by Moto et al. (2018), based on the formation of a colored complex produced by the reaction of GABA with ninhydrin in an alkaline medium in the presence of glutamate. The reaction mixture consisted of 200 µL of 0.14 M ninhydrin (prepared in 0.5 M carbonate-bicarbonate buffer, pH 9.9) and 100 µL of 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). To this mixture, 100 µL of tissue homogenate was added, and the solution was incubated at 60 °C for 30 min. After cooling, the contents of each tube were transferred into tubes containing 5000 µL of copper tartrate solution and incubated at 25 °C for 10 min. The absorbance was measured at 451 nm using a spectrophotometer against the blank. The absorbance was proportional to the GABA concentration in the sample. Results were expressed as µg of GABA per mg of tissue (Li et al., 2023, Lowe et al., 1958, Sutton and Simmonds, 1974).

Determination of GABA transaminase (GABA-T) activity

GABA transaminase (GABA-T) activity was evaluated according to the method described by Moto et al. (2018), and is based on the formation of a colored complex produced by succinic semialdehyde generated from the degradation of GABA by GABA-T. The reaction medium consisted of 15 µmol of α-oxoglutarate, 15 µmol of GABA, and 10 µg of pyridoxal phosphate. For each assay, either 100 µL of supernatant (test tubes) or 100 µL of 5% methanol (blank) was added, and the final volume of each tube was adjusted to 3 mL with Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4). The tubes were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, 500 µL of 20% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) was added to each tube, and 30 s later, 1000 µL of 12% FeCl₃ was added. The absorbance was immediately measured at 610 nm against the blank using a spectrophotometer, and a second reading was taken 60 s later. The absorbance was proportional to the GABA-T activity in the sample. GABA-T activity was expressed as nmol of GABA degraded per minute per mg of protein (Taberner et al., 1972, Vega Rasgado et al., 2018).

Determination of reduced glutathione (GSH) level

A volume of 1.5 mL of Ellman’s reagent (DTNB; 0.1 mM 5,5′-dithiobis−2-nitrobenzoic acid prepared in 0.3 M phosphate buffer containing 1% sodium citrate) was added to test tubes containing 100 µL of supernatant. Then, 100 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added, and the mixture was incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The absorbance was measured at 412 nm using a BIORAD SmartSpec 3000 spectrophotometer (USA) against the blank. Results were expressed as nmol of GSH per mg of wet tissue (Dimo et al., 2006).

Determination of catalase (CAT) activity

A volume of 375 µL of phosphate buffer (PBS) was added to a test tube containing 25 µL of tissue supernatant. Then, 100 µL of 50 mM hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) was added, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 1 min. Thereafter, 1000 µL of potassium dichromate–acetic acid solution (5%) was added to stop the reaction. The tubes were then heated in a boiling water bath for 10 min. After cooling, the absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a BIORAD SmartSpec 3000 spectrophotometer (USA) against the blank. Catalase activity was determined from a hydrogen peroxide calibration curve and expressed as µmol of H₂O₂ decomposed per minute per mg of protein (Dimo et al., 2006).

Determination of nitric oxide (NO) level

Nitric oxide (NO) levels were determined using the Griess reaction. In each test tube, 200 µL of tissue homogenate was mixed with 200 µL of 1% sulfanilamide (prepared in 5% orthophosphoric acid). For the blank, the homogenate was replaced with the homogenization buffer (PBS + 1% Triton X−100). After thorough mixing, the tubes were incubated in the dark for 5 min. Then, 200 µL of 1% N-(1-naphthyl) ethylenediamine dihydrochloride (NED) prepared in Tris-hydroxymethylaminomethane was added, and the tubes were incubated again in the dark for 5 min. The optical density was measured at 546 nm using a spectrophotometer. NO concentrations were calculated from a calibration curve established using known concentrations of sodium nitrite (NaNO₂). The Griess reaction was carried out under acidic conditions and in the absence of light. NO levels in the samples were expressed as µmol per mg of tissue (Sreejayan et al., 1997).

Determination of malondialdehyde (MDA) level

A volume of 250 µL of the supernatant was transferred into test tubes. Then, 250 µL of 20% trichloroacetic acid (TCA), 500 µL of 0.67% thiobarbituric acid (TBA), and 10 µL of 0.1% butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) were added to each tube. The blank solution contained all reagents except the tissue homogenate. The tubes were tightly closed and incubated in a water bath at 90 °C for 10 min, then cooled under running tap water. They were subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was collected, and the absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a BIORAD SmartSpec 3000 spectrophotometer (USA) against the blank. Results were expressed as nmol of MDA per mg of wet tissue (Dimo et al., 2006, Papastergiadis et al., 2012).

Quantification of TNF-α

Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), a key mediator of neuroinflammation, was measured using a rat-specific sandwich ELISA kit (Elabscience, Cat. No. E-EL-R2856). The assay had a detection range of 15.63–1000 pg/mL and a sensitivity of 9.38 pg/mL. It demonstrates high specificity with no significant cross-reactivity to related proteins. Both intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were < 10%, indicating high precision. All procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantification of IL−1β

Interleukin−1β (IL−1β), a key pro-inflammatory cytokine involved in neuronal hyperexcitability, was quantified using a rat-specific sandwich ELISA kit (Elabscience, Cat. No. E-EL-R0012). The assay had a detection range of 31.25–2000 pg/mL, with a sensitivity of 18.75 pg/mL. Intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were both < 10%, indicating high assay precision. All procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantification of MMP−9

Matrix metalloproteinase−9 (MMP−9), an enzyme involved in extracellular matrix degradation and blood–brain barrier disruption during epileptogenesis, was quantified in serum and brain homogenates using a rat-specific sandwich ELISA kit (Elabscience, Cat. No. E-EL-R3022). The assay had a detection range of 0.31–20 ng/mL and a sensitivity of 0.19 ng/mL. Intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were both < 10%, ensuring high reproducibility. All analyses were conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

The results obtained were analyzed using Graph Pad Prism software version 8.1. They were presented as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). Normal distribution of data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. The one-way ANOVA followed by Newman-keuls post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons of groups in one-variable tests, and two-way ANOVA followed by the Bonferonni test compared the averages for two-variable tests. The tests were significant when p < 0.05.

Results

Effect of the aqueous extract of Lagenaria siceraria on seizure progression and severity in PTZ-kindled Rats

Effect of the aqueous extract of Lagenaria siceraria on seizure progression

The effect of treatments on the day of onset of stages 3 and 4 seizures induced by PTZ kindling was evaluated using a two-way ANOVA, considering both the stage factor (3 and 4) and the treatment factor (negative control, valproate (300 mg/kg), and Lagenaria siceraria at doses of 200 and 400 mg/kg). On stage 3, a significant interaction was observed between the stage and treatment factors for the groups treated with Lagenaria siceraria at 200 mg/kg [F(4,54) = 9.837; p < 0.0001] and at 400 mg/kg [F(4,54) = 9.279; p < 0.0001]. Significant treatment effects with Lagenaria siceraria [F(2,54) = 13.97; p < 0.0001] was observed, compared to the negative control group. This effect was particularly marked at the 400 mg/kg dose. For stage 4, the effects observed followed a similar trend. A significant interaction between stage and treatment was found only with the 400 mg/kg dose [F(4,54) = 3.857; p = 0.0079]. The stage factor remained highly significant in all cases [F(2,54) = 70.63; p < 0.0001], reflecting the expected general progression of seizures. The treatment effect was also significant for valproate [F(2,57) = 10.07; p = 0.0002] and even more so for Lagenaria siceraria at 400 mg/kg [F(2,54) = 11.42; p < 0.0001] and 200 mg/kg [F(2,54) = 3.363; p = 0.0420] Fig 1.

Effect of the aqueous extract of Lagenaria siceraria on seizure severity

In order to assess seizure severity in kindled animals, four key parameters were evaluated: latency to the onset of stage 4 seizures, average seizure duration, frequency of stage 4 episodes, and the percentage of animals reaching stage 4. These indicators provide complementary insights into the intensity and behavioral impact of PTZ-induced seizures, and the potential protective effect of Lagenaria siceraria.

Fig. 2a depicts the variation in the onset time of appearance of a fully kindled state on the kindled day between various treatment groups. There was a kindled effect as animals treated with distilled water showed an average onset time of 5.324 ± 1.225 min to reach the kindled state [F (2, 18) = 202.9; p < 0.0001]. This latency time was increased following administration of different treatments. This latency which was 5.324 ± 1.225 min on the negative control was delayed to 26.54 ± 1.696 min at the dose of 200 mg/kg [F (2, 18) = 62.05; p < 0.0001] and 28.50 ± 1.213 min at the dose 400 mg/kg [F (2, 18) = 120.8; p < 0.0001]. Similarly, a significant increase was observed in animals treated with the reference drug showing an average of 16.26 ± 2.662 min [F (2, 18) = 7.635; p = 0.0040].

Fig. 2b depicts the average duration of seizures on the kindled day between various treatments. Animals from the negative control significantly increased the seizure duration on the kindled day, which moved from 0.000 ± 0.000 s in the vehicle group to 6.167 ± 1.153 s [F (2, 18) = 28.60; p < 0.0001] in the negative control group. The aqueous extract of Lagenaria siceraria at all the doses significantly decreased the duration to 1.264 ± 0.5498 s at the dose 200 mg/kg [F (2, 18) = 12.43; p = 0.0004] and 0.5586 ± 0.2720 s at the dose 400 mg/kg [F (2, 18) = 21.29; p < 0.0001]. Sodium Valproate significantly decreased the seizure duration of seizure to 2.469 ± 0.8244 [F (2, 18) = 5.121; p = 0.0174].

The frequency of stage 4 seizures, assessed on the kindled day, is illustrated in Fig. 2c. Animals from the vehicle group exhibited no seizures (0.000 ± 0.000), whereas those in the negative control group showed a significantly higher frequency of seizures 6.429 ± 1.066; [F(2, 18) = 18.19, p < 0.0001]. Treatment with Lagenaria siceraria significantly reduced the number of stage 4 seizures in a dose-dependent manner. At 200 mg/kg, the frequency dropped to 2.286 ± 0.8371 [F(2, 18) = 6.764, p = 0.0064], and at 400 mg/kg, it was further reduced to 1.000 ± 0.5774 [F(2, 18) = 16.35, p < 0.0001]. Sodium valproate also significantly decreased seizure frequency to 2.143 ± 0.6701 [F(2, 18) = 9.030, p = 0.0019].

In addition to frequency, we also evaluated the percentage of animals reaching stage 4 seizures at the end of the kindling protocol (Table 1). All animals in the negative control group (7/7; 100%) reached stage 4, while only 42.85%, 28.57%, and 14.28% reached this stage in the positive control, Lagenaria siceraria 200 mg/kg, and Lagenaria siceraria 400 mg/kg groups, respectively. This further supports the ability of Lagenaria siceraria to delay or prevent kindling progression. The data on the percentage of animals reaching stage 4 are summarized in the table below.

Discussion

The purpose of the study was to evaluate the antiepileptogenic and anti-epileptic potential of Lagenaria siceraria, following Pentylenetetrazole (PTZ)-induced kindling model. The research focused on the plant’s aqueous extract effect on some mechanisms involved in epilepsy. These include neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neurotransmitter such like GABA imbalance, as well as matrix metalloproteinase−9 (MMP−9) a marker associated with BBB disruption that exacerbates epileptognesis. Kindling is a widely used model in neuroscience to simulate epilepsy and investigate the mechanisms underlying epileptogenesis. In the PTZ (Pentylenetetrazol) kindling method, animals are repeatedly administered sub-threshold doses of PTZ, a chemical that induces seizures by acting as an antagonist of GABA-A receptors (Shimada and Yamagata, 2018). While the initial doses produce mild, often undetectable responses in the brain, continued exposure gradually leads to more intense and frequent seizures (Samokhina and Samokhin, 2018). This process mimics the way epilepsy can evolve into a chronic condition in humans, where seizures become more pronounced and spontaneous over time (Löscher et al., 2020; Tadayuki et al., 2018).

The progressive nature of seizures was clearly demonstrated in this study through the behavior of the negative control group, whose seizures increased in severity over time. This progression was evidenced by the successive advancement from Stage 0 (absence of seizures) to full kindling stages, with animals reaching Stage 4 after repeated sub-convulsive doses of PTZ. In fact, the animals of the control group consistently exhibited two consecutive Stage 4 seizures, a hallmark of the kindling model, indicating the establishment of a chronic epileptic state (Goddard, 1967, Racine, 1972, Zhen et al., 2016). The mean day of repetition of Stage 4 seizures was observed to be day 6, highlighting the rapid progression toward chronic seizure activity as the kindling model advanced illustrating the severity of the epileptic condition in the negative control group.

The plant’s significant delay in the appearance of both Stage 3 and Stage 4 seizures, mirroring the effects of valproate, suggests a promising therapeutic potential. Remarkably, Lagenaria siceraria outperformed valproate in delaying the onset of Stage 3 seizures, positioning it as an effective agent for early-stage seizure management. This effect may be due to its capacity to reduce the recruitment of new neurons into the epileptic network, thereby slowing epileptogenesis, an essential advantage over current treatments, which are not designed to address this critical aspect of epilepsy (Bertram, 2007). These effects suggest that Lagenaria siceraria prevents seizure progression and exerts antiepileptic effects.

To evaluate the progression of seizures, we also assessed seizure intensity, which reflects the degree of disruption within neural circuits (Xiao et al., 2024, Guo et al., 2024). Increased severity suggests heightened excitability and diminished resistance (Ngoupaye et al., 2022). In this study, intensity was measured through three parameters recorded on the kindled day: seizure onset latency, frequency, and duration. Effectively managing intensity is critical for protecting the brain’s structural and functional integrity. Lagenaria siceraria significantly reduced these severity measures, highlighting its ability to reinforce and stabilize neural circuits, offering neuroprotection and resilience against epileptogenesis progression and related epilepsy damage.

Kindling profoundly affects the GABAergic system, leading to significant alterations that disrupt inhibitory neurotransmission and promote epileptogenesis (Kajita et al., 2024). The kindling model is recognized for inducing progressive neuronal hyperexcitability, accompanied by profound alterations in the GABAergic system. A central mechanism involves the adaptive activation of GABA transaminase (GABA-T). Normally, this enzyme regulates GABA metabolism, but in the context of kindling, its activity is pathologically upregulated. This activation diverts GABA from its inhibitory role, converting it into succinate, an intermediate of the Krebs cycle, thereby allowing the brain to meet the increased energy demands associated with neuronal hyperexcitability. However, this metabolic survival strategy accelerates GABA loss and permanently weakens synaptic inhibition, creating a vicious cycle that sustains the progression of epileptogenesis (Ravasz et al., 2017).

In parallel, oxidative stress induced by repeated seizures increases the vulnerability of GABAergic interneurons, which constitute the primary source of GABA production. Their degeneration due to reactive oxygen species further reduces GABA synthesis, further compromising inhibitory neurotransmission and promoting excessive neuronal excitability (Imam et al., 2024). Therefore, the decrease in GABA levels observed during kindling results from both accelerated degradation through adaptively activated GABA-T and reduced production due to the loss of GABAergic interneurons under oxidative stress. These convergent mechanisms profoundly weaken inhibitory tone, favoring hyperexcitability and the progression toward a chronic epileptogenic state.

Building on the mechanistic framework established earlier, the observed effects of Lagenaria siceraria and valproate can be interpreted in light of their capacity to restore inhibitory balance within hyperexcitable neural circuits. In our study, the negative control group displayed the expected decrease in GABA levels alongside an increase in GABA-T activity, consistent with the kindling-induced disruption of inhibitory neurotransmission and the adaptive diversion of GABA into energy metabolism.

Remarkably, treatment with Lagenaria siceraria and valproate reversed these maladaptive changes. The increase in GABA levels and concomitant reduction in GABA-T activity suggest that both interventions successfully mitigate the kindling-induced depletion of inhibitory neurotransmitters. This effect likely reflects multiple interrelated processes: first, the preservation of GABAergic interneurons from oxidative stress-induced damage, thereby maintaining their capacity to produce GABA; second, the normalization of GABA-T activity, preventing excessive catabolism of GABA for energy purposes; and third, the overall restoration of inhibitory synaptic tone, which counters the hyperexcitable state induced by kindling (Ji et al., 2025).

In essence, these findings provide functional validation of the mechanisms outlined in the introduction: by modulating GABA metabolism, Lagenaria siceraria and valproate reinforce inhibitory signaling, reduce hyperexcitability, and contribute to the attenuation of epileptogenesis. This highlights the therapeutic potential of Lagenaria siceraria as a neuroprotective agent capable of intervening at multiple nodes of the pathological cascade initiated by kindling.

The disruption of inhibitory neurotransmission induced by kindling, notably by reducing GABA availability and upregulating GABA transaminase (GABA-T), the key enzyme responsible for its degradation, results in uncontrolled neuronal hyperexcitability (Kabysheva et al., 2009). Sustained hyperactivity imposes elevated metabolic demands on neurons, driving excessive mitochondrial activity. This overactivation, while initially compensatory, generates high levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) as byproducts of mitochondrial respiration. Under physiological conditions, antioxidant systems maintain ROS at manageable levels; however, during hyperexcitability, their overproduction surpasses endogenous defenses and triggers oxidative stress (Weidinger et al., 2023).

Oxidative stress profoundly disrupts neuronal integrity. ROS modify protein structures, impairing enzymatic function and intracellular signaling, while DNA oxidation activates apoptotic pathways that accelerate neurodegeneration. In parallel, lipid peroxidation destabilizes neuronal membranes, compromising structural integrity and synaptic communication (Holmström and Finkel, 2014; Koroveqssis et al., 2023). This process yields malondialdehyde (MDA), a well-recognized marker of lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage.In line with these mechanisms, our study revealed that the negative control group exhibited elevated MDA and nitric oxide (NO) levels, accompanied by a weakened antioxidant defense system characterized by reduced glutathione (GSH) and catalase (CAT) activity. The depletion of GSH restricts the neutralization of ROS, while diminished CAT activity limits hydrogen peroxide clearance, thereby aggravating oxidative injury (Roganovic et al., 2019).This cascade of oxidative damage intensifies neuronal dysfunction, particularly within already vulnerable inhibitory circuits, further weakening synaptic inhibition and promoting seizure propagation. Consequently, oxidative stress and hyperexcitability form a self-reinforcing vicious cycle that accelerates epileptogenesis (Vezzani et al., 2016).

Importantly, Lagenaria siceraria demonstrated the capacity to break this pathological cycle. Treatment significantly increased GSH and CAT levels while reducing MDA and NO, indicating enhanced antioxidant defenses and decreased lipid peroxidation. By limiting oxidative stress and preserving neuronal function, the plant contributes to the stabilization of neural circuits and restores inhibitory balance. These findings position Lagenaria siceraria as a promising therapeutic candidate capable of mitigating oxidative damage, curbing hyperexcitability, and interrupting the progression of epileptogenesis (Roganovic et al., 2018; Ngoupaye et al., 2022).

In the kindling model, oxidative stress and neuroinflammation appear as two closely linked processes. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), produced in excess during neuronal hyperexcitability, act as the primary trigger for inflammatory pathways (Teleanu et al., 2022). ROS specifically activate the TLR4–NF-κB pathway, stimulating the production of inflammatory mediators such as the metalloproteinase MMP−9 (Huang et al., 2016; Ghasemi-Tarie et al., 2022). Once activated, MMP−9 plays a pivotal role: on one hand, it promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL−1β, and on the other hand, it remodels the extracellular matrix by releasing stored factors and activating latent cytokines (Rempe et al., 2018, Pijet et al., 2024). This dual effect sustains a feedback loop in which TNF-α and IL−1β, in turn, further stimulate MMP−9, amplifying the inflammatory response (Frigerio et al., 2012).

Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin−1 beta (IL−1β) are central pro-inflammatory cytokines that significantly contribute to epileptogenesis and epilepsy by disrupting neuronal and glial function. TNF-α plays a critical role by altering the balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission. It enhances the surface expression of excitatory AMPA receptors on neurons while decreasing inhibitory GABA-A receptor expression. This imbalance shifts neural networks toward hyperexcitability, a key feature in the development and progression of seizures. Furthermore, TNF-α activates microglia and astrocytes, inducing them to release additional pro-inflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species (ROS), which exacerbate neuronal damage and hyperexcitability (Steinhäuser et al., 2016, Patel et al., 2019).

Similarly, IL−1β exerts its effects by binding to its receptor, IL−1R1, and enhancing excitatory neurotransmission. It increases NMDA receptor activity through phosphorylation, prolonging excitatory currents and making neurons more susceptible to seizure activity. IL−1β also promotes glutamate release while reducing GABAergic transmission, further tipping the balance toward excitation. This cytokine drives aberrant neurogenesis and synaptic remodeling, forming hyperexcitable circuits that sustain chronic seizures (Dayang et al., 2019). MMP−9 plays a central role in disrupting the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and amplifying neuroinflammation in epilepsy. As a matrix metalloproteinase, MMP−9 degrades extracellular matrix (ECM) components and basement membrane proteins essential for BBB integrity, including tight junction proteins such as occludin, claudins, and zonula occludens−1 (ZO−1) (Cudna et al., 2017, Pijet et al., 2018, Hu et al., 2024).

This degradation increases BBB permeability, allowing toxins, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and peripheral immune cells to infiltrate the brain parenchyma. Among these infiltrating cells, macrophages release cytokines such as IL−6 and TNF-α, while Th17 cells produce IL−17, further amplifying neuroinflammation and disrupting the excitatory–inhibitory balance. These peripheral cells interact with resident microglia, which respond by releasing additional pro-inflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species (ROS), intensifying neuronal hyperexcitability and contributing to seizure susceptibility (Furtado et al., 2018, Vitaliti et al., 2019).Chronic activation of these pathways sustains MMP−9 over expression, perpetuating BBB disruption, immune cell infiltration, and microglial activation. Collectively, these events drive synaptic remodeling, aberrant neurogenesis, and persistent seizure activity, establishing a self-perpetuating mechanism underlying seizure chronicity (Bronisz et al., 2023).

Our experimental results fit perfectly within this framework. In the negative control group, we observed a significant increase in MMP−9, TNF-α, and IL−1β, confirming that kindling promotes the establishment of this self-sustaining inflammatory loop. Conversely, treatment with Lagenaria siceraria led to a marked reduction in all three parameters. This effect reflects inhibition of the ROS–TLR4–NF-κB pathway and attenuation of the inflammatory loop maintained by MMP−9 and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL−1β and TNF-α.

By acting in this way, the Lagenaria siceraria extract contributes to stabilizing the blood–brain barrier, limiting immune cell infiltration, and protecting neuronal circuits against chronic hyperexcitability. These anti-inflammatory effects, combined with its antioxidant properties, position this plant as a promising candidate to interrupt the vicious cycle of oxidative stress–inflammation–epileptogenesis and offer a therapeutic alternative in epilepsy (Vezzani et al., 2016).

In conclusion, these results shows the antiepileptic and antiepileptogenic effects of Lagenaria siceraria aqueous extract and further highlight its potential activity as s an effective natural treatment for epilepsy. Further studies are still needed to clarify on bioactive constituents of Lagenaria siceraria driving its action.
