Deep learning-based high-information-content graph representation of early stage bacterial biofilms
Authors: Lev E. Nersesyan, Daniil A. Boiko, Saniyat Kurbanalieva, Lilya U. Dzhemileva, Konstantin S. Kozlov, Valentine P. Ananikov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial biofilms are complex, spatially organized microbial communities that exhibit enhanced resistance to antibiotics and contribute to chronic infections. Understanding their structure, especially during early formation stages, is critical for developing effective intervention strategies. Here, we present a high-resolution computational framework that models biofilms as undirected interaction graphs, where individual bacterial cells are vertices and predicted intercellular interactions are edges. Combining microscopy visualization and deep learning, we developed a pipeline that integrates Mask R-CNN for cell segmentation and a custom neural network (BINet) for interaction prediction. The described graph-based representation enables quantitative analysis of biofilm growth, identification of recurrent structural motifs, and classification of substrate-specific colonization patterns. We demonstrate the utility of this approach in predicting both the developmental stage and material type from image-derived graph features. This study provides the tool for revealing nonobvious patterns of biofilm organization and describes a scalable, high-information-content approach for the automated analysis of microbial communities, opening new possibilities for systems-level microbiological research.

Introduction

Currently, machine learning is playing an inestimable role in many scientific fields, with key applications in biology and life sciences1–3. Machine learning makes it possible to significantly accelerate research with the development of new orthogonal methods4,5. Using neural networks and classical machine learning algorithms, tasks that are extremely labor intensive and require much time are performed much quicker, more efficiently, and often even more accurately. In addition, machine learning allows to find non-obvious patterns, facilitating research and make discoveries in various fields6.

In cell biology, machine learning has shown promising application potential7–10. On the one hand, well-trained ML models allow to make predictions of a wide range of characteristics with high accuracy, but no less important application is the analysis of these predictions—it allows the study of complex relationships between different natural characteristics, which are often not obvious to humans11,12.

Biofilms are extremely widespread in nature13–15 and represent a distinctive form of bacterial existence, exhibiting characteristics analogous to those of a multicellular organism. The interconnection between individual bacterial cells within a biofilm presents unique survival opportunities16. The phenomenon of quorum sensing plays a pivotal role in the formation and dispersal of biofilms. Quorum sensing is subject to both intra- and extracellular signaling17. The formation of biofilms is associated with the chronic carriage of bacteria, the occurrence of nosocomial infections, and the development of antibiotic resistance18. Recent studies have demonstrated that bacteria in biofilms exhibit greater resistance to antibiotics than their planktonic counterparts do18. Furthermore, an imperative biofouling parameter is the regulation of biofilm formation based on quorum sensing (quorum suppression). To date, there is a scarcity of comprehensive sources of information on biofouling and/or biofilm formation in different environmental conditions and related problems, as well as strategies to control biofouling based on quorum suppression19.

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is one of the most extensively studied bacteria with respect to quorum sensing, in vitro biofilm formation, and the development of antibiotic tolerance. It is postulated that biofilms comprising P. aeruginosa contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of numerous chronic infections, including those affecting immunocompromised individuals and those with cystic fibrosis. Given that P. aeruginosa is highly stable in the environment, possesses a thick cell wall, and rapidly adapts to diverse changes due to a distinctive mechanism of interaction with extracellularly located DNA, it represents an optimal object for investigating the patterns of biolayer formation on a range of surfaces20.

As an example of the use of ML in biofilm research, there are known works that consider cells as separate units21,22. This approach allows initial conclusions about the rate of biofilm development to be drawn and even kinetic models to be built. Significant amount of work is done on predicting activity and designing chemicals against biofilms23–28. ML was also used to improve biofilm detection assays29–32. Moreover, previously we described an approach for studying biofilms using neural network-based pipeline for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image processing. This allowed us to observe kinetics of biofilm growth and analyze biocide effects33. Machine learning approach to access massive synthetic microscopy data was developed34.

Design of new antibacterial drugs and antiseptic formulations has shown that consideration at the biofilm level (rather than individual bacterial cells level) needs to be targeted35–37. The formation of biofilm is an extremely interesting and complex process, the analysis of which is greatly simplified with the use of ML38.

It is pivotal to consider cells not as separate (unconnected) units but as interacting parts of a whole system, if the corresponding numerical analysis and visualization could be performed39 (Fig. 1a). This could allow taking into account the interactions of cells with each other to study the development of bacterial cell clusters over time and supra-cellular organization of the whole system. However, a huge number of parallel processes can occur simultaneously in a biofilm (the analysis of each of which is extremely difficult even in a single cell)40, each of which can affect the whole system framework. Valuable conclusions can only be drawn with high-performance tracking and reconstruction of the biofilm network. Different mathematical frameworks exist for analysis of complex matter (including biofilms). Many of them rely on representation of matter as point clouds (2D or 3D) with the following topological data analysis (TDA) methods41–43. Point clouds for biofilm representation can be obtained with 3D reconstruction from confocal microscopy44. TDA can effectively model the motion of cell tissues45. Studying biofilm surface topology can help to predict their wetting with antibacterial agents46. Other methods for studying biofilm morphology and evolution in time require modeling and simulation47. There also exist analysis methodologies which use single cell segmentation on 3D reconstructions48. However, ignoring the geometric properties of objects (metric distances, angles, surfaces, or volumes) makes TDA methods scalable to a wide range of living and nonliving forms of matter. Nevertheless, the usage of geometric information in biofilm encoding can increase quality of performing downstream tasks.

A combination of imaging (with scanning electron microscopy) and machine learning to quantify biofilm growth (Fig. 1b) was employed in the work. We have found that these machine learning techniques represent versatile approaches to data analysis that can facilitate the interpretation of large datasets with minimal human intervention through tasks such as image segmentation and cell clustering.

In this work, we developed a mathematical approach to consider biofilms as a graph in which the vertices are the bacteria themselves, and the edges are the connections between the bacteria (Fig. 1). We have applied different machine learning algorithms to each step of our pipeline, including cell detection, interaction prediction, and downstream tasks. Graph representation captures cell–cell interactions, network structure, and motif patterns that are not apparent from pointwise or density-based models. Tracking the resulting graphs over time would allow analyzing how a bacterial colony develops with relevance to systems biology and parallels to multicellular organization. We have shown that this approach can be scaled to large fields of view.

Results

Pointwise consideration of cells in biofilms is a common approach, which has a significant disadvantage: it allows measuring only simple individual parameters (such as the number of bacteria, their size distribution, etc.), but is not able to describe the behavior of the colony. To overcome the limitation, in this work, we describe a new digital model of an undirected graph with vertices as cells and edges as the presence of interactions between them. The model allows to learn more about the biofilm as a whole system.

For details about studied system and methods selection, see Section Justification of the model system and computational approach below, which outlines the rationale behind the selection of the biological model, imaging technique, graph-based representation, and machine learning strategy.

We used two main stages in the construction of the interaction graph. First, we perform cell instance segmentation via the Mask R-CNN. Then, we select combinations of two bacteria from the set of obtained masks, and for a particular pair, we use a neural network with custom architecture to predict whether there is an interaction within this pair. In other words, at the first stage, we define the set of vertices of our graph, and at the second stage, we define the set of edges.

Individual cell detection

Mask R-CNN was chosen as the architecture for cell instance segmentation. This architecture is based on R-CNN (Region-CNN), which extracts region proposals and calculates CNN features for them, and then classifies the region into one of the classes; in our case, there are only two classes: background and bacteria.

It is important to acknowledge the complexity of the task at hand, which is characterized by challenges. In particular, it is difficult to determine whether a dividing cell no longer becomes a single cell but becomes two interacting cells. Similarly, it is quite difficult to grasp the transition when two or more interacting cells, releasing a polysaccharide matrix, become visually transformed into a single shapeless substance, which is characteristic of a mature biofilm and which, generally speaking, should no longer apply in our approach to the bacterial graph.

To improve the predictive ability of the segmentation model, the SEM images were augmented at training time: vertical flipping, horizontal flipping, and random variation in brightness and contrast were applied to each image of the training dataset with a fixed probability. The resulting metrics on the test dataset of the segmentation model are shown in Fig. 2b. The model is particularly good at identifying large and medium–sized bacteria; however, it has reduced performance in identifying small bacteria. This may be due to the similarity of small bacteria to various bacterial products or reduced visibility of small bacteria behind clusters of larger bacteria. Cell interaction prediction

The task of classifying the presence or absence of interactions between two cells is a complex problem. In addition to the nuances common to the segmentation task, bacteria can interact with each other in different ways. There can be various division states, and cells can be touching without bonds or with bonds. Sometimes, on a microscope image, it is not possible to determine whether the bacteria interact or are just next to each other; for example, when they overlap in the image of one bacterial cell on another. It is often difficult for even a human expert to determine whether there is an interaction between two bacteria.

As a result, in this study, we define an “interaction” based on 2D SEM morphology and manual annotations: an edge in the graph represents a visually detectable cell–cell association on the imaged surface (e.g., direct contact, with or without apparent connecting structures), rather than a specific biological mechanism. Therefore, the resulting interaction graph should be interpreted primarily as a quantitative description of early-stage biofilm architecture, rather than as a direct readout of underlying biology.

This definition inevitably introduces simplifications: SEM provides an effectively two-dimensional, surface view of a 3D system. Moreover, distinct biological processes can produce similar SEM-visible morphologies, making a strict mechanistic attribution of any single edge type impossible. Despite these limitations, the interaction graph provides a reproducible structural phenotype that enables systematic comparisons across substrates and time points using interpretable graph features.

To solve this problem, we attempted to use classical machine learning algorithms. However, the XGBoost implementation of the gradient boosting algorithm did not yield good results (F1 score of 0.81 for noninteraction and 0.68 for interaction). The model used the following features: the distance between the centers of the bacteria masks, the absolute value of the cosine of the angle between the principal axes, the harmonic mean of the bacteria areas, the product of the areas, and the absolute value of the ratio of the bacteria areas.

Finally, a custom architecture (Fig. 3a, visualized using VisualTorch package49), BacteriaInteractionNet (BINet), was developed to solve this problem. BINet is a sequence of 3 two-dimensional convolutions, after which the activation function (ReLU), batch normalization, and max pooling layers are located. A linear layer is placed after this sequence. Cross-entropy was chosen as the loss function, and Adam was chosen as the optimizer. Although the above architecture yields the best results of all the tested architectures, the accuracy is still not sufficient to use BINet as a tool. For example, prediction errors often occur when considering bacteria that are far apart. There were also outlier cells that the model identified as interacting with a large number of bacteria that could be absolutely any distance away. Basically, the model lacked a sense of distance: the observed limitation reflects fundamental characteristics of CNN architectures—namely, finite receptive fields that constrain global spatial perception. These architectural constraints are exacerbated by dataset-specific factors: variable magnification levels across SEM images and the relatively small annotated dataset (1500 images).

To solve this problem, a threshold value in pixels was manually selected for a fixed scale of microscope images. All bacterial pairs located on the image beyond this value are automatically considered noninteracting and are not fed into the model at all.

Since the images in our dataset are of different scales, using a single threshold value would not give good results. We precalculated the sum of the pixels in the image section responsible for the scale. We correlated these values with the corresponding scale, which was quite easy since the variety of scales was limited. Thus, we define the scale and then multiply the threshold value by the inverse of the proportionality factor between the scale we defined and the scale for which the threshold value was chosen. The approach is illustrated in Fig. 3c. The performance metrics can be seen in Fig. 3b.

Interaction motif mining

When considering biofilms as a unified system, the search for similar fragments is an interesting task that will allow to better understand the structure of biofilms. That is, patterns in the way bacteria are arranged in pairs and groups can be identified. The developed approach of representing biofilms as a graph can be useful to solve this problem. For this purpose, each edge in the graph is assigned a certain category (type) depending on how the pair of bacteria forming the edge is located relative to each other. Thus, the problem is reduced to searching for motifs (the most frequently occurring fragments) in a graph with several types of edges.

Before searching for motifs, it is first necessary to understand how many types of edges can be identified in general. Since biofilms are very complex systems, distinguishing their interaction types by a simple visual inspection is difficult. Instead, we calculate features that describe their relative location, such as the absolute value of the cosine and sine of the angle formed by the intersection of straight lines obtained by the continuation of the main axes of bacteria, the distance between the centers of bacteria, the lengths of the main axes, and the product of some of the mentioned features. In addition, we estimate the degree of interaction between bacteria, i.e., how close they are and how closely they interact. For example, the area of contact. However, this requires that we introduce a threshold at which we will assume that there is a contact. This parameter for one of the scales was selected manually, on the basis of how we would like to determine the interaction, and for the others, it was translated in proportion to the ratio of scales. This allows to determine the area of contact with fixed accuracy. Once the required features have been determined, we can proceed to motif mining. However, since the task is to find interaction motifs, only pairs of bacteria that directly interact are suitable for mining. To identify such pairs, we run the images of biofilms through a pipeline that transforms them into a bacterial graph. Traversing the resulting graph allows to obtain all the interacting pairs and compute the specified features for them directly.

In order to mine motifs from the dataset, we performed the following steps (Fig. 5a): first, the dimensionality of the data was reduced using t-SNE, then the resulting points were clustered using DBSCAN. Looking more closely at the features within the largest class (Fig. 5c), it was observed that the distribution of the absolute sine value is not uniform, and the largest class can be divided manually by the threshold value. This way, we got the labels of the edges in our undirected graph.

The resulting motifs and their distributions in the original dataset are shown on Fig. 5d, e. Now that the edge types have been defined, we can construct a graph with different edge types—going through all possible configurations of edges in a triangle and looking for isomorphic ones in graphs find the most frequently occurring fragments, i.e., motifs of fully connected graph on three vertices (Fig. 5f).

This study presents a computational methodology for the high-resolution graph-based representation and analysis of early-stage bacterial biofilms. While the graph encodes SEM-visible structural associations rather than molecular mechanisms, these architectural descriptors enable quantitative comparison across conditions (time, substrate) and provide hypotheses for future mechanistic in future studies.

Discussion

In this work, we have considered bacteria not individually, but architecturally, in the form of a graph of bacterial interactions. The bacteria are the vertices of the graph, and the presence of interactions between two bacteria is an edge.

A pipeline was developed to construct bacterial graphs. In the first step, individual bacteria are segmented from the image by Mask R-CNN. Next, our developed architecture determines whether there is interaction between two bacteria or not. For this step, a preprocessing step was added in the form of a threshold value of the distance in pixels between the bacteria masks. This threshold value is recalculated for each scale.

Using the constructed graphs and the subsequent calculation of their features, classifiers were trained to determine which material the microscope image belongs to. Additionally, regression models were trained to determine the age of the biofilm image relative to the moment of biofilm nucleation.

Then, with information about which bacterial pairs interact and which do not, we considered the interacting pairs and calculated features that could help to classify pairs by the mutual location of bacteria in them. Then, by reducing the dimensionality of the data and applying a clustering algorithm, we obtain the separated classes. In one of the most common classes, we select a separate class, which is separated using the threshold value of one of the calculated features at the previous stage. The obtained classification, in turn, allows us to find common, more complex structures, for example, different triplets of interacting bacteria.

The approach we have developed permits the visualization of different patterns when single bacteria and their clusters are visualized, thereby facilitating a better understanding of biofilm growth dynamics. This, in turn, allows for the accurate differentiation of the locations of individual bacteria and their clusters, which will facilitate the prediction of biofilm growth and provide an enhanced understanding of their architecture.

While this study demonstrates the effectiveness of a graph-based machine learning approach for analyzing early-stage biofilm formation using microscopy visualization, some limitations and future research directions should be acknowledged. Although the method was developed for early biofilm stages, its core principles—cell segmentation, interaction modeling, and motif analysis—can be transferred to later developmental phases, including mature biofilms with complex extracellular matrices. Further improvements in model generalization, such as training on diverse microbial species and surface types, as well as incorporating time-resolved imaging to capture dynamic interactions, would enhance the robustness and scope of the approach. Future work may also explore coupling this method with chemical or genetic data to link structural features with functional outcomes, ultimately advancing the understanding and control of microbial communities.

Methods

Justification of the model system and computational approach

Biofilm stage

We have particularly focused on the early stage of biofilm formation. At this stage, bacteria initiate surface adhesion, activate quorum sensing, and begin spatial organization—processes that determine biofilm structure and future resistance to antimicrobials. As mature biofilms exhibit heightened tolerance, early-stage analysis offers a strategic window for intervention. Moreover, the nascent biofilm is highly responsive to substrate properties, making it an ideal model for studying bacterial-material interactions and guiding anti-biofouling strategies.

Visualization method

SEM provides a powerful visualization technique for studying bacterial biofilms due to its ability to deliver high-resolution, surface-sensitive images at the nanometer scale. Unlike optical methods, SEM captures detailed morphological features of bacterial cells and their spatial organization within the biofilm matrix, which are critical for resolving cell–cell interfaces and extracellular structures. This level of detail is indispensable for accurate segmentation, interaction analysis, and graph-based modeling, particularly during early biofilm development when structural features are subtle yet biologically significant. In the context of this study, high-resolution SEM imaging was essential to validate the reliability of the developed machine learning pipeline by providing the structural granularity required for testing the computational approach.

Choice of microorganism

Pseudomonas aeruginosa was selected as the model organism due to its well-characterized biofilm-forming capabilities, robust environmental adaptability, and clinical relevance. As an environmentally persistent pathogen, it plays a noticeable role in chronic infections and exhibits a high degree of antibiotic resistance when embedded in biofilms. Its complex quorum sensing network and ability to form structured communities on diverse substrates make it a suitable candidate for studying biofilm architecture and validating machine-learning-based analytical approaches.

Rationale for graph-based modeling of biofilms

Modeling biofilms as undirected graphs, where individual bacterial cells are represented as vertices and intercellular interactions as edges, provides a powerful framework for capturing the structural complexity and spatial organization inherent in microbial communities. This abstraction enables the quantification of topological features such as connectivity, clustering, and motifs, which reflect underlying biological processes like adhesion, growth, and communication. Unlike traditional pixel-based or density-driven methods, graph-based models offer interpretable metrics that mirror the supracellular architecture of biofilms and allow for systematic comparison across time points and substrates.

Use of machine learning/deep learning approaches

The inherent complexity and heterogeneity of SEM biofilm images necessitate computational methods capable of learning non-linear, high-dimensional patterns. Deep learning models, particularly convolutional neural networks, are well-suited for cell instance segmentation and interaction prediction tasks due to their ability to extract hierarchical features from visual data. Classical machine learning methods, used in downstream regression and classification, complement these capabilities by enabling interpretable prediction based on engineered graph features. Together, these approaches form a robust pipeline for automated analysis of biofilm structure with minimal human intervention.

Selection of features and metrics

The selected graph features—such as edge-to-node ratio, average vertex degree, and motif frequency—encapsulate biologically relevant properties of biofilm networks, including interaction density and spatial organization. These features are both interpretable and sensitive to changes in biofilm development, enabling classification of surface materials and temporal progression. Their utility in both supervised learning and unsupervised clustering demonstrates their robustness and relevance to diverse analytical tasks, from substrate discrimination to motif mining.

Validation strategy

The model was validated experimentally using SEM images of Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilms grown on distinct polymer substrates (PLA, PC) over multiple time points (2–22 h). This setup enabled assessment of the computational pipeline across biologically and physically diverse conditions. The resulting graph features correlated meaningfully with substrate type and biofilm age, confirming that the model captures physiologically relevant patterns.

Scalability and generalization

The modular architecture of the computational pipeline ensures scalability to large datasets and broader applicability across microbial species and imaging modalities. By normalizing image scale and applying a flexible graph construction strategy, the approach is compatible with various SEM conditions and can be adapted to other high-resolution imaging techniques. Additionally, the underlying algorithms are general-purpose and can be retrained or fine-tuned for different bacterial morphologies or interaction types, facilitating widespread application in microbiology and biofilm research.

Microscopy samples preparation

Two distinct types of plastics were employed for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) studies with respect to the dynamics of biofouling and the formation of biofilms by the bacterium Pseudomonas aeruginosa. The selected plastics were polylactide (PLA) and polycarbonate (PC). All samples of the polymers under investigation were produced via a desktop 3D printer, the Picaso Designer X PRO (PICASO 3D, Russia). The printing procedure was conducted as follows: an appropriate adhesive, such as paper tape or polypropylene tape, was applied to the working surface of the glass table of the printer, depending on the material to be printed. The printing process was conducted via a preprepared job file. Once the printing process was complete, the printer table and the resulting samples were allowed to cool. The FDM objects of the circular cross section were then removed from the surface of the printer table. The samples were subsequently cleaned of residual adhesive and tape, and the edges were removed.

Bacterial membrane constriction is a process of transverse bacterial cell division in which the cytoplasmic membrane and cell wall undergo a structural change, with the result that they wither toward the center of the cell. This process forms a septum that divides the mother cell into two genetically identical daughter cells. This process, known as binary fission (or simple division in half), entails the synthesis of a new membrane, the divergence of DNA toward the poles, and the formation of a ring structure that “laces” the cell, thereby facilitating rapid bacterial growth and reproduction53.

The bacterial membrane jumper, otherwise known as a mesosome, is a structure that is formed by the constriction of the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane. Mesosomes fulfill a variety of essential functions, including the regulation of osmotic pressure, the transport of substances, and energy metabolism. In addition, these organelles are involved in the process of cell wall formation. Consequently, the term “bacterial jumper” is now considered obsolete, as mesosomes, which fulfill pivotal yet divergent functions in the life of the bacterial cell, have emerged as the prevailing terminology54,55.

The interactions of bacterial cells with neighboring cells can be categorized into several distinct classifications. These interactions include chemical communication (i.e., quorum sensing and gene exchange through conjugation), bacterial apoptosis (i.e., sacrificial suicide for the benefit of the colony or even cannibalism), and DNA transfer via viruses (i.e., transduction). These mechanisms are generally increasing in bacterial communities, including biofilm formation, and are often a means of adaptation and survival56.

Cultivation of biofilms on abiotic substrates

In this study, we examined the bacterium Pseudomonas aeruginosa. For the cultivation of microorganisms, a liquid medium of Lysogeny Broth (LB) was employed. This consisted of the following components: tryptone (Diaem)—10 g/dm³, casein (Diaem)—10 g/dm³, yeast extract (Diaem)—5 g/dm³, and sodium chloride (Diaem)—10 g/dm³. The nutrient medium was subjected to a pre-sterilization process in a MaXterile autoclave (Daihan Scientific, Korea) at a pressure of 1.15 atm for 45 min. The cultivation was conducted under aerobic conditions for a period of 22 hours in 750-ml shaking flasks at a temperature of 36 °C.

Cultivation of microorganisms

The investigation of biofouling in 3D-printed plastics employed sterile glass vials. The plastics were sterilized via a 96% ethanol solution. To examine biofilm formation by selected microorganisms, plastic samples were placed in sterile glass vials containing nutrient medium (LB) that had been seeded with microorganisms at a concentration of 5 × 107 CFU/mL for a period of 2–22 h. The glass vials containing the samples were subsequently heated at 36 °C. To prepare the samples for further examination by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), the microorganisms on the surface of the plastics were fixed with glutaraldehyde for one hour. Thereafter, the plastic samples were washed with distilled water for one hour and dried under vacuum to completely remove moisture.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging

Once the moisture had been completely removed from the surface of the plastics, the samples were affixed to an aluminum stage with double-sided conductive carbon tape (TedPella, Inc.). The junction between the sample and the stage was lubricated with conductive graphite adhesive (Pelco Colloidal Graphite, TedPella, Inc.). The samples were subjected to Au/Pd deposition via magnetron sputtering with a Cressington 208HR device. Subsequent analysis was conducted via a Hitachi SU8000 field emission scanning electron microscope. Images were obtained via a secondary electron detector at an accelerating voltage of 2–5 kV and at a magnification of ×5000–20,000. In total, approximately 2000 images were captured.

Machine learning pipeline

To implement the discussed machine learning pipeline, we used Python, including Numpy, Scikit-learn, OpenCV, PyTorch, PyTorch Lightning, Matplotlib, and Albumentations (for data augmentation) packages.

The segmentation model was based on a pretrained ResNet 50 FPN Mask R-CNN; as a box predictor, Fast R-CNN was chosen. The Adam optimizer and StepLR scheduler were also used, and the model was trained with the following parameters: momentum, 0.9; weight_decay, 0.0005; number of epochs, 70; and batch size, 10. The augmentation with Albumentations consisted of the following pipeline: VerticalFlip (p = 0.5), HorizontalFlip (p = 0.5), and RandomBrightnessContrast (p = 0.2), where p is the probability.

The classification model has the following architecture (1) convolutional blocks: Conv2d(5, 16, kernel_size=3, stride=1, padding=1), ReLU, BatchNorm2d(16), MaxPool2d(kernel_size=2, stride=2), Conv2d(16, 32, kernel_size=3, stride=1, padding=1), ReLU, BatchNorm2d(32), MaxPool2d(kernel_size=2, stride=2), Conv2d(32, 64, kernel_size=3, stride=1, padding=1), ReLU, BatchNorm2d(64), MaxPool2d(kernel_size=2, stride=2), and (2) fully-connected layer Linear(64, 10). The sizes are given in brackets.

Additionally, the cross-entropy loss function, Adam optimizer, and 2D adaptive average pooling were used in this model. The best model was trained with a batch size of 10, a learning rate of 0.01, and 45 epochs.

Motif search

Python packages such as Numpy, Scikit-learn, Matplotlib, and graph tools were used to find motifs.

The following features were collected for clustering: contact area, principal axis of contact area, absolute values of cosine and sine of principal axes, absolute values of scalar and vector products of principal axes of bacteria as vectors, lengths of principal axes, lengths of secondary axes, fold and vector products of secondary axes, distance between centers of bacterial masks and compactness (square of perimeter-to-area ratio). In the end, the best result in the separation of bacterial classes was given by the following set of features, the reduction of dimensionality of which can be seen in (Fig. 5b): the main axis of the contact area, the absolute value of the cosine and sine of the main axes, and the absolute values of the vector and scalar product of the main axes.

t-SNE from Scikit-learn was used for dimensionality reduction, after which classes were extracted through DBSCAN (from Scikit-learn as well) clustering. Manual inspection of the results revealed that the largest class contains 2 motifs: one has primary axes that are almost parallel, and the other is rather close to a right angle. These classes are easily separated by a feature responsible for the absolute sine value between the primary axes. Pairs with absolute sine values greater than 0.935 were assigned to one class, and the rest were assigned to another class. DBSCAN clustering was trained with the parameters eps and min_samples equal to 4.55 and 30, respectively.

After each pair of interacting bacteria was labeled using graph-tool Python package, three-bacterial motifs were found. For this purpose, an isomorphic search was performed on all possible combinations of three interacting bacteria and all dedicated classes using the graph_tool.topology.subgraph_isomorphism function.
