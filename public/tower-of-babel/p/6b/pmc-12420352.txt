A Comparative Analysis of Haemoglobinopathy Diagnostic Techniques—The Ghanaian Picture and the Way Forward: A Narrative Review
Authors: Stephen Twumasi, Angela Opoku, Allwell Adofo Ayirebi, Esther Serwaa, Daniel Nii Martey Antonio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction and AimAlthough there are efficient methods for diagnosing haemoglobinopathies all over the world, their widespread application in Ghana is constrained by cost, infrastructure issues, and restricted access. In this narrative review, haemoglobinopathy diagnostic methods are compared, including assessment of their use in Ghanaian healthcare. In addition, methods to enhance early diagnosis and disease treatment are suggested.MethodsA narrative review of papers published on haemoglobinopathies screening, and haemoglobin variants diagnosis were culled from three search engines, namely, PubMed, Mendeley, and Google Scholar. The search was limited to studies done in humans only and no language restrictions were used.Thematic OverviewIn Ghana, traditional techniques like cellulose acetate haemoglobin electrophoresis are still the most popular, whereas more advanced techniques like molecular diagnostics, isoelectric focusing, and high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) are not available. Novel point‐of‐care diagnostics are not yet commonly used, but they have potential for adoption in rural areas.Discussion and ConclusionIncreased infant and prenatal screening programs, molecular techniques and point‐of‐care testing investments, healthcare worker training, and primary care integration are all necessary to improve haemoglobinopathy diagnosis in Ghana.

Introduction

Haemoglobinopathies are a class of hereditary diseases that impact the synthesis or structure of haemoglobin. They are a major public health issue worldwide, especially in sub‐Saharan Africa. Haemoglobinopathies affect around 3%–7% of the global population [1]. Thalassaemia and sickle cell disease (SCD) are the most common of these conditions; SCD is particularly common in Ghana. With up to 30% of the population carrying a sickle cell trait and an estimated 2% of children in Ghana having SCD [2], there is an urgent need for effective and easily accessible diagnostic techniques. There are about 2000 different types of haemoglobin, and many more are still being discovered [3]. Haemoglobinopathies that cause symptoms include HbS, HbC, beta thalassaemias, symptomatic variants of alpha‐thalassaemias, Hb Lepore, HbD, and HbE, which can cause double heterozygous or homozygous conditions, with a few exceptions (Hb D and Hb E). In rare instances, heterozygotes may also exhibit symptoms, such as HbS Oman, dominant or inclusive beta thalassaemias, etc. [3]. Similarly, the genetic imbalance of alpha and beta globin chains essential for the disease to manifest clinically as thalassaemia syndrome is produced by heterozygotes of beta thalassaemias with coinheritance of many (more than four) copies of the normal alpha gene [4]. For over 50 years, numerous methods were developed in the lab to identify different haemoglobinopathies. While some of these tests are no longer relevant, others are still utilized in labs across the globe [5].

Haemoglobinopathy diagnostic services are not widely available in Ghana [6], and more advanced methods are frequently only available in teaching hospitals and private laboratories. Inadequate health infrastructure, lack of technological know‐how, and high costs continue to be significant obstacles to the broad use of advanced diagnostic instruments [7]. Because of this, a large number of cases go untreated or are misdiagnosed, which delays necessary therapies and raises avoidable morbidity and mortality.

In Ghana, cellulose acetate electrophoresis (CAE) is the most used technique for diagnosing haemoglobinopathies in almost all of the public hospitals [6, 8]. While international labs might brag about cutting‐edge methods like PCR, Ghana is struggling with standard early diagnosis tools. Despite the CAE being available, laboratory personnel are not as well‐trained in proper diagnosis, and they frequently misdiagnose patients. Most laboratories report haemoglobin phenotypes as haemoglobin genotypes when using CAE. When used appropriately, the CAE technique is a protein assay that may identify both normal and aberrant structural globin variations, including Hb F and Hb A2 [9], as well as Hb S, C, and D. However, the technique is generally silent on the thalassaemias. When a person inherits both an abnormal structural globin chain and a globin chain synthesis defect, in the case of HbS/α‐thalassaemia and HbC/β‐thalassaemia, for example, haemoglobin electrophoresis would estimate the S and C, respectively, but not the α‐ and β‐thalassaemias directly. To appropriately indicate that the experiment evaluated a protein, such results may therefore be presented as “Hb phenotype” at best. To appropriately deal with gene‐level assays, such data should only be presented as genotypes by assays that use PCR or other gene sequencing platforms that examine the genes expressed on chromosomes [8]. Furthermore, CAE results showing only one band in the S region may not always signify that the person is “Hb SS genotype.” Also, thalassaemia inheritance is not typically diagnosed by the Hb electrophoresis assay. The benefit of using both citrate agar and cellulose acetate electrophoresis on the same sample is that it provides more coverage [8]. This is not the case in practical practice in our hospital settings, though; even when both approaches are used, proteins are still involved.

The care, counselling, and treatment of people with haemoglobinopathies depend on an early and precise diagnosis. From more conventional approaches like haemoglobin electrophoresis to more sophisticated technologies like high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), isoelectric focusing (IEF), molecular diagnostics, and next‐generation sequencing [10], a variety of diagnostic techniques have been developed over time. However, there are major obstacles to the availability and application of these techniques in settings with limited resources, such as Ghana [11, 12].

This narrative review highlights the diagnostic methods used for haemoglobinopathies, with a particular focus on their application within the Ghanaian healthcare setting. Specifically, it will assess each method's advantages and disadvantages, evaluate the difficulties in putting them into practice, and suggest ways to improve diagnostic capabilities nationwide. This study aims to inform clinical practice, research, and policy to improve outcomes for people with hemoglobinopathies in Ghana by outlining the existing situation and offering workable solutions.

Methodology

Search Strategies for Identification of Studies

A narrative review of papers published on haemoglobinopathies screening, haemoglobin variants, the use of CAE, polymerase chain reaction and other techniques in the diagnosis of Hb variants were culled from three search engines, namely, PubMed, Mendeley, and Google Scholar to denote advantages and limitations of these methods and showcase the need for correct identification and confirmation of haemoglobin variants in Ghana.

Inclusion Criteria

Studies were included if they were based on haemoglobinopathies, haemoglobin variants, abnormal Hb detection, comparison of protein and genomic mode of screening the Hb variants, diagnostic challenges of haemoglobinopathies in Ghana and challenges associated with the protein methods of haemoglobin screening. The search was limited to studies done in humans only and no language restrictions were used. All articles that fell outside the inclusion criteria were excluded.

Search Strategy and Keywords

The primary databases searched included PubMed, Mendeley, and Google Scholar. These databases were selected to capture peer‐reviewed research articles published in international and regional journals. The search strategy was systematically developed using a combination of relevant keywords, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms where applicable (particularly for PubMed), and relevant phrases to capture the core concepts of the review: “haemoglobinopathies” or “hemoglobinopathies,” “diagnostic techniques,” “diagnostic challenges,” and terms related to the “molecular diagnostics”, “electrophoresis”, “point of care testing” and its constituent components. Keywords and phrases used in various combinations with Boolean operators (AND, OR) included: “haemoglobinopathies”, “sickle cell disease”, “SCD”, “thalassaemia”, “haemoglobin variants”, “diagnostic techniques”, “diagnostic challenges”, “PCR”, “Ghana”, “molecular techniques”, “molecular diagnostics”, “isoelectric focusing”, “mass spectrometry”, “cellulose acetate electrophoresis”, “haemoglobin stability tests”, “capillary electrophoresis”, “Kleihauer–Betke test”, “flow cytometry”, “point of care testing”, “high performance‐liquid chromatography”, “HPLC”, “sickle solubilty test”, “capillary electrophoresis”.

Search strings were meticulously constructed to capture studies and reports that addressed haemoglobinopathy diagnostic challenge in Ghana. For example, search strings included combinations such as [“diagnostic methods” AND “haemoglobinopathies”], [“Electrophoresis technique” AND “haemoglobinopathies”], [“PCR AND haemoglobin variants”], [“cellulose acetate haemoglobin electrophoresis” AND “haemoglobinopathies”], [“point of care testing” AND “haemoglobinopathies”], [“molecular diagnostics” AND “haemoglobinopathies”], [“Point‐of‐care testing” AND “sickle cell” OR “thalassaemia”] and [haemoglobinopathies AND diagnostics challenges AND Ghana]. Articles were selected based on the inclusion criteria, and all articles used have been cited in the manuscript.

Discussion

According to Trent (2016), a three‐tiered work‐up that includes DNA testing, particular haematological assays, and a full blood count is necessary for the identification and description of a haemoglobinopathy [13]. The full blood count is the first technique that gives the laboratory scientist the suspicion of a possible haemoglobinopathy. For example, a low MCV (mean cell volume) and MCH (mean corpuscular haemoglobin) could be a possible clue for thalassaemia, if one is investigating haemoglobinopathies, especially when the Mentzer index is less than 13 (< 13). Since such parameters (i.e., low MCV and MCH) are seen in other diseases such as iron deficiency anaemia, relying on only these indices might result in missing out on the detection of abnormal haemoglobin variants such as HbS. Specific diagnostic tests are the next step of diagnosis in establishing the haemoglobinopathy. These tests include sickle solubility and instability tests, rapid point‐of‐care testing, isoelectric focusing, high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), molecular techniques (polymerase chain reaction), and other methods (Table 1).

Sickle Solubility Test

The sickle solubility test is a quick and affordable way to detect haemoglobin S (HbS), the mutant type of haemoglobin that causes sickle cell disease and trait [14]. Based on the fact that deoxygenated HbS is less soluble in a high‐molarity phosphate buffer that contains a reducing agent, like sodium dithionite, the test is conducted. Upon deoxygenation, HbS polymerizes and forms insoluble crystals, giving the solution a turbid or opaque appearance. However, samples that only contain normal haemoglobin (HbA) do not change colour [10]. The test may identify both homozygous (sickle cell anaemia) and heterozygous (sickle trait) situations with good sensitivity. Nevertheless, it is unable to differentiate between these two conditions. Patients with severe anaemia (low HbS concentration), newborns (high HbF levels), and recently transfused patients might also result in erroneous negative results. Positive results must therefore be verified by electrophoresis, HPLC, or molecular testing, even though it is an excellent screening method [15].

Electrophoresis

In conventional haematological labs, electrophoresis has been the preferred technique for both qualitative and quantitative investigations of the different Hb fractions [16]. Citrate agar or agarose gel electrophoresis at acid pH (6.0–6.2) and cellulose acetate electrophoresis at alkaline pH (8.2–8.6) can be used to separate the major haemoglobins (i.e., Hb A, Hb F, Hb S/D, and Hb C/E/O‐Arab) and other less prevalent Hb variations. Although it is quicker, repeatable, and able to separate common haemoglobin types like S, F, A, and C, haemoglobin A2, O, and E, as well as many others, are not detected [16]. Electrophoresis, a test that depends on the migration of electrically charged molecules under an applied electric field, has performed one of the most important roles in the history of anomalous Hb detection. CAE is still one of the most often used techniques for Hb screening due to its ease of use, but it's prone to errors [17]. These errors are caused by a lack of experience, poor standards, malfunctioning equipment, phony results, and erratic laboratory monitoring [18]. However, in addition to being time‐consuming, electrophoretic methods have the drawback of having low Hb quantitation precision, sensitivity, and accuracy, which causes numerous aberrant haemoglobins to overlap. Additionally, haemoglobin E from O, haemoglobin D from G, and many other types cannot yet be separated using electrophoretic techniques. Another disadvantage that is seen with the cellulose acetate method is that quantitative measurements of abnormal haemoglobins are usually inaccurate at low concentrations, especially in HbA2 and HbF in adults. This, therefore, would substantiate the need for a more sensitive technique to be used in West African countries, specifically Ghana. Additionally, urea‐triton gel electrophoresis enables the quick study of extremely small amounts of haemoglobin from hemolysate as well as the investigation of globin synthesis ratios and globin chain composition [19]. While point mutations are typically the source of β thalassaemia and Hb variants, gene deletions are typically the primary cause of alpha‐thalassaemias.

Moving boundary electrophoresis was used by Pauling et al. in 1949 to find Hb S [β6 Glu > Val], the first aberrant Hb identified [20]. Many clinical laboratories continue to use zone electrophoresis on cellulose acetate strips. Despite having a poorer resolution than isoelectric focusing (IEF), this approach is nonetheless widely utilized in Hb screening due to its ease of use. This technique separates the Hb molecules at an alkaline pH. In these conditions, every Hb has a negative charge and travels towards the cathode. Due to its additional positive charge, Hb S migrates more slowly than Hb A. While Hb Tak can be recognised by a comprehensive analysis of the oxygen dissociation curve, Hb d‐Punjab can be separated from Hb S by electrophoresis at acid pH in which it co‐migrates with Hb A. PCR recognized Hb d‐Punjab [β121 Glu > Gln], whereas CAE could not detect from Hb S. It is essential to characterize a few frequent variations (O‐Arab and Hb d‐Punjab) that, when linked to Hb S, can cause sickle cell anaemia. Both have an impact on position β121 (GH4), where a Gln in the first instance and a Lys in the second replace the Glu. This residue is found in a location outside the Hb tetramer that aids in the sickling process by helping to construct the Hb S fibre. Due to the facilitation of polymerization by these two mutations, compound heterozygotes may exhibit clinical severity comparable to that of homozygous Hb S. It is difficult to distinguish Hb d‐Punjab from Hb S using standard CAE. This also applies to Hb O‐Arab, which might be mistaken for Hb E or Hb C. It is necessary to identify Hb d‐Punjab because it is the only variety with Hb D mobility that, when linked to HbS, causes a serious illness. Other variations, including Hb d‐Iran and Hb Korle‐Bu, are innocuous. Also, some silent clinically significant variants (e.g., Hb Adana, Hb Bronovo, Hb Taybe) may be missed by routine electrophoresis (CAE) [21], hence there is a need to consider other methods such as capillary electrophoresis, HPLC, and molecular techniques in Ghana.

Capillary electrophoresis uses an electric field to separate charged molecules (such as haemoglobins) according to their charge‐to‐mass ratio. The capillary is filled with an electrolyte buffer [22, 23]. Variants of haemoglobin can be identified and measured because they move at varying rates. Some in‐house capillary isoelectric focusing (CIEF) and capillary zone electrophoresis (CZE) assays have employed capillary electrophoresis (CE) to quantify HbA2 and HbF and detect haemoglobin (Hb) variations [24, 25]. Additionally, a commercial kit for thalassaemia screening and haemoglobin variant analysis has been developed. While many variants have been demonstrated to be detectable by CE methods, specialized testing, such as DNA technology, is required for the definitive identification of the variants [13, 26, 27]. Despite being accessible, CE HbA1c analysis cannot match automated HPLC commercial devices that provide accurate HbA1c results in 3 or 4 min in terms of turnaround time or cost. As a result, we do not see a promising future for haemoglobin analysis by CE. CE has the advantage of better separating haemoglobin variants than more conventional techniques like electrophoresis on cellulose acetate or agar for hemoglobinopathies [28, 29]. Additionally, it gives precise HbA, HbA2, HbF, and variation percentages, and it is ideal for high‐throughput laboratory settings. However, it has several limitations, such as the possibility of co‐migration of rare variations that need to be confirmed by molecular testing or HPLC. Furthermore, it is unable to accurately identify silent carrier states, such as α‐thalassemia minor. Further, the peaks of some instruments may overlap (e.g., HbD and HbG may overlap with HbS) [28, 29].

Haemoglobin Stability Tests: Heat and Isopropanol Precipitation

Haemoglobin stability tests are useful for identifying unstable haemoglobin variants that exhibit normal electrophoretic patterns but may manifest as jaundice, reticulocytosis, and chronic haemolytic anaemia [30]. These unstable variants are caused by point mutations that change the haemoglobin molecule's tertiary or quaternary structure, rendering it more susceptible to precipitation and denaturation. The isopropanol precipitation test and the heat stability test are the two traditional haemoglobin stability assays [31]. The heat stability test involves incubating hemolysates for 30–60 min at 50°C. In contrast to unstable haemoglobins, which denature and precipitate upon exposure to heat, normal haemoglobins stay in solution. Turbidity or a discernible flocculent precipitate during incubation is a sign of an unstable haemoglobin. Mostly used as a screening tool, this approach is qualitative [32, 33]. The isopropanol stability test exploits the use of unstable haemoglobins' reduced solubility in an organic solvent solution. Denatured haemoglobins form when a solution of hemolysate and buffered isopropanol is incubated at 37°C. The appearance of a precipitate indicates instability, much like in the heat test. Beta‐globin chain variants are especially sensitive to isopropanol testing [10]. Both tests lack specificity and should be followed by molecular or structural characterisation if anomalous results are found, although they are rather easy and affordable.

Kleihauer–Betke Test

The Kleihauer–Betke (K–B) test is a cytochemical staining technique that exploits the acid resistance of fetal haemoglobin (HbF) [34, 35]. When mature erythrocytes are subjected to an acidic solution, adult haemoglobin (HbA) is eluted from their cytoplasm, whereas HbF stays stable [36]. Foetal or HbF‐containing cells, which retain haemoglobin, tint pink‐red when stained with eosin, whereas HbA‐containing cells appear pale or ghostly. After that, the number of HbF‐containing red blood cells, or F cells, is counted under a microscope while submerged in oil; this is usually done per 1000 red blood cells. A percentage of all cells is used to represent the outcome [36]. Although the K–B test is most commonly used to measure foetal–maternal haemorrhage in Rh‐negative pregnancies, it can also be used to diagnose hemoglobinopathies with high HbF levels. HbF levels are frequently elevated in SCD patients receiving hydroxyurea treatment; response can be semi‐quantified using the K–B test. Although the distribution of β‐Thalassemia Major/Intermedia is often heterocellular, there may be varying elevations in HbF [37, 38]. The K–B test has merit, although it is manual, semi‐quantitative, and susceptible to interobserver variability. Its precision is less than that of flow cytometry [38], particularly when it comes to assessing low amounts of HbF. Some specific disadvantages include: it is labour‐intensive, subjective, requires skilled workers, has poor consistency in borderline or low‐F‐cell samples, and has low sensitivity for identifying minor variations in HbF% [39]. In contemporary haematology labs, flow cytometry is frequently used in place of or in addition to the K–B test, particularly for differentiating between pancellular and heterocellular HbF expression [40, 41].

Flow Cytometry for Foetal Haemoglobin (HbF) Distribution

Flow cytometry has become a quantitative and extremely sensitive technique for determining the distribution of red blood cells that contain HbF, or F cells [42]. This method is very helpful in differentiating between conditions with elevated levels of foetal haemoglobin (HbF), including sickle cell disease, δβ‐thalassemia, and hereditary persistence of foetal haemoglobin (HPFH). Intracellular HbF is labelled with fluorescently tagged monoclonal antibodies against the gamma‐globin chain as part of the experiment [43]. To ascertain the proportion of F cells and fluorescence intensity, which corresponds with HbF concentration, each cell is thereafter examined separately using flow cytometry [44]. In particular, flow cytometry is useful for distinguishing sickle cell anaemia (HbSS) from HbS‐HPFH, which is a disorder with uniformly (20%–40%) high HbF and less severe clinical signs. HbF is found in almost all red blood cells in HbS‐HPFH (pancellular distribution), whereas it is only found in a subset of red blood cells in the majority of other situations (heterocellular distribution) [45, 46].

Mass Spectrometry (MS) in Haemoglobin Variant Identification

Mass spectrometry (MS) is a state‐of‐the‐art analytical technique that is being utilized more and more to definitively identify and characterize haemoglobin variations, particularly when conventional chromatographic or electrophoretic approaches produce unclear results [34, 35]. Haemoglobin molecules are identified by mass spectrometry (MS) using their mass‐to‐charge ratio (m/z). Methods like electrospray ionisation (ESI) in conjunction with tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) and matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) enable comprehensive peptide mapping and sequence analysis. MS is capable of identifying both known and unknown mutations at the amino acid level [47, 48]. With little preparation, recent developments like MALDI‐In‐Source Decay (ISD) allow for the quick, high‐resolution detection of aberrant haemoglobins in whole blood. MS is especially useful for: distinguishing co‐migrating variations (such as HbD from HbG), determining compound heterozygous situations, describing unstable haemoglobins, and examining haemoglobin's posttranslational changes. Despite its unmatched specificity, mass spectrometry is nonetheless costly and technically complex. It is frequently only used in research settings or reference labs. However, its use in diagnostic algorithms is expanding, especially for haemoglobinopathies that are complex or atypical [32, 49].

Point‐of‐Care Testing

Point‐of‐care testing (POCT) kits have transformed medical care for a variety of conditions, from infection to genetic abnormalities like sickle cell disease (SCD), by providing fast results required for prompt therapy [50]. Sickle SCAN and HemoTypeSC which employs competitive lateral flow, are two promising point‐of‐care (POC) diagnostics that could make newborn SCD screening less complicated. In a study by Adegoke et al., HemotypeSC was compared with HPLC and ACE and found that HemoTypeSC is highly sensitive and specific. Advantages of HemotypeSC over CAE and HPLC include: user‐friendly, affordability, rapid, equipment‐free and can be used in both rural and urban areas. Despite its ready detection of Hb A, S, and C, other compound heterozygous haemoglobinopathies such as HbS‐thalassaemia, SO‐Arab, SD‐Punjab among others cannot be detected by HemoTypeSC [17]. In a comparative study [51], Sickle Scan and HemoTypeSC were both accurate and feasible using isoelectric focusing as the gold standard, though Sickle Scan was a preferred method due to its shorter time for interpretation and fewer test repetitions.

High Performance‐Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) and Isoelectric Focusing

Other methods, such as isoelectric focusing and HPLC, have their advantages and limitations. With great sensitivity and specificity, high‐performance liquid chromatographic techniques have been established for the screening and confirmation of haemoglobinopathies in neonates [52, 53]. The screening processes are sensitive enough to identify a small number of significant variations and offer the quick turnaround time (about 3 min per sample) needed for a large number of samples. It does not, however, have the precision required to distinguish between some frequently seen haemoglobin variants, such as E and Lepore from A2 and D from G [10, 54, 55]. Additionally, it measures HbA, HbA2, HbF, and variants (like HbS, HbC, and HbE) accurately, detects a wide range of common and uncommon haemoglobin variations, and can identify HbF and its variations, like HbS, at an early age. However, it lacks the resolution necessary to differentiate some commonly encountered haemoglobin variants, such as E and Lepore from A2 and D from G. In addition, it lacks the sensitivity required to identify haemoglobins with low concentrations [10, 54, 55].

Another crucial technique for diagnosing hemoglobinopathies is isoelectric focusing (IEF), particularly in newborn screening and screening initiatives in nations with abundant resources [3, 34, 52]. Isoelectric focusing (IEF) on agarose gels can be used to separate different Hb fractions and variants [56, 57] and globin chains. It offers high‐resolution haemoglobin variant isolation according to their isoelectric points (pI). Haemoglobin variations in IEF migrate in an agarose or polyacrylamide gel with an immobilized pH gradient. Every haemoglobin variation forms distinct, crisp bands by concentrating at its unique pI. When used in conjunction with capillary electrophoresis (capillary IEF), IEF enables the separation of Hb variants with isoelectric points that change by as small as 0.02 pH units and has shown enhanced resolution, detection sensitivity, and precise quantification [58]. IEF is better than electrophoresis and, in some cases, HPLC/CE for particular variations. Even at low quantities, HbF, HbA, HbS, HbC, and other variations are easily distinguished. This technique does not require the use of sophisticated equipment in comparison to HPLC and CE [59, 60]. It also has the potential to reveal variants that other methods might overlook, particularly when used in conjunction with family research. It is less accurate than HPLC or CE for measuring haemoglobin percentages (such as HbA2 or HbF), but it does a good job of indicating the existence of variants. It requires experience in determining how to evaluate tiny variations in band intensity and position. It is also more time‐consuming than HPLC/CE systems that are automated [59, 60].

Overview of Molecular Techniques

The most accurate way to screen for haemoglobinopathies is still through molecular techniques [61]. The first practical seeds were planted in the early days of recombinant DNA technology, even though the idea of using molecular diagnostics for haemoglobinopathies was established in 1949 when Linus Pauling and his colleagues introduced the term “molecular disease” in the medical vocabulary for sickle cell anaemia (SCA). The primary globin gene sequence was discovered using cDNA cloning and sequencing [62, 63, 64]. As a result, several DNA sequences were made available for use as probes, enabling the southern blot investigation of genomic areas and the development and application of restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis to track a mutant allele for diagnosis both during pregnancy and after delivery [65]. Using hybridization on DNA taken from foetal fibroblasts, Kan and colleagues in 1976 made the first prenatal diagnosis of a‐thalassemia [66]. Later, Kan and Dozy (1978) employed RFLP analysis to find SCA alleles of African heritage [67]. However, there was a major methodological bottleneck that needed to be addressed at the moment. Only by building a genomic DNA library from the afflicted person—first cloning the mutant allele, then figuring out its nucleotide sequence—could the disease‐causing mutation be detected. To expedite DNA sequencing, several exploratory techniques for detecting mutations in patients' DNA were developed. The initial techniques involved identifying mismatches in heteroduplexes of DNA or RNA [68].

Polymerase Chain Reaction

The polymerase chain reaction, the gold standard for detecting haemoglobin variations, is used to perform DNA testing. This genome amplification technique has transformed the turnaround time for DNA testing and broadened the range of tests available, especially for detecting single point mutations, which are generally the most prevalent mutations in haemoglobinopathy [13]. In 1985, the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was first explained. PCR significantly increased the variety of alpha thalassaemia faults that could be identified and made it easier to find single point mutations [69]. As a result, more and more couples were able to ask for a prenatal diagnosis based on DNA during their first pregnancy. Alpha and beta thalassaemia deletions could also be found using PCR [70, 71]. Couples who presented late might still have prenatal testing done because PCR results could be obtained faster than with Southern blot. The main limitation in this case was the legal deadline for pregnancy termination. Even though a specific PCR, like the one for alpha thalassaemia, is technically challenging, scientists who are less skilled in southern blot are forced to rely on PCR‐based techniques due to the overwhelming demand for PCR in labs that offer DNA diagnostic services for a variety of disorders, such as thalassaemia, Huntington's disease, and cystic fibrosis. When utilising PCR for prenatal diagnosis, certain measures are advised to lower the possibility of inaccuracy. These include restricting the number of amplification cycles, examining the chorionic villus sample and removing decidual tissue, performing repeat analyses using various molecular techniques, and employing microsatellites to keep an eye out for maternal contamination [69].

The Necessity of Implementing Polymerase Chain Reaction

In general, the Hb Electrophoresis method has no effect on thalassaemia. For instance, if the same individual inherits both an abnormal structural globin chain and a defective globin chain synthesis defect, haemoglobin electrophoresis would estimate the S and C for HbS/α‐thalassaemia and HbC/β‐thalassaemia, respectively, but not the α‐ and β‐thalassaemias directly [8]. Data are presented as genotypes by assays that use polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or other gene sequencing platforms that examine the genes expressed on chromosomes, since these assays accurately deal with the gene level. The different gene test platforms provide a great deal of power in terms of comprehending the pathophysiology of disease processes [8]. Another alternate method of diagnosis is the direct identification of the mutations producing these Hb variations using DNA analysis. Hb Tak and Hb d‐Punjab can be identified by allele‐specific PCR and PCRRFLP using EcoRI digestion, respectively, whereas the Hb S mutation can be found by either allele‐specific PCR or Polymerase Chain Reaction‐Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism (PCR‐RFLP) analysis with Mst II restriction endonuclease. The three Hb variants can be distinguished in a single PCR reaction with distinct allele discrimination by using multiplex PCR. The most used test is gap‐PCR, which may effectively identify minute mutations and support the detection of haemoglobin variations. Since the only step required after PCR is a traditional agarose gel electrophoresis for interpretation, this DNA analysis approach doesn't require any extra tools or sophistication. In a single gel, each Hb variation is easily diagnosed. For the molecular diagnosis of β‐thalassemia patients, conventional polymerase chain reaction (PCR)‐based techniques have been used, including restriction fragment length polymorphisms (RFLP), temporal temperature gradient gel electrophoresis (TGGE), denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis (DGGE), reverse dot blot hybridisation, amplification refractory mutation system (ARMS), multiplex Ligation‐dependent Probe Amplification (MLPA) and direct sequencing [71, 72, 73]. MLPA is generally more sensitive and reliable than RFLP, especially for detecting copy number variations (CNVs) like deletions and duplications [74]. Gel electrophoresis and ethidium bromide staining are necessary post‐PCR processing processes. Recent developments in molecular diagnosis have made it possible to undertake genotyping and PCR amplification in the same reaction tube [75].

DNA is taken from peripheral blood leucocytes in the great majority of tests for thalassaemia molecular diagnosis. Foetal DNA is often extracted from chorionic villus samples taken during an ultrasound scan at 9–12 weeks of gestation by transcervical or transabdominal aspiration for prenatal diagnosis. Additionally, it is possible to harvest foetal DNA from amniotic fluid cells. Retrieving foetal cells from the mother's blood is now the most appealing strategy [76]. As an alternative, foetal DNA can be extracted from the mother's serum and plasma [77, 78]. Despite their great appeal, these sample techniques are still in the early stages of research and are therefore not commonly available for the screening of globin gene mutations. Polar bodies or blastomeres, at either the cleavage or blastocyst stage, are separated from preimplantation embryos if preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) is necessary. DNA is then extracted from these cells to carry out the genetic diagnosis.

From all analysis, PCR seems to be the best technique for the confirmation of Hb variants. Techniques such as CAE and HPLC serve as diagnostic and identification tests for Hb variants diagnosis of hemoglobinopathies, especially in Ghana. There is the need for the improvement of traditional tests (CAE and HPLC), whilst PCR is introduced as a confirmatory test, which will give accurate and reliable diagnosis of haemoglobinopathies.

The Ghanaian Context of Haemoglobinopathies Diagnostics

In Ghana, haemoglobinopathies (especially sickle cell disease (SCD)) represent a significant public health concern. The burden of SCD is significant, with an estimated 15,000 babies born with the disease each year and almost one‐third of the population having at least one aberrant haemoglobin gene [12, 79, 80]. Despite this, there are still significant differences in access to efficient diagnostic tools between areas and healthcare levels, making the diagnostic landscape fragmented [81]. In Ghana, teaching hospitals like the Korle‐Bu Teaching Hospital in Accra and the Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital in Kumasi are the main locations for advanced haemoglobinopathy diagnostic services. According to Asare et al. [82], these facilities frequently have the ability to perform haemoglobin electrophoresis and, occasionally, HPLC. To perform these tests successfully, however, the majority of district and regional hospitals lack the required tools, skilled staff, or reagents [83]. Clinical signs like recurrent pain episodes or anaemia are frequently used as the only basis for diagnosis in rural and underserved settings, which might result in incorrect diagnoses or postponed therapies. Consequently, a large number of children with sickle cell disease are not recognised until significant difficulties have occurred [84].

Ghana started piloting newborn screening programs for sickle cell disease (SCD) in 1995 with assistance from international collaborators [12]. These initiatives have shown that early detection is both feasible and cost‐effective, despite their narrow focus [82]. Funding and logistical issues continue to impede the Ghana Health Service's and the Ministry of Health's commitment to expanding screening programs [7]. With a restricted implementation, the Ghanaian government started a National Newborn Screening Program for SCD in 2018. Despite its promise, this initiative has not yet become a universal service and only covers a small percentage of births each year [83].

The cost of testing, a lack of skilled workers, inconsistent supply chains, and restricted access to cutting‐edge equipment are the main obstacles to accurate diagnosis in Ghana. These issues are widely known to lead to informal diagnostics, reliance on out‐of‐pocket expenses, and underutilization of current screening procedures [7, 85].

Ghana has demonstrated innovation in filling in diagnostic gaps despite these obstacles. Low‐cost, regionally tailored screening techniques are being piloted by universities and research organizations such as the Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research. Rapid diagnostic kits and smartphone‐based microscopy are gaining popularity because they may enable health professionals to conduct screening in isolated areas with little infrastructure [86, 87]. International research alliances and partnerships with nongovernmental organizations, like the Sickle Cell Foundation of Ghana, have also made it easier to transfer knowledge, provide training, and introduce newer technology, such as point‐of‐care diagnostic devices [88, 89].

The need for diagnostic services is impacted by the lack of public awareness regarding haemoglobinopathies. Premarital or prenatal screening is not always done, and many Ghanaians are not aware of their haemoglobin status [85]. Early detection and long‐term disease management should be improved by attempts to incorporate haemoglobinopathy diagnostics into primary healthcare, particularly through prenatal clinics and school health initiatives [12].

The Way Forward: Recommendations for Improving Hemoglobinopathy Diagnostics in Ghana

A multifaceted strategy is needed to address the prevalence of haemoglobinopathies in Ghana, one that incorporates screening into the larger healthcare system, increases awareness, and fortifies diagnostic capabilities. Implementing statewide newborn screening for sickle cell disease on a large scale using proven techniques like isoelectric focusing or HPLC should be the government's top priority. Early detection of at‐risk couples through screening pregnant women during prenatal visits can facilitate informed reproductive decisions and early infant diagnosis. Moreover, HemoTypeSC and Sickle SCAN are examples of rapid diagnostic tests that ought to be considered for nationwide use and implemented in community clinics and district hospitals. These assessments are appropriate for low‐resource environments, provide fast results, and require little training. To increase cost and uptake, especially for poor people, the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) ought to think about paying for diagnostic testing for haemoglobinopathies. In addition, expanding the chances for laboratory scientists, nurses, and physicians to receive training in haemoglobinopathy diagnosis and counseling. To lessen dependency on teaching hospitals and guarantee that diagnostic knowledge is dispersed throughout the nation, establishing regional training facilities is needed. The provision of vital diagnostic tools, such as electrophoresis systems, HPLC and simple PCR machines, to laboratories in district and regional hospitals is crucial. To increase knowledge of sickle cell disease, the value of knowing one's genotype, and the availability of screening, public health campaigns should be launched. Lastly, introducing education on haemoglobinopathy in school curricula and encouraging premarital counselling that includes genotype screening.

Conclusion

The comparison of haemoglobinopathy diagnostic methods highlights Ghana's health system's strengths and weaknesses. Although there are useful tools available, their limited pricing and accessibility prevent their widespread use. The early detection and treatment of haemoglobinopathies in Ghana can be greatly enhanced by a calculated combination of increased screening programs, better diagnostic facilities, and enhanced public health messages. Although the conventional methods of diagnosing haemoglobinopathies have limitations, we recommend that these methods be improved by educating laboratory personnel, implementing suitable standard protocols, making sure that equipment is operating properly, and conducting routine device monitoring. This will enhance the results produced by the conventional methods. Additionally, we recommend using PCR technology to determine the confirmation of haemoglobinopathies.
