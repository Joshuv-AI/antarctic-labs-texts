Introduction

Acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI) is caused by sudden cut-off of blood flow to the intestines, causing cellular damage, intestinal necrosis, and death if untreated [1]. It may be occlusive or nonocclusive, the most common etiology being mesenteric arterial embolism (50 %), mesenteric arterial thrombosis (15–25 %), or mesenteric venous thrombosis (5–15 %) [2]. It is a relatively rare cause of abdominal pain, seen in only 0.09–0.2 % of emergency department visits, but a common cause of emergency laparotomy [3]. Thromboembolic events leading to AMI may be linked to inherited disorders, such as protein C, protein S, and antithrombin III (AT III) deficiency [4].

Protein S deficiency is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by hypercoagulable states with a sevenfold risk of venous thrombosis over that of the general population [5]. Less commonly, it can result in arterial thrombosis with most previous studies reporting instances of stroke and myocardial infarction [6, 7]. Protein S deficiency resulting in mesenteric vein thrombosis has been reported in previous studies however those causing superior mesenteric artery (SMA) thrombosis have been rarely reported [4,7].

CT angiography (CTA) of the abdomen and pelvis provides confirmation of the diagnosis and visualization of the thrombus guiding vascular surgical management [8]. To maximize patient outcomes, an interdisciplinary team is crucial to minimize the time-to-reperfusion interval. This paper, following the SCARE 2023 guidelines, describes the successful management of the case of a hypertensive woman in whom a small bowel infarct developed because of acute thrombotic occlusion of SMA due to protein S deficiency [9].

Case presentation

A 39-year-old non-smoker hypertensive female presented to the emergency department with complaints of epigastric pain and non-bilious vomiting for four days and inability to pass flatus and stool for one day. The epigastric pain was acute in onset, constant dull-aching, and severe with radiation to the back. Fever, chest pain, difficulty breathing, trauma, bleeding disorders, and use of hormonal contraceptives were absent. She had undergone lower segment cesarean section (LSCS) 4 months back, documentation of which is not available. On examination, her vitals were as follows: blood pressure 110/70 mmHg, respiratory rate 20 breaths/min, pulse rate 110 beats/min, spO2 95 %, and temperature of 98.6 °F. Generalized tenderness and rigidity were present over the abdomen. There was no organomegaly, palpable lump.Bowel sounds were heard. The rest of the systemic examinations were normal. Laboratory investigations revealed a decreased serum sodium level of 131 mEq/l (Normal level:135–145 mEq/l) and an increased fasting sugar level of 6.3 mmol/l (Normal level:3.5–6.1 mmol/l). Amylase, potassium, liver function tests, and renal function tests were within normal limits. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) revealed a partially occlusive thrombus in the SMA (Fig. 1). She was kept nil per os, started on analgesic and heparin and resuscitated with intravenous fluids. An exploratory laparotomy was performed and 100 ml of dirty collections were drained from the peritoneal cavity. Intraoperatively necrosed ileal segment measuring approximately 110 cm (cm), 190 cm distal to the duodenojejunal junction, and 140 cm proximal to the ileocecal junction with two perforations measuring 1 × 1 square cm and 0.5 × 0.5 square cm was noticed in the necrosed ileal segment. The necrotic ileal segment was removed and a double barrel ileostomy was placed on the right lower quadrant of the abdominal wall (Fig. 2). The intraoperative course was uneventful. On the 3rd postoperative day, her heart rate was elevated to 140 bpm with normal sinus rhythm and normal ECHO findings. The patient re-presented to the emergency department five days after discharge with generalized progressive abdominal pain for one day and two episodes of non-projectile, non-bilious vomiting; not stained with blood. There was no fever, chest pain, cough or difficulty in breathing. Her vitals were stable, and the midline wound appeared healthy. Investigations revealed a decreased hemoglobin of 10 g/dl (Normal level:12–16 g/dl) and increased fasting sugar level of 10.4 mmol/l, and an increased APTT level of 76 s (Normal level:30–40 s). She was admitted to the general surgery ward and started on unfractionated heparin. The Intervention Radiologists (IR) team was consulted for stenting. SMA was cannulated through the femoral puncture and the thrombus was lysed with streptokinase. An angiogram was repeated which showed normal opacification with a few areas of irregularities of the vessel and the sheath was removed. Post-stenting angiography was done 7 days later which showed normal flow and re-opening of SMA. On a detailed workup for thrombophilic state by the hematology team, protein S deficiency (free protein S = 15 %; via immunoassay) was found. AT III, Antiphospholipid antibodies, factor VIII, IX, and XI, protein C, peripheral blood smear, and anti-nuclear antibodies-Immunofluorescence were normal. Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH) was started during the course of the hospital stay and she was discharged after 13 days of hospital stay on rivaroxaban, aspirin, clopidogrel, and amlodipine and was advised to follow up in the general surgery and IR outpatient department (OPD). At the follow-up appointment after two weeks of discharge, rivaroxaban was discontinued. Bridging therapy with LMWH was initiated and continued for one week. LMWH was then discontinued 24 h before testing. The repeat Protein S level revealed a level of 20 %, confirming the diagnosis of Protein S deficiency. Following this confirmation, the patient was restarted on rivaroxaban. Stoma reversal and jejunal side-to-side anastomosis were performed at 3 months. She is currently on rivaroxaban, aspirin and metoprolol and does not have abdominal complaints on follow-up at 24 months.

Discussion

The prevalence of congenital protein S deficiency differs among geographic areas and ethnic groups ranging from 15 % in Caucasian population to 59 % in Chinese population [10]. The most frequent presentation of protein S deficiency are recurrent venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism [7]. It is more common during the third and fourth decades of life as in our case. Most thrombotic events in patients with protein S or C deficiency occur without a clear cause and remaining have triggering events such as sepsis, pregnancy, immobility, or vascular conditions such as atherosclerosis [11]. The predisposing factor in our patient could be a history of hypertension for 10 years leading to atherosclerosis of SMA. Patients with AMI present with severe pain out of proportion to physical exam findings, with a soft, non-tender abdomen and eventually develop distention and severe tenderness with rebound guarding due to bowel infarction [12].

Currently, there are no laboratory parameters to accurately identify the presence of ischemic or necrotic bowel, although elevated l-lactate, leukocytosis, and D-dimer can offer clues. CTA should be done immediately in patients with suspicion of AMI. Delay in diagnosis accounts for high mortality rates of 30–70 % despite familiarity with this condition [13]. The surgical treatment of the condition is well established and consists of revascularization and/or resection of nonviable bowel [14]. In recent years, advancements in endovascular techniques have emerged as an important alternative for patients with occlusion of the SMA. Studies have reported every 6-h delay doubles the mortality [1].

Recent studies suggest that treatment of occlusive AMI in “intestine stroke centers” with a multidisciplinary approach with emphasis on removal of non-viable bowel, revascularization, and intensive care leads to better patient outcomes [15]. The multidisciplinary team should comprise a general surgeon, a vascular surgeon, an interventional radiologist, and an intensivist. Additionally, for cases with thrombophilic conditions, the hematology team should be included from the beginning. If the hematology team had been involved from the start, the recurrence might have been prevented. Mesenteric infarction caused by inherited hypercoagulable conditions tend to have a more favorable prognosis compared to cases arising from other causes [16]. In the same way, our patient has been on anticoagulants and has no symptoms of recurrence at 24 months. Patients with protein C and/or S deficiency treated for AMI will require lifelong anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy to prevent relapse [17].

Conclusion

Hereditary thrombophilia should be suspected in young people with unusual thrombotic presentations. Mesenteric infarction caused by inherited hypercoagulable conditions tends to have a more favorable prognosis compared to cases arising from other causes. Earlier diagnosis and aggressive antithrombotic therapy in individuals with hypercoagulable states can improve outcomes. Treatment involving a multidisciplinary approach improves outcomes.
