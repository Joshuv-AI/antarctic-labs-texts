Double-Network Bilayer Hydrogel Loaded with Puerarin and Curcumin for Osteochondral Repair
Authors: Wenhui Chen, Huan Deng, Yuehan Dong, Xiangjie Wu, Zinuo Xia, Yang Zhou, Lin Yang, Zhijun Huang, Wenjin Xu, Peihu Xu, Haixing Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteochondral injuries, which could result in cartilage defects and damage to the subchondral bone, pose a significant challenge in the design of scaffolds for bone repair due to the different characteristics of bone and cartilage. In this study, we developed a double-network bilayer hydrogel scaffold composed of an upper layer and a lower layer reinforced with a ZnO nanoparticle. The double-network hydrogel consisted of carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS), oxidized sodium alginate (OSA), and poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) networks. The upper and lower layers were, respectively, loaded with puerarin (Pue) and curcumin (Cur) to facilitate simultaneous repair of cartilage and bone. The scaffold exhibited better water absorption, mechanical properties (tensile strength: 0.8 MPa), self-healing capacity (healing efficiency ≥ 90%), and adhesion (adhesive stress: 0.1 MPa). Approximately 70% of Pue was rapidly released within the first 5 days, while Cur, encapsulated in ZnO, exhibited sustained release over a period of 20 d. In vitro cell experiments revealed that soluble extracts from the scaffolds were noncytotoxic and promoted elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP) expression and calcium deposition in MC3T3-E1 subclone 14 (MC3T3-14) cells, thereby enhancing bone repair. Meanwhile, extracts fostered the proliferation of ATDC5 cells and glycosaminoglycan (GAG) production, contributing to cartilage repair. In conclusion, the bilayer hydrogel has good properties and is beneficial for the proliferation and differentiation of osteoblasts and chondrocytes.

Introduction

Osteochondral defects are characterized by lesions in articular cartilage and subchondral bone. Articular cartilage, a primary connective tissue devoid of vascular tissue, , possesses a smooth surface that minimizes the friction and vibration during bone movement, underscoring its vital role in human physiology. Despite this, it exhibits limited biological activity, low metabolic capacity, and an inability to self-repair. , When osteochondral defects manifest, they can progress from initial joint swelling and pain to severe osteoarthritis, potentially resulting in disability and loss of motor function, , leading to irreversible damage. Current treatment for osteochondral abnormalities encompasses allogeneic osteochondral transplantation, autologous chondrocyte implantation, microfracture surgery, and conservative medication therapy. , Although these methods have demonstrated efficacy in clinical practice, they still have some limitations, , including restricted donor availability, immune rejection, and the generation of suboptimal fibrous cartilage. , In contrast, bone tissue engineering emerges as a promising alternative for enhanced treatment efficacy.

Bone tissue engineering employs scaffolds, well-integrated cells, and biologically active growth factors and drugs to facilitate bone repair and regeneration. , Over recent decades, this field has witnessed rapid advancements. Consequently, there is a growing body of literature on the utilization of tissue engineering scaffolds for the repair of bone and cartilage defects, demonstrating promising therapeutic effects. , The subchondral bone plays a pivotal role by offering mechanical support and establishing a conductive environment for cartilage, making it indispensable for the restoration of both bone and cartilage. , Currently, a significant challenge in bone–cartilage repair is that most cartilage injuries also compromise subchondral bone. This, in turn, impacts cartilage metabolism and accelerates the deterioration of the condition. Solely focusing on cartilage repair while neglecting the subchondral bone often fails to yield the desired therapeutic results. , Thus, addressing bone–cartilage defects necessitates a comprehensive approach that considers both cartilage and subchondral bone. Osteochondral scaffolds are considered to be a good solution. However, the process of bone repair involves the repair of two different tissues, bone and cartilage; cartilage repair requires cells to proliferate or differentiate along the cartilage pathway, and bone tissue not only needs sufficient mechanical properties to support cartilage but also needs osteoconductivity to bind to the host bone. Therefore, in contrast to single-layer scaffolds, which can only meet the single needs of bone or cartilage, bilayer scaffolds can meet the different mechanical and biological requirements that guide the regeneration of two tissues. The bilayer scaffold can be tailored to meet the distinct functional requirements of its upper and lower layers, achieving a combined therapeutic effect. , Nevertheless, there are inherent limitations. Generally, bilayer scaffolds are constructed through adhesion or physical bonding between materials. This method lacks continuity or consistency between the upper and lower layers, resulting in weak adhesion and potential displacement of the materials in the body, thereby compromising the anticipated repair effect. , Consequently, enhancing the integration between the two-phase scaffolds remains a pressing challenge in tissue engineering for bone and cartilage defect repair.

PVA exhibits excellent hydrophilicity, stable chemical properties, and good biocompatibility. PVA hydrogels possess a water content akin to human tissue, a porous network structure, mechanical strength comparable to natural bone and cartilage, and notable self-healing properties. , These characteristics make them versatile for various applications in bone and cartilage healing. However, PVA hydrogels prepared using cross-linking agents such as borax demonstrate significant flexibility, ductility, and self-healing properties, but they suffer from poor stability, weak mechanical properties, and a short service life. , To address these issues, double-network (DN) hydrogels have been proposed as an effective solution. These hydrogels are constructed from two interpenetrating polymer networks. , The interlacing and entanglement of the two networks, based on different polymerization methods, effectively enhance pressure transfer and dispersion, thereby endowing the hydrogel with better mechanical qualities and extensibility. , Chitosan derivative carboxymethyl chitosan exhibits good water solubility, biocompatibility, and excellent biodegradability compared to chitosan. It can react with oxidized sodium alginate to create a hydrogel with self-healing properties, biocompatibility, and biodegradability, making it a frequent choice for mending bone and cartilage. ,

In this study, we fabricated a bilayer DN hydrogel scaffold for cartilage and bone repair. This scaffold comprised a network of PVA and borax as cross-linking agents along with a network of CMCS and OSA. Leveraging the self-healing property of the hydrogel, a bilayer structure was created. To enhance the inherently limited bone- and cartilage repair-promoting capabilities of the hydrogel, specific drugs were incorporated. Pue, known for its ability to protect cartilage and promote chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation, was added to the upper layer. The lower layer was enriched with ZnO nanoparticles loaded with Cur. While Cur is recognized for modulating osteoblast differentiation and proliferation, it exhibits low solubility and chemical stability, necessitating its encapsulation in a drug delivery system for sustained release. Zinc is a trace element found in bone that promotes osteoblast differentiation and activates proteins involved in bone homeostasis. ZnO nanoparticles as common zinc-containing nanoparticles are widely used in bone repair due to their biocompatibility, low toxicity, and promotion of bone formation and mineralization. , The incorporation of bioactive metallic nanoparticles into scaffolds is widely used for their ability to enhance bone regeneration. Combining these two components not only prolongs the Cur administration time but also mitigates the toxicity of ZnO nanoparticles, thereby enhancing the properties of the hydrogel and its bone repair promotion capability. The sustained release of Pue in the upper layer promotes cartilage regeneration, while Cur-loaded ZnO nanoparticles promote osteogenesis and stable mechanical support in the lower layer. With the release of the drug and degradation of the stent, both cartilage and subchondral bone defects can be repaired in a lasting manner, producing excellent long-term results. Furthermore, we characterized the morphology, mechanical attributes, self-healing capacities, and adhesion properties of the materials and assessed the cytotoxicity of the different hydrogels. We also evaluated the proliferation and differentiation potential of model osteoblasts and chondrocytes in the presence of soluble scaffold extracts.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Carboxymethyl chitosan, ethylene glycol, sodium alginate, sodium periodate acid (≥98%), zinc chloride, sodium hydroxide acid, 3-mercaptopropionic acid (MPA), 1′-carbonyl diimidazole (CDI), curcumin, puerarin, poly­(vinyl alcohol) (1750), borax, DMSO, and anhydrous ethanol were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). MC3T3-E1 subclone 14 (MC3T3-14) and ATDC5 cells were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), calcein-AM, alkaline phosphatase kit, BCIP/NBT alkaline phosphatase chromogenic kit, and Alizarin Red were purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Materials for DMEM, α-MEM, fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin–streptomycin (P/S), alcian blue, and other cellular experiments were purchased from Sangon (Shanghai, China).

Synthesis of ZnO Nanoparticles Loaded with Cur

Modified ZnO nanoparticles were synthesized by dissolving 2 g of ZnCl2 and 0.2 g of MPA in 60 mL of a water-anhydrous ethanol mixture (1:1, w/w); the pH was adjusted and maintained at 11 for a day. The modified ZnO nanoparticles were washed and dried to obtain modified ZnO nanoparticles, which were denoted as ZnO–MPA. The ZnO–MPA was dissolved in DMSO, the group was activated by adding a specific amount of CDI, and the mixture was stirred for 2 h. After centrifugation, the mixture was redissolved in DMSO, and Cur was added. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for a day, and the product was obtained after centrifugation and drying. It was denoted as ZnO–Cur. The unmodified ZnO was prepared using the same method.

Preparation of Two-Phase Hydrogels

The hydrogel was synthesized as follows: The precursor solutions were prepared by dissolving 10% PVA solution, 3% CMCS, and 3% OSA in RO separately. The three liquids were mixed in a 1:1:1 ratio at 60 °C with sufficient stirring. After 1 h, a total volume of 15% aqueous solution of borax (0.01 mol/L) was added to form a hydrogel. Then, it was put in a −20 °C refrigerator for 12 h and thawed at room temperature for 2 h; the operation was repeated 3 times to obtain the hydrogel.

The single-network (SN) hydrogel was synthesized as follows: Dissolving 10% PVA solution and adding a total volume of 15% aqueous solution of borax to form a hydrogel. The process of SN hydrogel formation was the same as the preparation of the hydrogel.

The upper hydrogel was synthesized as follows: 3.6 and 1.8 mg/mL of Pue were added to the precursor solutions (Pue is slightly soluble in water, and heating promotes solubility; in order to ensure the complete dissolution and stability of Pue, 60 °C was finally selected as the reaction temperature of the hydrogel). The process of upper hydrogel formation was the same as the preparation of the hydrogel.

The lower hydrogel was synthesized as follows: ZnO–Cur was added into the precursor solutions, and ultrasonication was performed to obtain a ZnO–Cur concentration of 1%. The process of lower hydrogel formation was the same as the preparation of the hydrogel.

The bilayer hydrogel was synthesized as follows: The lower and upper hydrogels were placed in contact with each other for 30 min to obtain the bilayer hydrogel.

Characterization of ZnO Nanoparticles Loaded with Cur

Dynamic light scattering (ZetaPlus, Brookhaven Instruments, Europe) and scanning electron microscopy (JSM-IT300, Dolee, Japan) were used to examine the nanoparticles’ microstructure, and the average particle size of the nanoparticles was calculated. The nanoparticles were point-scanned using an extra X-Max N80 energy dispersive spectrometer (JSM-7500F, Jeol, Japan) to obtain an elemental analysis.

Using MDI Jade 6.5 and an X-ray diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany), analysis was carried out using Cu K in the 2θ angle range of 10 to 60°. The crystallite size of the product was calculated using the Scherrer equation.

A Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer (Invenio, Bruker, Germany) was used to record the spectra of the different nanoparticles in the range 400–4000 cm–1, with a scanning resolution of 4 cm–1.

A standard curve for Cur at 430 nm was obtained using high-performance liquid chromatography (E2695-2489, Waters), with the mobile phase consisting of an acetonitrile–4% acetic acid aqueous solution (volume ratio 48:52). The supernatant from the synthesis and washing process of Cur-loaded ZnO nanoparticles was collected, and the Cur contents in the nanoparticles were calculated using the supernatant measurement method. The formula was as follows Cur content(%)=Cur(input‐supernatant)amount/Cur input amount×100%

Characterization of the Different Hydrogels

Water Absorption

To measure the water absorption rate of the hydrogel, after freeze-drying, the hydrogel’s weight was measured. The samples were placed in 15 mL of PBS (pH = 7.4) solution and removed from the buffer solution; after using filter paper to remove any remaining moisture from the samples’ surface, they were weighed and put back into the buffer solution. This process was repeated several times until the weight of the sample remained basically unchanged. The following formula was used to determine the water absorption rate water absorption rate Sw=Mp/Mc where M p was the weight of the hydrogel after absorption and M c was the initial weight of the hydrogel. Six tests were conducted on each hydrogel to obtain the average value.

Porosity

The porosity of each sample was evaluated using a liquid displacement technique. The different hydrogel groups were freeze-dried and placed in a pycnometer containing anhydrous ethanol, in which the volume of anhydrous ethanol was V 1. The volume change was observed at regular intervals until no bubbles overflowed. At this point, the total volume was V 2, and the remaining volume after removing materials was V 3. Each group was repeated six times. The porosity calculation formula was as follows porosity(%)=(V1−V3)/(V2−V3)×100%

Infrared Spectroscopy

A Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer (Invenio, Bruker, Germany) was used to record the spectra of the different groups in the range of 400–4000 cm–1 for analysis of the interactions between the system’s individual atoms or ions, as well as the hydrogels’ chemical makeup.

Scanning Electron Microscopy

In order to observe the internal state of the hydrogel scaffold, the morphology of the bilayer hydrogel was studied by scanning electron microscopy. The hydrogel was immersed in liquid nitrogen for half an hour, then freeze-dried, and crushed to form a cross-section. Gold was sprayed onto the hydrogel’s cross-section, and scanning electron microscopy was used to examine the hydrogel’s morphology.

Mechanical Strength

Mechanical compression and tensile tests were performed using a universal testing machine (Instron 5967, Canton). In identically sized molds (10 mm diameter and 10 mm height), the maximum strain was set to 100%, and the hydrogels were compressed at a speed of 2 mm/min. The hydrogel fracture energy was calculated by integrating the area under the compressive stress–strain curve. The tensile experiment was prepared by placing the hydrogel (10 × 4 × 3 mm3). The tensile experiment was performed on the hydrogel at a speed of 10 mm/min. The hydrogel toughness was determined by calculating the area integral under the tensile curve, and its Young’s modulus was determined by calculating the slope of the linear fit of the stress–strain curve at 5–15%.

Self-Healing Properties

The self-healing properties of the hydrogels were evaluated by macroscopic autonomous healing and tensile fracture experiments. The hydrogel was cut into two pieces, one of which was stained. The two hydrogels were placed in close contact to observe their self-healing properties. A certain tensile force was applied to the hydrogels at regular intervals to confirm the self-healing state of the hydrogels.

A hydrogel (10 × 4 × 3 mm3) was prepared and then cut into two pieces at the middle. The pieces were reheated, and their tensile strength was measured using a universal testing machine. The tensile strength of the repaired hydrogel was compared with that of the original hydrogel, and the healing efficiency was calculated using the following formula healing efficiency(%)=σH/σo×100% where σH was the tensile strength of the hydrogel after healing and σo was the tensile strength of the original hydrogel. Six measurements were made for each group.

Adhesion Properties

The adhesion and load-bearing behavior of the hydrogels on various substrates, including glass, rubber, plastic, metal, and bone, were investigated using a simple and intuitive adhesion test. Adhesive properties were measured by using lap-shear tests with a mechanical tester. In short, pigskin was cut into rectangles 3 cm long and 1 cm wide, and a square 1 cm long and 1 cm wide hydrogel was prepared and stuck between the two pieces of pigskin. The contact area between the two was noted as S cm2. The pigskin was placed in a stretching device and stretched at a speed of 1 mm per min until the three were separated, and the stretch curve was recorded. The following formula was used to determine the adhesive strength adhesive strength=F/S where F was the measured maximum load strength. The adhesive qualities of each sample were assessed by using the average result from six tests.

Rheological Properties

A rheometer (MCR 101, Anton Paar, Austria) with a parallel plate geometry was used to conduct rheological measurements. To make sure that the deformation imparted to the hydrogels was within the linear viscoelastic zone, dynamic viscoelastic measurements were carried out at a constant strain of 1%. The viscoelasticity of the different hydrogels was tested in a frequency range scanned from 100 to 0.1 rad/s.

Drug Release

The hydrogels were prepared at a ratio of 3.6 and 1.8 mg/mL Pue, and the groups were Pue-1 and Pue-2. The maximum detection wavelength of Pue was 250 nm, and the mobile phase was methanol/water = 30:70. The amount of Cur released was compared with pure drug-loaded ZnO nanoparticles and the nanoparticles in the hydrogel. The in vitro release behavior of the above materials in the hydrogel was determined by dialysis using PBS as the release medium. The above materials were placed in 50 mL of PBS medium and shaken at 37 °C and 120 rpm in a constant temperature shaker (LZY-200B, Yuejing, Shanghai). Every period, 3 mL of the release medium was taken and replaced with fresh PBS (3 mL). Each group was repeated six times. HPLC was used to measure the amount of Pue and Cur in the release medium, and the cumulative release rate was computed.

Degradation

In order to study the in vitro degradation ability of different groups, the prepared hydrogels were freeze-dried and weighed, and the initial weight of each sample was recorded as W 0. The samples were immersed in PBS buffer solution and were taken out at regular intervals. The surface moisture was wiped with filter paper, and freeze-drying was repeated. The mass of the hydrogel obtained each time was W t. The operation described above was repeated six times in parallel for each group. The degradation rate was calculated using the following formula degradationratio(%)=(W0−Wt)/W0×100%

Cell Proliferation and Toxicity Assays

ATDC5 cells were cultured in DMEM high glucose medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S, while MC3T3-14 cells were cultured in α-MEM medium under the same conditions. To prepare sterile extracts for cell culture, the different hydrogel groups were sterilized by ultraviolet irradiation for 30 min and then added to the medium prepared as described above. After being cultured in the cell incubator for a certain number of days, the hydrogels were removed, and sterile extracts were obtained by filtering through a 0.22 μm needle filter.

ATDC5 and MC3T3-14 cells were seeded at a density of 5000 cells per well in a 96-well plate (the volume of medium is 0.5 mL) and incubated with the extract for 1, 3, and 5 d. A CCK-8 kit was used to evaluate cell viability, and Calcein-AM reagent was used to stain the cells. Inverted fluorescence microscopy (FIM, Leica, Germany) was used to view the quantity and shape of live cells. The prepared hydrogel was soaked in ethanol for sterilization and then immersed in the basic culture medium. Then, it was inoculated in a 12-well plate with a density of 5 × 104 ATDC5 cells (The volume of medium is 2 mL). After 1 day of culture, live–dead cell staining was performed to assess cell viability on the hydrogel.

Bone-Promoting Evaluation of Cells

ALP Activity Analysis

MC3T3-14 cells were inoculated in 12-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well (The volume of medium is 2 mL), and after reaching 80% confluence, the medium was taken out and substituted with more α-MEM that included 10 nM dexamethasone, 10 mM sodium β-glycerophosphate, and 50 ug/mL ascorbic acid. Followed by incubation for 7 days as well as for 14 days (with the medium being changed every 3 days), the mixture was then analyzed qualitatively by the alkaline phosphatase assay kit for qualitative analysis, and expression was identified by measuring the absorbance at 405 nm. The BCIP/NBT alkaline phosphatase chromogenic kit was used for staining, which was then examined under an inverted microscope. The results were analyzed with Image-J.

Alizarin Red Staining

To assess the osteogenic differentiation potential of the hydrogels, this was achieved by in vitro ARS. MC3T3-14 cells were inoculated in six-well plates at a density of 8 × 104 cells per well (The volume of medium is 2 mL) and cultured in the medium as described in 2.2.1 for 21 d. After being cleaned with purified water, the cells were fixed for 30 min in 4% paraformaldehyde. Following five rounds of washing and 30 min of staining with an alizarin red solution, the fixed cells were examined under an inverted microscope and subjected to Image-J analysis. To quantify calcium deposition, the stained calcium deposits were added to cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC) solution to break up calcium nodules, and the absorbance at 562 nm was recorded.

Alcian Blue Staining

In order to observe the deposition of GAG, ATDC5 cells were inoculated in 12-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. (The volume of medium is 2 mL). After reaching 80% confluence, the medium was removed and replaced with additional DMEM containing 50 ug/mL ascorbic acid and 10 mM ITS (insulin–transferrin–selenium additive). Cells were then cultured for 7 d as well as 14 d. The cells were then fixed for 30 min in 4% paraformaldehyde, washed three times with PBS, and the pH was adjusted with 0.1 mol HCL. They were subsequently stained with 1% Alcian Blue 8-GX overnight at 4 °C, and an inverted microscope was used to view the staining. For quantitative analysis, the dye was extracted with 6 M guanidine hydrochloride, and an enzyme marker was used to detect its absorbance at 650 nm.

Statistical Analysis

For statistical analysis, SPSS software (version 19.0) was used. The mean and standard deviation were used to express all of the data. The cell experiment was examined using single-factor analysis of variance or ANOVA. Values *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001; ns, no significance was considered statistically significant. Then, the Tukey multiple comparison test was performed.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of ZnO

The synthesis pathways of the different nanoparticles are shown in Figure (A) and the real-life image in Figure (B). As seen from Figure (B),(D), most of the ZnO nanoparticles were hexagonal crystals. With the addition of MPA and Cur, the nanoparticles took on an irregular spherical shape. The average particle diameter of ZnO nanoparticles was 81 nm, and the average particle diameter of ZnO–MPA nanoparticles decreased to 36 nm. According to Table , because MPA was bonded to the surface of ZnO nanoparticles, the strong negative charge of the modified nanoparticles leads to reduced particle agglomeration, thereby synthesizing smaller spherical nanoparticles. The average particle size of ZnO–Cur nanoparticles increased slightly compared to that of ZnO–MPA nanoparticles, reaching 43 nm, and a core–shell structure could be seen. Based on the chemical reaction, it was speculated that ZnO was the inner layer, and MPA and Cur were the outer shell. As Cur was grafted onto the modified nanoparticles, the particle size increased slightly, but overall, it was still much smaller than that of ZnO. In Figure (C), EDS characterization of ZnO–MPA nanoparticles showed that the zinc content was 65.8%, oxygen content 22.8%, carbon content 10.6%, and sulfur content 0.82%; the presence of sulfur showed that MPA was present throughout the complex, thus proving that MPA had been successfully grafted onto the ZnO nanoparticles.

In order to confirm the impact of the structural properties of ZnO and the surface-grafted groups on the ZnO nanoparticles, the Figure (F) XRD pattern analysis’s findings revealed that the diffraction patterns of ZnO, ZnO–MPA, and ZnO–Cur all conformed to the hexagonal wurtzite structure (JCPDS 65-3411), and there was no significant difference between the three patterns, indicating that neither the grafted groups nor the loaded drugs had changed the crystal structure of the ZnO nanoparticles. After the MPA group was grafted, the nanoparticles were not prone to aggregation after being capped, and the crystal particle size decreased from 63 to 21 nm, which agreed with the SEM results as well.

In Figure (E), the infrared spectrum shows that all three nanoparticles had a broad peak between 3600 and 3400 cm–1, which was the O–H stretching vibration of physically adsorbed water on the ZnO surface. Compared with ZnO, ZnO–MPA clearly had a new peak at 1554 cm–1, corresponding to the asymmetric stretching vibration of the carboxylate and the carboxylate–iron complex. While there was no characteristic peak of the mercapto group at 2550 cm–1, indicating that the S atom on MPA replaced the oxygen atom in the O particle. And a substitution reaction occurred to form the ZnO–MPA complex, indicating that the −COOH group had been successfully grafted onto the ZnO nanoparticles. In the ZnO–Cur graph, there were the benzene ring stretching vibrations of Cur at 1629 cm–1, the CO and C–C vibrations at 1496 cm–1, and the aromatic C–O stretching vibrations at 1282 cm–1, whereas the C–O–C stretching vibration peaked at 1088 cm–1, which indicated the formation of an ester bond. The results demonstrated that Cur had been covalently bound to the surface of the ZnO nanoparticles by forming an ester bond.

The content of Cur measured by high-performance liquid chromatography was 20%. Alia Saif loaded Cur onto ZnO nanoparticles by electrostatic adsorption and obtained a drug loading of 5%. This showed that this method had a huge improvement in the capacity of Cur. This was because the addition of MPA provided carboxylic acid groups on ZnO, and CDI activated the carboxyl groups and formed ester bonds with Cur. Compared with electrostatic adsorption, chemical cross-linking provided more and more stable sites, thereby significantly increasing the drug loading capacity.

Characterization of Hydrogels

Water Absorption Rate

The high expansion rate and water absorption rate are important aspects for the design of bone and cartilage repair scaffolds, as they are conducive to the exchange of liquids and cell attachment. As seen from Figure (A), all three groups of hydrogels had relatively good water absorption rates. The initial water absorption rate increased rapidly, and basically equilibrium swelling was reached at 500 min. The lower group showed the greatest water absorption rate, and the reason might be the ZnO nanoparticles interacted with the incoming water molecules. And there were a large number of hydrogel groups on the PVA, which could interact with the ZnO nanoparticles to form additional hydrogen bonds. Therefore, the hydrogel was more tightly cross-linked, and the water absorption rate increased. In addition, as seen from the bilayer group test, the entire scaffold had a common water absorption capacity. During the water absorption process, the scaffold maintained its original shape without any rupture. This could avoid the impact of its shape change in the body and had a beneficial effect for implantation in vivo.

Porosity

Good porosity is conducive to the transport of nutrients and the exchange of small molecules, providing favorable conditions for cell growth. Figure (B) shows that the porosity of every group was greater than 70%, which satisfied the specifications for scaffolds used in bone tissue creation. With the addition of ZnO nanoparticles, the porosity of the hydrogel increased from 78 to 86%. The reason was that the groups on the nanoparticles could interact with the hydrogel, influencing the formation of a porous structure and consequently increasing hydrogel porosity. The porosity of the bilayer hydrogel was 81%, which was between the two.

Infrared Characterization

The infrared data of the PVA, OSA, CMCS, and hydrogel groups are shown in Figure (C) below. For PVA, the peak at 3356 cm–1 was relatively sharp, generated by the free hydrogel groups’ (O–H) stretching vibration. The value at 1150 cm–1 was the crystallinity-sensitive peak, which was related to the crystallinity. SA contained more −OH, which was prone to form hydrogel bonds with other groups containing lone pairs of electrons, resulting in a decrease in the O–H stretching vibration frequency and a broadening of the spectral band. At 3414 cm–1, a large and powerful peak emerged, while after modification, SA showed a stretching vibrational peak, which indicated that SA was successfully oxidized to OSA by sodium periodate. While in the hydrogel group, this characteristic peak disappeared because OSA and CMCS underwent a Schiff base reaction to form an imine bond. The characteristic absorption peak at 1650 cm–1 in the hydrogel group belonged to the imine bond, which also indicated the occurrence of the Schiff base reaction. The asymmetric stretching vibration of the B–O–C bond was responsible for the distinctive absorption peaks of the hydrogel group from 1377 to 1440 cm–1, indicating the presence of a borate bond in the hydrogel. The FTIR results presented above supported the hypothesis of a double-network structure with Schiff base reactions and borate bond cross-links in the hydrogel.

The infrared data of the lower hydrogel group, Pue, and the bilayer hydrogel group are shown in Figure (D). For the lower hydrogel group, compared to the pure hydrogel group, a new absorption peak at 1088 cm–1 could be found, which was the absorption peak of the ester bond on the ZnO nanoparticles. The results showed that the nanoparticles had been successfully loaded into the hydrogel. The bilayer hydrogel group showed characteristic peaks of Pue with 1515 cm–1 corresponding to the ring skeleton, 890 cm–1 to the benzene ring skeleton of Pue, and 1277 cm–1 representing the O–H stretching vibration. At the same time, the absorption peak of the ester bond of ZnO nanoparticles also appeared at 1088 cm–1. The above data all showed that the bilayer hydrogel contained ZnO nanoparticles loaded with Cur and Pue, proving the successful synthesis of the bilayer drug-loaded scaffold.

Morphological Characterization

The porous structure of the hydrogel plays a vital role in bone and cartilage tissue engineering. The porous structure of the hydrogel provides a framework for cell adhesion and promotes the formation of the extracellular matrix of bone and cartilage cells. As seen from Figure (A)–(F), the scanning and appearance images of the hydrogel showed the morphology of a well-formed bilayer hydrogel. Because the lower layer contained Cur-loaded ZnO nanoparticles, its color was yellow. And the hydrogel showed a distinct color change with layering. The interface between the two layers of hydrogel was also clearly visible in the scanning image. Both the upper and lower hydrogels showed a continuous porous structure, and the two layers were firmly connected without any separated areas. The upper scaffold and the lower scaffold had different morphologies. Compared with the upper layer, a denser structure was observed in the lower layer. This was because the addition of nanoparticles reduced the pore size.

Mechanical Properties

Mechanical properties are important for bone repair scaffolds, because the scaffolds need to support the regeneration of bone tissue in the defect area and maintain certain integrity during cell growth. The compression mechanical properties of the three hydrogel groups are shown by the compression stress–strain curves in Figure (A). Figure (B) shows that the fracture energy of the SN hydrogel was 2.1 ± 0.45 MJ/m3 and that of the upper hydrogel group was 2.6 ± 0.12 MJ/m3; the addition of CMCS/OSA networks enhanced the compressive performance of the hydrogel. The fracture energy of the lower hydrogel group increased to 5.3 ± 0.84 MJ/m3 after the addition of ZnO nanoparticles. This was because ZnO nanoparticles were added to the hydrogel as a chemical and physical cross-linking agent, which could improve the hydrogel’s mechanical qualities by making its network structure dense and complicated. The fracture energy of the bilayer hydrogel group was 4.3 ± 0.39 MJ/m3, which was between the data of the two groups.

In terms of tensile properties, the hydrogel scaffolds for bone and cartilage repair should have excellent mechanical properties to withstand deformation caused by movement. As seen from the tensile stress–strain curves in Figure (C)–(E), the SN hydrogel and upper hydrogel were broken when the tensile strengths were 180 ± 13 and 801 ± 34 KPa, and the tensile strains were 563 ± 25 and 275 ± 18%, respectively. Although the double-network hydrogel reduces the elongation break of the SN hydrogel, it significantly enhances the tensile strength. High tensile strength can ensure that objects have greater durability when subjected to external forces, which is beneficial for hydrogels to remain in the body for a longer time. Under the same experimental conditions, the lower group was broken when the tensile strength was 793 ± 29 KPa and the tensile strain was 283 ± 15%. The bilayer group had a tensile strength of 735 ± 21 KPa and failed at 213 ± 26% strain. All three hydrogel groups exhibited high mechanical properties and excellent flexibility. Young’s modulus and toughness of the upper group hydrogel were 0.72 ± 0.051 MPa and 1.3 ± 0.016 MJ/m3, while the lower group had values of 1.4 ± 0.091 MPa and 1.7 ± 0.087 MJ/m3. The hydrogel’s strength and characteristics were greatly enhanced by the addition of nanoparticles. The reason might be that the nanoparticles were dispersed in the hydrogel to enhance the chemical and physical cross-linking of the hydrogel. Young’s modulus and toughness of the bilayer group were 1.3 ± 0.065 MPa and 0.95 ± 0.013 MJ/m3, respectively. All of the above data showed that the hydrogel not only had good tensile strength and toughness but also had excellent application prospects in bone repair.

Self-Healing

Self-healing hydrogels can protect cells through weak interactions and heal themselves under physiological conditions without external stimuli. This prevents the hydrogel structure from being damaged due to the complex internal environment and prolongs the hydrogel’s useful life. It can be used in many different ways to heal bone and cartilage. To test the self-healing properties of hydrogels, the Schiff base reaction between the OSA and CMCS produced dynamic and reversible imine bonds. The PVA and borax produced dynamic and reversible borate ester bonds and hydrogen bonds, all of which gave the hydrogel excellent self-healing properties. The self-healing properties of different hydrogels could be demonstrated by macroscopic and tensile experiments. As shown in Figure (A), the hydrogels in different groups were cut and brought together again. Without applying any external force and after a certain self-healing time, the hydrogels were connected again and did not break when stretched to a certain extent, indicating that the hydrogels were completely healed. Tensile tests were used to further assess the hydrogels’ capacity for self-healing in Figure (B),(C). After a certain healing time, the fracture tensile strength of the self-healing upper group hydrogel was 687 ± 21 KPa, and the fracture elongation was 225 ± 13%. The values for the lower group were 737 ± 19 KPa and 220 ± 18%, and the values for the bilayer group were 735 ± 11 KPa and 213 ± 24%. Since the bilayer group itself was a self-healing hydrogel, its self-healing efficiency was obtained by calculating the average self-healing efficiency of the upper group and lower group. The self-healing efficiencies of the above three groups of hydrogels were, respectively, 86.9, 90.0, and 92.2%. All of the results indicated that the hydrogels had strong self-healing capabilities.

Adhesion

In addition to self-healing properties, the bilayer hydrogel needs to contact the bone and maintains certain adhesion in practical applications. As shown in Figure (A), the hydrogel could firmly adhere to various substrates, including glass, metal, plastic, etc. More importantly, the hydrogel could directly adhere to bone and had a certain load-bearing capacity. It indicated the hydrogel had good tissue adhesion behavior, which was more conducive to the application of hydrogel in bone and cartilage repair. Its adhesion ability might be attributed to the presence of a large number of hydrogen bonds and borate ester bonds in the hydrogel network, which could physically interact with different materials and coordinate with metals. Lap-shear tests in Figure (B) were used to evaluate the adhesion characteristics of several hydrogels. As seen from Figure (C), the adhesion strengths of the upper hydrogel, bilayer hydrogel, and lower hydrogel groups were 91, 82, and 71 KPa, respectively. The reason for the decrease in the adhesion strength of the hydrogel after the addition of nanoparticles might be that the nanoparticles had reacted chemically with the hydrogel, which led to a certain decrease in the adhesion strength. Good adhesion promotes adhesion and retention of the hydrogel at the application site, thereby helping to improve tissue regeneration.

Rheological Properties

Hydrogels are flexible soft materials with elastic properties, and their rheological properties strongly affect their application as biomaterials in bone repair. Monitoring the frequency-dependent change in the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) could be used to determine the relationship between mechanical properties and cross-linking and polymer chain interactions. G′ was usually related to the material’s ability to resist elastic deformation forces, while G″ was a measure of the amount of energy lost associated with the material’s viscous behavior. The viscoelastic behavior of the different hydrogel groups was examined by using an oscillatory shear test. As shown in Figure (A),(B), the G′ of all groups was much higher than G″, indicating that the hydrogels were highly elastic. The storage moduli of the three hydrogels reached 1207, 3544, and 5909 Pa, respectively. The addition of nanoparticles was the cause of the notable rise in storage modulus, which chemically and physically cross-linked the hydrogel. The nanoparticles were uniformly dispersed in the hydrogel and produced a more integrated and compacted hydrogel network. The storage modulus of the bilayer hydrogel exceeded that of the upper hydrogel at all frequency angles, indicating that the bilayer hydrogel had a stiffer network structure.

Drug Release Performance

As shown in Figure (C), Pue-1 released the drug into a plateau phase after about 4 days, with a cumulative drug release of 67.6%, while the drug release of the Pue-2 group at the same time was 55.4%. This indicated that the higher the drug content, the higher the cumulative drug release. In order to release more drugs more quickly, the drug loading ratio of the Pue-1 group was finally selected as the group for subsequent experiments. Figure (D) shows the effect of nanocomposite hydrogels and pure drug-loaded nanoparticles on the release of Cur. The nanocomposite hydrogel released a cumulative 23% within 24 h, while the nanoparticle group released 10%. This might be because Cur was grafted onto the surface of ZnO nanoparticles, and the hydrogel groups on Cur could bond with the amine group on the chitosan, resulting in the initial release of some Cur into the hydrogel. This could also be seen from the appearance of the bilayer hydrogel: the lower layer of the hydrogel was yellow, which also indicated that some Cur had been loaded into the hydrogel, resulting in a significant initial increase in the level of Cur released from the composite hydrogel group. Subsequently, the drug was slowly released. The composite hydrogel group reached a peak of about 63% at around 18 days, while the Cur release of the nanoparticle group was 37%. This was because over time the nanoparticles in the composite hydrogel were evenly distributed in the hydrogel, which allowed for more complete liquid exchange with the outside world, while the nanoparticles in the solution settled and were difficult to evenly disperse in the release solution, all agglomerating together to slowly release the drug. In summary, the release curves of the two drugs were shown. It could be clearly seen that Pue achieved rapid release in the early stage, while Cur, due to being grafted onto the nanoparticles, had a sustained-release effect. The combined use of the two drugs has potential application prospects in bone and cartilage repair.

Degradation Performance

The degradation rate is an important characteristic of hydrogels. An ideal scaffold should have a degradation rate that is consistent with the formation of new tissue, gradually regenerating new tissue while the scaffold degrades, and constantly providing spaces for cell and tissue regeneration. The in vitro degradation of the prepared hydrogels is shown in Figure (F). Compared with the lower and bilayer groups, the upper group underwent higher degradation. After 49 d, the three groups of hydrogels released 65, 71, and 76%, respectively. The hydrogel system of the nanocomposite showed a slower rate of breakdown, probably because the chemical bonds on the nanoparticles formed a higher cross-linking density and a stronger and more stable network structured with the hydrogel, effectively reducing the degradation rate of the hydrogel. In summary, all three hydrogels had good degradation properties. The slow degradation of the scaffold not only promoted drug release and nutrient exchange but also provided spaces for cell growth, which was beneficial in bone repair.

Cell Viability Assay

Good cytocompatibility is an important characteristic of bone repair scaffolds and can support cell growth and proliferation in vitro. Cell compatibility was assessed using CCK-8 cell viability assays and live/dead staining. The cell proliferation results showed that the soluble extracts from all of the hydrogel groups were noncytotoxic to ATDC5 and MC3T3-14 cells (cell viability greater than 85%). In Figure (A),(C), on days 1 and 3, there was no discernible difference between the various hydrogel groups and the control group. On day 5, the bilayer hydrogel’s cell proliferation rate was noticeably greater than the control group’s. As seen from Figure (D), the results showed that the hydrogel had good biocompatibility and could promote the proliferation of ATDC5 cells. In order to further observe the proliferation effect of the hydrogel on cells, Calcein-AM was used to stain the live cells. Using a fluorescence microscope, the condition of the cells was examined. The cells maintained their normal shape, and the cell density increased with time. Compared to the control group, the bilayer hydrogel group’s cell density was noticeably higher, further demonstrating that the hydrogel scaffold had good cell compatibility and cell proliferation ability. In Figure (E), the cells survived on the hydrogel shown in the picture, indicating that the prepared hydrogel was nontoxic to cells. In summary, the bilayer hydrogel scaffold prepared in this study had good cell compatibility and could be used for bone and cartilage repair.

Cell Osteogenic Ability

ALP

The membrane-bound enzyme ALP, which is responsible for the mineralization of the extracellular matrix’s mineralization, is considered a biomarker of osteogenic differentiation and new bone formation. Therefore, the activity of ALP could be measured by the ALP kit to reflect the cell differentiation ability. As seen from Figure (A),(C), the three hydrogel groups’ OD values were noticeably higher than those of the control group, especially the bilayer hydrogel was much higher than the monolayer hydrogel group, indicating that the combined use of Pue and Cur had a synergistic effect and could promote the differentiation of osteoblasts better than a single administration, which was also consistent with the expected effect. The staining images in Figure (B) also verified the mineralization of the different groups. Both on days 7 and 14, the bilayer hydrogel had more mineralized nodules and darker colors, which also indicated that the bilayer hydrogel group could promote early osteogenic differentiation and enhance ALP activity.

(A) ALP staining of MC3T3-14 cells for the different hydrogel groups. (B) ALP relative grayscale (of control) of different hydrogel groups on days 7 and 14. (C) ALP activity of different hydrogel groups after growing for 7 and 14 d. (*P < 0.05, ***P < 0.001, n = 6).

Alizarin Red Staining (ARS)

Calcium is the most abundant mineral in the bones and teeth of the human body. Cell mineralization is manifested as the formation of mineralized nodules or calcium deposits. ARS can react with calcium to produce a reddish deposit. Therefore, ARS can be used to assess the mineralization of MC3T3-14 cells and identify the region of calcium deposition in various hydrogel groups. As shown in Figure (A),(B), calcium nodules were observed in all groups. Compared with the control group and the material group, the lower, upper, and bilayer hydrogel groups showed more calcium content and stronger red staining. In addition, the bilayer hydrogel had a larger stained area and darker nodules than the other two groups. The formation of mineralized nodules was further quantified by evaluating ARS deposits and measuring the absorbance at 562 nm. As seen from Figure (C), according to the quantitative findings, the bilayer hydrogel group had the highest OD value on day 21 compared to the other groups, which further verified the results of the ARS staining. In summary, the designed bilayer scaffold could not only encourage the development of osteogenic differentiation and calcium deposits but also had better application prospects in bone–cartilage repair.

Evaluation of Chondrogenesis

ATDC5 cells can simulate the process of cartilage differentiation in vitro. During differentiation, they initially enter the proliferation phase, secreting type II collagen and proteoglycans, as well as other extracellular matrix, which can explore the potential mechanism of cartilage formation and differentiation. One crucial phenotypic factor for chondrogenesis and chondrogenic differentiation was the deposition of GAG. Alcian Blue, a tetravalent cationic dye, can chemically react with GAG under low pH conditions. It was used to observe GAG deposition. As determined by Alizarin Blue staining in Figure (A),(B), the deposition of GAG in the bilayer hydrogel group was significantly increased. The size and number of blue-stained nodules were much higher than those in the other groups on both days 7 and 14. According to quantitative analysis in Figure (C), the absorbance value of the bilayer hydrogel group was also much higher than that of the other groups. The above experimental interpretation showed that the designed bilayer scaffold can enhance the accumulation of GAG during the differentiation of ATDC5 cartilage, thereby promoting cartilage differentiation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, a double-network dual drug-loaded bilayer hydrogel was developed for bone–cartilage repair. The bilayer hydrogel had better pore size distribution, water absorption, mechanical properties, adhesiveness, and self-healing capabilities, making it a potential scaffold for tissue engineering. The upper layer of the hydrogel was loaded with Pue, which was rapidly released in the initial stage to promote the proliferation and differentiation of the chondrocytes. Conversely, the lower layer was infused with ZnO nanoparticles containing Cur, extending the drug release duration and facilitating a gradual release, which was beneficial for long-term osteogenic induction. The combined effect of these two drugs indicated synergy for bone and cartilage repair. Cell experiments confirmed the hydrogel’s nontoxicity. Furthermore, cell mineralization results, along with ARS and Alizarin Blue staining, underscored the hydrogel’s efficacy as a Pue and Cur delivery vehicle to promote alkaline phosphatase activity and mineral deposition of MC3T3-14 cells and GAG synthesis of ATDC5 cells. Overall, the crafted bilayer hydrogel scaffold emerges as a promising and effective material for tissue engineering applications in bone and cartilage.
