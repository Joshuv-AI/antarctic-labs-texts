Social isolation disrupts prefrontal excitatory–inhibitory balance to impair social novelty memory in adult male mice
Authors: Quan Yuan, Bingyu Ren, Laijian Wang, Xuefeng Zheng, Kedong Chen, Danlei Liu, Shuhan Cha, Sinyi Liu, Guoqing Guo, Jifeng Zhang, Caihui Cha, Hanjun Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSocial isolation is a pervasive stressor that disrupts social cognition, yet its neural mechanisms in adulthood remain unclear. Here, we showed that four weeks of social isolation in adult male mice selectively impaired social novelty memory without affecting general sociability, object recognition, working memory, or anxiety- and depression-like behaviors. This deficit was sex-specific, as female mice remained unaffected. Mechanistically, c-Fos mapping and in vivo calcium imaging revealed that social isolation induced a maladaptive shift in prefrontal excitatory–inhibitory (E/I) balance, characterized by both exaggerated activation of medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) glutamatergic neurons and attenuated recruitment of local GABAergic interneurons specifically during novel social exploration. Causal manipulations showed that chemogenetic or optogenetic activation of mPFC glutamatergic neurons in group-housed males was sufficient to recapitulate the social novelty memory impairment, whereas inhibition of these neurons in isolated mice rescued the deficit. At the cellular level, electrophysiological recordings demonstrated that SI enhanced the intrinsic excitability of mPFC pyramidal neurons and induced a synaptic E/I imbalance, characterized by increased excitatory and decreased inhibitory drive. Together, our findings establish that adult social isolation disrupts social novelty memory processing by shifting the mPFC excitatory-inhibitory balance toward a net hyperexcitable state, revealing a reversible, circuit-specific mechanism for isolation-induced social cognitive deficits. Subject terms: Psychiatric disorders, Learning and memory

Introduction

For mammals, social behavior plays an essential role in environmental adaptation, survival, and reproduction. It encompasses not only cooperation and competition but also involves resource sharing, group structure maintenance, and the sustenance of individual social bonds [1, 2]. Natural social interaction in rodents is typically characterized and measured across several key dimensions. These primarily include social motivation, which reflects the drive to initiate and sustain interactions with conspecifics, along with social novelty memory. Social novelty memory primarily measures social recognition, representing the ability to discriminate familiar from novel social stimuli. Additionally, it measures novelty preference, which is defined as a preferential exploration of novel social stimuli [3, 4]. Collectively, these dimensions reflect highly organized regulatory mechanisms within the nervous system. However, when individuals experience a prolonged lack of social contact, a condition referred to as social isolation (SI), this fundamental adaptive mechanism can be disrupted [5]. In modern society, SI has become a prevalent form of chronic social stress across ages and socioeconomic backgrounds. It has been demonstrated to significantly correlate with increased morbidity and mortality in cancer and cardiovascular disease [6, 7], as well as mental health risks such as anxiety and depression [8]. However, the underlying neurobiological mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) plays a central role in the “top-down” regulation of social cognition and social decision-making [9]. Numerous rodent model studies have shown that the mPFC is involved in social environment detection and behavioral flexibility [10, 11], thereby making it particularly important in social novelty memory [12, 13]. In these processes, the dynamic interplay between glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons within the mPFC is crucial for information integration and behavioral output. Glutamatergic neurons constitute the primary projection and integration units, receiving and processing environmental and behavioral signals to support the detection of and adaptation to unfamiliar social stimuli [14]. Meanwhile, their function is tightly regulated by local GABAergic interneurons, which sculpt neural activity patterns and maintain circuit stability [15]. A disruption of this excitatory-inhibitory (E/I) balance has been implicated in the pathophysiology of various psychiatric disorders characterized by social dysfunction, such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD), anxiety, and depression [16–18]. This suggests that maladaptive shifts in mPFC E/I balance may represent a fundamental neural substrate underlying impaired social cognition.

Regarding the impact of SI on mPFC function, existing research has primarily focused on early life or adolescent stages, revealing profound and often complex alterations in circuit structure. For instance, studies have found that mice isolated during juvenility or adolescence exhibit increased dendritic spine density and synaptic remodeling in the mPFC [19–21]. Concurrently, early-life isolation can reduce the expression of pre- and postsynaptic proteins in inhibitory neurons and decrease the number of GAD67-positive interneurons [22]. These opposing changes in excitatory and inhibitory circuitry indicate that SI during critical developmental periods may lead to lasting and complex structural alterations. At the functional level, a model of juvenile isolation has paradoxically reported a shift in mPFC E/I balance toward reduced net excitation, characterized by both decreased pyramidal neuron activity and increased SST+ inhibitory neuron excitability [23]. In addition, though both males and females exhibit persistent structural and functional alterations in mPFC during early-life or adolescent SI, several studies report sex-specific differences. For instance, post-weaning SI increases dendritic spine density in the mPFC in both sexes. However, this effect is significantly more pronounced in the infralimbic subregion in females [24]. Similarly, adolescent isolation induces greater perturbations in the oxytocin system in females, whereas males are more likely to exhibit cognitive deficits or dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis [25, 26]. These findings provide important insights into how SI during development affects brain circuits.

However, the mechanistic studies on adulthood SI, a condition that more closely resembles real-life human scenarios, remain limited. The neural plasticity potential in the adult brain, as well as its activation mechanisms and associated behavioral responses, operates through processes that extend beyond those during development [27, 28]. It’s unclear whether SI elicits comparable effects on the mPFC in adulthood as it does during early life and adolescent stages, or whether it leads to unique functional and structural adaptations. Therefore, in this study, we systematically evaluated the effects of SI during adulthood on social behavior in mice of both sexes. We found that adult male mice exhibited specific impairments in social novelty memory, whereas female mice were not significantly affected. This behavioral deficit in male mice was linked to a maladaptive shift in E/I balance within the mPFC. The shift was characterized by both hyperactivation of glutamatergic neurons and deficient recruitment of GABAergic interneurons during social novelty memory testing phase. Causal manipulations confirmed that increasing the activity of mPFC glutamatergic neurons in group-housed mice was sufficient to reproduce the memory deficit. Conversely, inhibiting these neurons in isolated mice rescued social novelty memory. At the cellular level, SI both enhanced the intrinsic excitability of mPFC glutamatergic neurons and induced an E/I imbalance at their synapses. Overall, our study identifies the disruption of prefrontal E/I homeostasis as a key circuit mechanism through which adult SI impairs social cognition in male mice.

Materials and methods

Animals and models

All animal experiments were conducted following the guidelines outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals by the National Institutes of Health, and were approved by the Jinan University Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee (Guangzhou, China). Eight-week-old male and female C57BL/6J mice (Zhuhai Best Bio-Tech) were group-housed (3–5 per cage) under standard conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity, 12/12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. After one week of acclimation, mice were randomly assigned to either continue group housing or undergo four weeks of social isolation (SI), following a previously described protocol with slight modifications [29]. Generally, SI mice were singly housed in standard cages placed on the same rack as controls. To minimize sensory contact, each isolation cage was wrapped on all four sides with opaque black plastic, and bedding was reduced by half.

Behavioral tests

All behavioral data were acquired and analyzed with the TopScanLite system (v2.0). Tests were performed sequentially with at least 24-h inter-test intervals to prevent interference. Behavioral testing was not blinded, but data extraction and analysis were done blind to group.

Social interaction test (SIT)

The test was conducted in an open field arena (40 × 40 × 40 cm) containing an empty, perforated transparent cage (10 × 6.5 × 40 cm). In the 150-second habituation phase, the test mouse explored freely. After a 30-min interval, a novel target mouse was placed inside the cage for the 150-second social phase. The time spent in the interaction zone (an 8 cm perimeter around the cage) and the opposite corner zone (9 × 9 cm) was measured. The social interaction ratio was calculated as (time in interaction zone with target) / (time in interaction zone without target).

Three chamber tests (TCST)

The three-chamber apparatus consisted of three interconnected compartments (20 × 40 × 20 cm each). Mice underwent a 10-min habituation with empty chambers, followed by a 10-min sociability phase in which an unfamiliar mouse (Stranger 1) was placed under a wire cage in one side chamber and an object in the opposite chamber. In the final 10-min social novelty memory testing phase, a novel mouse (Stranger 2) replaced the object. A 2-cm perimeter around each cage defined the interaction zone. The sociability recognition index was calculated as (time with Stranger 1 / total time with Stranger 1 and object) × 100%. The social novelty memory recognition index was (time with Stranger 2 / total time with both strangers) × 100%.

Novel object recognition test (NORT)

Twenty-four hours prior to the test, the mice were habituated to the empty open field arena for 10 min. The formal test consisted of two phases. In the 10-min training phase, they explored two identical cylindrical objects. After a 1-h interval, one object was replaced with a novel cubic block for the 10-min testing phase. The novel object recognition index was (time exploring the novel object / total exploration time) × 100%.

Y maze

The Y-maze had three identical opaque arms (35 × 5 × 10 cm). In the 10-min training phase, one arm was blocked. After a 2-h interval, all arms were open for a 5-min test phase. The movement of the mouse in all sessions were recorded, and the time spent in the novel arm in the testing phase was analyzed.

Open field test (OFT)

Mice were placed in the center of a dark blue opaque arena (40 × 40 × 40 cm). Each mouse was placed in the center at the beginning of the test and allowed to freely explore for 10 min. The time spent in the central zone and the total distance traveled of each mouse were analyzed.

Elevated plus maze (EPM)

Mice were placed on the central platform of a maze elevated 50 cm above the floor, consisting of two open arms and two enclosed arms (each 35 × 5 cm). During the 10-min test session, the time spent in and entries into the open arms of each mouse were quantified.

Forced swimming test (FST)

Mice were placed in a cylindrical glass tank (20 cm diameter, 27 cm high) filled with 20 cm of water maintained at 21–26 °C for 6 min. Immobility time during the final 4 min was recorded and analyzed. Immobility was defined as the absence of active movements that generate horizontal displacement, with the mouse making only the minimal movements necessary to keep its head above the water.

Tail suspension test (TST)

Mice were allowed to acclimate to the room for 45 min before test, then each mouse was suspended by the tail 30 cm above the floor for 6 min. Immobility time during the final 4 min was recorded. Immobility was defined as the absence of active escape-directed movements, with the mouse exhibiting only minimal motion necessary to maintain posture.

Immunofluorescence

To identify neuronal populations associated with social novelty recognition, we employed a behavioral tagging paradigm based on sequential social exposures. Adult mice were individually subjected to two social interaction sessions, each lasting 10 min and separated by a 1.5-h interval. In the “familiar–familiar” (F–F) condition, mice were exposed to the same conspecific in both sessions. In the “familiar–novel” (F–N) condition, mice were first exposed to a familiar conspecific in the initial session, followed by exposure to a novel conspecific in the second session. This paradigm allowed the identification of neuronal populations preferentially associated with social novelty, by comparing activation patterns between repeated familiar interaction and novel social exposure.

For c-Fos immunostaining, mice were perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) 90 min post-behavior under deep sodium pentobarbital (120 mg/kg) anesthesia. Brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA for 12 h at 4 °C, cryoprotected in 15 and 30% sucrose, and sectioned at 30 μm. Sections were blocked in 3% BSA with 1% Triton X-100, and incubated with primary antibodies for 48 h at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibodies for 2 h at RT. Images were acquired on a Leica DMI8 microscope. Primary antibodies: rabbit anti-c-Fos (1:300, CST2250s), mouse anti-CaMKIIα (1:300, ab22609), rabbit anti-GABA (1:500, A2052). Secondary antibodies for immunostaining were Alexa Fluor 555 goat anti-mouse (1:800, #A31570, Invitrogen, California, USA), Alexa Fluor 555 goat anti-rabbit (1:800, #A21428, Invitrogen), Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-goat (1:800, #A11055, Invitrogen), Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit (1:800, #A21206, Invitrogen).

Stereotaxic surgery

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% induction, 1% maintenance; RWD) and placed in a stereotaxic frame. Viral vectors were bilaterally injected into the mPFC (AP + 2.1 mm, ML ± 0.33 mm, DV − 2.1 mm) using a glass micropipette connected to a nanoliter injector at 50 nl/min (150–250 nl per side). The pipette was left in place for 10 min before withdrawal. For optogenetic stimulation or in vivo calcium recording, an optical fiber (200 μm diameter, 0.37 NA; Inper Tech) was implanted at the same coordinates. The fiber was secured with skull screws and dental cement.

In vivo Ca2+ fiber photometry

AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6m-WPRE-pA or AAV2/9-mDlx-GCaMP6m-WPRE-pA (0.2 μl, 2.30 × 10¹³ vg/ml; Taitool Bioscience) was bilaterally injected into the mPFC. An optical fiber (200 μm diameter, 0.37 NA; Inper Tech) was implanted for in vivo calcium recording. Following viral expression, mice were randomly assigned to control or SI groups and subjected to four weeks of housing manipulation. Calcium signals were recorded during behavioral testing using a fiber photometry system (FPS-410/470, Inper Tech) with 470 nm excitation for GCaMP6m fluorescence and 410 nm as an isosbestic control for motion and bleaching. Signals were analyzed using Inper Plot and custom MATLAB scripts. Fluorescence changes were calculated as ΔF/F = (F – F0)/F0, where F was obtained after motion and bleaching correction and F₀ represented the pre-task baseline.

In vivo and in vitro electrophysiology

In vivo electrophysiology

The in vivo electrophysiology was performed as previously reported [30]. A 16-channel electrode was implanted into the mPFC. After recovery, mice were randomly assigned to the SI and Ctrl groups and subjected to four weeks of modeling. Upon completion of the modeling procedure, extracellular single-unit activity was recorded under resting conditions, sorted by Offline Sorter, and analyzed using NeuroExplorer. For neuronal classification, putative pyramidal neurons (excitatory) and interneurons (inhibitory) were distinguished using κ-means clustering based on three features: half-spike width, trough-to-peak ratio, and baseline firing rate. Consistent with established criteria [31], interneurons were identified by narrow half-spike width (<250 μs), low trough-to-peak ratio (<1.5), and high firing rate (>5 Hz). Pyramidal neurons were identified by broad half-spike width (>250 μs), high trough-to-peak ratio (>1.5), and low firing rate (<5 Hz). After the electrophysiological recordings were completed, brain tissue was collected for histological verification of electrode placement. Mice in which the electrode track did not localize to the mPFC were excluded from the final analysis.

In vitro electrophysiology

All reagents used for electrophysiological experiments were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Whole-cell recordings were performed as previously reported [32]. Acute mPFC slices (300 μm) were prepared from mice deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with ice-cold, oxygenated cutting solution containing (in mM): 212.7 sucrose, 3 KCl, 1.25 NaH₂PO₄, 3 MgCl₂, 1 CaCl₂, 26 NaHCO₃, and 10 glucose. Slices were incubated in oxygenated ACSF (in mM: 124 NaCl, 3 KCl, 1.25 NaH₂PO₄, 1 MgCl₂, 2 CaCl₂, 26 NaHCO₃, 10 glucose) at 35 °C for 30 min and then maintained at room temperature.

Recordings were obtained from visually identified layer V pyramidal neurons using a Multiclamp 700B amplifier and Digidata 1550B digitizer (filtered at 2.8 kHz, sampled at 10 kHz). Intrinsic excitability was assessed in current-clamp mode by measuring rheobase and spike number in response to depolarizing current steps (0–500 pA, 20 pA increments) using a potassium gluconate–based internal solution.

Synaptic transmission was examined in voltage-clamp mode. AMPAR-mediated mEPSCs were recorded at −70 mV in ACSF containing TTX (1 μM), bicuculline (20 μM), and D,L-APV (100 μM) using a Cs-methylsulfonate–based internal solution. GABAAR-mediated mIPSCs were recorded at −60 mV in ACSF with TTX, CNQX (20 μM), and D,L-APV using a CsCl-based internal solution. Data were analyzed using Mini Analysis with a detection threshold set at three times the RMS noise.

Chemical genetics and optogenetic manipulations

Three weeks after viral expression, chemogenetic or optogenetic manipulations were performed during behavioral testing. For chemogenetics, mice received intraperitoneal CNO (3.3 mg/kg) 45 min before testing to activate hM3Dq- or inhibit hM4Di-expressing neurons. Optogenetic experiments were conducted in parallel, with 465 nm blue light (20 Hz, 3 mW, 3–5 ms) used for activation and 589 nm yellow light (20 Hz, 3 mW, 3–5 ms) for inhibition via implanted optical fibers targeting the mPFC.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with the exact sample size (n) for each group indicated in the corresponding figure legends, where n represents the number of biologically independent samples. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were chosen based on our previous studies [30] and common practices in the field to ensure adequate power to detect biologically relevant effects. No data points or animals were excluded from the analysis unless failure of electrophysiological recording stability or incorrect viral expression. GraphPad Prism 8 software was used for generating graphical representations and statistical comparisons. Before conducting group comparisons, homogeneity of variance was evaluated for all datasets. The results showed no statistically significant evidence of unequal variances across any of the compared groups thereby satisfying the assumption of homogeneity of variance. The data were then compared using unpaired, two-tailed t-test between two groups, and one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for groups more than two. The number of action potentials evoked by inward currents in Ctrl and SI were compared using Two-way analysis of variance. Cumulative probability distributions were compared using the two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001 were statistically significant.

Results

Social isolation selectively impaired social novelty memory in adult male mice

Previous studies indicate that SI during early life or adolescence produces sex-dependent behavioral outcomes. To determine whether similar sex-specific effects occur in adulthood, age-matched adult male and female mice were subjected to four weeks of SI (Fig. 1A), followed by assessment using the TCST. During the habituation phase, all groups showed no inherent side preference, regardless of sex or housing condition (Fig. 1C, I). In the sociability phase, both socially isolated male (Fig. 1D, E) and female mice (Fig. 1J, K) displayed a clear preference for interacting with a conspecific over an object, indicating intact basic sociability. However, during the social novelty memory testing phase, SI male mice failed to show a preference for a novel conspecific over a familiar one (Fig. 1F, G). In contrast, SI female mice retained robust novelty preference comparable to controls (Fig. 1L, M). These results demonstrate that adult SI selectively impairs social novelty memory in male mice, prompting us to focus subsequent mechanistic studies on males.

To further assess whether adult SI affects other aspects of social behavior or non-social cognition in male mice, a series of behavioral tests was conducted following the same SI protocol (Supplementary Fig. 1A). In the SIT, SI and control mice exhibited comparable exploration and similar increases in interaction-zone time in response to a social target (Supplementary Fig. 1B-D). In the NORT, both groups showed intact object discrimination with no difference in recognition index (Supplementary Fig. 1E-H). Similarly, SI and control mice displayed comparable performance in the Y-maze task, including normal preference for the novel arm (Supplementary Fig. 1I-J). Anxiety- and depression-like behaviors were also unaffected, as indicated by comparable performance in the OFT (Supplementary Fig. 2A-C), EPM (Supplementary Fig. 2D-F), TST (Supplementary Fig. 2G-H), and FST (Supplementary Fig. 2I-J). Together, these findings indicate that adult SI induces a highly specific deficit in social novelty memory in male mice, without altering general sociability, non-social learning and memory, or affective behaviors.

Social isolation induced a divergent E/I recruitment pattern in the mPFC during social novelty memory

To determine whether adult SI alters neural activation during social behavior, we quantified c-Fos expression across multiple social-related brain regions following the TCST (Fig. 2A). SI male mice exhibited a pronounced increase in c-Fos⁺ cell density, with the most robust activation observed in the mPFC and the ACC (Fig. 2B-C). The activation within the mPFC was prominent in both its prelimbic (PrL) and infralimbic (IL) subregions. Although the PrL and IL have partially distinct connectivity and functions, our c-Fos analysis revealed no subregion-specific differences between them. Therefore, in subsequent experiments, we did not distinguish between these two subregions for viral manipulation, electrophysiological recording, and data analysis. Moderate increases were also detected in several subcortical regions involved in social and emotional processing, whereas no significant changes were observed in regions such as the hippocampus, thalamus, midbrain, or raphe nuclei. Representative images for non-mPFC regions were shown in Supplementary Fig. 3B-C. Meanwhile, no significant differences in c-Fos expression were observed in female mice in either the PrL or IL subregions of the mPFC following the TCST (Supplementary Fig. 3D).

Based on previous findings that the ACC is linked to aggression and basic sociability [33–35] rather than complex social cognition, and given that our SI mice showed a selective deficit in social novelty memory which was closely associated with the mPFC [13], we focused our subsequent investigation on the mPFC. To identify neuronal populations selectively recruited by social novelty memory, we employed a behavioral tagging paradigm distinguishing general social interaction from novelty-related activation (Fig. 2D). In group-housed controls, both c-Fos⁺/CaMKIIα⁺ glutamatergic neurons and c-Fos⁺/GABA⁺ inhibitory neurons in the mPFC were robustly recruited during the novel social exposure (F – N condition). In contrast, SI mice exhibited a markedly altered recruitment pattern, characterized by exaggerated activation of glutamatergic neurons (Fig. 2F) and significantly reduced activation of GABAergic neurons (Fig. 2H), indicating a stage-specific disruption of local E/I balance during social novelty memory.

To further characterize the temporal dynamics of this imbalance, we performed fiber photometry recordings of calcium activity in mPFC glutamatergic (CaMKII-GCaMP6m) and GABAergic (mDlx-GCaMP6m) neurons throughout the TCST (Fig. 3A–B). During the habituation and sociability phases, calcium activity in both neuronal populations was comparable between control and SI mice (Supplementary Figs. 4–5). However, during the social novelty memory testing phase, SI mice displayed significantly enhanced calcium responses in glutamatergic neurons when interacting with the novel conspecific (Fig. 3C, D), while GABAergic neurons failed to show the novelty-associated activation observed in controls (Fig. 3E, F). To further assess the relationship between neural activity and behavioral performance at the individual level, we performed correlation analyses between calcium signals and the social novelty recognition index (Fig. 3G–H). No significant correlation was observed between CaMKII⁺ neuronal activity (ΔPeak or ΔAUC) and behavioral performance. Meanwhile, GABAergic neuronal activity showed a significant positive correlation with the recognition index, indicating that variability in social novelty memory is more closely associated with inhibitory neuronal activity. Together, these results demonstrate that adult social isolation selectively disrupts coordinated excitatory and inhibitory recruitment in the mPFC during social novelty memory, resulting in both glutamatergic hyperactivation and insufficient inhibitory engagement.

Bidirectional manipulation of mPFC glutamatergic neurons causally regulated social novelty memory

From a circuit perspective, the concurrent hyperactivation of glutamatergic neurons and attenuated recruitment of GABAergic neurons during social novelty memory suggests that excessive excitatory output may represent a key functional manifestation in the mPFC. Given that glutamatergic pyramidal neurons constitute the principal output, we tested whether artificial activation of mPFC glutamatergic (mPFCGlu) neurons is sufficient to induce social novelty memory deficits. Both chemogenetic (Supplementary Fig. 6A–E) and optogenetic (Supplementary Fig. 7A-E) activation of CaMKII-expressing neurons in the mPFC of group-housed male mice did not alter basal social motivation, as reflected by unchanged social interaction ratios and interaction-zone time in the SIT (Supplementary Fig. 6F–H; Supplementary Fig. 7F–H). Likewise, exploration patterns and sociability indices during the habituation and sociability phases of the TCST remained intact (Supplementary Fig. 6J–L; Supplementary Fig. 7J–L). In contrast, during the social novelty memory testing phase, mice with activated mPFCGlu neurons failed to show a preference for the novel conspecific, spending comparable time investigating familiar and novel mice (Supplementary Fig. 6M–N; Supplementary Fig. 7M–N). These results indicated that selective activation of mPFCGlu neurons was sufficient to recapitulate the social novelty memory impairment induced by adult SI.

We next examined whether suppressing excessive mPFCGlu activity could reverse social novelty memory deficits induced by adult SI. Chemogenetic (Supplementary Fig. 8A–E) and optogenetic inhibition (Fig. 4A–E) of CaMKII-expressing neurons in the mPFC were validated by viral expression, electrophysiological recordings, and c-Fos analyses. In the SIT, inhibition of mPFCGlu neurons did not affect locomotor activity, social interaction ratios, or time spent engaging with a social target across groups (Fig. 4F–H), indicating preserved basal social motivation. During the TCST, all groups displayed comparable locomotor trajectories and exploration times during the habituation and sociability phases, with intact sociability recognition indices (Fig. 4I–L). Notably, during the social novelty memory phase, SI-mCherry mice failed to discriminate between familiar and novel conspecifics, whereas optogenetic inhibition of mPFCGlu neurons restored a clear preference for the novel mouse (Fig. 4M). Consistently, the social novelty memory recognition index was significantly increased in SI-NpHR mice, reaching levels comparable to group-housed controls (Fig. 4N). Chemogenetic inhibition produced similar rescue effects (Supplementary Fig. 8). Together, these findings demonstrated that reducing excessive mPFCGlu output was sufficient to restore social novelty memory discrimination after adult SI.

Enhanced intrinsic excitability and synaptic E/I imbalance contributed to the hyperexcitable output of mPFCGlu neurons after social isolation

To investigate the cellular mechanisms underlying the hyperexcitable output of mPFCGlu neurons after social isolation, we assessed whether their exaggerated activation during social novelty memory reflects changes in intrinsic excitability and synaptic E/I balance using in vivo (Fig. 5A) and in vitro (Fig. 5H) electrophysiological recordings. In vivo single-unit recordings from the mPFC revealed that excitatory glutamatergic neurons in SI male mice exhibited markedly elevated firing rates compared with controls (Fig. 5B–F), indicating heightened neuronal activity under freely moving conditions. Notably, correlation analysis revealed a significant negative relationship between the average firing frequency of these neurons and the recognition index (Fig. 5G), suggesting that the aberrantly high firing activity in the mPFC is closely linked to impaired social memory performance. Then, whole-cell patch-clamp recordings in acute mPFC slices were conducted, to further dissect the intrinsic membrane properties underlying this increased excitability. Our results showed that pyramidal neurons from SI male mice fired significantly more action potentials in response to identical depolarizing current injections than those from control mice (Fig. 5I and J). In addition, the rheobase current required to evoke the first action potential was significantly lower in SI male mice (Fig. 5K and L), demonstrating that their excitatory glutamatergic neurons were more readily driven to fire.

Given the enhanced intrinsic excitability of mPFCGlu neurons after SI, we next examined whether synaptic excitatory and inhibitory inputs onto these neurons were also altered using whole-cell patch-clamp recordings (Fig. 6A). As shown in Fig. 6B, SI neurons displayed larger inward synaptic events compared with controls. Quantitative analysis demonstrated a rightward shift in the cumulative distribution of mEPSC amplitudes, and SI mice exhibited markedly increased averaged amplitudes relative to controls (Fig. 6C). The averaged mEPSC frequency was also significantly increased in SI neurons with a leftward shift in the cumulative distribution of inter-event intervals (Fig. 6D), indicating a higher frequency of excitatory events. To determine whether inhibitory drive was similarly affected, we next recorded miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs). As shown in Fig. 6E, representative mIPSC traces from SI neurons displayed smaller synaptic events compared with controls. Consistent with this observation, quantitative analysis revealed a significant reduction in the averaged mIPSC amplitude in SI mice with a leftward shift in the cumulative distribution (Fig. 6F), whereas the cumulative distribution of inter-event intervals and the averaged mIPSC frequency were not significantly altered (Fig. 6G). Together, these results demonstrated that adult SI induced a hyperexcitable state in mPFCGlu neurons through the combined enhancement of intrinsic membrane excitability and a shift in synaptic E/I balance. Specifically, increased excitatory synaptic drive together with weakened inhibitory synaptic strength contributed to the excessive output of mPFCGlu neurons.

Discussion

Most preclinical models of SI target early-life or adolescent developmental windows, based on the premise that perturbations during these critical periods induce lasting changes into adulthood [36, 37]. In contrast, we focused on isolation during adulthood, a stage characterized by stabilized yet plastic neural circuitry [38]. This allowed us to address a pivotal question of whether SI in adulthood can also precipitate maladaptive outcomes through specific circuit alterations. By directly modeling four-week social deprivation in adult mice, we found that adult SI in male mice selectively impairs social novelty memory, while sparing baseline sociability, general exploratory behavior, non-social memory, and anxiety or depression. The effect was sex-specific, as the female mice retained a normal preference for social novelty. These observations indicated that adult SI triggered a selective deficit in social novelty memory, rather than a broad impairment of social behavior or cognition.

Our discovery of a male-specific deficit in social novelty memory following adult SI highlights a sex-dependent vulnerability to social stress. This finding aligns with clinical evidence indicating that loneliness is associated with a significantly higher risk of all-cause mortality in men than in women [39, 40], and that socially isolated men are more susceptible to trauma-related psychopathology [41]. Consistently, in neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders characterized by social dysfunction, such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD), males show a markedly higher prevalence and often greater symptom severity than females [42, 43]. Preclinical studies further support the idea that social behaviors are regulated in a sex-dependent manner. In fear-conditioning paradigms, state-dependent stress-related memory formation selectively disrupts sociability in male but not female mice [44]. Vasopressin signaling in the lateral septum modulates social play by enhancing extracellular glutamate release in males while suppressing it in females [45]. Conversely, oxytocin signaling regulates social reward, pair-bonding, and aggression in a sex-specific manner, often exerting effects distinct from vasopressin [46, 47]. Correspondingly, our preliminary data revealed that adult female mice did not exhibit significant changes in c-Fos expression in either the PrL or IL subregions of the mPFC following the TCST, suggesting that the mPFC circuit in adult females may be more resistant to SI. To elucidate the mechanisms behind this sex difference, future studies are needed including whether E/I balance is preserved in the female mPFC under SI, how c-Fos activation patterns differ across neuronal subtypes, and to what extent hormonal factors, such as circulating corticosterone or estrogen, contribute to this resilience.

At the neural level, we identified a precise circuit dysfunction underlying the behavioral deficit. Adult SI did not cause global hyperactivity but instead induced a maladaptive and highly specific shift in mPFC E/I balance that occurred selectively during social novelty memory processing. This shift was evidenced by a divergent recruitment pattern where SI mice showed exaggerated activation of glutamatergic neurons together with a failure to recruit GABAergic interneurons specifically when exploring a novel social stimulus. Artificially recreating a similar excitatory-dominant state in group-housed mice via activation of mPFCGlu neurons recapitulated the social novelty memory deficit, while suppressing this hyperactivity in SI mice restored the impaired social novelty memory. At the cellular level, this pathological hyperexcitability stemmed from a dual alteration encompassing both an enhancement in the intrinsic excitability of mPFC pyramidal neurons, and a synaptic E/I imbalance characterized by increased excitatory and decreased inhibitory drive.

Mechanistically, it remains unclear whether the observed increase in intrinsic excitability of mPFCGlu neurons and the shift in their synaptic E/I balance happen one after another or at the same time during adult SI. One possibility is that enhanced intrinsic excitability arises autonomously in neurons because of chronic SI, which then makes these neurons overreact to inputs. Alternatively, these two changes might be independent but jointly disrupt the circuit function. Future studies with corresponding manipulations, such as blocking intrinsic excitability changes with ion channel modulators or normalizing synaptic scaling, will be necessary to confirm the temporal hierarchy of these maladaptive changes in neuroplasticity. Nevertheless, our causal manipulations indicated that the overall hyperexcitable state of mPFCGlu neurons is a key driver of the social novelty memory deficit.

Both excitatory and inhibitory neuronal populations in mPFC play essential and coordinated roles in social novelty memory processing and social memory. A subpopulation of socially activated pyramidal neurons in the mPFC has been identified to be essential for social recognition memory without affecting social motivation, with intrinsic firing properties resembling dopamine D1 receptor-expressing neurons [48]. Meanwhile, a critical contribution of parvalbumin-positive inhibitory interneurons has also been identified, as disruption of their event-locked activation during social interactions impairs proper social memory encoding [49]. In this context, our results demonstrate that SI in adulthood induced a net E/I imbalance within the mPFC. The enhanced intrinsic excitability in mPFCGlu neurons were accompanied by a shift of synaptic inputs favoring excitation. Although direct inhibitory synaptic measurements were not performed, our data including reduced c-Fos expression, decreased calcium activity during social novelty exploration, and diminished mIPSC amplitude recorded in pyramidal neurons suggest a downregulation of inhibitory control. Together with the enhanced excitatory drive, the reduced inhibitory control may also contribute to the exaggerated activation of mPFCGlu neurons specifically during exposure to a novel social stimulus.

Interestingly, most preclinical models of SI during juvenile or adolescent periods often report opposite changes in mPFC dynamics, with decreased excitability of excitatory neurons [50] and increased inhibitory drive [51]. Regarding mPFC circuitry, previous work using juvenile social isolation after weaning demonstrated that projections from mPFC to the nucleus accumbens shell, which are required for social recognition memory, exhibited reduced activity [52]. Consistently, another well-established juvenile isolation model has been shown to lead to reduced excitability of mPFC neurons projecting to the paraventricular thalamus, accompanied by an increase in inhibitory drive [23]. The opposite changes observed after juvenile versus adult SI suggest that the timing of social deprivation is a critical determinant of neural outcomes, as it not only defines the specific neural circuits affected but may also fundamentally reverse the direction of E/I imbalance, from a hypoactive to a hyperactive state.

This maladaptive E/I imbalance and circuit hyperreactivity of mPFCGlu neurons during adult SI may represent an early and potentially reversible stage of isolation-induced pathology. However, several critical questions remain. First, we did not directly assess the persistence of these phenotypes following re-socialization. Previous studies have reported that regrouping after post-weaning social isolation can partially restore social behaviors and normalize the maladaptive changes in mPFC [53, 54]. These observations raise the possibility that the social novelty memory deficits and mPFC E/I imbalance observed in our adult SI model may also be reversible. Future studies will be required to systematically examine whether re-socialization can restore mPFC circuit function and behavioral performance in our model, and to determine the temporal dynamics and underlying mechanisms of such recovery. Second, the upstream mechanisms driving increased excitability and E/I imbalance are still unclear and may involve altered neuromodulatory inputs, changes in intrinsic ion channel expression, or contributions from non-neuronal cells. Notably, recent studies report region- and sex-specific microglial alterations following adult social isolation [55], implicating glial regulation of excitatory circuits.

In addition, we did not distinguish between the PrL and IL subregions of the mPFC in the current study as our c-Fos results showed no subregion-specific differences. Nevertheless, given that PrL and IL have partially distinct connectivity and functions, future studies with more refined circuit tracing and manipulation are warranted to determine whether they play differential roles in the social novelty memory deficits caused by SI. While our findings suggest a reversible, functional form of maladaptive plasticity, prolonged or more severe isolation may ultimately induce structural abnormalities, including dendritic spine remodeling or myelination deficits, as observed in other models [56, 57]. Clarifying whether mPFC hyperexcitability precedes such structural deterioration will be essential for defining therapeutic windows.

In summary, our data reveal a form of maladaptive prefrontal plasticity induced by adult social isolation, characterized by increased excitability and an E/I imbalance in mPFCGlu neurons, which we show causally underlies a selective deficit in social novelty memory. These findings highlight the importance of the adult social environment for the maintenance of social cognitive function and identify mPFC E/I homeostasis as a promising target for therapeutic intervention.
