Trehalose priming enhances chromium resilience in barley plants by modulating antioxidant defense, hormonal balance, endogenous trehalose, and nutrient homeostasis
Authors: Adeeba Zafar, Muhammad Umer Chattha, Imran Khan, Muhammad Bilal Chattha, Jameel M Al-Khayri, Othman Al-Dossary, Bader Alsubaie, Mohammed I Aldaej
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCr stress represents a major threat to ecosystem sustainability, crop productivity, and human health. Tre is a non-reducing sugar with significant potential to alleviate abiotic stress toxicity. However, its role in mitigating Cr toxicity via seed priming remains poorly understood.Methodology Therefore, this study evaluated the effects of Tre priming on alleviating Cr toxicity in two barley cultivars, Sultan-17 and Haider-93, exposed to three Cr levels (0, 20, and 40 mg kg⁻¹ soil) and three seed priming treatments (hydropriming, 10 mM Tre, and 15 mM Tre).ResultsCr stress at 40 mg kg⁻¹ reduced growth and yield in both cultivars, an effect associated with increased oxidative damage and Cr accumulation in plant organs, along with decline in photosynthetic pigments, water content, nutrient uptake, and synthesis of IAA and GA. Tre seed priming notably alleviated these detrimental effects. Specifically, under 40 mg kg⁻¹ Cr stress, 15 mM Tre priming increased biological yield by 58% and 90%, and grain yield by 23.9% and 44.7% in Sultan-17 and Haider-93, respectively, relative to the non-primed controls. These improvements were associated with reduced levels of H₂O₂ (27.6% and 41.7%), MDA (28.5% and 46.2%), and ABA (21.2% and 22.9%); enhanced nutrient accumulation; increased CAT (9.1% and 9.2%) and POD (20.1% and 25.1%) activities; and higher ascorbic acid (14.5% and 10.7%) and APX (15.2% and 26.6%) levels. Furthermore, compared with non-primed controls, Tre priming improved osmotic balance, as reflected by increases in TSP (1.8% and 5.4%), FAA (27% and 34.8%), secondary metabolites, and endogenous Tre synthesis (31.5% and 31%) under Cr stress. These physiological adjustments contributed to enhanced biological and grain yields in both cultivars.ConclusionCollectively, these findings position Tre priming as an effective and economical strategy for improving Cr tolerance and crop production in Cr-contaminated soils, providing a base for developing eco-friendly approaches to ensure safer food production in polluted environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09469-8.Keywords: Barley, Hormonal balance, Metabolites, Nutrient homeostasis, Trehalose, Yield

Introduction

Heavy metals are serious environmental pollutants that pose significant threats to crop productivity and human health [1]. They accumulate continuously in soils and the environment through mining operations, sewage sludge application, and extensive use of fertilizers and pesticides [2]. Among these metals, chromium (Cr) is highly toxic metal, adversely affecting growth, photosynthetic efficiency, and yield [3, 4]. Cr induces cellular damage and oxidative stress by increasing malondialdehyde (MDA) production [5]. It also elevates reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, which damage DNA and proteins, disrupt physiological processes, and impair plant growth [6]. Furthermore, Cr interferes with ion transport and enzyme activity, thereby inhibiting plant growth, although its effects vary among species [7, 8]. In response, plants counteract Cr toxicity by enhancing osmolyte synthesis and antioxidant activities [9]. The concentration of Cr in agricultural lands has substantially increased owing to industrialization, driven largely by anthropogenic activities [10, 11]. This rising Cr concentration in soil has facilitated its entry into food chains and, ultimately, into humans [12]. The accumulation of Cr in food crops poses a serious challenge to food security and human health [13]. Moreover, Cr entry into food chains has been reported in several countries, including Pakistan [14], China [15], India [16], and Nigeria [17]. Therefore, developing effective strategies to prevent the entry of Cr into food crops is imperative for safeguarding human health.

Globally, various practices including the use of organic amendments, phytohormones, and osmolytes are employed to mitigate heavy metal toxicity in plants. Trehalose (Tre) is a non-reducing disaccharide composed of two glucose molecules that serves as an energy source [18]. Its distinctive physicochemical properties, including glycosidic bonding and hydrophobicity, help stabilize dehydrated proteins and cellular membranes, thereby aiding plants in coping with oxidative stress [19]. Furthermore, Tre transmits signals and activates genes involved in abiotic stress responses to scavenge ROS [20]. Nevertheless, endogenous Tre levels in most plants are insufficient to counteract stress conditions [21]. In this context, exogenous Tre application has been reported as an important alternative for increasing stress tolerance [22]. Supplemental Tre improves antioxidant activities, chelates toxic metals, and reduces their mobility, thereby lowering metal-induced ROS production [23]. Moreover, it increases chlorophyll content, maintains membrane stability, enhances osmolyte synthesis, and improves nutrient availability, all of which contribute to better growth under stress conditions [24].

Seed priming is a controlled hydration process followed by re-drying that activates metabolic processes within the seed without promoting radicle protrusion, offering several benefits, including uniform and rapid germination [25]. It is an economical method for improving germination, seedling development, vigor, and crop yield [26–28]. Priming with Tre allows seeds to maintain pre-active osmotic adjustment, antioxidant systems, and solute accumulation before germination, thereby providing a protective strategy. This method requires short, controlled exposure to Tre, followed by re-drying. It can reduce the required Tre concentration compared with exogenous application, thereby lowering metabolic costs and avoiding potential negative feedback effects on sugar metabolism. Furthermore, Tre priming synchronizes germination, providing an advantage for development under unfavorable conditions.

Although the role of Tre application in mitigating abiotic stress is well documented, its specific role under Cr stress remains unexplored. Notably, the effects of Tre priming on hormonal balance, antioxidant activities, osmolyte synthesis, and endogenous Tre accumulation under Cr stress have not yet been reported. Therefore, we hypothesized that Tre seed priming can create favorable conditions by modulating plant physiological and biochemical processes, primarily by decreasing Cr uptake, thereby counteracting Cr toxicity. This strategy is fundamental for enhancing crop productivity and reducing the toxic effects of Cr in plants. The specific aims of this study were: (i) to determine the effects of Tre on photosynthetic traits, leaf water content, and antioxidant defense; (ii) to quantify the effects of Tre on hormonal crosstalk, secondary metabolites, endogenous Tre, and osmolyte synthesis; and (iii) to evaluate the effects of Tre on Cr and nutrient accumulation and partitioning to plant organs. These findings provide a foundation for developing economical and eco-friendly measures to promote safer crop production in contaminated soils.

Materials and methods

Experimental details

The experiment was conducted at the University of Agriculture, Faisalabad, from November 2024 to April 2025. Soil was collected from an experimental field, sieved, and cleared of debris before being used to fill plastic pots (46 cm long, 25 cm in diameter). The soil and silt were mixed at a 3:1 ratio, and each pot was filled with 9 kg of dry soil. The soil was sandy loam with an alkaline pH of 7.35, organic matter content of 0.88%, nitrogen content of 0.041%, phosphorus content of 12.68%, and potassium content of 164 mg kg⁻¹. This study included two barley cultivars, Sultan-17 and Haider-93, and examined three Cr stress levels (control with no stress, 20 mg kg⁻¹ soil, and 40 mg kg⁻¹ soil) combined with three seed priming treatments (control with hydropriming, 10 mM Tre priming, and 15 mM Tre priming). Cr stress levels were established by mixing potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇) with the soil. The Cr salt was thoroughly mixed with the soil, and the soil was maintained at 70% field capacity throughout a two-month aging period. During this period, the soil was kept in the dark and stirred thoroughly once per week to ensure uniform Cr distribution and adequate aeration. Thereafter, seeds were primed with Tre at a seed-to-solution volume ratio of 1:5 (w/v) in a controlled incubator (25 ± 1 °C) under complete darkness for 12 h. The seeds were then rinsed three times with deionized water to remove residual Tre and subsequently redried with moderate air circulation for 24 h until the original seed weight (8–10% moisture content) was restored. After priming, 12 seeds were sown at a depth of 2 cm in each pot, and following germination, five plants were maintained per pot. The pots were irrigated according to crop demand and monitored regularly throughout the experimental period. The barley seeds used in this study were obtained from the Ayub Agricultural Research Institute, Faisalabad, Pakistan.

Measurement of morphological traits

Plants were harvested at maturity from each experimental unit to measure morphological characteristics. Roots were thoroughly rinsed, and the fresh weights of roots and shoots were recorded using an electronic balance. Plant height was measured with a meter rod, and leaf area was determined using a leaf area meter. The number of leaves per plant was counted manually. The harvested samples were subsequently dried in an oven at 105 °C to determine dry weight. Each trait was recorded for every replicate and then averaged.

Determination of physiological traits

Fresh leaves were weighed to record fresh weight (FW), soaked in distilled water for 24 h, and then reweighed to obtain turgid weight (TW). Dry weight (DW) was subsequently determined after drying the samples in an oven at 70 °C. Relative water content (RWC) was calculated following Chattha et al. [29] using the formula: RWC (%) = [(FW − DW) / (TW − DW)] × 100. Electrolyte leakage (EL) was measured according to the method of Blum and Ebercon [30]. Fresh leaf samples (0.5 g) were placed in distilled water at room temperature for 30 min, and the initial electrical conductivity (EC₁) was measured using an EC meter. The samples were then incubated at 90 °C for 30 min, after which the final electrical conductivity (EC₂) was recorded. EL was calculated as: EL (%) = (EC₁ / EC₂) × 100. Chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoid contents were determined following the method of Lichtenthaler [31]. Fresh leaves (0.5 g) were cut into small pieces and immersed in 5 mL of 80% methanol for 24 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was recorded at 663 nm, 645 nm, and 480 nm to estimate pigment concentrations.

Determination of oxidative markers

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) levels were measured following the method described by Velikova [32]. Leaf tissues (0.5 g) were homogenized in 5 mL of 5% TCA and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, 1 mL of the extract was mixed with 1 mL of KI and 100 µL of phosphate buffer, and absorbance was recorded at 390 nm to determine H₂O₂ levels. MDA content was determined according to the method of Buege and Aust [33]. Fresh leaves (0.5 g) were ground in 5% TCA and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Then, 1 mL of the extract was mixed with 1 mL of 0.5% TCA and 1 mL of 20% TBA, and the mixture was incubated at 95 °C for 50 min, followed by rapid cooling. Absorbance was measured at 532 nm, and non-specific turbidity was corrected by subtracting absorbance at 600 nm. MDA content was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 155 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹ and expressed as nmol g⁻¹ FW.

Determination of osmolyte, hormone and endogenous trehalose contents

Soluble protein content was measured according to the Bradford method [34]. Barley leaves were extracted in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer and centrifuged for 15 min. From the resulting supernatant, 100 µL of extract was mixed with 3 mL of Bradford solution, and absorbance was recorded at 590 nm. For free amino acid (FAA) determination, 1 mL of extract was combined with 1 mL of 2% ninhydrin and 10% pyridine. The mixture was incubated at 90 °C for 30 min, cooled, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm. To determine IAA and ABA concentrations, fresh barley leaves (0.5 g) were homogenized in 80% methanol containing 40 mg of butylated hydroxytoluene. The samples were incubated at 4 °C for 48 h and then centrifuged at 1,900 × g for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was passed through C18 Sep-Pak cartridges using 10 mL of ethanol and ether. The collected extract was then mixed with 1% gelatin (pH 7.5) and 0.1% Tween-20 in phosphate-buffered saline, and IAA and ABA concentrations were determined following the protocol of Weiler [35]. GA content was measured by extracting 0.1 g samples in 3 mL of 96% ethanol. Absorbance was recorded at 254 nm, and GA content was calculated using a linear regression equation as described by Berríos [36]. Trehalose concentration was determined according to the protocols of Carillo [37] and Jagdale and Grewal [38]. Fresh leaves (1 g) were homogenized with 5 mL of 80% ethanol and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min. The extract was then placed in tubes and evaporated at 80 °C. The dried samples were re-dissolved in 5 mL of water, and Tre concentration was measured following the protocol described by Kaya [39]. Trehalose content was expressed as nmol g⁻¹ FW.

Determination of antioxidant enzymes and secondary metabolites

For enzyme extraction, fresh leaf tissue (0.5 g) was homogenized in 10 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (PPB), and the homogenate was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 30 min. CAT activity was measured following the method of Chance and Maehly [40]. Briefly, 0.1 mL of extract was mixed with 2.5 mL of buffer and 0.1 mL of H₂O₂, and absorbance was recorded at 240 nm. POD activity was determined by mixing 100 µL of extract with 100 µL of 180 mM H₂O₂ and 100 µL of 180 mM guaiacol; this mixture was then combined with 700 µL of 50 mM phosphate buffer, and absorbance was measured at 470 nm. For APX activity, 100 µL of enzyme extract was mixed with 100 µL of ascorbic acid, 6.1 mM H₂O₂, and 700 µL of buffer, and absorbance was read at 290 nm [41]. Ascorbic acid content was measured following the method of Mukherjee and Choudhuri [42]. Leaf samples (0.5 g) were homogenized in 5 mL of 10% TCA and centrifuged for 15 min. The supernatant (2 mL) was collected, mixed with 0.5 mL of DTC reagent, incubated at 37 °C for 3 h, and then cooled on ice. Thereafter, 2.5 mL of chilled H₂SO₄ was added dropwise, the mixture was shaken for 30 min, and absorbance was recorded at 520 nm. Total phenolic content was determined following standard methods described by Principato [43]. Samples were prepared using the Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, and a standard curve was constructed using chlorogenic acid at 740 nm to calculate phenolic concentrations. Flavonoid content was measured by mixing 24 µL of leaf extract with 0.1 M sodium nitrite (pH 5.5) and incubating for 5 min. The mixture was then combined with 28 µL of aluminum chloride, and after 5 min, 120 µL of 1 mM NaOH was added. Absorbance was recorded at 510 nm [44, 45]. For the determination of Put, Spd, and Spm, fresh leaves (2 g) were sliced and homogenized in 20 mL of 5% HClO₄, and the mixture was then stored at − 70 °C. Concentrations of these polyamines were measured following the method of Flores and Galston [46], with minor modifications as described by Iqbal and Ashraf [47].

Measurement of Cr, yield attributes and nutrient concentrations in plant parts

Yield-related parameters, including tiller number per plant, productive spikes, and grains per spike, were recorded, counted, and averaged. Plants were weighed, and the spikes from each plant were subsequently threshed to obtain grain yield per pot. The 1000-grain weight was determined by weighing a subsample of 1000 seeds. Samples from different plant parts (roots, leaves, and grains) were dried and ground into a fine powder. The dried samples were then digested using a mixture of HNO₃ and HClO₄ in a 2:1 ratio [48]. Cr levels were determined via atomic absorption spectrophotometry (AAS, PerkinElmer Analyst™ 800). Concentrations of K, Ca, and Mg were measured using a flame photometer, while N and P concentrations were determined using the Kjeldahl method and spectrophotometric techniques, respectively.

Statistical analysis

The experiment employed a completely randomized design (CRD) with a three-factor factorial arrangement. All recorded parameters were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using Statistix 8.1 software. Treatment means were compared using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test at a significance level of p < 0.05 [49]. Principal component analysis (PCA) and Pearson’s correlation analysis were performed to evaluate the interrelationships among the measured traits. For PCA, principal components were extracted from scaled and centered quantitative variables to ensure that each trait contributed equally to the overall variance.

Results

Growth and yield traits

Cr stress significantly reduced growth attributes in both barley cultivars (Table 1). Compared with the unstressed controls, leaf area decreased by 17.65% in Sultan-17 and by 41.52% in Haider-93 under 40 mg kg⁻¹ Cr stress. Root fresh and dry biomass in Haider-93 declined by 78.55% and 88.23%, respectively, relative to control values. The number of leaves per plant decreased by 21–24% in both cultivars under high Cr stress (Table 1). In contrast, seed priming with 15 mM Tre enhanced root and shoot growth under Cr stress compared with non-primed plants (Table 1). Cr stress also significantly reduced yield traits in both cultivars (Table 2). Under 40 mg kg⁻¹ Cr, Sultan-17 showed a 29% reduction in grains per spike, a 40% reduction in productive tillers, and a 50.5% reduction in biological yield. Haider-93 exhibited greater reductions of 50% in grains per spike, 60% in productive tillers, and 78.5% in biological yield under the same stress level (Table 2). Overall grain yield decreased by 29% in Sultan-17 and by 44% in Haider-93 under 40 mg kg⁻¹ Cr stress. Tre priming (15 mM) improved yield traits under Cr stress. In Sultan-17, Tre priming increased tiller count by 21%, productive tillers by 33%, grains per spike by 19.2%, and biological yield by 58%. In Haider-93, Tre priming increased tiller count by 45.4% and biological yield by 90%. Consequently, grain yield increased by 23.9% in Sultan-17 and by 44.7% in Haider-93 following 15 mM Tre priming under Cr stress (Table 2).

Photosynthetic pigments and leaf water status

Chromium stress reduced chlorophyll content in both cultivars (Fig. 1). In Sultan-17, chlorophyll a content decreased by 30.66% under low Cr stress (20 mg kg⁻¹) and by 38.36% under high Cr stress (40 mg kg⁻¹) relative to unstressed controls. In Haider-93, the corresponding reductions were 35.5% and 42.96% under low- and high-Cr stress, respectively. A similar decreasing trend was observed for chlorophyll b levels in both cultivars. Carotenoid content decreased by 36.8% under low Cr stress and by 48% under high Cr stress compared with control conditions in Haider-93 (Fig. 1). Seed priming with 15 mM Tre increased carotenoid content by 10.6–10.9% in Sultan-17 and by 15.0–18.8% in Haider-93 under Cr stress (Fig. 1). Chromium stress also decreased leaf RWC in both cultivars (Fig. 2). Under low-Cr stress, leaf RWC was reduced by 16.4% in Sultan-17 and 22.7% in Haider-93. Under high Cr stress, leaf RWC similarly decreased. Seed priming with 15 mM Tre increased leaf RWC in Haider-93 plants by 19% under low Cr stress and by 39.5% under high Cr stress compared with non-primed plants under the same stress levels (Fig. 2).

Oxidative marker, osmolyte, hormone and antioxidant activities

Under control conditions, both cultivars exhibited nearly identical EL values, recording 30.0% for Haider-93 and 29.96% for Sultan-17. Exposure to chromium induced a significant, concentration-dependent increase in EL across both genotypes. In Haider-93, leakage increased from 43.33% at the lower Cr stress level to 55.30% at the higher Cr stress level. In Sultan-17, Cr exposure increased EL values from 49.5% at lower Cr stress to 61.9% at higher Cr stress, compared with the control. Trehalose priming at 15 mM significantly decreased EL by 31.1% and 32.6% in Sultan-17 and Haider-93, respectively, under high Cr toxicity (Fig. 2). Additionally, the H₂O₂ level increased by 86% in Sultan-17 and by 190% in Haider-93 under high Cr stress (Fig. 2). Moreover, Haider-93 exhibited MDA levels twofold higher than those of Sultan-17 under stress. Under high Cr stress, 15 mM Tre priming significantly lowered H₂O₂ levels by 41.7% in Haider-93 and 27.6% in Sultan-17 and reduced MDA levels by 28.5% in Sultan-17 and 46.2% in Haider-93 (Fig. 2).

Chromium toxicity increased TSP and FAA concentrations in both cultivars. In Sultan-17, TSP increased by 32.4% and 54% under low and high Cr stress, respectively, whereas in Haider-93, it increased by 35.8% and 50% under low and high Cr stress, respectively. FAA concentration was increased by 71% in Sultan-17 and 53.5% in Haider-93 under high stress. Furthermore, Tre seed priming significantly increased TSP and FAA synthesis in both cultivars (Fig. 3).

Secondary metabolites

Chromium stress increased secondary metabolite levels in both cultivars (Table 4). Compared with unstressed controls, Sultan-17 showed increases of 32% in putrescine, 33.2% in spermidine, 10.4% in phenolics, and 5.2% in flavonoids under 40 mg kg⁻¹ Cr stress. In Haider-93, the same stress level increased putrescine by 30.3%, spermidine by 29%, phenolics by 12.6%, and flavonoids by 11.2% (Table 4). Tre priming further enhanced the accumulation of these secondary metabolites under Cr stress. Under 40 mg kg⁻¹ Cr, 15 mM Tre priming increased putrescine by 37.5%, spermidine by 37.9%, phenolics by 29.9%, and flavonoids by 42.5% in Sultan-17 relative to non-primed plants under the same stress level. In Haider-93, the same priming treatment under 40 mg kg⁻¹ Cr increased putrescine by 39%, spermidine by 41.1%, phenolics by 31.2%, and flavonoids by 52.6% (Table 4).

Chromium and nutrient accumulation in plant organs

Chromium uptake in plant tissues increased under Cr stress relative to unstressed controls (Fig. 5). In Sultan-17, root Cr accumulation rose threefold under low Cr stress (20 mg kg⁻¹) and sixfold under high Cr stress (40 mg kg⁻¹). In Haider-93, it increased fivefold and eightfold under low- and high-Cr stress, respectively. Seed priming with 15 mM Tre reduced Cr accumulation under high Cr stress. In Sultan-17, Cr accumulation was 26.8% lower in roots, 30.5% lower in leaves, and 27.40% lower in grains compared with non-primed plants under the same stress level. In Haider-93, the same Tre priming treatment reduced Cr accumulation by 22.2% in roots, 31.0% in leaves, and 26.0% in grains (Fig. 5). Cr stress also decreased nutrient accumulation in barley plants, suggesting strong competition between Cr and nutrient uptake. In Sultan-17, high Cr stress (40 mg kg⁻¹) significantly reduced N (22.1% and 19%), P (26.3% and 22.9%), K (19.9% and 18.7%), Ca (25.5% and 19.7%), and Mg (18.2% and 16.6%) contents in roots and shoots, respectively. In Haider-93, high Cr stress similarly decreased N (31% and 26.6%), P (37% and 29.6%), K (26.6% and 24%), Ca (39.5% and 29.2%), and Mg (25.9% and 22.7%) contents in roots and shoots, respectively (Table S1). Tre priming enhanced nutrient accumulation in both cultivars, with 15 mM Tre priming being the most effective treatment.

Trait interrelationships

Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 6a–d) revealed strong associations among physiological, biochemical, and nutritional parameters, indicating that Cr tolerance in barley is associated with integrated networks rather than a single mechanism. Strong positive correlations were observed among leaf area, biomass, photosynthetic pigments, and RWC (Fig. 6a), pointing to coordinated responses. Under chromium stress, these coordinated parameters were negatively associated with EL; therefore, higher membrane damage was associated with lower growth, photosynthesis, and water-status parameters. Trehalose priming showed positive correlations with physiological parameters and negative correlations with EL, suggesting an association with improved membrane integrity. Antioxidant enzymes (Fig. 6b) positively correlated with osmolytes (proline, trehalose) and secondary metabolites (phenolic compounds, flavonoids, putrescine, spermidine), suggesting the involvement of a multicomponent defense system in the context of Cr toxicity. Oxidative markers positively correlated with antioxidants and negatively correlated with hormones (IAA, GA), indicating that, with trehalose priming, hormonal suppression and antioxidant defense activation were observed as co-occurring correlational patterns. Nutrient and metal analyses (Fig. 6c) showed that essential nutrients (N, P, K, Ca, and Mg) positively correlated with each other and negatively correlated with Cr accumulation. Finally, yield components were tightly inter-correlated (Fig. 6d). Collectively, these associations show that trehalose priming correlates with preserved membrane integrity, elevated antioxidant defense, restored hormonal balance, and improved nutrient homeostasis.

The PCA biplots (Fig. 7a–d) showed that trehalose priming was associated with multiple parameters linked to Cr tolerance. PC1 captured 93.6% of the variance, separating Cr-stressed plants from primed plants. EL was observed on PC1 for non-primed Cr-treated plants, a positioning consistent with higher membrane damage and EL in that group. The three priming treatments were associated with better physiological characteristics, suggesting an association between Tre priming and reduced toxic effects of Cr. The antioxidant biplot (Fig. 7b) showed 87.2% combined variance, with osmolytes and secondary metabolites loading on positive PC1, whereas antioxidant enzymes and H₂O₂ loaded on positive PC2. Furthermore, Tre priming was positioned on positive PC1, aligning with higher osmolyte loadings under Cr stress. Discussion

The present study showed that Cr toxicity suppressed growth and yield in both barley cultivars. Cr is a highly soluble and mobile oxyanion that competes with essential nutrients for root uptake [50, 51], thereby reducing nutrient accumulation. This reduced nutrient availability limits ATP synthesis, RuBisCO activity, and chlorophyll synthesis, thereby decreasing photosynthetic efficiency and plant growth [52]. In addition to nutrient competition, Cr stress inhibits root growth by inducing mitotic arrest in the root apical meristem. Cr triggers ROS production, which may suppress cyclin-dependent kinase activity and halt cell division [53]. This ROS-mediated inhibition of mitosis reduces root elongation, restricting nutrient and water uptake and further impairing growth, consistent with previous reports [54, 55]. Cr stress also caused membrane damage, as reflected by increased MDA content. Oxidation of thylakoid membranes reduces PS-II efficiency, leading to electron leakage and additional ROS production at reaction centers. These ROS damage the photosynthetic apparatus, ultimately contributing to growth and yield losses [56, 57].

Tre application considerably improved growth and yield in both cultivars through multiple interconnected mechanisms. Tre reduced MDA and H₂O₂ levels (Fig. 2) by enhancing the synthesis of antioxidants, anthocyanins, and phenolics, suggesting that it functions as a chemical chaperone that preserves chloroplast pigments and enzyme activities under oxidative stress [58]. It may also increase NADPH production and sustain glutathione (GSH) recycling, thereby lowering ROS levels, preserving membrane integrity and PS-II function, and supporting better photosynthesis (Fig. 1; Table 3). Improved pigment retention (Fig. 1) appears to be a key adaptive strategy for sustaining photosynthesis under Cr toxicity [59]. Tre reduced Cr accumulation in roots and shoots, possibly through the formation of Tre–Cr complexes at the root surface that may reduce Cr availability for symplastic uptake [60]. Tre also enhanced anthocyanin accumulation, which may chelate Cr in vacuoles and potentially sequester it away from the photosynthetic apparatus, further mitigating oxidative damage and promoting growth [61].

Tre priming significantly elevated endogenous Tre, IAA, and GA levels while reducing ABA content. Elevated IAA promotes root growth and development, whereas GA stimulates cell elongation [62] and RuBisCO activity [63], leading to enhanced chlorophyll synthesis and biomass production. ABA, which accumulates under stress, induces stomatal closure and limits CO₂ influx, thereby suppressing photosynthesis [64]. In this study, Tre reduced ABA synthesis and appeared to reverse ABA-mediated stomatal closure, as supported by the observed increase in RWC (Fig. 2). This likely facilitated better leaf gas exchange and water uptake [65].

Chlorophyll synthesis is an Mg-dependent process [66], yet Cr toxicity severely compromises Mg availability. In the present study, Cr stress increased MDA and H₂O₂ contents (Fig. 2), reflecting lipid peroxidation, and inhibited enzymes such as Mg-chelatase (CHLH). Reduced enzyme activity decreases chlorophyll content, leading to growth and biomass losses [67]. Tre priming reversed this decline in chlorophyll synthesis through two interconnected mechanisms: increasing Mg uptake (Table S1) and reducing ROS production to preserve Mg-chelatase activity. Tre treatment also decreased MDA and H₂O₂ levels, indicating that it not only boosts antioxidant activity but also helps restore redox homeostasis by enhancing enzyme activity and reducing ROS production, potentially through Cr complexation. Tre possesses multiple –OH groups that can bind superoxide and hydroxyl radicals, reducing their availability and subsequent oxidative damage [68]. Additionally, Tre may decrease membrane fluidity and limit lipid peroxidation by reducing substrate availability for lipoxygenase-mediated peroxidation. Tre participates in stress signaling and antioxidant regulation [69]; thus, it may directly modulate signaling networks and indirectly improve antioxidant defenses, helping to counteract Cr toxicity.

Chromium-stressed plants exhibited increased proline synthesis, which helps stabilize proteins and membranes, scavenge hydroxyl radicals, and buffer cellular redox potential by maintaining NADP⁺/NADPH ratios via the proline glutamate cycle [70]. However, Cr-stressed barley plants still showed reduced growth, indicating that endogenous proline levels were insufficient for full protection. Tre priming significantly boosted proline synthesis, possibly through the upregulation of enzymes in the proline biosynthetic pathway. Under Cr stress, IAA and GA levels decreased and these hormones play crucial roles in plant growth [71]. Tre priming increased IAA synthesis, likely by activating auxin response factors (ARFs), thereby promoting better growth. Notably, Tre application reduced ABA content, which helped maintain stomatal opening under Cr stress and increased RWC.

Seed priming with Tre elevated endogenous Tre levels, which in turn decreased oxidative damage (Fig. 8). Endogenous Tre activates trehalose-6-phosphate phosphatase (TPP), creating a positive feedback loop that ensures continued Tre synthesis under Cr stress and enhances tolerance [72]. The results showed that Tre priming caused a modest increase (~ 30%) in Tre synthesis. These findings suggest that Tre acts as a signaling molecule rather than directly as a functional molecule, with its effects likely mediated indirectly via increases in endogenous Tre. This ~ 30% increase is probably insufficient to directly scavenge ROS; instead, exogenous Tre may trigger an upstream signaling cascade that upregulates antioxidant and stress-response genes, potentially amplifying the secondary impact of endogenous Tre. FAA content increased significantly under chromium (Cr) stress (Fig. 3), and this accumulation was further amplified by Tre seed priming. The Cr-induced rise in FAAs is likely attributable to enhanced proteolysis of damaged proteins, coupled with upregulated de novo proline synthesis. Nitrogen serves as a fundamental precursor for protein and amino acid biosynthesis [73]; accordingly, Tre priming markedly improved nitrogen uptake, which in turn contributed to the elevated FAA levels observed. Tre also increased TSP content, a direct consequence of restored amino acid pools; enhanced protein synthesis contributes to cellular recovery under stress conditions [22, 74]. Cr stress caused a marked decline in nutrient accumulation (Table S1) in barley plants, consistent with previous studies reporting that Cr stress decreases nutrient uptake [75]. Tre application significantly enhanced nutrient accumulation. Tre reduces ROS production, which may mitigate damage to plasma membrane H⁺-ATPases, thereby ensuring a better driving force for cation (K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺) uptake. Tre may also lower apoplastic Cr concentration via extracellular complexation, which reduces Cr availability at the root surface and shifts competition in favor of nutrient uptake. Secondary metabolites contribute to tolerance against both biotic and abiotic stress. Under Cr stress, secondary metabolite synthesis increased significantly, consistent with previous reports that abiotic stress enhances secondary metabolite production to counteract toxicity [76]. Tre priming significantly enhanced secondary metabolite synthesis [77], suggesting positive cross-talk between Tre and secondary metabolism. Tre likely upregulates phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) and arginine decarboxylase biosynthesis; increased enzyme activity sustains secondary metabolism and helps counteract stress conditions. The findings indicate that barley roots accumulated more Cr than shoots, reflecting direct root exposure to Cr. Tre seed priming significantly reduced Cr accumulation in both roots and shoots, possibly through complex formation that reduces uptake. This aligns with earlier studies showing that Tre supply diminishes Cd and Pb accumulation in mung bean and rice [78].

Proposed model showing the role of trehalose in mitigating chromium toxicity

It has been hypothesized that the hydroxyl groups of Tre could potentially form complexes with Cr, which might lower Cr activity in the rhizosphere and decrease its diffusion into root cells [79]; however, this proposed mechanism remains speculative without direct molecular evidence. Similarly, Tre may possibly suppress the activity of certain transporters, such as SULTR1;1, SULTR1;2, and NRAMP, which could hypothetically contribute to reduced Cr uptake. Nevertheless, the specific involvement of these transporters in response to Tre priming remains speculative and requires direct experimental confirmation in future studies. These findings also raise the possibility that Tre seed priming may help retain stress-related effects throughout the growing period, consistent with observations from previous studies [80]. Trehalose could hypothetically contribute to epigenetic and metabolic stress memory, potentially by influencing mitotic division. Specifically, Tre might induce stable DNA hypomethylation, enhance endogenous Tre synthesis, and boost antioxidant activities, though these epigenetic effects, particularly DNA hypomethylation, remain speculative. In addition, Tre may also exert a hormonal priming effect. Taken together, it is possible that a combination of these changes might help retain stress memory and could hypothetically contribute to better plant performance under chromium stress. However, all proposed mechanisms, particularly those involving Tre–Cr complexation, transporter modulation, and epigenetic regulation, require direct experimental validation in future studies. Our results further revealed that Sultan-17 consistently outperformed Haider-93 in biomass and grain yield. This superior tolerance was associated with several factors. Sultan-17 maintained lower oxidative damage under Cr stress, suggesting a more efficient antioxidant system. Additionally, it sustained better IAA and GA levels, which supported improved root growth and subsequent plant development.

Conclusion

Chromium stress reduced barley growth and yield by disrupting physiological functions, hormonal balance, and nutrient homeostasis, while increasing oxidative damage. Tre priming effectively mitigated chromium toxicity by reducing metal accumulation, minimizing oxidative stress, and enhancing antioxidant activity. These protective effects were associated with increased endogenous trehalose, indole acetic acid, and gibberellin synthesis, improved nutrient uptake, and reduced abscisic acid levels. These findings position Tre as a cost-effective and eco-friendly priming agent for improving chromium tolerance and crop productivity. Future studies should examine the expression profiles of antioxidant and transporter genes and identify the signaling pathways involved in Tre-mediated responses. Furthermore, field-scale validations under variable environmental conditions are necessary. Such investigations will help elucidate the molecular basis of Tre-induced stress tolerance and support its practical application in sustainable agriculture.
