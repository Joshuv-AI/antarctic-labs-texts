The double-decker technique for transurethral water vapor therapy (Rezūm) in catheter-dependent patients: A step-by-step guide and results from a prospective registry

INTRODUCTION

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a prevalent histological condition for men and can give rise to lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) [12]. It imposes a substantial burden on patients’ quality of life and on healthcare systems, especially when complicated by acute urinary retention (AUR) [3]. While transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) and enucleation techniques remain the gold standard [4567], minimally invasive surgical therapies (MIST) offer viable alternatives for high surgical-risk patients [89]. Transurethral water vapor therapy (Rezūm; Boston Scientific Company Inc.) has emerged as an effective office-based MIST option under local anesthesia, with established efficacy for LUTS [101112] and growing evidence in catheter-dependent cohorts [1314].

Recent work by our group demonstrated that a treatment cycles per unit prostate volume (CPV) ratio >0.15 significantly improves 1-year International Prostate Symptom Scores (IPSS) and reduces 30-day readmissions in catheter-dependent patients—primarily due to less recurrent retention [15]. Prostates have highly variable morphology that can affect water vapor delivery—volume, length, anteroposterior (AP) diameter, etc. Thus, a standardized approach is required in optimizing water vapor delivery, distributing convective thermal energy uniformly and reproducibly. Based on conventional field-of-vision (FOV)-based methods, for a 50–60 cc prostate, usually around 2–3 treatment cycles are performed per lateral lobe. While that may suffice for non-catheter-dependent patients, catheter-dependent patients often require more radical ‘de-obstruction’ to achieve satisfactory voiding outcomes. We introduce the double-decker technique, and this guide details the technique step-by-step, supported by analysis from our prospective registry.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Step-by-step guide of the double-decker technique (video illustration in Supplementary Video)

1) Preoperative preparation

Transurethral water vapor therapy is performed using the Rezūm system under local anesthesia in an office-based or operating theatre setting. Prophylactic antibiotics and intravenous tramadol are administered preoperatively. Preoperative urine culture is mandatory and asymptomatic bacteriuria is treated with a one-week course of antibiotics. EMLA cream (topical lidocaine and prilocaine; Aspen Pharmacare UK Ltd.) is applied to the perineum one hour prior to the procedure for analgesia. Patients are positioned in the Lloyd-Davies position. Prostate volume is measured via transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) by the prolate ellipsoid formula. The minimum number of treatment cycles is calculated by multiplying the prostate volume in milliliters by CPV=0.15. For example, a prostate measuring 60 mL requires at least nine treatment cycles to achieve the target CPV threshold. The perineal skin is prepared with aqueous betadine and alcohol.

2) Local anesthesia

Under transrectal ultrasonography guidance, local anesthesia is administered using 30 mL of 1% lidocaine with adrenaline. This is distributed as 10 mL to the midline prerectal space below the Denonvilliers’ fascia and 10 mL to each of the bilateral posterolateral prostatic neurovascular bundles. Urethral instillation of 2% lidocaine gel follows.

3) Cystoscopy and landmark identification

The Rezūm device is advanced through the urethra to the bladder neck under direct vision. Extra care is taken to avoid contact bleeding which may impair the endoscopic view. During the advance of cystoscope, the prostate configuration is examined. The presence of median lobe, AP diameter, locations of external urethral sphincter and ureteral orifices should be noted. Cystoscopy is performed to exclude alternate pathologies such as bladder tumor or bladder stone. Prostate length is measured using FOV from the bladder neck to the verumontanum.

4) Double-decker vapor delivery

The prostatic urethra is conceptually divided into two identical decks—the anterior deck and the posterior deck. The line of division is at the middle. This is to ensure comprehensive delivery of convective thermal energy. Injections are administered at specific horizontal levels: at the 2 o’clock and 4 o’clock positions for the left lateral lobe, and at the 8 o’clock and 10 o’clock positions for the right lateral lobe. These directions minimize risk to critical structures, as more anterior or posterior injections could damage the external urethral sphincter or rectum respectively.

The first injection in each direction is delivered two FOVs distal to the bladder neck to prevent injury to the trigone and ureteric orifices. Subsequent injections proceed distally at intervals of two FOVs. Continuous reference to the verumontanum ensures injections do not extend beyond the prostatic apex. For anterior deck injections near the apex (2 o’clock and 10 o’clock positions), the trajectory is adjusted slightly posteriorly (toward 3 o’clock and 9 o’clock) to avoid compromising the external urethral sphincter. When a median lobe is present, injections can be delivered bilaterally at 45° angles. Surgeons may elect to administer additional injections to the most obstructive regions of the prostatic urethra after completing the double-decker protocol. The double-decker concept is illustrated as a diagram in Fig. 1. The video summary is presented in the Supplementary Video.

5) Postoperative management

A 16 Fr silicone Foley’s catheter with a 10 mL balloon is inserted postoperatively. Bladder washout is performed until the effluent appears pale pink. Postoperatively, a one-week course of antibiotics is given. The choice of antibiotics was either according to positive urine culture or according to local microbiogram. Catheter removal timing follows our institutional protocol, occurring at 14 days for catheter-dependent patients to allow the initial edema of prostate tissue to subside.

2. Conventional technique

The conventional technique followed the above pre-, intra- and postoperative steps, except for the water vapor treatment planning. Water vapor injections were planned based on the FOV approach [16]. The injections were performed 1 cm under the bladder neck and caudally down the length of the prostatic urethra to the proximal edge of the verumontanum each centimeter (i.e., each two FOVs).

RESULTS

Research approval was obtained from the Joint Chinese University of Hong Kong–New Territories East Cluster Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval number: CRE2022.049). A prospective registry (NCT05292235) was set up at three centers in Hong Kong, China—Prince of Wales Hospital, North District Hospital, and Alice Ho Miu Ling Nethersole Hospital—for patients undergoing Rezūm from February 2021 to September 2023. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Procedures were performed by three surgeons who had already performed at least 50 cases of Rezūm. The selection for double-decker technique versus conventional technique was based on the surgeon’s assessment of the prostate’s AP diameter. Patient demographics, presence of median lobe, prostate length or volume were not determining factors. Propensity score matched analysis of 144 consecutive catheter-dependent patients from February 2021 to September 2023 was published recently [15]. Baseline characteristics, intraoperative details and postoperative outcomes of those receiving conventional technique and those receiving the double-decker technique within this cohort were compared by the Student’s t-test in this study. Predictors of one-year IPSS were analyzed with multivariable linear regression analysis. All patients were catheter-dependent; thus, their preoperative IPSS, IPSS-QoL (quality of life), maximal flow rate (Qmax), and post void residual urine volume (PVR) were not available for comparison to postoperative data.

Baseline characteristics, including age, prostate volume, body mass index, American Society of Anesthesiologists grading, and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, were comparable between the double-decker and conventional technique groups (all p>0.05) as detailed in Table 1. The double-decker technique had a higher mean CPV (0.204±0.028) compared to the conventional technique (0.132±0.044) with a greater number of injections (10.4±1.9 vs. 6.7±2.6, p<0.001).

There was no statistically significant difference in the 30-day readmission rate between the double-decker and conventional technique groups (4.8% [2/42] vs. 12.7% [13/102], p=0.145). No Clavien–Dindo ≥3 complications occurred in either group. A potential concern for the high CPV/double-decker technique is the increased early irritative symptoms. There was no difference in the six-week IPSS and IPSS-QoL between the two techniques (p=0.848 and p=0.372, respectively). The catheter-free rates at sequential time points were not significantly different between the two groups: at 2 weeks, 52.9% vs. 47.6% (p=0.567); at 4 weeks, 70.6% vs. 64.3% (p=0.474); at 6 weeks, 86.3% vs. 83.3% (p=0.664); and at 12 weeks, 94.1% vs. 88.1% (p=0.284).

This standardized approach to optimizing vapor distribution in the double-decker technique was associated with a significantly greater percentage reduction in prostate volume at three months (34.3%±16.7% vs. 26.1%±17.6%, p=0.029) and a superior IPSS at one year (3.70±2.91 vs. 8.13±7.27, p=0.041), as shown in Table 2. No statistically significant differences were observed in Qmax or PVR between the groups at any time point. A multivariable linear regression was performed to identify independent predictors of one-year IPSS, adjusting for surgical technique, preoperative prostate volume, age, and the presence of a median lobe (Table 3). The double-decker technique was associated with a lower one-year IPSS (coefficient: -4.86, 95% confidence interval [CI] -9.61 to -0.12, p=0.045).

Fig. 2 shows the preoperative (done one month preoperative, before patient suffered from AUR) and postoperative (done 22 months postoperative) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) images. The patient was a 74-year-old male. He had a 40.0 cc prostate and received 10 treatment cycles with the double-decker technique, giving a CPV of 0.25. Alpha blockers and 5-alpha reductase inhibitors were discontinued three months postoperative. At three months, his prostate volume shrunk to 24.6 cc by TRUS. At one year, his IPSS was 0 with Qmax of 19.0 mL/s.

DISCUSSION

Transurethral water vapor therapy (Rezūm) is an ablative and ‘volume-reducing’ procedure [11]. The goal of the procedure should be to reduce the prostate volume maximally, optimize symptom control, prevent recurrence of BPH-related complications, while maintaining safety and patient tolerability. Extrapolating from the evidence from TURP, the weight of prostate adenoma resected in relation to total prostate volume is predictive of favorable symptoms and uroflowmetry results [17]. From our recently published analysis on CPV in Rezūm and this study, the double-decker/high CPV approach improved symptom scores at one year, while maintaining satisfactory safety profile.

In this study, we detailed the double-decker technique. We demonstrated that the double-decker technique was associated with greater prostate volume reduction and improved one-year IPSS. The magnitude of IPSS difference of 4.43 points is considered clinically significant, exceeding the commonly agreed minimal important difference of 3 points [18]. The double-decker technique was an independent statistically significant predictor of one-year IPSS (coefficient: -4.86, 95% CI -9.61 to -0.12, p=0.045).

A study by Mynderse et al. [19] with serial post-operative gadolinium-enhanced MRI aids to quantify Rezum’s ablative effects. The mean ablative lesion size by one water vapor injection was estimated in patients who received a single injection per lateral lobe. Each 208-calorie injection of vapor energy equated to a mean lesion size of 1.7 cm3 per lateral lobe. With this, we could hypothetically deduce the ‘ideal’ number of treatment cycles/CPV required for lesions to cover the entire transition zone, inducing maximize prostate shrinkage effect.

It should be noted that this calculation is likely an overestimation as the lesion size was calculated one-week postoperative instead of immediate postoperative. While it is impractical as the maximum number of injections is capped at 15, this serves to remind us that the conventional FOV-based approach is unlikely to cover the entire transition zone.

The “less is more” single-arm pilot study by Aladesuru et al. [20] demonstrated in a cohort of 52 men in a single urologic clinic that a simplified approach of a single vapor injection per prostate lobe provided significant improvements in IPSS, QoL, and Qmax at 12 months, with a favorable safety profile. The concepts of “less is more” and “injection density/CPV” have sparked interest and discussion [152122]. However, the double-decker/high CPV approach is not the opposite of the ‘less is more’ concept. Patient characteristics and prostate morphology are of utmost importance to treatment planning from our experience.

A 50-year-old male patient with mild to moderate LUTS, who has a 35 cc prostate with a short AP diameter and aims to be catheter-free in 3–5 days, will likely benefit from the‘less is more’ approach. On the other hand, an 80-year-old male patient with refractory retention and deemed high risk for anesthesia, who has an 80 cc prostate with a long AP diameter that can accommodate two ‘decks’ of injection, will likely benefit from the double-decker/high CPV approach to maximize prostate shrinkage. The choice of Rezūm treatment strategy should be tailored to individual patient characteristics, prostate anatomy, and treatment expectations through a process of shared decision-making.

Rezūm is a widely adopted MIST but literature regarding its technique is scarce. We demonstrated a safe, systematic and reproducible technique—the double-decker technique to optimize water vapor distribution in catheter-dependent patients. While TURP and enucleation of prostate should still be the standard ‘volume-reducing’ treatment for catheter-dependent patients, Rezūm with the double-decker technique can serve as a ‘middle ground’ for high-risk surgical candidates and for those who would like to preserve sexual function. Limitations of this study include its retrospective design which is prone to confounding and selection bias. The sample size is limited especially for the double-decker group.

The uroflowmetry parameters (Qmax, PVR) did not show the same difference between the two techniques, IPSS, or symptom scores in general, have been widely adopted as primary outcomes of BPH studies over Qmax/PVR, due to the subjective nature of LUTS [122324]. On the other hand, the Hald’s ring concept [25] describes that the presence of actual prostatic enlargement, the symptomatology, and the presence of urodynamic obstruction are not always present together. As a result, discordance between symptom score and uroflowmetry parameters have been commonly observed in BPH surgery studies [915262728]. In our cohort, the sample size might have been a limitation to demonstrating a significant difference in Qmax/PVR, resulting from the increased prostate volume reduction in the double-decker technique.

Urodynamic studies were not performed routinely, firstly due to doubtful benefit as demonstrated by the UPSTREAM trial [29], and due to poor patient acceptance due to its invasive nature. Thus, detrusor underactivity and bladder outlet obstruction were not able to be confidently differentiated in our cohort. Follow-up duration was twelve months, long-term outcomes such as surgical/medical retreatment rates remain unknown. Data collection is ongoing for analysis for outcomes at 3 years and beyond. While the double-decker technique demonstrated superior prostate volume reduction and one-year IPSS, larger scaled prospective and randomized studies are warranted to confirm its benefits against the conventional FOV-based technique.

CONCLUSIONS

The double-decker technique in transurethral water vapor therapy (Rezūm) is a systematic and reproducible method to increase CPV and optimize prostate volume shrinkage in catheter-dependent patients. It shows promise with better one-year IPSS and greater prostate volume shrinkage against the conventional technique. Prospective and randomized studies are warranted to confirm its advantage.
