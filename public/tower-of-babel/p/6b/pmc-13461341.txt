Concerns with the statistical analysis in “Effect of combined GLP-1 analogue and bupropion/naltrexone on weight loss: a retrospective cohort study”
Authors: Thirupathi Reddy Mokalla, Henry T. Robertson, Sherri Pals, David B. Allison
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Naude et al. [1] evaluated whether there were additive effects of prescribing bupropion/naltrexone on top of a glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) analogue as weight loss therapy. GLP-1 analogues are known to be an effective treatment for weight loss, and the authors stated based on their analysis that the addition of bupropion/naltrexone was associated with greater weight loss, including in patients who were initially non-responsive to GLP-1 analogues. On our initial review of the article, we found the description and results of the analysis to be hard to follow. Specifically, our concerns focus on the following:

The main conclusions: The article states that both the responder and non-responder groups had greater total body weight loss (TBWL) than the monotherapy group and based this conclusion on the linear regression models presented in Table 3 [1]. However, the regression coefficient for responders was positive (0.038) and the regression coefficient for non-responders was negative (−0.07). This would seem to indicate that the non-responders experienced less weight loss than the monotherapy group. If we have interpreted these data correctly, the conclusions as stated in the paper are invalid.

Clarity of methods: The description of methods, including the flowchart, is inadequate for understanding how patients were included or excluded in the final outcome analyses. The numbers in the flowchart do not match the numbers in the analytic models and no explanation is given for the differences. As such, it remains unclear whether the analyses and design as implemented answer the research questions.

We contacted the journal’s leadership to request raw data and code from the authors, allowing us to verify the reported results and evaluate the authors’ analyses. The authors cooperatively provided us with the raw data and some supplementary files.

Our first step, as a matter of standard operating procedure, was to attempt to reproduce (as defined in [2]) the results before exploring alternative analyses. We attempted reproduction using the authors’ carry-forward imputation methods. Regardless of the imputation methods employed, we were unable to reproduce the results stated in Table 2 of the paper [1]. We found that patients who received combined therapy lost less body weight than patients who received monotherapy, contrary to the stated conclusion.

We also found that even when provided with several additional documents by the authors, including a very limited data dictionary, we could not clearly determine whether primary variables of interest were coded in a way that matches the flowchart and other descriptions. Further, descriptions in text lacked the detail that would allow for a thorough understanding of how variables were calculated and which participants were included in analyses. For example, Table 3 gives no indication of the time period of the outcome variable (6 months, 12 months) and no indication of whether the number of participants in the non-responder and responder groups in that table matches either set of numbers presented in Table 2 [1]. Also, Table 2 does not include the monotherapy group, so the means for that group are not presented.

Design concerns: Additionally, we uncovered evidence that patients who did not lose weight on monotherapy at baseline were more likely to be assigned to combination therapy, raising concerns about confounding and/or regression to the mean.

Upon further correspondence with the author, we established that rather than referring to the two medications being taken at the same time, ‘combined therapy’ referred to any patient who had ever tried both GLP-1 and bupropion/naltrexone at different points in time. This violates the basic definition of combined therapy. The code book says that there was a field called ‘GLP-1’ which was set to 1 if the patient ever took a GLP-1 agonist. Table 1 seems to indicate that 70 patients who were coded as not receiving GLP-1 agonist on one variable were coded as receiving GLP-1 on the second variable. It also says that another 12 patients who never took GLP-1 did in fact receive combined therapy.

We also found that the number of non-responders varied throughout the analyses reported. The flowchart stated that there were 51 non-responders, Table 2 reported 49 non-responders at 6 months, and the regression model seemed to include 54 non-responders. There were no explanations of these differences in the paper [1]. The assignment of ‘responder’ or ‘non-responder’ labels, based on whether participants lost at least 5% of body weight, did not align with the observed values. Our review of the raw data suggested that weight measurements occurred haphazardly throughout the 12 months, and no clear assignments could be made to ‘6 months’ or ‘12 months’. Additionally, we could not determine which observations were assigned to ‘before’ and which to ‘after’ combined treatment at those time points. We asked for clarifications from the authors, and they stated that they included in the combined therapy group patients who had ever taken both drugs, rather than patients who had taken them simultaneously. This conflicts with the description of this group in the methods section of the article. Given the irreproducibility of the results, we ceased our analytical work at that point and reported our findings to the journal editors.

Our findings do not determine whether the authors’ conclusions are valid or invalid; we only demonstrate that the conclusions are not currently substantiated by the authors’ data and analyses. We commend the authors for sharing their data, which contributes to rigour in scientific research [2, 3].
