Frailty status and mortality among patients with cardiometabolic diseases in an international multi-cohort study
Authors: Rui Ren, Yao Zhang, Meilin Du, Wenxu Wang, Liying Wang, Jilong Bai, Yibin Lu, Yanan Ma, Difei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFrailty is an indicator of biological aging associated with adverse outcomes in cardiometabolic diseases (CMD). We investigated the association between frailty status and mortality risk using data from five prospective cohorts including the UK Biobank, NHANES, CHARLS, ELSA, and HRS. Across these cohorts, pre-frail participants had a 21%–66% higher risk of all-cause mortality, while frail participants had a 54%–132% higher risk compared with non-frail individuals. Frailty was also associated with increased risks of cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular mortality, while associations with cancer-related mortality were mainly observed among individuals classified as frail. Subgroup analyses revealed heterogeneity across age, body mass index (BMI), sex, and CMD burden, with stronger associations among younger individuals (<65 years), those with lower BMI, and individuals with multiple CMDs. Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent results. These findings suggest that frailty may serve as an important prognostic marker in CMD populations and support risk stratification in clinical management.Subject areas: cardiovascular medicine, endocrinology, health sciences, medicine

Introduction

Cardiometabolic diseases (CMD) refer to a group of disorders, including diabetes, hypertension, stroke, and coronary heart disease (CHD), that affect the metabolic and cardiovascular systems.1,2,3 CMD significantly contributes to the high morbidity and mortality rates observed globally and exerts a considerable burden on healthcare systems.4,5 According to a systematic study from the 2019 Global Burden of Disease Study, CMDs are among the leading causes of death worldwide.6 Given the rising prevalence of CMD and associated adverse impacts on both patients and healthcare systems, identifying prognostic factors is critical for recognizing vulnerable subgroups and implementing appropriate disease management and therapeutic interventions in clinical practice.

Previous studies on prognostic factors in patients with CMD have primarily focused on traditional biomarkers (e.g., blood lipids, blood glucose, and inflammatory markers) and lifestyle factors (e.g., diet, exercise, and smoking).7,8,9 Recently, an increasing research interest has been directed at the mechanisms underlying accelerated biological aging.10,11 Frailty, a key manifestation of biological aging, is characterized by a decline in stress compensation capacity due to the depletion of multiple physiological system reserves.12 In geriatric medicine, frailty has emerged as a crucial metric for evaluating the aging process. The frailty index (FI) model, developed by Rockwood et al., is one of the most used methods for assessing frailty. This model integrates various factors, incorporating chronic illnesses, psychosocial aspects, cognitive impairments, and various other geriatric manifestations.13 In the FI model, frailty is effectively quantified using the ratio of accumulated deficits to total potential deficits in an individual.13 Previous studies have shown that frailty notably heightens the likelihood of mortality, disability, falls, and fractures14 and is also closely associated with the development and course of various chronic diseases.15 A meta-analysis of 13 prospective studies, with follow-up durations ranging from 2 to 19 years and participants aged 15 to 108 years, found that increased frailty—as measured using the FI—was significantly correlated with higher mortality risk.16 Similarly, a meta-analysis of 11 prospective studies involving 35,508 patients aged ≥65 years demonstrated that individuals with frailty had a significantly higher mortality rate over an average follow-up of 6.1 years than healthy individuals.17 Moreover, several studies have revealed significant associations between frailty and cardiovascular events, functional decline, and mortality risk.18,19 Research indicates that approximately 25%–40% of patients with CMD (e.g., those with diabetes or CHD) are frail, with prevalence rates 2–3 times higher than those in age-matched community-dwelling older populations.20,21 While frailty is extensively used to forecast mortality and other adverse outcomes in the general population, its potential incremental value in predicting mortality risk among patients remains insufficiently explored. Particularly in individuals with early-stage frailty, the condition may be reversible,12 and screening these individuals for early intervention may delay or prevent the onset of late-stage comorbidities, improving their quality of life and extending their lifespan.

This study aimed to examine the impact of frailty status in patients with CMD, focusing on its impact on all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease (CVD)-related mortality, non-CVD-related mortality, and cancer mortality. By systematically analyzing these factors using data from five cohorts across different regions (the UK Biobank, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey [NHANES], China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study [CHARLS], English Longitudinal Study of Aging [ELSA], and Health and Retirement Study [HRS]), the study highlights the clinical relevance of frailty in patients with CMD and established a foundation for developing early screening and intervention strategies.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 129,025 participants (110,777 from the UK Biobank; 4,634 from the NHANES; 4,479 from the CHARLS; 2,674 from the ELSA; and 6,461 from the HRS) were included in this study. The proportion of women in the five cohorts ranged from 45.4% (UK Biobank), 46.1% (NHANES), 54.9% (CHARLS), 51.2% (ELSA), and 57.4% (HRS). The median age at baseline ranged from 60 to 72 years, and the median follow-up period between 8.9 and 12.5 years. The racial composition was predominantly White in the UK Biobank (94.6%) and ELSA (98.5%), Han Chinese in CHARLS (92.4%), and more ethnically diverse in NHANES (52.7% Non-Hispanic White) and HRS (81.8% White). Frailty status and mortality risk

Mortality events varied across cohorts. The UK Biobank recorded 13,706 total deaths, including 3,783 CVD-related, 9,923 non-CVD-related, and 5,376 cancer-related deaths. The NHANES documented 1,651 deaths, including 608 CVD-related, 1,043 non-CVD-related, and 311 cancer-related deaths. CHARLS, ELSA, and HRS reported 176, 203, and 3,659 all-cause deaths, respectively. Cumulative mortality risk curves, as shown in Figure 2, confirmed that frail participants had significantly higher cumulative mortality risk compared to pre-frail and non-frail individuals across all cohorts. These findings were consistent for cause-specific mortality in the UK Biobank and NHANES cohorts, as depicted in Figures S1 and S2.

In the multivariable adjusted model 2, the association between frailty status and all-cause mortality in CMD participants across all five cohorts was assessed (Figure 3). Pre-frail participants exhibited a 21%–66% higher risk (hazard ratio [HR] range: 1.21 [CHARLS] to 1.66 [ELSA]), while frail participants had a 54%–132% higher risk (HR range: 1.54 [CHARLS] to 2.32 [ELSA]) than non-frail participants. For each 0.1 increment in the continuous FI, the adjusted HR across cohorts ranged from 1.20 (CHARLS) to 1.48 (NHANES) across cohorts. In the pooled analysis, despite substantial between-cohort heterogeneity for frailty categories (I2 = 78.43%, p for heterogeneity <0.001), pre-frail participants had a significantly higher risk of mortality than non-frail individuals (pooled adjusted HR = 1.30, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.21–1.39), while frail participants exhibited an even greater risk (pooled HR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.73–2.27). When frailty was analyzed as a continuous variable, each 0.1-unit increase in the frailty index was associated with a 32% higher risk of mortality (pooled HR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.25–1.39), with even greater heterogeneity observed across cohorts (I2 = 87.20%, p for heterogeneity <0.001).

In cause-specific mortality analyses (Table S4), frailty was associated with increased risks across all cohorts. The pooled analysis revealed a 40% higher risk for CVD-related mortality (pooled HR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.27–1.56), a 38% increased risk for non-CVD-related mortality (pooled HR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.22–1.55), and an 11% increased risk for cancer-related mortality (pooled HR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.03–1.20) (Table S5).

Dose-response and nonlinear associations between frailty index and mortality

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with multivariable adjustment was used to explore dose-response associations between the continuous FI and all-cause mortality across five CMD cohorts (Figure 4). Nonlinear relationships were observed in the ELSA (p-non-linear = 0.038) and HRS (p-non-linear = 0.001) cohorts, while linear associations were noted in the UK Biobank (p-non-linear = 0.782), NHANES (p-non-linear = 0.963), and CHARLS (p-non-linear = 0.243) cohorts. In cause-specific mortality analyses for the UK Biobank and NHANES cohorts, Figures S3 and S4 revealed no significant nonlinear associations between the FI and CVD-related, non-CVD-related, or cancer-related mortality (all p-non-linear > 0.05).

For cohorts with nonlinear associations (ELSA and HRS), threshold effect analysis was conducted via two-piecewise linear regression (Table S6). In ELSA, the FI exhibited a threshold effect (p for likelihood ratio test = 0.03): no significant association with mortality was observed when the frailty index was ≥ 0.17, but a positive association emerged (adjusted HR = 822.00, 95% CI: 16.30–4143.00, p < 0.001) at values < 0.17. In HRS, the threshold was 0.32 (p for likelihood test = 0.001): the frailty index was positively associated with mortality both below (adjusted HR = 18.90, 95% CI: 12.10–29.50, p < 0.001) and above (adjusted HR = 5.19, 95% CI: 3.19–8.46, p < 0.001) this threshold, with a stronger effect below 0.32.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses (Figures S5–S8) showed consistent positive associations between frailty and mortality outcomes across subgroups defined by age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and number of CMDs. Significant interaction effects were observed in the UK Biobank cohort, where age modified the associations of frailty with all-cause (p-interaction = 0.002), CVD-related (p-interaction = 0.018), and non-CVD-related mortality (p-interaction = 0.048), with younger participants (<65 years) having higher mortality risk per 0.1 FI increment. BMI < 30 kg/m2 also increased frailty-related risks for all-cause (p-interaction = 0.005) and non-CVD-related mortality (p-interaction = 0.020) in the UK Biobank. In the HRS cohort, age also modified the all-cause mortality association (p-interaction < 0.001). In NHANES, sex modified the non-CVD-related mortality (p-interaction = 0.019), with stronger risks in women. For the number of CMDs, significant interaction was observed in ELSA for all-cause mortality (p-interaction = 0.039), where frailty conferred a substantially stronger risk in participants with ≥2 CMDs than in those with 1 CMD. No significant interactions were observed for cancer-related mortality across all subgroups (all p-interaction > 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis

The results remained robust after excluding participants who died within the first 2 years of follow-up, as shown in Tables S7 and S8. The E-values for the primary analysis further supported the robustness of the findings (Tables S9 and S10). When expanding to the broader definition of CMD in the UK Biobank and NHANES cohorts, the overall associations were largely unchanged (Table S11). Significant heterogeneity characterized the associations across the UK Biobank, NHANES, CHARLS, HRS, and ELSA cohorts, and sensitivity analyses revealed that NHANES cohort was the primary contributor to this heterogeneity (Table S12).

Discussion

Using five large, prospective cohorts from diverse geographic and sociocultural settings (UK Biobank, NHANES, CHARLS, ELSA, and HRS), this study systematically examined the association between frailty and all-cause as well as cause-specific mortality among patients with CMD. Across all cohorts, frailty showed a consistent positive association with mortality, and a graded increase in risk was observed with increasing frailty severity. Notably, the magnitude of these associations varied substantially across cohorts, indicating pronounced between-cohort heterogeneity. Additionally, the relationship between frailty condition and mortality risk showed cohort-specific effects across demographic characteristics (age, BMI, and sex) and the number of CMDs.

Our analysis demonstrated that frailty remained strongly associated with all-cause mortality across all five cohorts after multivariable adjustment, in line with findings from previous studies in general populations.22,23,24 When cause-specific mortality was examined, frailty showed a stronger association with CVD-related mortality than with non-CVD-related mortality. This association likely reflects the cumulative impact of multisystem physiological dysregulation captured by frailty, including chronic low-grade inflammation, metabolic imbalance, accelerated biological aging, and reduced physiological reserve.25,26,27 Frailty has been conceptualized as a clinical manifestation of biological aging, characterized by the accumulation of age-related deficits across multiple organ systems rather than dysfunction of a single pathway.27,28 Emerging evidence suggests that individuals with a higher frailty burden exhibit features of accelerated biological aging, including epigenetic age acceleration, ageing-related immune dysregulation, and reduced physiological resilience to stress.29,30,31,32 In the context of CMD, these ageing-related processes may be further amplified by metabolic-vascular axis dysregulation, manifested as endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and mitochondrial impairment, which may exacerbate cardiovascular dysfunction and contribute to the stronger association between frailty and CVD-related mortality.33,34,35 Some researchers have suggested that frailty is not significantly associated with cancer mortality.36,37 However, our findings showed that only individuals with frailty had a significantly higher risk of cancer-related death, whereas no significant association was observed in the pre-frail group. The association between the frailty status and cancer mortality persisted even after excluding participants who died within the first 2 years of follow-up, suggesting that reverse causation is unlikely to fully explain the observed relationship. Nevertheless, given the inconsistencies across studies, further research is needed to clarify the association between frailty and cancer-specific mortality.

Notwithstanding the consistent direction of the associations, substantial between-cohort heterogeneity was observed in the magnitude of effect estimates. This heterogeneity is likely attributable to differences in study design, population structure, CMD ascertainment, baseline health status, and follow-up duration across cohorts. Similar heterogeneity has been documented in prior multi-cohort and meta-analytic studies, in which meta-regression analyses identified follow-up length and population characteristics as modifiers of the frailty-mortality association.16,38 In addition, differences in covariate availability and FI construction across cohorts may further limit the direct comparability of effect sizes, consistent with the deficit accumulation framework underlying frailty measurement.39,40 Nevertheless, despite this heterogeneity, prior multi-cohort and meta-analytic studies have consistently reported a positive association between frailty and mortality in general populations,16,40,41 and our pooled analysis extends this evidence by demonstrating that the association remains positive in individuals with CMD.

RCS models provided additional insight into the dose-response relationship between FI and all-cause mortality. In most cohorts, including the UK Biobank, NHANES, and CHARLS, the association between FI and mortality appeared largely linear, indicating a steady accumulation of risk with increasing frailty burden. However, significant nonlinear relationships with identifiable inflection points were observed in ELSA (FI = 0.17) and HRS (FI = 0.32). In HRS, the association between FI and mortality was stronger when FI was below 0.32 than when FI was above this threshold, although risk remained significantly elevated in both segments. Such nonlinear patterns are consistent with the deficit accumulation framework of frailty, which posits that vulnerability may escalate disproportionately beyond certain levels of accumulated deficits.39 Cohort-specific inflection points provide key insights for individualized risk stratification, aiding early intervention and personalized treatment. While a linear relationship between FI and mortality risk is observed in most cohorts, this model may not fully capture the accelerated risk once certain thresholds of frailty are reached. Therefore, FI should be interpreted with consideration of nonlinear patterns in clinical practice.

Significant cohort-specific variations in the association between frailty and mortality were observed in subgroup analyses. Previous studies have indicated that a higher FI is associated with an elevated risk of all-cause mortality, although this association diminishes with age.36,42,43 A 30-year study of 1,477 Swedish adults showed that women aged <65 years had a greater correlation between the FI and all-cause mortality than those aged ≥65 years.43 In our study, the UK Biobank cohort similarly demonstrated that the association between frailty and all-cause, CVD-related, and non-CVD-related mortality among individuals with CMD was more pronounced in those aged <65 years and attenuated in older age groups. These findings indicate that frailty remains an important prognostic marker in individuals with CMD below 65 years of age, supporting its potential role in risk stratification beyond traditionally older populations. In contrast, the age interaction observed in HRS should be interpreted with caution, as the <65-year subgroup was small (n = 95), which may have resulted in imprecise and unstable estimates. Following the obesity paradox observed in diabetes and CHD cohorts, we discovered that in UK Biobank cohort, individuals with CMD and a BMI < 30 kg/m2 had a significantly higher risk of all-cause and non-CVD-related mortality than those with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2).44,45 This association may partly reflect differences in body composition and fitness. Greater lean mass and higher cardiorespiratory fitness have been proposed as potential contributors to the obesity paradox in CVD.44,46 In addition, inflammatory status may modify these relationships, as the prognostic implications of lean and fat mass appear to vary according to hs-CRP levels.47

Despite higher frailty levels among women, a meta-analysis reported lower mortality rates in women than in men at any given frailty level or age.48 However, findings from individual cohort studies have been inconsistent, with some reporting no significant sex interaction or even higher mortality risks among frail women.49,50 In line with this heterogeneity, our NHANES analysis showed that women had a greater risk of non-CVD-related mortality. These discrepancies may reflect differences in age range, health status, or cohort-specific factors. Moreover, in the ELSA cohort, CMD count significantly modified the association between frailty status and all-cause mortality (interaction p = 0.039), with frailty associated with substantially higher mortality risk among individuals with multiple CMDs. This finding suggests that the adverse impact of frailty may be amplified in individuals with a higher CMD burden.51

Conclusions

In conclusion, frailty status was significantly associated with increased risks of all-cause, CVD-related, and non-CVD-related mortality among individuals with CMD, while the association with cancer-related mortality was observed primarily in those classified as frail. Although effect sizes varied across cohorts, pooled analyses supported an overall positive association between frailty and mortality in CMD populations. Subgroup analyses indicated heterogeneity across age, BMI, sex, and CMD burden, with stronger associations observed among younger individuals and those with lower BMI. These findings suggest that frailty may serve as an important prognostic marker of CMD and may help identify individuals with elevated risk. Further research is needed to clarify the underlying biological mechanisms and to evaluate whether modifying frailty trajectories can influence clinical outcomes.

Limitations of the study

This study leveraged five large, prospective cohorts from diverse populations, enhancing the generalizability of the findings. The integration of categorical and continuous frailty measures, along with dose-response analyses, facilitated a more refined assessment of risk patterns. In addition, the evaluation of cause-specific mortality, subgroup interactions, and sensitivity analyses strengthened the robustness of the observed associations. Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, since the FI was constructed using self-reported data, classification errors or recall bias may have occurred during survey responses. However, the use of self-reported data in frailty indices has been validated in previous research.42,52 In addition, frailty is a dynamic condition that may change over time, with worsening being more common than improvement.53 Therefore, our study may have underestimated the strength of the association related to frailty. Second, although FI was derived under the deficit accumulation framework, differences in specific items, data sources, and covariate availability across cohorts may introduce measurement non-equivalence and limit the direct comparability of effect estimates. Third, substantial heterogeneity existed across the five cohorts in terms of population structure, follow-up duration, and baseline characteristics. In addition, differences in covariate availability and adjustment strategies across cohorts may have limited the direct comparability of effect estimates and introduce potential residual confounding. Fourth, no consensus exists on which specific diseases are included under CMD. To enhance the comparability of our findings with most previous studies, we incorporated T2D, hypertension, CHD, and stroke in our analysis. However, other conditions, such as peripheral artery disease, were not included.18 Fifth, the sample sizes for each subgroup were not determined prior to data collection, as subgroup analyses are conducted post hoc. Particularly, the results should be interpreted cautiously, as the UK Biobank’s non-white subgroup may have insufficient participants and events.54 Additionally, despite adjusting for key individual traits and comorbidities, residual confounding variables may still have influenced the findings. Lastly, the observational nature of these studies limited our ability to make causal inferences.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study design and participants

This multi-cohort study included five nationally representative longitudinal aging cohorts with comprehensive cardiometabolic data and mortality follow-up. Cohort selection was guided by the availability of longitudinal mortality data, suitability for FI construction under the deficit accumulation framework, and geographic diversity across Europe, America, and Asia.

UK Biobank

The UK Biobank is a large population-based prospective cohort study that enrolled over 500,000 individuals aged 40–70 years across 22 assessment centers in the UK between 2006 and 2010.55 This study was approved by the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent. In our research, participants with a history of cancer at baseline (n = 15,809), missing frailty data at baseline (n = 3,934), or incomplete covariate data (n = 36,355) were excluded. Ultimately, 110,777 individuals (median age of 61 years, 45.4% females) who had a baseline diagnosis of CMD were considered in the analysis (Figure S9).

NHANES

The NHANES is a cross-sectional, multi-stage, stratified, and cluster probability survey. It is conducted by the National Centre for Health Statistics (NCHS) of the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention.56 All respondents provided written informed consent, and the NCHS Institutional Review Board approved the survey protocol. Participants with missing frailty data at baseline (n = 20,801), a history of cancer at baseline (n = 1,794), missing follow-up data (n = 15), or incomplete covariate data (n = 5,441) were excluded. The final study population included 4,634 participants (median age of 65 years, 46.1% females) with CMD from the NHANES database, with data spanning 10 cycles (1999–2018) (Figure S9).

CHARLS, ELSA, and HRS

The CHARLS, ELSA, and HRS were prospective and nationally representative cohorts conducted in China, the UK, and the USA, respectively. In this study, wave 1 (2011) of CHARLS, wave 2 (2004–05) of ELSA, and wave 8 (2006) of HRS were regarded as the baseline. The CHARLS, ELSA, and HRS were approved by the Ethics Review Committees of Peking University, London Multi-Centre Research, and the University of Michigan, respectively. Informed consent was obtained from each participant in these three cohorts. The final follow-up survey which was wave 4 (2018) of CHARLS, wave 9 (2018–19) of ELSA, and wave 14 (2018) of HRS. Participants with missing frailty index data or a history of cancer were excluded, as were those with missing covariate information, resulting in a final study population of 4,479 participants (median age of 60 years, 54.9% females) from CHARLS, 2,674 participants (median age of 68 years, 51.2% females) from ELSA, and 6,461 participants (median age of 72 years, 57.4% females) from HRS (Figure S9).

Method details

Assessment of variables

Cardiometabolic diseases

Both the UK Biobank, NHANES, CHARLS, ELSA, and HRS included four common types of CMD: type 2 diabetes (T2D),CHD, stroke, and hypertension.57 These four conditions were used to define CMD across all five cohorts. In the UK Biobank, baseline CMD diagnoses were ascertained using a combination of physician diagnoses, self-reported data, and hospital inpatient records (Table S13). Baseline CMD diagnoses in NHANES, CHARLS, ELSA, and HRS were based on self-reported information.

Frailty assessment

Rockwood and Mitnitski initially introduced the FI, which included 92 deficit items.58 Dylan et al. used a standardized approach to construct a condensed version of the FI, involving 49 items covering physical function, chronic diseases, mental health, and sensory impairments.42 To eliminate overlap between the exposure variable (CMD) and the FI measurements, five items directly related to CMD, including diagnoses of diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia, were excluded. Additionally, to avoid reverse causality between outcome events (cancer death) and baseline frailty status, two items directly related to cancer diagnosis were further excluded. The final FI for the UK Biobank was constructed using 42 non-specific health deficit items (detailed in Table S14). Participants with ≥10 missing data points were excluded.42 Greater frailty is indicated by higher FI scores, which range from 0 to 1. Based on the FI score, the population was divided into three groups: non-frail (FI < 0.12), pre-frail (0.12≤ FI ≤0.24), and frail (FI > 0.24).59

The NHANES database uses a 32-item deficit model to calculate the FI and assess participants' frailty status.60 This study used the previously validated modified FI from the NHANES database, excluding variables directly related to CVD (including stroke, heart attack, hypertension, and CHD), T2D (such as blood glucose and haemoglobin A1c [HbA1c]), dyslipidaemia (total cholesterol), and cancer diagnoses (Table S15).61,62,63 The modifications made to the FI by eliminating items associated with CVD have been thoroughly validated in prior studies.64 Based on the data of CHARLS, ELSA, and HRS, we selected 28 items for FI construction (Table S16). The FI was a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 1, and a higher FI indicated a higher level of frailty. Participants were divided into three groups: non-frail (FI ≤ 0.1), pre-frail (0.1 < FI < 0.21), and frail (FI ≥ 0.21).65 During the analysis, the FI was employed as both a categorical and continuous variable (increment of 0.1).

Covariates

Covariates were obtained from questionnaires and physical examinations across the UK Biobank, NHANES, HRS, CHARLS, and ELSA. Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, and education level. Additional variables available only in specific cohorts included the Townsend Deprivation Index in the UK Biobank, the poverty income ratio in NHANES, and the living place in CHARLS and HRS. Lifestyle factors included smoking status, alcohol intake, and physical activity (PA). PA was classified according to WHO guidelines in the UK Biobank and NHANES (≥150 vs. <150 minutes of moderate-equivalent weekly activity),66 while in HRS and CHARLS it was categorized as vigorous, moderate, or inactive; PA was not included for ELSA because all eligible participants were classified as vigorous. Clinical measurements ((BMI, systolic blood pressure [SBP], diastolic blood pressure [DBP], fasting glucose, triglycerides [TG], high-density lipoprotein [HDL] cholesterol, and estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]) and family history of CMD were included when available (primarily in the UK Biobank and NHANES). Sex-specific GFR was calculated as follows: [(GFR[mL/min/1.73m2])=175×(creatinine/88.4)−1.154×(age)−0.203×(0.742iffemale)].67

Outcomes

Mortality data for the UK Biobank were sourced from death certificates issued by the National Health Service Information Centre for England and Wales and the National Health Service Central Registry for Scotland. The International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) codes were used to categorize deaths into all-cause mortality, CVD-related mortality (I00-I99), non-CVD-related mortality, and cancer-related mortality (C00-C97).68,69 The follow-up period ranged from the study start date to the participant's death date or the end date (England: 30 September 2021; Wales: 28 February 2018; Scotland: 31 July 2021).70 For the NHANES dataset, follow-up ended on 31 December 2015. Mortality data for CVD-related mortality, non-CVD-related mortality, cancer, and all-cause mortality were obtained from the National Death Index records provided by the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention. In CHARLS, deaths were ascertained through follow-up interviews (2013 and 2020 waves), based on reports from family members and verification via the household registration system. In HRS, mortality was determined through follow-up interviews and national administrative records. In ELSA, death information was obtained from administrative data and confirmed through exit interviews or reports from spouses/partners, with the last known alive date recorded when death was unconfirmed.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized overall and stratified by cohort, inclusion or exclusion owing to missing information, frailty status and mortality status. Continuous variables are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to calculate HRs and 95% confidence intervals for the associations between frailty and mortality outcomes, including all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease (CVD)-related mortality, non-CVD-related mortality, and cancer-related mortality. Schoenfeld residuals were used to validate the proportional hazards assumption, and all findings satisfied the predetermined standards. Based on a previous study,71 Model 1 adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 further adjusted for self-reported ethnicity, education level, TDI (UK Biobank only), PIR (NHANES only), BMI, smoking status, physical activity, alcohol use, SBP, DBP, TG, HDL, GFR, healthy diet, and family history of CMD. Owing to limited availability of covariates, Model 2 in the CHARLS and HRS cohorts was adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, education, living place, BMI, smoking status, drinking status, and physical activity. In the ELSA cohort, Model 2 included age, sex, ethnicity, education, BMI, smoking status, and drinking status. Frailty was analyzed both as a categorical variable (non-frail, pre-frail, frail) and continuous variable per 0.1 increment in the FI. Pooled estimates across cohorts were derived using random-effects meta-analysis models, accounting for between-cohort heterogeneity. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic and Cochran’s Q test.

Cumulative hazard curves were generated to compare mortality risk across frailty categories. By examining cumulative risk curves categorized by frailty state, the proportional hazards assumption was confirmed, with no significant violations detected. To compare the cumulative probability of death between different frailty status groups at baseline, cumulative mortality curves were used. The dose-response relationship between the FI and mortality was assessed using restricted cubic splines. For cohorts demonstrating significant nonlinear associations, two-piecewise linear regression models were further fitted to identify statistical inflection points (threshold effects), with likelihood ratio tests used to compare one-line and two-line models. Stratified analyses were conducted by age (<65 or ≥65 years), sex (male or female), BMI category (<30 or ≥30 kg/m2), and number of CMDs (1 or ≥2) to assess potential differences in the association between frailty and mortality. To evaluate multiplicative interactions between frailty status and possible risk variables, interaction terms were incorporated into the Cox proportional hazards models.

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to verify the robustness of the primary findings. These included: 1) eliminating participants who died within the first 2 years of follow-up to minimize the potential impact of reverse causality. This analysis was not performed in the CHARLS cohort, as no participants had follow-up durations shorter than the 2-year exclusion threshold; 2) redefining a broader range of CMD states using ICD-10 codes and self-reports, including CVD, diabetes, stroke, hypertension, obesity, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease; and 3) for statistically significant results from the primary analysis, calculating E-values to assess the strength of unmeasured confounding factors affecting both exposure and outcome; 4) leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed in pooled analyses by sequentially excluding each cohort to assess the influence of individual cohorts on the overall estimates and between-cohort heterogeneity.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All p-values were two-tailed, with a significance level of p < 0.05.
