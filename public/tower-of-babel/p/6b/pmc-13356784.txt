Bio-inspired polyphenol-polyelectrolyte WRAP system for geometrically controlled AAV delivery in diverse anatomical sites
Authors: Yong-Wook Son, Minjae Do, Mira Cho, Joowon Kim, Jun Sung Kim, Sang-woo Lee, Haeshin Lee, Jeongmin Kim, Wonhee Suh, Jae-Hyung Jang, Seung-Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSystemic delivery of therapeutics frequently requires elevated dosing to achieve effective local concentrations, often resulting in unintended toxicity and insufficient drug levels at the target site. To address this inherent limitation, localized drug and gene delivery strategies have been developed to deposit therapeutic payloads directly within diseased microenvironments. Herein, we report the development of a WRAP (Writable, Retentive, Adhesive, Permeable) drug delivery scaffold system, a biomaterial-mediated polyelectrolyte complex (PEC) film formed through spontaneous ionic complexation between chitosan and alginate. These naturally derived polymers form stable electrostatic interactions through their complementary charged groups, resulting in tunable film properties that maximize local therapeutic concentrations while minimizing adverse events during systemic exposure to therapeutics. To strengthen adhesion and sustain retention on wet tissue surfaces, chitosan-gallol (Chi-G) was incorporated as the cationic component of the PEC film, providing gallol-mediated surface binding and controlled loading of diverse therapeutic agents. When applied to adeno-associated virus (AAV), WRAP facilitated in situ viral retention, yielding significantly improved transgene delivery and prolonged transgene expression through improved spatial confinement. This enhanced spatial control represents a fundamental application of precision medicine principles, moving treatment beyond systemic toxicity toward highly efficient, site-specific interventions. Overall, WRAP functions as a versatile delivery platform that can be integrated with a range of therapeutic modalities requiring controlled, site-specific delivery.Keywords: Chitosan-gallol, Polyelectrolyte complex, Drug delivery system, Adeno-associated virus (AAV), Gene therapy

Introduction

Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based gene therapy has emerged as one of the most promising therapeutic modalities in advanced therapeutics, offering the potential for durable disease correction through a single administration [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Despite this promise, AAV-based gene therapy faces fundamental limitations that constrain its clinical utility. Because AAV-mediated transgene expression persists long-term in post-mitotic tissues and cannot be readily reversed or re-dosed once administered, achieving precise and sufficient vector concentration at the target site is not merely a matter of efficacy—it is a critical safety requirement [4,5]. Current clinical practice predominantly relies on systemic administration or direct tissue injection (e.g., intrathecal, subretinal, intracranial), yet these routes are inherently inefficient: vectors rapidly disseminate beyond the target tissue, resulting in high off-target exposure, dose-dependent toxicity, and unpredictable biodistribution [[6], [7], [8]]. The irreversible nature of gene editing further amplifies these concerns, as overdosing cannot be corrected after administration.

To address these challenges, diverse strategies have been explored, including alternative administration routes [9], antibody-AAV conjugates for targeted delivery [10], and machine learning-guided vector engineering for enhanced tissue selectivity [11]. However, approaches aimed at improving tissue targeting or modifying administration routes have not fully addressed the issue of spatial confinement at the delivery site—that is, ensuring that vectors remain concentrated at the intended tissue while minimizing off-target dissemination following administration. Biomaterial-mediated localized delivery, which has long been employed in conventional drug delivery to achieve spatial control of therapeutics, represents a conceptually attractive solution [[12], [13], [14]]. Yet, despite its widespread success with small-molecule drugs, this approach has remained relatively underexplored for AAV gene therapy. The primary obstacle lies in a fundamental incompatibility: most biomaterial platforms were originally designed around bulk encapsulation and sustained diffusion-based release from dense polymer matrices [15,16]. While effective for small molecules, these systems are not optimally suited for macro therapeutic biomolecules (e.g. viral vectors), which are readily immobilized or inactivated within tight polymer networks and subsequently cleared by the immune system [17]. As a result, biomaterial platforms capable of spatially confining AAV vectors without compromising vector bioactivity remain limited.

To overcome these limitations, crosslinker-free polyelectrolyte complex (PEC)-based biomaterials have emerged as promising platforms for surface-mediated gene delivery, enabling AAV immobilization without the need for chemical modification or additional coating steps [18]. For instance, heparin-based PECs engineered into fiber-like structures have been shown to immobilize AAV without additional coatings and to induce localized gene expression exclusively at the cell–material interface [19,20]. To address the lack of tissue adhesion, polyphenol-based surface chemistries—particularly catechol and gallol moieties—represent a promising strategy [[21], [22], [23]]. Gallol-containing polymers exhibit robust wet-tissue adhesion through synergistic hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, and metal-coordination mechanisms, and beyond adhesion, they possess the ability to accumulate proteins and viral particles on material surfaces via multivalent, reversible interactions—a property previously reported in gallol-rich hydrogels and ECM-mimetic bioinks that retain biomolecules even against concentration gradients [23,24]. Leveraging these bio-adhesive properties of polyphenols, surface immobilization of AAV onto material surfaces has been demonstrated to produce spatially confined gene expression.

However, AAV immobilization strategies relying solely on polyphenol coatings—without PEC scaffolding—are inherently limited in their ability to form free-standing film structures, and when applied as coatings onto other substrates, challenges remain in achieving uniform coverage and long-term stability. In contrast, a film-based PEC material that incorporates polyphenol chemistry to confer tissue adhesion can not only serve as an intrinsic AAV carrier, but also provides superior mechanical properties and elasticity, enabling uniform, large-area therapeutic delivery while preserving localization and minimizing systemic leakage.

In this study, we synthesized Chi-G, a gallol-functionalized chitosan derivative that integrates these adhesive and biomolecule-retentive properties into a polycationic backbone suited for PEC film formation. These characteristics make Chi-G uniquely suited for stabilizing large therapeutics—such as AAV vectors—at the material interface without the need for chemical crosslinkers or additional surface coatings. Building upon these properties, we developed a thin, elastic PEC film by pairing positively charged Chi-G with negatively charged alginate [25]. Among natural polysaccharides, chitosan and alginate spontaneously form stable PECs with excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, and the resulting film functions as a mechanically stable yet ultimately self-degradable constructs, avoiding the long-term persistence associated with covalently crosslinked systems [26]. While Chi-G provides adhesive functionality and molecular affinity for AAV retention, alginate contributes structural support and prevents nonspecific tissue binding, allowing the composite film to maintain intimate contact with complex anatomical surfaces while confining therapeutic agents to the intended region. Importantly, the nanoporous architecture of the resulting film permits passive diffusion of oxygen and small nutrients across the film interface, ensuring that covered tissue remains viable during the delivery period.

By combining crosslinker-free PEC assembly, gallol-mediated bioadhesion, biomolecule stabilization, we developed the WRAP (Writable, Retentive, Adhesive, and Permeable) system. WRAP addresses the key limitations of existing surface-immobilization approaches—namely limited adhesion and restricted treatment area—while preserving the localization benefits of PEC-based delivery. As demonstrated using AAV vectors, WRAP enables spatially precise, surface-patterned transduction with minimal systemic escape, offering a scalable and biocompatible strategy for precision gene therapy applications.

Materials & methods

Synthesis of Chi-G

Chi-G was synthesized using a previously described method [27,28]. In brief, 2 g of chitosan (Chi; Mw 100 kDa; 80% deacetylated, Wako, Japan) and 1.3 g of hydroxybenzotriazole (HOBt, Sigma-Aldrich, MA, USA) were fully dissolved in 200 mL of deionized distilled water (DDW) and ethanol as a co-solvent (25% v/v). Subsequently, 1 g of gallic acid (Sigma-Aldrich) and 1.8 g of 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC, TCI, Tokyo, Japan) were dissolved in 10 mL of DDW and ethanol (50% v/v) and slowly added to the chitosan/HOBt solution under gentle agitation. The Chi/HOBt/EDC solution was gently stirred for 16 h at room temperature. The Chi-G was then dialyzed (MWCO = 3500 Da; SpectraPor, Rancho Dominguez, CA, USA) for 2 days against a 100 mM sodium chloride solution and for an additional 2 days against DDW, lyophilized, and stored at −80 °C until use.

Fabrication of the PEC WRAP film

The WRAP (transparent, thin, and bio-adhesiveness) films were fabricated through PEC of Chi-G and alginate. In brief, alginate solution (10 mg/mL in DDW, 194-13325, Fujifilm Wako, Japan) was transferred to a 55−mm Petri dish and dried at room temperature. Subsequently, the Chi-G solution (2 mg/mL in DDW) was applied to the alginate film and rapidly dried at 60 °C. A PEC was formed at the interfaces between alginate and Chi-G. The residual polymer was removed by washing twice with PBS and sterilized with ultraviolet (UV) light in a biosafety cabinet before in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Morphological characterization of the WRAP film

The morphology, structure, and cross-sectional energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping of the WRAP film were observed utilizing a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; JSM-7601F-Plus, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. The transparency of WRAP films was assessed by measuring UV-visible light transmittance from 200 to 800 nm using a UV/Vis spectrophotometer (V-650, JASCO, Tokyo, Japan).

Mechanical characterization of WRAP films

A rotational rheometer (RheoStress 1, HAAKE, Karlsruhe, Germany) with a 20-mm parallel plate geometry was used to evaluate the structural integrity of the film composed of two water-soluble polymers. Frequency sweep measurements (0.1 to 10 Hz) were performed to determine the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) of the hydrated WRAP film at 25 °C with 1% strain amplitude.

Retention, adhesion strength and permeability test

To evaluate the retention and bio-adhesiveness capability of WRAP on wet tissue surfaces, a phenol red was applied onto freshly excised porcine skin in a Y-shaped pattern using a pipette tip. The tissue was then submerged in PBS at room temperature for 30 min, after which photographs were taken to assess pattern retention.

The retention of patterned GFP protein on the WRAP film was live monitored with a Xenogen in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). GFP patterned in a Y-shaped configuration on the WRAP film was allowed to diffuse into PBS, facilitated by the alginate supporting layer, and fluorescence intensity was measured at 0, and 2h.

Adhesiveness was assessed via tensile testing using a universal testing machine (UTM; MultiTest-i, Mecmesin, UK). Mouse skin (10 mm × 10 mm) was prepared, dried, and affixed to a polyethylene terephthalate (PET) film using double-sided tape. Similarly, the WRAP film (10 mm × 10 mm) was mounted on a separate PET film. The two substrates were brought into contact, and the PET terminal affixed to the tissue was gripped using the UTM holder. The samples were pulled apart at a 19-mm/min rate to assess interfacial adhesion. Furthermore, to evaluate tissue adhesiveness with visual confirmation, a WRAP film was applied onto the surface of mouse liver. The tissue was gently lifted using forceps, and the adhesion of the WRAP film to the tissue surface was visually confirmed by photograph.

To measure oxygen permeability, O2 penetration through the WRAP system was assessed by measuring UV-vis absorbance at 450 nm at 5, 30 min, 1, 2, 3, 6, 14 h, 1, 3 4, 5, and 6 d.

Cell culture

Three different cell types, including human cervical cancer cells (HeLa, CCL-2, ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), AAV293 cells (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA, USA), and HEK293T (CRL-3216, ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), were used in this study. The AAV293 and HEK293T cells were grown in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (10-013-CV, DMEM; Corning Cellgro, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (35-015-CV, FBS; Corning Cellgro) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified cell incubator containing 5% CO2. HeLa were grown in Minimum Essential Medium Eagle (LM007-01, MEM, Welgene, Gyeongsan, Korea) containing 10% FBS (0025, ScienCell) 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified chamber with 5% CO2.

Release kinetics of AAV

To characterize the release kinetics of AAV2-CMV-GFP and AAV9-CMV-GFP from the WRAP system, AAV solution (1 × 1013 vg/mL) was loaded onto the alginate film and WRAP system (4 μL/cm2). The droplet was air-dried to promote vector retention via electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions for a few minutes. Release studies were conducted by immersing 1 cm2 AAV9-loaded films (Chi-G side facing medium) in PBS containing 0.1% BSA at 37 °C. Release medium was collected and replaced at predetermined time points (2, 30 m, 2, 6, 12 h, 1, 2, and 3 d). Cumulative release genomic copies of AAV was calculated relative to the total loaded dose and compared between AAV2 and AAV9 to assess serotype-dependent differences in WRAP-mediated retention. Viral genomic copies were quantified by qPCR targeting the CMV transgene (forward primer: 5′−ACTACAACAGCCACAACGTCTATATCA−3′, reverse primer: 5′−GGCGGATCTTGAAGTTCACC−3′). To evaluate whether WRAP-mediated immobilization preserves AAV infectious bioactivity, the 30-min release fraction collected from WRAP films loaded with AAV2-CMV-GFP or AAV9-CMV-GFP was applied to HEK293T cells and analyzed by flow cytometry (Guava easyCyte, Cytek, CA, USA) at post-transduction day 2.

Writable, retentive, and penetration ability in vitro test

Bio-adhesive properties and localized gene delivery efficiency were evaluated using AAV2-CMV-GFP delivery with HEK293T cells. An adherent cell line (HEK293T, 80% confluency) was plated in 6-well plates. AAV vectors were applied to the WRAP film placed over the cell layer. After 48h, GFP-positive cells were imaged by fluorescence microscopy (IX73, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and transduction efficiency was quantified based on GFP expression (n = 4 per condition). For comparison, HeLa cells (20,000 cells/well) were incubated with AAV9-CMV-GFP vectors (multiplicity of infection = 20,000) in either WRAP film-mediated or direct medium application and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined by nuclear staining with Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and live/dead discrimination using fluorescein diacetate (FDA; Sigma-Aldrich) and propidium iodide (PI; Sigma-Aldrich) (n = 4 per condition). The viability of HeLa cells growing under WRAP film was determined using a WST-8 cell viability assay kit (Biomax, Seoul, Korea) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

In vivo AAV gene delivery

C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old, male, Orient Bio, Seongnam, Republic of Korea) were anesthetized with tribromoethanol (Merck, T48402-25G) solution (250 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection) and fur on the ventral area was carefully removed. Localized AAV delivery using the WRAP-AAV delivery system was investigated via the red fluorescent reporter gene expression. UV-sterilized WRAP films were placed, and purified AAV9-E2-Crimson (1 × 1011 vg) droplets were applied to the surface. The WRAP-AAV system was immediately wrapped around the pancreas to enable localized AAV delivery with complete sealing. Diluted AAV9-E2-Crimson solution (1 × 1011 vg, 10 μL) was administered via tail vein injection or directly injected into the pancreas for the control group.

For subcutaneous delivery, fur on dorsal side was removed. equivalent free AAV2-E2-Crimson were implanted into the dorsal subcutaneous space through a small incision, which was subsequently closed with sutures. At day 28, fluorescence imaging was performed using an IVIS imaging system. To spatially confirm the anatomical origin of the observed signal, gross fluorescence imaging was additionally performed ex vivo immediately following skin incision and exposure of the implantation site.

To vascular delivery, The thoracic cavity was opened to expose the ascending aorta near the carotid arteries. WRAP film and AAV (AAVp2CV, 5 × 109 vg, [29]) were applied to the aortic arch, while systemic administration groups received equivalent dose (2 × 1010 vg) or high dose (5 × 1011 vg) AAV via tail vein injection. These groups were established to evaluate off-target AAV biodistribution [29] and systemic toxicity after 21 days. Postoperatively, ketoprofen (Unibiotech, UNI-KETOPRO) was subcutaneously injected for 7 days to alleviate pain in WRAP group [[30], [31], [32]]. All experimental procedures for treating animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Yonsei University College of Medicine (IACUC-A-202409-1905-02).

In vivo safety profile of AAV or WRAP−AAV

Serum was obtained by collecting orbital blood samples and centrifuging them at 3000 rpm for 15 min. The collected serum that was analyzed using a serum multiple biochemical analyzer (DRI-CHEM4000i; FUJIFILM, Japan) and the levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT; 3250, FUJIFILM), aspartate aminotransferase (AST; 3150, FUJIFILM), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN; 1150, FUJIFILM) was measured according to the manufacturer's instructions.

To assess systemic humoral immune responses following AAV delivery, blood was collected at day 28 post-administration. Total anti-AAV9 antibody levels were quantified by ELISA (PAV-A009, ACRO Biosystems) at a 1:2000 serum dilution. Transduction efficiency was quantified by flow cytometry at 48 h post-transduction and expressed as relative infectivity normalized to a serum-free control.

Hematoxylin & eosin staining

Harvested samples were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P6148, Sigma-Aldrich) overnight at 4 °C, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm. Hematoxylin (S330930-2, Agilent) and eosin (E6003, Sigma) staining was performed according to the manufacturers' protocols for histological evaluation.

Immunohistochemistry

The immunohistochemistry protocol followed previous study about AAV-GFP delivery to local tissue [33]. The aorta was clearing (BRTC-002, Tissue Clearing kit, Binaree) and mounted (HRMO-006, Tissue Clearing Mounting Solution, Binaree). The primary antibodies used for this study can be summarized as follows: a goat polyclonal antibody for GFP (1:900 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After vigorous rinsing with PBS-T, the primary antibody-positive cells were visually amplified by incubation with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 day, donkey anti-goat Alexa Fluor 488 (1:2000; Invitrogen). The nuclei of the cells were stained with DAPI. The fluorescence images of all stained cells were acquired using a fluorescence confocal microscope (LSM700, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections (4 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in EDTA-based buffer (pH 9.0) at 100 °C for 15min. Sections were blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against F4/80 (30325S; Cell Signaling, 1:800), iNOS (ab178945; Abcam, 1:800), and CD206 (24595S; Cell Signaling, 1:800), marking total macrophages, M1, and M2 macrophage populations, respectively. Signals were detected using the Bond Polymer Refine Detection Kit (DS9800, Leica) and visualized with DAB chromogen. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Stained sections were dehydrated, mounted, and analyzed under a light microscope.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and the statistical significance was examined via one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's or Dunnett's post hoc test, as appropriate using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1) and SPSS 25.0 software (IBM Corporation, Somers, NY, USA.)

The schemes were created with BioRender.com.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and key characteristics of the WRAP system

WRAP films are a PEC fabricated through electrostatic interaction between the cationic Chi-G and the anionic alginate. This assembly forms a bilayer-like gradient structure composed of a thin, compact Chi-G–rich surface layer and a thicker, porous alginate support layer (Fig. 1A). Cross-sectional SEM imaging combined with EDS elemental mapping confirmed this structural organization: the nitrogen signal originating from Chi-G was confined predominantly to a ∼10 μm surface region, whereas the underlying alginate layer extended to approximately 100 μm and exhibited a highly porous morphology (Fig. 1B). To introduce adhesive functionality and gallol-mediated crosslinking capacity into the PEC system, the Chi-G used to construct these films was synthesized by conjugating gallic acid to chitosan via EDC/HOBt chemistry, yielding a gallol substitution degree of 4.9% and 5.1%. Conjugation was validated through FT-IR, 1H NMR, and UV–vis analyses, confirming successful incorporation of gallol groups required for subsequent PEC formation (Fig S1 A-C).

This two-layer gradient structure gives rise to four functional properties central to the WRAP platform. First, the compact Chi-G–rich surface provides a “Writable” interface capable of retaining diverse biomolecules [34], while the underlying alginate layer forms a thicker, mechanically supportive structure that reinforces the overall strength of the film. In addition, we have shown that Chi-G interacts with proteins and other biomacromolecules via electrostatic and gallol-mediated hydrogen-bond interactions, while also providing strong material adhesion that reinforces the film's mechanical robustness [21,28,35]. In Fig. 1C-F, these structural and functional characteristics establish WRAP as a writable, retentive, adhesive, and permeable film platform suitable for localized therapeutic delivery. The bio-adhesive property of Chi-G enabled stable attachment of the WRAP film to the humid liver surface ex vivo (Fig. 1G, top). Furthermore, a patterned payload deposited onto the WRAP surface was retained although following submersion in aqueous conditions. These findings demonstrate that the gallol-mediated adhesion and physical film encapsulation collectively maintain cargo confinement under physiologically relevant wet environments (Fig. 1G, bottom). The PEC formed between alginate and Chi-G confers mechanical flexibility under wet conditions, maintaining film integrity through electrostatic interaction (Fig. 1H). Beyond its bio-adhesive and retentive properties, the optical transparency of WRAP allows intraoperative visualization, allowing operators to confirm target site positioning during application (Fig. 1I).

Writable and spatially patterned drug delivery enabled by the WRAP system

One of the distinctive features of the WRAP system is its ability to support spatially patterned deposition of therapeutic molecules. The compact Chi-G–rich surface provides a stable interface capable of retaining biomolecules through a combination of electrostatic interactions and gallol-mediated hydrogen bonding, while the underlying alginate layer offers mechanical support to maintain the pattern until the film contacts the target tissue. The strong associative interactions between Chi-G and biomolecules are consistent with earlier reports demonstrating that Chi-G can stably bind therapeutic proteins and small molecules through its polyphenolic moieties [22,28,36].

We next tested whether this writability could be extended to AAV vectors for spatially restricted gene delivery. AAV9-CMV-GFP solution (1 × 1012 vg/mL) was applied to the WRAP surface as parallel line patterns spaced at 1 mm intervals. The patterned film was then placed film-side down onto adherent HEK293T cells and incubated for 48 h without additional transduction reagents (Fig. 2A). This configuration ensures that vector release is physically confined to regions directly contacting the patterned surface, similar to previously reported Chi-based biomaterial systems that support restricted release via hydrogen-bond interactions [28,37,38].

Fluorescence imaging revealed that GFP expression occurred exclusively beneath the patterned AAV lines, with minimal background signal outside these regions (Fig. 2B). Line-scan analysis using ImageJ showed distinct peaks corresponding to each patterned line, confirming that AAV release and subsequent transduction were spatially restricted by the WRAP film. The spatial confinement of gene expression aligns with earlier findings that Chi-G based materials can retain AAV on their surfaces and release vectors only upon close contact with target cells.

To evaluate the retention stability of patterned biomolecules, GFP protein was applied onto the Chi-G surface in a “Y”-shaped pattern, and the film was agitated in PBS (100 rpm, 37 °C) to mimic dynamic aqueous environments. Even after 2 h of agitation, the GFP pattern on the WRAP film remained sharply defined, whereas an identical pattern applied to an alginate-only film completely dissipated under the same conditions (Fig. 2C). Quantification of fluorescence intensity confirmed that 40.67 ± 12.01% of the original signal was retained on the WRAP surface, compared to 1.48 ± 0.43% on the alginate film (Fig. 2D), demonstrating that the Chi-G layer effectively restricts diffusion and stabilize surface-deposited molecules through hydrogen bond-mediated interactions. Although both Chi-G and alginate are individually soluble in aqueous media, their PEC structure allows the WRAP film to maintain integrity for sufficient time under physiologically relevant conditions.

To evaluate the drug reservoir function of the WRAP film, in vitro release kinetics of both AAV2-CMV-GFP and AAV9-CMV-GFP were characterized and compared with alginate-only films. Wild type AAV capsids interact with distinct cell-surface receptors in a serotype-dependent manner: AAV2 engages heparan sulfate proteoglycan (HSPG) to mediated endocytosis [39], whereas AAV9 utilize galactose as its primary receptor [40]. To confirm that AAV bioactivity and AAV capsid independency in utilize of WRAP system, is preserved following immobilization and release, the transduction capability of WRAP-released AAV was compared with that of non-immobilized AAV.

Alginate films exhibited rapid, uncontrolled AAV release due to their dissolution in physiological media and weak non-specific adsorption of AAV to the alginate surface. The transduction efficiency of 30 min WRAP released fraction was comparable to that of non-immobilized AAV for both AAV2 and AAV9 (Fig. 2E), confirming that WRAP immobilization preserves AAV capsid infectivity. In contrast, the WRAP system demonstrated controlled release, with only 8.20 ± 2.45% of AAV2 and 11.5 ± 5.89% of AAV9 released at 2 min, reaching cumulative releases of 50.75 ± 7.48% and 53.78 ± 18.90% by 3 days, respectively (Fig. 2F). The comparable release profiles across both serotypes suggest serotype-independent retention by WRAP. Structural studies have shown that AAV capsids present a heterogeneous surface composed of polar, charged, and hydrophobic residues [41]. As both AAV2 and AAV9 exhibited similar release profiles in the WRAP system, the interaction is unlikely to be mediated by serotype-specific receptor-binding domains. Instead, we speculate that nonspecific multivalent hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions between gallol groups of Chi-G and exposed AAV capsid surface residues, together with physical retention within the hydrogel network, contribute to AAV immobilization and sustained release. These findings support the general applicability of the WRAP system across different AAV serotypes. The controlled release is attributed to hydrogen bonding interactions between AAV vectors and gallol groups of Chi-G, which restrict vector diffusion. Unlike traditional alginate hydrogels cross-linked with divalent cations that exhibit unpredictable degradation, chitosan-alginate mediated PEC-based film demonstrates controlled degradation with ∼75% weight loss over 30 days [42], maintaining scaffold integrity throughout the transduction period.

Together, these results demonstrate that WRAP enables stable, writable, and spatially controlled deposition of therapeutic agents including small molecules and AAV, supporting its application as a platform for patterned and localized gene delivery.

Retentive drug delivery and adhesiveness of WRAP film

Retentive drug delivery strategies have been extensively investigated in drug delivery systems to achieve prolonged residence time and sustained release at target sites, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy and reducing systemic side effects [43,44]. In the WRAP system, retentive AAV delivery to the target site significantly improved gene transfer efficiency in HeLa cells using AAV9-CMV-GFP (Fig. 3A and B). The schematic in Fig. 3A illustrates the experimental setup, where WRAP film loaded with AAV vectors were positioned directly above cell monolayers, creating a localized high-concentration reservoir.

Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the AAV + WRAP group (96.13 ± 2.05%) exhibited a 5.7-fold increase in GFP-positive cells compared to the virus-only group (16.85 ± 1.55%) (Fig. 3C). This dramatic enhancement in transduction efficiency is attributed to the shortened diffusion distance between the AAV reservoir (WRAP surface) and target cells, maximizing vector-cell contact while minimizing viral dispersion into the surrounding medium. The enhanced transduction efficiency observed in the WRAP + AAV group is partly attributable to the increased local vector concentration at the cell-material interface relative to free AAV dispersed in bulk medium. Furthermore, the reduced path length between the WRAP surface and the underlying cell monolayer is expected to increase vector-cell collision frequency [45,46], collectively contributing to the superior transduction efficiency of the WRAP system. To delineate the contribution of vector concentration from the delivery mechanism itself, a dose-escalation experiment was performed using free AAV at 1-, 10-, and 20-fold the AAV dose with WRAP (MOI 50,000) (Fig. S2). WRAP + AAV at standard dose achieved transduction efficiency comparable to 20-fold free AAV (ns), suggesting that spatial confinement within WRAP effectively concentrates vector-cell interactions, enabling dose-efficient transduction equivalent to a 20-fold higher systemic dose. This reverse substrate-mediated delivery mechanism represents a key advantage for applications requiring high local concentrations while avoiding systemic exposure [17,33].

Adhesiveness of the WRAP system originates from the multiple hydroxyl groups of Chi-G, which form hydrogen bonds between the WRAP and wet tissue surfaces. Chi-G is known as improved bio-adhesiveness due to multiple hydroxyl group [21,27,38]. Therefore, the Chi-G exposed WRAP film demonstrated stable bio-adhesive properties under dynamic underwater shear stress conditions via gallol functional groups [38]. Robust tissue adhesion is critical for maintaining stable contact between drug delivery platforms and target organs, particularly in wet physiological environments. Tensile testing was conducted to quantitatively assess the adhesive strength of the WRAP system (Fig. 3D). The universal testing machine (UTM) setup, where WRAP or alginate films were brought into contact with mouse skin under controlled conditions, then subjected to tensile separation. WRAP films demonstrated significantly improved tissue adhesive strength on mouse skin compared to alginate controls (Fig. 3D and E). The measured strength of WRAP (2.80 ± 0.67 MPa, n = 3) was markedly higher than that of the alginate film (0.52 ± 0.14 MPa, n = 5, ∗∗∗, p < 0.001), representing a 5.4-fold enhancement. Representative force-displacement profile (Fig. 3E). WRAP films exhibited higher peak force and more gradual detachment compared to alginate, indicating robust interfacial bonding even under hydrated conditions. This superior adhesion is attributed to the gallol-mediated adhesion mechanism of Chi-G, where multiple hydroxyl groups form extensive hydrogen bonding networks with tissue surfaces [21,27]. The strong tissues adhesiveness of WRAP facilitates stable, retentive localized delivery and prolongs drug retention at target sites.

Permeability and biocompatibility of WRAP system

Although the WRAP system provides sealing and retentive properties necessary for localized drug delivery, it must simultaneously permit the passage of gases and small nutrients to avoid tissue or cellular hypoxia [20]. To assess the oxygen permeability of the WRAP film, we employed a pyrogallol-based assay in which dissolved O2 oxidizes pyrogallol to a chromogenic product with absorbance at 450 nm [28]. As shown in Fig. 4A, the WRAP-covered group exhibited a progressive increase in pyrogallol oxidation, reaching absorbance values of approximately ∼2.5–3.0 at 8000 min, closely approaching the levels observed in the open control (∼3.2–3.5). In contrast, samples sealed with parafilm showed markedly attenuated oxidation, with absorbance remaining below 0.5 over the same period. These results confirm that WRAP functions as a semi-permeable membrane capable of supporting substantial O2 diffusion while still providing a physical barrier of the underlying tissue or cells. We next examined whether this permeability was adequate to sustain cellular viability. HeLa cells cultured beneath WRAP films remained viable at levels comparable to those under standard tissue-culture conditions, as confirmed by FDA/PI staining (Fig. 4B). No significant increase in dead cells was observed, and CCK-8 assay confirmed that HeLa cell viability was not statistically different between with WRAP and without WRAP conditions, indicating that nutrient and oxygen exchange was not impaired by film coverage (Fig. 4C). This behavior is consistent with properties commonly observed in nanoporous biomaterials [14], where the pore architecture allows permeation of gases and small solutes while restricting the passage of larger macromolecules. Given that the Chi-G/alginate PEC forms a similar nanoporous network, it is reasonable to attribute the WRAP film's selective permeability to the intrinsic structural characteristics.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the WRAP film provides retentive coverage without compromising the mass transport of essential gases and nutrients, supporting its suitability for in vivo localized drug-delivery applications.

The writable and retentive properties of the WRAP system enable improved spatially patterned and localized gene delivery in vivo

To evaluate in vivo writability, WRAP films, polycaprolactone–AAV (PCL–AAV), and free AAV2 vectors encoding luciferase were implanted subcutaneously on the dorsal surface of mice (Fig. 5A). The PCL–AAV group, which contained nonspecifically adsorbed AAV2, served as a representative biomaterial-based gene delivery control capable of inducing localized expression. At early time points (days 7 and 14), the WRAP and PCL–AAV groups showed significantly different levels of luciferase expression (Fig. 5B and C), whereas this difference diminished by days 28 and 35.

(C) WRAP-AAV exhibited significantly higher luciferase expression at early time points relative to PCL-AAV and free AAV controls. (D–E) Localized pancreatic gene delivery. AAV9-E2-Crimson (1 × 1011 vg)-loaded WRAP films were wrapped around the pancreas and evaluated at 4 weeks. To further confirm the anatomical specificity of the observed fluorescence signal, gross fluorescence imaging was performed following skin exposure of the dorsal subcutaneous implantation site at day 28 using AAV2-E2-crimson (Fig. S3). E2-crimson fluorescence was localized to the WRAP + AAV implantation site, whereas the free-AAV group showed negligible signal, providing direct spatial confirmation that transgene expression corresponds precisely to the intended anatomical location.

In addition, to evaluate tissue-material interaction at the histological level, H&E staining was performed at day 0 and day 28 following subcutaneous implantation. At day 28, direct visualization of the WRAP film was limited due to partial degradation and loss of the transparent film during histological processing. At day 0, a structure presumed to be the WRAP film was observed adjacent to the underlying muscle tissue, consistent with gallol-mediated adhesion of the Chi-G surface to the target site (Fig. S4). Nevertheless, precise delineation of the film was challenging because of its transparent nature.

Total anti-AAV9 IgG was comparable between WRAP + AAV and I.V. groups (Fig. S5). Although WRAP-mediated localized delivery effectively restricted off-target biodistribution and minimized systemic organ exposure, comparable anti-AAV9 antibody and neutralizing antibody titers were nonetheless observed in both WRAP and I.V. groups at 4 weeks post-administration. This is consistent with the well-established immunogenicity of AAV capsids, wherein local antigen-presenting cells (APCs) at the implantation site migrate to draining lymph nodes and prime systemic humoral immune responses independently of the delivery route [47]. Notably, the dose employed in this study (2 × 1010 vg) is substantially below the threshold required for effective systemic gene delivery via intravenous administration, yet was sufficient to induce a humoral immune response upon AAV capsid exposure. Importantly, this dose falls within the range previously reported to trigger humoral immunity following local AAV administration [48]. Such route-independent immunogenic saturation has been similarly reported in other locally administered rAAV gene therapy studies, suggesting that circumventing AAV capsid immunogenicity will require orthogonal strategies such as transient immunosuppression or capsid engineering, rather than delivery route modification alone [49].

Chitosan has been reported to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and induce pro-inflammatory cytokine production in macrophages [50]. Therefore, macrophage recruitment was expected following implantation of the WRAP system. Fig. S5 presents representative immunohistochemistry images from the wild type mouse (No treatment) and WRAP-AAV (WRAP) delivered groups. Comparable iNOS staining (M1 macrophage marker) was observed between groups, whereas F4/80 (pan-macrophage marker) and CD206 (M2 macrophage marker) staining appeared more prominent in the WRAP group.

Notably, WRAP films preserved the initial “Y-shaped’’ spatial pattern throughout the later stages, consistent with the ability of the Chi-G–rich surface to restrain lateral diffusion of deposited vectors. To further examine in vivo retention, WRAP-based AAV delivery was tested in the pancreas (Fig. 5D). AAV9–E2-Crimson vectors (1 × 1011 vg) were applied to the WRAP film, which was then wrapped tightly around the pancreas. The elasticity, transparency, and bio-adhesive nature of WRAP enabled complete conformal contact with the irregular pancreatic surface. Four weeks later, IVIS imaging revealed only weak E2-Crimson signals following direct intrapancreatic injection (Fig. S6). The WRAP-AAV group showed improved localized delivery with reduced off-target distribution compared to the intrapancreatic injection group. Furthermore, the WRAP-AAV group demonstrated enhanced gene expression in the pancreas.

In contrast, the WRAP–AAV group exhibited approximately 5.19 ± 1.52-fold higher transgene expression compared with the negative control (Fig. 5E). This enhancement indicates that WRAP effectively retains viral vectors at the delivery site and that the sealed interface minimizes diffusion distance, thereby improving gene transfer efficiency. Frozen sections further confirmed strong, localized E2-Crimson expression within the WRAP-treated pancreas (Fig. 5D and E). These findings suggest that the hydrogen-bond–mediated molecular confinement provided by the Chi-G layer supports writable and retentive gene delivery. Additionally, H&E staining of tissues surrounding the implanted WRAP film showed only mild cellular infiltration (Fig. S7), indicating favorable biocompatibility and the potential of WRAP as a scaffold for localized drug delivery applications.

Off-target analysis and safety evaluation of WRAP-AAV gene delivery in vivo

To evaluate the safety profile and potential off-target effects of WRAP-AAV compared with systemic administration, we applied the system to the aortic arch as a representative vascular target (Fig. 6A). The flexibility of the WRAP system enabled conformal wrapping and stable adhesion for 3 weeks post-delivery, with no non-specific adhesion observed on the alginate backing side (Fig. 6B) [51]. Immunohistochemistry confirmed localized GFP expression concentrated at the whole aorta tissue (Fig. 6C), with minimal signal detected in off-target organs. Serum biochemistry markers including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), indicators of hepatic function, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), a marker of renal function, in WRAP-AAV-treated mice remained within normal ranges at 21 days post-surgery (Fig. 6D), comparable to the no-treatment control.

Scale bar:1 mm (D) Biodistribution analysis demonstrating minimal off-target delivery. Biodistribution analysis revealed substantially higher viral genome copy numbers at the target site, with the aorta showing 107- to 604-fold enrichment over major organs (liver: 107-fold; heart: 132-fold; kidney: 244-fold; spleen: 413-fold; lung: 604-fold) when normalized to organ baseline levels (Fig. 6E). In contrast, systemic administration at an equivalent dose (2 × 1010 vg, I.V.) resulted in negligible AAV accumulation at the target aortic site, with viral genome copies remaining comparable to off-target organs (liver: 0.42-fold; heart: 0.64-fold; kidney: 0.70-fold; spleen: 0.70-fold; lung: 1.62-fold) demonstrating the limited ability of conventional intravenous delivery to achieve site-specific vector concentration under these conditions. Even at a 25-fold higher dose (5 × 1011 vg, I.V.), systemic administration failed to achieve preferential target site enrichment, with viral genomes distributed broadly across multiple organs (liver: 0.30-fold; heart: 1.10-fold; kidney: 0.59-fold; spleen: 0.32-fold; lung: 2.27-fold). Furthermore, when target-site specificity was assessed relative to liver accumulation, the WRAP system demonstrated a 107-fold higher target-to-liver ratio, whereas intravenous administration yielded ratios of only 0.30 and 0.42 at high and low doses, respectively. These findings should not be interpreted as an intrinsic inability of AAV to transduce aortic tissue, but rather reflect the pharmacokinetic limitations of systemic delivery — wherein the majority of administered vector is sequestered by the liver and cleared prior to reaching the target site — highlighting the advantage of WRAP as a localized delivery platform that bypasses systemic clearance entirely. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that WRAP-mediated local delivery achieves substantially greater target-site specificity than systemic administration, even following dose escalation, while markedly reducing off-target vector distribution. These characteristics represent an important advantage for localized gene therapy applications. This exceptional tissue selectivity demonstrates that WRAP-mediated delivery confines AAV vectors to the application site while minimizing systemic exposure. Notably, minimal GFP was detected in the liver—the primary site of AAV clearance and a major concern for systemic toxicity—as well as in the heart and lungs, despite their anatomical proximity to the delivery site.

To contextualize the translational potential of WRAP, we compared its design features with B-VEC (Beremagene geperpavec, VYJUVEK®), the only FDA-approved biomaterial-assisted gene therapy product to date (Table S1) [52]. While B-VEC employs an HPMC excipient gel as a carrier for HSV-1-based vectors, it lacks active tissue adhesion and relies on repeated weekly topical application to maintain therapeutic effect, as the liquid formulation is rapidly cleared from the wound surface. In contrast, WRAP achieves gallol-mediated wet adhesion to tissue surfaces, enabling sustained local vector retention through physical film encapsulation without the need for repeated dosing. Together, these distinctions highlight WRAP as a next-generation localized gene delivery platform that addresses key limitations of current clinical approaches, including insufficient tissue adhesion, repeated dosing burden, and restricted cargo compatibility.

Conclusions

This study establishes WRAP as a localized drug delivery system that directly addresses dose- and distribution-related limitations in current gene therapy. The film is based on a chitosan–alginate PEC in which a gallol-functionalized Chi-G surface is combined with a mechanically supportive alginate layer. As a result, WRAP integrates four coordinated properties: a writable surface for spatially defined loading, retentive confinement of therapeutic agents, adhesion to wet tissue surface, and semi-permeability that preserves gas and nutrient exchange.

Using AAV vectors, we showed that WRAP markedly enhances local transgene expression while limiting off-target delivery in subcutaneous, pancreatic, and vascular applications. These findings highlight that physical confinement of vectors at the tissue surface can improve efficacy within a narrower and safer dosing range, an issue of relevance given recent reports of dose-limiting toxicities with high-dose systemic AAV administration. While WRAP effectively minimized systemic vector exposure, humoral immune responses against AAV capsids were observed regardless of delivery route, suggesting that capsid engineering or immunosuppressive strategies remain necessary complements to localized delivery.

The modular nature of the WRAP formulation suggests that the approach is not restricted to AAV. In future works, by adjusting polymer composition, layer thickness, and loading conditions, the same platform could be adapted to deliver small molecules, proteins, or nucleic acid therapeutics that benefit from high local concentration and prolonged residence time. Future studies should systematically optimize release profiles for specific payloads, evaluate long-term biocompatibility and degradation in disease-relevant models, and address manufacturing and handling parameters required for clinical use. Overall, WRAP illustrates how combining writability, retention, adhesion, and permeability in a single material system can provide a practical route toward safer and more precise local therapies.
