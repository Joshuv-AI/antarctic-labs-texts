Integrated transcriptomic, metabolomic and lipidomic analyses uncover the crucial roles of lipid metabolism pathways in oat (Avena sativa) responses to heat stress
Authors: Yan Sun, Haiqing Jing, Zhanqian Li, Kun Wan, Juhua Ma, Haiping Zhang, Jinai Xue, Runzhi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOat (Avena sativa), an economically important cereal crop globally, is highly vulnerable to high-temperature stress, challenging its geographic distribution and grain production. However, the mechanisms underlying oat’s response to heat stress remain pooly understood.ResultsA time-course transcriptome revealed significant enrichment in lipid metabolism pathways during heat stress, which was corroborated by metabolomic findings. Integrated co-expression network analysis and KEGG enrichment further underscored the critical role of lipid metabolism in oat’s adaptive response to heat stress. Comprehensive lipidomic profiling of heat-stressed oat seedlings demonstrated a substantial increase in the proportion of neutral lipids, suggesting an evolutionarily conserved protective strategy. Synergistic transcriptional responses indicated that heat-induced triacylglycerol (TAG) accumulation primarily originated from extensive membrane lipid turnover rather than de novo fatty acid (FA) synthesis, with the Kennedy pathway serving as the dominant route for TAG production. Enhanced phospholipid hydrolysis, acyl editing, and endoplasmic reticulum-localized FA desaturation collectively contributed to TAG enrichment in polyunsaturated FAs. Additionally, elevated levels of phosphatidylglycerol (PG) and phosphatidylinositol (PI) in oat may confer adaptive benefits under heat stress.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that lipid metabolism critically regulates heat stress response in oat. The findings provide valuable target genes for genetic improvement in enhancing oat thermotolerance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11972-5.

Introduction

Oat (Avena sativa), ranking as the seventh most important cereal crop globally, is valued as a functional food due to its unique nutritional profile and health benefits [1, 2]. It also serves as a high-quality forage grass, offering advantages such as high biomass, good palatability, perennial growth, and simplified cultivation requirements [3]. Since oat grows best in a cool climatic condition during its entire growth, it is particularly susceptible to high temperature, posing a significant threat to its productivity [4, 5]. Currently, global climate change has intensified the frequency of extreme heat events, potentially restricting oat’s geographic distribution and compromising yield and quality, thereby incurring substantial economic losses [6]. Consequently, developing heat-tolerant oat varieties will be likely prioritized in future agricultural production.

Understanding oat’s molecular response to heat stress is essential for devising genetic engineering strategies to enhance thermotolerance. While research has historically emphasized oat’s physicochemical properties and cropping practices [7, 8], the molecular mechanisms underlying its heat stress response remain largely unexplored. Upon heat stress, plants are not passively damaged but can force metabolic adjustments, including reprogramming multiple resistance-related genes and metabolites, which are considered sophisticated defense mechanisms to avoid the harmful impact of heat stress [9]. Despite of their complex nature of plant stress tolerance, recent development in different omics approaches has facilitated the prompt understanding of molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying heat stress response in plants. For instance, comparative metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses in cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) predicted the significance of flavonoids and gibberellin metabolic pathways under high temperature conditions [10]. The Wu et al. (2022) study showed that heat shock factor (HSF) genes MpHSFA1 and MpHSFB1 may play a key role in providing thermal stress tolerance in liverwort Marchantia polymorpha [11]. Recently, changes in MAPK signaling pathway and plant hormones revealed by transcriptome and metabolome data were shown to be involved in high-temperature resistance in roses [12]. However, a comprehensive understanding of heat-responsive regulatory and metabolic networks in oat awaits interpretation despite these insights.

Lipid metabolism is one of the most important metabolic pathways, and plays an important role in stress adaptation [13]. For example, enhancing the transcriptional activity of lipid metabolic enzymes significantly improved the heat tolerance in sacha inchi [14]. The Arabidopsis T-DNA insertion mutant disrupted in monogalactosyldiacylglycerol (a major type of glycolipid) catabolism has been shown to have a heat stress-sensitive phenotype [15]. Furthermore, heat-induced lipid remodeling events and related transcriptional adaptations have been comprehensively studied by lipidomic, metabolomic, proteomic, and transcriptomic analyses in Arabidopsis [16], peanut [17], Nicotiana tabacum [18], and Chlamydomonas reinhardtii [19]. Nonetheless, the dynamics of lipid species and genetic basis of lipid metabolism in heat-stressed oat remain unknown.

In this study, we integrate transcriptomic, metabolomic and lipidomic analyses to investigate metabolic pathways and regulatory genes conferring heat tolerance in oat. To the best of our knowledge, this work provides the first comprehensive analysis of lipid-centric biological processes underpinning oat thermotolerance. Our findings elucidate molecular mechanisms of heat adaptation, and the gene resources identified have potential applications in plant biotechnology to develop heat-resilient crops, ensuring stable productivity of agricultural crops under adverse conditions.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and heat treatment

A widely cultivated oat variety “Baiyan No. 7” in China was selected as the experiment material. Oat seeds were surface-sterilized with 1% (v/v) NaClO for 10 min, and then rinsed thoroughly, followed by seed soaking in distilled water for 30 min, and germinating on moist filter paper. Germinated seeds were transplanted into plastic pots containing a mixture of sterilized peat and perlite in a 2:1 ratio and grown in a climate-controlled chamber (22/18°C day/night, 16-h photoperiod, 60% relative humidity). Seedlings were irrigated with half-strength Hoagland’s nutrient solution every two days. Uniform 14-day-old seedlings were subjected to heat stress at 37 °C. Leaves were harvested at 0, 3, 6, and 9 h after heat treatment, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. After heat treatment, seedlings were returned to normal conditions (22℃ for 24 h) and were defined as the recovery group. A completely randomized design with three biological replicates per time point was used.

RNA extraction and transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from aforementioned samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and treated with DNase I (Takara) to remove genomic DNA. RNA purity (A260/A280 > 2.0) and integrity (RIN > 8.0) were verified using Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific), respectively. The cDNA libraries were constructed using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa) and sequenced on the BGISEQ platform (BGI Shenzhen) by MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

After removing adapter sequences and low-quality reads, the trimmed clean reads were aligned to the oat reference genome (https://wheat.pw.usda.gov/jb/?data=/ggds/oat-sang) using HISAT with default parameters. FPKM (fragments per kilobase of script per million fragments mapped) method was used to normalize gene expression levels. DEGs (Differentially Expressed Genes) were identified using DESeq2 with threshold of |log2 (Fold Change)| ≥ 1 and False Discovery Rate ≤ 0.05) [20]. KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) database was used to annotate gene function and corresponding pathway. KEGG enrichment analysis was performed via the MetWare Cloud Platform (https://cloud.metware.cn/) based on a hypergeometric test.

Metabolomics analysis

Metabolome analysis was performed with the assistance of Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Freeze-dried leaf powder (50 mg) was dissolved in 1.2 mL of 70% (v/v) methanol solution and stored overnight at 4 °C. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was filtered and stored for UPLC-MS/MS analysis. Identified metabolites were annotated against KEGG Compound database and mapped to KEGG pathway database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/). Differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were screened by VIP ≥ 1 and |log₂ (Fold Change)| ≥ 1. Enriched pathways were identified using metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA) with significance determined by hypergeometric test’s P-values. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on unit variance-scaled data using the prcomp function in R (https://www.r-project.org/).

High-coverage targeted lipidomic analysis

Lipid was extracted using a modified methanol-MTBE protocol [21]. Briefly, leaf tissue (20 mg) was collected into centrifuge tube containing 1 mL MeOH/MTBE (3:1, v/v), and centrifuged 15 min at 2500 r/min. After adding 300 µL Ultrapure Water and vortexing for one minute, the mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 10 min, and then centrifuged for 10 min at 12,000 r/min and 4 °C. The upper organic layer was dried, re-dissolved in 200 µL ACN/IPA (1:1, v/v), and analyzed via an LC-ESI-MS/MS system (ExionUPLC™ AD coupled with a QTRAP® 6500 PLUS mass spectrometer). Lipid species were quantified using MRM (multiple reaction monitoring) and an in-house lipid database (MetWare).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) assay

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR reactions were run in a Bio-RAD CFX96™ Touch real-time PCR detection system using SYBR Green reagent (Takara). The amplification conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 10 min, 39 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and Asactin gene was used as the internal control [22]. Three technical repeats and three biological replicates were adopted for each reaction, and the 2−ΔΔCt method was used to normalize the relative gene expression level. The primers used for qRT-PCR are provided in Table S1. The specificity of the reactions was verified using melting curve analysis and product size was further confirmed with agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. S1).

Results

Transcriptomic profiling of oat seedlings in response to heat stress

Phenotypic changes in oat seedlings were observed after the heat treatment (Fig. S2). Compared with the control (0 h), the leaves showed wilting and yellowing at 3 h, and the symptoms became more severe at 9 h. Time-course transcriptomic analysis was further conducted to examine global gene expression alterations of oat seedlings exposed to heat stress. Following stringent quality control, 12 samples (3 replicates per time point) yielded a total of 93.6 Gb high-quality paired-end reads (Table S2). Alignment of these reads against the oat reference genome (https://wheat.pw.usda.gov/GG3/content/avena-sang-download) resulted in an average uniquely mapped rate of 87% (Table S2), thus providing a reliable transcriptomic dataset for gene expression and functional analyses. The result of PCA plot showed an acceptable variation among the biological replicates at each time point (Fig. 1A), supporting experimental reproducibility. Relative to the control (0 h), 3303 genes (2257 upregulated, 1046 downregulated) were differentially expressed in heat-treated oat seedlings at 3 h, and the number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) increased consecutively up to 8166 at 9 h (Fig. 1B), demonstrating a duration-dependent transcriptional reprogramming. A venn diagram identified 1241 core DEGs that were consistently responsive to heat stress across all three time points (Fig. 1C).

To investigate potential biological functions of heat-responsive genes in oat, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis for the filtered DEGs. The pathways of linoleic acid metabolism, flavonoid biosynthesis, glycerolipid metabolism, plant hormone signal transduction, and MAPK signaling were significantly enriched at 3 h after heat treatment (Fig. 2A). Among the 5823 DEGs at 6 h, top enriched pathways encompassed photosynthesis, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, glutathione metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, and glycerolipid metabolism (Fig. 2B). The DEGs at 9 h were enriched in starch and sucrose metabolism, plant hormone signal transduction, fatty acid elongation, pyruvate metabolism, and alpha-linolenic acid metabolism (Fig. 2C). Critically, the 1241 core DEGs shared across all time points were robustly enriched in linoleic acid metabolism, flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, brassinosteroid biosynthesis, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (Fig. 2D). Collectively, these results demonstrate persistent activation or repression of lipid metabolism genes (e.g., linoleic/α-linolenic acid pathways, glycerolipid/unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis) across multiple time points, underscoring their central roles in oat’s transcriptional reprogramming during heat stress.

Metabolome overview and functional analyses of differential accumulated metabolites (DAMs)

To complement transcriptomic studies, metabolic reprogramming of oat in response to heat stress was examined using UPLC-MS/MS system, which led to the detection of 936 different metabolite compounds (Fig. S3A). PCA plot showed a clear temporal separation of metabolic profiles (Fig. S3B), confirming extensive heat-induced metabolic reprogramming. By comparing the metabolite chromatograms at each time point, 507 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were screened, with 211, 280, and 346 DAMs found at 3 h (125 upregulated and 86 downregulated), 6 h (166 upregulated and 114 downregulated), and 9 h (215 upregulated and 131 downregulated) after heat treatment, respectively (Fig. S3C). This progressive accumulation pattern was consistent with transcriptional dynamics, further suggesting the occurrence of dramatic changes at the late stage of heat stress. According to the chemical entities, these DAMs could be classified into nine major classes, dominated by lipids (21.3%), amino acid derivatives (18.13%), and alkaloids (17.43%) (Fig. S3D).

KEGG analysis of the DAMs at 3 h revealed a profound enrichment in pathways such as phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, glycerolipid metabolism, and carbon metabolism (Fig. 3A). The DAMs identified at 6 h exhibited a marked enrichment in pathways like phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, linoleic acid metabolism, and alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism (Fig. 3B). We also observed that alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, flavonoid biosynthesis, and fatty acid biosynthesis were significantly enriched at 9 h (Fig. 3C). Critically, lipid metabolism pathways, including sphingolipid metabolism, fatty acid elongation, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, were persistently enriched across all time points (Fig. 3), further highlighting their essential roles in sustained heat adaptation.

Correlation analysis of transcriptome combined with metabolome

Using rigorous threshold of Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) value of genes and metabolites ≥ 0.8 and P-value ≤ 0.05, nine-quadrant diagram of DEG-DAM correlations was generated, where the genes showing robust positive correlations with metabolites were located in quadrants 3 and 7 (Fig. 4A). KEGG analysis of the genes/metabolites in the third and seventh quadrants identified plant hormone signal transduction, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, flavone biosynthesis, linoleic acid metabolism, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, carbon metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids, and ABC transporters as significantly enriched pathways involved in the heat response in oat (Fig. 4B). Notably, lipid metabolism pathways, specifically fatty acid biosynthesis and glycerolipid metabolism, were extremely significantly enriched (P-value < 0.01). This demonstrates tight transcriptional-metabolic coordination governing lipid remodeling during oat heat adaptation.

Heat-induced lipid dynamics in oat seedlings

To further specify the role of lipid metabolism under heat exposure, we monitored global lipid profile changes in oat seedlings sampled at 0 (CK), and 9 h (HS) after treatment using established lipidomic analysis. A total of 246 individual lipid species were annotated across all samples (Table S3). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed that heat stress significantly altered the overall lipid metabolite profiles in the HS group compared to CK (Fig. S4).

Covering the major lipid classes, we separately detected glycolipids (MGDG, DGDG and SQDG), neutral lipids (TAG and DAG), phospholipids (PA, PC, PE, PG, PI, and PS), lysophospholipids (LPC, LPI, LPG, LPE and LPA) and free fatty acids (FFAs). Glycolipids, primarily MGDG and DGDG, constituted the dominant components (> 52% of total detected lipids), while LPI was detected only in trace amounts (Fig. 5B). Total lipid content decreased significantly in HS relative to CK (Fig. 5A). Comparing different lipid classes revealed strong decreases in MGDG, DGDG, PC, PE, and PA content following heat treatment. Conversely, DAG, TAG, PI, PG, LPA, LPC, and LPE levels increased significantly. The abundance of SQDG, FFAs, and PS remained largely unchanged (Fig. 5C-F).

Within the Kennedy pathway for TAG assembly, genes encoding glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (GPAT), lysophospholipid acid actyltransferase (LPAAT), phosphatidic acid phosphatase (PAP), and diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 1 (DGAT1) were substantially upregulated in heat-stressed seedlings, while DGAT2 genes were downregulated (Fig. 7B). Transcript levels of key phospholipid synthesis genes, including aminoalcoholphosphotransferase (AAPT), phosphatidylserine synthase 1 (PSS1), PSS2, and ethanolaminephosphate cytidylyltransferase (PECT), were lower in HS than CK (Fig. 7B). Conversely, genes involved in phospholipid degradation, including phospholipase D (PLD), non-specific phospholipase C (NPC), and PLA, were highly upregulated. Genes participating in acyl editing of PC, including lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase (LPCAT) and phosphatidylcholine: diacylglycerol cholinephosphotransferase (PDCT), were also strongly induced by heat stress (Fig. 7B). Notably, endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-localized fatty acid desaturase genes (FAD2, FAD3), which desaturate phospholipids, were upregulated (Fig. 7B). In contrast, plastidial desaturase genes (FAD6, FAD7, FAD8), responsible for glycolipid unsaturation, were unaffected by heat stress (Fig. 7B). The expression of genes encoding UDP-sulfoquinovose synthase (SQD1) and SQDG synthase (SQD2) remained unchanged (Fig. 7B). However, MGDG synthase (MGD) and DGDG synthase (DGD) genes were upregulated (Fig. 7B), suggesting activation of MGDG and DGDG biosynthesis under heat stress. Concurrently, genes involved in MGDG/DGDG degradation, including HIL1 (HEAT INDUCIBLE LIPASE1), and PLIP2 (PLASTID LIPASE2), were highly induced by heat treatment (Fig. 7B).

To confirm the transcriptome sequencing results, nine candidate genes (GPAT, DGAT1, LPAAT, MGD, DGD, FAD2, FAD3, HIL1, PLIP2) with upregulated expression levels under heat stress were randomly selected and verified by performing qRT-PCR. The results showed that the expression trends of these genes were consistent with the transcriptome data (Fig. S5), indicating their active roles in oat response to heat stress. Additionally, we assessed the expression of these heat-induced genes during a 24-hour recovery period at 22 °C (RHS). Upon relief of heat stress, most upregulated genes reverted to levels comparable to the control (CK) (Fig. S5). To further investigate the effect of heat stress on lipid metabolism, we quantitatively isolated the glycolipid, phospholipid and neutral lipid from the oat seedlings in the CK, HS, and RHS groups using Cleanert Silica solid phase extraction Cartridge (Tianjin Bonna Co., Ltd., China). As a result, we found that total lipid content decreased under heat stress (HS) compared to the control (CK) (Fig. S6), which was consistent with our lipidomic result. After recovery from HS, the content of total lipid in RSH was slight higher than that in HS, but lower than that in the CK group (Fig. S6). Similarly, heat stress significantly reduced phospholipid and glycolipid content but elevated neutral lipid level (Fig. S6), which was also consistent with our lipidomic result. These new data support the experimental reproducibility of our previous lipidomics data. After 24-hour recovery under normal temperature, these stress-induced alterations of lipid classes showed the partial recovery from the stressed oat seedlings compared to the CK (Fig. S6). This data suggest that lipid metabolism may function as a temporary buffer during heat stress.

Discussion

Cultivating oat in arid mountain regions enhances farmer incomes while protecting fragile ecosystems [23]. As a cool-season crop, oat is particularly vulnerable to elevated temperatures exacerbated by global climate change. Plants undergo extensive molecular reprogramming to adapt to environmental challenges [24]. Therefore, integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses of heat-stressed oat seedlings were designed to identify key regulatory genes and pathways that could be targeted for intervention, thus facilitating the progress of genetic improvement in oat.

Lipids are fundamental biomolecules in all biological systems, and their metabolism is closely linked to plant stress tolerance [25]. Cellular membranes, mainly composed of a complex mixture of lipids, serve as the first barriers that interface with temperature fluctuations [26]. Heat stress typically restricts plant growth and development but activates lipid metabolism to maintain membrane fluidity and integrity, protecting plants from damage [27, 28]. Consistent with established determinants of stress tolerance, heat-induced lipid metabolic changes occur in diverse species, including Arabidopsis thaliana [29], wheat [30], Brassica carinata [31], and rice [32]. Here, we leveraged transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets to elucidate critical regulatory pathways associated with oat response to heat stress. Investigating gene-metabolite interactions has made it possible to better understand complex stress tolerance mechanisms in plants [33]. Our transcriptome and metabolome correlation analyses revealed that oat response to heat stress were predominately related to pathways like plant hormone signal transduction, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and flavone biosynthesis (Fig. 4), which are known contributors to plant stress resilience [34, 35]. Specifically, only fatty acid biosynthesis and glycerolipid metabolism were characterized as the extremely significantly enriched biological pathways (P-value < 0.01) in the heat-stressed oat seedlings. This highlights lipids or their derivatives as critical metabolites governing oat’s heat stress response, which was on a par with previous reports that enriched lipid metabolism pathways play key roles in imparting heat tolerance [36, 37]. This suggests that adjusting lipid metabolism may be a critical yet conserved mechanism for plants to combat the heat stress. Notably, lipid metabolism can intricately interact with phenylpropanoid, flavonoid and hormone metabolic pathways [38, 39], supporting its role as a central regulatory hub in plant defense.

After observing there was a more pronounced effect of heat stress on lipid metabolism (Figs. 2, 3 and 4), it would be interesting to know how heat stress affected metabolic balance of diverse lipids in oat. To address this question, we conducted a qualitative and quantitative analysis of different lipid species using high-coverage targeted lipidomics, a novel methodology that has been validated in Arabidopsis [29], castor bean [40], and maize [41]. A key finding was the accumulation of neutral lipids, particularly TAG, under heat stress (Fig. 5). While neutral lipids typically remain low in vegetative tissues [42], their increase during high-temperature stress is well-documented [14, 42, 43]. Notably, Mueller et al.. discovered that heat-accumulated TAG rapidly declined when heat stress was subsequently relieved [44]. Transgenic Arabidopsis lines with elevated TAG exhibited enhanced heat tolerance, whereas TAG-deficient mutants showed compromised basal thermotolerance [45]. As TAG serves as transient energy storage during stress recovery, sequesters toxic lipid intermediates like FFAs, and possesses strong ROS-scavenging capacity [43, 46], its accumulation likely represents an evolutionarily conserved protective mechanism against heat stress in plants.

The observed decrease in total lipid content (Fig. 5A) raised an intriguing question regarding the metabolic origin of accumulated TAG. The concurrent increase in neutral lipids with decreases in glycolipids and phospholipids (Fig. 5B) suggested that membrane lipid breakdown may be an important precursor for neutral lipid synthesis under heat stress. Previous studies also provided good evidence that there was a concurrent trend toward reduced membrane lipids under adverse conditions, with galactolipids and phospholipids providing FFAs for TAG synthesis in heat-stressed plants [40, 47, 48]. In leaves, MGDG and DGDG are the most abundant thylakoid lipids vulnerable to heat damage [49]. Galactolipid synthesis can be accomplished via endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-compartmentalized (eukaryotic) or plastid-compartmentalized (prokaryotic) pathway, distinguished by sn−2 C18:3 or C16:3 FAs [50]. Here, sn−2 C18:3 FAs dominated MGDG and DGDG molecules (72%) (Fig. S7), identifying oat as a typical C18:3 plant. In such plants, ER-assembled PA and PC are key galactolipid precursors [51]. Heat stress significantly reduced PA (18:3_18:3), PA (18:2_18:3), PC (18:2_18:2), PC (18:3_18:3), and PC (18:2_18:3) (Fig. 6), suggesting these species serve as preferred precursors for glycolipid synthesis, resulting in predominant sn−2 C18 FAs. Furthermore, glycolipid degradation pathways were strongly induced by heat stress (Fig. 7), consistent with the observed decrease in total MGDG and DGDG (Fig. 5). Paradoxically, transcriptomics indicated heat-activated glycolipid biosynthesis (Fig. 7). As glycolipid levels are reflected by the balance between its synthesis and degradation rate, their decrease likely results from accelerated turnover in heat-stressed seedlings. The specific reduction in polyunsaturated MGDG and DGDG species (Fig. 6) further supports the model where glycolipid breakdown fuels polyunsaturated TAG accumulation, tightly linked to heat adaptation [47, 48].

Concerning phospholipids, PC and PE decreased significantly under heat stress (Fig. 5C). Declines in PC (18:2_18:2), PC (18:3_18:3), PC (18:2_18:3) and PE (18:1_18:1) contributed to the increases in LPC (18:3), LPC (18:2), and LPE (18:1), which is likely catalyzed by PLA enzymes. The different acyl chains suggest that PLA enzymes may exhibit distinct substrate selectivity, as reported in Arabidopsis, Physaria fendleri and castor [52]. Phospholipids, mainly PC, can also be degraded by PLD and NPC to produce PA and DAG, respectively. In our transcriptome data, activated PLD and NPC genes facilitate the flow of phospholipid-derived products into neutral lipid and glycolipid synthesis. While acyl editing and ER-localized fatty acid desaturation are two major mechanisms that occur on the sn−2 position of PC and regulate polyunsaturated FA synthesis [53], we observed no increase in polyunsaturated PC species (Fig. 6). Given the prominence of phospholipid hydrolysis, the transcriptional induction of acyl editing and ER desaturases (FAD2, FAD3) may instead support polyunsaturated TAG synthesis. Conversely, expression of plastidial desaturase genes (FAD6, FAD7, FAD8), responsible for glycolipid unsaturation, was inhibited (Fig. 7). Downregulation of plastidial desaturases under heat stress was also reported in castor bean and maize [40, 54], and plastidic FAD mutants exhibited enhanced thermotolerance [55, 56]. Although total phospholipids decreased, PG and PI levels increased (Fig. 5). PG is essential for photosystem II repair in chloroplasts, while PI and its derivatives (e.g., PI3P) are crucial signaling molecules [57]. Thus, PG and PI accumulation may benefit oat’s heat stress adaptation. Accordingly, genes encoding CDP-diacylglycerol synthase (CDS), producing the common PG/PI precursor CDP-DAG, were strongly upregulated (Fig. 7), which has been found to be critical for mitochondrial function in Arabidopsis [58]. The specific contributions of PG and PI to heat defense warrant further investigation.

Based on our current understanding of lipid metabolism in plants, an alternative route for TAG synthesis involves direct incorporation of de novo synthesized FAs [48]. However, total FFA levels remained stable after heat stress (Fig. 5B), and de novo FA synthesis pathway was significantly inhibited in leaves (Fig. 7). Critically, for de novo FA synthesis, their end products are principally C18:1 and C16:0, while the accumulated TAGs here were rich in polyunsaturated FAs (Fig. 6), which are proven to be closely related to membrane lipid turnover. Therefore, we believe that heat-induced accumulation of TAG is mainly sourced from membrane lipid turnover rather than de novo FA synthesis. There are two distinct routes that generate TAG, an acyl-CoA-dependent Kennedy pathway and an acyl-CoA-independent pathway [48]. Here, the upregulation of genes encoding Kennedy pathway acyltransferases (Fig. 7) indicates their contribution to heat-induced TAG accumulation in oat. Although we detected no changes in PDAT gene expression (Fig. 7), PDAT activity may still influence thermotolerance, as PDAT1 mutants exhibit heat sensitivity despite lacking induction during heat stress in Arabidopsis [45].

Conclusion

This integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic study highlighted the vital role of lipid metabolism in mediating oat’s response to heat stress. Notably, the contents of neutral lipids, especially TAG, were substantially increased under heat stress, implying their contribution to the enhanced heat tolerance in oat. Heat-induced TAG accumulation strongly relied on recycling of FFAs released from membrane lipids, especially MGDG, DGDG, PC and PE, rather than de novo FA biosynthesis. Additionally, heat stress induced the expression of genes involved in acyl editing and ER-localized FA desaturation process, thereby leading to the increased unsaturation levels of TAG species and maintainace of the membrane fluidity under heat stress. Overall, this study not only positions lipid metabolism as an evolutionary conserved hub for thermotolerance, but also identifies candidate genes for genetic improvement of oat with enhanced heat tolerance.
