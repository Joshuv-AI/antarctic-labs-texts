Public risk perceptions of advanced water purification in an arid urban region of the U.S. southwest: A mixed methods study
Authors: Amanda M. Wilson, Yoonhee Jung, Mohammed Shafae, Nana Adwoa A. Amoh-Asante, Ahamed Ashraf, Hunter Quon, Kerry A. Hamilton, Dawn Morrison, Lance Larkin, Kerri Hickenbottom, A. Eduardo Sáez, Luisa A. Ikner, Walter Betancourt, Robert A. Norwood, Andrea Achilli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As water utilities implement potable reuse technology, there is a need to understand how to increase public acceptance and trust in public water supplies. The study objective was to use surveys and interviews in a large metropolitan area in Arizona to characterize tap water and advanced purified water acceptability, and factors contributing to (un)acceptability. Participants were recruited through a water utility email listserv for participation in an online REDCap survey and/or 1-hr Zoom interview. Surveys and interviews inquired about perceptions of tap water safety, familiarity with water reuse terms, acceptability of direct potable reuse (called “advanced water purification” in our study for consistency with state messaging), and rationales related to acceptance. Four hundred seventy-nine individuals participated in the survey, and twenty-two individuals participated in the interviews, with roughly comparable demographics for our city of interest but with slightly higher levels of household income and education. Only 36 % of survey respondents use their tap water for drinking water supplies, but (42 %) would be open to drinking advanced purified water. Semi-structured interviews were conducted in 2024 on risk-based thinking to evaluate how advanced purified water may compare to current drinking water safety and analyzed with inductive thematic analysis. Survey and interview participants wanted more reassurances (e.g., third party testing and opportunities for hands-on testing). Water utilities should prioritize transparent communication strategies, including sharing detailed third-party testing data and direct community engagement initiatives, to enhance public acceptance. Utilities can build trust through clear comparisons between advanced purified water and current tap water quality.

Introduction

Water scarcity in the Southwestern United States

Water scarcity is a growing health threat around the world, especially in the Southwestern United States (U.S.), which is challenged by drought, high population growth, and predictions of increased temperature stress (Fuller., 2010; Wang et al., 2024). In response, the development and implementation of water reuse technologies are an increasing priority of government entities, such as departments of environmental quality and water utilities. Water reuse, also related to “water recycling” and “water reclamation,” includes a vast set of approaches, with potable uses including buffers between wastewater, graywater, or stormwater and drinking water supplies (e.g., use of aquifers, lakes, rivers as a step in between treatment of water for potable uses), or without buffers (i.e., wastewater being treated and directly inputted into potable water supplies) (U.S. EPA, 2024), and de facto water reuse, where wastewater inputs upstream enter surface water supplies downstream (Rice et al., 2016). Herein, we focus on direct potable reuse (potable reuse without a buffer), which involves the “treatment and distribution of water without an environmental buffer” (U.S. EPA, 2024). This was the main focus of this work due to the increasing number of water utilities in the southwestern U.S. that are considering or planning to implement direct potable reuse programs. We focus on the branding, “advanced water purification,” or “AWP,” for consistency with the Arizona Department of Environmental Quality definition (Arizona Department of Environmental Quality, 2025a), where they state “AWP, previously referred to as direct potable reuse or DPR” (Arizona Department of Environmental Quality, 2025b).

In 2023, the State Water Resources Control Board in California approved regulations for direct potable reuse. The Arizona Department of Environmental Quality is revising regulations to increase the implementation of direct potable reuse and is engaging experts, similar to the process used by California, to guide required water quality standards (California Water Boards, 2023). Texas is also following a five-year state water plan to provide enough water to its residents (Nielsen-Gammon et al., 2020). The Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment is partnering with counties and other agencies to provide and maintain water quality (Blomquist et al., 2004). New Mexico has proposed regulations on direct and indirect potable reuse (New Mexico Environmental Department, n.d.), and the Florida Department of Environmental Protection published a notice of proposed regulations in November 2024 (Department of Environmental Protection, 2024). This national movement toward water reuse raises questions about how to effectively implement potable reuse and to garner public support.

Risk perceptions of water reuse

Water reuse, and direct potable reuse, specifically can yield a broad range of reactions from constituents (e.g., disgust at the idea of wastewater being “turned into” drinking water vs. celebration of sustainable practices), depending on a suite of demographic factors, such as age, sex, income, education (Gerrity et al., 2013), and considerations about potential uses for recycled water. For example, a previous study involving telephone surveys in Arizona showed that people with lower education attainment tended to have more negative perceptions about water reuse than those with higher education attainment (Rock et al., 2012). In a study conducted in Southeast U.S., participants' openness to using recycled water varied by activity, with the least openness to use being for activities that could risk ingestion or direct contact (Pathiranage et al., 2024). It has also been documented that reasons for acceptability (or lack thereof) can vary. For example, in a survey of water utility customers in Albuquerque, New Mexico, rationales for support and lack of support for direct potable reuse and indirect potable reuse were captured and included reasons like water shortages, trust in the technology, greater efficiency, and more (Distler and Scruggs, 2020). While it is known that demographic factors, trust in municipalities (Barnes et al., 2023), and concerns about health outcomes (Pathiranage et al., 2024) influence acceptability, more data are needed to understand how individuals consider the quality of their current municipal water supplies with potentially new supplies, as water utilities explore advanced water purification (AWP), direct potable reuse, and other water reuse approaches or programs. This is especially true in the Southwestern U.S. where an increasing number of states are engaging in regulation for expanding direct potable reuse efforts (Arizona Department of Environmental Quality, 2025a; State Water Resources Control Board, 2019; Texas Commission on Environmental Quality, 2022). While prior work has been done to characterize perceptions of water reuse in Arizona (Rock et al., 2012), this was conducted in 2008. In March 2025, a new AWP rule in Arizona took effect, with utilities actively making plans to implement AWP, increasing the need for support of water utilities in engaging the public to understand current gaps in familiarity and acceptability (Arizona Department of Environmental Quality, 2025a).

Acceptability of water reuse and language used to communicate with the public has implications for the successful implementation of water reuse programs. For example, in the 2018 report from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, “Mainstreaming Potable Water Reuse in the United States: Strategies for Leveling the Playing Field,” they describe the harmful branding, “toilet to tap,” used by those opposed to potable reuse, which ultimately contributed to miscommunicates with the public, eroded trust, and a stalling of the project (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2018). Understanding how specific branding influences acceptability and continuing to gather contextual insights regarding communities' trust is vital for advancing research needed to guide effective implementation strategies.

Research gaps and study objectives

Existing literature has extensively examined public perceptions of water reuse primarily through surveys, but few studies have integrated qualitative methods to explore underlying reasons behind public acceptance (or lack thereof) of advanced purified water. Qualitative methods provide additional value in characterizing perceptions that may not emerge alone in surveys, adding valuable context (Long and Jiang, 2023) by eliciting people's individual experiences expressed in a conversational format. The use of qualitative methods in conjunction with quantitative methods provides not only additional insights into “why” people have specific perceptions about water reuse or engage or do not engage in specific behaviors (Agius, 2013); it also gives a voice to communities in the process, increasing the ethical value of the work. Additionally, there is a limited understanding of how demographic factors and household-level water usage preferences influence perceptions and acceptance across various water reuse activities, especially within specific regional contexts characterized by acute water scarcity challenges. Specifically, the Southwestern United States presents unique environmental pressures and regulatory landscapes related to water reuse that require region-specific insights to inform effective policy and management practices.

This research addresses these critical gaps by employing a comprehensive mixed-methods approach—combining quantitative and qualitative survey data and interviews—to not only quantify levels of acceptance but also uncover nuanced rationales influencing public attitudes toward advanced purified water. By exploring aspects of trust, including transparency, independent testing, and direct community engagement, this study expands current methodological approaches by revealing deeper emotional, cognitive, and trust-based dimensions that surveys alone often fail to capture. Furthermore, detailed demographic stratification and household-level analysis of water-use practices enable highly targeted engagement strategies for diverse community segments.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to characterize perceptions of current drinking water supplies and anticipated supplies if AWP is pursued. To accomplish this objective, we used a mixed methods approach (surveyed and interviewed residents of a large metropolitan area in Arizona) to inventory drinking water sources and current concerns about drinking water, familiarity with water reuse terminology, and determine acceptability of water reuse for a variety of household purposes, including drinking. This combined methodological framework provides novel insights directly informing targeted educational and engagement strategies by water utilities and policymakers aimed at enhancing public trust and acceptance in potable water reuse, particularly within water-scarce regions.

Methods

Survey and interview recruitment

The survey was developed in partnership with an Arizona-based water utility in a large metropolitan area, initiated and led by a team from the University of Arizona. The survey was designed in REDCap (Harris et al., 2009, 2019), a software enabling secure collection and storage of data via surveys and other instruments, for online distribution. Recruitment was conducted via an email from the water utility through the water utility's listserv of customers who utilize their online paying system. Participants were eligible to apply if they were 18 years or older and invited via the listserv. Surveys and interviews were available in English. The potential for selection bias associated with this approach is discussed in detail in Section 4.3.

The survey was developed with subject-matter experts and was then piloted with 30 randomly selected customers from the listserv in April 2024. The survey comprised of both open- and closed-ended questions, and participants could skip any of the questions they did not want to answer. Response rate and quality of responses (e.g., errors in data recording or clear misinterpretations of open-ended questions) were evaluated. The survey was then modified to address any issues found during piloting and distributed via email to the full listserv in May 2024, including the original recipients to reduce burden of the water utility for tracking participants and because researchers had a record of which email addresses were entered more than once by respondents. After approximately one month from the initial recruitment email sent to the full listserv, a reminder email was sent. The survey was modified with a question asking if participants had taken the survey before and ended the survey if they indicated they had already taken it. In situations where a person had participated more than once (i.e., an email address appeared twice), the first entry was treated as the entry for this person, and subsequent entries were removed from analysis. This ended up applying to only one participant from pilot but applied to a number of participants in the full recruitment following the reminder email to the full listserv. Incomplete entries (determined by REDCap's labeling of “not completed”) were also removed from analysis, as these were assumed to be either a participant removing themselves from the study (i.e., deciding not to participate and complete the survey) or a technical glitch in which a participant may have started the survey and later restarted and finished. This did not conflict with participants' ability to skip questions, as skipping a question (e.g., not participating in the open-ended questions or not choosing a race or gender), did not result in a “not complete” labeling. This labeling occurred for situations in which participants did not answer any or a vast majority of the questions. Data were also removed in cases where participants entered the survey twice (i.e., listed the same email address at the end for more than one entry), and the first entry was treated as the primary entry. Participants were compensated with a $10 gift card (a modest amount of money in the U.S. for those in our sampled population) to respect their time in completing the survey and to increase likelihood of participation without undue influence.

Survey questions included demographics (i.e., sex, race and ethnicity, household income, household size, age) with categories informed by prior surveys, consulting the research team, and categories listed by the National Institutes of Health, primary drinking water source at home, level of concern about safety of their drinking water using a Likert scale (1 to 5, 1 = not at all concerned, 2 = not very concerned, 3 = neutral, 4 = a little concerned, 5 = very concerned), an open-ended question about concerns, familiarity with water reuse terms, and sources of knowledge about water reuse terms. A brief definition of AWP was provided to participants:

Advanced Water Purification (AWP) involves robust treatment of recycled water that is then directly added to drinking water supplies. It is a technology that has been implemented domestically and internationally to address water scarcity challenges. However, it has not been implemented in your zip code yet.

Following this definition, participants were asked which types of applications they may use AWP for, including drinking water, showering, bathing, bathing children, watering lawn or plants, toilet flushing, brushing teeth, and/or cooking. Open-ended questions were asked about types of considerations that influenced the potential uses of AWP and what would make them feel more comfortable about the AWP process and about drinking advanced purified water. Participants were then asked to select the three most important considerations that would increase AWP acceptability for them, with six choices including: water scarcity concerns; cost relative to other choices such as bottled water; water safety; water smell, taste, and appearance; acceptance of the water by their neighbors, family, and friends; and/or their health status.

In the recruitment email, participants were also told about an opportunity to participate in an interview (≤1 h) via Zoom or phone and were compensated with a $20 gift card. Participants reached out to AMW to schedule an interview. All interviews were conducted by AMW, PhD, MS, a female assistant professor, who has training and mentors in qualitative methods and implementation science. AMW did not have any prior relationships or familiarity with any of the interview participants. Participants were briefed on the purpose of the study and told that there is no “right or wrong” answer – that the research team was truly interested to hear their specific views. Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. The transcription feature in Zoom was used to draft the initial transcript. This was then manually checked and corrected by the research team using recorded audio files. Interview questions included basic demographics that were included in the survey but were left open-ended. Other interview questions included those related to the survey, such as drinking water source, opinions about how utilities can build or maintain trust with customers, their familiarity with AWP and factors that would influence their acceptance of using advanced purified water for a variety of household purposes. Responses were coded into categories used for surveys by the research staff member who conducted the interviews.

Survey data analysis

For survey analyses, R (version 4.5.1) was used (R Core Team, 2025). Summary statistics were calculated across all closed-ended survey questions. Open-ended questions underwent content analysis with two researchers separately coding responses into categories seen in Table 1. Any discrepancies in how responses were coded were resolved through discussion, and, if necessary, a third researcher was involved to make a final decision on any unresolved coding judgments. Potential coding categories were decided upon inductively and deductively.

Statistically significant relationships between demographic variables (i.e., race and ethnicity, age, sex, education level, household income level) and three main themes of this survey were explored through chi square tests: 1) water safety perception, 2) water reuse familiarity, and 3) water reuse risk perceptions (e.g., for what uses participants would use advanced purified water). All demographic variables were treated as categorical based on the design of the survey (available in supplemental materials). If fewer than five individual responses were observed for a group, Fisher's exact tests were used. Entries for which there was no response for one of the two categories being compared were removed from that particular analysis. These relationships were explored due to evidence revealed in previous studies of relationships between demographic variables, including education level and household income level, and perceptions of drinking water safety in past studies (Park et al., 2019; Pierce and Gonzalez, 2017). Statistically significant relationships were also explored between 1) primary drinking water source and concerns about current drinking water, and 2) water reuse familiarity and water reuse risk perceptions. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method and a False Discovery Rate (FDR) threshold of 0.05, where adjusted p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. This was done using the “p.adjust” function in R, with function inputs including the unadjusted p-values for all inference tests, the specified adjustment method (i.e., Benjamini-Hochberg), and the total number of comparisons made. This function then outputted corresponding adjusted p-values for all inputted p-values.

Interview transcript analysis

Recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were inputted into Dedoose (version 9.2.22, Sociocultural Research Consultants, LLC) software and coded independently by two researchers. Codes were inductively and deductively decided upon by discussing the interview recordings, the purpose of the project, and through practice coding of one transcript. Discrepancies in coding were resolved through discussion, and a third researcher was involved in making a final decision on any unresolved coding judgments. Codes for transcripts can be seen in Table 1, and included “parent codes,” (overarching concepts) and “sibling codes” (related concepts) for ease of tabulating how specific areas of overarching concepts were potentially related. Co-occurrences of codes were explored to guide thematic analysis, where a co-occurrence was identified by the Dedoose software (i.e., where two codes overlapped in the transcript text). Co-occurrences with higher frequencies relative to others were discussed by AMW and YJ along with codes in each transcript to inform inductive thematic analysis, identifying emerging themes across interviews from directly observing and discussion the data, and to evaluate saturation of these emergent themes. Summary statistics were used to explore the frequency of code appearances and co-occurrence of codes to spur further identification of emergent themes. Memos were also used to identify key findings or meaningful quotes, added through initial rounds of analysis of transcripts and through research team discussions.

Results

Survey participant demographics

There were 565 entries for the survey. Fifty-three were marked as not complete (i.e., a vast majority of questions were not or none of the questions were answered for the entry), and 33 were removed due to the email address being listed in a previous entry. Therefore, the complete data set contained entries for four hundred seventy-nine participants. Demographics of survey participants can be seen in Table 2. Due to lack of overlap of some of the demographic categories, a statistical comparison of the participants to Census Bureau data was not possible. However, the demographics are reasonably comparable racially, with survey participants being wealthier and more educated. For example, 65 % (310/479) of participants were white with 274/479 (57 %) being white alone (i.e., non-Hispanic and no other race category), while 53.7 % of people in the city according to Census Bureau were white alone. Black or African American individuals represented 6 % of survey participants, with the Census Bureau reporting 7.8 % Black individuals in this city. Census Bureau reported 84.4 % of the city's population holds a high school degree or higher (percent of persons age 25+ years) and 32.3 % of the population with a bachelor's degree or higher, and among all survey participants, compared to 93 % (445/479) of all survey participants who hold a high school degree or higher and 61 % (290/475) reporting Bachelor's degree or higher. Median household income for this city was reported to be $77,041, and 40 % (193/479) of survey participants reporting household income of under $100,000 and 61 % (293/ 479) reporting household income under $150,000.

Interview participant demographics

Twenty-two participants completed the interview. Thematic saturation occurred in the 22 interviews, where identified themes present in the final interviews were consistent with those in the rest of the interviews. This is consistent with the number of interviews needed for saturation in other qualitative research studies (Ahmed, 2025; Hennink and Kaiser, 2022). These themes are discussed in further detail in Section 3.3. While more community members were interested in participating in the interviews, budget restrictions did not allow for recruitment to continue for both the survey and interview beyond the number of participants selected.

Demographics of interview participants were comparable to those of the survey, with a little over half of participants being female (13/22, 59 %), and over half being white (15/22, 64 %) and non-Hispanic/non-Latino (18/22, 82 %). Sixty-four percent reported having a bachelor's or master's degree, and 55 % reported an annual household income of between $100,000 and $199,999 (Table 2).

Survey analysis results

Tap water was the most commonly reported source for drinking water (171/479, 36 %), followed by individual bottled water (112/479, 23 %) and refillable water jugs (109/479, 23 %). It should be noted that packaged water options, combined (i.e., individual and refillable water containers), together represented a greater proportion than that of tap water. Concern about drinking water was, on average, 3.5 (SD = 1.4), just slightly above the neutral Likert score of 3, where 1 = not at all concerned, 2 = not very concerned, 3 = neutral, 4 = a little concerned, 5 = very concerned. The level of concern about drinking water source varied by their dominant source of drinking water (p-adjusted = 0.01) (Table S1), where a larger proportion of those using refillable water jugs expressed greater concerns about drinking water supplies (Fig. S1). Primary drinking source varied by education level (p-adjusted = 0.007) (Table S1), where those with higher education levels tended to drink tap water (Fig. S2). There was a statistically significant association (p-adjusted = 0.0006) between sex and concern level about water safety, where females tended to view drinking water as riskier than males (Fig. 1).

The terms most recognized by participants were “reclaimed water” (348/479, 73 %) and “recycled water” (337/479, 70 %), with “indirect potable reuse” being the least familiar (79/479, 16 %) (Table 3). Water reuse familiarity varied significantly by education level (p-adjusted = 0.03) (Table S1, Fig. S3), where those with some college and no degree (87 % familiar), bachelor's degrees (92 % familiar), or master's degrees (95 % familiar) tended to be more familiar with listed terms than those with high school graduate/GED or equivalent education (76 % familiar) or an associate's degree (76 % familiar). The most commonly reported source of knowledge about these terms was online news (198/479, 41 %) (Table 3).

Even when told to consider that “proven technology assures that drinking water exceeds all state and federal standards,” the most accepted hypothetical uses for advanced purified water included toilet flushing (419/479, 87 %), and watering lawns or plants (412/479, 86 %), while the least accepted use was for drinking water (200/479, 42 %), followed by bathing one's children (252/479, 53 %) (Table 3, Fig. 2). It should be noted that participants without children or without lawns/plants likely had to consider these activities as hypothetical.

Interestingly, a greater proportion of participants reported being willing to use advanced purified water for drinking relative to the current proportion of participants who drink tap water (42 % vs 36 %) (Table 3, Fig. S4), implying AWP may increase the proportion of those who use tap water for drinking. There were significant associations in acceptability of using advanced purified water for watering lawns/plants and toilet flushing by education level (Figs. S5 and S6), race (Figs. S7-S10), sex (toilet flushing only, Fig. S11) and income (Figs. S12 and S13).

White respondents showed higher acceptance levels, whereas Hispanic/Latino respondents reported significantly lower acceptance for watering lawns/plants and toilet flushing. Males tended to show more acceptance relative to females for using advanced purified water for toilet flushing (Fig. S11). Figs. S12 and S13 illustrate clear variations in the acceptance of using AWP water for outdoor and toilet-flushing activities by income level. Generally, higher-income respondents exhibited somewhat higher acceptance rates, potentially due to greater access to educational resources regarding water safety and reuse technologies, or greater perceived control over water choices. The top considerations related to increased acceptability of advanced purified water were water smell/taste/appearance (381/479, 80 %) and water safety (377/429, 79 %) (Table 3, Fig. 3).

The responses to open-ended questions were analyzed using survey codes (Table 1). Multiple codes can be applied to a single response. For the question asking about participants' concerns regarding the safety of drinking water, the most common response was “chemical” (137/479, 29 %), followed by “overall uncertainty or unknowns about treatment” (120/479, 25 %). Other concerns mentioned by respondents include “taste/smell/appearance” (62/479, 13 %), “minerals/salt” (56/479, 12 %), and “microbial” (11/479, 2 %). Only 9 respondents (2 %) expressed they have no concern about the safety of drinking water. The major considerations answered through open response that influenced respondents' level of comfort in using advanced purified water were “meeting/exceeding standards” (81/479, 17 %), “overall uncertainty about safety” (79/479, 16 %), and “doubts about treatment reliability” (55/479, 11 %). Among the additional considerations mentioned were “distrust in government” (31/479, 6 %), “concerns about taste/smell/appearance” (24/479, 5 %), “concerns about chemicals” (23/479, 5 %), “personal controls such as reverse osmosis” (19/479, 4 %), “knowledge about water scarcity” (10/479, 2 %), “awareness of water quality issues elsewhere” (7/479, 1 %), and “potential financial impact” (3/479, 1 %). Respondents also emphasized the importance of “data/reports on the quality of AWP water” (136/479, 28 %) and “more education about treatment process” (100/479, 21 %). Respondents also noted wanting “more information on how quality is assessed” (66/479, 14 %), to “see others try it first” (38/479, 8 %), “knowing it meets standards” (37/479, 8 %), “independent testing” (29/479, 6 %), a “comparison of AWP water with current water” (20/479, 4 %), and “more trust in government” (17/479, 4 %). However, 35 respondents (7 %) expressed nothing could change their previous responses, whether with positive or negative views of AWP.

Interview analysis results: thematic analysis

Code frequencies and co-occurrences in the interview transcripts provided qualitative insights that guided thematic analysis. The most frequently detected code was “safety” (217 times), indicating that “safety” is a key point of discussion among interview participants regarding the use of AWP technology. “Safety” frequently appeared alongside “transparency” (36 co-occurrences), suggesting a conceptual link between these codes. The “transparency” code also appeared frequently with “testing” (33 co-occurrences) and “community outreach” (24 co-occurrences). These co-occurrences supported the identification of themes, including “reporting results,” and “trust in government and utilities,” discussed in more detail below. Participants indicated a desire for transparent results of the quality of advanced purified water by regular testing or independent testing, which are consistent with the findings described in the survey analysis for open-ended questions. Participants expressed a desire for community outreach opportunities to obtain information on AWP treatment and management, helping them trust its safety.

There were differences in how males and females perceived and discussed health concerns related to advanced purified water. Female participants represented a greater proportion of quotes associated with codes related to “chemicals” (female: 69.2 %, male: 30.8 %), “chlorine” (female: 75.7 %, male: 24.3 %), “contaminants” (female: 66.6 %, male: 33.4 %), and “microbes” (female: 73.5 %, male: 26.5 %). Male participants represented a greater proportion of quotes associated with codes related to “microplastics” (male: 59.1 %, female: 40.9 %) and “sustainability/scarcity” (male: 53.4 %, female: 46.6 %). Male participants also represented a greater proportion of quotes among those who noted their “sensory experience” (male: 59.1 %, female: 40.9 %) related to water use. Mentions of “management” (male: 76.5 %, female: 23.5 %), infrastructure” (male: 61.4 %, female: 38.6 %), and “testing” (male: 66.7 %, female: 33.3 %) were also more frequently associated with quotes from male participants, while “regulations” (male: 34.7 %, female: 65.3 %,) and “community outreach” (male: 54.9 %, female: 54.9 %) text were more often from female participants within the context of trust building between customer and water utilities. Mentions of “cost” were more frequently from lower-income respondents than higher-income respondents regarding their concerns of advanced purified water usage, where 25.1 % of codes for cost were from participants with a household income between $75,000–99,000 and only 12.6 % from those with income of $200,000 or more. Mentions of “transparency” were more frequently associated with those with higher household income ($200,000 or more: 26.2 %, $50,000–$75,000: 15.9 %), often linked to concerns about trusting water utilities and governments.

In discussing codes and their context within transcripts, identified themes included “comfort with using advanced purified water,” “reporting results,” “rationales for (un)acceptance of AWP,” and “trust in government and utilities.” Evidence from exemplary quotes supporting these themes is discussed below.

Comfort with using advanced purified water and reporting results

Participants noted wanting reassurance that proper treatment was occurring and that receiving education on how this is done was a form of transparency:

P15: As long as- as long as they put out enough discussion of the- the end quality and- and their treatment and what they expect and what they do and what they're testing results are and how, you know, what sort of things they can remove, you know, with the filtration and whatever particular advanced stuff they're doing that would make it really good, then I think they need to- I think they would need to do that. I mean, it would sort of be, you know, [an] educational thing.

A recurring pattern in the interviews was the simultaneous discussion of “safety” and “taste” (23 co-occurrences), indicating that some participants use “taste” as one parameter of “safety”:

P11: You know, if it doesn't smell great or taste great, you know, my family would probably be like, we're not gonna drink that, you know, so then that would- I'd have a hard time convincing them that it was okay and safe.

P7: For me it's both because if it taste strange, then there's something possibly wrong with the water and I don't know how healthy it is.

The conflation of taste and safety may be due to a number of factors, including few options for consumers to evaluate the safety of their water any other way. This is addressed further in the Discussion.

Rationales for (un)acceptance of AWP

Opinions about the safety of AWP were mixed, with some interviewees expressing dread about what may happen in the face of a system failure or, conversely, appreciating the redundancies in place in the event that something goes wrong:

P5: Knowing that there were multiple failsafe monitoring approaches for water quality was- was, I think, one aspect that made me feel confident that, okay, this stuff really is being checked and it's not being checked just at one position but at multiple- with multiple approaches and fail-safes and redundancy in the system. So, so knowing that the, for example, that the monitoring systems have redundancy built into them was an important confidence builder for me.

P17: I think the redundancies and systems and the reporting when something goes wrong, so there is a confidence that the oversight is really working is really important. And I would say, also, if this is used, I would like to also be reassured that the water is safe.

While some interviewees were skeptical of AWP for drinking, others used comparisons to current water supplies to justify AWP safety and discussed knowledge of the treatment being robust with hopes that the water quality may actually improve and will meet high standards. One participant expressed ideas related to de facto reuse, or the idea that treated wastewater is already present in current water supplies (Rice et al., 2016):

P1: Where does ours come from - the Colorado River? Is that right? Okay, so that already has birds pooping in it and fish pooping in it.

Another discussed comparative water quality:

P2: What I've- what I've heard about this, like using reclaimed water in this capacity, is that the- it's often cleaner and safer than, you know, standard water because it has to go through more regulation.

Trust in government and utilities

This concept of quality arose for many participants, some noting distrust in government and wanting the ability to test the water for themselves or have it tested by another party. This was consistent with opinions expressed in the survey.

P17: Government agencies are not necessarily trusted, so how do we have the right systems and oversight and tests and what not and the loop, feedback loop to the community to make sure that it continues safe and monitored.

P13: I used to have, actually just got rid of it, water tester you know, and so I don't know how many people have those, but that's probably, I would probably test it myself just out of curiosity, you know, we'll see what happens.

P16: You know, if the company gave little kits where every now and then I can test myself like the level of whatever I'm supposed to be looking for so that she have, you know, you have confidence. And you know what the city is telling you versus like okay I can see it for myself.

When discussing trust with utilities, participants noted several efforts that could maintain or build trust: One participant noted that even being engaged in the research to elicit their opinion was a form of trust building.

P16: And then like this also builds trust, like getting our opinion about using for public use. And like, you know, I'm not a scientist. I'm not anybody special, but you're still, like, want my opinion…

Discussion

Key findings

Together, the survey and interview results indicate mixed opinions about AWP for drinking water. However, a large majority (>80 %) of respondents indicated acceptance of AWP for some of the most water intensive practices, including watering plants/lawns, toilet flushing, and showering (Table 3), accounting for roughly 65 gal per day per person (outdoor uses), 14 gal per day per person (toilets), and 13 gal per day per person (showering/bathing), respectively (Phoenix.gov, n.d.). There may also be greater acceptance of AWP for drinking than acceptance of current tap water (Fig. S4, Table 1). Influential factors related to acceptance of AWP included trust in government, whether the water will meet regulatory requirements, transparency in testing and AWP water quality (even third-party testing), and a need for increased educational opportunities about the treatment process itself, and how the water quality is managed and regulated.

Other educational opportunities were identified based on participants' survey and interview responses that revealed a desire for more knowledge of how water is treated and even hands-on experience in testing the water quality themselves for transparency. Participants of the survey and interview discussed water hardness as a concern, in some cases related to safety. While water hardness has traditionally been treated as merely an aesthetic or infrastructural issue (e.g., causing build up and corrosion in pipes), there is emerging evidence of a link between all-cause and cause-specific cancers with exposures to domestic hard water for high levels of water hardness (>180 mg/L) (Yang et al., 2024). It is unknown whether participants who mentioned water hardness as a concern were aware of this.

Participants noted wanting to be able to test at their own home or see third party testing results as a form of accountability, stemming from distrust in government. Trust of utilities is an important factor related to AWP acceptability and can influence perceptions related to current drinking water quality. Respondents indicated that participating in this survey and interview itself resulted in trust building as a form of community engagement. Our work reiterates the importance of community engagement as a form of trust building, especially using methods that allow for meaningful dialogue, such as interviews or focus groups. In the continuum of community engagement, methods that include the public in decision-making are viewed as higher levels on the continuum, or encouraging deeper levels of trust and meaningful engagement (Key et al., 2019). Interviews and focus groups can contribute meaningful input into implementation of AWP technologies when partnering with water utilities and reporting back findings to utilities for informing AWP outreach efforts. Other forms of community engagement for water reuse, specifically are outlined elsewhere by Wilson et al. (2025).

Generalizability

This research adds to the growing body of literature related to the complexities of public perceptions regarding direct potable reuse (e.g., AWP) and tap water, in general (Pathiranage et al., 2024; Pierce and Gonzalez, 2017; Pierce et al., 2019; Victory et al., 2022). While differences across communities can lead to varying perceptions of safety and acceptability of AWP, our results show some consistencies with research on water reuse acceptability and general risk perceptions in other parts of the U.S. (Pathiranage et al., 2024). For example, participants of the survey indicated that uses not involving ingestion (e.g., outdoor watering) would be more acceptable than using AWP water for drinking. Our interviews revealed that some participants tend to closely associate taste with drinking water safety, consistent with other research. For example, Schubert et al. (2024) reported approximately two-thirds of participants in interviews described aesthetics when providing rationales behind ratings for drinking water quality at the faucet (Schubert et al., 2024).

Compared to the survey of Arizona residents conducted in 2008, 35 % of respondents said that they strongly support or somewhat support using advanced treated reclaimed water for drinking (Rock et al., 2012). This proportion is lower than the percent of survey respondents in our study that said they would be willing to use advanced purified water for drinking (42 %). In a more recent study conducted in the Southeast U.S. (published in 2024), 33.5 % of survey respondents said they strongly agree or agree that reclaimed water undergoing further treatment should be used for drinking (Pathiranage et al., 2024). It is unknown whether these differences are a function of perceptions of different communities, the phrasing of the question or branding of potable water reuse, background knowledge of participants, or the current state of water resource urgency at the times or in the places of study. It is interesting to observe differences in how information is received, however, whether the most commonly reported source of water reuse information for survey respondents in the 2008 survey was the newspaper (29 %), while, in our study, the most commonly reported source was online news (41 %). Communication strategies are adapting to the rapidly changing ways in which people seek out or are exposed to information and how this influences their perceptions and decision-making.

Trust, identified in this study as a key factor related to acceptability, has been demonstrated in other studies as vital to water reuse acceptance, with more risk averseness/lower acceptance among females (Barnes et al., 2023). This is consistent with our finding that females tended to be more wary of current drinking water safety standards than males, and this is overall consistent with a larger pattern in risk perception literature that females tend to be more risk averse than males (Slovic, 2000). This could be an important factor, depending upon household dynamics and who drives the household drinking water decisions. For example, a participant in the interviews noted that while he does not have substantial concerns about drinking tap water, his wife and children do, ultimately leading to little household level consumption of tap water. An additional contributor to trust is the level of responsiveness of water utilities to communities with water quality concerns, observed during the Flint, Michigan water crisis (Sobeck et al., 2020), and expressed by one of our participants who highlighted that being asked their opinion was a trust-building experience for them. Engaging communities in research by eliciting their opinions and concerns can build trust that will be vital for successful implementation of new water reuse programs.

While this work adds to a growing body of evidence regarding drivers of direct potable reuse acceptance, it also contributes new knowledge regarding how the potential branding of direct potable reuse could influence acceptability, especially “advanced water purification.” The use of this branding is beyond just our partnering water utility but, rather, is being used at the state level (Arizona Department of Environmental Quality, 2025a) and in other parts of the U.S., such as in San Diego, California (U.S. EPA, 2025), El Paso, Texas (U.S. Department of the Interior, 2024), and Reno, Nevada (OneWater Nevada, n.d.). While the acceptability of advanced purified water for drinking among our survey participants was only 42 %, this was higher than the proportion of those currently drinking municipal water supplies (36 %). Interview participants also described rationales for overcoming the idea that, if AWP was implemented, their water would have come into recent contact with feces, rationalizing that their current water supplies have also, at some point in time, come into contact with feces. Our results show promise for public acceptability of advanced purified water, which may influence future water reuse campaigns in other regions of the U.S. or abroad.

Strengths & limitations

A limitation of this study is common among those in which participants are invited to participate through a community partner as opposed to at random, which is that there may be selection bias. For example, those who sign up for the utility company's email listserv may have differing opinions than those who do not opt in to this form of communication with utilities. Those who wish to participate may have stronger opinions than those who do not, and they may be more predisposed to following trending issues related to the utility. Additionally, those who use an online payment service and are therefore on the mailing list may also be of higher education and household income levels than others, explaining why our participants appear to be, on average, more educated and with higher household income than the general population of our city of interest, as shown through Census Bureau data. It is also possible that some of the owners of the accounts corresponding to email addresses in the listservs are landlords and do not currently live in our geographical area of focus. Providing the possibility to participant in other languages, such as Spanish, and engaging customers who are not on the online listserv would also have increased our reach within our population of interest. Offering compensation, as in this study, may motivate those with less strong opinions to still participate. Future studies could address this bias by recruiting through multiple means, such as in person at community events, via mailed materials, and more. This requires additional cost and personnel resources. While the potential for selection bias is a weakness, there are many strengths to partnership with a utility, including a more streamlined process for what to do with results and increased meaningful community engagement. By recruiting through a community partner, the results can be directly shared with leadership, empowering customers' voices and leading to the utility's informed decision-making in how to implement AWP.

While consistent with other literature across multiple findings, our study contributes novelty to the field through the use of qualitative and quantitative data. While most recent studies on water reuse perceptions have been survey based (Barnes et al., 2023; Victory et al., 2022), the use of qualitative approaches allows for deeper questioning, where one can explore not just how people feel about water reuse technologies or applications, but also why they feel that way. In our study, for example, survey and interview data revealed mixed opinions about AWP. Interviews further revealed certain rationales that supported acceptability of AWP, such as thinking through the fact that current drinking water supplies may have, at some recent point in the past, come into contact with feces from animals defecating in surface water sources and that AWP water may not be comparatively less safe than current drinking water supplies. Interviews also revealed that many people wanted reassurances that water was safe and that third party testing would increase confidence, even if there was uncertainty as to what should be tested. In this way, interviews reveal a richness of insight into both the emotional and logical components that contribute to AWP acceptability. At the very least, these efforts also advance community engagement and build trust.

Conclusions

Perceptions of AWP water and acceptability of its use are mixed, but generally positive regarding activities that use high volumes of water, such as outdoor landscaping. Concerns about AWP water or drinking water, in general, may stem from safety concerns but also distrust in how water is tested and regulated and a general lack of awareness about how water treatment systems work. If there is existing trust, individuals may accept AWP if it meets regulations even if they are uncertain of what those regulations or guarantees of safety mean. Transparency is paramount for maintaining trust, including access to water quality data, side by side comparisons of current tap water supplies and AWP water, and even, ideally, third party testing kits where individuals can test water themselves, for which some interview participants expressed a desire. Regarding trust with water utilities, being consulted on the topic or involved in decision-making in some form related to AWP water emerged as important factors for some individuals. These concepts are consistent with the continuum of community engagement in which increasingly meaningful forms of engagement of the public in decision-making leads to increased trust (Wilson et al., 2025). Different ways in which some utilities, entities, consortiums, and research institutions are engaging in more outreach includes the creation of educational centers (CAP Central Arizona Project, 2025), the production and dissemination of educational documentaries (ReWater Center, n.d.), and even use of virtual reality to expand access to AWP facilities (ASU News, 2025).

While it is encouraging that there was a generally high acceptance of uses of water that contribute to a large proportion of households' overall water consumption, continued decreases in use of municipal water for drinking could have unknown health impacts, such as a higher reliance on plastic water bottles and increased microplastics exposure (Gambino et al., 2022), lower fluoride intake with dental health implications (Victory et al., 2017), and higher household costs (Arnold and Larsen, 2006). Conversely, high (>180 mg/L) water hardness present in municipal sources could have negative health impacts (Yang et al., 2024) that may need to be weighed among other tradeoffs, including convenience. More research is needed to better understand how specific uses of AWP water at household levels will impact public health and efforts to increase water security.
