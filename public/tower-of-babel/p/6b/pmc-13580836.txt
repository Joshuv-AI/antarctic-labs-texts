Orthotopic Esophageal Cancer Xenograft Model in Immunosuppressed Microminipigs for Near‐Infrared Fluorescence Endoscopy
Authors: Takanori Miyake, Yutaka Kawano, Takanori Kashihara, Masaki Takasu, Kentaroh Takagaki, Shota Fujimoto, Tomoyuki Kawaguchi, Kaizo Kagemoto, Yoshifumi Kida, Koichi Okamoto, Masahiro Sogabe, Naoki Muguruma, Koichi Tsuneyama, Yasushi Sato, Tetsuji Takayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEsophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) often presents as flat lesions difficult to detect with conventional endoscopy, leading to poor prognosis. To overcome this, we developed a near‐infrared (NIR) molecular imaging strategy using an epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)‐targeted antibody and established a novel orthotopic microminipig (MMP) model for translational evaluation. First, cetuximab was labeled with Alexa Fluor 680 (AF680) and validated in vitro for specific binding. Among five ESCC cell lines profiled for proliferation and antigen density, KYSE410 was selected for in vivo studies due to its optimal balance of these factors. In mouse xenografts, AF680‐cetuximab tumor fluorescence peaked at 96 h, achieving high signal‐to‐noise ratios. Subsequently, to establish a clinically relevant large‐animal platform, we optimized an immunosuppression protocol for MMPs. Through iterative testing, we determined that a combination of neonatal thymectomy and pharmacologic immunosuppression (tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisolone), supplemented with antibiotics and proton‐pump inhibitors, was essential to prevent rejection, sepsis, and gastric ulcers. This optimized regimen enabled stable orthotopic engraftment of human ESCC via endoscopic submucosal injection in the MMP esophagus. Using a custom‐developed NIR fluorescence endoscope mimicking a clinical setting, we successfully visualized orthotopic tumors with clear margins at 96 h post‐injection, subsequently confirmed by immunohistochemistry. In conclusion, AF680‐cetuximab enables specific, high‐contrast detection of ESCC. Furthermore, we report the first successful orthotopic engraftment of human cancer cells in immunosuppressed MMPs. This model provides a powerful platform for developing fluorescence‐guided endoscopic diagnostics and theranostics for early‐stage esophageal cancer, bridging the gap to future clinical application.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is one of the most aggressive malignancies, ranking as the seventh leading cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide [1]. It is primarily classified into esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and adenocarcinoma, with ESCC being predominant in Asia [2]. The diagnostic methodology for ESCC is mainly endoscopic imaging and biopsy, and other imaging modalities including computed tomography, positron emission tomography, and endoscopic ultrasound [3]. Several endoscopic techniques, including image enhanced endoscopy (IEE) such as narrow band imaging and linked color imaging, chromoendoscopy such as iodine staining, and magnifying endoscopy are reported to increase the detection rate of early‐stage ESCC [4]. In particular, routine use of IEE during observation of the esophagus in upper GI endoscopy has been reported to increase the detection of ESCC. However, even with the use of IEE, it is often difficult to detect early‐stage ESCCs that extend through the submucosal layer covered with normal epithelia or exhibit minimal color change with unclear surface vascular patterns [5]. Therefore, a new diagnostic modality for detection of early‐stage ESCCs is needed.

Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is a receptor‐type tyrosine kinase, which plays a crucial role in cell proliferation and differentiation of ESCC [6, 7]. Overexpression of EGFR has been observed in approximately 60%–80% of ESCC cases, correlating with aggressive tumor behavior and poor prognosis [8, 9, 10, 11]. Therefore, EGFR‐targeted molecular imaging is expected to provide more specific and sensitive diagnosis of ESCC. Molecular imaging is a next‐generation endoscopic modality with high sensitivity and high specificity. However, compared with esophageal adenocarcinoma, studies on molecular imaging for ESCC remain relatively underdeveloped, highlighting a clear need for further research.

Previous animal studies on in vivo molecular imaging using fluorescence‐labeled antibodies have used various rodent models [12, 13]. Although these studies of small animals help with understanding efficacy and availability, it is often hard to extrapolate the results to human clinical models. Therefore, as a preclinical phase, it is important to establish a large animal model in which cancer cells are easily transplantable. Recently, the microminipigs (MMPs) have been developed as a non‐rodent large experimental model [14]. This MMP was optimized for human life science research including pharmacology, toxicology, and pharmacokinetics [15, 16], and various disease models of MMPs have been generated [17, 18]. However, immunodeficient MMP models permissive for the transplantation of human tumor cells have not yet been established, and no studies have reported in vivo imaging of xenografts in large animals.

Therefore, in this study, we first generated a fluorescence‐labeled anti‐EGFR antibody probe and performed in vitro and in vivo imaging experiments using a nude mouse xenograft model and in‐house‐developed fluorescent endoscopy instruments. We then established a new large animal model using MMPs for orthotopic transplantation of human ESCC cells to perform fluorescent molecular imaging studies using our newly developed fluorescence endoscopy technique.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

Five cell lines of human esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), KYSE150, KYSE190, KYSE350, KYSE410, and KYSE510 were purchased from the National Institutes of Biomedical Innovation, Health and Nutrition (Osaka, Japan). KYSE150 cell line was cultured in Ham's F‐12 medium supplemented with L‐glutamine, and the other 4 cell lines were cultured in a 1:1 mixture of RPMI1640 with L‐glutamine and Ham's medium supplemented with 5% FBS. All cell lines were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Cell Proliferation

We examined the proliferation speed of each ESCC. Three thousand ESCC cell lines were seeded in 96‐well plates and incubated at 37°C. Subsequently, cell proliferation at 24, 48, and 96 h was determined using a Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8, Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Absorbance at 450 nm and 600 nm was measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x Multi‐Mode Microplate Reader; Molecular Devices, CA).

EGFR Quantification by Flow Cytometry

The EGFR expression in each cell line was analyzed by flow cytometry using Alexa Fluor 488 labeled anti‐EGFR antibody (Cat. No. SC‐120 AF488: Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., TX) and negative control IgG (Cat. No. sc‐3891: Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc.). The ESCC cells (2 × 106) were incubated with either SC‐120 AF488 or negative control IgG (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc.). After incubation for 60 min on ice, the cells were washed with PBS and then analyzed for fluorescence intensity histograms using flow cytometry (EPICS XL‐MCL; Beckman Coulter Inc., CA). The mean fluorescence intensity was calculated from triplicate samples. Moreover, the number of EGFR on the cell surface (number per cell) was determined using a Quantum Simply Cellular Kit (Bangs Laboratories Inc., Fishers, IN). To generate the calibration curve, 5 sets of calibration beads with defined IgG binding capacities were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488‐labeled anti‐EGFR antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc.). The number of EGFR per cell was calculated based on the calibration curve using the mean fluorescence intensity value for each cell line.

Synthesis of AF680‐Labeled Anti‐EGFR Antibody

We used cetuximab (ERBITUX: Merck Biopharma Co. Ltd., Japan), an IgG1 monoclonal human/mouse chimera type anti‐human EGFR monoclonal antibody. After purification of cetuximab with a spin column‐based antibody purification kit (Cosmo Bio Co. LTD., Japan), cetuximab was labeled with Alexa Fluor (AF) 680 using an Alexa Fluor 680 antibody labeling kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) per the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, purified cetuximab (100 μg/100 μL) was incubated with AF680 in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) containing 100 mM potassium buffer (pH: 8.5) at 28°C for 1 h in the dark. Labeled antibodies were then purified using Zeba Desalting Spin Columns, 7 K MWCO (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). We also labeled negative control IgG (ChromPure Human IgG whole molecule, Cat. No. 009–000‐003, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories Inc., PA) with AF680 as described above. The protein concentration and dye per protein (D/P) ratio of AF680‐labeled antibody were determined by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm and 679 nm using a Nanodrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) with the following formulas [19].

Protein concentration (mg/mL) = [MWProtein × [A280‐(0.05 × A679)]]/εProtein, Dye per Protein ratio = [A280‐(0.05 × A680)/εProtein]/[A680/εDye], where MW indicates the molecular weight of the antibody, εProtein and εDye are molar extinction coefficients for the dye (AF680) and protein (antibody). The D/P ratio of AF680‐labeled anti‐EGFR antibody ranged from 4.7 to 5.2.

SDS‐Page

AF680‐labeled anti‐EGFR antibody (2.5 μg) was loaded into a 4%–20% gradient polyacrylamide gel (Mini‐PROTEAN TGX Gels; Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) and subjected to SDS‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) at 200 V for 30 min. The gels were then either stained with coomassie blue (Bio‐Rad Laboratories) or imaged with fluorescence at 638 nm using an excitation laser and 700/75 nm bandpass emission filter with a ChemiDocTM MP imager (Bio‐Rad Laboratories).

Western Blotting

Western blotting was performed as described previously [20]. Briefly, the lysates (25 μg protein/lane) of KYSE410 cells were loaded into 4%–20% gradient polyacrylamide gel and separated by SDS‐PAGE at 200 V for 30 min. The separated proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The following were used as primary antibodies: unlabeled cetuximab (10 μg/mL), AF680‐labeled cetuximab (10 μg/mL), and anti‐β‐actin monoclonal antibody (1:10000; Sigma‐Aldrich; Cat. No. A5441). Horseradish peroxidase‐labeled antibody against human IgG (1:10000; Cytiva Inc., Tokyo, Japan; Cat. No. NA933V) or mouse IgG (1:20000; Cytiva Inc.; Cat. No. NA931V) were used as secondary antibodies, and the incubation was performed at room temperature for 1 h. The signals were then visualized using ECL Select Western Blotting Detection Reagent (Cytiva Inc.) and imaged using ChemiDoc XRS Plus (590/110 emission filter: Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc.).

In Vitro Live Cell Imaging

The KYSE410 cells (5 × 103) were cultured in 35‐mm glass bottom dishes (Greiner Bio‐One) for 48 h. KYSE410 cells were then stained with AF680‐labeled cetuximab, followed by nuclear staining with Hoechst 33342 (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) on ice for 15 min in the dark. After washing with PBS, cells were observed under confocal microscopy (Nikon A1R; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) using a 638 nm excitation laser with a 700/75 nm band pass emission filter to detect AF680, and a 375 nm excitation laser with a 460 nm band pass emission filter to detect Hoechst 33342, respectively.

In Vivo Imaging of Heterotopic ESCC Xenografts With Mice

All animal experiments used in this study including mouse and pig models were performed according to the guidelines of the Committee on Animal Care and Use of Tokushima University and Gifu University. Five‐week‐old female BALB/c nu/nu mice (CLEA Japan Inc., Tokyo, Japan) were injected intraperitoneally with cyclophosphamide (0.2 mg/g per body weight, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) to promote KYSE410 engraftment. Two days after administration, KYSE410 cells (5 × 106) were transplanted into the right lower back of the mice under anesthesia, and the animals were housed until experimentation. When the tumor had grown to approximately 10 mm in diameter, AF680‐labeled cetuximab (20 μg) or AF680‐labeled negative control IgG (20 μg) was injected via the tail vein. Fluorescence images were acquired using an IVIS Spectrum (Perkin Elmer Inc.) with a 675/30 nm excitation filter and a 720/20 nm emission filter. The fluorescent areas in the tumor were designated as regions of interest (ROI), and the average fluorescence intensity in each ROI was measured to determine the chronological change up to 504 h.

Custom‐Developed Fluorescence Endoscopy System

An in‐house fluorescence endoscopy system was developed based on our previous report [21] using a conventional video endoscope (EG‐580NW2; Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan). The system comprised a thin‐type excitation light source and a custom tip hood equipped with a cut filter for fluorescence observation. The excitation light source was a 685‐nm diode laser. The laser beam was collimated and delivered to the observation field through the forceps channel of the endoscope. The tip hood was fitted with a cut filter designed to block light within the 650–710 nm wavelength range. For imaging, the collimator attached to the optical fiber from the laser source was positioned approximately 10 mm from the target tissue, producing a beam with an 11‐mm diameter and a power density of 147 mW/cm2. This configuration enabled visualization of AF680 fluorescence while effectively filtering out reflected excitation light.

Endoscopic Imaging of Heterotopic ESCC Xenografts in Mice

For fluorescence endoscopic observation of the heterotopic ESCC xenografts, KYSE410 cells were engrafted in mice as previously described. At 96 h after administration of either AF680‐labeled cetuximab or AF680‐labeled negative control IgG (20 μg in 100 μL saline) via the tail vein, the xenografted tumors were examined using the custom‐developed fluorescence endoscopy system described above.

Establishment of Novel Orthotopic ESCC Xenograft Model in Immunosuppressed Microminipigs

Male microminipigs (MMPs) were purchased from Fuji Micra Ltd. (Shizuoka, Japan). Because there are no previous reports about orthotopic engraftment of ESCC into MMP esophagus, we performed 4 different experiments by changing various conditions, such as the concentration of tacrolimus (Prograf capsule 5 mg; Astellas Pharma Inc., Japan), the use of antibiotics, i.e., cefalexin (PO BID, 18.75 mg/kg/day, cefaclear300, Veterinarian Medical Development Co. Ltd. Japan), thymectomy immediately after birth [22], and use of a proton pump inhibitor (rabeprazole sodium; Pariet tablets 10 mg; Eisai Co. Ltd. Japan). As other immunosuppressive medications, mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) (CELLCEPT Powder for Oral Suspension; Chugai Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., Japan) and prednisolone (prednisolone powder 1%; Teva Takeda Yakuhin Ltd., Japan) were also used, and the dosages of these medications were determined with reference to previously described methods [22, 23].

MMPs were housed under pathogen‐free conditions until the experiment. Immunosuppressive medication was initiated when the MMPs reached eight months of age and weighed 10.5 kg. The practical dosage of tacrolimus was adjusted to maintain a trough blood concentration of 10–20 ng/mL, as measured on Days 3, 8, and 15. All drugs were mixed with feed and administered orally.

Eight days after medication, the KYSE410 cells (1 × 106) in 1 mL PBS and 1 mL of Matrigel Matrix (356,231, Corning) were mixed and then injected into the submucosa of the esophagus through a catheter using an endoscope (EG‐580NW2; Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) under general anesthesia. After confirming the engraftment of KYSE410 cells in MMP esophagus by endoscopy, AF680‐labeled anti‐EGFR antibody (1400 μg/body) in 100 mL of saline was slowly injected via the auricular vein under general anesthesia for 3 h. Ninety‐six h after antibody injection, we observed tumor xenografts using fluorescence endoscopy. After endoscopic observation in vivo, MMPs were sacrificed under deep anesthesia, and the esophagus with tumor was extracted. The tumor was again observed using fluorescence endoscopy ex vivo. Finally, the tumor xenograft was excised and embedded in OCT compound, followed by snap freezing in liquid nitrogen. Tumor tissue was then cut into 5‐μm sections and stained with anti‐human IgG antibody (A80‐219P, Bethyl Laboratories Inc.) to detect the anti‐EGFR antibody. The excised tumor tissue was also fixed with formalin and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H‐E).

Statistics

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences among multiple groups were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. Differences in fluorescence intensity between groups over time were analyzed by two‐way mixed ANOVA, followed by t‐tests with Bonferroni correction for post hoc comparisons at each time point. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.2 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Proliferation and EGFR Expression of ESCC Cell Lines

To identify an appropriate ESCC cell line for transplantation experiments in mice and MMPs, we first evaluated proliferative capacity using a WST assay and assessed surface EGFR expression, including quantification of surface EGFR molecules, by flow cytometry in five ESCC cell lines. KYSE510 exhibited the highest proliferation rate, followed by KYSE410 and KYSE350, whereas KYSE190 and KYSE150 showed relatively lower proliferation (Figure 1A).

EGFR expression was detected in all cell lines, with the highest mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) observed in KYSE150, followed by KYSE190, KYSE410, KYSE350, and KYSE510 (Figure 1B). EGFR expression levels differed significantly among the cell lines (one‐way ANOVA, F = 523.1, p < 0.0001). Tukey's post hoc analysis demonstrated significant differences between most pairs, except for KYSE350 and KYSE510 (p = 0.41). Similarly, quantification of surface EGFR molecules per cell revealed a comparable expression pattern (Figure 1C). Differences among the five cell lines were significant (one‐way ANOVA, F = 677.7, p < 0.0001). Post hoc Tukey analysis demonstrated statistically significant differences between all pairs of cell lines, with p < 0.0001 for all comparisons except for KYSE510 versus KYSE350 (p = 0.0325). Given that higher proliferative capacity facilitates successful tumor establishment in transplantation models [24], whereas higher EGFR expression enhances molecular imaging performance, we next considered the balance between these parameters. KYSE510 showed the highest proliferation but the lowest EGFR expression, while KYSE150 exhibited the highest EGFR expression but relatively low proliferation. Thus, neither cell line alone satisfied both criteria. In contrast, KYSE410 demonstrated the second‐highest proliferative capacity together with an intermediate‐to‐high level of EGFR expression, representing the most balanced profile among the five cell lines. Based on these considerations, KYSE410 was selected for subsequent in vivo experiments.

Characteristics of AF680‐Labeled Anti‐EGFR Antibody Derived From Cetuximab

Cetuximab and a negative control IgG were labeled with the near‐infrared fluorescent dye AF680 and purified for characterization. On a coomassie‐stained SDS‐PAGE gel, a clear, single band for AF680‐labeled cetuximab was observed at an approximate molecular weight of 150 kDa (Figure 1D). Excitation light irradiation revealed a corresponding single fluorescent band of comparable intensity to the negative control IgG, confirming successful labeling (Figure 1E). The antigen‐binding capacity was then examined by western blotting. The AF680‐labeled cetuximab yielded a stronger EGFR‐binding signal compared with unlabeled cetuximab, which may be attributable to a fluorophore‐dependent enhancement of detection efficiency (Figure 1F). Finally, in live‐cell imaging of KYSE410 cells, AF680‐labeled cetuximab was observed to localize predominantly to the cell membrane, whereas negligible signal was detected from the negative control IgG (Figure 1G). These results confirm that the AF680‐labeled cetuximab specifically binds to EGFR on the surface of ESCC cells.

In Vivo Imaging of an ESCC Mouse Xenograft Model

To investigate the in vivo dynamics and distribution of the fluorescence probe, AF680‐labeled cetuximab or AF680‐labeled negative control IgG was administered to BALB/c nu/nu mice bearing ESCC xenografts, and tumor imaging was performed using the IVIS Spectrum system (Figure 2A–C). Representative chronological images and corresponding fluorescence intensity curves are shown in Figure 2B,C. In mice treated with AF680‐labeled cetuximab, tumor fluorescence intensity gradually increased, reaching a peak of 32.16 ± 4.43 × 105 counts/s/cm2/str at 96 h post‐injection, and subsequently declined (Figure 2C). By contrast, in mice injected with AF680‐labeled negative control IgG, fluorescence intensity reached a weaker peak of 19.97 ± 1.96 × 105 counts/s/cm2/str at 24 h and then decreased. A significant difference in fluorescence intensity between the 2 groups was observed from 96 to 288 h post‐injection (Figure 2C).

Fluorescence Endoscopic Imaging of an ESCC Mouse Xenograft Model

Next, we observed the xenografted tumors at 96 h post‐injection using a custom‐developed fluorescence endoscopy system (a schematic is shown in Figure 2D). In mice that received AF680‐labeled cetuximab, upon irradiation with a 685‐nm excitation laser delivered through the forceps channel, a clear, red fluorescence signal with a sharp demarcation was observed from the tumor region. In contrast, no significant fluorescence was detected from adjacent non‐tumor tissue in the same mouse, or from the tumors of mice that received the AF680‐labeled negative control IgG (Figure 2E). These results demonstrate that the fluorescence endoscopy system could clearly distinguish tumors accumulating the AF680‐labeled cetuximab from surrounding tissue and negative controls.

Establishment of an ESCC Orthotopic Xenograft Model in Immunosuppressed Microminipigs

To establish orthotopic ESCC xenografts in immunosuppressed MMPs, we performed the following 4 experiments (Figure 3A). We first administered tacrolimus (TAC) (1.5 mg/kg/day), prednisolone (PSL) (2.6 mg/kg/day), and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) (60 mg/kg/day) to MMPs from days 1–24 (Experiment 1). Although ESCC was successfully engrafted, the MMP died due to sepsis on day 24 after medication with immunosuppressants. To avoid this immunocompromising complication, we reduced the dose of TAC by half (to 0.75 mg/kg/day) and additionally administered cefalexin (18.75 mg/kg/day) (Experiment 2). Whereas the MMP was able to survive without severe infection, ESCC engraftment was not observed. Therefore, referring to models using nude mice, which have no thymus gland, we decided to use thymectomized MMPs and administered TAC, PSL, MMF and cefalexin for 24 days (Experiment 3). Although ESCC successfully engrafted to the MMP, the MMP died from hemorrhagic shock due to gastric ulcer on day 24 after medication with immunosuppressants. To prevent gastric ulcer formation due to PSL and stress, we added rabeprazole (10 mg/day) as well as TAC (0.75 mg/kg/day), PSL (2.6 mg/kg/day), MMF (60 mg/kg/day), and cefalexin (18.75 mg/kg/day) for 24 days (Experiment 4) (Figure 3A). Finally, MMPs were able to survive without severe infection and gastric ulcer formation, and the ESCC was successfully engrafted into the esophagus of the MMPs. The timeline for transplantation and subsequent fluorescence endoscopic imaging in the MMPs is shown in Figure 3B, alongside representative endoscopic images. Throughout the experimental period, biochemical and complete blood count data for the MMP revealed no significant abnormalities (Table S1).

Fluorescence Endoscopic Imaging of ESCC in the MMP Orthotopic Xenograft Model

On day 19, we performed endoscopy to confirm orthotopic esophageal tumor engraftment in the MMP. We then administered AF680‐labeled cetuximab intravenously via the auricular vein.

The 96‐h imaging time point was selected based on the in vivo mouse kinetics data (Figure 2B,C), which demonstrated peak tumor fluorescence intensity and optimal tumor‐to‐background contrast at this time. At 96 h after antibody injection, the xenograft was visualized with our custom near‐infrared fluorescence endoscope. Under white light, the submucosal tumor appeared as a smooth, well‐defined elevation (Figure 4A). After the scope was withdrawn, a tip hood equipped with a long‐pass cutoff filter was attached to the scope, and the endoscope was reinserted for fluorescence imaging. With 685 nm excitation delivered through the forceps channel, a clear red fluorescent signal with a sharp margin was detected through the esophageal mucosa at the corresponding white‐light site. After in vivo imaging, the MMP was euthanized under deep anesthesia, and the esophagus containing the tumor was excised for ex vivo fluorescence endoscopy. Consistent with the in vivo findings, a clear red fluorescent signal was observed in the corresponding area, which presented as a smooth, well‐defined elevation under white light (Figure 4B). The excised tumor was processed for histology to confirm engraftment of human ESCC: H&E at low magnification showed a tumor predominantly composed of cancer cells (Figure 4C), and high magnification showed a high nuclear‐to‐cytoplasmic ratio with prominent nucleoli (Figure 4D). Finally, immunohistochemistry using an anti‐human IgG antibody (to detect cetuximab) demonstrated strong staining in the tumor injected with AF680‐labeled cetuximab compared with the no–AF680‐cetuximab control (Figure 4E–H).

Discussion

In this study, fluorescence (AF680)‐labeled anti‐EGFR antibody showed strong fluorescent signals not only in heterotopic ESCC xenografts of mice but also in orthotopic xenografts of MMPs. An in‐house‐developed fluorescence endoscope was able to detect specific, high‐intensity fluorescence arising from AF680‐labeled anti‐EGFR antibody accumulated in ESCC xenografts. Additionally, we established a large animal orthotopic tumor xenograft model using thymectomized MMPs under pharmacologic immunosuppression. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating that human cancer cells readily engraft in thymectomized large‐animal hosts under pharmacologic immunosuppression. This platform enables a broad range of studies in cancer diagnosis and therapy, including endoscopic diagnosis of GI cancers.

Regarding the fluorescent molecular imaging targeting EGFR for the diagnosis of cancers, we previously reported successful in vivo imaging of colorectal tumors in mouse/rat models using AF488‐labeled anti‐human EGFR antibody [25]. We could detect colorectal tumors in the colorectum of rats treated with azoxymethane using veterinary fluorescent endoscopy. However, the S/N ratio in tumors was relatively low (4.1 ± 0.7) due to autofluorescence of the mucosa. Therefore, a higher S/N ratio is needed when the tumor is small in size and of flat type such as early‐stage esophageal cancer. In this respect, AF680 is a near‐infrared fluorescent (NIR) dye (excitation/emission ≈679/702 nm) that allows deeper tissue penetration and minimizes autofluorescence, thereby making it well suited for fluorescence imaging [13]. Indeed, the S/N ratios achieved in the current study were substantially higher, measuring 7.1 ± 0.4 in the mouse model (Figure 2E).

The EGFR expression in ESCCs is reported to be 60%–80%, which may not be sufficiently high. However, the positivity rates of the other molecules including c‐MET, Glut‐1, and EpCAM, which have been reported as targets of molecular imaging for ESCC, ranges from 45%–80% [26]. Thus, the positivity rates of EGFR in ESCC are among the highest. Therefore, it may be an effective strategy in the future for generating a bispecific antibody against the 2 target molecules (i.e., EGFR and c‐MET).

In this study, we purchased wild‐type or thymectomized MMPs and housed them in a conventional laboratory animal facility. Although some of the MMPs treated with strong immunosuppressants (tacrolimus 1.5 mg/kg) died due to severe infection (sepsis), no other (wild‐type and thymectomized) MMPs with mild immunosuppressant (tacrolimus 0.75 mg/kg) developed infectious complications during the study. Moreover, as in clinical practice, we inserted a clinical‐grade human endoscope into MMPs under anesthesia and clearly visualized xenografts and the esophagus (Figure 4). Thus, the MMP model is more realistic and translationally relevant for human medicine in the setting of pharmacokinetics, drug metabolism, and safety (adverse events), although relatively higher costs and staff skilled in husbandry for MMPs may be associated.

Engraftment of cancer cells to the esophagus in wild‐type MMPs failed, despite administration of tacrolimus 1.5 mg/kg/day, prednisolone 2.6 mg/kg/day, and MMF 60 mg/kg/day, consistent with previous reports [13]. When we measured the serum tacrolimus level at day 8, it was 29.1 ng/mL, which was excessively high. On the other hand, when tacrolimus 0.75 mg/kg/day, prednisolone 2.6 mg/kg/day, and MMF 60 mg/kg/day were administered, cancer cells were rejected, although the whole‐blood tacrolimus level was 9.02 ng/mL, which is within the range of 8–12 ng/mL, applicable for early‐stage renal transplantation. This suggests that the protocol may not provide sufficient immunosuppression for cancer cell engraftment. Moreover, the most important and difficult challenge is that the immunosuppressed condition must be continued for the entire study period, which was 3 weeks in this study. To overcome this problem, we used thymectomized MMPs in combination with mild immunosuppressant treatment (tacrolimus 0.75 mg/kg/day), an antibiotic (cefalexin), and a proton pump inhibitor (PPI; rabeprazole), and successfully engrafted cancer cells.

The fluorescence molecular imaging technology and the orthotopic MMP xenograft model developed in this study hold promise for a wide range of future applications. Clinically, this technology is expected to contribute to the high‐precision diagnosis of early‐stage esophageal cancers that are difficult to detect with conventional endoscopy and to improve diagnostic accuracy through fluorescence‐guided “pinpoint” biopsies. Furthermore, the MMP model, with its physiological similarity to humans, can be utilized as a powerful preclinical platform to evaluate the efficacy and safety of new molecular‐targeted drugs. Looking forward, there is potential for this platform to evolve into a theranostic approach—integrating diagnostics and therapeutics—by replacing the diagnostic fluorescent probe with a therapeutic agent.

However, several challenges must be addressed before this technology can be translated into clinical practice. First, while cetuximab is a clinically approved therapeutic antibody, in vivo pharmacokinetics and safety of the AF680‐conjugated probe require further evaluation to address any potential effects of the AF680 dye itself. Second, the custom‐developed fluorescence endoscopy system used in this study requires further optimization for routine clinical use, including improvements in usability and system integration. Third, although KYSE410 was selected based on a balanced profile of proliferative capacity and EGFR expression, the inclusion of additional ESCC cell lines with divergent EGFR expression levels would further enhance the generalizability and robustness of the present findings. In addition, the present model represents a submucosal xenograft generated by direct injection of ESCC cells and therefore does not fully recapitulate the multistep epithelial carcinogenesis observed in spontaneous ESCC. Differences in tumor–microenvironment interactions and mucosal architecture may exist compared with naturally occurring epithelial esophageal carcinoma. Nevertheless, this model enables controlled evaluation of fluorescence‐guided detection of submucosal or minimally visible lesions, which remain clinically challenging in endoscopic practice. Despite these limitations, the successful preclinical results in our newly established MMP model provide a strong rationale for planning future clinical trials to validate the efficacy and safety of this approach in human patients.

In conclusion, we successfully detected clear fluorescence signals in ESCC xenografts of mice and MMPs after administration of an AF680‐labeled anti‐EGFR antibody using our in‐house developed fluorescent endoscopy system. We established an orthotopic ESCC xenograft model using thymectomized MMPs in combination with immunosuppressants, an antibiotic, and a PPI. This molecular imaging method has the potential to enable an early diagnosis of ESCC with high sensitivity and specificity.
