Determinants of the use of insecticide-treated nets and intermittent preventive treatment of malaria among pregnant women in Angola
Authors: Ai Aoki, Makoto Saito, Olukunmi O Balogun, Hirotsugu Aiga, Ketha R Francisco, Kenji Takehara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe determinants of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) use and uptake of three doses or more of intermittent preventive treatment with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp 3+) among pregnant women can vary between regions and is not well studied in Angola.MethodsThis study analysed secondary data of a cluster-randomized controlled trial that evaluated the impact of the Maternal and Child Health Handbook (MCH-HB) on the continuum of care among pregnant women and mothers in Angola from 2019 to 2020. Those who received antenatal care (ANC) were analysed. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to assess factors associated with ITN use and IPTp 3+ uptake.ResultsAmong 8336 participants, 62.7% used ITNs and 48.7% achieved IPTp 3+. Only 31.7% achieved both. Single women were less likely to use ITNs. Women who completed secondary education, were wealthier and achieved IPTp 3+ were more likely to use ITNs. Teenagers, multiparous mothers, rural area residents and those who attended ANC later were less likely to achieve IPTp 3+. Those who completed secondary education, were wealthier, had longer travel times to health facilities and were in the MCH-HB group were more likely to achieve IPTp 3+.ConclusionsThe identified risk groups can be targeted for interventions to improve coverage.Keywords: Angola, insecticide-treated nets, intermittent preventive treatment, malaria infection during pregnancy, Maternal and Child Health Handbook

Introduction

Malaria infection during pregnancy is a serious health issue in malaria-endemic areas, especially in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). It leads to anaemia in pregnant women, stillbirth, premature delivery and low birthweight (intrauterine growth restriction) of newborns.1 According to the World Malaria Report, in 33 moderate to high malaria transmission countries in Africa, including Angola, 12.4 million women were estimated to suffer from malaria infection during pregnancy and 351 000 neonates with low birthweight were estimated to be due to malaria infection during pregnancy in 2023.2

As preventive measures, the effectiveness of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp) with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) have been established. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends ITNs, case management and prompt treatment with effective antimalarials, and in moderate- to high-transmission areas, IPTp for pregnant women.3 ITN is a widespread vector control intervention that kills or sterilizes host-seeking mosquitos.4 ITN is estimated to have contributed to 68% of malaria cases averted by malaria preventive measures between 2000 and 2015 in endemic African counties.5 The WHO guideline recommends to start IPTp-SP in the second trimester, at least 1 month apart, and at least three doses in total during pregnancy.4 Antenatal care (ANC) is an important opportunity to provide IPTp and also ITNs.4 IPTp is demonstrated to reduce maternal anaemia and placental and maternal malaria infection at delivery and increase the birthweight of newborns.4 Universal coverage is recommended by the WHO for both ITNs and IPTp.2,6 Normally, a minimum of 80% coverage is targeted to achieve universal coverage.7

Despite continuous efforts to promote malaria prevention measures, the coverage of ITNs and IPTp remains below the target levels. In 2023, 59% of pregnant women were sleeping under ITNs and 44% received three or more doses of IPTp (IPTp 3+) in SSA.2 In the 33 malaria-endemic African countries, IPTp is estimated to avert an additional 152 954 cases of low birthweight if the coverage of IPTp 3+ is scaled up to the level of ANC at least once.2 A previous systematic literature review showed that there were some key barriers to ITNs and IPTp; some were common among different SSA countries while others were context specific.8 Recent studies also demonstrated context-specific determinants.9–11 Identifying both common and context-specific key barriers is thus crucial to improving the situation.8

Angola, where this study was conducted, is one of the 10 countries with the highest number of malaria infections2 and malaria is one of the top priority health issues. In 2011, malaria contributed to 40% of prenatal mortality, 25% of maternal mortality and 35% of infant mortality.12 The National Health Development Plan 2012–2025 determined pregnant women and children <5 y of age are the target population for malaria prevention.13 In the latest Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) in 2015–2016, the coverage of ITNs was 23.0% and IPTp 3+ was 19.0% among pregnant women.12 The National Development Plan 2018–2022 defined the ITN coverage target as 60% of households with long-lasting ITNs by 2022 and the IPTp coverage target was 50% of pregnant women receiving four or more doses of IPTp (IPTp 4+) by 2022.14 Although efforts have been made to increase the coverage since then, the latest coverage of ITN use and the uptake of IPTp 3+ remains unknown, and the key barriers to their uptake have not been well studied. To improve the coverage, key barriers to ITN use and IPTp uptake should be identified within the specific context of Angola. This study aims to elucidate these barriers among pregnant women who attended ANC at least once to determine targets for future interventions.

Methods

Study design

This study analysed secondary data of a cluster-randomized trial (ISRCTN20510127) that examined the effectiveness of the Maternal and Child Health Handbook (MCH-HB) on the continuum of care among pregnant women and mothers in Benguela Province, Angola between June 2019 and October 2020 (MCH-HB RCT). The original study was conducted by our research team. The details of the original study are published elsewhere.15,16

Study settings

The population of Benguela Province is approximately 2.2 million.17 Benguela is a coastal province with a tropical climate. Malaria is in a meso-endemic state with stable transmission;12 the annual incidence was 181 per 1000 population in 2018.18

Participants

All women who became pregnant between March and April 2019 and used maternal, neonatal and child health (MNCH) services at least once were included in the original study.16 As ANC is a key opportunity to provide ITNs and IPTp to pregnant women, and ANC practice is an opportunity to expand the coverage of ITNs and IPTp, only the participants who attended ANC at least once were analysed in this study.

Measures

The dataset contains demographic, socio-economic and MNCH service utilization variables, including the use of ITNs and the uptake of IPTp 3+. The data were collected through interviewer-administered structured questionnaires. Where applicable, data reliability was enhanced by combining self-recall and home-based records.

Definition of outcomes

ITN use was a binary variable, i.e. used or not used, depending on whether pregnant women slept under ITNs the night before the interview. IPTp 3+ and IPTp 4+ were defined by the greater number among participants’ recall and home-based records. As the Angolan government set the goal of IPTp 4+,14 an additional analysis with a dependent variable of IPTp 4+ was conducted.

Definition of explanatory variables

The known determinants of ITN use and IPTp 3+ uptake identified in a previous systematic review and meta-analysis were selected a priori as explanatory variables.8 As this study used a dataset of an interventional study, intervention allocation was also included.

For ITN use, maternal age, maternal education, marital status, maternal employment, wealth index, residential area, municipality classification, parity, IPTp 3+ uptake and intervention allocation were assessed.

For IPTp 3+ uptake, maternal age, maternal education, marital status, maternal employment, wealth index, residential area, municipality classification, parity, time of the first ANC, travel time to the health facility, ITN use and intervention allocation were assessed. Although the number of ANC visits was shown to be a determinant of IPTp, it was not included as an explanatory variable for IPTp because it was highly correlated with the first ANC timing.

Maternal age was a categorical variable: teenagers, 20–34 y old (reference group) and ≥35 y old. Marital status was a binary variable: married and/or cohabitating and single/divorced/widowed. Employment status was a binary variable: employed and unemployed. Education level was a categorical variable: no formal education, primary education and secondary education or higher. The wealth index is a quintile variable from the poorest to the richest based on household assets. Parity was a binary variable: nulliparous and multiparous. Timing of the first ANC visit was a categorical variable: first trimester, (2) second trimester and third trimester. Travel time to the health facility was a categorical variable: <30 min, 30 min–1 h and ≥1 h. Residential area by health facility level was a binary variable: urban and rural. Residential area classifications were defined by the local project team members of the MCH-HB RCT and personnel of the Benguela Provincial Health Office, considering access, infrastructure and economic levels. Municipality classification was a binary variable: urban and rural. Intervention allocation was a binary variable: control and intervention. The intervention was the MCH-HB program. Women in the intervention group were given the MCH-HB at their first visit to the MNCH service and then received the baseline interview of the MCH-HB RCT. The MCH-HB is a tool for recording key health indicators from pregnancy until the child reaches the age of 5 y. It also contains educational content, including ITN use and IPTp uptake.19 Women in the control group received conventional mother and child cards with no educational content.

Statistical analyses

To evaluate the factors influencing the use of ITNs and the uptake of IPTp 3+, we performed univariable and multivariable two-level random effects logistic regression analyses, with random intercepts for municipality accounting for clustering effects. In multivariable analyses, the entire predefined set of explanatory variables was incorporated into the model. Variance inflation factor was used for assessing multicollinearity. The statistical significance threshold was set at p=0.05. For missing information, multilevel joint modelling multiple imputation using a multivariate normal model was conducted. Twenty imputed datasets were generated based on the proportion of the missingness (approximately 15%). In the imputation model, outcomes and all the explanatory variables were included. The participants with missing outcomes were included in the imputation model but were excluded from the analyses. This was a secondary data analysis and therefore sample size calculation was not conducted.

The analysis was conducted using the lme4 package and jomo package in R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).20,21

Ethics

The MCH-HB RCT was approved by the ethics committees of the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Angola (protocol no. 15/2018) and the National Center for Child Health and Development, Japan (no. 1721). All the participants provided written informed consent.

Results

Among 9039 enrolled participants, 8336 who visited ANC at least once were eligible and thus included in the present analysis. The mean age was 24.7 y (standard deviation [SD] 6.3). Among them, 5226 (62.7%) used ITNs. The mean number of IPTp received was 2.5 (SD 1.2): 7692 (95.0%) had at least 1 dose, 4060 (48.7%) completed three or more doses and 1656 (20.5%) completed four or more doses. Among 4514 women who visited ANC four or more times, 3184 (70.6%) achieved IPTp 3+. A total of 2644 participants (31.7%) achieved both ITN use and IPTp 3+. The coverage of IPTp 3+ demonstrated more variation among different groups compared with the coverage of ITN use. The background characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Discussion

This study demonstrated the latest coverage and determinants of ITNs and IPTp 3+ among pregnant women who visited ANC at least once in Angola in 2019–2020. The coverage was 62.7% and 48.7%, respectively. We identified determinants that are common in other countries as well as those specific to Angola. Women who were single, less educated, poorer, with IPTp 3+ not achieved were less likely to use ITNs. Teenagers, those who were less educated, poorer, living in rural areas, multiparous, visiting ANC later, requiring short travel time and allocated to the control group during the MCH-HB RCT were less likely to achieve IPTp 3+. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first population-based study to evaluate the determinants of ITNs and IPTp in Angola despite the fact that Angola is one of the 10 countries with the highest burden of malaria.2

When we compare our results with previous studies in other countries, there were common determinants and the Angolan context-specific determinants. Among the key common determinants of ITN use that were demonstrated in the previous meta-analysis, such as marital status, education and the uptake of IPTp,8 our multivariable analysis replicated most of them. In our study, higher wealth index was significantly associated with increased ITN use. The previous meta-analysis showed significant regional variation in the association between economic status and ITNs.8 In Angola, this might be explained by frequent stockout of products and medications in health facilities, which requires women to purchase ITNs or IPTp outside the health facility despite the free distribution policy.13,22 In such a case, the economic situation reasonably affects their usage.

Regarding IPTp 3+, our multivariable analysis also replicated most of the known determinants, such as education, economic status, parity, timing of the first ANC and ITN use.8 In addition to these, specific to Angola, this study demonstrated residential area, travel time to the health facility and allocation to the MCH-HB group were significantly associated with IPTp 3+ uptake. The previous meta-analysis noted that the association of residential area showed regional variation, with completely opposite effects among different countries.8 In our study, rural residence by health facility level and rural municipality showed significantly lower odds of the uptake of IPTp 3+. This strong association was a characteristic of the determinants of IPTp 3+ in Angola. The difference by rural residence was also highlighted in the latest DHS in 2015–2016. This might be explained by the systematic difference in the quality of health services and the health service utilization between urban and rural areas in Angola. First, the common challenges of insufficient human resources, equipment and medication and associated inadequate function are noted in rural health facilities in Angola. Reports of the MCH-HB project noted a big difference in basic knowledge between health workers in urban and rural areas.23 Also, the MCH-HB study showed that the implementation of MCH-HB was better in urban facilities compared with rural facilities.22,29 There was assumed to be a similar difference in the provision of IPTp between urban and rural facilities and rural facilities are assumed to have difficulty in providing IPTp complying with the national recommendations. Implementation factors such as stock management and health workers’ adherence to the IPTp guidelines may contribute to the difference.

From the pregnant women's perspectives, those in rural areas utilize MNCH services less compared with those in urban areas, which was also demonstrated in the MCH-HB RCT.16,24 Multiple factors such as access to health facilities, quality of health services, out-of-pocket payments due to stockouts and health illiteracy are reported to exist.12 In our study, longer travel time to the health facility showed a null association with IPTp 3+ in univariable analyses but showed a significant positive association after adjusting for other explanatory variables. This was counterintuitive, as longer travel time caused by distance and transport availability is an obvious barrier to health service uptake.12,25 We suspect that this association, which appeared after adjustment, was a chance finding or overadjustment, as there were more socio-economically disadvantaged populations in remote areas and the same travelling time could have had different meanings in rural and urban areas. Furthermore, allocation to the MCH-HB group was associated with improved IPTp 3+ uptake. The MCH-HB might have supported health workers to provide IPTp following the records, as well as encouraged pregnant women to receive IPTp and sleep under ITNs through its educational content.16 Its educational content includes a message to instruct pregnant women and children to sleep under ITNs, accompanied by an intuitive illustration that conveys the message. The effectiveness of educational programs on ITN use was also shown in a systematic review.19,26

In the most recent DHS in 2015–2016, 81.6% of pregnant women received ANC at least once, ITN coverage was 23.0% and IPTp 3+ coverage was 19.0%.12 In this study, although they are not directly comparable, coverage among women who received ANC was 62.7% for ITNs and 48.7% for IPTp 3+. Assuming that at least the same level of ANC attendance with the 2015–2016 DHS was maintained during the study period, ITN and IPTp coverage among all pregnant women was assumed to have increased. However, both need further improvement to achieve universal coverage. In addition, it should be noted that inequity in IPTp 3+ coverage by pregnant women's residential area, education level and economic status was replicated in the present study.12 Equitable scale-up of the coverage of malaria prevention is needed.

Based on the results, to further promote ITNs, from the health workers’ perspective, stock management and distribution without out-of-pocket payment, which has been a national policy in Angola,13 must be ensured at ANC visits. To prevent stockouts, stock management should be conducted based on estimated needs rather than demand from health facilities. A potential strategy to reach pregnant women who do not visit ANC or who cannot get ITNs at ANC visits could be mass distribution campaigns. Furthermore, women with the determinants associated with lower use of ITNs need to be identified and educated at ANC visits so that they sleep under ITNs. To facilitate health education at health facilities, the use of the educational contents of the MCH-HB can be promoted. Community education is another strategy. The MCH-HB implementation study suggested the importance of involving community and religious leaders for effective community engagement.22

IPTp requires more procedures for health workers than ITNs, and more health service–related factors are thought to contribute to the uptake of IPTp.8 This study only includes rurality by health facility location and municipality as factors common to both individuals and health facilities and rurality was associated with inadequate IPTp uptake in Angola. To solve the aforementioned challenges, a higher level of commitment to strengthening the functioning of rural health facilities and health workers is necessary. To achieve a high level of commitment, national, regional and municipal health offices must establish a shared objective of fortifying rural health facilities through the effective allocation of funding, human resources, equipment, medication and supervision. To support health workers’ practice, a thorough analysis of the barriers to IPTp and identification of supportive strategies is necessary. The same considerations should be used for ITNs, including stock management, a targeted approach to women with determinants associated with lower uptake of IPTp 3+ and community education. Given teenagers are a risk group, preconception health education is also important. If these approaches are not sufficient, alternative IPTp delivery strategies need to be considered.1 Community delivery of IPTp was shown to increase IPTp coverage in a trial in four SSA countries to overcome access issues.27 Finally, as ITN use and IPTp 3+ uptake were bidirectional determinants of the other, and share the strategies to improve coverage, both need to be promoted simultaneously.

This study has several limitations. First, because the original study did not include women who did not visit any MNCH services, this study included only those who visited ANC services. The coverage of ITNs and IPTp 3+ demonstrated in this study cannot be directly compared with coverage among all pregnant women. Second, we did not assess specific knowledge about malaria, ITNs and IPTp in our study, although they were demonstrated to be a key determinant of both ITNs and IPTp in the previous meta-analysis.8 Third, we assessed few health facility–related factors. Health facilities play an important role in the provision of malaria prevention. A number of barriers at this level have been reported, such as poor stock management and poor knowledge about IPTp among health workers. Determinants focusing on health workers, health facilities and health systems need to be considered in the future to improve implementation. Fourth, we could not ignore the impact of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic and associated changes in health service utilization, as the MCH-HB RCT was conducted between 2019 and 2020. COVID-19 was reported to affect health-seeking behaviour as well as malaria programs globally,2 which could have resulted in a decrease in the coverage of ITNs and uptake of IPTp 3+ during this period compared with before COVID-19.

Conclusions

This study showed that the coverage of ITNs and IPTp 3+ needs to be further strengthened. Most of the determinants that were common across countries were replicated while demonstrating Angolan-specific determinants such as economic status for ITNs and rurality for IPTp 3+. Pregnant women of identified risk groups, the community and the health system need to be targeted to further increase coverage and address inequities in coverage. Preconception health education for teenagers, including encouragement of earlier ANC attendance, followed by reinforcement during pregnancy through the MCH-HB, can be effective as a targeted public health intervention. To combat the Angolan-specific challenge of IPTp 3+ coverage in rural areas, high-level commitment is also indispensable.
