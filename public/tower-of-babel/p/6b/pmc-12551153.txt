Association between housework frequency and cognitive impairment among Chinese older adults: a prospective cohort study based on CLHLS
Authors: Zhenni Wang, Hui Han, Ying Chen, Chengxiu Ling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHousework is a crucial aspect of instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), essential for maintaining the independence of older adults. This study aims to explore the association between housework frequency and cognitive impairment by incorporating a multidimensional assessment of cognitive impairment risk factors.MethodsThis study utilized data from the Chinese Longitudinal Health and Longevity Survey (CLHLS, 2014–2018), including 2797 older adults, after excluding individuals aged < 65 years, with cognitive impairment or self-reported dementia, or missing education data at baseline, along with those missing Chinese Mini-Mental State Examination (CMMSE) scores at either baseline or follow-up. Cognitive function was assessed using the education-adjusted CMMSE scores, while housework frequency was classified into three levels. Logistic regression models were employed to examine the association of housework frequency with the risk of cognitive impairment, after adjusting for demographics, living habits, and health-related factors. Further adjustments were made sequentially for baseline CMMSE scores and exercise.ResultsThe risk of cognitive impairment was higher among females, those aged 85 and above and non-exercisers. Compared with those who did housework almost everyday, the risk of cognitive impairment was significantly increased among those who did housework sometimes (OR = 1.50; 95% CI, 1.03–2.17), and even higher among those who never did housework (OR = 1.98; 95% CI, 1.49–2.65) after adjusting for demographics, living habits, and health-related factors. Similar associations were obtained after further adjustments sequentially for baseline CMMSE scores and exercise. In addition, hearing loss, social isolation, and non-smoking were identified as risk factors influencing cognitive impairment. No significant interaction effects were found between age and gender with housework frequency.ConclusionsOur study revealed that Chinese older adults who engaged in less housework had a higher risk of cognitive impairment. Doing housework could serve as a cost-effective strategy to reduce this risk in the context of a healthy aging population in China.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24331-6.Keywords: Aging population, Housework activity, Cognitive impairment, Cohort study

Introduction

Cognitive decline is a significant public health challenge that can lead to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or dementia. In 2019, dementia was the seventh leading cause of death globally, with an estimated economic cost of $1.3 trillion [1]. MCI, a risk state for dementia, has a prevalence ranging from 3 to 19% of adults aged 65 and above [2]. As the global population ages, the prevalence of cognitive impairment is projected to increase substantially, adversely influencing both the mental health and well-being of affected individuals and their caregivers, while also imposing higher societal costs [3]. China currently has the world’s largest aging population, with 400 million Chinese citizens aged 65 years and above by 2050, and 150 million aged 80 years and older, indicating that the burden of cognitive impairment in China is poised to escalate steadily [4, 5]. Given these challenges, identifying risk factors and prevention strategies is critical for mitigating cognitive impairment and improving quality-of-life outcomes.

Current research has identified various demographic characteristics, lifestyles and health-related risk factors associated with cognitive impairment [6–15]. Aging remains the strongest non-modifiable risk factor, with prevalence doubling every five years after age 65 [6]. Conversely, higher educational level and socioeconomic status demonstrate protective benefits, reducing dementia risk by 7% per additional year of education and mitigating disparities linked to income inequality [7, 8]. Meanwhile, poor lifestyles (e.g., short sleeping, unhealthy diets, smoking, excessive alcohol intake, and physical inactivity) [7, 9–12] and adverse health status (e.g., cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) and physical functional impairment) [13, 14] are strongly associated with elevated risk of cognitive impairment. Prior evidence indicates that the co-occurrence of depression and cognitive impairment appears to accelerate both cognitive and functional decline [15].

Given that current pharmacological treatments for cognitive impairment provide only modest symptom relief, adjusting risk factors remains crucial for prevention [16]. Housework, as common instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), requires minimal supervision and instruction, making them a convenient and cost-effective method for prevention [17]. Critically, these activities provide health benefits, including enhanced cognitive performance, preserved brain volume, and improved executive functions [18, 19]. Engagement in housework has been observed to exhibit a negative association with frailty risk, particularly among individuals aged 80 and above [20, 21]. Research has found that older individuals who actively participate in housework activities have a significantly lower prevalence of MCI compared to those who engage less in such tasks [22]. For those who frequently engage in housework, the risk of developing dementia is reduced by 21% [23]. Housework, which occupies a significant portion of older adults’ daily waking hours and contributes substantially to their total energy expenditure [24], plays a crucial role in improving their health and well-being.

Although previous studies have explored the association between housework and the risk of cognitive impairment in older adults, they often have some limitations. Specifically, they either rely on cross-sectional data or use self-reported doctor diagnoses or the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores to determine the status of cognitive impairment [17, 25, 26]. Moreover, the identified associations are inconsistent across different countries: shorter durations of housework correlate with better health in some European countries [26], while other studies showed that housework could improve physical and cognitive functions in Singapore and China [17, 25]. These inconsistent conclusions might have been due to the different study populations, measurements of housework, study designs, or lengths of follow-up. This study will address the aforementioned limitations with a comprehensive understanding of cognitive impairment. Although the MMSE is an effective and commonly used tool to assess cognitive status in epidemiological studies, it can be affected by individuals’ education levels. Therefore, we used the Chinese version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (CMMSE) scores adjusted for education level to assess the cognitive function, which has demonstrated good reliability and validity [27–29]. In this study, we hypothesized that the protective effect of housework activities on cognitive impairment would vary with frequency among older adults in China, with those engaging in housework almost every day experiencing greater benefits compared to those with less frequent involvement. We utilized longitudinal data from the Chinese Longitudinal Health and Longevity Survey (CLHLS), and multiple potential confounding factors were taken into account.

Methods

Database and study sample

The CLHLS is an ongoing study on older individuals aged 60 years and above [30, 31]. Initiated in 1998, a multi-stage cluster random sampling method was used to randomly select samples from 631 cities and counties across 22 provinces, representing approximately 85% of China’s total population. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews and health examinations, covering aspects such as family structure, economic resources, health behaviour, cognitive function, and physiological indicators. The CLHLS has conducted follow-up surveys in 2000, 2002, 2005, 2008, 2011, 2014 and 2018, ensuring high data quality, except for self-reported chronic conditions [32]. The study received ethical approval from the ethics committees of Peking University and Duke University, with all participants providing informed consent. Data are accessible through Peking University’s Open Research Data Platform.

We collected data from CLHLS participants with available lifestyle and health status data in 2014 and in the follow-up of 2018. Initially, 5301 out of 7192 initial participants were identified with normal cognitive function in 2014, excluding 85 participants aged < 65, 1177 participants with cognitive impairment (assessed based on CMMSE scores adjusted for education level), 81 participants with dementia, and 548 participants with missing data on CMMSE scores or education level. The final study population for our analysis consisted of 2797 participants after further excluding 1525 participants who were lost to follow-up and 979 participants with missing CMMSE scores at the end of follow-up in 2018. Outcome variable

Cognitive impairment at the 2018 follow-up was identified using the CMMSE scores adjusted for education level, with participants’ education levels at baseline in 2014. The CMMSE scores in the CLHLS serve as a useful tool for assessing the cognitive function and have been validated for reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.827) and structural validity [12, 33–36]. The scale consists of 24 questions across 6 dimensions (orientation, registration, attention and calculation, recall, language and naming), with a total score ranging from 0 to 30 points (Supplementary Table S1). Given the impact of education level on the performance of CMMSE scores, we defined the presence of cognitive impairment according to the CMMSE scores adjusted for education level [37]: (1) for individuals with no formal education and CMMSE scores ≤ 17; (2) for individuals with primary education (1–6 years) and CMMSE scores ≤ 20; (3) for individuals with more than secondary education (> 6 years) and CMMSE scores ≤ 24 [38].

Exposure

Housework frequency was measured as a categorical variable with five levels using the question “Do you do housework at present?” at baseline. We retained the levels of “almost everyday” and “never”, while merging the other three levels into “sometimes” due to their small sample sizes, including “not everyday, but at least once a week” (237, 8.49%), “not every week, but at least once a month” (43, 1.54%) and “not every month, but sometimes” (93, 3.33%).

Covariates

The covariates were assessed at baseline, and selected based on clinical significance, scientific knowledge, and predictors identified in existing studies [6, 12, 39–41], which included demographics, living habits, and health conditions.

Demographics variables were collected, including gender (male, female), age group (65 to 74, 75 to 84, and ≥ 85 years), ethnicity (Han, other; where Han represents the majority ethnic group in China and “other” includes minority groups with distinct cultural and lifestyle characteristics that may influence housework frequency), marital status (married or partnered, unmarried or others), residence (urban, rural), education level (illiteracy, primary (1–6 years), secondary or higher (> 6 years)), economic status (very poor, poor, moderate, rich, very rich). We merged the five levels of economic status into three groups: low (combining very poor and poor), moderate, and high (combining rich and very rich) due to the low proportions of respondents in the extreme economic categories (very poor: 1.18%; very rich: 1.57%).

Because of data availability constraints, variables about living habits, including smoking status, drinking status, exercise and raising pets, were dichotomized (yes, no), while sleep duration was categorized into three levels (< 6 h, 6–9 h, >9 h). Additionally, research has shown that social isolation (SI) and a healthy plant-based diet are risk factors for cognitive impairment [6, 12]. Following previous literature, we assessed SI using five dimensions: living alone, marital status, contact with children and siblings and social activity participation, with a score ranging from 0 to 5, where higher scores indicate greater isolation. One point was given for each of the following: if participants lived alone, lacked a spouse, were infrequently visited by children/siblings, or had limited social participation [39]. The healthful plant-based diet index (hPDI) was used to evaluate the healthful plant-based diet pattern, which included whole grains, fresh fruits, fresh vegetables, legumes, garlic, vegetable oils, nuts and tea [12]. For whole grains and vegetable oil, the questionnaire provided two options: “yes” and “no”; for fresh fruits and vegetables, the questionnaire included four options: “almost everyday”, “quite often”, “occasionally” or “rarely or never”, and for the remaining items, the questionnaire offered five options: “almost everyday”, “≥ 1 time per week”, “≥ 1 time per month”, “occasionally” or “rarely or never” (Supplementary Table S2).

Health conditions included the presence of chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, CVDs, chronic lung diseases (CLDs), Parkinson’s disease, dyslipidaemia and arthritis), depression, body mass index (BMI), frailty index (FI) and baseline CMMSE scores. Depression was assessed based on two questions: “Have you felt sad, blue, or depressed for two weeks or more in the last 12 months?” and “Have you lost interest in most things like hobbies, work, or similar activities?” and a positive response to either question was considered as indicative of depression [40]. The FI was used to assess frailty across 39 dimensions, including self-reported health, cognitive function, disability, sensory abilities, depression, heart rhythm and chronic diseases (Supplementary Table S3), which was calculated as the ratio of existing deficits to the total items assessed, with scores ranging from 0 to 1, where higher scores indicated greater frailty [41].

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were described according to cognitive impairment status at the end of follow-up in 2018. Categorical variables (e.g., gender, age group and exercise status) were presented as frequencies (percentages), and continuous variables (BMI, baseline CMMSE scores, FI, hPDI and SI) were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Chi-square test was employed to show the association of categorical variables with the risk of cognitive impairment, and Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normal distributed continuous variables, which was confirmed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. In addition, the crude incidence rate of cognitive impairment was calculated as the number of new cases in 2018 divided by the total number of individuals in each subgroup. We constructed three logistic regression models to explore the association between housework frequency and cognitive impairment: Model 1 used stepwise selection (α-entry = 0.05, α-removal = 0.10) to identify covariates from demographics, living habits and health-related factors, maintaining VIF < 3 to avoid multicollinearity; Model 2 added baseline CMMSE scores to Model 1, controlling for the influence of baseline cognitive function; and Model 3 further included exercise status to explore whether housework influenced cognitive function through attributes beyond exercise. For sensitivity analysis, we introduced variables demonstrating significant between-group differences (p values < 0.05) from Table 1 as covariates in the model. Furthermore, to evaluate potential effect modification by age and gender in the association between housework frequency and cognitive impairment, we incorporated interaction terms (housework frequency × age and housework frequency × gender) into the logistic regression models adjusted for all covariates.

Missing data (3.78% of total dataset) were imputed using multiple imputation with chained equations via the ‘mice’ package in R [42]. One imputation model was employed, including the following variables: age, gender, ethnicity, education level, marital status, residence, smoking and drinking status, exercise, sleep duration, housework frequency, BMI, FI, SI, hPDI, baseline CMMSE scores and diseases. We performed thirty imputations, each with ten iterations.

Statistical analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 and R 4.4.1, and two-sided p values < 0.05 was considered to be significant.

Results

Table 1 presented the baseline characteristics of study population. The mean age of 2797 participants was 80.22 (SD, 8.23) years, and 1403 (50.16%) individuals were females. About two-thirds (65.68%) of participants reported doing housework “almost everyday”, while 13.34% and 20.99% participants did it “sometimes” and “never”, respectively. Most variables were significantly associated with cognitive impairment (e.g., age group, gender, smoking status, exercise, hearing problems, depression). Notably, a greater proportion of cognitively impaired individuals reported no engagement in exercise (74.14% vs. 65.95%) and exhibited higher levels of social isolation (2.07 ± 0.96 vs. 1.50 ± 1.05). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in ethnicity, residence, economic status, and the presence of chronic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes and CVDs (all p values > 0.05).

Figure 2 illustrated that the incidence of cognitive impairment increased with decreasing housework frequency across all levels of gender, age group and exercise status, generally exhibiting a downward trajectory. Among individuals who sometimes or never performed housework, females exhibited significantly higher incidence of cognitive impairment than males (both p values < 0.0001). However, the gender difference was non-significant in “almost everyday” group (p = 0.0902). Cognitive impairment incidence increased significantly with age (all p values < 0.0001). We also observed that in “almost everyday” group, non-exercisers had significantly higher incidence than exercisers (p = 0.0082), while exercise status showed no significant association in “sometimes” group (p = 0.3482) or “never” group (p = 0.2370).

Housework frequency specified comparison of the incidence of cognitive impairment across gender, age, and exercise group. Significant difference is examined via chi-square tests

Table 2 showed the risk factors for cognitive impairment. Model 1 was constructed using stepwise regression, with the final variables including housework, age group, smoking status, vision problems, hearing problems, FI and SI, selected from all variables other than baseline CMMSE scores and exercise in Table 1. Baseline CMMSE scores and exercise were sequentially added to Model 1 (that is, Models 2 and 3) for comparison. Consistent associations between housework frequency and the risk of cognitive impairment were observed across the three multivariate logistic regression models. Based on Model 1, compared with those who did housework almost everyday, the risk of cognitive impairment was significantly higher among those who did housework sometimes (OR = 1.50; 95% CI, 1.03–2.17) and even higher among those who never did housework (OR = 1.98; 95% CI, 1.49–2.65). Similar associations were obtained in Models 2 and 3. The association between housework frequency and cognitive impairment remained robust in the sensitivity analysis incorporating additional confounding variables beyond the original model covariates (Supplementary Table S4). Meanwhile, older age groups, hearing problems and higher SI scores in Models 1 to 3, and lower baseline CMMSE scores in Models 2 and 3 consistently showed significantly increased risk of cognitive impairment. In contrast, FI and smoking status exhibited a shift in significance across models, being significant in Model 1 (p = 0.0171 and 0.0395, respectively) but not in Models 2 and 3 (both p values > 0.05), while exercise was not found to be significant (p = 0.2654). The analysis across three models indicated a non-significant positive association between vision problems and cognitive impairment (all p values > 0.09). As shown in Supplementary Table S5, no significant interaction effects were identified between age and gender with housework frequency. This suggested that the association between housework frequency and cognitive health does not differ across age or gender groups.

Discussion

In this large-scale study of older adults in China, univariate analyses identified vulnerable populations and multiple factors significantly associated with the risk of cognitive impairment. First, consistent with established evidence, females demonstrated elevated risk of cognitive impairment, possibly attributable to postmenopausal oestrogen decline [43, 44], while non-exercisers showed comparable vulnerability, likely mediated by reduced neuroprotective factors such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor [45–47]. Conversely, higher education level showed a protective association against cognitive impairment, potentially through more frequent cognitively stimulating activities [48]. Our findings revealed that married or partnered individuals were more prevalent in the cognitively normal group, suggesting potential benefits from spousal support mechanisms (e.g., visits from children and financial assistance) [49]. Notably, alcohol consumers showed significantly higher proportion in the cognitively impaired group, supporting previous evidence that chronic heavy alcohol use increases risk of cognitive impairment, though moderate consumption effects remain controversial [50–52]. Furthermore, pet ownership was more common among cognitively normal individuals, possibly offering protection through enhanced social interactions like pet-related conversations and activities [53–55].

Our findings on sleep duration align with prior research based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), indicating a statistically significant inverted U-shaped association between sleep duration and cognitive function, as well as subsequent cognitive decline. Extreme sleep durations (≤ 4 h or ≥ 10 h per night) were not only associated with poorer baseline cognition but also with accelerated cognitive decline over follow-up assessments [56]. Additionally, the cognitively normal group displayed significantly higher hPDI scores than the impaired group, consistent with Fang et al.‘s findings [12]. While their research also suggested that overweight/obesity may lower risk of cognitive impairment whereas underweight increased it, our finding indicated that although the mean BMI in the cognitively normal group was significantly higher than that in the cognitively impaired group, both groups maintained normal BMI range. Moreover, the cognitively impaired group exhibited significantly higher prevalence of visual impairment, depression, and elevated frailty scores, supported by international research [57–59]. The highest risk of cognitive impairment occurred among the oldest adults who never engaged in housework, potentially reflecting that complete inactivity may signal underlying functional decline and contribute to cognitive deterioration by reducing cognitive stimulation and social interaction [45, 60].

Two community-based cross-sectional studies from Suzhou, China, and Singapore consistently identified significant associations between housework engagement and better cognitive performance in older adults, particularly in attention and memory domains [22, 25]. Our findings corroborated this evidence and the association remains statistically significant even after adjusting for baseline cognitive function. Considering the possibility that early cognitive decline may reduce activity participation, models with baseline CMMSE scores adjustment confirmed an independent protective association between frequent housework engagement and subsequent cognitive outcomes [61].

In this study, we accounted for the effects of physical activities other than housework, such as exercise, to further explore whether housework contributed to cognitive health through mechanisms beyond general physical activity. Many studies have established associations between cognitive impairment and physical activities [62, 63]. As a form of non-exercise physical activity (i.e., physical activity not primarily aimed at health promotion), housework has been shown to have a positive impact on health, particularly for older individuals who are unable to engage in regular exercise or have limited physical capabilities [64]. Critically, even after adjusting for exercise participation, less housework engagement remained significantly associated with higher risk of cognitive impairment, revealing effects independent of general physical activity. Unlike traditional forms of exercise (e.g., running), housework often involved multitasking, such as time management during cooking or spatial planning while cleaning, which activated prefrontal and parietal brain regions essential for attention and working memory [65]. This cognitively demanding nature of housework may offer unique stimulation that supports cognitive function. In addition, engaging in housework may enhance psychological well-being by fostering a sense of control and self-efficacy, mitigating depressive symptoms and anxiety, and strengthening emotional bonds within families [66–69]. A proactive lifestyle characterized by regular housework engagement may further cognitive resilience support through neural compensation mechanisms and reduced beta-amyloid accumulation [70, 71].

Beyond housework frequency, several covariates, including hearing loss, social isolation, and smoking, were consistently associated with the risk of cognitive impairment across all three models. Consistent with Azeem and colleagues’ findings, our findings supported the association between hearing loss and cognitive impairment, which may partly operate through increased social isolation and reduced cognitive stimulation [72, 73]. In our analysis, however, both hearing loss and social isolation remained significant, indicating they may affect cognitive function through relatively independent pathways. Our study also revealed a reduced risk of cognitive impairment in smokers, which may be attributed to nicotine’s cognitive-enhancing effects [74, 75]. Both preclinical animal models and human studies have demonstrated that nicotine selectively improves key cognitive functions, including fine motor skills, alertness, working memory, and episodic memory [74]. However, a longitudinal study has indicated that chronic smoking significantly elevates the risk of cognitive impairment in older age, with the risk escalating with both the duration and intensity of smoking exposure [76]. These findings suggested a complex association between smoking and cognitive impairment, warranting further investigation into the underlying mechanisms.

The main strength of this study lied in its large, nationally representative sample of older individuals in China, enabling robust analyses across this demographically diverse population. Moreover, the CMMSE scores adjusted for education level provided a more comprehensive assessment of cognitive status than relying solely on the CMMSE scores or physician diagnoses based on self and proxy reports [27]. In our analytical methods, we employed stepwise regression models and conducted sensitivity analysis to account for potential confounding factors, ensuring the robustness of our findings from both statistical modelling and practical perspectives. To conservatively assess effects of housework frequency on incident cognitive impairment during 2014–2018 period, we rigorously controlled for baseline CMMSE scores differences. Notably, we accounted for the influence of physical activities beyond housework (e.g., exercise), to investigate whether the benefits of housework on cognitive health stem from mechanisms distinct from those associated with general physical activity. Unlike cross-sectional studies vulnerable to reverse causation, our cohort design measured housework exposure prior to cognitive assessment, providing stronger evidence for temporal sequence [77].

There were some limitations in this study. First, the cohort study design using the CLHLS dataset inherently involved selection bias within the system. For instance, the sample in this study was more likely to include individuals of being younger, healthier, better-educated, and more socially engaged (Supplementary Table S6). Secondly, the CLHLS relied on self-reported health behaviours (e.g., housework frequency, smoking status, etc.), which may be subject to recall bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of the findings. Thirdly, the current study revealed how the cognitive impairment was affected by the frequency of housework activity, which was obtained via a single question. However, factors of specific types, duration, and intensity of housework activities were not available in the CLHLS database. Finally, it should be noted that dichotomized categorization of behavioural variables (e.g., smoking status) may obscure the complexity and dose-response information, such as duration, frequency or quantity, all of which could have varying impacts on cognitive function.

Given the positive impact of housework activities on cognitive health, future research should employ more advanced study designs and modelling approaches to further explore how different types and intensities of household chores affect cognitive health. Additionally, it is imperative to develop personalized guidance tailored to individuals’ health status and preferences. Furthermore, future research should examine the effective implementation of these preventive strategies across diverse global cultural contexts.

Conclusions

The findings from our cohort study indicated an association between less housework frequency and higher risk of cognitive function among Chinese older adults. The integration of housework into public health guidelines and strategies offers promise in cognitive impairment prevention, improving quality of life and reducing future healthcare burdens.
