Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation is associated with improved functional recovery and time-dependent changes in apoptosis-related execution-phase markers after spinal cord injury
Authors: Dandan Hu, Qingqin Xu, Qian Lei, Mengxuan Hu, Hemu Chen, Jian Li, Ying Chen, Xiaoping Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to determine whether repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is associated with changes in motor recovery and apoptosis-related pathways after spinal cord injury (SCI), and to characterize the temporal dependence of these effects. A rat model of SCI was established using Allen’s method, and rTMS was initiated on day 2 post-injury. Western blot and immunofluorescence were used to quantify apoptosis-related execution-phase proteins, including caspase-3, cleaved caspase-3, caspase-7, PARP1, and cleaved PARP1, and to assess their cellular distribution. Double immunofluorescence of NeuN and cleaved caspase-3 was performed to identify the localization of apoptotic signals. TUNEL staining, HE staining, and BBB scores were used to assess apoptotic cell death, tissue injury, and motor recovery. rTMS was associated with time-dependent changes, with limited effects at early stages but reduced apoptosis-related signaling and improved tissue preservation and functional recovery at later time points. Co-localization of NeuN and cleaved caspase-3 indicated that apoptotic signaling was localized in neurons. These findings suggest that rTMS is associated with reduced activation of apoptosis-related execution-phase signaling after SCI in a time-dependent manner, together with enhanced tissue preservation and functional recovery. The caspase-3/caspase-7/PARP1 cleavage axis may be involved in the biological changes associated with rTMS treatment after SCI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52917-6.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a severe traumatic disease of the central nervous system that can lead to long-term motor dysfunction and even permanent disability1. Current approaches to the management of post-SCI motor dysfunction primarily include surgical decompression, pharmacological interventions, and rehabilitative training2–4. Although these strategies can partially mitigate secondary injury and support limited functional recovery, substantial limitations remain5. Surgical intervention primarily addresses structural abnormalities such as compression and instability; however, its applicability is restricted and it cannot reverse established neural tissue damage6. Pharmacological treatments can modulate inflammatory and metabolic processes but are often associated with systemic side effects and limited long-term efficacy7. Rehabilitative training relies largely on activity-dependent plasticity but often fails to elicit neuroplastic changes of sufficient magnitude to support functional reorganization8,9. Therefore, a novel intervention strategy is urgently needed that may enhance central nervous system plasticity while avoiding invasive injury and systemic side effects.

In response to this unmet need, non-invasive neuromodulation has emerged as a promising approach for promoting functional recovery after SCI. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a non-invasive neuromodulatory technique that alters cortical excitability and large-scale network activity via electromagnetic induction10. rTMS has been reported to modulate corticospinal excitability and plastic reorganization and has been widely applied in the treatment of stroke, major depression, and chronic pain11–13. In recent years, both preclinical and clinical studies have explored the use of rTMS in SCI. Experimental studies suggest that rTMS may promote motor recovery and influence neuroplasticity-related processes, including neuronal activation, synaptic plasticity, and neurodegeneration-associated changes14,15. Clinical studies have further shown that rTMS may improve motor outcomes and enhance corticospinal excitability in individuals with incomplete SCI16–18. However, most existing studies have focused primarily on behavioral recovery, cortical excitability, or plasticity-related indices, and many have relied on fixed stimulation paradigms or single end-point assessments19. As a result, the time-dependent effects of rTMS on downstream spinal pathological processes remain insufficiently understood, particularly with respect to apoptosis-related molecular events under different stimulation durations. A systematic understanding of how different durations of rTMS modulate secondary injury-related molecular pathways is therefore essential for elucidating its therapeutic mechanisms.

Despite these encouraging findings regarding neural plasticity and corticospinal modulation, the downstream spinal molecular mechanisms associated with rTMS remain incompletely understood. Among the diverse pathological processes triggered by SCI, apoptosis is considered a major contributor to the progressive loss of neurons and glial cells20. Activation of apoptotic signaling is typically associated with increased activity of executioner caspases (such as caspase-3 and caspase-7) and cleavage of downstream substrates, including PARP1, thereby inducing DNA damage and programmed cell death21,22. Excessive apoptosis not only expands the lesion but also disrupts spinal cord architecture, thereby severely constraining the potential for spontaneous functional recovery. Caspase-3 and caspase-7 serve as the principal effector proteases of the apoptotic execution phase, mediating the cleavage of numerous intracellular substrates23. PARP1 is a nuclear enzyme involved in DNA repair, and its cleaved form is widely regarded as a hallmark of irreversible apoptotic progression24. Cleaved caspase-3 reflects activation of apoptotic signaling. Accordingly, simultaneous assessment of caspase-3, caspase-7, PARP1, and their cleaved forms enables a systematic evaluation of apoptotic execution and its downstream consequences. However, most existing studies are limited to single time-point analyses and do not capture the dynamic evolution of these markers across injury progression and recovery.

In summary, this study aims to systematically investigate the time-dependent associations of rTMS with apoptosis-related processes and motor function recovery after SCI. We assessed apoptosis-related protein expression, apoptotic cell death, histopathological changes, and motor function across 3, 7, and 14 days after intervention. This design allows characterization of the dynamic evolution of apoptotic processes and their temporal association with rTMS treatment. The main innovations of this study include: (1) applying rTMS over the motor cortex and analyzing its associations with distant spinal molecular and cellular processes, thereby providing a cross-level framework linking cortical stimulation with spinal pathological changes; (2) performing multi-time-point analysis rather than a single time point, to characterize the temporal dynamics associated with rTMS across different stages of injury and recovery; (3) integrating molecular, cellular, tissue, and behavioral evidence, to provide a comprehensive understanding of the relationships between rTMS treatment, apoptosis-related changes, and functional recovery after SCI.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental design

All procedures involving animals complied with the ARRIVE guidelines and the Code of Welfare and Ethics for Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (approval no. LLSC20232177, September 18, 2023). Housing conditions, animal care, and grouping strategy were implemented in accordance with our previously reported protocols with minor adjustments to fit the present experimental design25,26. Forty-five specific pathogen-free female Sprague-Dawley rats (10–12 weeks old, 210–230 g) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Anhui Medical University (license no. SCXK [Anhui] 2017-006). Animals were maintained at 22–28 °C and approximately 60% humidity under a 12-h light/dark cycle with free access to food and water and allowed one week of acclimatization. Female rats were selected due to their suitability for postoperative bladder management and their widespread use in preclinical SCI studies25,26. Animals were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 15 per group): Control, SCI, and SCI+rTMS. Control animals underwent laminectomy at the tenth thoracic vertebra (T10) without spinal cord damage, whereas rats in the SCI and SCI+rTMS groups received a standardized contusive injury. rTMS intervention began on postoperative day 2 and continued for 14 consecutive days. All euthanasia procedures complied with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. As illustrated in Fig. 1, molecular, histological, and behavioral assessments were conducted at 3, 7, and 14 days post-intervention. Animals in each group were followed longitudinally for BBB scoring. At these time points, spinal cord tissue encompassing the lesion site was collected for molecular and histological processing. In selected animals, the tissue was divided into two portions after dissection: one was snap-frozen for Western blot analysis, and the other was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for paraffin embedding and histological analyses.

Spinal cord injury rat model

The T10 contusive SCI model was established following our previously validated modified Allen’s impact method25,26. Briefly, rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1.25% tribromoethanol (10 mL/kg). After loss of the eyelid reflex, animals were placed in the prone position, and a 15-mm midline dorsal incision was made. The paraspinal muscles were bilaterally dissected to expose the vertebrae, and a dorsal laminectomy was performed at the T10 level to expose the spinal cord without disrupting the dura mater27. To induce SCI, a 10-g weight was dropped from a height of 25 mm onto the exposed spinal cord in all groups except the Control group, and the weight was maintained in place for 15 s to produce a standardized contusive-compressive injury. Successful injury induction was confirmed by the presence of hindlimb tremor and tail-flick reflexes, as previously reported28. In addition, injury severity was further validated by the uniform loss of hindlimb locomotor function, as indicated by BBB scores of 0 in all animals on post-injury day 1, confirming a consistent and severe SCI model. Postoperatively, rats received intraperitoneal antibiotics (160 mg/kg) once daily for three consecutive days to prevent infection. Manual bladder expression was performed twice daily in SCI animals until spontaneous voiding was restored.

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation treatment

Resting motor threshold (RMT) assessment, electrode configuration, and stimulation procedures were conducted according to our previously published rTMS methodology with minor modifications25,26. To avoid anesthetic interference with electrophysiological recordings, all measurements were performed in awake rats secured in a stereotaxic frame29,30. Recording electrodes were placed over the tibialis anterior muscle, reference electrodes were placed 1 cm distally, and a ground electrode was placed on the tail. RMT was defined as the minimum intensity producing motor-evoked potentials > 50 µV in at least 5 of 10 pulses delivered to the contralateral motor cortex31. A customized animal coil connected to a MagTD40 stimulator (Yiruide, China) was positioned over the bilateral primary motor cortex using stereotaxic landmarks32. Rats in the SCI+rTMS group received daily sessions (750 pulses, 10 trains, 75 pulses/train, 10 Hz, 120% RMT) for 14 days. These parameters were selected based on previous preclinical studies employing high-frequency rTMS in SCI models. In particular, 10 Hz stimulation has been associated with modulation of neuroplasticity-related mechanisms33, whereas stimulation at 120% RMT with 750 pulses/day has been used in comparable preclinical protocols and has been reported to induce effective cortical activation and improve functional recovery25,26. Control and SCI rats underwent sham procedures with the same awake restraint, stereotaxic positioning, coil placement over the bilateral primary motor cortex, and session duration as the SCI+rTMS group. During sham stimulation, the stimulator was kept on to preserve the acoustic exposure associated with the operating device, while the coil was tilted and positioned at an increased distance from the motor cortex to minimize effective magnetic field delivery to the brain34. All animals completed the procedures without observable signs of distress during the intervention period.

Western blot

Protein extraction and immunoblotting procedures followed our previously reported protocol25,26. Following the final intervention, rats were euthanized by intraperitoneal overdose of tribromoethanol anesthesia, followed by transcardial perfusion with 0.9% saline (1000 mL/kg). For Western blot analysis, one portion of the thoracic spinal cord tissue encompassing the lesion site from each selected animal was rapidly dissected and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent analysis. Injured tissue samples (20 mg) were homogenized in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of protein (6 µg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. This loading amount was optimized in our preliminary experiments to ensure clear and reproducible band detection. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against caspase-3 (19677-1-AP, 1:500), cleaved caspase-3 (25128-1-AP, 1:500), caspase-7 (27155-1-AP, 1:500), PARP1 (13371-1-AP, 1:1000), cleaved PARP1 (60555-1-Ig, 1:5000), or β-actin (66009-1-Ig, 1:5000; Proteintech, China). After washing, membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG or goat anti-mouse IgG, 1:5000; Proteintech). Protein bands were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence and imaged using a gel documentation system (AL600RGB, Cytiva, USA). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). Band intensities were normalized to β-actin to correct for loading variability. Western blot analyses were performed using samples from individual animals (n = 3 per group). For each animal, normalized band intensities were calculated and the per-animal values were used as biological replicates for statistical analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence procedures were performed as previously described in our studies25,26. For histological analyses, the remaining portion of the lesion-containing spinal cord tissue from each selected animal was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Samples were embedded in paraffin, and serial transverse Sect. (5 μm) were prepared. Antigen retrieval was performed on sections obtained from the T10 spinal cord level, followed by three washes in PBS and blocking with normal goat serum for 30 min. For single immunofluorescence staining, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against caspase-3 (19677-1-AP, 1:500), caspase-7 (27155-1-AP, 1:500), PARP1 (13371-1-AP, 1:1000), or cleaved PARP1 (60555-1-Ig, 1:5000; Proteintech, China). For double immunofluorescence staining, sections were co-incubated with cleaved caspase-3 (25128-1-AP, 1:500) and NeuN (66836-1-Ig, 1:200; Proteintech, China). On the following day, sections were washed and incubated with species-specific Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG and/or goat anti-mouse IgG), applied simultaneously for double staining or individually for single staining, as appropriate. After washing, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (BL739B, Labgic) for 10 min. Immunofluorescent signals were visualized and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (DM6B, Leica, Germany) at 200× magnification. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA), and all images were processed under a uniform threshold setting. Comparable regions around the lesion epicenter were selected across groups at each time point for image acquisition and quantitative analysis. Within these regions, three non-overlapping sections were selected for each animal (n = 3 per group), and three randomly chosen fields were analyzed per section. Positive-stained regions were identified under the same threshold, and the positive-stained area was measured. Measurements were averaged within each animal, and the resulting per-animal mean was used as one biological replicate for statistical analysis.

TUNEL staining

Apoptotic cells were detected using a commercial TUNEL kit (C1086-20T, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and our previously established protocol26. Transverse sections of the T10 spinal cord were prepared from the fixed tissue described in Sect. 2.5. Sections were incubated with TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark and subsequently washed three times in phosphate-buffered saline. Slides were mounted with an antifade medium containing DAPI (BL739B, Labgic). Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (DM6B, Leica, Germany) at 200× magnification. Comparable regions around the lesion epicenter were selected across groups at each time point for image acquisition and quantitative analysis. Within these regions, three non-overlapping sections were selected for each animal (n = 3 per group), and three randomly selected fields were analyzed per section. The proportion of apoptotic cells was first averaged within each animal, and the per-animal mean was used for statistical analysis.

Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining

HE staining of T10 spinal cord sections was conducted following standard histological procedures consistent with our previous work25,26. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, stained with hematoxylin (G1120, Solarbio, China) for 5 min, and rinsed under running water until nuclei appeared blue. Sections were then briefly counterstained with eosin for 3 s, dehydrated, cleared, and coverslipped with neutral resin. Histological images were acquired using a light microscope (DM6, Leica, Germany) at both low and high magnifications. Low-magnification overview images were used to illustrate lesion context, and corresponding higher-magnification images were used for qualitative histopathological evaluation. For each group (n = 3 per group), representative sections from individual animals were selected.

Basso-Beattie-Bresnahan (BBB) scores

Locomotor function was assessed using the BBB score35 on intervention days 1, 3, 7, and 14. Rats were individually placed in an open field, and hindlimb joint movement, coordination, and trunk stability were evaluated using the BBB scoring system as previously described. BBB scores were recorded separately for the left and right hindlimbs. Scoring was performed independently by two blinded investigators for each animal (n = 15 per group), and the mean of the two scores was used for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical procedures were consistent with those described in our earlier publications25,26. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed with Levene’s test. For datasets meeting both assumptions, group differences were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with the Student-Newman-Keuls (SNK-q) test applied for post-hoc multiple comparisons. Normally distributed two-group data were compared using independent-samples t-tests. When either assumption was violated, multiple-group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test, and two-group comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney rank-sum test. For BBB scores, which were repeatedly measured in the same animals over time, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used, with group as the between-subject factor and time as the within-subject factor. Bonferroni-corrected post hoc comparisons were performed where appropriate. If the assumption of sphericity was violated, Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normal variables are reported as M (P25, P75). Differences with P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Changes in apoptosis-related protein expression after spinal cord injury associated with repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation

As shown in Fig. 2, Western blot analysis showed that, compared with the Control group, the SCI group exhibited significantly increased levels of cleaved caspase-3, caspase-7, and cleaved PARP1 at 3, 7, and 14 days after intervention (all P < 0.05), whereas caspase-3 was significantly decreased at all three time points (all P < 0.05). PARP1 was significantly increased at 3 and 7 days (both P < 0.05). rTMS treatment was associated with time-dependent changes in these alterations. At 3 days, cleaved caspase-3 levels did not differ significantly between the SCI and SCI+rTMS groups, whereas cleaved PARP1 remained elevated. In contrast, at 7 and 14 days, cleaved caspase-3, caspase-7, and cleaved PARP1 were significantly reduced in the SCI+rTMS group and progressively approached control levels (all P < 0.05), whereas PARP1 expression was reduced at 7 days. In addition, time-course analysis of the same SCI+rTMS samples, which were re-run on the same membrane for within-group comparison, showed that cleaved caspase-3, caspase-7, and cleaved PARP1 levels gradually decreased over time after rTMS treatment, whereas caspase-3 and PARP1 levels showed no marked time-dependent changes. Collectively, these findings indicate that SCI was associated with persistent activation of apoptosis-related pathways, whereas rTMS intervention was associated with attenuation of apoptosis-related alterations at later time points after injury.

Changes in caspase-3, caspase-7, PARP1, and cleaved PARP1 expression after spinal cord injury associated with repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation

As shown in Figs. 3, 4, 5 and 6, cell nuclei are stained blue; caspase-3-, caspase-7-, and cleaved PARP1-positive cells are labeled in green; and PARP1-positive cells are labeled in red. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that, compared with the Control group, the SCI group exhibited significant alterations in apoptosis-related protein expression after intervention. Specifically, caspase-7-positive cells were significantly increased at 3, 7, and 14 days, whereas caspase-3-positive cells were significantly decreased at all three time points (all P < 0.05). PARP1-positive and cleaved PARP1-positive cells were significantly increased at 3 and 7 days (all P < 0.05). rTMS treatment was associated with time-dependent changes in these alterations. At 3 days, no significant difference was observed between the SCI and SCI+rTMS groups for caspase-3, caspase-7, and PARP1. At 7 days, the SCI+rTMS group showed significantly fewer caspase-7-, PARP1-, and cleaved PARP1-positive cells and a significantly higher number of caspase-3-positive cells than the SCI group (all P < 0.05). At 14 days, the SCI+rTMS group showed significantly fewer caspase-7- and cleaved PARP1-positive cells than the SCI group (all P < 0.05), with values progressively approaching those of the Control group. The fourth column in each figure represents time-course quantification of the same SCI+rTMS samples shown in the first three columns. In these within-group analyses, caspase-3- and PARP1-positive cell areas showed no marked time-dependent changes, whereas caspase-7- and cleaved PARP1-positive cell areas decreased at later time points. Overall, these results indicate that SCI is associated with sustained cellular alterations in apoptosis-related protein expression, and that rTMS treatment is associated with attenuation of several of these changes predominantly at later time points after injury.

Changes in neuronal cleaved caspase-3 expression after spinal cord injury associated with repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation

Double immunofluorescence staining was performed to examine the cellular distribution of cleaved caspase-3 in neurons after intervention. Cell nuclei were counterstained in blue, cleaved caspase-3 was labeled in green, and the neuronal marker NeuN was labeled in red. As shown in Fig. 7, the cleaved caspase-3-positive area was significantly increased in the SCI group compared with the Control group at 3, 7, and 14 days after intervention (all P < 0.05). rTMS treatment was associated with time-dependent changes in cleaved caspase-3 expression. At 3 days, the SCI+rTMS group exhibited a further increase in cleaved caspase-3-positive area compared with the SCI group (P < 0.05). At 7 days, cleaved caspase-3-positive area remained elevated in the SCI group and was significantly reduced in the SCI+rTMS group relative to the SCI group (P < 0.05). At 14 days, the cleaved caspase-3-positive area in the SCI+rTMS group was markedly lower than that in the SCI group and approached control levels (P < 0.001). The fourth column in Fig. 7D shows the time-course of the SCI+rTMS group alone, highlighting that cleaved caspase-3-positive cell areas further increased at 3 days, remained high at 7 days, and declined at 14 days. Merged images showed substantial colocalization of cleaved caspase-3 with NeuN, suggesting that cleaved caspase-3 immunoreactivity was present in neurons. These findings indicate that neuronal cleaved caspase-3 expression increased after SCI and was attenuated by rTMS at later time points.

Changes in apoptotic cell death after spinal cord injury associated with repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation

TUNEL staining was performed to evaluate apoptotic cell death after SCI. As shown in Fig. 8, few TUNEL-positive cells were detected in the Control group at all time points. In contrast, the SCI group exhibited a marked increase in the number of TUNEL-positive cells at 3, 7, and 14 days after intervention. rTMS treatment was associated with a time-dependent reduction in the number of TUNEL-positive cells. At 3 days, the SCI+rTMS group showed a modest decrease in TUNEL-positive cell numbers relative to the SCI group (P < 0.05). This reduction became more pronounced at 7 and 14 days, with TUNEL-positive cell numbers in the SCI+rTMS group being significantly lower than those in the SCI group (all P < 0.05) and showing a progressive decline toward Control levels. The fourth column in Fig. 8D shows time-course quantification of the same SCI+rTMS samples from the first three columns, revealing that TUNEL-positive cell numbers gradually decreased over 3, 7, and 14 days, approaching control levels at the latest time point. Overall, these results indicate that SCI induced sustained apoptotic cell death, whereas rTMS intervention progressively attenuated apoptosis, particularly at later time points.

Histological changes after spinal cord injury associated with repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation

Histological analysis was performed to evaluate tissue morphology after SCI. As shown in Fig. 9, the Control group exhibited preserved tissue architecture with intact gray and white matter organization at all time points. In contrast, the SCI group showed marked histopathological alterations. At 3 days after intervention, pronounced tissue disruption, cavitary changes, and loss of normal structural organization were observed at the lesion site. At 7 days, lesion cavities and disrupted tissue architecture remained evident in the SCI group. By 14 days, persistent cavitary changes and structural disorganization were still observed. rTMS treatment was associated with time-dependent attenuation of these histological abnormalities. At 3 days, the SCI+rTMS group showed less extensive tissue disruption and cavitary change than the SCI group. At 7 days, tissue continuity and overall histological appearance were partially improved in the SCI+rTMS group, with reduced cavitary change and more preserved structural organization. By 14 days, the SCI+rTMS group showed further improvement in tissue morphology, characterized by reduced lesion-related disruption and better preservation of spinal cord architecture relative to the SCI group. Collectively, these findings indicate that rTMS treatment is associated with reduced SCI-related histological damage and greater structural preservation of spinal cord tissue over time.

Locomotor recovery after spinal cord injury associated with repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation

As shown in Fig. 10, locomotor function was evaluated using the BBB score. BBB scores were reanalyzed using a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, with group as the between-subject factor and time as the within-subject factor. Mauchly’s test indicated that the assumption of sphericity was violated for time (W = 0.665, P = 0.005); therefore, Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of group (F(2, 42) = 19714.927, P < 0.001, partial η² = 0.999), a significant main effect of time (F(2.397, 100.689) = 413.881, P < 0.001, partial η² = 0.908), and a significant group × time interaction (F(4.795, 100.689) = 110.322, P < 0.001, partial η² = 0.840), indicating that locomotor recovery trajectories differed significantly among the three groups. Post hoc comparisons showed no significant difference between the SCI and SCI+rTMS groups at 3 and 7 days, whereas the SCI+rTMS group exhibited significantly higher BBB scores than the SCI group at 14 days. These findings indicate that rTMS treatment was associated with improved locomotor recovery over time after SCI.

Discussion

This study systematically compared the time-dependent associations of different durations of rTMS treatment with motor recovery and apoptosis-related pathways after SCI from a temporal perspective and revealed clear stage-dependent patterns in these changes. This study found persistent activation of apoptosis-related pathways after SCI, as reflected by elevated levels of cleaved caspase-3, caspase-7, and cleaved PARP1, accompanied by increased apoptotic cell death, spinal tissue disruption, and impaired motor function. In contrast, rTMS treatment was associated with progressive reductions in these apoptosis-related markers over time, with more pronounced changes at days 7 and 14, in parallel with reduced tissue damage and improved motor outcomes. These findings indicate that the associations of rTMS with recovery-related changes are not static but vary with both stimulation duration and post-injury pathological progression, and that apoptosis-related pathways may represent one important process associated with these effects. On this basis, we discuss these results in the context of prior studies, focusing on neuromodulatory pathways, apoptotic mechanisms, temporal dynamics, and cross-level regulation.

Previous studies suggest that apoptosis after SCI is not initiated at the execution phase itself, but is typically driven by upstream pathological processes such as inflammation, oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, and disruption of cell survival signaling. Consistent with this framework, previous studies have shown that rTMS may enhance blood-spinal cord barrier integrity and attenuate post-injury inflammation, thereby reducing the sustained pro-apoptotic pressure on injured spinal tissue25,36,37. rTMS has also been reported to influence MAPK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways involved in cell survival, which may be relevant to limiting the initiation and amplification of apoptotic programs26. In addition, rTMS has been reported to be associated with corticospinal axonal regeneration and restoration of descending functional connectivity15,38, supporting the possibility that cortical stimulation may influence pathological processes in the injured spinal cord through descending regulatory mechanisms. Because rTMS primarily acts on the motor cortex and its downstream corticospinal projections rather than directly on the injured spinal cord, we speculate that it may reshape aberrant cortical output and thereby influence the spinal microenvironment. Such changes could reduce the sustained impact of multiple upstream injurious processes, including inflammation, oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, and impaired survival-related signaling. In this context, the reductions in apoptosis-related execution-phase markers observed in the present study may be interpreted as downstream readouts associated with broader upstream regulation.

As summarized conceptually in Fig. 11, apoptosis after SCI may be driven by multiple upstream pathological processes that ultimately converge on execution-phase signaling39,40. Within this framework, sustained activation of executioner caspase-related signaling, reflected by increased cleaved caspase-3 together with persistent caspase-7 expression, may indicate persistent engagement of the apoptotic execution phase. Downstream of this process, PARP1 and its cleaved form may represent later execution-phase events associated with progression toward irreversible apoptotic injury. PARP1 cleavage compromises its role in DNA repair, leading to the accumulation of DNA strand breaks and the progression toward irreversible programmed cell death24. Accordingly, the markers assessed in the present study should be interpreted primarily as downstream readouts of apoptosis-related signaling, rather than as direct indicators of any single upstream trigger. Our results showed sustained increases in these execution-phase markers after SCI, whereas rTMS treatment was associated with reductions in these markers at later time points. These findings suggest attenuation of downstream apoptotic signaling, but do not establish that rTMS directly targets a specific upstream apoptotic pathway. Instead, they are more consistent with a broader association between rTMS and reduced apoptotic signaling, together with improved cellular, tissue, and functional outcomes in the present study.

This study further analyzed the temporal dynamics of apoptosis-related changes associated with rTMS treatment. At 3 days of stimulation, the effects were limited and some markers even exhibited transient increases, whereas more evident suppression was observed at days 7 and 14. This temporal pattern suggests that post-SCI apoptosis-related signaling is not static but evolves over time, with early changes likely related to acute secondary injury and later changes potentially associated with the tissue microenvironment and plasticity-related processes. In this context, the later post-injury period may represent a more responsive window for rTMS-associated modulation of apoptosis-related and reparative processes. Double immunofluorescence for cleaved caspase-3 and NeuN showed substantial colocalization, suggesting that cleaved caspase-3 immunoreactivity was present in neurons. Given the limited regenerative capacity of neurons, their injury is more likely to contribute to persistent functional deficits. Reduced neuron-associated cleaved caspase-3 signaling observed in the rTMS-treated group at later stages may therefore be associated with its tissue-protective and functional benefits. Together, these findings suggest that neuromodulation may be associated not only with neural network activity but also with injury-related pathological processes across multiple levels of organization.

To further examine these multi-level associations, we evaluated cellular, tissue, and functional outcomes in relation to rTMS treatment. TUNEL staining is commonly used to assess DNA fragmentation during apoptosis and serves as a canonical indicator of injury-induced programmed cell death41. We found that rTMS treatment was associated with lower numbers of TUNEL-positive cells, with more evident differences at later time points. This suggests that rTMS treatment is associated with reduced ongoing programmed cell death and may help limit the loss of neurons and supporting glial cells. HE staining reflects overall tissue architecture and pathological alterations and provides an important morphological index of spinal tissue preservation. After SCI, marked structural disruption and cavitary changes were observed, whereas rTMS treatment was associated with partial preservation of tissue architecture and reduced lesion-related disruption. The rTMS-treated group showed improved tissue continuity and less extensive cavitary change. BBB score was used to evaluate hindlimb motor recovery and represents a key behavioral outcome measure42. The rTMS-treated group showed higher BBB scores over time, with significant differences emerging at later time points. In summary, these findings support an association between improved locomotor recovery and concurrent reductions in apoptosis-related cell death together with greater tissue preservation in the rTMS-treated group.

Although this study provides a systematic analysis of rTMS in SCI, several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, our analysis focused primarily on execution-phase apoptotic markers and did not directly assess upstream pro-apoptotic triggers. Therefore, how rTMS may influence upstream pathological processes remains unclear. Future studies should examine upstream mechanisms such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction to clarify the pathways linking rTMS treatment with apoptosis-related changes43–45. Second, only female rats were included, and potential sex-specific effects cannot be excluded. Because sex may influence locomotor recovery and neuroprotection after SCI, caution is warranted when extrapolating the present findings to male animals46,47. Future studies including both sexes will be important for determining whether the effects of rTMS are sex dependent and for improving the generalizability of the findings48. Third, as with other coil-tilt sham approaches, residual magnetic effects cannot be completely excluded49. Future studies should further optimize sham stimulation conditions to better control nonspecific factors while minimizing unintended magnetic field delivery.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the time-dependent associations of different durations of rTMS treatment with apoptosis-related processes and motor recovery after SCI. We found persistent activation of apoptosis-related execution-phase signaling after SCI, whereas rTMS treatment was associated with reduced apoptosis-related signaling at later time points, together with decreased apoptotic cell death, improved tissue preservation, and enhanced locomotor recovery. Co-localization of NeuN and cleaved caspase-3 further suggested neuronal involvement in these apoptosis-related changes. Overall, these findings indicate that apoptosis-related execution-phase signaling may represent an important process associated with rTMS treatment after SCI and may help inform optimization of the timing and duration of intervention.
