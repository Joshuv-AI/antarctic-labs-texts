Association between serum cotinine and total testosterone in adult males based on NHANES 2011–2016
Authors: Ming Liu, Yunpeng Gou, Bing Zou, Xianhui Li, Ping Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe relationship between smoking and testosterone levels in adult males remains a topic of ongoing debate. Serum cotinine is considered a reliable marker of both smoking intensity and exposure to tobacco smoke. Therefore, we aim to examine the association between serum cotinine levels and total testosterone concentrations in adult males using data from the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database. Our study assessed the relationship between serum cotinine and total testosterone using weighted linear regression models and subgroup analysis. A fully adjusted model with smooth curve fitting was employed to investigate the potential nonlinear association between serum cotinine and total testosterone. Threshold effects were analyzed to identify the inflection point between serum cotinine and total testosterone. Indeed, a total of 7797 participants were included in our study. After adjusting for potential confounding variables, the findings indicate a positive association between serum cotinine levels and total testosterone levels (β: 0.05, 95%CI: 0.02, 0.09). Furthermore, applying smoothed curve fitting analysis and threshold effects, an inflection point was detected at a serum cotinine level of 487 ng/ml. Above this threshold, total testosterone levels declined with increasing serum cotinine levels. In conclusion, the findings of our study suggest a positive association between elevated serum cotinine levels and total testosterone levels in adult men. However, it is essential to note that this association may be reversed at excessively high serum cotinine concentrations.Keywords: Smoking, Serum cotinine, Total testosterone, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES)Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Health occupations

Introduction

Testosterone is a crucial hormone in male reproductive physiology, playing a vital role in developing and maintaining secondary sexual characteristics and fertility. Research has demonstrated that diminished levels of testosterone can lead to a range of adverse health outcomes, including sexual dysfunction, reduced energy and muscle mass, mood disorders, alterations in bone mineral density, cardiovascular complications, depression, diminished sexual desire, erectile dysfunction, decreased ejaculatory volume, and loss of body and facial hair1–4.

According to statistics, approximately one-third of adult males worldwide smoke5. The prevalence of smoking varies by country and region, with higher rates typically found in Asian and European countries. The smoking rate among Chinese men is about 47.6%6, while the smoking rate among Russian men is about 60%6. In contrast, the smoking rate among American men is about 15.5%7. It is widely acknowledged that smoking can have adverse effects on male reproductive health, including reduced sperm count and diminished semen quality8–11. Numerous studies have examined the impact of smoking on male reproductive hormone levels, yielding conflicting results. Some research suggests that smoking may enhance hepatic testosterone metabolism, potentially resulting in decreased serum testosterone levels8. At the same time, other studies12,13 have found no significant disparity in testosterone levels between smokers and nonsmokers. Nevertheless, some studies have also demonstrated a positive association between smoking and elevated testosterone levels14–17. The discrepancy in findings may be attributed to variations in the definitions of smoking, types of tobacco used, levels of exposure, and the control of confounding variables.

Serum cotinine, a primary metabolite of nicotine, is directly proportional to the extent of tobacco smoke exposure and can serve as a reliable indicator of smoking intensity and tobacco smoke exposure18,19. Consequently, to quantify the link between smoking and testosterone levels in men, we studied the impact of serum cotinine levels on total testosterone levels in adult men using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination (NHANES) database from 2011 to 2016.

Materials and methods

Data source

The data utilized in this study was sourced from the NHANES conducted between 2011 and 2016. NHANES is a cross-sectional survey nationally representative of nutrition and health conditions in the United States, administered by the National Centers for Disease Control (CDC) and the National Center for Health Statistics of Prevention. Further details about NHANES can be accessed on the NHANES website (www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes), and previous research has comprehensively described the survey. The NHANES protocol was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants or their parents provided written informed consent. It was not deemed necessary for this secondary analysis to receive additional Institutional Review Board approval; all methods were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

A total of 29,902 participants were initially included in the three consecutive survey cycles of NHANES from 2011 to 2016. Following exclusions of females (n = 15,151), individuals under 18 years of age (n = 6,051), those with missing serum cotinine data (n = 821), and those with missing total testosterone data (n = 82), the final sample size for this study consisted of 7,797 participants (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the sample selection from NHANES 2011–2016.

Serum cotinine

The main independent variable in this study was serum cotinine. Serum cotinine is measured by an isotope dilution-high performance liquid chromatography/atmosphe-ric pressure chemical ionization tandem mass spectrometry (ID HPLC-APCI MS/MS). Details regarding evaluation of serum cotinine levels are available at: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhanes/2011-2012/labmethods/cot_g_met_cotinine.pdf and https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Nhanes/2011-2012/COTNAL_G.htm.

Total testosterone

The primary outcome of this study is serum total testosterone level. Total testosterone measurement is performed via the isotope dilution liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (ID-LC-MS/MS) method for routine quantitation of serum testosterone based on the National Institute for Standards and Technology’s (NIST) reference method. A detailed description of serum total testosterone testing can be found at: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhanes/2011-2012/labmethods/tst_g_met.pdf and https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Nhanes/2011-2012/TST_G.htm.

Covariates

Based on previous literature20–23 and clinical practice, confounders were selected. Continuous variables included age in years at the time of participation, serum cholesterol (mmol/L), uric acid (umol/L), serum triglycerides (mmol/L), hemoglobin (g/dL). Categorical variable contained race/ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), education level, marital status, ratio of family income to poverty (PIR), alcohol consumption, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, and physical activity level.

In NHANES 2011–2016, race/ethnicity included categories of Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Non-Hispanic Asian, and Other Race - Including Multi-Racial. In addition, BMI was calculated as weight/height2 (kg/m2) and categorized into three groups: normal weight (< 25.0 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–30.0 kg/m2), and obesity (≥ 30.0 kg/m2). Education levels of participants were classified as less than high school, high school or equivalent, and more than high school. Marital status was categorized as never married, widowed/divorced/separated, and married/living with partner. The PIR was categorized as < 1, 1–3, and ≥ 3.

Furthermore, based on the definitions from the National Institute of Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA), alcohol consumption was classified as none, moderate (1–2 drinks/day for men), heavy (≥ 3 drinks/day for men). Smoking status was categorized into three groups: current, former, and never smokers. Never smokers were defined as individuals who had smoked no more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime. Former smokers were those who had smoked more than 100 cigarettes but had since quit. Current smokers were defined as those who had smoked more than 100 cigarettes and were still smoking24. Hypertension was diagnosed based on self-reported high blood pressure or use of medication for high blood pressure by the participants. And participants were also considered as hypertensive if their systolic BP was ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥ 80 mmHg25.

Moreover, diabetes was determined through self-report of a doctor’s diagnosis or the use of oral hypoglycemic drugs or insulin. According to the American Physical Activity Councils chronic health conditions recommendation26 (≥ 75 min/week of vigorous or ≥ 150 min/week of moderate activity), participants were divided into three groups, active (≥ the level of recommended activity), less active (< the level of recommended activity), and inactive (no physical activity).

Statistical analysis

We selected “wtmec2yr” as the weighting variable for the years 2011 to 2016. A detailed description of wtmec2yr can be found at: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Nhanes/2011-2012/DEMO_G.htm#WTMEC2YR. Continuous variables were presented as weighted mean ± standard error (SE), while categorical variables were reported as weighted percentages(%). Serum cotinine levels were stratified into quartiles. Baseline characteristics were analyzed using a weighted linear regression model for continuous variables and a weighted chi-square test for categorical variables.

We assessed the association between serum cotinine and total testosterone using weighted univariate and multiple linear regression models. And three models were built: an unadjusted model, a minimally adjusted model (adjusted for age and race), and a fully adjusted model (adjusted for age, race, education, marital status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, physical activity status, and BMI). In addition, there were subgroup analyses stratified by age, race, marital status, BMI and alcohol consumption. In order to examine the potential non-linear association between serum cotinine and total testosterone, a fully adjusted model with smooth curve fitting was constructed. Analyzing threshold effects was used to find the inflection point between serum cotinine and total testosterone. In addition, the age-based subgroup was also analyzed using the same statistical procedures described previously.

All statistical analyses were performed with R software (version 3.4.3, http://www.R-project.org), Empowerstats (http://www.empowerstats.com, X&Y Solutions, Inc, CA, USA), and STATA 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). An appropriate examination weight was applied to represent the complex survey design. The p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant; the p-value < 0.01 was considered highly significant.

The serum cotinine levels were stratified by quartiles: Q1(≤ 0.011ng/ml), Q2(0.011-0.054ng/ml), Q3(0.054-57.7ng/ml) and Q4(> 57.7 ng/ml). The relationship between serum cotinine and total testosterone was assessed using stratified analyses by age, race, marital status, BMI, and alcohol consumption, as shown in Fig. 2. Subgroup interaction analysis revealed significant interactions between serum cotinine and age, as well as between serum cotinine and race (p for interaction < 0.05). The positive correlation between serum cotinine and testosterone was stronger in the 18–30 age group compared to the 30–50 age group (adjusted β: 0.08 vs. 0.07). Similarly, this positive correlation was stronger among Non-Hispanic Asians. However, the interaction tests for marital status, BMI, and alcohol consumption were not significant (p for interaction > 0.05).

In order to investigate whether there was a nonlinear relationship between serum cotinine and total testosterone, we discarded several values considered to be outliers and serum cotinine was limited to 1000 ng/mL or less, and smoothed curve fitting analysis was performed (Figs. 3 and 4). We found a nonlinear relationship between serum cotinine and total testosterone, with an inflection point at serum cotinine = 487 ng/ml. Beyond the inflection point, total testosterone levels were decreased as serum cotinine increase. Discussion

The research demonstrates a positive association between elevated serum cotinine levels and total testosterone levels in adult males. This association remained statistically significant following adjustments for potential confounding variables. Notably, an inflection point was identified at a serum cotinine level of 487 ng/ml, beyond which total testosterone levels decreased with increasing serum cotinine levels.

The association between smoking and testosterone levels has been a topic of debate in the literature. Dai J-B et al8. proposed that smoking may lead to a reduction in serum testosterone levels. Conversely, other studies12,27 have indicated that smoking does not have a substantial impact on testosterone levels, while additional studies17,28,29 have shown that smoking may elevate testosterone levels. The variations in results observed across these studies can be attributed to heterogeneity, encompassing distinctions in geographical location, demographic composition, sample size, research methodology, and management of potential confounding variables.

Our study indicates a potential positive association between smoking and testosterone levels. However, the specific mechanism through which elevated cotinine may lead to an increase in total testosterone remains uncertain, prompting consideration of various potential explanations. Specifically, nicotine and its primary metabolite cotinine are metabolized by the uridine 5’-diphospho-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT) superfamily enzymes: specifically UGT2B10 for nicotine and cotinine, and UGT2B7, UGT2B15, and UGT2B17 are responsible for metabolizing androgens. Consequently, there is a potential for competitive inhibition of androgen glucuronidation by nicotine and its metabolites, leading to an increase in androgen levels14,16,30–32. Moreover, another study33 has speculated whether cigarette smoke and cotinine may act as aromatase inhibitors and reduce the conversion of testosterone to estradiol, thereby contributing to elevated testosterone levels.

Agnes Kimblad34 similarly demonstrated a positive association between cotinine and testosterone levels; however, their study was limited by a small sample size, the use of urine cotinine levels as the independent variable, and a focus solely on snuff tobacco. In contrast, our study employed a larger sample size, utilized serum cotinine levels as the independent variable, examined a more comprehensive range of tobacco products, and implemented rigorous control measures for confounding variables.

However, our study confirms that testosterone levels do not increase arbitrarily with the increase of serum cotinine, which has not been reported in previous studies. The precise mechanism by which testosterone decreases as serum cotinine concentration rises beyond a certain threshold remains unclear. However, it is hypothesized that the activation of autophagy or apoptosis in Leydig cells will directly inhibit testosterone biosynthesis35–37. We recommend that further research be conducted to explore the impact of elevated serum cotinine levels on total testosterone concentrations.

In this study, we found that the association between serum cotinine and testosterone exhibited significant differences across various age groups. Specifically, in the group aged 50 and above, the association between serum cotinine and testosterone demonstrated a significant nonlinear relationship, where testosterone levels decreased as serum cotinine increased when serum cotinine concentration exceeded 508 ng/ml. Conversely, in the 18–30 and 30–50 age groups, the relationship between serum cotinine and testosterone was a linear positive association. This positive association was stronger in the 18–30 age group, where testosterone levels increased consistently with rising serum cotinine levels. This age difference suggests that the relationship between serum cotinine and testosterone may be influenced by age-related factors. We speculate that this phenomenon may be attributed to the gradual decline in serum testosterone levels with age, commencing in men in their mid-30s and decreasing at an average annual rate of 1.6%38–43. This mechanism may attenuate the effect of cotinine on testosterone. However, the specific mechanisms remain unclear, future research should further explore the reasons for age differences to validate and refine our findings.

Nevertheless, it is vital to acknowledge the limitations of our study. Specifically, our research focused solely on the impact of cotinine on total testosterone levels, neglecting to examine the potential associations between cotinine and free testosterone or sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG). Additionally, while we observed a positive relationship between elevated serum cotinine levels and total testosterone levels, it remains unclear whether this association is reversible and if testosterone levels decrease upon smoking cessation. We hope that future investigations will delve into this area of inquiry. Furthermore, it is essential to note that this study is cross-sectional, limiting the ability to establish causality. Additionally, there may be unidentified confounding variables that could impact the outcomes. It is also worth mentioning that the results may not be generalizable to individuals under the age of 18 due to the age restrictions of the NHANES 2011–2016 questionnaire. Moreover, the study was constrained by the inability to gather new data as it relied on secondary analysis.

Conclusions

In summary, the findings of this study suggest a positive association between elevated serum cotinine levels and total testosterone levels in adult men. However, it is essential to note that this association may be reversed at excessively high serum cotinine concentrations. Further research is warranted to explore the impact of heavy smoking on testosterone levels.
