Assessing the environmental costs of multi-scale recurrent neural networks for sustainable extreme rainfall nowcasting
Authors: Douglas Brum, Luan Teylo, Fabricio Polifke da Silva, Fernanda Cerqueira Vasconcellos, Gabriel Berto Breder, Lívia de Azevedo, Eduardo Bezerra, Fabio Porto, Mariza Ferro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The nowcasting of extreme rainfall poses significant daily challenges on a global scale, especially in vulnerable regions of the Global South. Conventional Numerical Weather Prediction models often fail to deliver accurate and timely forecasts for extreme weather events, exacerbating socioeconomic inequalities and increasing climate vulnerability. Deep learning approaches present a promising opportunity to uncover more precise predictive patterns; however, their application remains constrained by the high computational costs associated to their large parameter spaces. This study evaluates the effectiveness of the MS-RNN framework for improving computational efficiency and predictive accuracy in extreme precipitation nowcasting, using real weather radar data from the TAASRAD19 and Rio de Janeiro datasets. While the framework has been extensively validated both theoretically and experimentally in other scenarios, this work examines its application to real radar data. Metrics related to sustainability, such as energy consumption, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CO_{2}$$\end{document} emissions, and water usage, have not been calculated in this specific context and are rarely addressed in current literature. Our findings demonstrate the potential of the solution to enhance computational efficiency maintaining predictive performance when applied to real weather radar data, supporting sustainable and accessible AI solutions for climate resilience in resource-limited regions.

Introduction

The intensification of extreme weather events, such as torrential rains, characterized by large rainfall accumulations in a short period of time, and storms, which are not inherently extreme weather events but become unusual when they surpass specific thresholds, has caused severe impacts in urban areas, especially in countries in the global South 1. These events not only threaten urban infrastructure and the safety of populations, but also exacerbate economic and social inequalities, worsening the vulnerability of already disadvantaged communities. Forecasting and mitigating these events are urgent challenges faced by monitoring centers and decision makers, especially in regions facing financial and structural limitations to cope with natural disasters. Recent studies show that climate change is increasing the frequency and intensity of extreme events, with more pronounced impacts in tropical areas such as Brazil 2–4. Projections indicate that, as global temperatures continue to rise, events such as torrential rains, characterized by high hourly rainfall rates, will become even more frequent and intense, causing catastrophic effects in densely populated urban regions 2,5,6.

In the Brazilian context, the city of Rio de Janeiro stands out as an emblematic example. As one of the most vulnerable regions in the country, the city of Rio de Janeiro faces constant challenges with floods, landslides, and other disasters related to extreme rainfall 7,8. Furthermore, the city’s specific characteristics make identifying extreme events and preventing their consequences even more challenging. Its topographical features, blending mountainous massifs with flat and coastal regions, uniquely influence the rainfall distribution. Such characteristics lead to prediction models for the city that cannot capture and forecast the rainfall’s high spatial and temporal variability 9.

Typically, rainfall forecasting is done using physics-based models known as Numerical Weather Prediction (NWP). These models solve physical equations that describe atmospheric motion through time integration under given initial and boundary conditions to predict the future atmospheric state 10. NWP models have proven successful in providing reliable forecasts for various weather conditions, and several meteorological service centers around the world have established their own numerical forecasting models to accommodate their local climate characteristics 11. This is the case, for example, for ECMWF from Europe 12, GRAPES from China 13, WRF from the United States 14, and BRAMS from Brazil 15. However, these models have certain limitations and are affected by the present initial and boundary condition fields, which can lead to poor predictions at 0–2-hour lead times 11,16–the time frame typically required to reliably characterize the possible occurrence of extreme weather events. While high-resolution NWP models can improve the accuracy of extreme event predictions, they demand extensive computing power, limiting their practicality for fast, large-scale forecasts.

In this context, Artificial Intelligence (AI) emerges as a promising alternative to overcome the limitations of traditional models by enabling the analysis of complex patterns in meteorological data, generating more detailed forecasts, and providing valuable tools where traditional NWP models are not successful 17,18. Unlike NWP, which relies on complex physical equations, these methods deduce rainfall evolution through massive datasets and samples used during their training phases. One such method uses weather radar images, which provide high-resolution information on reflectivity patterns and approach rain forecasting as a video prediction problem. For short-term precipitation forecasting (also called precipitation nowcasting), several studies have demonstrated the capability of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), including ConvLSTM 19 and its successors (PredRNN 20, MotionRNN 21), which have demonstrated superior accuracy over NWP for short-term forecasts 22,23.

However, although RNN models can improve forecast accuracy and require far fewer resources than NWP models 24, they still require substantial computational resources, exacerbating inequities: regions most vulnerable to climate disasters often lack the infrastructure to deploy them 25. This technological inequality perpetuates a vicious cycle in which less advantaged regions, which suffer the most from the impacts of climate change, have the least access to advanced forecasting solutions. The high computational costs of training and maintaining these models amplify the disparity, leaving resource-constrained regions unable to implement effective early warning systems. Consequently, the populations most vulnerable to climate impacts remain marginalized. Worse, the energy-intensive training of AI models contributes to the very environmental degradation they aim to mitigate – a paradox underscored by our systematic literature review (see Methods).

Our review revealed a critical gap: no existing studies apply Green AI principles (e.g., energy efficiency, carbon footprint analysis) to precipitation nowcasting, despite the field’s reliance on resource-intensive deep learning. While generative models (e.g., GANs, diffusion models) improve forecast quality 22,26, none evaluate sustainability metrics. Hybrid physics-AI approaches 27 similarly prioritize accuracy over environmental impact. This neglect perpetuates a cycle where climate solutions inadvertently worsen the crisis they address.

The Multi-scale RNN (MS-RNN) framework proposed by Mei et al. 28 was designed to improve existing RNN models by reducing their memory footprint and overhead while enhancing performance. In their work, the authors demonstrated that MS-RNN significantly reduces the costs of the training phase. For example, compared to the PrecipLSTM model, the framework achieved results in which memory usage was reduced by more than 50%, while the prediction quality, measured by MSE, improved by more than 4.2%. Furthermore, the framework also reduced the computational complexity in terms of FLOPS by at least 56.2%. However, its environmental benefits remain unquantified.

Our study bridges this gap by evaluating energy consumption, CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} equivalent (CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}eq) emissions (via CodeCarbon 29), and water footprint 30 of three deep learning models – PredRNN-V2, MotionRNN, and ConvLSTM – alongside their MS-RNN-enhanced counterparts across two radar datasets: https://github.com/MPBA/TAASRAD19TAASRAD19 (Alps) 31, and a tropical Rio de Janeiro dataset 32.

Therefore, the question guiding this study is: Can a sustainable AI predict extreme rainfall? By coupling MS-RNN framework with empirical environmental metrics, we demonstrate that it is possible to create solutions that not only meet the demands for precision but also take into account the limitations and operational realities of developing regions, promoting greater global equity and climate resilience.

Results

Guided by the principles of Green AI, as defined by Schwartz et al. 33, this study first assesses the computational efficiency and predictive accuracy of three deep learning models–PredRNN-V2, MotionRNN, and ConvLSTM–compared with their MS-RNN counterparts. The evaluation was carried out using two weather radar datasets: the TAASRAD19 dataset, which covers the Italian Alps, and a dataset covering the state of Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. We analyze computational performance metrics, including GPU memory usage, energy consumption, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CO_{2}$$\end{document}eq emissions, alongside predictive accuracy metrics such as Mean Squared Error (MSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Structural Similarity Index (SSIM), and Critical Success Index (CSI). More details on how these metrics were estimated and the machines used in this study are provided in the Methodology Section.

Computational performance

We began by analyzing the results for both the TAASRAD19 and Rio de Janeiro datasets, focusing on computational performance metrics: GPU memory usage, execution time, and GPU energy consumption. All reported values represent the average of five independent runs for each model. Figure 1 illustrates the impact of the MS-RNN framework on memory consumption (left) and execution time (right) for each model across both datasets. As can be seen in Fig. 1, on average, the MS-RNN framework reduced memory usage by 41%, 48%, and 50% for ConvLSTM, PredRNN-V2, and MotionRNN, respectively, on TAASRAD19. In the Rio de Janeiro dataset, significant reductions were also observed: approximately 24.3% for ConvLSTM and 22.3% for PredRNN-V2. Note that, to save time and computational resources, we excluded the MotionRNN model from the evaluation on the Rio de Janeiro dataset, since it exhibited the highest execution time and, consequently, the largest environmental footprint (as well as the lowest predictive accuracy – see the Predictive Accuracy section).

This reduction in memory usage positively impacted other performance metrics, particularly execution time. As shown in the right plot in Fig. 1, the MS-RNN framework consistently reduced execution time across all evaluated models. Specifically, reductions of 52.66% for ConvLSTM, 66% for PredRNN-V2, and 50% for MotionRNN were observed on TAASRAD19. While on the Rio de Janeiro dataset, the reductions were: 10.40% for ConvLSTM and 6.81% for PredRNN-V2.

Therefore, the MS-RNN framework was effective in reducing both memory footprint and execution time. However, unlike the results obtained with the TAASRAD19 dataset, the savings observed in the Rio de Janeiro dataset were smaller. This difference can be attributed to the dimensions of the input images: in TAASRAD19, the images are 480\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}480, whereas in the Rio dataset, they are only 32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}32. Since the MS-RNN framework applies successive downsampling by half (followed by upsampling), the computational impact of compression is more significant when working with larger images. The reductions in memory usage and execution time also resulted in improvements in environmental metrics, such as energy consumption, carbon footprint, and water usage. For energy consumption, we used the nvidia-smi tool to measure GPU-level energy consumption during application execution. As shown in Fig. 2, for the TAASRAD19 dataset (left figure), the MS-RNN framework reduced energy consumption by 64.61% for ConvLSTM, 71.05% for PredRNN-V2, and 54.45% for MotionRNN. For the Rio de Janeiro dataset (right figure), we observed reductions of 24.50% and 22.40% for the ConvLSTM and PredRNN-V2 models, respectively. These values are closely aligned with the reductions in execution time, indicating that runtime is the primary factor contributing to the MS-RNN framework’s improved energy efficiency.

In Fig. 2, we also include the SSIM metric (shown as a line plot) to assess the impact of the MS-RNN framework on model output quality. As shown in the figure, the MS-RNN-enhanced models achieved similar quality compared to their baseline counterparts. Therefore, the MS-RNN framework successfully reduces energy consumption while preserving the predictive quality of the RNN models.

Next, we employed the CodeCarbon library to estimate environment-related metrics, such as carbon emissions and water usage. Table 1 presents the codeCarbon metrics for both datasets. As expected, the framework reduced environmental impact in both cases, although the gains were more pronounced for TAASRAD19. In contrast, on the TAASRAD19 dataset, MS-PredRNN-V2 reduced CO2eq emissions and water usage by 61.7% and 62.7%, respectively, and MS-ConvLSTM achieved reductions of 57.1% and 58.2%. Predictive accuracy

Predictive accuracy was evaluated using the following metrics: SSIM, MSE, MAE, their balanced counterparts B-MSE and B-MAE, the loss function (Loss), and the Critical Success Index (CSI), which provides a clear understanding of the quality of reflectivity images generated by weather radar. While MSE and MAE assess the overall magnitude of error, B-MSE and B-MAE penalize more heavily the errors in underrepresented or extreme precipitation values, offering a more informative evaluation of the model’s ability to capture rare but impactful events. The results in Table 2 reveal a detailed picture of predictive accuracy in the Rio de Janeiro dataset from the perspective of the MS-enhanced models. Compared to their baseline counterparts, MS models showed modest reductions in performance across most metrics. For MS-PredRNN-V2, SSIM decreased by 2.4%, while MSE and MAE increased by 7.2% and 4.3%, respectively, and the combined Loss rose by 5.9%. For MS-ConvLSTM, SSIM was 3.3% lower, with increases of 2.4% in MSE, 1.0% in MAE, and 1.6% in Loss. These moderate differences suggest that MS-enhanced models faced greater challenges in the more complex and variable conditions of the Rio de Janeiro dataset. Nevertheless, they still delivered competitive predictive results while significantly reducing computational demands.

For the Rio de Janeiro dataset, the CSI metrics provide a more balanced picture: although MS-enhanced models do not achieve the highest scores at every threshold, their performance remains comparable, with differences below 5% at most levels. Discussion

This study explored the use of deep learning models for precipitation nowcasting, focusing not only on predictive precision but also on the environmental and operational costs of these models. Beyond traditional metrics (e.g., MSE, MAE), the work advocates for a green AI perspective, comparing computational efficiency, energy consumption, CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}eq emissions, and water use. Our aim is to raise the critical need to balance precision with sustainability in climate-related AI applications, especially in resource-limited regions in the Global South.

Through experiments on two radar datasets: TAASRAD19 and a complex regional dataset from Rio de Janeiro, we compared the performance of three models (PredRNN-V2, MotionRNN, ConvLSTM) with their MS-RNN-enhanced counterparts, focusing on both predictive accuracy and computational efficiency. The results showed that MS-RNN models achieved substantial reductions in computational costs while maintaining, and in some cases even improving, predictive performance. This efficiency made it possible to execute models like MS-PredRNN-V2 on local machines, highlighting their potential for applications in environments with limited computational infrastructure. In contrast, on the more challenging Rio de Janeiro dataset, MS models showed a slight reduction in predictive performance compared to their original versions. Nonetheless, they still delivered competitive results–especially when weighing predictive performance against energy savings and environmental impact.

However, challenges remain in predicting extreme events, as evidenced by the high values of the balanced metrics B-MSE and B-MAE across both datasets. These values indicate that all models, including the MS-RNN-enhanced variants, still face difficulties in forecasting extreme precipitation events. These metrics, which penalize errors more heavily in less frequent intensity ranges (such as extreme rainfall events), suggest the need to improve the models’ sensitivity to high-impact weather phenomena.

To address this limitation, recent studies have integrated auxiliary data, such as orographic features 34, or employed deep generative models like Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and Diffusion models. These approaches have shown promise in generating higher-quality, detailed radar images 22,35–38. Furthermore, incorporating physical principles into model training has proven effective for extreme precipitation accuracy 22,27. For instance, NowcastNet 22 is considered a benchmark, capable of predicting convective evolution over longer periods, whereas models like PredRNN suffer from fast dissipation. However, these state-of-the-art generative models generally lack sustainability assessments. NowcastNet, which processes 3D dynamics and integrates physics, is inherently more complex than traditional RNN. Consequently, its training demands substantial computational resources and energy. Similarly, GANs often require heavy architectures due to their dual-network structure. The lack of sustainability metrics in these studies creates uncertainty regarding their feasibility in resource-constrained environments and raises concerns about their environmental footprint.

These findings are reinforced by our systematic review, which identified a complete absence of Green AI applications in precipitation nowcasting literature, a widespread neglect of energy efficiency metrics in AI weather prediction research; and no existing studies that balance computational sustainability with extreme event forecasting. This gap emphasizes the importance of guiding the development of new architectures that explicitly incorporate sustainability and computational metrics, enabling an evaluation of the trade-off between accuracy and resource consumption. Such knowledge supports more informed decisions about how to train models and which architectures to adopt in the context of the precipitation nowcasting problem.

Despite the limitations of MS-RNN-enhanced models, this study brings important contributions and achieves its objective of discussing the use of more sustainable AI approaches to predict extreme rainfall. While high-precision solutions are desirable for saving lives and supporting local economies, their associated costs may render the development and operational execution unfeasible in developing countries. Such technological inequality can perpetuate a vicious cycle in which less-advantaged regions, which suffer the most from the impacts of climate change, have the least access to advanced forecasting solutions. This situation maintains the disparity, leaving resource-constrained regions unable to implement effective early warning systems. Consequently, the populations most vulnerable to climate impacts remain marginalized.

Regarding limitations, although only a fraction of the TAASRAD19 dataset (2010–2013) was used, the models achieved consistent and robust results comparable to the state of the art in short-term rainfall prediction. We acknowledge that extending the training to the full 2010–2019 dataset could improve predictive accuracy. Future work will evaluate this broader configuration to further explore the trade-off between accuracy gains and environmental impact. Additionally, we plan to assess NowcastNet and a spatiotemporal mixed-enhanced GAN, optimized with lightweight convolutions and quantization methods, evaluating their accuracy alongside computational and environmental demands guided by Green AI principles.

Methodology

For this study, we selected three widely recognized models for precipitation forecasting using radar data: PredRNN-V2, ConvLSTM, and MotionRNN. In Mei et al. 28, the authors made these models MS-RNN-compliant and presented a series of evaluations demonstrating improved accuracy while maintaining lower memory costs for spatiotemporal predictive learning. However, their analysis was limited to assessments of memory footprint and FLOPS reduction. Furthermore, they did not explore the environmental impact of the MS framework, such as its effects on energy consumption, CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} emissions, or water usage – critical metrics for evaluating the sustainability of AI-driven solutions.

A schematic overview of the architecture of the MS-RNN framework is shown in Fig. 5. Conceptually, MS-RNN arranges recurrent layers in a multi-scale pyramid: feature maps are progressively downsampled (blue arrows) to coarser resolutions and then upsampled (red arrows) back to the original scale, while multi-scale memory lists and skip connections propagate information across scales and time. This design allows the model to combine large-scale structures and fine-grained details in the radar fields more effectively.Figure 5Simplified schematic overview of the MS-RNN framework, adapted from Fig. 2 of Mei et al. 28. The diagram highlights how multi-scale memory lists interact with the recurrent layers.

Simplified schematic overview of the MS-RNN framework, adapted from Fig. 2 of Mei et al. 28. The diagram highlights how multi-scale memory lists interact with the recurrent layers.

We also selected these models for their proficiency in handling spatiotemporal dependencies within intricate datasets. ConvLSTM, developed by Shi et al.39, enhances traditional LSTM by incorporating convolutional layers, facilitating the concurrent modeling of spatial and temporal dynamics, which is particularly advantageous for radar data. PredRNN-V240 introduces spatiotemporal LSTM (ST-LSTM) with a zig-zag computational framework, allowing for a more refined capture of complex dependencies in both time and space. Lastly, MotionRNN21 uses MotionGRU mechanisms to differentiate stable long-term trends from fast-changing patterns, making it highly adaptable to dynamic weather conditions.

Therefore, in order to perform the evaluation, we implemented a practical methodology by establishing a testbed setup. This setup enabled us to collect and assess the relevant metrics. In the following, we provide a detailed overview of this testbed, such as the tools used for metrics measurements and the input data utilized. The scripts employed in our evaluations, along with their detailed results, are available at https://gitlab.inria.fr/ai_project/ms-predGitlab link.

Experimental setup

All experiments in this study were performed on PLAFRIM, an experimental platform located at the Inria Bordeaux Research Center. PLAFRIM is a 192-node platform divided into 2 clusters. For our tests, we used the Sirocco cluster, whose nodes are each powered by two AMD Zen3 Milan EPYC 7513 processors, totaling 64 cores at 2.60 GHz, 512 GB of RAM, and 4 TB of RAID0 storage. Moreover, all Sirocco nodes are equipped with NVIDIA GPUs of different generations. For the tests, we selected the nodes with NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

The decision to use A100 GPUs was due the fact that this model had the largest available memory (40 GB), enabling us to run not only the MS-RNN-compliant version (which reduces memory footprint), but also the regular models, thus allowing a fair comparison in terms of performance and the environmental impact of the MS-RNN framework.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_{n,i,j}$$\end{document} be the weight at pixel \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(i,j)$$\end{document} in frame \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{x}_{n,i,j}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{n,i,j}$$\end{document} the predicted and ground-truth reflectivity values. The B-MSE and B-MAE metrics are computed according to Eq. 2 and 3, respectively, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W$$\end{document} are the height and width of each frame, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N$$\end{document} is the number of frames. In our context, the CSI is evaluated for multiple precipitation thresholds (e.g., 0.5 mm/h, 2 mm/h, 5 mm/h, 10 mm/h, and 30 mm/h) to analyze the performance of the models under varying rainfall intensities, reflecting different meteorological conditions and applications.

In addition to these individual metrics, the loss function used in training is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Loss = MSE + MAE$$\end{document}, combining the strengths of both metrics: while MSE penalizes large errors more intensively, MAE provides stability during training, ensuring that smaller errors are not ignored.

To monitor execution, we used nvidia-smi and the codeCarbon library. The nvidia-smi is a command-line utility that allows us to monitor NVIDIA GPU devices. The tool can be run in the background during execution to capture a series of metrics at a predefined time interval, for instance, every second. In the end, it generates a time series that shows the application’s (and the GPU’s) behavior throughout execution. In this study, we used the following command:

nvidia-smi --query-gpu=timestamp,name,pci.bus_id,utilization.gpu,memory.used,power.draw -l 1

Among the metrics, we focused on analyzing the GPU memory footprint of the application (memory.used) and energy usage (the power.draw metric).

For the training step, we used the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 and a batch size of 1, configured for a maximum of 25 epochs. Early stopping, with a patience of three epochs without improvement, was applied to prevent overfitting. The same configuration was used across all model architectures. To minimize variability due to the shared training environment, each model was executed five times, and results were reported as the mean and standard deviation.

During the hyperparameter tuning stage, we also conducted tests without early stopping. These runs resulted in significantly longer training times and poorer predictive performance, without any relevant gains in accuracy. Therefore, early stopping was maintained in all final experiments as it provided the best balance between predictive performance and computational cost.

To capture the carbon footprint of the models, we used the Python library codeCarbon 29. This library is designed to track the carbon emissions of applications and requires slight code modifications to instrument the sections where tracking is needed. We applied these modifications to all models by wrapping their main functions, ensuring full monitoring during execution. At the end of each run, the library returned the total energy consumption in kilowatt-hours (kWh). Since the experiments were conducted in France, which has a predominantly nuclear energy mix45, the carbon intensity was significantly reduced. However, because precise local data were unavailable at the time of analysis, a global average of 475 g CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}eq/kWh was adopted, based on aggregated international electricity generation statistics reported by the IEA46. This ensured consistency with other sustainability assessments. Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) is a metric for assessing a data center’s energy efficiency; due to the experiment being conducted in a small facility, the standard value of 1 was utilized.

To estimate water consumption, we applied the equation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Water\ Consumption = e \cdot \left[ \rho _{s_1} + \theta \cdot \rho _{s_2} \right]$$\end{document} 30, where e is the energy consumed, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _{s_1}$$\end{document} is the on-site Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _{s_2}$$\end{document} is the off-site WUE, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} is the Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) of the server. We set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _{s_1} = 0$$\end{document} since the PLAFRIM cluster does not use water for cooling. For \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _{s_2}$$\end{document}, we used the national grid average for France 47, which corresponds to 3.67145 L/kWh.

Datasets

Weather radar data are obtained via electromagnetic pulses that reflect upon encountering various targets, including water or ice particles (hydrometeors) suspended in the atmosphere, as well as biological entities such as birds, bats, and insects, which can introduce noise into the measurements. These reflections, recorded as reflectivity in dBZ, can be translated into precipitation rates 48.

In this work, we use two main datasets composed of several images collected from weather radar observations. The first is TAASRAD19, a detailed collection of events recorded over nine years (from 2010 to 2019) by the Civil Protection Agency of the Trentino-South Tyrol region in the Italian Alps31. Data were captured with a radar system operating at a temporal resolution of 5 minutes, generating up to 288 scans per day and covering a 240 km diameter at a 500-meter resolution.

In our study, events were labeled with meteorological tags, including rain, hail, storm, downpour, and snow, as determined by expert meteorologists. Events classified as snow and those without tags were excluded to reduce noise caused by other climatic conditions. The dataset was intentionally divided chronologically into training (2010–2011), validation (2012), and testing (2013) to preserve temporal independence between samples and prevent information leakage. The decision was taken for the purpose of ensuring a robust and unbiased evaluation of the models under distinct meteorological conditions, and also allowed for the validation of the MS-RNN framework in a well-documented environment before applying it to the more complex conditions of Rio de Janeiro, where extreme rainfall events are notoriously difficult to predict.

The decision to use only the 2010–2013 period from the TAASRAD19 dataset was intentional and aligned with the objectives of this study. This choice aimed to establish a controlled and comparable experimental setup before applying the models to the more complex Rio de Janeiro scenario, using years with continuous and well-documented meteorological records. Beyond the technical scope, this decision also reflects the principles of computational sustainability and technological equity that guide this work. Training on the entire 2010–2019 period would require substantially higher computational power and energy consumption.

The second dataset originates from a weather radar operated by the State Environmental Institute (INEA) obtained in the context of the https://rionowcast.dexl.lncc.br/RioNowcast project, located in Guaratiba, Rio de Janeiro (-22.993, -43.588). This radar has been operational since 2015 and monitors atmospheric phenomena within a 250 km radius. It performs complete 360-degree scans every 5 minutes at eight elevation angles ranging from 1.0 to 8.6 degrees. The collected data are stored in HDF format files, with each file representing a complete scan. On average, the radar generates 288 files per day, amounting to approximately 1.3 GB of data daily. These data were preprocessed and converted into a tabular format (Parquet) containing fields such as datetime, latitude, longitude, altitude (in km), and horizontal reflectivity (in dBZ).

Between 2016 and 2023, data from 224 days were made available, though gaps in data were present throughout the dataset. Although the radar measures 11 parameters, this study focused solely on DBZ, which indicates horizontal reflectivity in dBZ units, as recommended by meteorologists. The original data has high spatial (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 500$$\end{document} meters) and temporal (5–10 minutes) resolution. For this study, we retained the 10-minute temporal resolution and projected the reflectivity values onto a fixed spatial grid with 17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}29 points, covering the metropolitan region of Rio de Janeiro. No spatial or temporal averaging was applied; each grid cell was assigned the radar measurement from the nearest available point in each time step.

To ensure compatibility with the input requirements of the MS-RNN framework, the original \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$17 \times 29$$\end{document} radar reflectivity grid of the Rio de Janeiro dataset was zero-padded to a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$32 \times 32$$\end{document} frame. The valid reflectivity region was placed in the upper-left corner, and the remaining area was filled with zeros. This adjustment was necessary because the MS-RNN architecture performs repeated spatial downsampling and upsampling operations, which require dimensions divisible by powers of two.

Baseline models such as PredRNN-V2, MotionRNN, and ConvLSTM do not require this adjustment and were therefore tested using both the original \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$17 \times 29$$\end{document} resolution and the padded \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$32 \times 32$$\end{document} version. No significant differences were observed between these configurations, indicating that the models effectively ignore the padded regions and focus exclusively on the valid meteorological data. As a result, the zero-padding does not artificially inflate the metrics reported in this study.

For the Rio de Janeiro dataset, evaluation metrics, for both predictions and targets, are computed over the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$32 \times 32$$\end{document} grid to ensure a consistent comparison between the MS-RNN and baseline models. Since both predictions and targets contain zeros in the padded regions across all tested architectures, the padding does not introduce comparative bias in the reported metrics. Furthermore, the predictive quality and spatial coherence of the radar frames were qualitatively validated by professional meteorologists, confirming that the models accurately capture real-world meteorological patterns rather than padding artifacts.

Following the data standardization, we conducted model development and evaluation by dividing the Rio de Janeiro radar dataset into three distinct subsets using a chronological split: training (2016–2018), validation (2019–2020), and testing (through April 2021–2022). After preprocessing and mapping the reflectivity data onto a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$32 \times 32$$\end{document} spatial grid (the image of this grid can be seen in the project repository) with a 10-minute temporal resolution, this division resulted in 20,494 samples for training, 18,471 for validation, and 12,462 for testing. This approach ensures temporal independence between the subsets and reflects the increasing availability and reliability of the radar over time. The image displayed in this study corresponds to a radar observation from March 31, 2022, at 23:10 (UTC–3), a day marked by severe rainfall events across Rio de Janeiro that led to substantial damage and loss of life.

It is important to emphasize that, in this study, extreme precipitation is defined as a rainfall intensity equal to or greater than 50 mm/h 49. However, due to the rarity of events exceeding this threshold in our datasets, the highest categorical threshold reported in the CSI analysis (Table 3) is 30 mm/h, which still lies in the upper tail of the intensity distribution while ensuring a sufficient sample size for statistically stable skill estimates.

To estimate precipitation reflectivity (in mm/h), we used the Z–R relationship, calibrated with an emphasis on the city of Petrópolis 50, which has a higher altitude but presents meteorological conditions similar to those of Rio de Janeiro. Based on this calibration, we define that values from 47 dBZ onwards characterize intense rainfall.

Systematic literature review protocol

To examine related proposals and provide a meaningful discussion on the contemporary developments in the integration of Green AI principles in precipitation nowcasting, we conducted systematic reviews. The complete protocol, including search string, digital library sources, and inclusion and exclusion criteria is available in our https://gitlab.inria.fr/ai_project/ms-predGitlab Repository.

The initial review focused on the application of Green AI to precipitation nowcasting, particularly with regard to extreme weather events. However, no studies were found that fulfilled the criteria after searching digital databases.

To expand the scope of the review but still aligned with Green AI, this study conducted another review of the literature in the domain of AI applied to precipitation nowcasting, concentrating on extreme events. Nevertheless, the investigation explored whether there has been any effort dedicated to creating solutions that prioritize energy-efficient usage, or at the very least, assess the energy usage of these solutions, despite the fact that enhancing energy efficiency was not the main objective. This review resulted in any valid work, just as the previous one using the Green AI keywords. These lack of results in two reviews indicates an absence of concern to develop solutions to precipitation nowcasting in a sustainable manner, and how important it is to start to address this issue and also have more transparency.

Ethical considerations

In line with the principles of Green AI and scientific transparency, we report the total computational costs associated with this study. The entire experimental process consumed approximately 1521.56 hours of computing time. Of this total, 504 hours were dedicated to processing the weather radar dataset, including data conversion, preprocessing, temporal aggregation, and spatial grid projection. An additional 272.02 hours were used for model adaptation, hyperparameter tuning, and preparation of the execution environments to ensure stability and adequate performance. Finally, model training was carried out on the PlaFRIM cluster and consumed 145.54 hours.

A portion of the total runtime (145.54 hours) corresponds to the training stage conducted on the PlaFRIM cluster in France, for which energy consumption, CO2eq emissions, and water usage were estimated using the CodeCarbon library with a low-carbon energy profile. The remaining 1376.02 hours refer to data processing on a local workstation in Brazil (equipped with an Intel Core i9-13900 processor and an NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU with 24 GB of memory). For this stage, we estimated environmental metrics using a conservative approximation based on the machine’s power consumption (325W) and national emission and WUE factors (0.1 kg CO2eq/kWh and 18.59 L/kWh, respectively). Altogether, the project consumed approximately 487.30 kWh, resulting in 47.01 kg of CO2eq emissions and 8462.65 liters of water usage.

Finally, we encourage future research to adopt similar assessments and reporting practices, particularly in climate-focused applications, where the paradox between computational emissions and environmental mitigation must be addressed with transparency and responsibility. By doing so, we strengthen the collective commitment to developing technologies that are not only effective–but also ethically and environmentally sound.
