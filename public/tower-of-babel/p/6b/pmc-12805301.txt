Introduction

Implantable cardiac defibrillators (ICDs) reduce the risk of sudden cardiac death in high-risk populations1, but traditional transvenous devices carry additional risks for certain patients.2 These risks can include venous obstruction, lead failure, and/or infection (eg, pocket infection, bacteremia, and endocarditis) that might require lead extraction and another implant procedure.3 Competitive and recreational athletes face additional risks with transvenous devices given their higher-level activity and associated risk for lead injury or dislodgement.4 Although subcutaneous ICD (S-ICD) can be an alternative, these devices cannot deliver anti-tachycardia pacing (ATP), and about 10% of patients fail the screening test and are not candidates for the device.5,6 A newer alternative is the extravascular ICD (EV-ICD), which is designed for use in the substernal space and can deliver both ATP and defibrillation.7,8 We describe a case of implant of the extravascular ICD as secondary prevention in a professional powerlifter after patient-centered shared decision-making.

Case report

A 49-year-old male professional power lifter with a history of hypertension suffered an out-of-hospital ventricular fibrillation (VF) arrest while resting at home. His event was witnessed by his spouse, who performed cardiopulmonary resuscitation until emergency medical services responded within 5 minutes and successfully defibrillated him, achieving return of spontaneous circulation. During his post-arrest evaluation in the intensive care unit, his echocardiogram demonstrated a severely dilated left ventricle (6.3 cm left ventricle internal diastolic diameter) with severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 20%. His coronary angiogram demonstrated mild non-obstructive coronary artery disease. The cardiac magnetic resonance imaging redemonstrated a severely dilated left ventricle with reduced LVEF, and late gadolinium enhancement in a pattern that was not consistent with ischemic injury. He was discharged with a wearable cardioverter-defibrillator and a plan for outpatient cardiology follow-up. At follow-up, he revealed that he was previously taking anabolic steroids and supratherapeutic testosterone as performance-enhancing drugs (now abstinent), which were thought to be contributing to his cardiomyopathy. A secondary prevention ICD was recommended, and the available device options were discussed with the patient. He expressed a desire to continue his professional powerlifting career, despite education from our team that high-intensity resistance exercises (including those associated with powerlifting) are typically not recommended for those with severe cardiomyopathy, given the risk that associated hemodynamic changes will be poorly tolerated. However, studies have demonstrated that moderate-intensity exercises can improve LVEF9 and a recent American Heart Association statement has endorsed the routine use of moderate-intensity resistance training programs among those with cardiovascular disease.10 Given the patient’s desire to continue powerlifting, he declined a transvenous-ICD and opted for a S-ICD. He failed pre-implant screening for an S-ICD, likely because of oversensing of T-waves (Figure 1). After extensive patient-centered shared decision-making, the patient was referred to our institution for EV-ICD implant.

The EV-ICD implant was performed in the hybrid operating room with a cardiac electrophysiologist and cardiac surgeon as primary operators. An incision was made near the xiphoid fascia where electrocautery and blunt dissection were used to enter the substernal space, where lateral fluoroscopy was used to tunnel the EV-ICD lead just posterior to the sternum (Figure 2A). The appropriate sensing parameters were confirmed, and the lead was secured via sutures. An incision was then made in the left axillary for the generator pocket, and the tunneling tool was used to tunnel the lead from the xiphoid incision to the axillary pocket. A pulse generator was placed in the axillary pocket, and the lead was connected. Defibrillation threshold testing was performed by inducing VF with 3 seconds of 50 Hz stimulation. VF was adequately detected and successfully defibrillated with a 31J shock (52-ohm shock impedance, 7.25 second time to defibrillation) on the first attempt. Final imaging confirming device placement was taken (Figure 2B). The incisions were then closed, and sterile dressings were placed. The patient was monitored overnight and discharged the next day with minimal implant site pain. At 3 month follow-up the patient reported no clinical events, well-healed incisions, and had returned to powerlifting. Follow-up device interrogations have demonstrated a well-functioning device with no recorded arrhythmia events or delivered therapies. Repeat echocardiogram demonstrated improvement in LVEF to 45%–50% with slight decrease in left ventricle internal diastolic diameter to 6.1 cm.

Discussion

The EV-ICD is a novel device able to deliver both ATP and shock therapies, which can be implanted in patients who are not candidates for transvenous or S-ICDs. To our knowledge, this is the first published case where an EV-ICD device was implanted in a professional athlete to meet both the needs of secondary prevention and the patient’s desire to continue a sport with clear risk to integrity of a transvenous lead. Until 2015, consensus statements advised athletes with an ICD against participation in vigorous sports, given the hypothesized risks of arrhythmia-related syncope, shock, device damage, or even death.11 Recent data from athletes with ICDs who participated in organized and high-risk sports have found no occurrences of death, arrhythmia, or shock-related injuries leading to a shift in guidelines, which now recommend shared decision-making on returning to competition.12 Among athletes, the estimated rate of lead malfunction at 5 years following implant is 7%, increasing to 16% at 10 years.13 Contact sports and those requiring repetitive arm motion (such as golf and weight lifting) apply increased pressure on the device and can lead to subclavian crush syndrome or lead dislodgement, rare complications of transvenous devices.14, 15, 16, 17 Although S-ICDs might be an alternative for some athletes, these devices are further from the heart than transvenous-ICDs and thus carry an increased risk of T-wave oversensing, which can cause inappropriate shocks. The risk of inappropriate shocks following EV-ICD implant among athletes is not well-described in the literature, representing an important area for future research. Some populations, including those with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and Brugada syndrome, are known to be at increased risk for T-wave oversensing with S-ICDs.18,19 Additionally, generator proximity to actively-used muscles in the chest wall among weightlifters, swimmers, and those playing contact sports, could theoretically cause S-ICD device dislodgement or damage and increase the risk of oversensing myopotentials and inappropriate shocks.20An advantage of the EV-ICD is the smaller generator, a similar size to the traditional Medtronic ICD, which is made possible by the capability to deliver energy from under the sternum.21 Finally, about 10% of patients fail pre-implant electrocardiogram screening and are thus not eligible for S-ICD placement.22 For athletes in whom transvenous-ICD and S-ICD devices are not suitable options, the EV-ICD is an important alternative that can support shared decision-making around optimizing safety for return to play.
