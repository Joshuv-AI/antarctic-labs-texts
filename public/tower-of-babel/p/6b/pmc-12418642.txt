Dietary inflammatory index and the risk of colorectal adenomas and cancer: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis
Authors: Yi-Jun Wu, Wen-Hua Wang, Yu-Ping Wang, Hong Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe potential association between dietary inflammatory index (DII) and colorectal cancer (CRC) risk, as well as colorectal adenomas (CRA) risk, has been extensively studied, but the findings remain inconclusive. We conducted this systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis to investigate the relationship between the DII and CRC and CRA.MethodsWe comprehensively searched the PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases for cohort and case-control studies reporting the relationship between DII and CRA, or between DII and CRC, as of 15 July 2025. The pooled odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using a random-effects model. Dose-response analysis was conducted where possible. Subgroup analyses were conducted to account for possible sources of heterogeneity. Funnel plot, Egger’s test, and Begg’s test were utilized to assess publication bias.ResultsTwenty-two studies were included, involving 7,612 CRA patients, 25,359 CRC patients, and 896,592 controls. The pooled ORs for CRC and CRA in total population comparing the highest DII to the lowest DII were 1.61 (95% CI, 1.42–1.83) and 1.19 (95% CI, 0.96–1.47), respectively. The pooled ORs for CRC and CRA per unit increase in DII were 1.14 (95% CI, 1.08–1.20) and 1.19 (95% CI, 1.03–1.38), respectively. Stratification by sex revealed a positive association between DII and CRC risk in both males and females. However, this association was stronger in males (Highest DII vs. lowest DII: OR = 1.67, 95% CI [1.39, 2.00]) (Continues DII: OR = 1.16, 95%CI [1.06, 1.26]). Dose-response analysis revealed a nonlinear positive correlation between CRC risk and DII (P for non−linearity < 0.0001).ConclusionsOur findings suggest that dietary habits characterized by a high inflammatory index may increase the risk of CRA and CRC.Trial registration PROSPERO registration number: CRD42025641934.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01202-9.

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer globally. In 2020, there were 1.93 million newly confirmed CRC cases worldwide, accounting for approximately 10% of all cancer cases. Projections suggest that the number of new CRC cases globally could reach 3.2 million by 2040, which will significantly increase the global health burden [1]. Most sporadic CRC develop through the adenoma-carcinoma sequence, with 0.25% of colorectal adenomas (CRA) progressing to cancer annually [2]. Chronic inflammation, driven by inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or dietary factors plays a pivotal role in carcinogenesis [3, 4], as evidenced by the anti-CRC effects of NSAIDs and experimental models linking inflammation to tumorigenesis [5].

While non-modifiable risk factors (e.g., genetics, age, personal history) contribute to CRC, dietary habits may represent a modifiable target [6, 7]. Dietary intake is thought to influence colorectal carcinogenesis, potentially through modulating the inflammatory milieu in the host [8, 9]. Pro-inflammatory diets, such as those high in red meat, processed meat, and fat, increase CRC risk [10, 11]. Conversely, anti-inflammatory diets rich in fiber, cereals [10], and supplemental vitamin D are protective [12, 13].

Shivappa et al. developed a scoring system for dietary inflammatory index (DII) based on prior publications assessing the effects of specific food components on six major inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, and IL-10) [14]. This system scientifically and comprehensively evaluates the inflammatory potential of food ingredients, ranging from a high DII score (pro-inflammatory capacity) to a low DII score (anti-inflammatory capacity). Recently, researchers have found that a high DII is associated with the development of liver, lung, and breast cancers [15–17]. Numerous studies have investigated the association between DII and colorectal cancer, as well as precancerous lesions. Two meta-analyses on DII and colorectal cancer risk were published in 2018 [18] and 2022 [19], but neither focused on the relationship between DII and the CRA. Given the importance of early detection and intervention for precancerous lesions, we conducted a meta-analysis to evaluate the association between DII and CRA to fill this gap. Additionally, we performed a dose-response meta-analysis to assess the relationship between DII and CRC risk. We updated the search period and expanded the number of included articles to enhance the comprehensiveness and reliability of the evidence.

Materials and methods

This meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The study protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) and can be accessed using the registration number: CRD42025641934.

Search strategy

We searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases until 15 July 2025 for studies reporting the relationship between DII and CRA or DII and CRC. The search keywords were as follows: (“dietary inflammatory index” OR “DII”) AND (“Adenomatous Polyp” OR “adenomas” OR “Colonic Neoplasms” OR “Neoplasm, Colonic” OR “Colon Neoplasms” OR “Neoplasm, Colon” OR “Cancer of Colon” OR “Colon Cancers” OR “Colonic Cancer” OR “Colon Adenocarcinoma” OR “Rectal Neoplasms” OR “Rectum Neoplasms” OR “Rectal Tumors” OR “Rectum Cancers” OR “Rectal Cancer” OR “Colorectal Neoplasms” OR “Colorectal Tumors” OR “Colorectal Cancer” OR “Colorectal Carcinomas”). We limited our search to articles published in English and articles studying humans. Detailed search strategies are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Using the PubMed search strategy as an example.

Study selection

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Studies investigating the relationship between DII and the risk of CRA or CRC; (2) Studies comparing the risk of the highest DII and the lowest DII, providing the hazard ratio (HR), relative risk ratio (RR), or odds ratio (OR) with a 95% confidence interval; (3) Studies with a case-control or cohort design; (4) Studies providing data for dose-response analysis, including quantitative data for DII, number of cases, total number of participants, or person-years of exposure. Additionally, studies were excluded if they (1) could not provide relevant data; (2) were reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, non-human studies, and congress abstracts; (3) were clinical trials; (4) were not in English.

Literature screening was performed with EndNote 20. During this process, two authors (Y.W. and W.W.) independently screened the literature and compared their results by reviewing titles, abstracts, and full texts against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved by discussion between two authors. If consensus was not reached, a third author (Y.W.) was consulted for arbitration.

Data extraction

The following data were independently extracted from the included articles by two authors (Y.W. and W.W.) using a pre-designed data extraction form: (1) first author’s name; (2) year of publication; (3) country or region; (4) study type; (5) sample size; (6) DII measurement and assessment; (7) participants mean age; (8) Male/Female ratio; (9) study duration; (10) DII Categories; (11) Adjustment for covariates; (12) HR, RR, or OR with 95% confidence interval (CI) from the most adjusted model. If the authors dose not report the OR value of the total population but reports the research results of men and women separately, we will consider them as two separate studies. Disagreements were resolved by discussion between two authors (Y.W. and W.W.), with a third author (Y.W.) serving as arbiter when needed.

Quality assessment

All included studies were assessed independently by two authors (Y.W. and W.W.) using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). For case-control studies and cohort studies, the NOS evaluates studies based on three domains: selection, comparability, and outcome. Any discrepancies in the risk of bias judgments were resolved through discussion, with a third author (Y.W.) consulted for arbitration.

Statistical methods

Regardless of whether included articles reported RR, HR, or OR, we used OR as the effect size to calculate the natural logarithm (log OR) and standard error (SE). I2 statistics and the Cochran Q test were used to assess heterogeneity. Significant inter-study heterogeneity was indicated when I2 > 50% or the P-value from the Q test < 0.1. The random-effects model was employed to combine the results when significant heterogeneity was present. Otherwise, the fixed-effects model was applied. To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, we performed subgroup analyses based on study type, region, DII measurement, study quality, number of cases, and adjustment factors.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess each study’s potential impact on the pooled results by omitting them individually. We assessed publication bias using funnel plot analysis, Egger’s test, and Begg’s test of the combined results. Funnel plot asymmetry was quantified using Egger’s and Begg’s tests. Publication bias was indicated if the p-value for Egger’s or Begg’s test was less than 0.05. The trim-and-fill method was conducted to adjust for publication bias. If no significant change occurred in the results before and after the trim-and-fill method, this indicated that publication bias had minimal effect, and the results were relatively robust.

Due to insufficient data in the included CRA studies, dose-response analysis between CRA and DII could not be conducted. Therefore, we focused solely on the potential linear or non-linear associations between DII and CRC risk. Studies were required to include DII dose groups with at least three categories. Each dose group needed to include the overall sample size, number of cases, OR with 95% CI, and median DII value. Some studies did not directly report median DII values for each category. For closed DII interval, the midpoint of the upper and lower boundaries was taken as the exposure dose. For open minimum dose groups, it was assumed that the starting point was zero, and the median of this interval was taken as the exposure dose. For open maximum dose groups, the exposure dose was calculated as the median of the previous category DII minus its starting value plus the starting value of the maximum dose group [20]. The non-linear relationship between DII and CRC incidence risk was modeled using a restricted cubic spline with knots fixed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the DII distribution. The P-value for nonlinearity was determined by testing the null hypothesis that the second spline coefficient was zero [21]. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1 (the ‘meta’, ‘dosresmeta’, and ‘arms’ packages).

Results

Literature search and study characteristics

A total of 2382 studies were identified across the PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane databases, with one additional study identified through manual searching. We excluded 585 duplicate articles. An additional 1206 articles were excluded after initial screening of titles and abstracts as they were deemed irrelevant to our research question. Next, we excluded 499 studies that did not meet our inclusion criteria: meta-analyses (n = 48), reviews (n = 34), conference abstracts (n = 6), animal experiments (n = 405), and case reports (n = 6). We excluded an additional 71 articles for the following reasons: study subjects were not relevant to our research question (n = 9), study indicators were not DII (n = 16), insufficient relevant data (n = 40), results did not meet the inclusion criteria (n = 2), and full text unavailable (n = 4). Finally, 22 articles were included in this meta-analysis, with the detailed screening flow diagram presented in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of the search and selection process

All included studies were published between 2014 and 2023, of which 6 were cohort studies and 16 were case-control studies. A total of 929,563 participants were enrolled in the study, including 25,359 CRC patients and 7,612 CRA patients.

Six included studies were from Asia, nine from North America, one from South America, three from Europe, and three from the Middle East. Almost all studies collected dietary information via food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) to calculate DII. All studies adjusted for confounding factors such as age. The overall quality of the included studies was moderately high (mean ± SD = 7.95 ± 0.72). No studies had quality assessment scores below 6 (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Meta-analysis of DII and CRA

A total of 8 studies investigated the relationship between DII and CRA risk, involving 7,612 CRA cases and 110,668 controls. When comparing the highest DII to the lowest DII, a non-significant positive association was observed between high DII and CRA risk, with high heterogeneity (OR = 1.19, 95% CI [0.96,1.47], P = 0.11, I2 = 81%). The pooled OR for CRA risk per unit increase in DII was 1.19 (95% CI [1.03,1.38], P = 0.02), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 57%). (Figs. 2 and 3).

Meta-analysis of DII and CRC

Eighteen studies investigated the association between DII and CRC, involving 25,359 CRC patients and 847,584 controls. For the association of the highest DII compared with lowest DII, a total of 16 studies reported the overall (both-sex) OR value, 10 studies provided female-specific estimates, and 8 studies provided male-specific estimates. Overall, participants in the highest DII category had a 61% increased CRC risk compared to those in the lowest DII category, with high heterogeneity in the combined results (OR = 1.61, 95% CI [1.42,1.83], P < 0.0001, I2 = 78%). After stratification by sex, the association was stronger in man (OR = 1.67, 95% CI [1.39,2.00], P < 0.0001, I2 = 84.9%), whereas women also exhibited a significant and modest increase in risk (OR = 1.21, 95% CI [1.14,1.29], P < 0.0001, I2 = 47.5%). (Fig. 4) As shown in Fig. 5, for each unit increase in DII, a total of 10, 4 and 5 studies provided the overall, male-speific and female-specific estimates, respectively. The pooled OR for CRC risk per unit increment in DII was 1.14 (95% CI [1.08, 1.20], P < 0.0001), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 86%). After sex stratification, the relationship was more pronounced in men (OR = 1.16, 95% CI [1.06,1.26], P = 0.0011, I2 = 94%), whereas women exhibited a significant but modest increase in risk (OR = 1.06, 95% CI [1.02,1,10], P = 0.0039, I2 = 60%).

Dose-response analysis between DII and CRC

Fifteen studies providing DII cutoff values were included in the dose-response analysis. We analyzed the dose-response relationship between DII and CRC risk using restricted cubic splines. The results showed a non-linear association between DII and CRC risk (P fornon−linearity < 0.0001). When DII exceeded 0.15, CRC risk increased non-linearly with increasing DII, as shown in Fig. 6.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

In the sensitivity analysis of high DII vs. low DII for CRA, we excluded the study by Nor Hamizah Shafiee et al. (OR = 1.27, 95% CI [1.04,1.55], P = 0.0175, I2 = 77%) and the study by Zhuyue Li et al. (OR = 1.26, 95% CI [1.01,1.56], P = 0.0413, I2 = 78%), which significantly affected the combined results. In the sensitivity analysis of continuous DII for CRA, the combined results shifted from a significant positive association to non-significance after excluding the study by Zhuo-Jie He et al. (OR = 1.16, 95% CI [0.96,1.41], P = 0.1165, I2 = 72%) and the study by Pegah Rafiee et al. (OR = 1.14, 95% CI [0.95,1.36], P = 0.1504, I2 = 51%). However, in the sensitivity analysis for CRC, excluding individual studies did not significantly change the combined results, indicating stability of the findings. (Supplementary Table 4, Supplementary Table 5, Supplementary Tables 6, and Supplementary Table 7)

We used funnel plots, Egger’s test, and Begg’s test to assess for publication bias. The results of Egger’s tests (P = 0.0026) and Begg’s tests (P = 0.0090) indicated a significant publication bias in the association between high DII and CRC. The trim-and-fill method was employed to impute seven virtual study data, with unchanged combined results (OR = 1.36, 95% CI [1.14, 1.63], z = 3.35, P = 0.0008). Significant publication bias was also observed in the analysis of continuous DII for CRC (P for Egger’s = 0.0071). Similarly, after incorporating five virtual studies using the trim-and-fill method, the pooled results remained significantly positively correlated (OR = 1.07, 95% CI [1.02, 1.13], z = 2.67, P = 0.0075). Additionally, no significant publication bias was observed in the analysis of CRA (P for Egger’s=0.8962; P for Begg’s=0.9015). Funnel plots are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Subgroup analysis

Owing to the high heterogeneity in the combined results, we performed subgroup analyses based on study type, region, DII measurement, study quality, number of cases, and adjustment factors to explore potential heterogeneity sources. Results are presented in Tables 3, 4 and 5. We found a significant positive association between high DII and CRC risk in regional subgroup analyses, with lower heterogeneity, such as European (I2 = 28%, P < 0.0001), North American (I2 = 56%, P < 0.0001), and Middle Eastern groups (I2 = 54%, P = 0.0001). Additionally, in the subgroup without adjustment for smoking, the heterogeneity of the combined results was zero (I2 = 0.0%, P < 0.0001). In the North American (OR = 1.05, 95% CI [1.01,1.10], P = 0.0254, I2 = 35%), European (OR = 1.13, 95% CI [1.10,1.16], P < 0.0001, I2 = 4%), and Middle Eastern groups (OR = 1.64, 95% CI [1.41,1.91], P < 0.0001, I2 = 5%), we observed a significant positive association between each unit increase in DII and CRC prevalence, with low heterogeneity. In subgroups adjusted for total calorie intake (OR = 1.12, 95% CI [1.08, 1.16], P < 0.0001, I2 = 2%]) and not adjusted for physical activity (OR = 1.05, 95% CI [1.02, 1.08], P = 0.0003, I2 = 43%]), we found a positive association between each unit increase in DII and CRC prevalence, accompanied by a significant reduction in heterogeneity. In subgroup analyses without adjusted for BMI (OR = 1.36, 95% CI [0.88,2.09], P = 0.1634, I2 = 94%), we observed a shift from a significant positive association to non-significance in the pooled effect size. In subgroup analyses adjusted for alcohol consumption factors, we observed lower heterogeneity (I2 = 48%, P < 0.0001). In subgroup analyses adjusted for ethnicity variables, high DII was significantly positively associated with CRA prevalence (OR = 1.28, 95% CI [1.02,1.59], P = 0.03, I2 = 67%). The above results suggest that region, and adjustments for smoking, BMI, alcohol consumption, ethnic factors, total calorie intake, and physical activity may be potential sources of heterogeneity.

Discussion

Major findings

In our meta-analysis, compared to the lowest DII level, CRC risk increased by 61% in the highest DII level, with a 14% increase in CRC risk per unit increase in DII. The results after sex stratification indicated that the inflammatory dietary pattern was associated with elevated colorectal cancer risk in both sexes, with men appearing to be more susceptible. Although we observed a positive trend between DII and CRA risk when comparing the highest DII to the lowest DII levels, this association was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Nonetheless, each unit increase in DII was associated with a 19% increase in CRA risk. Zhang et al. [18] and Soffian et al. [19] conducted meta-analyses on the relationship between CRC risk and DII. They found that the highest DII level was associated with a 43% and 16% higher CRC risk compared to the lowest DII levels. Additionally, for each unit increment in DII, the CRC risk increased by 8% and 34%, respectively. Our results are consistent with their findings, indicating that rising DII increases CRC risk. Both our findings and those of Zhang et al. indicated a non-linear tendency supporting a positive correlation between DII and colorectal cancer risk.

Most subgroup analysis results were consistent with the combined results. However, we found that the Middle Eastern subgroup exhibited a marked increase in CRC risk. We also observed varying degrees of heterogeneity reduction across regional subgroups. Different regions may have different dietary habits, lifestyles, environmental factors, or genetic backgrounds, which may influence the relationship between DII and CRC. Regional differences may thus be an important source of the observed heterogeneity in our study. Additionally, subgroup analyses adjusted for confounders revealed changes in the pooled results and subgroup heterogeneity, including smoking history, drinking history, race, BMI, total calorie intake, and physical activity. Obesity is a well-established risk factor for CRC and CRA [22–24]. A meta-analysis showed that a high early-age BMI increased CRC risk by 39% in adult males and 19% in adult females. Moreover, a high BMI increased the risk of colorectal serrated adenoma by 40% [24, 25]. Studies have demonstrated a link between physical activity and colon cancer risk [26, 27]. A recent cross-sectional study revealed that individuals with low physical activity levels had a significant 22.4% increased colon cancer risk, whereas those with high physical activity levels experienced a 53% reduction in risk [28]. Caloric restriction has beneficial effects in delaying aging and slowing disease progression, particularly in cancer prevention [29]. Caloric restriction was found to alter specific gut microbiota and components of gut metabolites in CRC mice, and to inhibit the growth of CRC cells [30, 31]. Both alcohol and tobacco usage are related to a significant risk of CRC [32]. Alcohol and tobacco appear to be more associated with colorectal polyps than colorectal cancer [33]. Given the well-documented roles of these factors in modulating the risk of CRC and CRA, the observed changes in the combined outcome and heterogeneity of subgroups may be explained.

Potential mechanism

The precise mechanisms through which high DII dietary patterns increase the risk of colorectal cancer and colorectal adenomas are not yet fully understood. The DII score reflects the pro-inflammatory potential of a diet. Thus, it is hypothesized that the pro-inflammatory diets may accelerate the progression of CRA and CRC by inducing chronic inflammation within the host.

It has been established that the heme in red meat, as well as heterocyclic amines produced during high-temperature cooking of processed meat, can induce inflammatory responses, alter the intestinal microenvironment, and promote carcinogenesis [34, 35]. In a DSS-induced inflammation mouse model, mice supplemented with dietary heme exhibited higher levels of intestinal inflammation markers (Lcn2 and IL-6), more severe intestinal inflammation, and more intestinal adenomas [36]. Dietary heme supplementation has been shown to stimulate the infiltration of myeloid cells, particularly granulocytes and monocytes, into the intestinal epithelium of mice, thereby increasing the number of COX-2 positive cells [37]. COX-2-derived prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) signaling prompts macrophages to recruit pro-inflammatory leukocytes to the inflammatory site. This process exacerbates colonic inflammation and promotes the growth of inflammation-associated cancers [38].

Meat-based diets are often accompanied by high fat and protein intake. A high-fat diet augments the liver’s bile acid production. Primary bile acids enter the gut via bile and are converted into secondary bile acids by the gut microbiota, including deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA) [39]. Elevated secondary bile acids levels induce localized gut inflammatory and excessive colonic mucosal proliferation. Moreover, secondary bile acids contribute to colorectal carcinogenesis by inducing oxidative stress and DNA damage [40, 41]. However, the association between dietary fat and CRC risk also depends on the type of fatty acid. Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) and certain omega-6 PUFAs, such as α-linolenic acid, eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), and linoleic acid, possess anti-inflammatory properties and may serve as protective agents against CRC [42–44].

The mechanisms through which dietary fiber protects against CRC and CRA are complex. Insoluble dietary fiber increases fecal volume and shortens transit time, thereby minimizing carcinogen exposure to colonocytes [45]. Soluble fibers are fermented by gut microbiota to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as butyric acid, acetic acid, and propionic acid, which demonstrate anti-inflammatory and anti-CRC effects [46, 47]. Additionally, dietary fiber can bind to secondary bile acids in the intestinal lumen, lower the fecal PH, and counteract the carcinogenic effects of toxic bile acids [48].

In recent years, an increasing number of studies have found that gut bacteria may play an important role in the development of CRA and CRC [49]. Diet is a key determinant in shaping the structure of the gut microbiota. A Westernized dietary pattern increases the abundance of CRC-associated pathogenic bacteria, such as Escherichia/Shigella, Enterococcus, and Streptococcus. Conversely, it reduces the abundance of beneficial bacteria, such as Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Blautia, Akkermansia, Ruminococcus, and Faecalibacterium [50–52]. Gut microbiota dysbiosis damages the intestinal barrier, increasing permeability and promoting flora translocation [53]. Imbalances in the symbiotic relationship between gut microbiota and the mucosal immune system trigger inflammatory responses via multiple signaling pathways, increasing pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, and IL-23 [54]. These cytokines activate the STAT3 and NF-κB signaling pathways, leading to reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, oxidative stress, and DNA damage, which ultimately contribute to the formation of CRA and CRC [55].

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, this is the first meta-analysis to assess the relationship between the DII and CRA risk. Second, we have updated the search period, increased the number of articles included, re-conducted the dose-response meta-analysis between DII and CRC risk, and further stratified the association between DII and CRC risk by sex, thereby enhancing the comprehensiveness of our findings. Additionally, the inclusion of studies from diverse regions improves the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, the large sample size in the present study increases the statistical power and reliability of our results. Finally, we conduct subgroup analyses to explore potential heterogeneity sources, providing valuable insights into how different factors might influence the observed associations. However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, most of the studies we included are case-control studies. Due to selection and recall bias, the results may be less accurate than those from cohort studies. Second, the DII derived from the included studies does not represent the long-term dietary status of participants. The DII in this meta-analysis is primarily derived from self-reported dietary frequency questionnaires. Given that recall bias and misclassification of dietary exposure factors are inevitable, this evaluation method may obscure the true relationship between DII, CRA, and CRC. Third, the meta-analysis reveals high heterogeneity, potentially explained by geographic location and confounding factor correction. Although all included studies adjusted for multiple confounders, residual confounding could still have affected the limitations of results.

Conclusion

Overall, our meta-analysis suggests that DII may be a novel risk factor for CRA and CRC independent of traditional risk factors. However, due to the limitations of this study, it is hoped that additional prospective studies with stronger designs and better control of confounding factors will become available to further support our findings.
