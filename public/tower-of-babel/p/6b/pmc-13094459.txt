Oridonin‐Loaded PDA@Gel@GO Nanocapsules Modulate NLRP3 and Epithelial Repair in Colitis
Authors: Wenbo Yuan, Qirui Zhao, Jing Hua, Lingyao Xu, Yufan Wang, Tongtong Xu, Zuchao Du, Liping Wu, Daxiang Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUlcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease marked by sustained mucosal inflammation and epithelial barrier dysfunction, closely associated with aberrant NOD‐like receptor family pyrin domain‐containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation. Targeted oral therapies that simultaneously suppress inflammation and promote mucosal repair remain limited. We developed an orally administrable nanomicrocapsule system (PDA@Gel@GO) for colon‐targeted delivery of oridonin, a natural diterpenoid with anti‐inflammatory activity. The system was constructed by encapsulating galactosylated carboxymethyl chitosan–based oridonin nanomicelles (GO) within gelatin microspheres, followed by polydopamine coating to enhance gastrointestinal stability and mucosal adhesion. In a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)‐induced UC mouse model, PDA@Gel@GO exhibited preferential accumulation in inflamed colonic tissues and significantly improved body weight loss, disease activity index, and colon shortening. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that PDA@Gel@GO treatment suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome activation, as evidenced by reduced expression of NLRP3, ASC, caspase‐1, and IL‐1β, accompanied by attenuation of oxidative stress and restoration of intestinal barrier proteins. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed activation of gene programs associated with epithelial regeneration, cell cycle progression, and DNA repair, suggesting coordinated inflammation resolution and mucosal repair. Collectively, this study presents a macrophage‐targeted, multilayered nanomicrocapsule platform that enhances the therapeutic efficacy of oridonin through precise colon targeting and integrated anti‐inflammatory and tissue‐protective effects, providing a promising strategy for precision treatment of UC.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic immune‐mediated condition marked by relapsing intestinal inflammation, mainly including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD) [1]. With ongoing industrialization and lifestyle changes, the global incidence of IBD has increased steadily, imposing a growing public health burden [2, 3]. Current clinical management relies mainly on glucocorticoids, 5‐aminosalicylic acid–based drugs, and biologic agents. Although these therapies can relieve symptoms and slow disease progression, their long‐term use is frequently accompanied by serious systemic side effects, such as immunosuppression, increased susceptibility to infection, and hepatic or renal toxicity [4, 5]. In addition, conventional oral formulations frequently fail to overcome gastrointestinal barriers or achieve sufficient drug accumulation at inflamed intestinal sites, necessitating high systemic doses that further increase patient burden [6, 7]. Consequently, the development of novel oral nanodelivery systems with high targeting specificity, low toxicity, and responsiveness to the pathological intestinal microenvironment has emerged as a promising strategy for IBD treatment [8].

In the complex pathogenesis of IBD, dysregulated activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome is a key driver of intestinal inflammation [9, 10]. NLRP3 activation triggers caspase‐1 cleavage, resulting in the maturation and release of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL‐1β and IL‐18, thereby amplifying local inflammatory responses [11, 12]. Increasing evidence suggests that persistent NLRP3 activation not only aggravates intestinal inflammation but also promotes epithelial cell apoptosis and compromises the mucosal barrier, forming a vicious cycle of inflammation, barrier disruption, and disease recurrence [13, 14, 15]. Given its elevated expression in IBD lesions and close correlation with disease severity, targeting NLRP3 inflammasome activation is considered a promising therapeutic strategy for IBD management [16]. Oridonin, a natural diterpenoid compound, exhibits potent anti‐inflammatory activity and has been shown to ameliorate tissue inflammation through inhibition of the NLRP3 pathway [17]. However, its poor aqueous solubility and low bioavailability substantially limit its clinical application [18], highlighting the need for nanocarrier‐based delivery strategies to improve therapeutic efficacy.

In response to the limitations of conventional therapies, a range of advanced nanodelivery strategies has emerged in recent years. Biohybrid microrobots with autonomous motility, such as algae–macrophage hybrid systems, have demonstrated prolonged intestinal retention and effective neutralization of inflammatory cytokines [19]. Tungsten–zinc–based nanoparticles capable of modulating cellular functions have also been reported to restore barrier homeostasis by remodeling the mucus layer and regulating Paneth cell activity [20]. Moreover, emerging biomimetic nanoscavengers and intelligent responsive materials are designed to eliminate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and cell‐free DNA or to enhance mucosal adhesion in response to the intestinal microenvironment, thereby interrupting inflammatory feed‐forward loops [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Despite these advances, the development of an oral delivery system that is simple to fabricate, scalable, and capable of integrating active targeting with effective mucosal repair remains a significant challenge.

Although graphene oxide (GO)–based carriers possess distinct advantages, including high surface area and efficient drug‐loading capacity [26, 27], the present study seeks to recapitulate these delivery efficiencies using polymeric materials with superior biocompatibility. To this end, a ligand‐mediated active targeting strategy was employed. While the asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR) has been widely exploited for hepatic targeting, increasing evidence indicates that macrophage galactose‐type lectin (MGL/CD301) is selectively overexpressed on macrophages infiltrating inflamed colonic tissues during colitis [28]. By leveraging this pathological feature, galactose modification enables avoidance of hepatic uptake and facilitates targeted drug accumulation in colonic macrophages [29, 30]. Based on this rationale, galactosylated carboxymethyl chitosan (Gal‐CMCS) was synthesized and used to construct polymeric micelles encapsulating oridonin (hereafter termed “GO,” referring specifically to Gal‐CMCS/oridonin micelles rather than graphene oxide).

To further enhance oral stability, GO micelles were encapsulated within gelatin microspheres and subsequently coated with polydopamine (PDA). This design leverages the biodegradability of gelatin to facilitate colon‐specific release, while the PDA coating enhances acid resistance and mucosal adhesion of the microcapsules [31, 32]. The resulting PDA@Gel@GO nanomicrocapsule system integrates the high drug‐loading capacity of polymeric micelles, galactose‐mediated active targeting, and the protective functions of the PDA/gelatin shell. Collectively, this multifunctional platform is intended to enable colon‐targeted delivery of oridonin, thereby synergistically inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation and promoting mucosal regeneration, offering a promising strategy for precision oral therapy in IBD.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis of Gal‐N‐CMCS Polymer

The Gal‐N‐CMCS polymer was synthesized via a two‐step EDC/NHS coupling reaction. Initially, 2 g of carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS, deacetylation degree ≥ 90%, Aladdin, China) was added, followed by equimolar amounts of 1‐(3‐dimethylaminopropyl)‐3‐ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC; A11708, Aladdin, China) and N‐hydroxysuccinimide (NHS; A11360, Aladdin, China). The reaction mixture was magnetically stirred at ambient temperature for 12 h, followed by dialysis (MWCO 3500 Da, Solarbio, China) for 3 days and subsequent freeze‐drying to obtain the Gal‐N‐CMCS polymer.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy Analysis (FTIR)

The chemical structures of CMCS and Gal‐N‐CMCS were analyzed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Samples were prepared using the KBr pellet method, and spectra were collected over a wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1 with 32 scans at a resolution of 4 cm−1.

Determination of Critical Micelle Concentration (CMC)

The CMC of Gal‐N‐CMCS and CMCS‐N was determined using the pyrene probe fluorescence method. Pyrene (P3685, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) was added to polymer solutions at varying concentrations to achieve a final concentration of 5 × 10−7 M. After incubating in the dark for 12 h, the I3/I1 ratio was recorded at an excitation wavelength of 370 nm using a fluorescence spectrophotometer, and CMC values were calculated.

Preparation of GO Nanomicelles

GO nanomicelles were prepared by a self‐assembly method. Briefly, 20 mg of Gal‐N‐CMCS was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water, followed by the addition of 2 mg of oridonin dissolved in ethanol (2 mg/mL). The mixture was emulsified using an ultrasonic cell disruptor (JY92‐IIDN, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology, China) at a power of 100 W, employing 10‐s sonication pulses with 5‐s intervals for a total duration of 15 min. The resulting emulsion was then stirred at ambient temperature for 3 h. Ethanol was removed by rotary evaporation, and the solution was dialyzed to remove free oridonin. Finally, the product was freeze‐dried to obtain GO nanomicelles. The average preparation yield of the GO nanomicelles was 88.5% ± 2.3%.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Gal‐CMCS/GO nanomicelles were diluted to 0.1 mg/mL, deposited onto carbon‐coated copper grids, air‐dried, and imaged using a TEM (JEM‐2100, JEOL, Japan) operated at 200 kV.

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) Analysis

The particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of GO nanomicelles were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Panalytical, UK) at a concentration of 0.2 mg/mL and 25°C. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as mean values.

Analysis of Drug Loading (DL%) and Encapsulation Efficiency (EE%)

The oridonin content in GO nanomicelles was quantified using ultra‐performance liquid chromatography (ACQUITY UPLC, Waters, USA). Chromatographic separation was performed on a C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) with a mobile phase of methanol/water (65:35, v/v) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Detection was carried out at 254 nm. EE% and DL% were determined after disrupting the micellar structure to release encapsulated oridonin. The encapsulation efficiency of oridonin in GO nanomicelles was 85.7%, with a drug loading content of 15.2%.

In Vitro Drug Release Experiment

The in vitro release profile of Gal‐CMCS/GO nanomicelles was evaluated in buffer solutions at pH 1.2, 6.8, and 7.4 to simulate gastric, intestinal, and colonic environments, respectively. Briefly, 1 mL of nanomicelle suspension containing 1 mg of oridonin was loaded into a dialysis bag (MWCO 3500 Da) and immersed in 50 mL of phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) at the corresponding pH. The system was maintained at 37°C in a shaking water bath with gentle agitation. At predetermined intervals, aliquots were withdrawn and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS. The released oridonin was quantified by UPLC.

Isolation and Culture of Bone Marrow–Derived Macrophages (BMDMs)

Eight‐week‐old C57BL/6 mice (strain code: 201; 20–22 g) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. Following euthanasia, femurs and tibias were aseptically collected, and bone marrow cells were flushed from the cavities with sterile PBS using a 25‐gauge needle. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. Red blood cells were removed by brief incubation with ACK lysis buffer, followed by centrifugation. After cell counting, cells were seeded at a density of 1.0–1.5 × 106 cells per 10 cm culture dish in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, USA), and 25 ng/mL recombinant mouse macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (M‐CSF; BioLegend, USA). Cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Half of the medium was replaced on days 3 and 5, with M‐CSF replenished to the original concentration. After 6 days, adherent cells displaying typical macrophage morphology were considered fully differentiated BMDMs.

Cytotoxicity Assay (MTT)

Caco‐2 cells (ATCC HTB‐37) and BMDMs were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, USA) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator (Heracell VIOS 160i, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cells were seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Cells were then treated with free oridonin, GO nanomicelles, or blank micelles and incubated for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, 20 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL; M5655, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) was added to each well, followed by incubation for 4 h. The culture medium was removed, and the resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 150 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Absorbance was recorded at 570 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD5, Molecular Devices, USA).

Observation of GO Uptake via Confocal Microscopy

BMDMs were seeded onto glass coverslips and stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 1 μg/mL) for 6 h. Subsequently, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)‐labeled GO nanomicelles were added and incubated for 4 h. FITC labeling was performed with FITC NHS ester (F6434, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). The cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; P6148, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) for 10 min. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI, 1 μg/mL) for 10 min in the dark. The uptake of micelles by the cells was then observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM980, ZEISS, Germany).

Preparation of GO‐Loaded Gelatin Nanocapsules

GO nanomicelles containing oridonin (1 mg/mL) were homogeneously mixed with a gelatin solution (10 mg/mL; Type A, G2625, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) at 37°C. The mixture was then added dropwise into 5 mL of a 2% (w/v) sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP; P7185, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) solution under continuous magnetic stirring. Ionic crosslinking was allowed to proceed for 30 min, resulting in the formation of gelatin nanocapsules encapsulating GO nanomicelles (denoted as Gel@GO). The nanocapsules were collected by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min and washed three times with PBS to remove residual reagents prior to further coating.

PDA Coating Encapsulation of Nanocapsules

Disperse the Gel@GO nanocapsules in 10 mL of 10 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 8.5, Sangon Biotech, China), and add dopamine hydrochloride at a concentration of 2 mg/mL (H8502, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). Stir the mixture under light‐protected conditions at ambient temperature for 6 h to complete the self‐polymerization coating process. The resulting PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules were collected by centrifugation (5000 rpm, 10 min), washed three times with deionized water, and freeze‐dried for storage. The average yield of this step was 92.1% ± 1.8%.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

Lyophilized nanocapsules were mounted on conductive adhesive tape and sputter‐coated with a thin layer of gold for 60 s. Surface morphology was examined using a scanning electron microscope (GeminiSEM 500, ZEISS, Germany) operated at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV, with images acquired at magnifications ranging from ×1000 to ×5000.

Particle Size Measurement

PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules were dispersed in deionized water at 0.1 mg/mL and briefly sonicated for 10 s. Particle size distribution and uniformity were determined using a laser particle size analyzer (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern Panalytical, UK). Measurements were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as mean values.

Simulated Gastrointestinal Degradation Experiment

Gel@GO and PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules were placed in four different solutions to simulate morphological changes during the oral delivery pathway. In each experimental group, the concentration of nanocapsules was maintained at 1 mg/mL. The treatment conditions were as follows: deionized water (30 min), artificial gastric fluid (AGF, pH 1.0, containing 0.2% NaCl and 0.32% pepsin, Solarbio, China, 2 h), artificial small intestinal fluid (ASF, pH 6.8, containing 0.5% bile salts and pancreatin, Solarbio, China, 4 h), and artificial colonic fluid (ACF, pH 7.8, containing 3% polysaccharidase, Solarbio, China, 30 min). At the end of each digestion stage, nanocapsule samples were gently rinsed with deionized water. One portion was collected for morphological analysis using optical microscopy (DM750, Leica Microsystems, Germany) and SEM. Another portion was subjected to demulsification, and the residual oridonin content was quantified by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to determine drug retention (%). In parallel, changes in the average particle size of the nanocapsules at each digestion stage were examined using a laser particle size analyzer (Mastersizer 3000) to quantitatively assess structural integrity and stability. Additionally, variations in zeta potential under different simulated digestive conditions were monitored using a Zetasizer (Malvern Panalytical, UK) to assess the influence of surface charge on nanocapsule stability. The physicochemical properties and drug retention data obtained at each digestion stage are summarized in Table S2.

Ex Vivo Organ Distribution and Tissue Fluorescence Imaging

To evaluate tissue distribution and targeting specificity, DSS‐induced colitis mice were euthanized 12 h after oral administration of Cy5.5‐labeled PDA@Gel@GO microcapsules. Major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and colon, were rapidly harvested and rinsed with normal saline, followed by ex vivo fluorescence imaging using an IVIS Spectrum small‐animal imaging system. Images were acquired at an excitation wavelength of 640 nm and an emission wavelength of 710 nm. Quantitative analysis of average radiant efficiency within regions of interest (ROIs) was conducted using Living Image software (version 4.5) to assess microcapsule accumulation in colonic lesions and off‐target organs.

Biosafety Analysis

Eight‐week‐old C57BL/6 mice (20–22 g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. Mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 5 per group): Healthy, GO, and PDA@Gel@GO. Treatments were administered once daily by oral gavage for seven consecutive days. At the end of the treatment period, mice were euthanized, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were harvested. Tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm thickness, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E; G1120, Solarbio, China). Histological evaluation was performed using a light microscope (Olympus BX53, Japan).

Following the final administration, blood samples were collected from the orbital sinus into EDTA‐anticoagulated tubes for complete blood count analysis. Red blood cell (RBC), white blood cell (WBC), hemoglobin (HGB), and platelet (PLT) levels were measured using a fully automated hematology analyzer (BC‐5000Vet, Mindray, China). In parallel, serum was isolated for biochemical analysis of liver function markers (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] and aspartate aminotransferase [AST]) and kidney function markers (blood urea nitrogen [BUN] and creatinine [CRE]) using an automated biochemical analyzer (AU5800, Beckman Coulter, USA).

In Vivo Pharmacokinetic Study

Healthy C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old, 20–22 g) were fasted for 12 h with free access to water prior to the experiment. Mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6 per group) and orally administered either free oridonin or PDA@Gel@GO at an equivalent oridonin dose of 10 mg/kg. At predetermined time points (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h) post‐administration, approximately 200 μL of blood was collected from the retro‐orbital plexus into heparinized tubes. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min and stored at −80°C until analysis. For sample preparation, 50 μL of plasma was mixed with 150 μL of acetonitrile containing ponicidin as an internal standard to precipitate proteins. After vortexing and centrifugation (12 000 rpm, 10 min), the supernatant was evaporated under nitrogen, reconstituted in 50% methanol, centrifuged again, and subjected to liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Oridonin concentrations were quantified using an LC–MS/MS system (Shimadzu 8050) equipped with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm). Gradient elution was performed using 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, with the column temperature maintained at 40°C. Detection was conducted in positive electrospray ionization mode using multiple reaction monitoring, with transitions of m/z 365.2 → 347.2 for oridonin and m/z 363.3 → 345.2 for the internal standard. Pharmacokinetic parameters, including C_max, T_max, and AUC, were calculated using DAS 3.0 software based on a non‐compartmental analysis. Plasma concentrations below the lower limit of quantification (2.0 ng/mL) were recorded as not detected.

RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from collected cells or tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and its concentration was determined with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript RT kit (Takara, Japan), while qPCR was conducted with SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara) on an ABI 7500 instrument (Applied Biosystems, USA). Primer sequences and amplification conditions are detailed in Table S1.

Western Blot (WB) Analysis

Cells or tissues were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, China) containing protease inhibitors, followed by ultrasonic disruption and centrifugation at 12 000 × g for 10 min to obtain the supernatant. Protein concentrations were measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Pierce, USA). Equal amounts of protein (20 μg per lane) were resolved on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk (BD Biosciences, USA) for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against NLRP3 (Abcam, ab263899, UK), ASC (Abcam, ab180799, UK), Caspase‐1 (Abcam, ab207802, UK), IL‐1β (Abcam, ab254360, UK), and β‐actin (Cell Signaling, #8457, USA), each diluted 1:1000. After washing, membranes were incubated at ambient temperature for 1 h with an horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Abcam, ab205718, 1:3000). Immunoreactive bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Thermo Fisher, USA) and imaged with a ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio‐Rad, USA), followed by densitometric analysis using ImageJ software.

ROS Fluorescence Imaging

Cells were seeded into 24‐well plates at a density of 4 × 105 cells/mL and treated as indicated. Subsequently, cells were incubated with 10 μm 2′,7′‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH‐DA; Beyotime, China) at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS. Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Axio Observer, ZEISS, Germany) with excitation at 488 nm. Five random fields per group were selected, and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Analysis of Malondialdehyde (MDA), Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), and Glutathione Peroxidase (GSH‐Px)

Cells were collected, homogenized, and centrifuged, and the supernatants were used for biochemical analysis. Commercial assay kits for MDA, SOD, and GSH‐Px were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (China). Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm for MDA and 560 nm for SOD and GSH‐Px using a microplate reader. Three parallel samples were analyzed for each group.

Immunofluorescence (IF) Assay

Cells were seeded onto coverslips and, following treatment, fixed with 4% formaldehyde (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) for 15 min. Permeabilization was conducted using 0.1% Triton X‐100 (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 10 min. Blocking was performed with 5% BSA (Gibco, USA) for 1 h. Primary antibodies, Anti‐NLRP3 (Abcam, ab263899, 1:200) and Anti‐ASC (Abcam, ab180799, 1:200), were incubated overnight at 4°C. The next day, after three washes with PBS, secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor488 goat anti‐rabbit IgG (Abcam, ab150077, 1:500) and Alexa Fluor594 goat anti‐mouse IgG (Abcam, ab150116, 1:500) were incubated for 1 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Cell Signaling, #8961, 1:1000) for 5 min. After washing with PBS, imaging was performed using a confocal microscope (Leica STELLARIS 5, Germany), with three random fields captured per group.

For mouse tissue specimens, colonic tissues fixed in a 10% neutral formaldehyde solution underwent routine dehydration and paraffin embedding before sectioning into slices of 5 μm thickness for IF staining on paraffin sections. Sections were baked at 60°C in an oven for 2 h, followed by deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through graded ethanol. Antigen retrieval was achieved via microwave heating in a citrate buffer solution (0.01 mol/L, pH 6.0). Permeabilization was then carried out using 0.3% Triton X‐100 in PBS for 10 min and blocked with 1% BSA for 60 min. Anti‐NLRP3 (ab263899, 1:200, Abcam) and Anti‐ASC (ab180799, 1:200, Abcam) antibodies were incubated overnight at 4°C. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor488 Goat Anti‐Rabbit IgG (ab150077, 1:500, Abcam) and Alexa Fluor594 Goat Anti‐Mouse IgG (ab150116, 1:500, Abcam) at ambient temperature in the dark for 1 h. DAPI (#8961, Cell Signaling Technology; dilution of 1:1000) was used to stain the nuclei for 5 min. After washing and mounting, images were acquired using a confocal microscope (STELLARIS 5; Leica Microsystems, Germany).

To assess cellular localization of the drug, frozen colon sections collected 12 h after administration were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked, followed by incubation with a macrophage‐specific antibody against F4/80. Sections were then incubated with a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Colocalization of Cy5.5 (red) and F4/80 (green) fluorescence signals was examined using confocal microscopy.

Establishment and Grouping of a Mouse Dextran Sulfate Sodium (DSS)‐Induced UC Model

Eight‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mice (20–22 g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. and acclimatized for one week under standard conditions with ad libitum access to food and water. Experimental colitis was induced by administering 3% (w/v) dextran sulfate sodium (DSS; MW 36 000–50 000, MP Biomedicals, USA) in drinking water for 7 consecutive days. Mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 6 per group): Control (normal drinking water), DSS (3% DSS), Empty nanocapsule (3% DSS plus daily oral gavage of 200 μL PDA@Gel@G carrier suspension), Free oridonin (3% DSS plus oridonin at 10 mg/kg/day), and PDA@Gel@GO (3% DSS plus PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules containing oridonin at an equivalent dose of 10 mg/kg/day). Body weight, stool consistency, and fecal bleeding were recorded daily to calculate the disease activity index (DAI; 0–4). On day 8, mice were euthanized, and the entire colon was collected to measure colon length. The colon coefficient was calculated as colon weight/body weight × 100% [33, 34].

H&E Staining

Distal colon tissues (~1 cm) were excised and fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h, followed by routine dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning at 5 μm thickness. Sections were stained with H&E using a standard protocol (Solarbio, China). Histopathological changes were observed under an optical microscope (Olympus BX53, Japan). Five random fields per section were selected for evaluation. Epithelial damage, inflammatory cell infiltration, and glandular destruction were semi‐quantitatively scored on a scale of 0–4 for each parameter. The sum of these scores was used to generate a total histological score ranging from 0 to 12.

Myeloperoxidase (MPO) IF Staining

Colon tissue sections (5 μm) were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 for 10 min, followed by blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h. The sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with Anti‐MPO primary antibody (Abcam, ab208670, 1:200). The next day, Alexa Fluor488 donkey anti‐rabbit IgG (Invitrogen, A‐21206, 1:500) was applied for 1 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Cell Signaling, #8961, 1:1000) for 5 min. After washing with PBS, imaging was performed using a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal microscope. Three random fields per section were selected, and MPO fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Detection of Inflammatory Factors in Colitis Using LISA

Colonic tissues were homogenized and centrifuged (5000 × g, 10 min), and the supernatants were collected for analysis. Levels of tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), and interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) were quantified using commercially available mouse ELISA kits (TNF‐α: ab208348; IL‐6: ab222503; IL‐1β: ab197742; Abcam, UK). Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Colon tissues were excised, fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h, and subsequently embedded in paraffin. Sections were cut to a thickness of 5 μm. Following deparaffinization with xylene and rehydration through a graded alcohol series, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was performed using high‐temperature treatment in 0.01 mol/L sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). The sections were then blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h before incubation with primary antibodies: Anti‐NLRP3 (Abcam, ab263899, 1:200) and Anti‐IL‐1β (Abcam, ab254360, 1:200) at 4°C overnight. The following day, sections were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, ab6721, 1:300) for 1 h. Color development was achieved using a DAB kit (ZSGB‐Bio, China), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Images were captured using an Olympus BX53 microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Isolation of Intestinal Lamina Propria Immune Cells and Flow Cytometric Analysis

To evaluate cellular uptake and targeting specificity of PDA@Gel@GO microcapsules in inflamed colonic tissues, lamina propria mononuclear cells (LPMCs) were isolated and analyzed by flow cytometry. DSS‐induced colitis mice were orally administered Cy5.5‐labeled microcapsules and euthanized 12 h later. Colons were aseptically harvested, longitudinally opened, thoroughly rinsed with cold PBS to remove luminal contents, and cut into approximately 1 cm fragments. Tissue fragments were incubated in Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS) containing 5 mM EDTA and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 37°C with gentle agitation for 20 min to remove epithelial cells. The remaining tissues were washed, minced, and enzymatically digested in a solution containing collagenase IV (1 mg/mL) and DNase I (0.1 mg/mL) at 37°C for 45–60 min. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and collected by centrifugation. Cells were resuspended in PBS and incubated with an Fc receptor–blocking antibody (anti‐CD16/32) for 10 min to reduce nonspecific binding. Cells were then stained with fluorophore‐conjugated antibodies against mouse CD11b‐FITC and F4/80‐PE at 4°C for 30 min in the dark. After washing, samples were analyzed using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer, and data were processed with FlowJo software (version 10). Gating strategies involved initial exclusion of debris and doublets based on FSC/SSC parameters, followed by identification of CD11b+F4/80+ macrophages. Cy5.5 fluorescence intensity within this population was quantified to assess microcapsule uptake efficiency. In parallel, the proportion of F4/80+ cells within the Cy5.5‐positive population was analyzed to evaluate targeting specificity.

Collection of Colonic Tissue Samples and RNA Extraction

Following euthanasia, distal colonic tissues were rapidly collected from mice in the DSS and PDA@Gel@GO treatment groups, immediately snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until use. Three biological replicates from each group were randomly selected for RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq). Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (15596018, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, while RNA integrity was evaluated with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). All samples exhibited RNA integrity number (RIN) values ≥ 8.0 and OD260/280 ratios between 1.9–2.1, meeting the quality requirements for library construction.

Library Construction and High‐Throughput Sequencing

RNA‐seq libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (E7530, New England Biolabs, USA). The procedure included mRNA enrichment, fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, end repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. After quality control, paired‐end sequencing (PE150) was carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA). Each sample generated no less than 6 Gb of raw data. Library construction and sequencing were conducted by Thermo Fisher Scientific (Guangzhou) Co. Ltd.

Processing of Raw Data and Differential Expression Analysis

Raw sequencing reads were processed using fastp software (v0.23.2) to remove adapter sequences and low‐quality reads, yielding clean reads. Clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1), with alignment rates exceeding 95%. Gene‐level counts were obtained with featureCounts (v2.0.1), and expression levels were normalized as transcripts per million (TPM). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (v1.38.3) in the R environment (v4.2.2). The selection criteria were |log2Fold Change| ≥ 1 and a Benjamini‐Hochberg adjusted FDR < 0.05, resulting in 579 significantly upregulated and 311 downregulated genes.

Heatmap and Cluster Analysis

Normalized expression data of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were used to generate a bidirectional hierarchical clustering heatmap with the pheatmap package (v1.0.12). Clustering was performed based on Euclidean distance using the complete linkage method to visualize expression patterns between DSS and treatment groups. In addition, volcano plots were generated using the ggplot2 package (v3.4.0) to illustrate the distribution of DEGs.

Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Functional Enrichment Analysis

Significantly upregulated genes were subjected to Gene Ontology and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler package (v4.6.0), with a significance threshold set at FDR < 0.05. Gene Ontology annotations were performed across three categories: Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF). The KEGG pathway database used was the version updated in March 2023. Results were visualized using bubble plots generated by enrichplot (v1.18.4) and ggplot2.

Analysis of Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) Networks and Module Identification

Upregulated genes were imported into the STRING database (v11.5, https://string‐db.org/) to construct a PPI network using a minimum interaction confidence score of 0.9. The resulting network was visualized with Cytoscape software (v3.9.1). Functional modules were identified using the MCODE plugin (v1.6.1) with the following parameters: degree cutoff = 2, node score cutoff = 0.2, k‐core = 2, and max depth = 100.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5. Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using a two‐tailed Student's t‐test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Sample sizes (n) are specified in the corresponding figure legends. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Construction and Characterization of Gal‐N‐CMCS/Oridonin Nanomicelles

Gal‐N‐CMCS/oridonin (GO) nanomicelles were successfully constructed to enable colon‐ and macrophage‐targeted oral delivery of oridonin. The nanomicelles were formed through the self‐assembly of Gal‐N‐CMCS, which served as the polymeric framework for drug encapsulation (Figure 1A). The formation of Gal‐N‐CMCS was validated by FTIR spectroscopy. As shown in Figure 1B, characteristic absorption changes corresponding to lactobionic acid grafting were observed, indicating successful galactose modification of CMCS. CMC analysis using the pyrene fluorescence probe method revealed that the CMC of Gal‐N‐CMCS (~0.008 mg/mL) was slightly higher than that of CMCS‐N (~0.005 mg/mL) (Figure 1C,D), suggesting increased hydrophilicity following galactose conjugation.

(A) Schematic illustration showing the synthesis of the Gal‐N‐CMCS polymer and the self‐assembly process of GO nanomicelles, as well as the overall construction and characterization strategy; (B) FTIR analysis of the chemical modification of Gal‐N‐CMCS; (C, D) Fluorescent probe method for determining the CMC of Gal‐N‐CMCS and CMCS‐N; (E) TEM for observing the spherical morphology and distribution of GO nanomicelles; (F) DLS analysis for assessing the particle size and uniformity of GO nanomicelles; (G) High‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) for evaluating the drug EE% and DL% of GO nanomicelles; (H) In vitro cumulative release profiles of free oridonin and GO nanomicelles under different pH conditions (pH 1.2, 6.8, and 7.4), measured by UV–visible spectrophotometry; (I, J) MTT assay to assess the biocompatibility of GO nanomicelles with Caco‐2 cells and BMDMs; (K) Confocal microscopy to observe the uptake of GO nanomicelles in LPS‐stimulated BMDMs. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments (n = 3). **p < 0.001.

GO nanomicelles prepared by ultrasonication exhibited a uniform spherical morphology, as observed by TEM, with an average diameter of 205 ± 4 nm (Figure 1E). The DLS results were consistent with the TEM observations (Figure 1F). Furthermore, no significant changes in particle size, PDI, or zeta potential were observed after storage at 4°C or ambient temperature for 7 days, indicating good colloidal stability (Figure S1A–C). The EE% of Oridonin in Gal‐N‐CMCS/Oridonin exceeded 85%, with a DL% of over 15%, indicating efficient drug incorporation (Figure 1G).

The in vitro release profiles (Figure 1H) demonstrated that free oridonin exhibited limited release at pH 1.2 and 6.8, consistent with its hydrophobic nature, whereas GO nanomicelles significantly enhanced drug release under these conditions, indicating improved solubilization. Under pH 7.4 conditions simulating the colonic environment, free oridonin showed a rapid burst release (> 80%), while GO nanomicelles displayed a more gradual and pH‐responsive release profile, with approximately 60% cumulative release over 24 h. This sustained‐release behavior suggests that GO nanomicelles may help maintain prolonged local drug availability in the colon.

The cytocompatibility of GO nanomicelles was assessed in Caco‐2 cells and BMDMs using the MTT assay. Apart from free oridonin, both GO nanomicelles and blank carriers maintained cell viabilities above 85% after 24 h incubation, indicating favorable biocompatibility (Figure 1I,J). In addition, confocal laser scanning microscopy revealed markedly enhanced intracellular fluorescence in LPS‐stimulated BMDMs treated with GO nanomicelles compared with controls, suggesting improved cellular uptake mediated by galactose modification (Figure 1K).

Inhibition of LPS‐Induced NLRP3 Activation in BMDMs by GO Micelles

In the inflammation model of BMDMs stimulated by LPS, we systematically evaluated the intervention effect of GO on the NLRP3 (Figure 2A). RT‐qPCR analysis revealed that NLRP3, ASC, caspase‐1, and IL‐1β mRNA levels were markedly elevated in LPS‐stimulated cells compared with controls. No significant differences were detected between the blank micelle (G) group and the LPS group, indicating that the carrier itself had no regulatory effect on NLRP3‐related gene expression. In contrast, free oridonin significantly suppressed the expression of these genes, whereas GO micelles produced a more pronounced inhibitory effect (Figure 2B). Consistent with the transcriptional data, WB analysis showed marked upregulation of NLRP3, ASC, caspase‐1, and IL‐1β proteins in the LPS group relative to controls. Protein expression levels in the G group remained comparable to those in the LPS group, whereas both free oridonin and GO micelles significantly reduced the expression of these inflammasome‐related proteins, with a greater reduction observed in the GO group (Figure 2C).

Intracellular ROS levels were further assessed using fluorescence probes. As shown in Figure 2D, ROS fluorescence intensity was substantially increased in LPS‐stimulated BMDMs compared with control cells. No significant reduction was detected in the G group, whereas both free oridonin and GO micelles markedly decreased ROS levels. Notably, ROS intensity in the GO group was reduced to levels approaching those observed in the control group (Figure 2D).

IF staining revealed enhanced expression and punctate aggregation of NLRP3 and ASC in the LPS group compared with the control group, whereas no appreciable changes were observed in the blank micelle (G) group. Treatment with free oridonin reduced the fluorescence intensity of NLRP3 and ASC, while GO micelles led to a further decrease in fluorescence signals and visibly attenuated ASC speck formation (Figure 2E,F). Consistent with these observations, oxidative stress assays showed that LPS stimulation significantly increased MDA levels and reduced SOD and GSH‐Px activities compared with controls. No significant improvement was detected in the G group. In contrast, both free oridonin and GO micelles significantly decreased MDA levels and partially restored SOD and GSH‐Px activities, with the GO group exhibiting a more pronounced effect (Figure 2G).

Collectively, these results indicate that GO‐mediated delivery of oridonin effectively suppresses oxidative stress and attenuates the activation of key components of the NLRP3 inflammasome in vitro. Compared with free oridonin, GO micelles exhibit enhanced anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant effects in LPS‐stimulated macrophages.

Fabrication of PDA@Gel@GO Nanocapsules

PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules were successfully fabricated through a stepwise assembly strategy, in which Gal‐CMCS/GO nanomicelles were first encapsulated within gelatin microspheres via ionic crosslinking, followed by surface coating with PDA through dopamine self‐polymerization (Figure 3A). SEM revealed that PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules exhibited a uniform and distinctly rough porous surface with a pronounced granular texture compared with uncoated Gel@GO nanocapsules (Figure 3B). Laser particle size analysis showed a narrow and stable size distribution centered at approximately 20 μm, consistent with SEM observations (Figure 3C). To evaluate gastrointestinal stability and multi‐stage release behavior, Gel@GO and PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules were sequentially incubated in deionized water (30 min), artificial gastric fluid (AGF, pH 1.0, 2 h), artificial small intestinal fluid (ASF, pH 6.8, 4 h), and artificial colonic fluid (ACF, pH 7.8, 30 min). As shown in Figure 3D, both nanocapsules maintained structural integrity in water. Gel@GO nanocapsules exhibited marked structural disruption after AGF treatment and further disintegration in ASF and ACF. In contrast, PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules remained largely intact in AGF, showed only minor surface damage in ASF, and underwent pronounced rupture upon exposure to ACF. To quantitatively assess these observations, changes in particle size and drug retention of PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules were analyzed during simulated gastrointestinal digestion [35] (Figure 3E). During incubation in water and AGF, particle size remained stable and oridonin retention exceeded 90%. Corresponding data summarized in Table S2 indicated a pH‐dependent surface charge transition, with zeta potential shifting from −22.4 mV in water to +8.6 mV in AGF. This charge reversal, attributed to protonation of amine groups in the PDA coating, likely contributed to reduced aggregation and enhanced stability under acidic conditions. In ASF, moderate swelling of the nanocapsules was observed, accompanied by a slight increase in particle size and a decrease in drug retention to approximately 80%. Upon exposure to ACF, rapid structural collapse occurred, and drug retention decreased to below 30%, consistent with enzyme‐mediated degradation in the colonic environment. These quantitative results were in good agreement with SEM‐based morphological observations and collectively demonstrate the stage‐dependent stability and release behavior of PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules.

Combined with the zeta potential data summarized in Table S2, these results confirm the underlying mechanism of gastrointestinal stability. The in vivo biosafety of PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules was further evaluated. H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) revealed no apparent histopathological abnormalities in the GO or PDA@Gel@GO groups compared with the healthy group (Figure S2A). Complete blood count analysis showed no significant differences in RBC, WBC, HGB, or PLT levels among groups (Figure S2B). In addition, serum biochemical parameters related to liver (ALT, AST) and kidney (BUN, CRE) function remained within normal ranges and were comparable to those of healthy mice (Figure S2C).

Collectively, these results indicate that PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules exhibit favorable gastrointestinal stability, controlled stage‐dependent disassembly, and acceptable systemic biosafety in vivo.

PDA@Gel@GO Microcapsules Exhibit Good Biocompatibility and Achieve Inflammation‐Site–Targeted Accumulation in Colitis Models

To assess the inflammation‐targeting capability of the GO‐loaded system, an in vitro macrophage model was first established. BMDMs were polarized toward the M1 phenotype by LPS stimulation and subsequently incubated with Cy5.5‐labeled GO nanomicelles (GO–Cy5.5). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed markedly enhanced colocalization between Cy5.5 fluorescence and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) in the GO–Cy5.5 group compared with the PBS control, indicating preferential uptake of GO nanomicelles by proinflammatory macrophages (Figure 4A). To evaluate whether PDA coating improved gastrointestinal stability and enabled colonic delivery in vivo, DSS‐induced colitis mice were orally administered Cy5.5‐labeled PDA@Gel@GO or Gel@GO microcapsules (200 mg/kg). Six hours after gavage, the entire gastrointestinal tract was harvested for fluorescence imaging (Figure 4B). Compared with Gel@GO, PDA@Gel@GO microcapsules exhibited substantially stronger fluorescence signals in the colonic region, suggesting improved protection against gastrointestinal degradation and enhanced colonic accumulation conferred by the PDA coating (Figure 4C).

Inflammation‐dependent targeting was further examined by longitudinal in vivo fluorescence imaging following oral administration of Cy5.5‐labeled PDA@Gel@GO microcapsules to healthy and DSS‐induced colitis mice. At 6 h post‐gavage, fluorescence intensity in the colonic region of DSS‐treated mice was markedly higher than that in healthy controls, and elevated signals persisted up to 12 h (Figure 4D), indicating enhanced accumulation and prolonged retention under inflammatory conditions.

To exclude the potential contribution of altered gastrointestinal motility, gastric emptying and small intestinal transit were assessed using the phenol red method. No significant differences were observed between healthy and DSS‐treated mice (Figure S3), suggesting that enhanced colonic accumulation was not attributable to changes in gastrointestinal kinetics. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of major organs and colonic tissues collected 12 h after administration further demonstrated selective enrichment of fluorescence signals in the colon, with minimal signal detected in non‐target organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys (Figure 4E). Consistently, pharmacokinetic analysis showed that plasma oridonin concentrations in the PDA@Gel@GO group remained extremely low throughout the observation period compared with the free oridonin group (Figure S2D), indicating limited systemic exposure.

To quantitatively assess macrophage‐specific uptake within inflamed colonic tissue, LPMCs were isolated and analyzed by flow cytometry. Cy5.5 fluorescence intensity was significantly increased in colonic macrophages (CD11b+F4/80+) following PDA@Gel@GO administration (Figure 4F). Moreover, F4/80+ macrophages constituted the predominant cell population among Cy5.5‐positive cells (Figure 4G). Confocal imaging of colonic tissue sections further confirmed pronounced colocalization of Cy5.5 fluorescence with F4/80‐labeled macrophages (Figure 4H), providing morphological evidence of preferential delivery of PDA@Gel@GO microcapsules to inflammatory macrophages in colitic lesions.

Therapeutic Effects of PDA@Gel@GO Nanocapsules on DSS‐Induced UC in Mice

A DSS‐induced UC mouse model was successfully established by administering 3% DSS in drinking water for 7 days. Compared with control mice, DSS‐treated animals exhibited pronounced weight loss, diarrhea, and rectal bleeding, leading to a significant increase in the DAI. In addition, pronounced colon shortening and an increased colon‐to‐body weight ratio (colon coefficient) were observed, indicative of colonic edema and inflammation. Mice treated with empty nanocapsules showed disease indices comparable to those of the DSS group, suggesting that the carrier alone did not exert a therapeutic effect. Treatment with free oridonin partially alleviated body weight loss, reduced DAI scores, and improved colon length; however, these parameters did not return to control levels, and the reduction in colon coefficient was modest. In contrast, mice treated with PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules exhibited more pronounced improvement, including enhanced body weight recovery, a significant decrease in DAI score, partial restoration of colon length, and a marked reduction in colon coefficient toward control values (Figure 5A–D). H&E staining revealed severe disruption of colonic mucosal architecture in DSS‐treated mice, characterized by disorganized glandular structure and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration. Similar pathological features were observed in the empty nanocapsule group. Treatment with free oridonin resulted in partial histological improvement, whereas PDA@Gel@GO treatment significantly reduced histopathological injury scores, with improved epithelial integrity and decreased inflammatory infiltration compared with the DSS group (Figure 5E).

WB analysis revealed that DSS treatment markedly reduced the expression of tight junction proteins, including ZO‐1, occludin, and claudin‐1, in colonic tissues. In contrast, PDA@Gel@GO treatment significantly restored the levels of these barrier‐associated proteins compared with both the DSS and free oridonin groups, indicating improved intestinal barrier integrity (Figure 5F). MPO IF staining showed a pronounced increase in MPO expression in the DSS group, reflecting enhanced neutrophil infiltration. Similar MPO positivity was observed in the empty nanocapsule group. Free oridonin treatment reduced MPO fluorescence intensity to some extent, whereas PDA@Gel@GO treatment resulted in a more pronounced decrease in MPO signal compared with the DSS group (Figure 5G). Consistent with these observations, colonic levels of the proinflammatory cytokines TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β were markedly increased in DSS‐treated mice, with no significant reduction observed in the empty nanocapsule group. Free oridonin treatment showed a downward trend in cytokine levels, while PDA@Gel@GO treatment led to a further and significant reduction in these inflammatory markers (Figure 5H).

Collectively, these results indicate that PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules effectively alleviate DSS‐induced colitis symptoms, attenuate histopathological damage, preserve intestinal barrier integrity, and suppress local inflammatory responses in vivo.

Regulatory Effects of PDA@Gel@GO Nanocapsules on NLRP3 in DSS‐Induced UC Mice

To evaluate the in vivo regulatory effects of PDA@Gel@GO nanocapsules on NLRP3 inflammasome activation, DSS‐induced colitis mice were treated by oral gavage according to the established protocol. WB analysis demonstrated that colonic tissues from DSS‐treated mice exhibited markedly elevated protein levels of NLRP3, ASC, caspase‐1, and IL‐1β compared with controls, indicating robust inflammasome activation. Protein expression in the empty nanocapsule group was comparable to that in the DSS group, confirming that the carrier alone had no regulatory effect. In contrast, free oridonin significantly reduced the expression of these inflammasome‐related proteins, while PDA@Gel@GO treatment produced a further and more pronounced downregulation (Figure 6A). Immunohistochemical staining further supported these findings. DSS‐treated mice displayed extensive brown‐yellow positive staining for NLRP3 and IL‐1β in colonic tissues, whereas positive areas were moderately reduced in the free oridonin group. In contrast, PDA@Gel@GO treatment markedly decreased NLRP3‐ and IL‐1β‐positive staining and was associated with improved preservation of mucosal epithelial architecture (Figure 6B).

Transcriptome Sequencing Reveals PDA@Gel@GO Enhances Colonic Epithelial Repair

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which PDA@Gel@GO facilitates the treatment of colitis, we conducted RNA‐seq analysis on colonic tissues from mice before (DSS group) and after treatment (Treated group) (Figure 7A). Differential expression analysis identified 579 upregulated and 311 downregulated genes (Figure 7B). A heatmap of representative DEGs revealed distinct expression patterns related to inflammatory responses, epithelial repair, cell proliferation, and mucosal barrier function. Notably, multiple inflammation‐associated genes, including Il6, Cxcl10, Rorc, Batf2, and Ltf, were downregulated in the treated group compared with the DSS group. In contrast, genes related to intestinal epithelial regeneration and barrier function, such as Lgr5, Mki67, Cldn2, Fabp2, and Reg4, along with goblet cell markers (Retnlb, Itln1, and Clca1), were markedly upregulated following treatment. Hierarchical clustering analysis based on these differentially expressed genes clearly separated DSS and treated samples, indicating substantial transcriptomic remodeling associated with PDA@Gel@GO administration (Figure 7C).

To further characterize the functional relevance of upregulated genes, pathway enrichment analyses were conducted. KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment of pathways related to cell proliferation and tissue repair, with DNA replication and Cell cycle pathways among the most prominently enriched. In addition, pathways involved in genomic maintenance, including Homologous recombination, Base excision repair, and Mismatch repair, were significantly enriched, suggesting enhanced activation of DNA repair–associated processes following treatment. In addition, the IL‐17 signaling pathway was significantly enriched among upregulated genes, suggesting modulation of immune‐related signaling following treatment (Figure 7D).

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis further supported these findings. In the BP category, upregulated genes were mainly enriched in processes related to cell cycle progression, including chromosome segregation, DNA replication, nuclear division, and organelle fission. CC analysis highlighted enrichment in mitosis‐associated structures, such as chromosomal regions, centromeric regions, kinetochores, and the mitotic spindle. MF analysis revealed enrichment of DNA helicase activity, ATP‐dependent DNA helicase activity, and single‐stranded DNA binding, collectively suggesting activation of DNA replication and repair–related functional programs (Figure 7E).

PPI network analysis combined with MCODE clustering further demonstrated that upregulated genes formed several densely connected functional modules associated with DNA replication, mitotic spindle organization, centromere–kinetochore function, and chromosome condensation (Figure S4). These interaction networks are consistent with the coordinated activation of epithelial proliferation and regeneration‐associated processes in treated colonic tissues.

RT‐qPCR was used to confirm RNA‐seq–identified changes in epithelial regeneration–related genes. Compared with the DSS group, PDA@Gel@GO–treated mice exhibited significantly increased mRNA expression levels of Lgr5, Mki67, and Reg4 (Figure 7F–H; p < 0.05), consistent with the transcriptomic data. Collectively, transcriptomic profiling and validation analyses indicate that PDA@Gel@GO treatment is associated with suppression of inflammatory gene expression and activation of gene programs related to epithelial proliferation, regeneration, and mucosal barrier repair in DSS‐induced colitis.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a PDA@Gel@GO nanomicrocapsule system for oral, colon‐targeted delivery of oridonin and demonstrated its therapeutic efficacy in a DSS‐induced murine colitis model. The results indicate that this delivery platform maintains structural integrity during gastrointestinal transit while enabling preferential accumulation at inflamed colonic sites, thereby increasing local drug availability at disease lesions. Consistent with this targeted distribution, PDA@Gel@GO treatment significantly alleviated key clinical manifestations of colitis, including body weight loss, elevated DAI, and colon shortening. Importantly, the empty microcapsule group exhibited neither therapeutic benefit nor detectable toxicity (Figures 5, 6, and S2), confirming that the observed effects were attributable to oridonin rather than the carrier materials themselves. Beyond symptomatic improvement, transcriptomic profiling revealed that PDA@Gel@GO treatment was associated with suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome–related inflammatory signaling and concurrent activation of gene programs involved in cell cycle progression and DNA repair. These coordinated transcriptional changes suggest that the therapeutic effects of PDA@Gel@GO may arise from a combination of inflammation attenuation and promotion of epithelial regeneration. This dual regulatory pattern is particularly relevant for IBD, in which persistent inflammation and impaired mucosal repair act synergistically to drive disease progression.

Oridonin is a natural diterpenoid compound with well‐documented anti‐inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties and has been shown to inhibit key inflammatory pathways, including NF‐κB and NLRP3 signaling [36, 37, 38]. However, its clinical application is hindered by poor water solubility, low oral bioavailability, and limited tissue specificity [39, 40]. Previous efforts employing delivery vehicles such as liposomes or single‐component polymeric nanoparticles have improved certain pharmacokinetic parameters but generally failed to simultaneously achieve gastrointestinal stability, prolonged local retention, and precise site‐specific release [41, 42, 43]. The PDA@Gel@GO system developed in this study effectively overcomes these challenges through a multilayered structural design. The GO polymeric micelles provide a high‐capacity drug‐loading platform and enable galactose‐mediated targeting; the gelatin matrix facilitates colon‐specific enzymatic degradation; and the PDA coating confers acid resistance and enhanced lesion adhesion. This multifunctional integration not only addresses the key barriers associated with oral delivery of oridonin but also offers a versatile strategy for improving the therapeutic application of other hydrophobic natural products.

With respect to the targeting mechanism, this design differs fundamentally from conventional galactose‐based strategies that primarily target the ASGPR on hepatocytes [44, 45]. Instead, our system is specifically engineered to recognize macrophage galactose‐type lectin (MGL/CD301), which is highly expressed on inflammatory macrophages infiltrating the colonic mucosa in IBD [28]. This approach is consistent with emerging evidence in the field of IBD nanomedicine, demonstrating that galactose ligands can circumvent hepatic sequestration and promote selective accumulation within colonic inflammatory macrophages [29, 30, 44]. Beyond receptor‐mediated active targeting, the system also follows a distinct intracellular kinetic principle characterized by a “cellular uptake first, intracellular release later” mechanism. The concordance between the in vitro sustained‐release profiles (Figure 1H) and in vivo macrophage endocytosis data (Figure 4F–H) indicates that, at the time of rapid uptake by target cells, the majority of oridonin remains sequestered within the hydrophobic core of the GO nanomicelles. This intracellularly oriented delivery mode minimizes premature extracellular drug leakage and ensures high cytosolic accumulation of oridonin, thereby creating a prerequisite for effective inhibition of cytoplasmic NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and activation.

Mechanistic investigations demonstrated that the PDA@Gel@GO system was associated with marked suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, accompanied by reduced caspase‐1 activity and decreased maturation and release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β [40, 46]. Notably, this anti‐inflammatory effect coincided with the upregulation of genes involved in intestinal epithelial regeneration and barrier repair. We propose that this transcriptional shift reflects a secondary, inflammation‐resolving–associated activation of tissue repair rather than a direct proliferative stimulus. Previous studies have demonstrated that sustained NLRP3 hyperactivation and elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF‐α and IL‐1β) profoundly suppress intestinal stem cell proliferation and exacerbate DNA damage [47, 48]. By effectively interrupting this inflammatory cascade, the PDA@Gel@GO system may relieve epithelial cells from inflammation‐induced inhibitory constraints, thereby allowing endogenous regenerative programs to resume. In this context, the observed enhancement of cell cycle– and DNA repair–related pathways is more likely to represent a permissive regenerative response following inflammation resolution, rather than a direct cell cycle–activating effect of oridonin. This coordinated “inflammation suppression–repair activation” paradigm constitutes a comprehensive pathological intervention axis and may be of greater relevance to long‐term disease control in IBD than strategies focused solely on inflammatory suppression [49, 50].

In summary, the PDA@Gel@GO system developed in this study enables precise, colon‐targeted delivery of oridonin through a multilayered architectural design combined with active macrophage targeting, thereby maximizing therapeutic efficacy in experimental colitis. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although transcriptomic analyses revealed activation of DNA repair– and cell cycle–associated pathways, the present study cannot definitively distinguish whether these changes arise from direct molecular actions of oridonin or represent secondary regenerative responses following inflammation resolution. Elucidation of causal relationships will require further investigation using non‐inflammatory in vitro models. Second, long‐term toxicological and pharmacokinetic evaluations are currently lacking, and therapeutic efficacy has been validated only in an acute DSS‐induced colitis model. Future studies will focus on assessing long‐term safety and efficacy in large animal models and chronic or relapsing colitis models. In addition, future studies will explore the translational potential of this platform in combination with imaging‐guided diagnostic and therapeutic strategies [51], aiming to provide a theoretical basis for precision treatment of IBD.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed Oridonin‐loaded nanomicelles (GO) based on Gal‐CMCS, which were encapsulated with PDA to form an orally deliverable microcapsule system. This system achieved stable delivery and targeted accumulation at inflammation sites in a UC model. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that GO significantly alleviates inflammatory responses and epithelial damage by suppressing ROS generation, restoring antioxidant enzyme activity, downregulating key components of the NLRP3 inflammasome, and inhibiting inflammasome assembly. Transcriptomic analysis further showed that GO treatment activated gene programs involved in epithelial regeneration, cell cycle progression, and DNA repair, suggesting coordinated restoration of intestinal barrier function alongside inflammation resolution. Collectively, these findings elucidate the immunoregulatory and tissue‐protective effects of GO in experimental colitis and highlight a dual‐action therapeutic paradigm integrating inflammation suppression with mucosal repair. The study introduces an innovative targeted nanocapsule system that enhances the stability of Oridonin in the gastrointestinal environment and facilitates targeted accumulation, exhibiting good biocompatibility and therapeutic efficacy with potential translational applications. However, this research primarily relies on mouse models, necessitating further evaluation of safety and efficacy in human tissues or large animal models. Additionally, systematic studies on the release behavior and tissue distribution of nanocapsules at different stages of inflammation are required. Future studies integrating multi‐omics analyses with intelligent targeting and responsive delivery strategies may further optimize this platform for precision intervention and mucosal regeneration in IBD.
