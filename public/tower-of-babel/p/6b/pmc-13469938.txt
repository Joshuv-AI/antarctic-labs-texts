Architecture of the Wza-Wzc complex that mediates colanic acid translocation across the cell envelope in Gram-negative bacteria
Authors: Jiao Liu, Yanlong Han, Guanxue Huang, Manfeng Zhang, Junlei Du, Haizhen Zhou, Dahua Chen, Yihua Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Wzx/Wzy-dependent pathway is a highly conserved mechanism for bacterial polysaccharide biosynthesis. This pathway is exemplified by the assembly and export of colanic acid mediated by the Wza-Wzc complex. While recent structural investigations have yielded valuable insights into the molecular basis of colanic acid biosynthesis, the overall architecture of the Wza-Wzc complex and the regulatory mechanism of colanic acid export remain incompletely understood. Here, we report cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of the Wza-Wzc complex from E. coli K12 in two functionally states: the autophosphorylated wild-type (Wza-WzcWT) state and the non-phosphorylated (Wza-WzcK540M) state. Both structures reveal that Wza and Wzc assemble into a continuous channel spanning the entire bacterial cell envelope, indicating that phosphorylation of Wzc does not disassemble the intact export channel. Relative to Wza-WzcK540M, the octameric periplasmic domain of Wzc in Wza-WzcWT undergoes a significant conformational rearrangement. This rearrangement reduces constriction region’s diameter and confers a negatively charged property to the interior of the Wzc octamer, which is proposed to inhibit colanic acid export. Collectively, these structural findings confirm that the Wza-Wzc complex forms a colanic acid export channel throughout Wzc’s phosphorylation-dephosphorylation cycle, advancing our mechanistic understanding of the Wzx/Wzy-dependent polysaccharide biosynthesis pathway in Gram-negative bacteria.

Introduction

Bacterial extracellular polysaccharides, which include capsular polysaccharides (CPSs) and exopolysaccharides (EPSs), play a pivotal role in mediating pathogen-host interactions and enhancing bacterial adaptability to diverse environmental niches1–3. Despite their extensive structural, functional, and physicochemical diversity, bacterial polysaccharide biosynthesis shares three evolutionarily conserved biosynthesis pathways: the Wzx/Wzy-dependent pathway, the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter-dependent pathway, and the synthase-dependent pathway1,4–6. Among these, the Wzx/Wzy-dependent pathway is the most widespread in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and relies on a set of core components to mediate polysaccharide synthesis and translocation across cell membranes7.

The Wzx/Wzy-dependent pathway initiates through the synthesis of polyprenol diphosphate-linked oligosaccharide repeats at the cytoplasmic face of the inner membrane (IM)4,7 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). These lipid-linked repeats are flipped across the IM by the Wzx flippase8, which then serve as substrates for the Wzy polymerase, an integral membrane protein with its catalytic sites located at the periplasmic face of the IM9. A critical downstream component is the polysaccharide copolymerase (PCP) protein, which regulates the size distribution and export of the final polymer product10. A classic example of this pathway is the biosynthesis of colanic acid (CA), a capsular polysaccharide (CPS) produced by Gram-negative bacteria (e.g., Escherichia coli K12)11. CA is assembled from repeat units of glucose (Glc), galactose (Gal), fucose (Fuc), and glucuronic acid (GlcA) in a 1:2:2:1 ratio, with non-stoichiometric acetyl and pyruvic acid attached as modifications (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Biosynthesis of CA relies on a set of 20 genes, including wza, wzb, wzc, and wcaA-L, that encode proteins for sugar precursor synthesis, repeat unit assembly, and CA export (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Among these genes, wza, wzb, wzc and wcaD mediate the polymerization of CA and its export from the IM to the bacterial cell surface. Central to this export process is the Wza-Wzc complex: a transmembrane macromolecular machinery proposed to serve as the structural and regulatory core of the entire pathway.

Wza and Wzc are members of two functionally conserved protein families: the outer membrane polysaccharide exporter (OPX) family (Wza)12–15 and the polysaccharide copolymerase (PCP) family (Wzc)7,16,17 (Fig. 1a). Crystallographic studies have revealed that Wza from Escherichia coli (E. coli) K30 (K30-Wza) assembles into a sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-stable octamer embedded in the OM18. K30-Wza exhibits a conserved three-domain architecture (D1–D3), wherein the D1 domain contains the PES motif (Pfam PF02563)—a signature motif of OPX proteins that mediates interactions with cognate PCP family members. In contrast, Wzc belongs to the PCP family, whose members share a conserved membrane topology: a large periplasmic domain flanked by two transmembrane helices (TMs), with some members additionally harboring an extra cytoplasmic tyrosine kinase domain19–22 (Fig. 1a). Wzc is further classified into the PCP-2a subfamily, featuring a C-terminal cytoplasmic region that harbors a tyrosine autokinase domain, followed by a tyrosine-rich C-terminal tail (Y-cluster) (Fig. 1a). This domain mediates autophosphorylation of the adjacent Y-cluster upon oligomerization23,24. Autophosphorylation of Y-cluster is proposed to induce the disassembly of Wzc octamers. Conversely, the phosphatase Wzb facilitates Wzc oligomerization through dephosphorylation of the Y-cluster20,25. This phosphorylation-dephosphorylation cycling of Wzc is indispensable for EPS and CPS biosynthesis26.

Structural studies of Wzc (a PCP-2a member) from Escherichia coli K30 (K30-Wzc), WzzB (a PCP1 family member), and KpsE (a PCP3 family member) reveal that PCP proteins generally form octameric assemblies, with large central cavities formed by periplasmic domains23,24,27–29. Specifically, previous studies show that the isolated kinase-inactive WzcK540M mutant from E. coli K30 (K30-WzcK540M), which shares 51.7% amino acid sequence identity with Wzc from E. coli K12, forms octamers (Supplementary Fig. 2), whereas autophosphorylated wild-type K30-Wzc is thought to be predominantly monomeric24. The cryo-EM structure of non-phosphorylated K30-WzcK540M mutant confirms its octameric assembly but reveals that its periplasmic domains extend only 52 Å into the periplasm, with the apical α-helical bundles folding downward to the IM and each adopting different conformations24. These findings have led to a cyclic phosphorylation-dependent assembly-disassembly model for Wzc in Wza-Wzc-mediated CPS biosynthesis. However, given that Wza’s periplasmic domains extend ~120 Å into the periplasm, two key questions remain unresolved: the nature of the Wza-Wzc interaction, and whether the 172-Å-thick periplasm in bacteria can physically accommodate the intact Wza-Wzc complex.

To address these unresolved questions, we determined the cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of two Wza-Wzc complexes that mediate CA biosynthesis in E. coli K12: the wild-type complex (Wza-WzcWT) and a kinase-inactive mutant complex (Wza-WzcK540M). These structures demonstrate that both complexes assemble into a transmembrane hexadecamer, consisting of one OM-localized Wza octamer and one IM-localized Wzc octamer, indicating that the periplasm has a thickness of approximately 230 Å. This assembly is stabilized by interactions between the PES motif (residues 82-170 in D1 domain) of Wza and the loops at the apices of Wzc’s periplasmic helical bundles, forming a continuous channel that traverses the periplasm and spans the OM. Notably, the octameric periplasmic domain of Wzc remains associated with the Wza octamer, demonstrating that Wzc phosphorylation does not induce disassembly of the Wzc octamer into monomers. Furthermore, compared with Wza-WzcK540M, phosphorylated Wzc in Wza-WzcWT exhibits distinct features arising from conformational changes. These findings illustrate that the Wza-Wzc complex constitutes a constant CA export channel, whereas its significant conformational rearrangements, orchestrated by Wzc’s phosphorylation-dephosphorylation cycle, may regulate CA polymerization and export.

Result

Overall structure of the Wza-Wzc complex

We co-expressed Wza (379 residues) and Wzc (720 residues) proteins from E. coli K12 in C43(DE3) cells and purified the resulting wild-type Wza-Wzc complex (Wza-WzcWT) and kinase-inactive mutant complex (Wza-WzcK540M) via the Strep tag fused to Wzc (Fig. 1a, b). To be noted, prompt solubilization of cell membranes with a relatively low concentration of suitable detergent is critical: prolonged solubilization (>2 h) using high detergent concentrations (>1 % n-dodecyl-β-D-maltopyranoside, DDM) causes Wza to dissociate from Wzc and triggers promiscuous reassembly of Wzc oligomers. This phenomenon is presumably driven by weak Wza-Wzc interactions and weak inter-subunit interactions within Wzc.

We first acquired cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) data for the Wza-WzcWT complex. Two-dimensional (2D) classification revealed predominantly side views of the complex, each showing two prominent membrane-boundary densities but diffuse densities corresponding to cytoplasmic regions. The cryo-EM structure of the eightfold symmetric Wza-WzcWT complex (C8 symmetry) was determined at a resolution of 3.0 Å (Supplementary Fig. 3a–e). Using this same experimental strategy, we further determined the cryo-EM structure of the Wza-WzcK540M mutant complex, achieving a higher resolution of 3.3 Å. In contrast to the Wza-WzcWT map, the densities corresponding to the transmembrane (TM) regions and cytoplasmic domains of Wza-WzcK540M are clearly resolved (Supplementary Fig. 4a–e). For model building of both complexes, we fit the cryo-EM maps with three structural templates: the K30-Wza octamer (PDB: 2J58), the K30-WzcK540M octamer (PDB: 7NHR), and an AlphaFold-predicted Wzc octamer model, with subsequent manual adjustment in Coot30 and real-space refinement in Phenix31.

Both complexes assemble into a transmembrane hexadecamer, consisting of one OM-localized Wza octamer and one IM-localized Wzc octamer (Fig. 1c). A key structural difference between the two lies in the visibility of Wzc domains: in the Wza-WzcWT complex, the transmembrane domains (TMs) and cytoplasmic tyrosine-kinase domain of the Wzc octamer are not visible, whereas these corresponding regions are clearly resolved in Wza-WzcK540M. Across both complexes, Wza forms an octameric structure, with each subunit containing three periplasmic domains (D1–D3) that extend 120 Å into the periplasm (Fig. 1c, right). In Wza-WzcK540M, specifically, the Wzc octamer is embedded in the IM via two TMs, with its helical domains localized to the periplasm and its C-terminal tyrosine kinase domain residing in the cytoplasm. This Wzc octamer spans 220 Å in length, and its periplasmic region protrudes ~110 Å into the periplasm—resulting in the full Wza-WzcK540M complex spanning the entire cell envelope, measuring 360 Å in length and reaching a maximum width of 140 Å (Fig. 1c). The conduit of both complexes contains two major constriction regions: a 6 Å-diameter constriction at the D1 ring of Wza in both states, and a second constriction at the periplasm-IM interface—with diameters of 8 Å in Wza-WzcWT and 17.6 Å in Wza-WzcK540M, respectively (Fig. 1d). The inner surfaces of both structures are primarily polar, which aligns with the negatively charged nature of CA.

Conformational changes of Wzc in Wza-WzcWT and Wza-WzcK540M

Wzc from E. coli K12 shares 51.7% sequence identity with Wzc from E. coli K30 (K30-Wzc), for which the structure of the isolated kinase-inactive mutant (K30-WzcK540M) has been reported23,24. Structural superposition analysis demonstrates that the TM and cytoplasmic regions of octameric WzcK540M in Wza-WzcK540M adopt a conformation analogous to those of the isolated octameric K30-WzcK540M, with a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 1.80 Å across 1735 Cα atoms. However, the periplasmic domains of Wzc in both Wza-WzcWT and Wza-WzcK540M differ substantially from those of K30-WzcK540M: the motif 3 in Wzc adopts an upright conformation that mediates its interaction with Wza—this extends the periplasmic region of Wzc into the periplasm by ~110 Å, which stands in stark contrast to the conformation of motif 3 in K30-WzcK540M (Fig. 2a). These observations suggest that the phosphorylated wild-type Wzc oligomerizes into octamers via its periplasmic domain and further demonstrate that Wza stabilizes the overall conformation of motif 3 in the Wza-Wzc complexes.

Further structural comparisons of the Wzc periplasmic domains between the Wza-WzcWT and Wza-WzcK540M complexes identify three key conformational and functional divergences. First, structural superposition analysis reveals substantial conformational discrepancies between the periplasmic domains of Wzc in the two complexes, with an RMSD of 5.69 Å over 360 Cα atoms (Fig. 2b, left;). Overlay of the Wza octamers in the two complexes indicates that conformational discrepancies within the Wzc octamers progressively intensify from the top to the bottom region, accompanied by a 22° rotational offset of the carbohydrate-binding domains between the two states (Fig. 2b, middle and right). Second, the constriction region within the Wzc octamer is primarily constituted by eight flexible loops, with each protomer contributing one individual loop (Residues 74-86) to form this structural module (Fig. 2c). The WzcK540M monomer contains a loop within motif 2 that forms a pore constriction with a diameter of 17.6 Å in the octameric assembly (Figs. 1d and 2c, top). In contrast, the pore constriction in Wza-WzcWT is lined by a short α-helices forming a constriction in the octameric assembly with a diameter of 8 Å, which might impede the export of CA (Supplementary Fig. 1b, Figs. 1d and 2c, bottom). Third, electrostatic potential analysis shows that in Wza-WzcK540M, alternating negatively and positively charged residues delineate circular bands lining the inner lumen of the periplasm-OM conduit (Fig. 2d, left). By comparison, the luminal surface of the Wzc octamer in Wza-WzcWT is predominantly negatively charged, which may be unfavorable to negatively-charged CA export along the conduit (Fig. 2d, right). Collectively, these structural findings indicate that phosphorylation-induced conformational rearrangements and altered luminal electrostatic properties of Wzc may serve as important determinants that impede CA assembly and export.

Close-up view of Wza-Wzc interactions

Although Wzc exhibits a significant conformational difference in Wza-WzcWT and Wza-WzcK540M complexes, Wza octamers adopt nearly identical conformations in the two complexes, with an RMSD of 5.9 Å over 2608 Cα atoms. Notably, compared to the isolated octameric K30-Wza structure, binding to Wzc does not induce expansion of the Wza constriction, which has an atom-to-atom diameter of approximately 6.3 Å, close to that of the isolated K-30 Wza octamer18 (Supplementary Fig. 5). However, close-up analysis of Wza-Wzc interactions demonstrates that WzcWT and WzcK540M mediate interaction with Wza through distinct residue subsets in the two complexes. For instance, in Wza-WzcK540M, the main-chain atoms of Wza residues T154 and A154, which are localized within the PES motif, form two hydrogen bonds: one with the side chain of Wzc T335 and the other with the main chain of Wzc L333(Fig. 3a, left). In contrast, in Wza-WzcWT, the side-chain atoms of Wza E103 and H101 form two hydrogen bonds: one with the side chain of Wzc Y334 and the other with the main chain of Wzc L333(Fig. 3a, right). To validate these intermolecular interactions, we performed pull-down assays in a co-expression system where Wzc variants were fused with a Strep tag. As shown in Fig. 3b, both WzcK540M and wild-type Wzc efficiently pulled down Wza when co-expressed. In contrast, the WzcK540M-T335A mutant, which disrupts the hydrogen bond formed in Wza-WzcK540M, failed to pull down Wza. Additionally, the WzaH101A and WzcY334A mutants, each abrogating a hydrogen bond in Wza-WzcWT structure, markedly weakened the Wza-Wzc interaction and barely pulled down Wza. As prior studies have shown, the wild-type K30-Wzc forms monomers due to autophosphorylation23,24. Here, we investigated whether octameric Wza can bind to monomeric Wzc and further induce the formation of an intact export channel in vitro. Consistent with previous findings, purified WzcK540M and WzcWT exhibited distinct elution volume peaks (P1 and P2) in size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) (Fig. 3c), indicating that, in contrast to WzcK540M, WzcWT does not form a stable octameric assembly on its own. However, when co-expressed with Wza, both the purified Wza-WzcK540M and Wza-WzcWT complexes yielded similar oligomerization peaks (P1 and P2) (Fig. 3d), from which their cryo-EM structures were determined. This demonstrates that the Wza-Wzc interaction stabilizes WzcWT octamers during co-expression. By contrast, when purified octameric Wza was mixed with purified WzcK540M or WzcWT in vitro and the mixtures were subjected to SEC, an elution peak (P1) corresponding to the Wza-WzcK540M complex was observed (Fig. 3e, f, Supplementary Fig. 6a–c), whereas no peak for the Wza-WzcWT complex was detected (Fig. 3e, f and Supplementary Fig. 6a–d). These results indicate that WzcWT monomers cannot bind to Wza. Instead, as confirmed by cryo-EM analysis (Fig. 3g), octameric Wza can interact with preformed octameric WzcK540M to form a hexadecamer. Collectively, these analyses suggest that in vivo, octameric Wza maintains the octameric state of Wzc even under conditions that alter its phosphorylation status, via the interactions elucidated by the Wza-WzcWT structure—particularly since octameric Wza does not bind to monomeric Wzc at all.

Analysis of Wzc phosphorylation states

Similar to K30-Wzc, Wzc from E. coli K12 contains a cytoplasmic tyrosine kinase domain, followed by a C-terminal Y-cluster that mediates inter-subunit interactions (Fig. 4a). Previous structural and functional studies have shown that autophosphorylation occurs upon oligomerization, whereas dephosphorylation is catalyzed by the phosphatase Wzb in vivo25. Given the significant conformational differences of Wzc between the Wza-WzcWT and Wza-WzcK540M complexes, we examined the phosphorylation status of Wzc in purified Wza-Wzc complexes. Western blot analysis revealed that Wzc in purified Wza-WzcWT is highly phosphorylated, whereas Wzc in Wza-WzcK540M is non-phosphorylated—consistent with previous observations for K30-Wzc (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, co-expression of Wza-WzcWT with Wzb resulted in weak phosphorylation of Wzc, indicating that the phosphatase Wzb can dephosphorylate Wzc in vivo (Fig. 4b). Consistent with this, addition of purified Wzb converted the highly phosphorylated Wzc in Wza-WzcWT to a hypophosphorylated state (Fig. 4c). Conversely, supplementation with ATP and magnesium ions converted hypophosphorylated Wza-WzcWT to a hyperphosphorylated state (Fig. 4d). In line with these findings, mass spectrometry (MS) analysis demonstrated that tyrosine residues Y705, Y708, Y710, Y711, Y713 and Y715 in Wzc are phosphorylated in purified Wza-WzcWT complex (Supplementary Fig. 7). Among these residues, Y705, Y711, Y713, and Y715 appear to play important roles in mediating inter-protomer interactions. For instance, Y705, Y711, and Y713 are within van der Waals distance of residues F449, D675, and L647 from the adjacent protomer; the side chain of Y715 located in a hydrophobic concave formed by residues M540, P536, and P645 from the adjacent protomer (Fig. 4a). Both the presence of the negatively charged D675 in the tyrosine pocket and the existence of hydrophobic interactions would be incompatible with binding a negatively charged phosphorylated tyrosine, which may facilitate the disassembly of the octameric BY-kinase domain in the Wza-Wzc complex. Discussion

Here, we report cryo-EM structures of two Escherichia coli K12 Wza-Wzc complexes responsible for colanic acid (CA) biosynthesis: the autophosphorylated wild-type complex (Wza-Wzc) and a kinase-dead mutant complex (Wza-WzcK540M). A recent study by Yuan et al. also resolved the cryo-EM structure of the E. coli K12 Wza-WzcK540M complex, and our reported Wza-WzcK540M structure is consistent with one of their characterized conformations32. The additional Wzc assemblies captured in their work, such as single Wzc octamers associated with two Wza translocons, likely reflect the high structural dynamics of the Wza-Wzc interaction or artifactual reassembly of Wza and Wzc oligomers during membrane solubilization and protein purification.

Prior studies have proposed that Wza-Wzc-mediated CA biosynthesis relies on a cyclic phosphorylation-dephosphorylation cycle that governs the assembly and disassembly of Wzc octamers into monomers23,24. However, our combined structural and biochemical data demonstrate that both wild-type and mutant Wza-Wzc complexes form stable hexadecameric trans-envelope conduits. This finding indicates that the Wza-Wzc channel is constitutively assembled across the periplasm and outer membrane (OM) and remains intact throughout the physiological Wzc phosphorylation-dephosphorylation cycle in vivo. We support this model with two key lines of evidence. First, structural analysis of phosphorylated Wza-Wzc reveals a stable hexadecameric architecture. Our structure shows that Wzc phosphorylation only destabilizes the octameric packing of the transmembrane (TM) region and cytoplasmic tyrosine kinase domains, while leaving the overall integrity of the CA export conduit unperturbed. Second, in vitro binding assays confirm that octameric Wza fails to interact with monomeric Wzc. Although recombinant octameric Wza can assemble with purified octameric WzcK540M into hexadecamers resembling the co-expressed complex, this reconstitution is unlikely to occur in vivo. As Wza localizes exclusively to the OM and Wzc to the inner membrane (IM), the native periplasmic distance prevents direct OM-IM protein contact when the Wzc periplasmic helical domains adopt the downward-folded conformation observed in isolated WzcK540M octamers. Collectively, these results suggest that Wzc autophosphorylation disassembles TM and cytoplasmic-domain octamers, whereas the Wzc periplasmic domains remain octameric and are structurally stabilized by Wza, thereby sustaining a constitutively intact CA export channel.

Beyond maintaining channel integrity, Wzc autophosphorylation induces global and local conformational rearrangements that terminate CA biosynthesis and export. Structural comparison identifies two inhibitory phosphorylation-dependent alterations in the channel: narrowing of the luminal diameter to 8 Å and accumulation of negative charge on the inner channel surface. These structural changes may block CA translocation, demonstrating that the Wza-Wzc complex serves as a constitutive export channel whose activity is tightly controlled by Wzc phosphorylation-dephosphorylation-dependent conformational remodeling, thereby regulating CA polymerization and secretion.

Notably, Wza acts as a critical structural stabilizer for the Wzc periplasmic helical domain. In the absence of Wza, the apical helical bundle of isolated Wzc periplasmic domains adopts heterogeneous, downward-folded conformations, which are not observed in intact Wza-Wzc complexes. Similar conformational instability is conserved among other polysaccharide copolymerase (PCP) proteins, including KpsE29, indicating a universal evolutionary strategy in Gram-negative bacteria to assemble robust, dynamically regulated trans-envelope nanomachines for polysaccharide secretion.

Integrating our structural and biochemical findings, we propose a revised working model for Wza-Wzc-mediated CA assembly and export (Fig. 5), as outlined below. In the beginning state, Wza and Wzc assemble into a trans-envelope translocation conduit with unphosphorylated Wzc. In this conformation, Wzc transmembrane helices (TMHs) and cytosolic BY-kinase domains form compact, stable octamers, with inter-protomer interactions primarily mediated by the C-terminal tail of individual Wzc subunits. Within the membrane bilayer, the Wzy polymerase is presumably embedded in the central lumen enclosed by the eight Wzc two-helical bundles33. This configuration locks Wzy in a catalytically competent state, allowing continuous incorporation of CA repeat units via lateral membrane diffusion and supporting sustained CA biosynthesis and export. Elevated intracellular ATP and Mg2+ levels trigger autophosphorylation of the Y-cluster within the C-terminal BY-kinase domain of Wzc. Tyrosine phosphorylation across adjacent Wzc protomers weakens inter-subunit interactions, destabilizing the octameric assembly of both cytoplasmic kinase and TM domains. This remodeling reorients Wzy within the Wzc-luminal cavity, switching Wzy to a catalytically inactive conformation and thereby terminating CA polymerization and export. Finally, the cognate phosphatase Wzb mediates dephosphorylation of the Wzc C-terminal tyrosine cluster. Dephosphorylation restores the canonical octameric packing of Wzc TM and BY-kinase domains, reverting Wzy to its functional state and resuming CA biosynthesis and export. Methods

Plasmid construction

E. coli B strain C43(DE3) (Transgen) and E. coli JM109 were employed for all biochemical experiments, while E. coli K-12 DH5α was used for cloning and mutagenesis. Bacterial cells were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with ampicillin (100 μg/mL) and chloramphenicol (25–50 μg/mL) as required. Three genes—wza, wzc, and wzb—were amplified from the genomic DNA of E. coli K-12 DH5α via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and subcloned into the pQLink vector34, which harbors an isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG)-inducible T5 promoter. A Strep-tag II was fused to the C-terminus of Wzc, and a His-tag was attached to the C-terminus of Wza. For co-expression of the protein complex, individual gene-containing plasmids were assembled using the previously reported ligation-independent cloning (LIC) approach. Plasmids encoding Wza or Wzc variants were constructed via site-directed mutagenesis.

Purification of the Wza-Wzc complex

E. coli strain C43 cells were transformed with plasmids expressing Wzc-Strep-Tag II and Wza-His. Bacterial cells were cultured at 37 °C in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin sodium until the culture reached an optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) of 1.0. Protein expression was induced with 0.2 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 20 °C overnight. All purification steps were performed at 4 °C or on ice unless otherwise specified.

Cell pellets were collected by centrifugation at 1500 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C and resuspended in Buffer A (20 mM HEPES, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl). Resuspended cells were lysed by sonication using a Misonix Sonicator S-4000 (Cole Parmer, Vernon Hills, IL, USA), followed by centrifugation at 39,000 ×g (rotor: JA25.50, Avanti Centrifuge J-26XP, Beckman Coulter) for 1 h at 4 °C to collect total cell membranes.

Total membranes were solubilized in Buffer B (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1% n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside [DDM, Anatrace]) at 4 °C for 1 h. Supernatants were collected after centrifugation at 39,000 ×g for 1 h at 4 °C and incubated with pre-equilibrated Strep-Tactin beads (DiNing) for 45 min at 4 °C.

After rinsing the Strep-Tactin beads with Wash Buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, and 0.03% glyco-diosgenin [GDN]), the Wza-Wzc complex was eluted with Buffer C (Buffer A supplemented with 5 mM D-desthiobiotin [cat. no. 1169249, Leyan] and 0.01% GDN) and concentrated to 1 mL using a 100-kDa Millipore centrifugal filter (Merck Millipore).

The concentrated protein was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) using a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column equilibrated with Buffer D (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.8, 150 mM NaCl, 0.006% GDN). Peak fractions were collected, concentrated to 5–6 mg/mL using a 100-kDa centrifugal filter, and analyzed by 15% SDS-PAGE alongside a pre-stained protein marker.

In vitro assembly of the Wza-Wzc complex

E. coli strain C43 cells were transformed with plasmids expressing Wzc-Strep-Tag II and Wza-His, respectively. Bacterial cells were grown at 37 °C in Luria broth (LB) medium supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin sodium until the culture reached an optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) of 1.0, consistent with the above conditions. Protein expression was induced with 0.2 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 20 °C overnight.

Wza and Wzc proteins were purified as described above. Subsequently, the purified Wza and Wzc proteins were mixed at a defined ratio and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. The resulting mixture was then subjected to gel filtration chromatography. Concurrently, the Strep-Tag fused to Wzc was utilized to pull down Wza from the mixture.

SDS-PAGE analysis and immunoblotting

SDS-PAGE analyses were performed at 180 V for 60–75 min using precast 15% Tris-HCl polyacrylamide gradient gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For Western blotting, proteins were transferred onto Immuno-Blot PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad) at a constant voltage of 25 V for 15 min. Membranes were blocked with TBS-T buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20) containing 5–10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 hour at room temperature (RT). Membranes were then probed with an anti-phosphotyrosine (anti-PY) antibody (1:3000 dilution, cat. no. P-Y-1000, Cell Signaling Technology; validation available on the manufacturer’s website) for 1 hour at RT. Following primary antibody incubation, membranes were washed three times with TBS-T buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween 20) and then incubated with the goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:3000 dilution, cat. no. S0101, Lablead; validation available on the manufacturer’s website) conjugated to Alexa Fluor 680 dye for 1 hour at RT. Finally, membranes were washed three times with TBS-T buffer, and fluorescence signals were visualized using a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). All antibodies were used at a 1:3000 dilution.

Proteomics analysis

A purified WzcWT band from an SDS-PAGE gel was excised, dehydrated in 100% acetonitrile for 10 min, and dried in a SpeedVac for 15 min. The sample was then reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 56 °C for 45 min, followed by alkylation with 40 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature for 45 min in the dark. The gel plugs were subsequently dried using a SpeedVac and digested with trypsin overnight at 37 °C for LC-MS/MS analysis.

All nanoLC-MS/MS experiments were conducted on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled with an Easy n-LC 1200 HPLC system (Thermo Scientific). Peptides were loaded onto a home-packed 100 μm i.d. × 2 cm fused silica trap column (Reprosil-Pur C18 AQ, 5 μm, Dr. Maisch GmbH) and then separated on a 75 μm i.d. × 25 cm C18 analytical column (Reprosil-Pur C18 AQ, 1.9 μm, Dr. Maisch GmbH). Buffer A contained 0.1% formic acid in water, and Buffer B consisted of 80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. A segmented gradient was employed at a flow rate of 300 nl/min: 4–9% B for 3 min; 9–20% B for 22 min; 20–30% B for 20 min; 30–40% B for 15 min; 40–95% B for 3 min; and 95% B for 10 min.

MS analysis was performed using the Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer equipped with a FAIMS Pro interface (Thermo Scientific). The instrument parameters were set as follows: maximum fill time of 22 ms, and dynamic exclusion of precursors within 10 ppm for 40 s. Raw data were analyzed with Proteome Discoverer (version 2.4.1.15) using the Sequest HT search engine for protein identification. Key search parameters were configured as: trypsin as the digestive enzyme with up to two missed cleavages allowed; carbamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification; oxidation of methionine as a variable modification; and phosphorylation of serine, threonine, and tyrosine as variable modifications.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data acquisition

The eluted protein was concentrated and desalted using Zeba Spin Desalting Columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Finally, 3 μL of the Wza-Wzc complex at a concentration of 2 mg/mL was applied to glow-discharged Quantifoil holey carbon grids (2/1, 300 mesh). Grids were blotted for 5 s at 75% relative humidity and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen using an automatic plunging device (EMGP, Leica). Cryo-EM datasets of the Wza-WzcWT and Wza-WzcK540M complexes were collected using a 300-kV Titan Krios G2 microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a K2 direct electron detector (Gatan) and a GIF Quantum energy filter (energy width: 20 eV), employing the beam-image shift data collection method. Micrographs of Wza-WzcWT and Wza-WzcK540M were recorded at defocus ranges of −1.5 to −2.5 μm with pixel sizes of 0.82 Å. Each movie stack consisted of 32 frames with a total accumulated electron dose of 60 e⁻/Å². Beam-induced motion of each micrograph stack was corrected using MotionCor2, and defocus parameters for each micrograph were determined using CTFFIND4.

Cryo-EM data processing

A total of 4,718 and 12,026 cryo-EM movies were collected for the Wza-Wzc and Wza-WzcK540M complexes, respectively. Data processing was performed using CryoSPARC v4.3.0. Movie stacks were motion-corrected using the Patch Motion algorithm, followed by contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation with Patch CTF. Micrographs with a resolution better than 6 Å and astigmatism <500 Å were manually selected for further analysis. Particles were manually picked using a 520 Å-diameter template, extracted with a 640-pixel box size, and binned 4-fold to a pixel size of 3.28 Å/pixel.

After several rounds of 2D classification to remove contaminants, the remaining particles were re-extracted at the original pixel size (0.82 Å) and subjected to two rounds of ab initio reconstruction into three classes. The class displaying canonical transmembrane helix densities was used as the initial reference for heterogeneous refinement of all binned particles. The optimal class from heterogeneous refinement was re-extracted without binning and processed through additional rounds of ab initio reconstruction and non-uniform refinement. Final maps were improved via iterative local refinement and 3D classification without alignment using a soft mask (mask focused on the restriction region and the cytoplasmic domain), yielding 3D reconstructions with resolutions of 3.01 Å and 3.25 Å for Wza-Wzc and Wza-WzcK540M, respectively.

Model building and refinement

For model building of the Wza-Wzc complex, the crystal structure of E. coli K30 WzcK540M (PDB ID: 3LA6), the crystal structure of E. coli K30 Wza (PDB ID: 2J58), and the AlphaFold-predicted structure of Wzc (PDB ID: AF-P76387-F1) were fitted into the cryo-EM map using UCSF Chimera35. All amino acids were then adjusted to match the correct Wza and Wzc sequences, followed by manual model building in Coot30. The initial model was refined via real-space refinement in PHENIX31 and further manually adjusted in Coot.

For model building of the Wza-WzcK540M complex, the previously determined Wza-Wzc structure was used as the starting model and fitted into the ~3.25 Å cryo-EM map by model morphing in Coot. After deleting the missing regions of Wzc, the model was manually built in Coot and refined using real-space refinement in PHENIX. Cryo-EM data collection and refinement statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.
