IGFBP2 promotes immunosuppression by regulating macrophage PD-L1 expression in NEUROD1-high small cell lung cancer
Authors: Siyi He, Chunyan Liang, Qiqi Huang, Xiang Tan, Jiali Meng, Xinling Qin, Yan Zhang, Jiaqing Liao, Geng Su, Rensheng Wang, Weimei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe 5-year survival rate for small cell lung cancer (SCLC) remains low. Immune tolerance is a significant characteristic of this disease and plays a crucial role in its malignant progression. Insulin-like Growth Factor Binding Protein 2 (IGFBP2) has attracted widespread attention as a potential therapeutic target in cancer, but its role in SCLC remains to be elucidated.MethodsThis study investigated the association between IGFBP2 secreted by NEUROD1-high small cell lung cancer (SCLC-N) and the expression of programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) in tumor associated macrophages (TAMs) using multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC), western blotting (WB), quantitative PCR (qPCR) and immunofluorescence (IF) following co-culture TAMs with SCLC cells. The infiltration and exhaustion of CD8 + T lymphocytes were detected using qPCR, mIHC, flow cytometry and ELISA. Subcutaneous syngeneic model using Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells employed to elucidate the functions of IGFBP2. The upstream regulation mode of IGFBP2 high expression in SCLC-N was explored through integrated analysis of ChIP-seq and CUT&Tag data. Furthermore, the mechanism by which IGFBP2 promotes PD-L1 expression in TAMs was defined by constructing wild-type (WT) and NLSmutant (MUT) IGFBP2 overexpression plasmids.ResultsThis study reveals that IGFBP2 is upregulated in SCLC-N cell lines and tissues. Furthermore, high IGFBP2 expression in SCLC-N promotes the shift of TAMs toward M2-type TAMs (M2-TAMs) and induces PD-L1 expression. This IGFBP2-induced PD-L1 elevation in TAMs correlates with reduced CD8 + T cell infiltration and enhanced T-cell exhaustion. Functionally, in vivo administration of recombinant IGFBP2 promotes tumor growth and attenuates the efficacy of PD-L1 inhibitors. Mechanistically, NEUROD1 directly binds to the IGFBP2 promoter and activates its expression in SCLC-N. Moreover, nuclear translocation of IGFBP2 is required for promoting PD-L1 expression in TAMs.ConclusionsOur research supports the idea that IGFBP2 secreted by SCLC-N cells stimulates immunosuppression via promoting the PD-L1 expression in TAMs.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08112-2.Keywords: SCLC, IGFBP2, PD-L1, TAMs, Immunosuppression

Introduction

According to GLOBOCAN, lung cancer is the second most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide yet claims the highest number of lives, with nearly 1,800,000 deaths annually [1]. Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) arises from pulmonary neuroendocrine stem cells and accounts for approximately 13%-15% of all lung carcinomas [2–3]. Over the past two decades, therapeutic progress in SCLC has remained limited, standing in stark contrast to the substantial advances achieved in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). While NSCLC has benefited from rapid developments in targeted therapies and immunotherapies—resulting in an increase in two-year survival from 34% in 2009 to 42% in 2016—survival outcomes for SCLC have shown minimal improvement over the same period, with two-year survival increasing only marginally from 14% to 15% [4]. Although anti-programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) antibodies combined with chemotherapy have been approved by the FDA as first-line treatment for extensive-stage SCLC, the clinical gains afforded by immunotherapy in SCLC remain markedly more modest than those achieved in most other malignancies [5–6]. A principal obstacle to improving the treatment of SCLC appears to be our still-limited understanding of its underlying biology. Based on the expression levels of the transcription factors Achaete-Scute Family BHLH Transcription Factor 1 (ASCL1), Neuronal Differentiation 1 (NEUROD1), and POU Class 2 Homeobox 3 (POU2F3), SCLC can be classified into four molecular subtypes: SCLC-A, SCLC-N, SCLC-P and SCLC-I [7–10]. Emerging evidence shows that each subtype has a distinct therapeutic profile, and SCLC-N is sensitive to chemotherapy but insensitive to immunotherapy [11–12]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular characteristics of the SCLC-N subtypes, exploring novel therapeutic targets, and improving the immunotherapeutic efficacy are of great significance for delivering precision treatment and improving the long-term prognosis of patients with SCLC.

Insulin-like Growth Factor Binding Protein 2 (IGFBP2) was first characterized as a secreted protein that sequesters and modulates the bioactivity of IGF-I and IGF-II growth hormones. A large number of studies demonstrate that elevated IGFBP2 expression drives the progression of multiple malignancies such as glioma, NSCLC, hepatocellular carcinoma and pancreatic ductal carcinoma [13–15]. Preclinical studies demonstrated that IGFBP2-neutralizing antibodies hold therapeutic promise for glioma and other high-burden malignancies [16–17]. In addition, emerging studies have shown that IGFBP2 can affect the progression of malignant tumors by modulating tumor immune function. Specifically, in malignant melanoma, IGFBP2 can promote the expression of PD-L1 [18]. Notably, in tumors with high baseline tumor burden, IGFBP2 can exert immunosuppressive function by promoting the expression of PD-L1 in M2 macrophages [17]. During tumor progression, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) polarize from naïve M0 macrophages into immunosuppressive M2 phenotypes in response to changes in signaling molecules within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [19–21]. However, whether IGFBP2 governs PD-L1 expression and orchestrates tumor immune microenvironment remodeling via M2-TAMs in SCLC remains unclear.

In this study, we aimed to establish whether IGFBP2 regulates the expression of PD-L1 in TAMs and thereby fosters an immunosuppressive microenvironment in SCLC-N subtype. The findings will provide a mechanistic rationale for predicting immunotherapy response and the development of IGFBP2-targeted therapy combined with immunotherapy, advancing stratified precision therapy for SCLC.

Materials and methods

Clinical specimens and patient information

Sixty paraffin-embedded SCLC specimens and twenty-nine adjacent tissues were collected between January 2018 and May 2024 from the Minzu Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region. The inclusion criterion was a confirmed pathological diagnosis of SCLC. The study was approved by the Minzu Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region Ethics Committee. Prior to sample collection, written informed consent was obtained from each patient. RNA was extracted from these tissues for reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), while paraffin sections were used for multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC). Survival time was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of death or last follow-up.

Cell culture and transfections

The cell lines (NCI-H209, NCI-H524, NCI-H82, NCI-H69, NCI-H446, NCI-H196, THP-1) used in this study were purchased from Pricella (Wuhan, China). All cells were cultivated in specialized medium (RPMI 1640, Gibco, USA; Cat#12633020) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA; Cat#A5256701) at 37℃ in an incubator containing 5% CO2. CD8 + T cells were stimulated in medium supplemented with 1 µg/mL anti-CD3/CD28 antibody (BioLegend, USA; Cat#317326, 302934) and 50 ng/mL IL-2 (MCE, USA; Cat#HY-P7037B).

Single-guide RNA (IGFBP2-sg) and overexpression plasmid (IGFBP2-OE) were synthesized by GENE (Shanghai, China). The sgRNA sequences are shown in the Supplementary Table 1. Lentivirus was transduced into SCLC-N cells at MOI 20 (H446) or 50 (H524) according to viral titer, and stably transduced cells were selected with 0.5 µg/mL puromycin (Sigma, USA; Cat#P9620) in complete medium for a minimum of 2 weeks. For overexpression, a cell suspension (H446: 1 × 105/ml; H209: 2.5 × 105/ml) was inoculated to a 6-well plate. Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Scientific, USA; Cat#L3000015) was used to conduct transfection when cells reached 50–60% density. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for subsequent experiments.

RNA isolation and reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using an RNA Quick Extraction Kit (TransGen, China; Cat#ER111-01) and the FFPE RNA Kit (Omega Omega, USA; Cat#R6954-02) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using 5×PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan; Cat#RR037Q) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, RT-qPCR was performed in a total reaction volume of 20 µL containing 10 µL of SYBR Green qPCR Premix (Solarbio, China; Cat#SR1121), 0.2 µM of each primer, 100 ng cDNA template, and nuclease-free water. The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Melting curve analysis was performed to confirm amplification specificity. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences for IGFBP2, PD-L1, NEUROD1, CD163, HLA, TIM-3, LAG-3, PD-1 and β-actin are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Western blotting (WB)

RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China; Cat#P0013B) supplemented with a protein phosphatase inhibitor (Solarbio, China; Cat#P1260) was added to cell pellets. Cells were lysed on ice for 15 min and centrifuged at 15 000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was supplemented with loading buffer (Beyotime, China; Cat#P0015), vortexed, and boiled for 5 min at 100 °C. Proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China; Cat#PN1012) at 150 V, 40 min and then transferred to PVDF membranes (Merck, Germany; Cat#IPFL00010) at 400 mA, 30 min. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk (Solarbio, China; Cat#LP0033B) or 3% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. After overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies (PD-L1, GAPDH, β-Actin, Proteintech, China, Cat#83600-2-RR, 60004-1-Ig, 60008-1-Ig ; NEUROD1, CD163, HLA, HUABIO, China, Cat#ET1703-73, ET1704-43, ET1610-66; IGFBP2, Nature Biosciences, China, Cat#A46212; IGF1-R, pIGF1-R, AKT, pAKT, Zenbio, China Cat#R26882, R24720, 342529, 310021), membranes were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (Epizyme, China; Cat#SQ201L).

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

The sections were deparaffinized and then citric acid buffer (Servicebio, China; Cat#C1032) was used for heat-mediated antigen retrieval. Staining was performed using a multiplex fluorescence immunohistochemical staining kit (Aifang Biological, China; Cat#AFIHC035) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sections were incubated overnight at 4℃ in the dark with primary antibodies diluted as follows: IGFBP2 (1:1500), NEUROD1 (1:1500), YAP1 (1:2500) (Zenbio, China; Cat#R380482), POU2F3 (1:2000) (ImmunoWay, USA; Cat#YT6917), ASCL1 (1:2500) (ImmunoWay, USA; Cat#YT0367), CD68 (1:1500)(HUABIO, China; Cat#HA601115), CD163 (1:1500), CD8 (1:1000) (HUABIO, China; Cat#ET1606-31), and PD-1 (1:1200) (HUABIO, China; Cat#ET1606-41). After three washes, polymer-HRP secondary antibody was applied dropwise to cover the tissue and incubated at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. TSA fluorescent dye working solution (1:400) was applied to sections and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Antibody complexes were stripped by high-pressure heat retrieval in citric acid antigen retrieval buffer. After complete elution, the next primary antibody-fluorophore cycle was initiated. Following all staining rounds, sections were counterstained with DAPI and mounted.

Bioinformatics analysis

To investigate the association between IGFBP2 and NEUROD1 expression in SCLC, we analyzed data from Supplementary Tables S3, S5, and S6 published by Chan et al. (detailed methodologies are described in the original article’s STAR METHODS section) [22]. To validate the association between IGFBP2 expression and clinical outcomes, the publicly available RNA-sequencing dataset GSE60052 reported by Higgs et al. (2016) was used for analysis [23]. This dataset consists of primary human small cell lung cancer (SCLC) tissue samples and includes corresponding survival data as well as basic clinical characteristics for each patient. A total of 79 tumor samples and 7 adjacent normal tissue samples were included in the analysis, providing an adequate sample size for downstream expression profiling and survival analyses. For RNASeq data, the average read count per mate was 50 million. The sequence reads were aligned to the human reference genome hg19 (GRCh37) using TopHat2 (v2.0.9) with the corresponding annotation file (GRCh37.68). Immune cell infiltration scores were estimated from the gene expression profiles using the CIBERSORT algorithm. Correlations among IGFBP2, NEUROD1, FOXP3, and CD274 were assessed with the cor.test function and visualized in scatter plots. Samples were stratified into NEUROD1-high and NEUROD1-low groups, and differential IGFBP2 expression was compared using the t.test function. To analyze the binding signals of NEUROD1 at the IGFBP2 locus, we examined data from GSE69394 (ChIP-seq) and GSE277353 (CUT&Tag-seq) using a sliding window approach (window size = 100 bp) [24–25]. For each window, the log2 fold change (log2FC) of IP relative to Input (or IgG) was calculated. Statistical significance was then evaluated using binomial tests with FDR correction, with thresholds set at log2FC > 1 and FDR < 0.05.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cytokine levels (TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-10, and IL-12) were quantified by ELISA using the following kits (Human TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-10, and IL-12 ELISA Kits; HUABIO, China; Cat#EH0002, EH0039, EH0009, MQ00501S). All assays were performed exactly as detailed in the manufacturer’s ELISA Technical Guideline. Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded on a microplate reader.

Co-culture system

A total of two independent co-culture systems were established. The first was a tumor cell-macrophage model: SCLC cells were seeded in 0.4 μm transwell chambers (BIOFIL, China; Cat#TCS001006), cultured for 24 h, and then co-cultured with M0-THP-1 macrophages for 48 h; M0-THP-1 cells and supernatants were harvested for further detection. The second was a tumor cell-macrophage-T-cell model: SCLC were seeded in transwell inserts and co-cultured with M0-THP-1 cells for 48 h. Subsequently, T lymphocytes were added to M0-THP-1 previously co-cultured with SCLC cells for an additional 72 h. T cells and conditioned media were collected for further analysis.

Extraction of lymphocytes and macrophages from peripheral blood

Peripheral blood was obtained from healthy volunteers who signed written informed consent. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by Ficoll-Hypaque density-gradient centrifugation using Human Lymphocyte Separation Medium (Solarbio, China; Cat#R1010): 750×g for 25 min at room temperature. Subsequently, an appropriate amount of CD3+ magnetic beads was added according to the instructions of the reagent supplier (RWD, China; Cat#K1201-10). After incubation at 4℃, cells were extracted using an MS column and separated using a magnetic cell sorter (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany; Cat#130-042-201). CD3+ T lymphocytes were obtained and the purity was measured by flow cytometry.

Peripheral blood CD14 + monocytes were isolated using magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany; Cat#130-097-052) and cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 25 ng/mL human M-CSF at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. After 6 days of differentiation, M0 macrophages (M0-Mc) were obtained and used for subsequent experiments.

Mice were euthanized, the abdominal wall was sterilized with 70% ethanol, and 3 mL precold PBS was injected intraperitoneally. After gentle abdominal massage for 5 min, peritoneal lavage fluid was collected with a sterile needle. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 10 min, resuspended in RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS, and incubated at 37℃ with 5% CO2 for 3 h. Non-adherent cells were removed, and adherent peritoneal macrophages (PMs) were retained for subsequent experiments.

Flow cytometry

To analyze CD8 + T cells within the co-culture system, single-cell suspensions were prepared. Fc receptor blockers were added and incubated for 10–20 min, followed by staining with PE-conjugated anti-CD3 and APC-conjugated anti-CD8a antibodies (Biolegend, USA; Cat#300308, 301014) for 30 min at 4 °C. After two washes in staining solution, samples were acquired on a BD flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo v10 software.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed three times with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 20 min at room temperature. After blocking with 3% BSA for 1 h, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (IGFBP2, 1:200; PD-L1, 1:200), washed three times with PBS, and incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI-containing antifade mounting medium, and images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope.

Mouse model construction

A total of 38 C57BL/6J mice (6 weeks, 12–14 g) were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of the Guangxi Medical University of China. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangxi Medical University.

Eighteen C57BL/6J mice were randomly allocated to an experimental group and a control group (n = 9). Experimental mice received recombinant mouse IGFBP2 (rmIGFBP2) protein (MCE, USA; Cat#HY-P74846) (50 µg/kg) via tail-vein injection; controls received an equal volume of sterile saline. Three days before sampling, each mouse received an intraperitoneal injection of 3% β-mercaptoethanol (3 mL per mouse, autoclaved).

Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells (1.5 × 106 in 100 µL PBS) were inoculated subcutaneously into the axilla. Once tumors became touchable, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5): rmIGFBP2 (50 µg /kg), PD-L1 inhibitor (10 mg/kg), rmIGFBP2 + PD-L1 inhibitor (same doses), or vehicle (physiological saline). Treatments were administered intraperitoneally every 2 days for three cycles. Tumor dimensions were measured daily with calipers, and volume was calculated using the formula: (length × width2)/2. Humane endpoints were defined as tumor volume > 1,500 mm3 or maximum diameter > 1.5 cm. At study termination, mice were euthanized; tumors were immediately dissected and photographed.

Statistics and reproducibility

Bioinformatic analyses were conducted with R v4.2.2. Statistical analyses were performed by SPSS 24.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.0. All experiments were carried out in triplicate under similar conditions and data were presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were evaluated by Student’s t-test (normally distributed) or Mann-Whitney U test (non-normally distributed). Multiple-group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA (parametric) or Kruskal-Wallis test (non-parametric) as appropriate. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify prognostic risk factors in patients. All statistical data underwent normalization to ensure comparability across groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001). Figures were prepared using R v4.2.2, GraphPad Prism 8.0, Adobe Photoshop, Adobe Illustrator, and ImageJ. Graphical abstract created in BioRender. Bolin, L. (2026) https://BioRender.com/wovu414.

Results

IGFBP2 is up-regulated in SCLC-N subtype and is associated with poor prognosis in SCLC patients

First, we analyzed the scRNA-seq data reported by Chan et al. to identify differentially expressed genes across SCLC subtypes [22]. As shown in Fig. 1A, IGFBP2 expression was markedly elevated in SCLC-N subtype, whereas it was significantly downregulated in SCLC-P subtype (Supplemental Fig. 1A). Although IGFBP2 was not identified as a differentially expressed gene in SCLC-A in Chan et al.‘s analysis, our examination of the “SCLC-A vs. SCLC-N” differential gene results revealed that IGFBP2 expression was lower in SCLC-A compared to SCLC-N (Supplemental Fig. 1B). Subsequently, analysis of the GSE60052 dataset showed a positive correlation between IGFBP2 and NEUROD1 transcript levels in SCLC, with IGFBP2 RNA levels being significantly higher in NEUROD1-high tissues than in NEUROD1-low tissues (Fig. 1B, C). Consistently, tissues from SCLC patients were subjected to RT-qPCR and mIHC, revealing a positive correlation between IGFBP2 and NEUROD1 levels in the patients’ tissues (Fig. 1D, E). Furthermore, RT-qPCR and WB show markedly higher IGFBP2 expression in SCLC-N cell lines compared to non-SCLC-N cell lines [24–26](Fig. 1F and G). Collectively, the above experiments confirm the selective up-regulation of IGFBP2 in the SCLC-N subtype.

Then, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of the GSE60052 cohort revealed that high IGFBP2 expression was associated with poor prognosis in SCLC patients (Fig. 1H). Using our own clinical and follow-up data, we performed an independent Kaplan-Meier analysis, which further demonstrated that patients with high IGFBP2 expression exhibited worse prognosis (P = 0.00092) (Supplemental Fig. 1C). Consistently, the patients with high NEUROD1 expression had significantly poorer overall survival compared with those with low NEUROD1 expression (P = 0.017) (Supplemental Fig. 1D). When patients were stratified into four groups based on combined IGFBP2 and NEUROD1 expression levels, those with high expression of both markers exhibited significantly poorer outcomes compared to the group with low expression of both markers (P = 0.002) (Fig. 1I, Supplemental Table 3). Cox regression analyses showed that both IGFBP2 expression and disease stage were significant prognostic factors in univariate analysis (Supplemental Fig. 1E). However, multivariate analysis revealed that IGFBP2 was not an independent prognostic factor (Supplemental Fig. 1F), possibly due to its interaction with disease stage. Consequently, interaction analysis was conducted and found that IGFBP2 serves as an independent prognostic biomarker specifically in patients with limited-stage disease, with high IGFBP2 expression in this subgroup being significantly associated with poorer survival outcomes (Supplemental Fig. 1G, 1H). Collectively, these data establish that elevated IGFBP2 expression is specific to the SCLC-N subtype and portends poor prognosis in patients with SCLC.

IGFBP2 secreted by SCLC-N cells educates macrophages phenotypic shift

In our current study, CD68 + CD163+ cells (M2 phenotype) were found to infiltrate SCLC tissues at higher levels than in adjacent normal tissues, indicating a higher abundance of M2-TAMs in SCLC tissues (Fig. 2A, B). K-M analysis, incorporating clinical follow-up data, revealed that increased infiltration of M2-TAMs portends a worse prognosis in SCLC patients (Fig. 2C). Subsequently, we further explored the infiltration of TAMs in different SCLC subtypes. The RT-qPCR and mIHC analyses of SCLC patient tissues revealed a remarkable positive correlation between NEUROD1 expression and the M2 macrophage marker CD163 (Fig. 2D, E). Meanwhile, mIHC results also showed a significant positive correlation between YAP1 and CD163, indicating that both the SCLC-N and SCLC-Y subtypes are associated with higher levels of M2-TAM infiltration (Supplemental Fig. 2). We corroborated these findings in cell-line co-cultured models. qPCR and WB revealed that M0-THP-1 cells shifted to a CD163-high/HLA-low phenotype upon co-culture with SCLC, and SCLC-N cells drove this transition more intensively than non-SCLC-N cells (Fig. 2F, G). Simultaneously, analysis of the co-culture supernatant using ELISA demonstrated that SCLC-N cells induce higher IL-10 and lower IL-12 secretion than non-SCLC-N cells (Fig. 2H). Collectively, these results indicate a potential role of SCLC-N cells in shaping a cytokine environment conducive to M2-like TAM polarization.

Accumulating evidence indicates that IGFBP2 can orchestrate macrophage polarization [18, 27–28], prompting us to investigate whether this phenomenon is conserved in SCLC. First, we performed immune profiling of the GSE60052 dataset using CIBERSORT. We found that IGFBP2 expression exhibited a positive correlation with both M0-TAM and M2-TAM signatures but inversely correlated with the M1-TAM signature (Fig. 3A). As expected, mIHC and qPCR analyses also demonstrated a positive correlation between IGFBP2 expression and the M2-TAM marker CD163 in SCLC specimens (Fig. 3B, C). Subsequently, recombinant human IGFBP2 protein (rhIGFBP2) was added to the M0-THP-1 macrophage culture system, and phenotypic shifts were tested. Following treatment with rhIGFBP2, macrophages exhibited increased CD163 and decreased HLA expression, together with higher IL-10 and lower IL-12 secretion, suggesting a shift toward an M2-like phenotype (Fig. 3D-F). To further examine the role of SCLC-N-secreted IGFBP2, IGFBP2 was knocked down in SCLC-N cells (H524 and H446) (Supplemental Fig. 3A-D), which were then co-cultured with M0-THP-1. The analyses showed a reduction in M2-associated markers in the macrophages (Fig. 3G-L), suggesting that SCLC-N-induced M2-like polarization of TAMs may depend on secreted IGFBP2.

IGFBP2 secreted by SCLC-N cells promotes macrophages PD-L1 expression in vitro and in vivo

M2-TAMs are known to contribute to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and promote tumor progression by facilitating immune evasion [21, 29], with PD-L1 identified as a key mediator of this process. In our study, bioinformatics analysis of the GSE60052 dataset revealed a positive correlation between PD-L1 expression and the Treg marker FOXP3 in SCLC tissues (Fig. 4A). These observations led us to hypothesize that M2-TAMs may participate in tumor immune evasion in SCLC, and that SCLC-N-secreted IGFBP2 could influence this mechanism.

IGFBP2 promotes the expression of PD-L1 in macrophages. (A) Correlation analysis showing the association between PD-L1 and FOXP3 transcriptional levels in SCLC tissues. (B-E) RT-qPCR and Western blotting were used to assess the impact of IGFBP2 on PD-L1 expression in M0-THP-1 and M0-Mc macrophages (Mc, macrophages derived from human peripheral blood monocytes). (F-H) RT-qPCR, Western blotting and IF assay were used to assess IGFBP protein accumulation in M0-PM (PM, mouse peritoneal macrophages). (I-J) RT-qPCR and Western blotting showed that the SCLC-N subtype elicited a markedly greater up-regulation of PD-L1 in macrophages than any other subtype

To explore this, rhIGFBP2 was added to cultures of M0 macrophages. The results revealed that treatment with rhIGFBP2 promotes PD-L1 expression in macrophages, including M0-THP-1 derived from THP-1 cells and M0-Mc derived from PBMCs (Fig. 4B-E and Supplemental Fig. 4A, B). To validate this finding in vivo, we isolated mouse peritoneal macrophages (PM) after tail-vein injection of rmIGFBP2 (Supplemental Fig. 4C). RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses showed that PD-L1 expression was elevated in macrophages from rmIGFBP2-treated mice, and IF revealed increased IGFBP2 enrichment in these cells, suggesting a potential role of IGFBP2 in modulating PD-L1 levels in vivo (Fig. 4F-H). Subsequently, after co-culturing M0-THP-1 macrophages with SCLC cell lines, we found that both mRNA and protein levels of PD-L1 were higher in macrophages exposed to SCLC-N cells than in those exposed to non-SCLC-N cells (Fig. 4I, J).

To further investigate whether SCLC-N-mediated upregulation of PD-L1 in TAMs depends on its high IGFBP2 expression, we proceeded with experimental validation. M0-THP-1 macrophages were co-cultured with IGFBP2-knockdown or IGFBP2 overexpressed SCLC cells. The results indicated that IGFBP2 overexpression notably up-regulated PD-L1 expression in M0-THP-1, whereas IGFBP2 knockdown suppressed it (Fig. 5A-D). Meanwhile, intracellular IGFBP2 and PD-L1 levels in M0-THP-1 were assessed by IF. Co-culture with IGFBP2-overexpressing non-SCLC-N cells increased intracellular IGFBP2 and PD-L1 expression, whereas co-culture with IGFBP2-knockdown SCLC-N cells reduced both (Fig. 5E, F). These findings were similarly observed in M0-Mc macrophages under the same co-culture conditions (Supplemental Fig. 5). Taken together, these results suggest that SCLC-N cells may promote PD-L1 expression in TAMs through the secretion of IGFBP2.

SCLC-N-secreted IGFBP2 plays an immunosuppressive role in the TME by up-regulating PD-L1 expression in TAMs

Given the resistance of SCLC-N tumors to immunotherapy and their enrichment of M2-TAMs that promote immune evasion [30–31], we investigated macrophage-CD8 + T-cell interactions in SCLC. Exhausted CD8 + T cells are known to express high levels of immune checkpoints such as PD-1, LAG3, and TIM3 [32–33]. Consistently, mIHC of patient specimens revealed a positive correlation between M2-TAM density and infiltration of exhausted CD8 + T cells in SCLC (Fig. 6A). M0-THP-1 were pretreated with rhIGFBP2 for 48 h and then co-cultured with human peripheral blood lymphocytes for an additional 72 h. Flow cytometry revealed a significant reduction in the CD8+ T-cell fraction in the presence of TAMs treated with IGFBP2 (Fig. 6C). Similarly, M0-THP-1 were co-cultured with IGFBP2-knockdown SCLC-N cell lines or IGFBP2-overexpressing non-SCLC-N cell lines for 48 h and then with T lymphocytes for an additional 72 h. Flow cytometry showed that pretreatment with IGFBP2-overexpressing cells reduced the proportion of CD8 + T cells, whereas IGFBP2 knockdown in SCLC-N cells increased CD8 + T-cell levels (Fig. 6D-G). RT-qPCR analysis of co-cultured T cells revealed that IGFBP2 overexpression upregulated LAG3, TIM3 and PD-1 expression, whereas its knockdown reduced their levels, indicating that SCLC cell-derived IGFBP2 drives CD8+ T-cell exhaustion (Supplemental Fig. 6B-F). T-cell activation was further evaluated by measuring TNF-α and IFN-γ levels in co-culture supernatants. IGFBP2 overexpressed SCLC cells significantly reduced, whereas IGFBP2 knockdown increased, the secretion of TNF-α and IFN-γ by T lymphocytes, indicating higher IGFBP2 expression in SCLC cells is associated with impaired T-cell activation (Supplemental Fig. 6G-P).

IGFBP2 orchestrates macrophage-dependent suppression of CD8 + T-Cell function in SCLC. (A) M2-TAMs were positively correlated with exhausted T cells in SCLC. (B) Description of co-culture system. (C-G)The proportion of CD8+ T cells was assessed by flow cytometry after T cells were co-cultured with M0-THP-1 cells that had been pre-cultured with the indicated SCLC cells

To further define the role of PD-L1 in SCLC-N-mediated T-cell suppression, rescue experiments were performed. T lymphocytes were co-cultured with preconditioned M0-THP-1 under rhIGFBP2 treatment or in co-culture with IGFBP2-high SCLC cells, in the presence of a PD-L1 inhibitor. Flow cytometric analysis showed that PD-L1 blockade fully restored CD8 + cytotoxic T-cell infiltration that had been suppressed by elevated IGFBP2 (Fig. 7B-F). RT-qPCR revealed that PD-L1 blockade down-regulated LAG3, TIM3, and PD-1 expression in T cells (Supplemental Fig. 7B-F). ELISA results further demonstrated that the PD-L1 inhibitor reversed IGFBP2-mediated reductions in TNF-α and IFN-γ secretion, confirming that macrophage PD-L1 suppresses T-cell activation (Supplemental Fig. 7G-P).

(B-F) Flow cytometry confirmed that PD-L1 blockade can reverse the suppression of CD8 + T-cell infiltration caused by high IGFBP2 expression in SCLC cells. (G) Tumor size of mice in different treatment groups. (H) Growth curves of tumors from mice with different treatments

To validate the immunomodulatory role of IGFBP2, we established a subcutaneous syngeneic tumor model using Lewis lung carcinoma cells. Mice injected with rmIGFBP2 exhibited the largest and fastest-growing tumors, whereas PD-L1 blockade produced the smallest tumors. Co-administration of rmIGFBP2 and the PD-L1 inhibitor yielded tumor volumes intermediate between the PD-L1-inhibitor and vehicle groups (Fig. 7G, H).

These results collectively indicate that SCLC-derived IGFBP2 may promote CD8 + T-cell exhaustion and suppress T-cell activation by inducing PD-L1 expression in macrophages.

Transport of IGFBP2 into the nucleus is necessary to promote the expression of PD-L1 in TAMs

IGFBP2 and IGF1 are central regulators of the insulin-like growth factor (IGF) system [34]. Studies demonstrate that IGFBP2 enhances tumor-cell survival and proliferation by enhancing IGF1-IGF1R engagement and activating PI3K signal transduction [35]. To explore how IGFBP2 modulates PD-L1 in TAMs, we treated M0-THP-1 with exogenous IGF1 and rhIGFBP2 and assessed IGF1R-PI3K-AKT pathway activation by WB. The results showed that IGF1 rapidly phosphorylated IGF1R and AKT in THP-1 cells, whereas IGFBP2 reversed the activation (Fig. 8A), indicating that IGFBP2 modulates PD-L1 in macrophages independently of the IGF axis and PI3K-AKT pathway.

The mechanism of IGFBP2 regulating PD-L1 expression in macrophages. (A) Western blotting showed that IGFBP2 modulates PD-L1 in macrophages independently of the IGF axis and PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. (B-C) NLS structure domain and cell localization diagram. (D-E) RT-qPCR analysis of IGFBP2 and PD-L1 expression in cells after transfection. (F) Western blot showing IGFBP2 and PD-L1 protein levels following transfection. (G) IF showing IGFBP2 and PD-L1 expression in M0-THP-1 cells after transfection

A classical NLS within IGFBP2 directs its nuclear translocation, allowing the protein to exert functions independently of IGF/IGFR signaling [36–37]. In melanoma, this nuclear import is the dominant mechanism underlying PD-L1 up-regulation [18]. Therefore, we constructed wild-type (IGFBP2-WT) and NLS-mutant (IGFBP2-NLSmut) overexpression plasmids (Fig. 8B, C) and transfected them into M0-THP-1 cells respectively. RT-qPCR and WB confirmed that PD-L1 expression was significantly upregulated in macrophages overexpressing IGFBP2-WT, whereas PD-L1 levels showed no significant change in the IGFBP2-NLSmut group (Fig. 8E-F). Consistently, IF revealed that IGFBP2-NLSmut was largely excluded from the nucleus, and that PD-L1 expression remained low, in contrast to IGFBP2-WT, which localized to the nucleus and induced PD-L1 upregulation (Fig. 8G).

Taken together, these findings indicate that SCLC-N may promote PD-L1 upregulation in macrophages primarily through nuclear translocation of IGFBP2, thereby contributing to an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

NEUROD1 exhibits cell type-specific regulatory patterns on IGFBP2

Transcription factors regulate gene expression primarily through sequence-specific binding to cis-regulatory elements, including core promoters and distal enhancers, and through the recruitment of co-activators and transcriptional complexes [38–39]. To elucidate the mechanism by which NEUROD1 regulates IGFBP2 expression in SCLC, we first performed in silico promoter analysis using the JASPAR 2026 database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/), which identified multiple putative NEUROD1 binding sites within the IGFBP2 promoter region (Supplemental Fig. 8A), suggesting potential direct transcriptional regulation. Consistently, analysis of NEUROD1 ChIP-seq data from the GSE69394 dataset demonstrated robust NEUROD1 enrichment across the IGFBP2 locus in SCLC-N cell lines H524 and H82. In H82 cells, 45.1% of the analyzed genomic windows exhibited significant enrichment, with nine prominent binding peaks localized within the core promoter region (Supplemental Fig. 8B). Similarly, in H524 cells, 33.1% of the windows showed significant enrichment, and six distinct NEUROD1 binding signals were detected within the core promoter region (Supplemental Fig. 8C). Further analysis of the top 10 highest-confidence NEUROD1 binding regions revealed that the majority contained canonical E-box motifs (Supplementary Tables 4–6). Together with the robust enrichment observed in the ChIP-seq data, these findings support direct and sequence-specific binding of NEUROD1 to the IGFBP2 promoter in SCLC-N cells. To assess whether NEUROD1-mediated regulation of IGFBP2 is cell type-specific, we analyzed NEUROD1 CUT&Tag-seq data from the GSE277353 dataset in non-SCLC-N cell lines. As shown in Supplemental Fig. 8D, the Lu134A cell line exhibited a markedly lower proportion of specific NEUROD1 binding (7.2%) compared with SCLC-N cell lines, and no significant binding signals were detected within the IGFBP2 core promoter region. Collectively, these results indicate two distinct regulatory modes of NEUROD1 on IGFBP2: direct, high-affinity binding to the proximal promoter in SCLC-N cells, and a potentially indirect mode of regulation via distal regulatory elements in non-SCLC-N cells.

Discussion

Because SCLC is characterized by rapid progression, high invasiveness, and a strong propensity for metastasis, the majority of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage [40]. According to statistics, the 5-year overall survival rate is merely 10–13% for limited-stage SCLC and only 1–2% for extensive-stage disease [41–42]. Although immune-checkpoint inhibitors have been incorporated into standard regimens for extensive-stage SCLC, their efficacy remains unsatisfactory. The data demonstrate that the introduction of immunotherapy has conferred only a modest survival benefit, prolonging the median overall survival of patients with SCLC by approximately two months [5, 43]. Defining specific biomarkers that distinguish the different subtypes of SCLC is an essential prerequisite for implementing treatment stratification and innovative precision-medicine strategies. Studies have shown that SCLC-N shows less immune infiltration and greater T cell dysfunction than non-SCLC-N [22]. Improved stratification of patients with limited immunotherapy responsiveness and a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying immune tolerance may facilitate the development of effective sensitization strategies and expand the population eligible for immunotherapy. In contrast, evidence supporting such combination strategies in SCLC remains limited. In this context, the present study investigates the molecular basis of immune tolerance in the SCLC-N subtype and identifies potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets to inform rational combination strategies and improve patient outcomes.

IGFBP2 is commonly upregulated in multiple malignancies and is associated with therapeutic response and clinical outcomes, highlighting its potential as a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target. Notably, IGFBP2 may modulate the tumor immune microenvironment by interacting with M2-TAMs, which play a critical role in promoting tumor progression [17–18]. Preliminary studies have shown that M2-TAMs can impair CD8 + T-cell cytotoxicity by secreting immunosuppressive factors (e.g., PD-L1, IL-10) [44–45]. Elucidating the interplay between IGFBP2 and M2-TAMs, and its effects on CD8 + T-cell function, may therefore identify novel strategies to enhance immunotherapeutic efficacy in cancer.

In our present study, we found that IGFBP2 is markedly upregulated in the SCLC-N subtype and positively correlates with NEUROD1 expression, with both factors associated with poor prognosis in SCLC patients. Functional experiments revealed that high IGFBP2 levels in SCLC-N cells may drive TAM polarization toward an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype and enhance PD-L1 expression, potentially leading to CD8 + T-cell exhaustion in a macrophage-dependent manner. The partial reversal of T-cell dysfunction by PD-L1 blockade indicates that IGFBP2-mediated immune suppression may involve PD-L1 signaling. These findings suggest that IGFBP2 could act as a key regulator of the tumor immune microenvironment in SCLC-N, modulating both innate and adaptive immune components. Moreover, they raise the possibility that targeting IGFBP2, alone or in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors, may provide a strategy to overcome immune evasion and enhance immunotherapeutic efficacy. Future studies exploring the upstream regulation of IGFBP2, its interactions with TAMs, and its broader effects on immune cell dynamics may further clarify its role as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the clinical cohort was not stratified according to SCLC molecular subtypes, which may limit the generalizability of subtype-specific conclusions derived from cell line-based models. Second, both the analyses of publicly available datasets and the size of the clinical cohort warrant further expansion to more robustly validate our findings. Third, the direct binding of NEUROD1 to the IGFBP2 promoter and its transcriptional regulatory function have not yet been experimentally confirmed. Fourth, the precise molecular mechanisms by which IGFBP2 regulates macrophage PD-L1 expression, as well as the downstream signaling pathways involved, remain incompletely elucidated. Finally, the potential role of IGFBP2 in tumor microenvironment remodeling through direct interactions with other immune cell populations, including T cells and dendritic cells, requires further investigation. Addressing these limitations will be essential to strengthen the robustness of our conclusions and to more precisely define the molecular and immunoregulatory functions of IGFBP2 in SCLC. Collectively, these unresolved questions outline important directions for future studies aimed at dissecting IGFBP2-mediated immune regulation within the SCLC microenvironment at greater mechanistic depth.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that IGFBP2 secreted by SCLC-N cells contributes to an immunosuppressive microenvironment by increasing PD-L1 expression in TAMs. Clinically, IGFBP2 may serve as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response in SCLC-N and represents a potential target for combination strategies integrating tumor-intrinsic modulation with immune checkpoint inhibition, providing a rationale for more precise, subtype-specific therapeutic approaches.
