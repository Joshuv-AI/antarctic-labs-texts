Primary Total Hip Arthroplasty With Concomitant Gluteus Medius Repair: Mid-term Outcomes With Nested Propensity-Matched Benchmark Control
Authors: Roger Quesada-Jimenez, Yasemin E Kingham, Tyler R McCarroll, Jessica C Keane, Ady H Kahana-Rojkind, Benjamin G Domb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients who undergo total hip arthroplasty (THA) without addressing gluteus medius (GM) pathology tend to experience inferior outcomes. The study aims to evaluate mid-term outcomes in patients who underwent a primary THA with concomitant GM tear repair, as compared to a benchmark control group of primary THA patients that did not have GM pathology.MethodsData were retrospectively analyzed from patients who underwent a primary THA with a concomitant GM repair between 2015 and 2018. Included patients completed preoperative and minimum 5-year questionnaires for Harris Hip Score, visual analog scale, Veteran's Rand 12-item (VR-12) health survey, 12-item Short Form (SF-12) heath surveys, Forgotten Joint Score, and the Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement. The GM cohort was matched in a 1:3 ratio based on age at surgery, body mass index, sex, approach, and robotic-assisted surgery to a control group of primary THAs without GM pathology. Thresholds for functional outcomes were included.ResultsForty patients who underwent primary THA with GM repair were included successfully match to 120 control patients. There were significant improvements in the reported Harris Hip Score, visual analog scale, VR-12 Physical, VR-12 Mental, SF-12 Physical, and SF-12 Mental scores in the study group. Despite their gluteus medius pathology, with concomitant repair, the study group achieved patient-reported outcomes improvements and rates of minimal clinically important difference and patient acceptable symptom state that were comparable to the control group.ConclusionsTHA with concomitant GM repair yielded significant improvement in functional status and clinical outcomes, which compared favorably to a benchmark control group without GM tears. Surgeons may consider addressing GM tears during a primary THA.Level of EvidenceLevel III.Keywords: Hip, Arthroplasty, Total hip arthroplasty, Gluteus medius

Introduction

Pain experienced over the lateral aspect of the hip is commonly referred to as greater trochanteric pain syndrome [[1], [2], [3]]. This syndrome encompasses a wide spectrum of pain sources, including intra-articular, extra-articular, and neurological-related pathologies. Patients typically present with symptoms such as lateral hip pain, limping, tenderness over the greater trochanter, and sometimes weakness, often demonstrated as a positive Trendelenburg gait [2,4].

Greater trochanteric pain syndrome is a prevalent regional pain syndrome and is frequently misdiagnosed as trochanteric bursitis. However, the utilization of diagnostic imaging techniques, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasound, as diagnostic and therapeutic tools have enhanced the recognition of hip abductor pathologies as underlying causes of hip pain. Improved recognition combined with the aging population will likely result in an overall increased incidence of gluteal tendinopathy. This pathology has been noted in a substantial proportion of patients with osteoarthritis undergoing total hip arthroplasty (THA), impacting approximately 20% to 25% of this patient population [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Women in particular are affected at a higher incidence [9]. It has been established that patients undergoing primary THAs without addressing concomitant gluteus medius (GM) tear experience inferior short-term patient-reported outcomes (PROs) [5,10]. Addressing underlying GM pathology, even if initially asymptomatic, could contribute to improved outcomes and overall patient satisfaction following THA [11].

The primary purpose of this study was to report PROs at a minimum of 5-year follow-up for patients diagnosed with osteoarthritis and a GM tear who underwent primary THA, as well as to compare these outcomes with a benchmark propensity-matched control group who underwent a primary THA without GM pathology.

Material and methods

Patient selection criteria

Intraoperative and demographic data were prospectively collected and retrospectively analyzed from all patients who received a primary THA between January 2015 and September 2018. Patients were considered eligible if they received a primary THA with a GM repair, had completed at least one preoperative and minimum 5-year questionnaires for Harris Hip Score (HHS), visual analog scale (VAS) for pain, Veteran's Rand 12-item (VR-12), and 12-item Short Form (SF-12), patient satisfaction, Forgotten Joint Score (FJS), and Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (HOOS-JR). Patients were excluded from the study if they were unwilling to participate in the study, had worker’s compensation claims, or had previous ipsilateral hip surgery. Intraoperative data were prospectively collected from the operative reports and retrospectively analyzed.

Participation in the American Hip Institute Hip Replacement Registry

All participants took part in the American Hip Institute Hip Replacement Registry. Some of the data included in this study may have been reported in other studies; however, this current study represents a unique analysis. All data collection and reporting received institutional review board approval (IRB#20242189).

Surgical indications

Patients were considered for THA with GM repair following a comprehensive clinical history, physical examination, and radiographic analysis to diagnose advanced hip osteoarthritis and a symptomatic GM tear. Positive indications for GM pathology include lateral hip tenderness upon palpation, abductor weakness, positive results on a modified resisted internal rotation test, or observable findings during a Trendelenburg gait test [2,4,10,11]. In cases where there was a high level of suspicion for GM tendinopathy that was not entirely confirmed by the physical examination, an MRI was recommended to assess the GM tendon [[12], [13], [14]]. Additionally, ultrasound-guided diagnostic injections were performed to aid in the diagnostic process [15,16]. Intraoperative assessment was employed to categorize the severity of the GM pathology. Patients with GM pathology were graded based on the status of the GM tendon: tendinosis, low-grade partial-thickness tear (<50%), high-grade partial-thickness tear (>50%), or full-thickness tear [17].

All patients underwent a minimum of 3 months of unsuccessful conservative treatment focus on their hip osteoarthritis before surgical consideration, incorporating various interventions such as activity modification, physical therapy, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and ultrasound-guided intra-articular and peritrochanteric corticosteroid injections

Surgical technique

THA procedures were conducted using either the direct anterior (DAA) or posterior approach (PA) in a standardized manner [18]. The assessment of the GM tendon tear took place before the neck cut to ascertain the required type of repair. For the DAA, the operative leg was placed in 20° abduction and 10-15° of internal rotation and retracting laterally the iliotibial band for adequate visualization of the GM tendon. In PA cases, the GM was assessed initially then carefully retracted proximally during the approach. In both cases, the repair was performed after the THA implants were positioned. THAs were performed using press-fit implants and a ceramic head. Partial-thickness GM tears graded as less than 50% were repaired using the suture-staple technique, while tears greater than 50% were repaired with the transtendinous repair technique, using the previously described techniques [18,19].

GM repair technique

Suture-staple GM repair technique

After debriding the GM tendon, an assessment of the lateral aspect of the greater trochanter of the femur was carried out. To improve the biologic response, a micropuncture marrow stimulation technique was applied. Knotless anchors (Arthrex, Inc., Naples, FL) were utilized and placed both in the anterior and posterior regions of the lateral facet of the greater trochanter to reattach the partial tear. This approach resulted in the formation of a horizontal mattress suture-staple configuration [18] (Fig. 1).

Transtendinous GM repair technique

A lengthwise incision was made from the midsection to the footprint of the GM tendon. The layer of nonviable tendon tissue beneath was excised. The tendon footprint on the lateral facet was debrided, and a minimum of 2 double-loaded suture anchors were introduced through the tendon split onto the footprint. One end of each suture was threaded through the anterior and posterior tendon leaflets and secured in a horizontal mattress style (Fig. 2) [20].

Postoperative rehabilitation

Patients who underwent suture-staple technique procedures for THA with GM repair were instructed to utilize crutches or a walker while remaining weight bearing as tolerated with limitation to active abduction of 20°. Transtendinous technique procedure recipients were placed in a Donjoy VERSAROM Hip Brace (Enovis, Wilmington, DE) restricting abduction and instructed to partial weight-bearing (20 lbs.) with crutches or a walker for 6 weeks. Patients followed a standard rehabilitative protocol, which included home physical therapy and nursing care. After 7 to 14 days of home care, patients progressed to 6 to 8 weeks of outpatient physical therapy to enhance range of motion and strength. Postoperative follow-up appointments were scheduled at the 2-week, 4-month, and annual intervals.

Surgical outcomes tools

Patients were required to fill out questionnaires containing PROs both before and after the surgery, specifically at 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, and subsequently on an annual basis. The PROs recorded include HHS, the FJS, VAS, and patient satisfaction [19]. Patients completed these questionnaires either during in-person clinic visits, through secure email channels, or via telephone interviews.

Clinical improvement was evaluated using the Patient Acceptable Symptom State (PASS) and the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for HHS, HOOS-JR, and FJS. Postoperative outcomes scores were compared to values defined in previous literature, which were found to be 94, 76.7, and 66.48, for HHS, HOOS-JR, and FJS respectfully. Additionally, the MCID for HHS and VAS was calculated using the distribution method and was found to be 12.41 and 8.76 respectively [[21], [22], [23]].

Propensity matching

Patients who received a primary THA with GM repair were propensity match in a 1:3 ratio to a benchmark control group of patients who received a primary THA without GM pathology based on age at surgery, body mass index (BMI), sex, surgical approach, and laterality.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in Excel with the Real Statistics add-in package. Propensity score matching was done using R-Studio (version 2023.06.2). Normality and variance were measured using the Shapiro–Wilk’s test and F-test, respectively. The two-tailed paired t-test, or its nonparametric equivalent, were used for all continuous data analyses. The experimental P value was set to 0.05 to determine statistical significance. A priori power analysis was performed to determine the minimum sample size in the 2 matched groups to obtain a minimum of 80% power using a 1:1 matching ratio. The anticipated effect size of modified HHS (Cohen’s d) set to 1.5 resulted in the minimum total sample size being 46 patients with 23 patients in each group.

Results

A total of 52 patients were eligible, of which 8 patients were unwilling to participate, 1 patient had active worker’s compensation claims at the time of surgery, and 3 patients were lost to follow-up, leaving 40 (93%) patients included in the study successfully match to 120 control cases. Of the GM repair group 38 patients were women and had a mean age at surgery of 62.23 ± 8.59 years and mean BMI of 30.30 ± 5.28 kg/m2. Of the 40 hips, 22 (55%) were right hips. (Table 1).

Demographics

No statistically significant differences were found for BMI, age at surgery, sex, laterality, and surgical approach between the matched groups. All demographic data can be found in Table 1.

Preoperative and postoperative evaluation

Preoperative evaluation of the GM repair group revealed that all patients reported lateral-side pain. Among them, 6 (15%) patients had a normal gait, 4 (10%) patients exhibited an antalgic gait, and 30 (75%) patients presented with a Trendelenburg gait. Postoperatively, 38 (95%) patients achieved a normal gait, while 2 (5%) patients continued to exhibit a Trendelenburg gait. Furthermore, 19 (47.5%) patients underwent an ultrasound-guided diagnostic therapeutic injection (steroid and lidocaine) into the peritrochanteric region, all of whom showed a positive response. Additionally, 12 (30%) patients had MRI confirmation before surgery, revealing 6 (50%) high-grade partial-thickness tears, 4 (33.3%) full-thickness tears, and 2 (16.7%) partial-thickness tears. Furthermore, fatty infiltration was observed in 8 patients (66.6%) at grade 1, while the remaining 4 patients (44.4%) exhibited grade 2 fatty infiltration [24].

Intraoperative data

Of the 40 patients within the cohort that underwent a GM repair during a primary THA, 12 (30%) were diagnosed with a low-grade partial thickness GM tear intraoperatively, 14 (35%) had a high-grade partial thickness tear, and the remaining 14 (35%) had a complete tear. The posterior approach was utilized for 23 patients (57.5%), while 17 patients (42.5%) were approached anteriorly. Complete intraoperative data can be found in Table 2.

Complications

Only 1 patient from the study group had a deep venous thrombosis in their left leg, which was treated with anticoagulation without further event.

Discussion

The primary finding of the current study was that patients who underwent THA with GM repair demonstrated significant improvement in HHS, VAS, VR-12 Physical, and SF-12 Physical (P < .05) at a minimum of 5-year follow-up. Furthermore, when comparing this group to a benchmark propensity-matched control group that underwent a primary THA without GM pathology, we observed similar improvement and postoperative scores for all PROs. The MCID and PASS achievement rates were also comparable between the 2 propensity-matched groups.

Previous studies have provided evidence supporting the surgical repair of GM tears as a second stage procedure after previous THA. Rajkumar et al conducted a study with 11 patients who underwent a second-stage GM repair surgery with an average of 16 months between the primary THA and secondary GM repair surgery [25] They observed a notable improvement in the HHS, with scores increasing from an average of 77.4 (range, 55 to 87) to 86.97 (range, 79 to 96). In a similar study, Lübbeke et al also reported positive results of GM repair, conducted at an average of 19 months following primary THA in symptomatic patients. The authors noted a significant improvement in the HHS at the latest follow-up [26] Additionally, 12 out of 19 patients improved their previous limp gait. These previous findings align with our findings that treating GM tears improves postoperative outcomes.

In addition to previous literature supporting the surgical treatment of GM tears to improve PROs, there is a growing body of evidence that supports addressing GM pathology during the same surgical procedure to reduce the morbidity associated with a second surgical intervention. In a recent publication, Maldonado et al presented the results of a cohort of 30 patients (97% women) who underwent concomitant primary THA and GM repairs [18] The study demonstrated significant improvements in PROs at a short-term follow-up (range, 19 to 24 months), with an average HHS of 75.8 ± 14.8, a VAS score of 1.9 ± 1.7, and a mean patient satisfaction rating of 8.1 ± 2.3. Our results build upon the results of this previous study by demonstrating that patients continue to experience favorable improvement at their mid-term follow-up.

In our cohort, 95% of the patients were female, a pattern consistent with previous reports, and all presented with lateral pain to some degree. Notably, 30 patients (75%) exhibited weakness and a Trendelenburg gait with 38 (95%) of patients showing a normal gait postoperative. For those with inconclusive findings on physical examination, our treatment approach aligns with the findings of Rosinsky et al [27] Their study focused on a cohort of 50 patients with asymptomatic GM tears, matched to a control group without abductor pathology that underwent primary THA. Rosinsky’s study highlighted that even in asymptomatic patients, those with GM tears experienced inferior 2-year PROs compared to the matched group without such pathology. Recently, Xing et al reported a cohort of 56 patients with asymptomatic gluteal tendinosis that underwent primary THA without addressing the gluteal pathology [28]. They achieved comparable outcomes to a matched control group that did not have gluteal pathology. However, in contrast to our cohort’s findings, their intraoperative data revealed tendinitis without evidence of partial or complete tears.

Strengths

The current study aims to address the paucity of literature regarding the outcomes of concomitant GM repair and primary THA by reporting the minimum 5-year outcomes with a propensity-matched benchmark control group. The inclusion of multiple validated functional hip outcome scores also enhanced the reliability of the results. Finally, to provide clinical context of the mean PROs, we reported the proportion of patients who achieved MCID for modified HHS, HHS and VAS.

Limitations

There are limitations to the current study that must be acknowledged. The retrospective design introduces potential bias. However, this bias was limited because of the prospective collection of all data. Furthermore, all the procedures were conducted by a single institution which limits the generalizability of the results. The decision to undergo primary THA via either the DAA or PA approach was based on the senior author's expertise and patient counseling, which may have introduced bias. Finally while we observed comparable outcomes when compared to the control group, this improvement is attributed to the overall treatment approach and cannot be solely linked to the GM repair. Although our study compares the outcomes of THA patients with GM pathology to a benchmark control group, we were unable to directly compare patients with GM pathology who did and did not receive a GM repair, as addressing GM pathology during primary THA is the standard of care at this institution.

Conclusions

GM tears are common among patients undergoing primary THA. Concomitant THA and GM repair yielded significant improvement in functional status and clinical outcomes, which compared favorably to a benchmark control group without GM tears. Given that THA patients with unrepaired GM tears have previously been associated with inferior outcomes and that the present study showed positive outcomes with concomitant GM repair and THA, surgeons may consider addressing GM tears during a primary THA.
