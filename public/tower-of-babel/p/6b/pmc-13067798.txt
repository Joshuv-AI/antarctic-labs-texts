Whole‐Genome Bisulfite Sequencing Identifies Blood‐Based DNA Methylation Biomarker for Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Junsheng Zhao, Sijia Shen, Junjie Zhang, Ying Xu, Jing Peng, Hainv Gao, Lanjuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFunctional DNA methylation abnormalities are a hallmark of human cancers and may be a promising biomarker for their early diagnosis. Moreover, the largest methylation differences can improve the sensitivity of noninvasive diagnoses of solid tumors. We combined whole‐genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) and mRNA‐seq data from 33 paired hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and adjacent tissues to identify methylation markers that could be used for noninvasive diagnosis in blood samples. Methylation markers were selected according to the following criteria: differentially methylated regions (DMR) located in the promoter region with large differences in methylation (Δβ > 0.3) and inverse correlation with matched gene expression (cor < −0.3). Cell‐free DNA (cfDNA) from 48 patients with HCC and 24 normal participants was used to verify the performance of meTSPYL5 using qMSP. Integrated WGBS and transcriptomic data analysis identified eight target promoter hyper‐DMRs. After confirming the WGBS profiles of genes in peripheral blood mononuclear cells, meTSPYL5 was selected to further verify the plasma cfDNA samples by qMSP. The results of plasma validation showed that the methylation detection of meTSPYL5 was sensitive for identifying HCC, with a sensitivity and specificity of 85.4% and 100%, respectively. Pan‐cancer analysis found that the methylation level of TSPYL5 was elevated in multiple cancer types, indicating that it lacks cancer‐type specificity; however, this result does not affect its application value in monitoring high‐risk populations of HCC. By analyzing and integrating all available high‐throughput epigenomic and transcriptomic data from human HCC tissues, we identified eight regions as potential diagnostic biomarkers for HCC. Integrative analyses of epigenomic and transcriptomic data provide an efficient method to identify diagnostic biomarkers for human cancers. Methylated TSPYL5 in plasma is a promising biomarker for the detection and screening of HCC.Keywords: biomarker, cell‐free DNA, hepatocellular carcinoma, TSPYL5, whole‐genome bisulfite sequencing

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common cancer and the cause of 600 thousand deaths worldwide per year and rising [1, 2]. HCC usually occurs in patients with cirrhosis and is caused by long‐term liver inflammation and repair. In most cases, hepatitis is caused by chronic viral (mainly hepatitis B and C virus) infection or alcoholic/nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Owing to the lack of obvious symptoms in the early stages, most patients with HCC are already in the advanced stages by the time they receive a definitive diagnosis. Although the survival time of patients with intermediate‐ and early‐stage liver cancer has substantially improved in the past decade, the prognosis of those with advanced‐stage remains very poor. With limited therapeutic interventions, the median survival time is frequently only 1–2 years, and the 5‐year survival rate is below 16% [10, 11, 12, 13]. As a result, early detection of HCC is critical to timely treatment and to reduce the high mortality rate.

The current guidelines recommend ultrasound screening every 6 months for patients at a high risk of HCC. However, the sensitivity of ultrasonography and the high technical and experience requirements of the operator limit its detection rate for early‐stage liver cancer (sensitivity, 51%; specificity, 91%). Due to its low sensitivity and positive predictive value, the clinical value of alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP) for the screening of HCC remains controversial [14]. Although magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography have higher sensitivity for early HCC, they have the disadvantages of high cost and invasiveness and fail to satisfy the demand for HCC mass screening. Despite many noninvasive biomarkers have been studied, there are few emerged as a bona fide diagnosis or treatment target in HCC so far. It stems from the biological complexity of the disease, the challenging clinical context of cirrhosis, and the high bar for proving utility over or in addition to existing standards. New robust, noninvasive methods are urgently needed to improve the early diagnosis of HCC diagnosis [15].

Changes in DNA methylation levels are one of the main features of many cancers, including HCC [16, 17, 18, 19]. Hypermethylation in gene promoter regions is typically related to gene silencing and has a consistent modification status in the CpG island (CGI) [20, 21]. Moss et al. [22] found that the main sources of plasma cell‐free DNA (cfDNA) in healthy individuals were leukocytes (55%), erythroid progenitor cells (30%), vascular endothelial cells (10%), and hepatocytes (1%). The presence of a small amount of circulating tumor DNA offers great potential for the clinical application of noninvasive tumor diagnosis or early screening [23, 24, 25]. With its chemical and biological stability, detecting aberrant DNA methylation in serum or plasma provides a novel and promising strategy [26, 27, 28]. Array‐based genome‐wide methylation studies have been conducted, and some diagnostic models have been constructed for HCC detection, which include the methylation level (β) of some CpG sites [29]. For example, Hlady et al. [30] based on cfDNA methylation array profiles, identified a panel of five CpGs for HCC identification in a small cohort. However, methylation array covers only a small fraction of the genome‐wide methylome, and whole‐genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) is unequivocally superior to microarrays. Its comprehensive, unbiased nature allows researchers to paint a complete picture of the cancer methylome, uncovering markers in previously unexplored genomic territories, and providing integrated genomic and epigenomic insights [31].

In the present study, WGBS coupled with transcriptome profiling of tumors and paired adjacent tissues from patients with HCC was performed to identify a specific subset of genes aberrantly hyper‐methylated with significant silencing of gene expression. Subsequently, we compared the methylation levels of the target regions in normal leukocytes, and identified a specific region of TSPYL5 promoter hypermethylation. Finally, we determined the clinical performance of the cfDNA‐based meTSPYL5 quantitative methylation‐specific PCR (qMSP) test for detecting HCC.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study was designed in two phases. In phase I, using WGBS and gene expression profiles in multiple data sets, candidate DNA methylation markers were selected. In phase II, the selected marker was validated by independent plasma samples from patients with HCC and healthy controls. An overview of the workflow is illustrated in Figure 1.

Human Participants and Sample Acquisition

The samples used in this analysis were derived from four cohorts. Thirty‐three patients with HCC were recruited for the biomarker discovery cohort, and paired HCC and peritumoral normal liver tissues were used in this study [32]. Seventy‐two peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples from cancer‐free individuals were used in this study; detailed descriptions can be found in our previous studies [33, 34, 35].

Biomarker identification was conducted using a combination of WGBS and publicly available array methylation data. Eight publicly available methylation data sets were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and NCBI GEO repositories, which included six data sets based on the Illumina Infinium Human Methylation 450 BeadChip Array (TCGA‐LIHC, GSE54503 [36], GSE56588 [37], GSE89852 [38], GSE99036 [39], and GSE113017 [40]) and two data sets based on the Illumina Infinium Human Methylation 27 BeadChip Array (GSE37988 [41] and GSE57956 [42]) (see Supporting Information S2: Additional File 2: Table S1).

Peripheral blood samples (5–10 mL) were collected in Cell‐Free DNA BCT tubes (Streck, La Vista, NE, USA) from 48 patients with HCC and 24 healthy controls from the Shulan (Hangzhou) Hospital. HCC diagnosis was performed based on radiographic criteria. Healthy controls were verified by a medical record review to be free of any cancer. Furthermore, to separate the plasma, all peripheral blood samples were centrifuged according to the instructions of Cell‐free DNA BCT (Streck). The Ethical Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, approved this study, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants according to the institutional guidelines. Clinicodemographic data are summarized in Supporting Information S2: Additional File 2: Table S2.

In addition, 643 non‐cancer and 7843 tumor samples (29 types of non‐hepatic solid tumors) from the TCGA repository were collected for cancer‐specific analysis of candidate methylation markers (see Supporting Information S2: Additional File 2: Table S3).

Data Processing

To explore methylation markers that can be used for noninvasive diagnosis of HCC, we identified all differentially methylated regions (DMRs) located in the promoter region by analyzing WGBS‐based DNA methylation profiles of the discovery cohort tissue samples. The resultant beta (β) values were used to identify changes in DNA methylation (Δβ) between the tumor and non‐tumor samples. The DMR identification pipelines are described in our previous study [32]. Briefly, a single Baumgartner‐Weiß‐Schindler (BWS) test was performed in three age groups, and three one‐sided p‐values were combined. The combined BWS test was determined by 2.0 × 10E‐08 time permutations [43]. Each CpG with a combined BWS p‐value of < 1.0E‐05 was identified as an age‐adjusted differentially methylated locus (DML), and one region of < 200 bp with more than 5 DMLs was defined as a DMR. DMRs that overlapped with at least one promoter (upstream 1500 bp and downstream 500 bp of the transcriptional start site of each transcript) in any sample were identified as promoter DMR (pDMR).

For transcriptome sequencing files of the discovery cohort, genes with an absolute value of log2 fold change (FC) > 0.5 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs) by comparing the normalized expression (transcripts per million reads) level of each gene.

Integrative analysis of methylation and expression was conducted by determining the intersection between pDMRs and DEGs to improve the reliability of cfDNA methylation markers. Moreover, to analyze the cis‐regulatory effects of pDMRs, the Spearman's correlation coefficient between the pDMR methylation level (β) and the matched gene expression level was calculated.

Our selection of target DMRs was based on the following stringent criteria: (1) methylation differences between tumor and adjacent tissues larger than 0.3 (hyper‐DMR); (2) DMR located in the promoter region (pDMR) and corresponding gene with significant expression level differences between tumor and adjacent tissues; and (3) the Spearman's correlation coefficient between DMR and corresponding DEG less than −0.3. We referred to the DMR that met these criteria as target hyper‐pDMR.

Evaluation of the Methylation Difference in Candidate Diagnostic Biomarkers in External Cohorts

Eight public methylation data profiles were normalized independently using the betaqn function of the “wateRmelon” package in R. To validate the methylation differences in the target hyper‐pDMR, the public data sets were used as test sets. The methylation levels (β) of the CpG sites located in the target hyper‐pDMR were extracted and compared between tumor and non‐tumor tissues.

Therefore, as “background methylation” of cfDNA DMR markers, the methylation of PBMC DNA in candidate regions should be hypomethylated to ensure the specificity of these markers. Moreover, considering the length limitation of qMSP, which detects assays of cfDNA methylation markers, we selected our markers from eight target hyper‐pDMRs based on the following criteria: (1) CpG sites within a 150‐bp window owing to cfDNA with a median length of < 200 bp; (2) four or more CpG sites in a single window, which would improve the specificity of the test and the detection limit of the assay; and (3) every CpG site consistently exhibited hyper‐methylation (β > 0.6) in tumor tissue and hypomethylation (β < 0.2) in the adjacent tissue and PBMC. Genomic illustration of CpG methylation levels in the eight target hyper‐pDMRs in the tumor tissue, adjacent tissue, and PBMC was performed using the “Gviz” package.

Isolation and Bisulfite Modification of Plasma cfDNA

cfDNA was isolated from plasma samples using the MagMAX cfDNA Isolation Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions and stored at −80°C until needed. In addition, the quality of the purified cfDNA was quantified using Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Bisulfite treatment was implemented on 10 ng cfDNA using the EZ DNA Methylation kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's protocol.

qMSP

qMSP was performed to detect the presence of methylated TSPYL5 and GNA14 in plasma cfDNA. Specific primers for the promoter regions of target genes were designed, and the ACTB gene was used as a reference for bisulfite‐converted cfDNA input. The sequences of specific primers and probes were given in Supporting Information S2: Additional File 2: Table S4, and were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and amplicon size was verified.

qMSP has been described in detail by Lehmann and Kreipe [44]. PCR amplification was performed on an ABI 7500DX instrument (Applied Biosystems). PCR was performed in a total reaction volume of 20 µL using TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), 2 nM of each primer, 1 nM of each probe, and 3 µL of the bisulfite‐modified template. The amplification consisted of 5 min at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95°C and 60 s at a primer annealing temperature of 60°C.

Because C T values for 91.6% of all healthy control participants were undetectable by real‐time PCR, we determined the sensitivity and specificity using a pre‐specified cutoff (C T = 38) for TSPYL5 methylation based on the presence or absence of detectable target methylation without quantitative methylation in this study. Based on the ROC sensitivity and specificity analysis, the best C T threshold is 38.08. The test sample was determined to be positive when the C T value was ≤ 38.

Statistical Analysis

Age‐adjusted analysis of CpG methylation level (β) differences between tumors and adjacent tissues was conducted using the combined BWS test and p‐value of < 1.0E‐5 was identified as a DML for subsequent DMR identification. For DEG identification, an absolute value of log2FC > 0.5 and FDR < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The correlation between gene expression and promoter methylation was estimated using Spearman's correlation analysis. Wilcoxon‐test and Fisher's exact test were performed to assess the relationship between cfDNA test and BCLC stage. Wilcoxon‐test and Fisher's exact test were two‐sided and considered statistically significant at p‐value < 0.05.

Results

Differential Analysis of Methylation and Expression

WGBS was used to investigate the methylation difference of genomic CpG sites between tumor and paracancerous tissues from the discovery cohort. Approximately 34% (9,867,700) of the CpG sites showed significant differences in methylation levels between cancerous and paracancerous tissues. Among them, 157,320 and 9,710,380 sites were hypermethylated and hypomethylated, respectively. The gene coordinate positions showed that the hypermethylated sites were mainly located in the gene promoter region, whereas the hypomethylated sites were primarily located in the gene body. Furthermore, 608,279 DMRs were identified, of which 6924 and 601,355 were hypermethylated in HCC (hyper‐DMR) and hypomethylated (hypo‐DMRs), respectively (Figure 2A).

Differential expression analysis was performed using the mRNA‐Seq data from the discovery cohort. The results revealed 11,672 genes with significant differences in expression levels (DEGs) (Figure 2B). Among them, there were 6912 and 4760 highly and lowly expressed genes, respectively.

Integrative Analysis of DMR and DEG

To select DMRs with silencing functional effects on the corresponding gene expression (cis‐regulation), intersection analysis between hyper‐pDMRs (Δβ > 0.3) and DEGs was conducted, followed by Spearmen's correlation analysis between methylation and expression of the same gene. Cis‐regulation is mediated by promoter methylation directly regulates the expression of matched genes [45]. It was found that 8 of the 29 intersecting hyper‐pDMRs were negatively correlated with the corresponding gene expression (Figure 2C and Table 1), which were called target hyper‐pDMRs.

Biomarker Selection

The genomic organization of the eight target hyper‐pDMRs is shown in Figure 4 and Supporting Information S1: Additional File 1: Figure S1. It has been demonstrated that PBMC DNA has low methylation levels in these regions. As shown in Figure 4, chr8:97277329_97278175 (matched TSPYL5 gene promoter region) and chr9:77647531_77648367 (matched GNA14 gene promoter region) were the two DMRs with the largest methylation differences (Δβ) between tumor and normal tissues, achieving 0.416 and 0.400, respectively. The β value in the tumor samples was stable above 0.6, while that in the normal tissue was stable below 0.2 (Figure 4a,b and Supporting Information S1: Additional File 1: Figure S1 and Figure S2). We designed the qMSP primers of GNA14 promoter region but we could not amplify sufficient PCR products; therefore, the methylation of the TSPYL5 promoter region (meTSPYL5) was used as a target for further plasma cfDNA validation.

No significant differences (Wilcoxon test, p > 0.05) were observed in TSPYL5 methylation levels between age groups, sex, tumor diameter, Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage, hepatitis B status, and serum AFP status of HCC in the discovery cohort (Figure 5). Cancer type specificity of meTSPYL5 was analyzed using pan‐cancer tissue methylation data from TCGA. Among cancer types, only cholangiocarcinoma and thymoma had hypomethylated meTSPYL5 compared with the corresponding normal tissues; however, the differences were not significant (Wilcoxon test, p > 0.05). In addition, lung adenocarcinoma, glioma, lung squamous carcinoma, uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma, bladder uroepithelial carcinoma, esophageal cancer, pancreatic cancer, renal papillary cell carcinoma, cervical cancer, breast cancer, colon cancer, renal clear cell carcinoma, thyroid cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and rectal adenocarcinoma were significantly hypermethylated compared with normal tissues (Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05). The median methylation level (β) of TSPYL5 was over 0.5 in lower‐grade glioma, diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma, and uterine carcinosarcoma (see Supporting Information S1: Additional File 1: Figure S3). These results suggest that meTAPYL5 may not be HCC cancer‐type specific. The methylation profile of TSPYL5 in plasma cfDNA in these cancers remains to be determined.

Clinical Performance of the meTSPYL5 qMSP Test for Detecting HCC in Plasma cfDNA

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of the meTSPYL5 qMSP assay in detecting HCC using plasma cfDNA, we tested 72 valid samples, including 48 HCCs and 24 healthy participants. The detection results of aberrant TSPYL5 methylation in plasma cfDNA samples from patients are summarized in Supporting Information S2: Additional File 2: Table S5. Area under ROC curve (AUC) value of the CT value was 0.921 (95% CI 0.859–0.969) for HCC detection (Supporting Information S1: Additional File 1: Figure S4). The CT threshold of 38 was selected to dichotomize the result of qMSP for methylated TSPYL5 mainly to maximize sensitivity and minimize false positive rate. For the 48 patients with HCC, the meTSPYL5 qMSP test in plasma cfDNA had an overall sensitivity of 85.4% (41/48). Compared to BCLC‐stage A, the earlier BCLC‐stage 0 had a lower CT value, indicting higher methylation (Wilcoxon test p‐value = 0.047, Supporting Information S2: Additional File 2: Table S5 and Supporting Information S1: Additional file 1: Figure S5a). Among the seven patients with HCC with false‐negative plasma meTSPYL5 test results, 3, 2, 1, and 1 were from patients with BCLC‐stage 0, A, B, and C, respectively (Supporting Information S1: Additional File 1: Figure S5b). Among the seven patients, excluding one case without AFP test results, the other six had positive AFP results. This result suggests that combining AFP and meTSPYL5 methylation assays can improve diagnostic sensitivity. However, serum AFP may increase the false positive rate [46]. The specificity of the 24 participants without cancer was 100% (24/24). Therefore, meTSPYL5 is a good candidate for the noninvasive detection of HCC.

Subsequently, we listed the previously published noninvasive detection methods. In the cfDNA validation cohort, of the 44 patients with HCC who underwent serum AFP testing, only 31 were detected as positive, with a sensitivity of 70.5% (Supporting Information S1: Additional File 1: Figure S6a). In contrast, meTSPYL5 methylation has better sensitivity for HCC detection. When AFP and meTSPYL5 methylation assays were combined, the detection sensitivity reached 100%. Correlation analysis in the discovery cohort showed that the methylation level of TSPYL5 had a very weak correlation with the expression level of AFP (R = 0.28, p = 0.021). Together with the results presented in Figure 5, this result further demonstrates the mutual independence between TSPYL5 methylation and AFP levels. Compared with other studies, the diagnostic performance of meTSPYL5 methylation was not the best (Table 2).

Discussion

The epigenetic biomarkers of HCC have been extensively studied based on methylation arrays; however, none have been commercialized to date. Here, we performed an integrated epigenomic‐transcriptomic study on tissue samples collected from 33 patients with HCC (discovery cohort) to identify methylation signatures that can be used for the noninvasive diagnosis of HCC. Conventional methods, including ultrasound (US) and serum AFP, are not widely used for HCC screening worldwide, partially due to their inherent weaknesses. Consequently, no international consensus has been reached regarding ideal biomarkers for HCC surveillance.

Tumorigenesis in HCC arises from the contribution of genetic and epigenetic abnormalities in the genome [52, 53]. Although there are many studies on somatic mutations in HCC, and those using gene mutations for early surveillance of HCC have been reported [48], the high heterogeneity of HCC results in a low prevalence rate of mutations in specific genes, thereby limiting its performance in the detection of HCC [54, 55]. As DNA methylation alterations usually begin to appear early in the tumor and are relatively stable over time [56], DNA methylation has promising applications as a diagnostic biomarker for tumor early surveillance. Several tumor methylation‐related studies have been successfully translated clinically, such as the gene methylation detection kits for meSEPT9 and meSDC2 in colorectal, mePTGER4/meSHOX2 in lung, and meGSTP1 in prostate cancers, which have received registration certificates for clinical class III in vitro diagnostic reagents. Notably, methylation test kits for colorectal and lung cancer have been applied to noninvasive samples (stool, peripheral blood), which highlights the possibility of the application of cancer early screening and surveillance in high‐risk groups. However, methylation alterations in specific genes in HCC diagnosis have not yet achieved consistently good clinical performance.

WGBS has emerged as a powerful method that determines DNA methylation levels on a genome‐wide scale. Integrative analysis of WGBS and mRNA‐Seq data provides an effective strategy to improve the specificity of methylated markers to transform findings from study into the next step in population‐based clinical studies. To our knowledge, this study is the first to use WGBS data to identify diagnostic biomarkers for HCC. Integrative analysis of WGBS and matched gene expression data identified eight hypermethylated promoter regions with a significant inverse correlation (Spearman Cor < − 0.3, p < 0.05). These hyper‐DMRs were located in the promoter regions of TSPYL5, GNA14, LRRC4, CYP26A1, TACSTD2, FAM83F, TBX15, and STEAP4. Six of the eight genes have been previously reported to be highly methylated with silencing expression [57, 58, 59, 60, 61]. Hypermethylation of CYP26A1 and FAM83F is a novel finding in patients with HCC. These findings indicate the effectiveness and superiority of the integrated WGBS and expression profiles as diagnostic methylation markers.

Differences in methylation of these genes were confirmed using an externally independent cohort. In addition, these eight genes were hypomethylated in PBMC, indicating that the methylation of these genes could be potential noninvasive diagnostic biomarkers in clinical practice. Among these eight markers, TSPYL5 and GNA14 had the largest Δβ and the strongest inverse correlation with the expression. We implemented rigorous filtering criteria at every stage of biomarker selection, prioritizing specificity. Because the amplification of meGNA14 failed in the assay development stage, meTSPYL5 was selected for validation in plasma cfDNA. Notably, meTSPYL5 was found in 85.4% of HCC plasma samples (41/48), which is significantly higher than the detection rate of serum AFP (70.5%) in patients with HCC (31/44). These data suggest that meTSPYL5 is a potential early predictive biomarker in patients with HCC, encouraging further investigation in large cohorts. In our results, the correlation coefficient of DNA methylation and expression was −0.62 for TSPYL5 (p = 5.70E‐08), indicating that TSPYL5 methylation has a strong regulatory role and may drive cancer development. Consistent with our findings, Shen et al. [62] reported that neither loss of copy number nor over‐expressed miRNAs, but hypermethylation was the major silencing factor for TSPYL5 expression.

Our ultimate goal was to develop a DNA methylation‐based assay for early HCC screening. The large number of genes identified in previous epigenome‐wide association studies [30, 32, 62, 63] makes it difficult to select the most promising candidates for validation in larger population‐based studies and determine their clinical utility as risk factors in a cost‐effective manner, as well as diagnostic/prognostic markers. meTSPYL5 was included in previous multitarget panels and showed promising results [64, 65]. However, multi‐target panels and β‐value‐based algorithms face challenges related to obtaining sufficient cfDNA volume and a stable ratio of methylated cfDNA in plasma or serum. Therefore, in this study, qMSP was used to determine the presence or absence of meTSPYL5, which is simple to operate, analyze, inexpensive, and scalable.

In this study, pan‐cancer analysis revealed that TSPYL5 is hypermethylated in various cancers, consistent with a previous report that TSPYL5 is hypermethylated and under‐expressed in gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, and glioma [66, 67, 68]. Although meTSPYL5 is not HCC‐specific, its high methylation differential can improve the sensitivity of detection. Further study on the application value of meTSPYL5 in the identification of benign and malignant liver diseases becomes a necessary research task. By detecting meTSPYL5 in high‐risk populations, clinicians may identify HCC at an earlier stage, when surgical resection is feasible and effective. The clinical value of using this assay to monitor minimal residual disease (MRD) after HCC surgery warrants further research. Tracking plasma meTSPYL5 after surgery may allow clinicians to personalize adjunctive therapy for individual patients. Furthermore, because meTSPYL5 in plasma is frequently detected across all HCC stages, it may also be a way to monitor relapses earlier.

However, there are still certain limitations associated with our current investigation. First, the meTSPYL5 detection assay should be optimized to meet the registration standards for Class III in vitro diagnostic reagents. Second, the sample size, particularly regarding pathological data on hepatitis and cirrhosis, is limited lacked, which reduces the power to accurately estimate the assay's sensitivity and to perform further covariate analysis of these precancerous lesions. Larger cohort studies are needed to validate meTSPYL5 marker for population‐based HCC screening. While we observed a minor, non‐significant trend towards lower methylation levels of TSPYL5 in older patients and no significant association between gender, tumor stage, HBV infection status, and AFP levels with methylation level of TSPYL5 (Figure 5 and Figure S5), notable age and gender imbalances in the cohort remains a limitation. The performance of meTSPYL5 assay in this hospital‐based cohort is not guaranteed to translate to mass screening and requires further assessed in patients with various hepatopathies, including liver cirrhosis. Third, further adequate and well‐designed investigations are required to validate the intended applications in monitoring MRD and recurrence.

Other candidate genes have been reported as methylation markers for HCC detection [29, 49, 65, 69, 70]. Two of these studies used multi‐target testing with sensitivity of 71% and 82% for early‐stage HCC [65, 69], in which meTSPYL5 was contained in their panels. More recently, Zhao et al [70] identified two novel methylation biomarkers, GNB4 and Riplet through a genome‐wide discovery, and showed sensitivity of 84.39% for any‐stage HCC detection in plasma samples. meRASSF1A was found to be hypermethylated in HCC patients but with insufficient sensitivity in early HCC [47, 50]. When meRASSF1A was combined with meTSPYL5 and serum AFP, the sensitivity of early HCC was increased to 81.8% [51]. Our WGBS revealed no methylation differences for GNB4 and Riplet. Whether the meTSPYL5 assay outperforms other HCC methylation biomarkers requires further head‐to‐head comparative studies. This study is primarily an academic validation at this stage, and cost‐effectiveness will require formal health economic assessment in prospective, multicenter, large‐scale clinical trials with specific clinical outcomes, including improved survival, reduction in unnecessary treatment, and overall health care savings. This will be a critical step in future translational research. Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into identifying HCC biomarkers by integrating transcriptome and WGBS data, emphasizing the clinical potential of single meTSPYL5 assays to screen for HCC.

Conclusions

Aberrant DNA methylation plays a vital role in gene expression and HCC carcinogenesis. This study is the first to use an integrative analysis of WGBS and mRNA‐Seq data to identified genomie‐wide methylation biomarker and validate meTSPYL5 as a potential plasma biomarker for HCC detection via qMSP.
