Respiratory sound analysis in a rabbit tracheomalacia model
Authors: Arhans Chairul Ismael, Yasuhiro Omiya, Masakazu Higuchi, Mitsuteru Nakamura, Hiroko Komura, Shinichi Tokuno, Makoto Komura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTracheomalacia, which softens the tracheal wall and leads to airway collapse during breathing, can be challenging to detect, especially in children, due to its vague symptoms and the invasive nature of standard diagnostic procedures. In this study, we developed a rabbit model of tracheomalacia by surgically removing a portion of the tracheal cartilage in New Zealand White rabbits. Breath sounds and airway pressure were recorded using a clinical-grade stethoscope setup. From each exhaled breath, 6,373 acoustic features were extracted, and 51 of these, which showed consistent and statistically significant differences (p < 0.01), were selected in at least four out of five animals. We trained and evaluated three machine learning models—LightGBM, logistic regression, and support vector machines—using cross-validation. Among them, LightGBM delivered the highest performance, with area under the curve values > 0.78 for individual breaths and > 0.80 when averaged per subject. The most important features were the low-frequency components of Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients, which likely detected subtle changes in airflow caused by airway collapse. These findings support the potential use of respiratory sound analysis combined with machine learning as a practical and non-invasive tool for identifying tracheomalacia. Introduction

Tracheomalacia occurs due to structural weakness of the trachea or external compression. In congenital cases, it results from abnormalities in the development of the tracheal cartilage or from structurally thin tracheal walls. In acquired cases, factors such as compression from the aorta or tumors, chronic inflammation, and smoking contribute to the issue1. The incidence of congenital tracheomalacia is reported to range from 1 in 1,445 to 1 in 2,100 live births. However, this incidence may be underestimated due to the possibility of severe cases being misdiagnosed and mild cases resolving spontaneously2.

Acquired tracheomalacia is observed in approximately 4.5% of patients undergoing bronchoscopy3. In patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, the frequency is even higher, reaching about 20%4. Tracheomalacia is characterized by several symptoms, with stridor—a wheezing sound heard during breathing—being one of the primary manifestations. Patients may also experience dyspnea, cyanosis, and a distinctive cough, indicating difficulty in breathing and reduced oxygen saturation. In severe cases, tracheomalacia can lead to apnea or even loss of consciousness, often referred to as “dying spells”5.

The diagnostic methods for tracheomalacia include the use of chest X-rays and computed tomography (CT) scans to evaluate the structure of the trachea, followed by bronchoscopy, which allows for direct observation of the narrowing or collapse of the trachea. These diagnostic procedures are invasive; therefore, it is essential to consider less invasive methods for evaluating tracheomalacia. Most existing research focuses on developing treatments using animal models of tracheomalacia6,7.

Building on our previous work, we have developed speech biomarkers for various diseases, including heart failure8, respiratory infections9, neurodegenerative diseases10, and psychiatric disorders11. Furthermore, our technology for identifying disease trends through speech analysis is non-invasive, cost-effective, and easy to implement. Thus, it can be utilized as a diagnostic support technology in medical institutions. Research using respiratory sound analysis for detecting abnormal breath sounds has been actively explored, including automated identification of crackles and wheezes using acoustic features12,13. The challenges in breath sound analysis in clinical cases include a lack of model interpretability, sensitivity to noise, variations among devices, and physiological variability14. The challenges in breath sound analysis in clinical settings mainly relate to real-world recording conditions. Breath sounds are easily affected by ambient noise, differences between recording devices, and variations among patients. These factors make stable feature extraction difficult and can reduce the reliability of machine-learning approaches. It is also often unclear how the models reach their decisions, which makes interpretation harder for clinical use.

Recent advances in respiratory acoustics have led to the application of machine-learning and deep-learning techniques for detecting and classifying pathological breath sounds. Classical machine-learning studies have used time–frequency and cepstral features with supervised classifiers for automated identification of crackles, wheezes, and other adventitious sounds12,15,16. More recently, convolutional neural network–based models have been introduced for end-to-end respiratory sound classification, including large-scale approaches for multi-class lung sound recognition and intelligent stethoscope systems13,14,17,18. These approaches have improved the performance and robustness of automated respiratory sound analysis; however, almost all prior studies rely solely on clinical lung sound recordings without controlled manipulation of airway anatomy or simultaneous airway pressure measurements. To our knowledge, no previous study has investigated respiratory acoustics in an experimental tracheomalacia model while quantifying intraluminal airway pressure, highlighting a methodological gap that the present work aims to address.

Therefore, we recorded auscultation sounds from the rabbit tracheomalacia model using a single standardized setup consisting of the NEXTATE SDNX-01G auscultation unit with a neonatal Littmann chest piece and a portable digital recorder. Using the same equipment for all recordings ensured consistent sound quality and avoided differences caused by variations between devices. The aim of this study was to analyze these auscultation sounds using supervised machine-learning models and identify acoustic features characteristic of tracheomalacia.

Materials and methods

Animal model

The animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care Committee of Tokyo University (Approval No. A2023M040-01). All animal procedures were conducted in strict accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and are reported in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines. The experiments were conducted using 15-week-old New Zealand White rabbits (Japan SLC, Inc., Shizuoka, Japan) weighing approximately 3.0 kg. In total, five rabbits were used in this study, in accordance with the principles of animal experimentation and to minimize animal burden. Briefly, the rabbits were anesthetized with Propofol (Maruisihi Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., Osaka, Japan) and Isoflurane (Viatris Inc., Tokyo, Japan). They were placed in the supine position, and a median cervical incision was made to expose the trachea. Blunt and sharp dissection techniques were employed to incise the perichondrium along the central axis of the ring. The mid-anterior wall of the cartilage was transected, and the cartilage was detached from the trachea using the same maneuvers without tracheal mucosa injury. The severity of tracheomalacia was determined by resecting the eight cervical tracheal cartilage rings at approximately 40% of their circumference (Fig. 1.), leaving only the submucosa of the airway. We validated the tracheomalacia model by noting that the trachea expanded during inspiration and constricted during expiration (Supplement data: video). Following the recording of sound data and airway pressure, all rabbits were euthanized in accordance with humane protocols. Deep anesthesia was achieved through increased inhalation of isoflurane, after which intravenous potassium chloride (KCl) was administered.

Surgical creation of rabbit tracheomalacia model.

Sound recording and airway pressure recording

Auscultation sounds in the cervical trachea and lung field were collected using an auscultation device (NEXTATE SDNX-01G, Sharmedical Corporation) with a neonatal chest piece (3 M™ Littmann™ Stethoscope Series: Classic II). The area around the auscultation position was de-haired using electric clippers and hair removal cream to prevent the rabbit’s body hair from interfering with the transmission of sound vibrations. Auscultation data were collected as an analog sound source on the auscultation device, digitized with a portable recorder (R26, Roland Corporation, Shizuoka, Japan) for use as an audio interface, imported into a laptop computer, and recorded with the audio editing software Audacity (The Audacity Team, version 3.3.2)19.

Airway pressure was measured simultaneously with the respiratory sound recordings using a pneumatic pressure transducer (PSE533, SMC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). A side-port adapter was inserted in-line in the respiratory circuit between the endotracheal tube and the anesthesia circuit. The sensing tip of the pressure transducer was directly inserted into the side port, allowing the sensor to measure intraluminal airway pressure without interfering with airflow through the circuit.

The pressure signal was recorded using a voltage logger (LR5043, HIOKI E.E. CORPORATION, Nagano, Japan), which provides a resolution of 1 mV and a minimum sampling interval of 1 s. Synchronization between the pressure and audio recordings was achieved by initiating both devices simultaneously and aligning their timestamps during post-processing.

The rabbits breathed room air through the anesthesia circuit without positive-pressure ventilation, except for brief stabilization periods required during induction. Under these conditions at 0 cmH₂O, the trachea expanded during inspiration and constricted during expiration. When the airway pressure exceeded approximately 3 cmH₂O, the tracheal lumen remained sufficiently patent in some rabbits, making it difficult to obtain detectable respiratory sounds. Therefore, all recordings analyzed in this study were acquired at a mean airway pressure of 0 cmH₂O.

Signal processing and feature extraction

In the cervical auscultatory sound analysis, respiratory phases were categorized into three classes: expiratory, inspiratory, and other. Labeling was performed manually by an expert in voice analysis using predefined criteria based on the temporal structure of the breathing cycle. Segments with ambiguous respiratory-phase characteristics or contaminated by non-stationary noise—such as environmental sounds or biological artifacts—were assigned to the “other” category and excluded from further analysis. Although no formal inter-rater or intra-rater assessment was conducted, the use of a single experienced annotator ensured consistent application of the labeling criteria across all recordings.

While expiratory sounds were consistently audible across all subjects, inspiratory sounds were faint or absent in some recordings, likely due to insufficient turbulence to generate detectable acoustic energy at 0 cmH₂O airway pressure. Therefore, only expiratory segments were used for feature extraction and analysis. After excluding recordings affected by non-stationary noise, a clean dataset of labeled expiratory sounds was obtained for both normal and tracheomalacia conditions in all five rabbits. Although only expiratory sounds were analyzed, we use the term “respiratory sounds” in this study because the recordings covered the full breathing process and reflect airway behavior throughout the respiratory cycle.

Various acoustic features of the respiratory sounds were compared based on the final classes assigned to the normal auscultation sounds and those of the tracheomalacia model. The Munich open-source Media Interpretation by Large feature-space Extraction (openSMILE) toolkit, widely used in speech recognition tasks, was employed for feature extraction. This toolkit enables the extraction of a large number of acoustic features and their statistics, commonly referred to as low-level descriptors. We used the openSMILE ‘ComParE_2016’ feature set to extract 6,373 acoustic features from the labeled expiratory sound segments. To address the non-stationary nature of respiratory sound signals, we performed feature extraction using a short-time framing approach. The audio waveform was segmented into 60-ms frames with a 10-ms frame shift, applying a Hamming window to analyze time-varying acoustic characteristics. Low-level descriptors (LLDs) were computed for each frame, including time-domain features (e.g., root-mean-square energy, zero-crossing rate) and frequency-domain features (e.g., spectral centroid, spectral slope, bandwidth, Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients, and FFT-based spectral skewness). Statistical functionals (mean, standard deviation, percentiles, peak counts, and linear-regression coefficients, among others) were subsequently derived across each entire expiratory segment, resulting in fixed-length vectors. Following the openSMILE ComParE_2016 configuration, this process generated 6,373 static acoustic features per segment, enabling robust analysis of the non-stationary respiratory sound properties. The resulting features were used as inputs for machine-learning classification. Finally, three algorithms—LightGBM, logistic regression, and support vector machines—were trained and evaluated through cross-validation to assess classification performance. To prevent potential data leakage arising from breath-by-breath classification, we employed a grouped cross-validation scheme. Each rabbit was recorded under two distinct airway conditions (control and tracheomalacia), and each “rabbit condition” pair was treated as an independent grouping unit. All expiratory breaths belonging to the same group were assigned to the same fold, ensuring that no breath from a given physiological state appeared in both training and validation sets. This corresponds to a GroupKFold-style validation approach and provides an appropriate balance between mitigating data leakage and maintaining sufficient fold structure for model evaluation.

Results

Among the 6,373 extracted features, 51 demonstrated statistically significant differences (p < 0.01) in at least four out of the five rabbits and were subsequently selected for model development. Although a large number of statistical comparisons were performed, this study was exploratory in nature and aimed at identifying candidate features for subsequent validation. Therefore, a nominal significance threshold of p < 0.01 was adopted without applying a strict multiple comparison correction, such as Bonferroni adjustment, to avoid an overly conservative selection that might exclude biologically relevant features.

Model performance was evaluated using grouped cross-validation. We acknowledge that this method is slightly less conservative than a fully subject-wise split in which all recordings from the same rabbit across both conditions would remain in a single fold. However, we selected the grouped strategy because (1) the two airway conditions were created at separate time points and represent distinct physiological states, and (2) a fully subject-wise split would greatly reduce the number of folds and worsen class balance, impairing model evaluation. We believe this provides an appropriate balance between avoiding data leakage and preserving statistical stability. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.7 or higher in the breath-by-breath analysis and 0.8 or higher in the analysis of the averaged results for each individual, indicating high performance. Furthermore, the features with high importance in the LightGBM model for predicting lower airway stenosis are mainly related to MFCC (Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients), which are closely related to the frequency characteristics and fluctuations of breath sounds. In particular, fluctuations in the low-frequency components of the expiratory auscultation sounds appeared to play an important role in identifying tracheomalacia. These features likely capture subtle changes in airflow caused by airway narrowing. In addition, differences in the amplitude spectrum and distortion of the expiratory sounds contributed to the model’s predictions, suggesting that these acoustic patterns are useful for distinguishing normal from stenotic airway conditions.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the effectiveness of respiratory sound analysis, utilizing clinical-grade equipment and machine learning, to differentiate between normal airways and tracheomalacia. This system successfully captured features indicative of tracheomalacia. The findings of this study show that the classification models, particularly LightGBM, were effective in identifying abnormal breath sounds. At the individual tracheomalacia level, the models achieved AUC values exceeding 0.78, suggesting that even with a small dataset, measurable acoustic differences exist between healthy and affected states. These results reinforce the idea that respiratory sounds encode pathological information reflective of airway behavior. In this study, inspiratory sounds were not consistently audible in all rabbits, so only expiratory segments were used for feature extraction and classification. The absence of consistently audible inspiratory sounds in our recordings likely reflects the lower turbulence generated during inspiration at 0 cmH₂O airway pressure, resulting in acoustic signals that fall below the detection threshold of surface auscultation. Future studies incorporating higher-sensitivity sensors or alternative microphone placement may enable more reliable acquisition of inspiratory signals. Although only expiratory segments were analyzed, the recordings captured the full breathing cycle, and the objective of this study was to evaluate airway behavior across respiration. These findings suggest that acoustic analysis of tracheal sounds can support the diagnosis of tracheomalacia.

Neonates and infants have a tracheal diameter of approximately 4 mm, similar to that of rabbits weighing 3 kg, making them ideal subjects for studies on tracheomalacia. Thus, creating rabbit tracheomalacia models is suitable at this stage of growth. In the current study, respiratory sounds were recorded from the body surface using standard auscultation equipment and procedures.

MFCCs were identified as the most informative features, capturing turbulence from airway collapse in lower frequencies. This finding supports clinical observations associating tracheomalacia with altered sound generation. Our work demonstrates the potential of using MFCCs in a non-invasive, machine-learning-based diagnostic approach.

The conventional diagnosis of tracheomalacia often involves procedures such as chest X-rays, CT scans, and bronchoscopy. Chest X-rays show a normal or dilated trachea during inspiration in frontal views, while lateral projections reveal a narrowed trachea in patients with tracheomalacia. However, chest X-rays are infrequently performed due to their qualitative nature and inherent uncertainties. CT imaging with inspiratory and expiratory views is preferred for assessing tracheomalacia. Repeated testing increases radiation exposure and sedation risks. The gold standard for diagnosing tracheomalacia is direct observation of the airway luminal motion via bronchoscopy. Although flexible bronchoscopy can be performed in low-birth-weight children, minor and major complications have been reported with abnormal airways20. Invasive bronchoscopy should precede non-invasive methods to diagnose and assess severity. Using auscultation to suspect tracheomalacia can help identify patients who need bronchoscopy.

The method used in the current study provides an accessible alternative by utilizing standard auscultation equipment and basic signal processing techniques. Unlike previous studies that focused on clear abnormalities, such as wheezing12,13, the present study identifies subtle acoustic patterns in breaths that appear clinically normal.

However, this study has some limitations. Tracheomalacia can be categorized into two types: crescent moon and saber-sheath trachea1. In this study, the front portion of the tracheal cartilage was excised to create a model similar to the saber sheath tracheal softening disease6,7. Our results were comparable to the disease, but not identical to the deformity seen in true tracheomalacia. Furthermore, the sample size in this study was limited to five rabbits, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the disease model assumes consistent lesion severity and controlled recording conditions—factors that may not hold in clinical environments. While pressure sensor data helped support our acoustic observations, future studies should aim to better synchronize with respiratory phases and expand the dataset to include greater biological and environmental variability.

Despite these constraints, this work provides a promising starting point for the non-invasive diagnosis of tracheomalacia. Further research should explore the translation of findings to human subjects and the development of real-time diagnostic tools suitable for clinical settings. Strengthening the robustness and interpretability of the models is essential for broader adoption. The findings of the present study provide a foundation for further development of acoustic biomarkers and highlight the potential utility of machine learning in non-invasive screening for respiratory disorders. Furthermore, integrating the diagnostic system into clinical workflows and validating its performance across diverse pediatric populations will be crucial for its practical implementation in real-world settings.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated that respiratory sound analysis using clinical-grade auscultation equipment, combined with machine learning techniques, can effectively distinguish between normal and tracheomalacia-affected airways in a rabbit model. The classification models, particularly LightGBM, achieved high performance. MFCCs emerged as key features reflecting subtle changes in airflow due to tracheal collapse. These results suggest the potential of using non-invasive acoustic biomarkers to support the diagnosis of tracheomalacia.

Given the anatomical similarity between rabbit and neonatal airways, this model provides a valuable platform for exploring pediatric applications. While limitations such as small sample size and model-specific constraints remain, our findings lay the groundwork for the future development of practical, non-invasive diagnostic tools. Further research is needed to validate this approach in human subjects, increase model robustness, and move toward real-time clinical implementation.
