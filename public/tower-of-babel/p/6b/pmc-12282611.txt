Palliative Care for Dialysis-Dependent Pediatric Patients: A Survey of Providers, Nurses, and Caregivers
Authors: Joshua Lipsitz, Mark Stockton Beveridge, Katherine Maddox
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Key Points Providers and nurses recognized the potential added value of palliative care for dialysis-dependent pediatric patients.Most caregivers of dialysis-dependent pediatric patients did not hold positive or negative preconceived notions about a palliative care consultation.Perceived obstacles to palliative care consultation among providers and nurses did not reflect the views of most caregivers. BackgroundDialysis-dependent pediatric patients and their families face significant biopsychosocial burdens and low health-related quality of life. Palliative care consultations can alleviate some degree of suffering for patients and families, but remain underutilized within pediatric nephrology. Little is known about how providers, nurses, and caregivers perceive palliative care integration into the multidisciplinary care of dialysis-dependent pediatric patients.MethodsThis study surveyed pediatric nephrology providers (physicians and advanced practice providers), inpatient pediatric nephrology bedside nurses, and caregivers of dialysis-dependent pediatric patients at a freestanding tertiary care children's hospital in Dallas, TX. Participants completed a survey regarding knowledge about, experiences with, and perceptions of palliative care in pediatric nephrology.ResultsTen providers, 20 nurses, and 18 caregivers completed the survey. Although 80% of providers and all nurses agreed that palliative care would benefit dialysis-dependent pediatric patients, most believed that palliative care is not as important in pediatric nephrology as it should be. Seventy percent of providers and 45% of nurses believed that they understand the scope of palliative care. Ninety percent of providers and all nurses desired more palliative care education. Of the 22% of caregivers whose child had already received palliative care services, all found the consultation to be helpful. Many providers and nurses worried that a palliative care consultation would signal to families that the nephrology team would be giving up on their child and that their child is approaching the end of life. However, no caregivers thought that a consultation would mean that the nephrology team would be giving up on their child, and only 6% worried that it would indicate that their child is approaching the end of life.ConclusionsThese data support further palliative care education for pediatric nephrology providers and nurses and more robust and systematic involvement of subspecialty palliative care for dialysis-dependent pediatric patients.

Introduction

Pediatric ESKD represents a life-altering and life-limiting disease with profound impacts on patients and families. When kidney transplantation is not available as an initial treatment modality for ESKD, hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis become necessary to sustain life. Advancements in pediatric ESKD management have improved morbidity and mortality for affected children.1,2 However, despite this clinical progress, dialysis-dependent patients and their families continue to suffer complex medical, psychosocial, and existential challenges throughout the disease course. Common examples of these burdens include frequent and prolonged hospitalizations, reliance on medical technology, and caregiver burnout.3–5 In comparison with pediatric patients with some other chronic diseases, those with ESKD experience lower health-related quality of life.6 This proves especially true for patients with dialysis dependence as opposed to those who have undergone renal transplantation.6 Currently, no standardized practice guidelines exist to address quality of life concerns for dialysis-dependent pediatric patients.

Complementing medical and surgical management, palliative care can mitigate the multidimensional suffering associated with pediatric ESKD through its focus on effective communication, individualized symptom management, and advanced care planning. However, despite the high symptom burden and complex trajectory of ESKD, subspecialty palliative care consultations remain an underutilized resource in pediatric nephrology.7 Highlighting this reality, a multicenter study demonstrated that fewer than 3% of inpatient pediatric palliative care consultations were for patients with renal disease.8 Potential barriers to consultation include the stigma of palliative care hastening death, the lack of awareness of the scope of palliative care, and institutional limitations in palliative care bandwidth. Mitigation of these obstacles can lay the groundwork for broader integration of palliative care in pediatric nephrology, thereby improving care for dialysis-dependent patients with complex communication, symptom management, and advanced care planning needs.

To date, limited data exist about the perspectives of physicians, advanced practice providers (APPs), bedside nurses, and caregivers of dialysis-dependent patients about palliative care in pediatric nephrology. Understanding the perspectives of these key stakeholders is crucial to identifying opportunities to improve holistic care for dialysis-dependent pediatric patients. By surveying these groups, we aimed to explore the local landscape of palliative care in pediatric nephrology and discover areas for growth.

Methods

This study took place at a freestanding tertiary care children's hospital in Dallas, TX with a large cohort of dialysis-dependent pediatric patients. This study's research protocol, which involved human participants for the survey, received approval from the UT Southwestern Institutional Review Board. Three populations were surveyed: pediatric nephrology providers (physicians and APPs), inpatient pediatric nephrology bedside nurses, and caregivers of dialysis-dependent pediatric patients. Three distinct surveys were created, each with general and population-specific questions. Questions for providers and nurses focused on general attitudes toward palliative care, perceived importance of palliative care in pediatric nephrology, obstacles to consultation, and possible benefits of additional palliative care education. Providers were also queried about their comfort level with palliative care topics, such as shared decision making, goals of care, and end-of-life care. Caregivers were asked about the potential benefits of palliative care, perceived implications of a palliative care consultation, and experience with palliative care services if their child had already received them. Most questions were formulated by the research team, while three questions in the caregiver survey were adopted from the validated Palliative Care Attitudes Scale-9.9

Pediatric nephrology providers and inpatient pediatric nephrology bedside nurses were identified using hospital staffing lists. Caregivers of dialysis-dependent pediatric patients were identified using a department-specific census list in the electronic health record. Eligible providers and nurses were contacted through email with a link to complete the survey on the electronic REDCap platform. Eligible caregivers were approached in person in either the outpatient clinic or inpatient setting to complete a hardcopy version of the survey. The research team manually inputted responses from caregiver surveys into REDCap. For each population, the anonymous one-time survey took approximately 5 to 10 minutes to complete. Survey responses were then extracted from REDCap for the purpose of data analysis. Summary results were reported as percentages and frequencies of responses to each survey question.

Results

Pediatric Nephrology Providers

A total of 16 providers (physicians and APPs) were eligible for participation in the study. Ten providers completed the study, yielding a 63% response rate (Figure 1). Sixty percent of respondents were physicians with the remaining 40% being APPs (Figure 1). Eighty percent of providers strongly agreed or agreed that palliative care would add quality to the care of pediatric patients with ESKD (Figure 2). Although 30% of providers viewed palliative care as currently very important or important in the field of pediatric nephrology, 90% believed that palliative care should be very important or important in pediatric nephrology (Figure 2). Providers perceived two main obstacles to involving subspecialty palliative care for dialysis-dependent patients to be: (1) concern that involving palliative care may signal to families that their child is approaching the end of life (60%) and (2) concern that involving palliative care may signal to families that the medical team is giving up on their child (50%) (Figure 3). A majority (70%) of providers strongly agreed or agreed that they adequately understand the scope of palliative care. Ninety percent of providers strongly believed or believed that they would benefit from more education about palliative care topics, such as communication, shared decision making, symptom management, and end-of-life care.

No families worried that a palliative care consultation would indicate that the medical team is giving up on their child, and very few believed that it would mean that their child would die sooner.

Inpatient Pediatric Nephrology Bedside Nurses

A total of 31 inpatient pediatric nephrology bedside nurses were eligible for participation in the study. Twenty nurses completed the study, yielding a 65% response rate (Figure 1). All nurses strongly agreed or agreed that palliative care would add quality to the care of pediatric patients with ESKD (Figure 2). Although 60% of nurses viewed palliative care as currently very important or important in the field of pediatric nephrology, all believed that palliative care should be very important or important in pediatric nephrology (Figure 2). Eighty percent believed that palliative care services are underutilized for dialysis-dependent pediatric patients. Nurses perceived two main obstacles to involving subspecialty palliative care for dialysis-dependent patients to be: (1) concern that involving palliative care may signal to families that their child is approaching the end of life (85%) and (2) concern that involving palliative care may signal to families that the medical team is giving up on their child (75%) (Figure 3). In contrast to providers, a minority (45%) of nurses strongly agreed or agreed that they adequately understand the scope of palliative care. All nurses strongly believed or believed that they would benefit from more education about palliative care topics, such as communication, shared decision making, symptom management, and end-of-life care.

Caregivers of Dialysis-Dependent Pediatric Patients

A total of 18 eligible caregivers were approached to participate in the study, all of whom completed the survey (Figure 1). Fifty percent of caregivers identified as White or Caucasian with the remaining being Black or African American (16%); Hispanic or Latino (16%); Asian or Pacific Islander (6%); multiracial or biracial (6%); or other (6%). Fifty percent had heard of palliative care, 39% had not, and 11% were unsure. Of note, no caregivers worried that a palliative care consultation would mean that the nephrology team would be giving up on their child, and only 6% worried that it would mean that their child would die sooner (Figure 3). Caregivers were generally unsure about whether a palliative care consultation would help with physical quality of life (61%), feelings of sadness and depression (67%), and life prolongation (56%) (Figure 4). Twenty-two percent of caregivers endorsed that their child had already received palliative care services (Figure 5). Of these caregivers, all found palliative care services to be helpful, and 75% would have preferred earlier palliative care involvement (Figure 5). Sixty-one percent of caregivers reported that they would find it very helpful or helpful for a dedicated team to assist with managing uncomfortable physical symptoms, making difficult value-based medical decisions, and coping with the many challenges of living with ESKD.

Caregiver perceptions of potential benefits of palliative care consultations. Most caregivers were uncertain about whether a palliative care consultation would help with life prolongation, feelings of sadness and depression, and physical quality of life. Some caregivers held positive preconceptions about palliative care consultations, and very few did not believe that it would be helpful.

Caregiver perspectives on helpfulness and timing of palliative care consultations. Of the families who had received a palliative care consultation, all found it to be helpful and most would have preferred earlier involvement of the team.

Discussion

This study exposed a clear disparity between the perceived value and actual utilization of palliative care consultations for dialysis-dependent pediatric patients. Most providers and nurses recognized the potential value of palliative care consultations for dialysis-dependent patients, while also reporting that palliative care is not currently as important in pediatric nephrology as it should be. Demonstrating this underutilization, only a small minority of surveyed caregivers reported that their child had already received palliative care services. All caregivers who had previously engaged with palliative care found the services to be helpful and most would have preferred earlier palliative care involvement. Regardless of whether their child had already received a palliative care consultation, most caregivers would find it helpful to receive assistance with symptom management, medical decision-making, and coping with the challenges of ESKD. Palliative care teams can offer these necessary and desired services for families, furthering the argument for more systematic involvement of palliative care for dialysis-dependent pediatric patients.

Providers and nurses identified two main obstacles to involving palliative care for dialysis-dependent pediatric patients: (1) concern that a consultation may signal to families that the medical team is giving up on their child and (2) concern that a consultation may signal to families that their child is approaching the end of life. However, no caregivers shared the concern that palliative care involvement would signify that the nephrology team would be giving up on their child, and only a very small percentage worried that it would indicate that their child would die sooner. This discrepancy suggests that the primary hurdles to involving palliative care for this patient population may be predicated on erroneous assumptions about how caregivers would perceive the consultation. Accordingly, when considering palliative care consultations for this group, pediatric nephrology teams may be able to easily overcome barriers by eliciting caregiver perspectives and providing appropriate introductions to the concept of palliative care.

While most providers believed that they adequately grasp the scope of palliative care, fewer than half of bedside nurses endorsed the same degree of understanding. If true for the broader population, then nurse education about the role of subspecialty palliative care may constitute a key area for improvement. By fully understanding what palliative care teams can offer, nurses can more successfully identify dialysis-dependent pediatric patients with subspecialty palliative care needs. There also seems to be an appetite for education about substantive palliative care topics because nearly all providers and bedside nurses believed that they would benefit from more instruction about communication, shared decision making, symptom management, and end-of-life care.

When asked the validated questions of whether a palliative care consultation would help with physical quality of life, feelings of sadness and depression, and life prolongation, most caregivers were unsure. This prevailing sense of uncertainty suggests that most caregivers do not hold preconceived notions, either positive or negative, about palliative care. Therefore, pediatric nephrology and palliative care teams may not need to combat deeply entrenched stigma about palliative care in this population. Regardless of a family's preconceptions, appropriate introduction of and education about palliative care allows families to make a fully informed decision about whether they desire a consultation based upon correct information rather than stigma-based views.

This study conveys novel information but contains some limitations. Surveys contain inherent subjectivity, and, therefore, bias, in how participants interpret questions and answer choices. Several survey questions were validated in other studies, though others were original and not yet validated. Not all providers and bedside nurses invited to participate in the study ultimately completed the survey and, because of limitations of the research team, only a third of caregivers of dialysis-dependent pediatric patients who receive medical care at our institution were included. This resulted in relatively small sample sizes. As a single-center study, there may be idiosyncrasies about the providers, nurses, and caregivers at our institution that limit the generalizability of findings.

Overall, this study offers further understanding about the nexus between palliative care and pediatric nephrology and can serve as a basis to support more cross-pollination between the two subspecialties. Implementation of a new care model often requires cultural and logistical shifts, both of which frequently come with challenges. For instance, challenges at our institution include a limited ability to meet the increased demand for palliative care services and hesitation among some pediatric nephrologists to consult palliative care for dialysis-dependent patients for a variety of reasons. Nevertheless, bolstering support for dialysis-dependent pediatric patients and their families through palliative care integration into the multidisciplinary care model is well worth the effort.

When applied to the clinical setting, these data support further education for providers and bedside nurses about palliative care, as well as early and robust palliative care involvement for dialysis-dependent pediatric patients. To reduce the cognitive burden on medical teams and promote standardized care for all dialysis-dependent patients, pediatric nephrology groups could consider criteria for automatic palliative care consultations for patients who require chronic maintenance dialysis. In much the same way that families of dialysis-dependent pediatric patients routinely meet with dieticians and social workers as part of the multidisciplinary care model, they can visit with the palliative care team for additional support. This standardized approach can normalize palliative care consultations so that they become a regular part of the care model for patients and families. Studies at other pediatric centers are necessary to determine whether these findings are institutionally specific or generalizable to other groups of providers, nurses, and caregivers.
