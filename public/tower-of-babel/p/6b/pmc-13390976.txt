INTRODUCTION

Cancer cachexia is the tumor-induced, unintentional loss of weight. Symptoms include loss of appetite, weakness, fatigue, and the loss of fat and muscle tissue. Despite being highly prevalent among many cancer types, cancer cachexia remains underdiagnosed in patients and underappreciated among providers [1▪,2,3]. The experience of learning about cachexia is a frustrating one for patients and their caregivers. It is often through self-education, driven by a sense of helplessness in the face of the relentless symptoms of cachexia, that patients and their caregivers embark on a search for answers and solutions. Their journeys are unique, but they point toward a common set of unmet needs centered around awareness, education, and guidance.

Through my experience as one of my father’s caregivers during his 6-year fight with pancreatic cancer, as well as through founding and co-chairing the patient and caregiver advocacy group of the Cancer Cachexia Society, the Cancer Cachexia Network, I have lived and observed the struggles of patients and caregivers navigating cancer cachexia. This perspective-style review aims to bring the patient experience of cachexia to the forefront, with insights highlighting areas of frequent frustration. A review of some of the more established advocacy efforts is presented. For providers, this review can serve as a glimpse into what patients and caregivers experience, and the efforts some take to help themselves or their loved ones. For patients, it might serve as evidence that you are not alone on your journey.

KNOWLEDGE GAPS AROUND CACHEXIA EXIST

Cachexia is better understood today than ever before, but this understanding has not fully translated to the clinic. As the field has advanced, the understanding of the causes of cachexia has grown [4]. The definition of cachexia has evolved over time but has consistently centered on a degree of weight loss over a recent period of time. Guidelines around clinical care for patients at risk for or experiencing cancer cachexia have followed in the wake of these evolutions in definition and fundamental understandings [5,6]. It is no wonder, then, that providers struggle to stay abreast of the current state of the field. Qualitative studies exploring the patient and caregiver experience have been reviewed and indicate gaps in the clinical addressment of cachexia, as reported by patients and caregivers [7▪]. Gaps in provider knowledge have been identified [2,8]. Thus, patients can find themselves discovering and learning about cachexia outside the context of their clinical care.

HOW PATIENTS LEARN ABOUT CACHEXIA

Learning about cachexia is often exasperating and is an appropriate starting point for framing the patient experience. In the best scenarios, well-informed providers discuss cachexia at the appropriate time for the patient. When this happens, particularly at established cancer research centers where providers have knowledge of or even expertise in cachexia, a wider care team can be deployed to specifically tackle cachexia with the patient and their family. This team might include dietitians, physical therapists, and psychologists who can provide dietary support, exercise interventions, and psychological support. But many cachectic patients are not treated by providers who discuss cachexia or its possibility, and they, along with their caregivers, search symptoms on the internet and start to piece together their own picture of what is going on.

Patients in this situation struggle to find consistent answers to basic questions. WEIGHT LOSS: A LEADING INDICATOR

In the online forum for the Cancer Cachexia Network, weight loss is the most frequent topic of discussion. Members report that weight loss can be sudden and drastic, which is alarming. Not infrequently, it is weight loss itself that spurs patients to seek medical attention, leading to the discovery of cancer. The double-disclosure of a cancer diagnosis and cachexia, when it happens, can be overwhelming. Cachexia, being unfamiliar to patients and caregivers, can easily fall by the wayside while the focus remains on cancer. This is despite evidence that weight loss could potentially affect survival [9].

CACHEXIA PROGRESSION: WILL IT STOP?

After learning about cachexia, it is natural and common to wonder how it progresses. Cancer is discussed in the context of stages, and this framing can translate to the notion that cachexia has stages. The distressing nature of cachexia, specifically in its outward physical manifestation, can be psychologically difficult to accept [10], and patients naturally want to know if it can be reversed. Stages of cachexia have indeed been defined: precachexia, cachexia, and refractory cachexia [11]. However, the notion that cachexia can be halted or even reversed is not well-established in the literature, and patients find themselves unsure about the nature of the progression of cachexia and what might contribute to its halting and reversal. Moreover, cancer therapy itself can cause the same symptoms as cachexia. For example, it is common to lose weight after major surgeries, during chemotherapy, and during radiation therapy. Fatigue and loss of appetite are also common. These complications only add to the confusion.

TAKING MATTERS INTO THEIR OWN HANDS: THE SEARCH FOR ANSWERS

When patients conduct their own research on interventions for cachexia, they are likely to encounter a body of literature that risks leading them to pursue therapies that never translated to the clinic. The life-science research literature on cachexia is extensive and spans decades. Lacking approved therapies, patients and caregivers often find themselves delving deep into this literature, which is filled with studies that showed early promise in animal models but have thus far failed to translate to clinical stages. The tendency of publishing in the sciences can be overtly positive when early studies are successful. Moreover, it is difficult to find evidence of when avenues of research reach a dead end. An unintended consequence is a plethora of positive-sounding literature on therapies that never translated to the clinic. Patients encounter hundreds of articles reporting early success on a large number of potential therapies. Some of these therapies even advanced to early clinical studies in humans. These studies can give false hope and potentially lead patients down self-prescribed avenues of therapy that range from ineffective to potentially dangerous. CANCER CACHEXIA TREATMENTS

Worldwide, there is one approved therapy for cachexia: anamorelin was approved for appetite stimulation in Japan in 2021 and remains the only approved drug for cancer cachexia. However, at the time of this writing, there are several clinical trials in advanced phases that have global reach. Clinical guidelines exist but are targeted toward providers [5,6]. Treatments for cancer cachexia have been reviewed, but this literature is also targeted at providers [12]. Treatments tend to fall into the categories of nutrition (dietary counseling, nutritional interventions), exercise, and drugs (steroids, appetite stimulation). The psychosocial effects of cachexia on patients and their families remain an underappreciated area of attention [10].

A REVIEW OF CACHEXIA ADVOCACY EFFORTS AND THEIR EDUCATIONAL MATERIALS

Cachexia and cancer advocacy groups are promoting awareness and creating quality educational materials for patients. What follows is an informal list of organizations that are currently performing advocacy work in cancer cachexia, which are easily discoverable online and thus often found by patients and caregivers searching for information. These organizations also produce various forms of patient-focused materials. These materials take different forms, such as educational videos, patient flyers, and online publications.

The Cancer Cachexia Network [13] was founded in 2021 and is the advocacy group of the Cancer Cachexia Society. Their mission is to ‘empower patients with cachexia to live their lives to the fullest through awareness, education, and community.’ The Cancer Cachexia Network creates patient-focused educational material, hosts a Facebook community dedicated to patients and caregivers, and publishes an informal map of current clinical trials for cachexia.

The Cancer Support Community is a global nonprofit organization whose mission is to ‘strive to improve medical outcomes and create better experiences for patients, survivors, caregivers, and families impacted by cancer.’ With a strong emphasis on community, they focus on conducting psychosocial, behavioral, and survivorship research, running a global network of cancer support centers, and advancing policy that supports their mission. The Cancer Support Community conducted an Externally-Led Patient-Focused Drug Development Meeting in November 2021. A result of this meeting was the Cachexia Voice of the Patient Report [14]. This report is focused on bringing the patient and caregiver voice forward.

CanCan [15] is a multidisciplinary team that consists of researchers, thought leaders, and clinicians from around the world, focused on guiding treatment development for cancer cachexia [16]. They are supported through the Cancer Grand Challenges initiative. The organization has produced patient-focused educational materials, available on their website.

LUNGevity Foundation is a nonprofit focused on advocacy for patients with lung cancer, which has a high prevalence of cachexia. In October 2020, LUNGevity conducted a comprehensive Unmet Needs Workshop focused on cachexia. This workshop centered on the patient experience and brought together a wide range of stakeholders, including clinicians, patients, life science researchers, regulators, industry representatives, and advocates. The output of this meeting was published [8] The LUNGevity Foundation also produces patient-focused educational material that they host on their website, including an informational flyer that can be printed.

The American Cancer Society is a large, nationwide nonprofit aimed at promoting the prevention, detection, and early treatment of cancer. Their website has many educational resources for patients and caregivers, including material about cachexia.

The Pancreatic Cancer Action Network (PanCAN) is a large organization ‘dedicated to advancing progress against pancreatic cancer for patients today and in the future.’ Cachexia has an extremely high prevalence in pancreatic cancer patients, and PanCAN has educational materials for patients that address cachexia.

CONCLUSION

Cachectic patients too often find themselves on a self-guided journey of diagnosis, prognosis, and intervention selection. While there is a dedicated and amazing community of healthcare organizations and professionals trying to provide high-quality educational materials, the diversity of care settings, healthcare literacy, and the current lack of approved therapies mean patients are left in the dark. However, new potential therapies are on the horizon, and the effort to expand awareness and education among providers and patients is moving forward.
