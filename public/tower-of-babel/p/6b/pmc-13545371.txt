The Impact of Persistent Iron Overload and Ferritin Trend on the Clinical Outcome of Peritoneal Dialysis Patients: A Retrospective Study Over 10 Years
Authors: Vanessa Wing-Lam Tao, Vickie Wai-Ki Kwong, Jack Kit-Chung Ng, Winston Wing-Shing Fung, Gordon Chun-Kau Chan, Wing-Fai Pang, Kai-Ming Chow, Cheuk-Chun Szeto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rationale & ObjectiveAnemia is a major complication in chronic kidney disease. Although iron replacement therapies are commonly used, concerns about iron overload exist, for which data are limited, particularly in patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis (PD). This study investigated the impact of persistent iron overload and ferritin trends on clinical outcomes.Study DesignSingle-center retrospective study.Setting & ParticipantsA single dialysis unit; 586 new adult patients undergoing PD between 2011 and 2020; a subgroup of 345 patients was analyzed using their persistent or reversal of high iron status.PredictorsChange in iron status in 12 months after start of PD, classified into 5 groups.OutcomesPatient survival, technique survival, peritonitis-free survival, and number and length of hospitalization.Analytical ApproachCox regression models for survival analysis; linear regression after log transformation for hospitalization.ResultsThe 3-year patient survival rates for groups of patients with a decrease or increase in serum ferritin levels of ≤ 250 ng/mL, a decrease in serum ferritin levels of > 250-750 ng/mL, a decrease in serum ferritin levels of > 750 ng/mL, an increase in serum ferritin levels of > 250-750 ng/mL, and an increase in serum ferritin levels of > 750 ng/mL were 84.0%, 77.3%, 79.2%, 81.1%, and 67.3%, respectively (P = 0.007), although the association became insignificant after adjusting for confounding factors. Notably, 70.0% of a subgroup had persistent iron overload after the first year of PD. There were no significant differences in survival rates among the control, persistent iron overload, and reversal groups.LimitationsSingle-center retrospective study.ConclusionsA rise in serum ferritin levels of > 750 ng/mL was not associated with patient survival in a multivariable analysis. For patients with iron overload at the initiation of PD, the reversal of iron overload was not associated with any improvement in clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Anemia is a major complication in chronic kidney disease, associated with increased risks of cardiovascular diseases, hospitalizations, higher morbidities, and mortalities.1,2 The severity of anemia correlates with worse clinical outcomes.3 Management typically includes the use of iron replacement therapies, erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs), and hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase enzyme inhibitors.

Iron deficiency is the main cause of inadequate response to ESA. Because ESAs carry risks such as cerebrovascular events and vascular access thrombosis,4 iron is commonly prescribed to reduce ESA dose and minimize transfusion. The KDIGO anemia guideline recommends a trial of oral or intravenous (IV) iron for chronic kidney disease anemia when serum transferrin saturation (TSAT) levels are ≤ 30% and serum ferritin levels are ≤ 500 ng/mL.1 The proactive intravenous iron therapy in hemodialysis patients trial showed that proactive monthly administration of IV iron significantly reduced ESA use and was associated with a lower composite risk of death and other outcomes.5

Despite widespread iron use for chronic kidney disease anemia, evidence of iron overload in patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis (PD) is limited and inconsistent. Published estimates suggest a 10%-28.6% prevalence,6 whereas magnetic resonance imaging scan-based studies indicate iron overload is uncommon in patients undergoing PD7 but frequent in hemodialysis.8 Excessive iron deposition can lead to oxidative stress, liver and cardiac complications,4,9 and may impair host immunity responses, though infection risk from IV iron in patients undergoing dialysis remains uncertain.1 Functional iron deficiency, or iron-restricted erythropoiesis, is defined by low serum TSAT levels with normal or high serum ferritin levels.10 Increased inflammation raises circulating ferritin levels, and high levels are linked to myocardial infarction and carotid plaques in the general population.4 In Guangdong studies, functional iron deficiency was associated with higher mortality,11 and high iron status with a higher incidence and more treatment failure of peritonitis in patients undergoing PD.12 Local Hong Kong data showed that functional iron deficiency was associated with a trend of lower patient survival and higher hospitalization, although the differences were insignificant in a multivariable analysis.13

However, most outcome-focused studies have focused on hemodialysis, leaving uncertainty in PD about the true prevalence of iron overload, the optimal ferritin level range/trajectory to target, and whether persistent iron overload or rising levels of ferritin trends are associated with clinically important outcomes (eg, survival, hospitalization, and peritonitis risk). We hypothesized that persistent iron overload is associated with worse clinical outcomes, including a higher risk of adverse events, and that an unfavorable ferritin trend over time is independently associated with poorer clinical outcomes compared with stable, nonelevated ferritin level patterns.

Methods

This study was approved by the Joint Chinese University of Hong Kong, New Territories East Cluster Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval number CREC-2023.647). All study procedures followed the guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent for participation in the study.

Study Population and Data Collection

This retrospective cohort included 2,029 adults who initiated PD in the New Territories East Cluster between 1 January 2011 and 31 December 2020. Baseline data were retrieved from the Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System (Figure S1). Iron status parameters (serum iron levels, total iron binding capacity, serum TSAT levels, and serum ferritin levels) were reviewed at PD initiation and 1 year after. Other baseline variables included total weekly Kt/V values, residual glomerular filtration rate at 1 year, Charlson comorbidity index value, hemoglobin level, mean corpuscular volume, thalassemia trait, adjusted serum calcium level, serum phosphate level, serum albumin level, fasting blood glucose level, glycated hemoglobin value, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol level, triglycerides level, vitamin B12 level, folate level, and C-reactive protein level. The use of IV iron, oral iron, ESA, and blood transfusions within the first year was recorded.

Definition and Outcomes

Patients were classified into 5 groups according to change in serum ferritin level in the first year of PD: a change in serum ferritin level of ± 250 ng/mL as reference, a decrease in serum ferritin level of > 250-750 ng/mL, a decrease in serum ferritin level of > 750 ng/mL, an increase in serum ferritin level of > 250-750 ng/mL, and an increase in serum ferritin level of > 750 ng/mL. The cut-off values were selected because they represented 50% and 150% of the serum ferritin level that was used to define iron overload.11

A subgroup analysis classified patients by their iron status, as previously described by Luo et al11: reference iron status (serum TSAT level ≥ 20% and serum ferritin level 100-500 ng/mL), absolute iron deficiency (serum TSAT level < 20% and serum ferritin level < 100 ng/mL), low iron storage (serum TSAT level ≥ 20% and serum ferritin < 100 ng/mL), functional iron deficiency (TSAT level < 20% and serum ferritin level > 100 ng/mL), and high iron status (TSAT level ≥ 20% and serum ferritin level > 500 ng/mL). Patients persistently in reference iron status at baseline and 12 months were controls; those persistently in high iron status were the iron overload group; those who changed from high iron status at baseline to reference iron status at 12 months formed the reversed group because their iron status was considered to have normalized.

Patients were followed until December 31, 2021. Outcome measures included patient survival, technique survival, peritonitis-free survival, and number and length of hospitalization. Death within 3 months of transfer to hemodialysis or transplantation was counted as an event for patient survival. Technique survival was censored at transfer to other centers, loss to follow-up, or renal recovery. Patients with peritonitis during the first PD year were excluded from peritonitis-free survival analysis.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM). Baseline characteristics were compared using the χ2 test, 1-way analysis of variance, or Kruskal-Wallis tests. Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed for patient survival, technique survival, and peritonitis-free survival; groups were compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazard models were applied to examine associations between ferritin trend and patient, technique, and peritonitis-free survival. Results were expressed in hazard ratio and 95% confidence interval. The number and length of hospitalization were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test, and multiple linear regression models with log-transformed outcomes were used to adjust for clinical confounders. The same analysis models were applied in the subgroup of persistent or reversed high iron status compared with the control. For Cox regression and linear regression analyses, covariates included age, gender, total weekly Kt/V values and residual glomerular filtration rate in 1 year, Charlson comorbidity index value, hemoglobin level, mean corpuscular volume, thalassemia trait, adjusted serum calcium level, serum phosphate level, C-reactive protein level, serum albumin level, vitamin B12 level, folate level, glycated hemoglobin value, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol level, triglyceride level, use of IV or oral iron, ESA use, and transfusion within the first year of PD. A P-value of < .05 was considered statistically significant. All probabilities were 2-tailed.

Results

We identified 2,029 incidents of patients undergoing PD; 1,443 were excluded because of incomplete data on their iron status at baseline or 12th month from initiation of PD or death within the first year of dialysis. A total of 586 patients were analyzed (Figure S1).

Change in Iron Status and Survival

During a median follow-up of 41.8 (IQR, 29.5-60.8) months, 291 (49.7%) patients passed away. The causes of death were nonperitonitis infections (100 cases), ischemic heart disease (47 cases), peritonitis (38 cases), sudden cardiac death (33 cases), stroke (25 cases), malignancy (6 cases), termination of dialysis (11 cases), other (16 cases), and undetermined causes (15 cases). The Kaplan-Meier plot on patients' survival, grouped according to their change in serum ferritin level, is shown in Figure 1A. Reversal of Iron Overload

A subgroup of 345 patients was analyzed for the effect of reversal of high iron status. In this subgroup, 64 (18.6%) patients remained in reference iron status after the first year of PD (control group); 231 (67.0%) remained in high iron status (persistent iron overload group); and 50 (14.5%) converted from high to reference iron status after the first year of PD (reversed iron overload group) (Figure S1). Their baseline demographics and biochemical parameters were shown in Tables S1 and S2, respectively. Patients with persistent iron overload had the lowest hemoglobin levels, albumin levels, and glycated hemoglobin values compared with the other groups. More patients with persistent iron overload had received ESA, oral iron supplementation, and blood transfusion. The use of intravenous iron was similar.

Reversal of Iron Overload and Survival

In this subgroup analysis, during a median follow-up of 44.1 (IQR, 29.8-63.1) months, 166 (48.1%) patients died. The causes of death were nonperitonitis infections (57 cases), peritonitis (26 cases), sudden cardiac death (20 cases), ischemic heart disease (18 cases), stroke (16 cases), malignancy (5 cases), termination of dialysis (8 cases), other (8 cases), and undetermined causes (8 cases). Reversal of Iron Overload and Hospitalization

The average number of hospitalizations was 2.39 ± 2.57 episodes per year of follow-up, and the average length of hospitalization was 20.21 ± 24.79 days per year. The number and duration of hospitalization were not significantly different between groups of persistent and reversed iron overload (Figure S3). With multiple linear regression models, the reversal of iron overload was not associated with the number or duration of hospitalization (Table S3).

Discussion

In this study, we found that 27% of patients experienced a rise in serum ferritin levels of > 250 ng/mL in the first year of PD, with one-third exceeding 750 ng/mL. Patients with the greatest increase had lower Kt/V values and residual glomerular filtration rate after the first year of dialysis, which is consistent with the previous report by a Korean group.14

Beyond reflecting iron storage, serum ferritin level is a positive acute-phase reactant.15 It is associated with erythropoietin resistance,16 malnutrition, and an increased inflammatory state.17 It is important to note that in this study, we did not classify the iron status by using the latest KDIGO guideline recommendations18 but according to the system described by Luo et al.11 The latest KDIGO guideline recommendations replace “functional iron deficiency” with “iron-restricted erythropoiesis,” defined by using low serum TSAT levels and normal or high serum ferritin levels, characterizing impaired iron release from body stores.10,18 This condition is associated with increased all-cause mortality in patients undergoing PD11 and nondialysis-dependent patients with chronic kidney disease.19,20 High serum ferritin levels are also associated with increased mortality in patients undergoing hemodialysis.21, 22, 23

In our study, a rise in serum ferritin level of > 750 ng/mL was associated with a marginally lower patient survival that was statistically insignificant after adjusting for other clinical confounding factors. Although not statistically significant, a trend of lower technique and peritonitis-free survival was observed in those with a marked rise in serum ferritin levels. Notably, the Kaplan-Meier curves started to diverge after 24 months. A previous study reported that a dialysis vintage of < 2 years was associated with better hydration, nutritional state, and survival in patients who underwent PD compared with patients who underwent hemodialysis, but a longer dialysis vintage reduces these benefits.24 As dialysis vintage increases, there is an accumulation of uremic toxins, hence increased chronic inflammation and malnutrition.25 A rising level of serum ferritin may reflect excess iron storage or impaired utilization; both may be signs of a worsening inflammatory state and/or poor control of underlying comorbidities, predicting adverse clinical outcomes.

Furthermore, iron overload itself may contribute to worse outcomes. Excess iron levels can cause liver fibrosis26 and cardiomyocyte dysfunction.27,28 In our study, 70% of the subgroup of patients had persistent iron overload after 1 year of PD. The prevalence of iron overload differs among previously published reports. Hepatic magnetic resonance imaging scan studies showed severe hepatic iron overload in 30% of patients who underwent hemodialysis.9 Another study found normal iron status predicted favorable survival, whereas functional iron deficiency or high iron storage predicted worse survival in patients who underwent hemodialysis.29 In a study of Chinese patients who underwent PD, high iron status was present in only 5.5%11; both functional iron deficiency and high iron status were independent predictors for patient mortality, and high iron status was associated with increased cardiovascular mortality risk.11 In another study, high iron status was present in 15.3% of patients; both absolute iron deficiency and high iron status were associated with peritonitis risk and treatment failure.12

Our study explored whether reversal of high iron status led to better clinical outcomes. Although not statistically significant, persistent iron overload had a trend of worse patient and technique survival, whereas the reversal of iron overload may be marginally better. Limited data are available regarding any threshold levels of serum ferritin to guide iron replacement therapy. In this regard, lessons learned from the management of iron overload in patients with thalassemia and iron overload because of repeated transfusion can provide valuable insights into the management of iron overload in patients with kidney disease.30

In this study, the change in serum ferritin levels and persistent iron overload were not associated with the number or length of hospitalization. In theory, iron plays a key role in the biological processes of host-pathogen interaction, including DNA synthesis and mitochondrial respiration of many microorganisms. Although the evidence was not robust, the current KDIGO guideline recommends against IV iron use in patients with active systemic infections.4

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study from a single center with a high proportion of patients undergoing PD. As a result, the causal relationship between serum ferritin trends and the reversal of iron overload with clinical outcomes could not be tested, and the generalizability of the results needs to be validated in other patient populations. Second, 1,443 of the 2,029 identified patients were excluded because of incomplete data or death within 1 year of PD. The potential of attrition bias is considerable. Unfortunately, we do not have the necessary data to compare the baseline characteristics of the included and excluded patients and determine the magnitude of the bias. In addition, there was a lack of clinical markers that accurately measure body iron stores. Serum ferritin level reflects iron storage in bone marrow,31 but not the liver parenchyma or myocardium. Although magnetic resonance imaging scans were utilized in previous studies to estimate liver iron content, there is uncertainty about whether magnetic resonance imaging scans overestimate total iron storage in patients undergoing dialysis.32 Further, serum ferritin level was used routinely, but it may be a misleading marker for iron overload.33 It is well known that serum ferritin is a positive acute-phase reactant, whereas TSAT level is a negative acute-phase reactant. Serial measurement of other inflammatory marker levels, such as interleukin-6 level or hepcidin level, was unavailable, where correction of these markers may attenuate the risk associated with high serum ferritin level.4 Although we attempted to adjust the effect of iron status by all available clinical and biochemical factors, there remain residual confounding factors that may not have been considered. Nonetheless, our study provided important epidemiological data on iron overload and the trend in serum ferritin levels in a cohort of an unselected PD population.

In summary, our study is the first to examine the impact of persistence and reversal of iron overload and change in serum ferritin level on clinical outcomes in patients with PD. A rise in serum ferritin levels > 750 ng/mL was associated with worse patient survival, although the association became insignificant after adjusting for other clinical confounding factors. The efficacy and safety of iron supplementation in patients with functional iron deficiency and whether persistent iron overload would lead to worse clinical outcomes remain uncertain. With the current limited methods to assess total iron storage in patients undergoing dialysis, the combination of serum ferritin and TSAT levels remains the most commonly used and readily available way to measure total body iron stores. Future studies are required to determine any threshold levels of serum ferritin and TSAT levels that could guide iron replacement therapies and whether preemptive iron replacement or a withdrawal of iron supplementation would affect clinical outcomes in patients undergoing PD.
