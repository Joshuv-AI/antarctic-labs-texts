Views and reported practices of South African speech-language therapy and audiology undergraduate students on linguistic and cultural integration in training
Authors: Katijah Khoza-Shangase, Farieda Abrahams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSouth Africa’s speech-language therapy (SLT) and audiology professions face challenges in achieving linguistic and cultural integration (i.e., the meaningful incorporation of diverse linguistic and cultural perspectives into training and practice), a critical aspect for effectively serving the country’s diverse population. Limited curriculum content on indigenous languages and cultural competence, along with low diversity of academic and clinical training staff (staff), may hinder students’ preparedness for multilingual and multicultural clinical practice. This study explored undergraduate students’ views and self-reported practices regarding linguistic and cultural integration during their professional training.AimTo explore the views and practices of South African SLT and audiology undergraduate students concerning linguistic and cultural integration in their training.MethodsA cross-sectional convergent mixed-methods survey design was used to gather quantitative and qualitative data from a purposive sample of 48 third- and fourth-year SLT and audiology students across four South African universities. Data were collected using a structured online questionnaire, including Likert-scale items and open-ended questions. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive and inferential statistics, while thematic analysis was applied to qualitative responses.ResultsWithin this sample, participants recognised the importance of linguistic and cultural competence but report feeling inadequately prepared to implement these skills in clinical settings. Key barriers included insufficient curriculum coverage of cultural topics, reliance on untrained interpreters, and a lack of bilingual resources. Students from indigenous language backgrounds reported higher ratings of the importance of linguistic integration than their English-speaking peers (p < .05). Qualitative themes suggested a perceived need for greater curriculum responsiveness, increased staff diversity, and enhanced institutional support to facilitate culturally competent practice.ConclusionThese exploratory findings suggest that curriculum responsiveness, institutional support, and staff diversity may warrant further consideration within ongoing efforts aimed at improving student preparedness for practice in South Africa’s multilingual and multicultural healthcare environment. Introduction

In South Africa, speech-language therapy (SLT) and audiology have a deep-rooted history shaped by colonial and apartheid influences, resulting in professional practices and educational curricula dominated by Western paradigms [1, 2]. This legacy has led to disparities in representation within these professions and has created challenges in aligning healthcare delivery with the linguistic and cultural diversity of the South African population [2, 3]. Despite strides toward transformation, the demographic profile of SLT and audiology students and practitioners still does not fully reflect the racial, linguistic, or cultural landscape of the nation [4, 5]. The healthcare sector in South Africa, including SLT and audiology, predominantly utilises English and Afrikaans, languages that are not the first language for the majority Black African population, where languages like isiZulu, isiXhosa, and Sepedi are more widely spoken [6]. Studies reveal that the limited linguistic diversity among practitioners hinders effective communication and culturally responsive care for many South African patients [2, 7]. For instance, research by Mdlalo et al. [8] showed that the majority of SLT professionals were not conversant in indigenous languages, which impacts patient-centred care as these practitioners rely on English or Afrikaans, often failing to incorporate patients’ cultural and linguistic needs into their practice. Within this context, linguistic integration refers to the ability of practitioners and trainees to engage meaningfully across multiple languages in clinical and educational settings, while cultural integration refers to the incorporation of diverse cultural values, beliefs, and practices into training and service delivery. These constructs are closely aligned with, but distinct from, cultural competence, which broadly encompasses the knowledge, skills, and attitudes required to provide effective care across diverse populations [1, 2, 5, 8].

The academic environment further reflects this lack of integration [9, 10]. Many SLT and audiology programmes are anchored in Western methodologies that overlook or undervalue indigenous knowledge systems, creating an environment where Black students may feel marginalised [9]. The dominant “White gaze,” as described by scholars like Khunou et al. [11], perpetuates a sense of otherness and alienation among Black students. Despite efforts to diversify student bodies, informal segregation often persists within academic settings, and Black students report feeling pressured to assimilate into a culture that undervalues their identities and lived experiences [9, 12]. There are also gaps in SLT and audiology curricula concerning training on cultural competence and linguistically appropriate practices [13, 14]. This lack of integration means that Black students are often inadequately supported in learning how to navigate both the dominant academic culture and the requirements of culturally sensitive practice for diverse communities [15]. Additionally, student experiences indicate that lecturers and clinical supervisors may recognise cultural divides but do not actively address them, leaving students without sufficient guidance on managing linguistic and cultural diversity in clinical settings [10, 13].

The South African government’s higher education transformation policies, such as the Education White Paper 3 of 1997, have mandated initiatives to increase diversity and inclusion within academic institutions [16]. Yet, the pace of transformation within SLT and audiology departments has been slow, often confined to surface-level diversity metrics rather than deep structural changes that foster inclusive environments. Transformation in these professions would entail revising curricula to incorporate local languages, histories, and health beliefs, enabling students to better connect with and serve South Africa’s culturally diverse population. Within SLT and audiology training, integration across linguistic and cultural divides can be understood as the extent to which educational programmes intentionally create opportunities for students to engage with, learn from, and apply diverse linguistic and cultural perspectives in both classroom and clinical contexts. This includes not only formal curriculum content but also informal student interactions, clinical supervision practices, and institutional culture. Integration across linguistic and cultural divides in training is thus both a challenge and a critical necessity. Bridging these divides is essential for producing SLT and audiology professionals who can provide ethical, equitable and effective care in a multilingual society. While existing literature has documented many of these systemic challenges, there remains a need to better understand how students themselves experience and navigate these dynamics during their training, particularly in relation to their day-to-day practices and perceptions of preparedness in these fields. This study seeks to capture the experiences and practices of SLT and audiology students as they navigate this complex landscape, highlighting their perspectives on integration and their practices around managing diversity in their professional preparation. Understanding these views and practices may contribute to ongoing discussions regarding curriculum development and transformation efforts that seek to embrace South Africa’s cultural and linguistic plurality, while supporting the preparation of future SLT and audiology professionals to serve diverse populations.

Methods

Aim

The aim of this study was to explore the views and practices of South African SLT and audiology undergraduate students regarding linguistic and cultural integration during their training.

Objectives

The study addressed the following specific objectives:

To explore the views of South African SLT and audiology undergraduate students regarding linguistic and cultural integration during their training.

To explore the views of South African SLT and audiology undergraduate students regarding linguistic and cultural integration during their training.

To describe the practices of South African SLT and audiology undergraduate students related to linguistic and cultural integration during their training.

To describe the practices of South African SLT and audiology undergraduate students related to linguistic and cultural integration during their training.

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design, chosen for its ability to capture a snapshot of participants’ views and practices at a specific point in time [17]. A convergent mixed-methods design was adopted, in which quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently, analysed separately, and then integrated during interpretation. In this design, quantitative data (Likert-scale items) were prioritised to describe patterns in students’ views, while qualitative data (open-ended responses) were used to elaborate and contextualise these findings. Integration occurred at the interpretation stage through the systematic comparison and triangulation of quantitative trends and qualitative themes, rather than simple cross-referencing.

Study setting and participants

The study was conducted across South African universities offering undergraduate programmes in SLT and audiology. At the time of the study, seven public universities offered such programmes. One university (the University of Fort Hare) was excluded as it did not yet have third- or fourth-year students. Of the remaining six universities approached, four agreed to participate: the University of KwaZulu-Natal, the University of Pretoria, the University of the Witwatersrand, and the University of Cape Town. These institutions represent a mix of historically advantaged and historically disadvantaged contexts. Participants were recruited from third and fourth-year SLT and audiology students, as they are more likely to have sufficient exposure to clinical settings and experience with multicultural and multilingual patient populations.

Sampling strategy

A non-probability purposive sampling strategy was used to recruit participants, targeting students with direct experiences of linguistically and culturally diverse training environments. Recruitment involved contacting Heads of Departments (HODs) at participating universities, who distributed the survey link to eligible students. The final sample consisted of 48 participants. The total number of eligible students across institutions could not be definitively established, as only three universities provided enrolment numbers due to Protection of Personal Information (POPI) Act restrictions. Consequently, a precise response rate could not be calculated. However, based on available enrolment data and reasonable estimates, it is assumed that approximately 50 third- and fourth-year students are enrolled per institution across the seven public universities offering SLT and audiology programmes, yielding an estimated national population of approximately 350 eligible students. Within this context, the sample of 48 participants represents approximately 13.7% of the national cohort, and an estimated 24% of students across the four participating institutions. These estimates should be interpreted cautiously but provide contextual insight into the relative scale of the sample. Recruitment was further affected by institutional gatekeeping processes and the sensitive nature of the study topic, all of which may have contributed to selection bias. The sample comprised approximately 50% SLT students, 39.6% audiology students, and 10.4% enrolled in dual programmes. In terms of year of study, 55.8% were fourth-year students and 44.2% third-year students.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a structured online questionnaire developed specifically for this study. The questionnaire used in the present study was developed as part of a broader investigation into transformation and diversity within South African speech-language therapy and audiology education. Findings from the same dataset have previously been reported in relation to curriculum and clinical service transformation within a decolonisation framework [18]. However, the current analysis focuses specifically on linguistic and cultural integration, students’ perceived preparedness for multicultural practice, and their self-reported integration practices. The instrument was developed inductively using Google Forms, informed by a review of literature on cultural competence, institutional transformation, and linguistic diversity in healthcare education. The questionnaire included sections designed to capture:

Demographic Information: Age, gender, year of study, university attended, and linguistic background.

Demographic Information: Age, gender, year of study, university attended, and linguistic background.

Views on Linguistic and Cultural Integration: Using Likert-scale items to measure students’ beliefs and attitudes towards the importance and impact of integrating linguistic and cultural competence in their training.

Views on Linguistic and Cultural Integration: Using Likert-scale items to measure students’ beliefs and attitudes towards the importance and impact of integrating linguistic and cultural competence in their training.

Reported Practices: Open-ended questions and multiple-choice items to capture the types of practices and interactions students experience in relation to cultural and linguistic integration.

Reported Practices: Open-ended questions and multiple-choice items to capture the types of practices and interactions students experience in relation to cultural and linguistic integration.

Perceived Challenges and Support Needs: Open-ended questions that allowed students to discuss barriers to integration and support they feel would improve their training experience in this area.

Perceived Challenges and Support Needs: Open-ended questions that allowed students to discuss barriers to integration and support they feel would improve their training experience in this area.

The survey included a combination of Likert-scale items (5-point scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree), multiple-choice questions, and open-ended questions. The questionnaire comprised a total of 48 items across six sections, with approximately 15–20 Likert-scale items distributed across domains assessing perceived importance, preparedness, and curriculum adequacy. Likert-scale items were analysed both as individual items and, where conceptually appropriate, as conceptual groupings (rather than formal psychometric scales) of perceived preparedness and perceived importance. The instrument was self-developed and has not been previously published.

Internal consistency reliability (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha) was not calculated, as the instrument included a combination of distinct constructs rather than a single unidimensional scale. Likert-scale items were analysed primarily at the individual-item level, and where conceptually appropriate, grouped descriptively into domains of perceived preparedness and perceived importance. These domains were not intended to function as formal psychometric scales, but rather as conceptually related indicators developed for exploratory purposes. Consequently, calculation of Cronbach’s alpha was not considered appropriate. Instead, content validity and clarity were strengthened through pilot testing, which resulted in refinement of item wording and structure. Methodological triangulation, through the integration of quantitative and qualitative data, was used to enhance interpretive robustness rather than to establish measurement reliability. An English-language version of the survey is provided as a supplementary file.

Pilot testing

A pilot study was conducted with five third- and fourth-year students to assess clarity, relevance, and usability of the questionnaire. These participants were not included in the final study sample. Feedback from the pilot resulted in refinement and shortening of the questionnaire, as well as the inclusion of an additional item addressing segregation among students.

Data collection procedure

The survey was administered online using a secure platform. A link to the survey was distributed via email and institutional communication channels through HODs. Participation was voluntary. Participants were provided with an information sheet detailing the study purpose, confidentiality measures, and their rights. Electronic informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Data collection occurred over a one-month period.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive and inferential statistics in SPSS [19]. Descriptive statistics included frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Likert-scale responses were treated as ordinal data, with mean scores reported for interpretive purposes. Inferential analyses included independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA to examine group differences in key outcome variables (e.g., perceived importance, preparedness, and curriculum adequacy). These tests were selected as appropriate for comparing mean differences across groups based on the level and distribution of the data. Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed prior to analysis. Assumption testing indicated that the data did not significantly violate normality (Shapiro–Wilk tests p > .05) or homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test p > .05), supporting the appropriateness of parametric analyses. Although Likert-scale data are ordinal in nature, parametric tests were retained because the assumptions for these analyses were met and because such approaches are commonly used for exploratory analyses of Likert-scale responses. Nevertheless, given the modest sample size and unequal subgroup distributions, inferential findings should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Where significant differences were identified, post-hoc tests (Tukey HSD) were conducted. Effect sizes (e.g., Cohen’s d, eta squared) were calculated to support interpretation of findings.

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative data from open-ended responses were analysed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s [20] six-step framework. All 48 participants provided at least one qualitative response, with response lengths ranging from brief statements to more detailed reflections. Initial coding was conducted by the primary researcher, who generated a preliminary codebook. Approximately 20–25 initial codes were generated through iterative engagement with the dataset and subsequently organised into broader categories through constant comparison. These codes were refined and collapsed into four overarching themes that captured patterned meaning across the dataset.

Two additional researchers independently reviewed and refined the codes. Themes were developed through an iterative process involving coding, comparison, and abstraction. To enhance rigor, peer debriefing and reflexive practices were employed. Formal inter-rater reliability statistics (e.g., Cohen’s kappa) were not calculated, as this study adopted a reflexive thematic analysis approach, which prioritises interpretive depth over coding quantification. Instead, rigour was ensured through peer debriefing, whereby supervisors acted as critical “devil’s advocates” to interrogate coding decisions, challenge potential researcher bias, and validate the coherence and credibility of the thematic interpretation.

The primary researcher maintained a reflexive journal documenting analytic decisions and potential biases. Supervisors acted as independent reviewers (“outsiders”) to challenge interpretations and ensure analytic depth. Discrepancies in coding were resolved through structured consensus discussions rather than simple informal agreement. While formal data saturation was not explicitly assessed due to the survey-based nature of the qualitative data, thematic analytic sufficiency was considered achieved when no substantially new patterns emerged and when supervisors, acting as independent “outsiders,” confirmed that the final themes adequately represented the breadth and depth of the dataset. Given the nature of survey-based qualitative data, thematic analysis was applied cautiously, with explicit acknowledgment of the limited depth and breadth of responses. Accordingly, the qualitative findings should be interpreted as thematic patterns derived from brief participant reflections rather than as deeply elaborated accounts that might emerge from interviews or focus groups.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative data

Integration of data occurred at the interpretation stage, where qualitative themes were used to explain, expand, or nuance quantitative findings. This process involved identifying points of convergence (agreement), divergence (disagreement), and complementarity between datasets, thereby strengthening the overall validity of the findings.

Ethical considerations

Prior to the study being conducted, permission to conduct the study was obtained from all relevant stakeholders with ethical clearance secured from the University of the Witwatersrand Human Research Ethics Committee (Non-Medical) (number H19/09/01). Informed consent was obtained electronically from each participant before starting the survey, with assurances of confidentiality and anonymity. No personally identifiable information was collected, and all responses were stored on a secure server accessible only to the research team. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. Furthermore, the work adhered to the World Medical Association (WMA) Declaration of Helsinki on Research with Human Subjects of 1964 (revised 2001) throughout.

Rigor and trustworthiness

To ensure rigor and reliability, the study used methodological triangulation, combining quantitative and qualitative data. The pilot test contributed to the validity of the instrument, and thematic analysis by three independent researchers contributed to the trustworthiness of the qualitative findings [17]. Credibility was enhanced through peer debriefing, reflexive journaling, and iterative coding. Dependability was supported through transparent documentation of analytic procedures, and confirmability was strengthened through collaborative coding and consensus-building among researchers. These strategies were prioritised over statistical measures of agreement to align with the interpretive nature of reflexive thematic analysis. Given the exploratory nature of the study and the use of a self-developed instrument, findings should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive.

Data management

For this study, data management protocols were established to ensure the secure, confidential, and accurate handling of participant information [21]. Data collected via the online survey platform were encrypted and stored on a secure, password-protected server accessible only to the research team. Each participant’s responses were anonymised by assigning a unique identifier. Raw data were exported to SPSS for quantitative analysis and NVivo for qualitative coding. All data files were stored in encrypted formats and will be retained for five years in accordance with institutional policies, after which they will be securely destroyed.

Relationship to prior work

The data presented in this manuscript were collected as part of a larger, multi-institutional investigation into the landscape of transformation and diversity in South African speech-language therapy and audiology education. While both this study and a previously published report [18] utilize the same purposive sample of 48 third- and fourth-year students and the same 48-item structured online questionnaire, they address distinct and mutually exclusive research objectives. The prior publication focused on structural curriculum transformation, specifically investigating the inclusion of Afrocentric content and indigenous languages within a decolonization framework. In contrast, the current manuscript focuses on the functional constructs of linguistic and cultural integration, exploring students’ self-reported clinical practices, perceived preparedness, and the specific barriers (such as reliance on untrained interpreters) they encounter in multicultural settings. Furthermore, the analytical approach in this study differs: it utilizes inferential analysis (t-tests and ANOVA) to compare perceptions across years of study and student linguistic backgrounds, whereas the 2025 publication employed Chi-square analysis to examine the association between indigenous language proficiency and clinical confidence. These distinct lenses provide a complementary yet unique understanding of the challenges facing healthcare education in South Africa.

Participants reported a range of practices used to navigate linguistic and cultural diversity during clinical training (Table 3). Inferential analysis of views and practices

Given the study’s exploratory design and sample size, inferential analyses focused on group mean comparisons rather than broader association testing across multiple categorical variables. A one-way ANOVA was conducted to examine differences in perceived importance of linguistic and cultural integration across linguistic groups (English, IsiZulu, Afrikaans/other combined to address small cell sizes). Results indicated a statistically significant difference between groups F(2, 45) = 4.12, p = .022, η² = 0.16 (moderate effect). Post-hoc comparisons (Tukey HSD) suggested that students identifying with indigenous language groups reported higher importance ratings (M = 4.50 ± 0.62) compared to English-speaking students (M = 3.80 ± 0.95). Given the unequal group sizes, modest sample size, and exploratory nature of the analysis, these findings should be interpreted with considerable caution and viewed as preliminary. Replication in larger and more representative samples is required before any broader conclusions can be drawn.

Discussion

This study provides insights into the views and self-reported practices of South African undergraduate SLT and audiology students regarding linguistic and cultural integration during their training. While these data originate from the same broader study that informed a previous publication on curriculum and clinical service transformation [18], the present analysis provides a distinct examination of linguistic and cultural integration as experienced in students’ day-to-day educational and clinical contexts. The results highlight the complexities these students face, including a lack of preparation for multicultural clinical practice, challenges with language barriers, limited institutional support, and a strong desire for a more inclusive curriculum and diverse staff representation. While these findings are drawn from a cross-sectional, non-probability sample and should be interpreted cautiously, they nonetheless point to a consistent pattern: students value linguistic and cultural integration but report variability in preparedness and identify structural and curricular gaps in their training environments, resonating with broader calls for decolonising and transforming South African healthcare education to better reflect the diversity of the population [22, 23, 24].

As far as preparedness for multicultural clinical practice was concerned, the majority of participants reported feeling inadequately prepared to address cultural and linguistic diversity in clinical settings. This aligns with prior research indicating that SLT and audiology training programmes in South Africa still heavily reflect Western, Eurocentric models [3, 25], which often do not incorporate the cultural nuances and linguistic diversity that are critical to effective healthcare in a South African context. A lack of comprehensive training in cultural competence was frequently noted, and students reported a need for curricula that engage more deeply with local languages, cultural values, and social determinants of health. While participants demonstrated awareness of the importance of cultural competence, this did not consistently translate into confidence in clinical application, suggesting a gap between theoretical emphasis and practical training. International studies echo similar challenges, emphasising that healthcare education often fails to provide students with the requisite skills to work across cultures [26]. However, in South Africa, where over 80% of the population identifies as Black and where 11 official languages are spoken, this gap has more significant implications. In this context, even modest deficits in training may have disproportionate consequences for equity in care. The lack of cultural competence training has led to missed opportunities for fostering understanding, trust, and effective communication with patients, particularly those from indigenous and rural backgrounds. Within this sample, participants perceived current training approaches as insufficiently preparing them for these realities. Therefore, while causal claims cannot be made from the current data, these exploratory findings suggest a need to strengthen curricular approaches in SLT and audiology to better prepare practitioners for diverse populations.

Language barriers emerged as a central challenge, with many students reporting reliance on interpreters, although often with limited training on how to use them effectively. This finding is consistent with literature highlighting the impact of language discordance on healthcare outcomes in multilingual contexts [6, 8]. However, the current findings further suggest that interpreter use is often informal and insufficiently supported by training, which may limit its effectiveness as a communication strategy. While interpreters can help bridge linguistic gaps, participants in this study reported feeling uncomfortable and disconnected when working through interpreters, as it impeded direct patient engagement. The South African context, with its history of linguistic exclusion, particularly of African languages, amplifies this issue. SLT and audiology students are often trained in English and Afrikaans, leaving them ill-prepared to serve patients who speak isiZulu, isiXhosa, or other indigenous languages [2]. Despite recognition in literature of the need for interpreter services, including specific training on interpreter use [27], many South African institutions still lack formalised interpreter services or bilingual resources, forcing students into makeshift communication strategies that may undermine patient-centred care. It is important to acknowledge, however, that these observations are based on self-reported student experiences and were not triangulated with direct observations of clinical practice. Consequently, they should be interpreted as perceptions rather than objective measures of communication effectiveness or clinical outcomes.

The results indicate a strong demand among students for inclusive curricula that incorporate indigenous knowledge and languages, as well as for a staff that reflects the diversity of South Africa’s population. Similar findings have been reported in studies emphasising the need for culturally and linguistically relevant education in SLT and audiology, particularly in post-colonial contexts where training programmes are often entrenched in Western paradigms [1, 8]. The current study participants noted that the lack of diversity in both the curriculum and staff limits their understanding of and preparedness for the realities of practice in a multicultural society. The desire for diverse staff representation is particularly noteworthy. Previous research has shown that staff diversity enhances cultural sensitivity and provides role models for students from minority backgrounds, fostering a more inclusive educational environment [10, 11]. In the South African context, staff diversity could also provide a counter-narrative to the “White gaze,” which many Black students find alienating. The need for inclusive staff and curriculum is a significant factor in the broader movement for decolonising South African higher education, reflecting student-led demands for representation and relevance in training [23]. Within this sample, students perceived staff diversity as an important contributor to inclusion and professional development. However, because the current study did not directly measure the impact of staff diversity on educational or clinical outcomes, these perceptions should be viewed as generating hypotheses and priorities for future research rather than providing evidence of causality. The current findings complement, rather than duplicate, those reported by Abrahams and Khoza-Shangase [18]. Whereas the earlier study examined students’ perceptions of curriculum transformation and the inclusion of Afrocentric and indigenous language content, the present analysis demonstrates how these broader structural issues may manifest in students’ perceived preparedness for multicultural practice, reliance on interpreters, and experiences of linguistic and cultural integration within clinical training environments.

The qualitative findings also pointed to perceived gaps in institutional support, with students citing a lack of structured resources, such as bilingual materials, standardised interpreter services, and formal support programmes. The reliance on ad-hoc strategies to manage linguistic and cultural diversity not only adds stress to students but also risks limiting the quality of care provided to patients. Participants’ comments echo findings from previous studies suggesting that the absence of institutional support in addressing cultural competence contributes to clinician discomfort and ineffective patient care [8, 14, 26]. In South Africa, the importance of institutional support is heightened by the linguistic and cultural complexity of the population. The lack of resources, as highlighted by students, may reflect broader systemic challenges within the healthcare and educational systems, where disparities in resource allocation persist as remnants of apartheid [10, 12]. Within this sample, students frequently identified the need for bilingual teaching materials and more structured interpreter support. These findings suggest potential areas for institutional attention and future investigation. Although not emanating directly from the current findings, the potential role of technological innovations in supporting language access may warrant further exploration in future research, particularly in relation to how such tools could be integrated into training and clinical practice in resource-constrained contexts [28]. Any such approaches would require empirical evaluation before implementation recommendations could be made.

The inferential findings indicating differences across linguistic groups suggest that students from indigenous language backgrounds may attribute greater importance to linguistic and cultural integration. This aligns with literature indicating that individuals who experience linguistic marginalisation may be more attuned to its impact [29]. Given the modest sample size, unequal subgroup distributions, and exploratory nature of the statistical analyses, this finding should be interpreted with caution and requires replication in larger studies before firm conclusions can be drawn. In South Africa, where English-speaking students may not face the same linguistic and cultural barriers as their indigenous language-speaking counterparts, this difference raises the need for training programmes to instil an awareness of linguistic privilege and its implications in healthcare. These findings also speak to the broader issue of equity in SLT and audiology training, where English-speaking students may unintentionally perpetuate linguistic dominance in clinical practice. Encouraging students to engage with indigenous languages and providing cross-cultural communication training may help foster a more equitable healthcare environment, as outlined by the South African Department of Health’s principles of Batho Pele, which emphasise patient-centred and accessible healthcare for all citizens. It is important to note that, while differences between linguistic groups were observed, the study did not assess behavioural outcomes or clinical practices directly. Therefore, interpretations regarding broader systemic dynamics, such as linguistic dominance, should be approached with caution and framed as areas for further investigation rather than definitive conclusions. The findings of this study are consistent with evidence from both local and international literature, which emphasises the importance of cultural and linguistic integration in healthcare training. In line with studies from other multicultural and multilingual LMICs, the experiences reported by participants in this sample suggest challenges related to culturally relevant resources and cultural competence training [30, 31]. Additionally, the need for an inclusive curriculum and diverse staff representation resonates with calls for transformation within South African higher education more broadly, as reflected in student-led movements for decolonisation and curriculum reform [23].

While this study provides valuable insights into the experiences of SLT and audiology students in South Africa, its findings should be interpreted within the context of several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small and limited to students from universities who were willing to participate, which may reduce the generalisability of the findings to all South African SLT and audiology students. Furthermore, the inability to determine the total eligible sample, due to institutional data-sharing constraints (e.g., POPIA), limits the calculation of a response rate and introduces potential selection bias. Students with particularly strong views or experiences regarding linguistic and cultural integration may have been more likely to participate, potentially influencing the findings. Additionally, the cross-sectional design provides a snapshot of students’ views and practices at a single point in time, limiting insights into how these perceptions might change throughout their training. Lastly, self-reported data may introduce bias, as students might provide socially desirable responses rather than fully reflecting their experiences and practices around linguistic and cultural integration. The study also lacked triangulation with observed clinical practice, meaning that reported practices and preparedness could not be verified against actual clinical behaviour. Furthermore, the qualitative findings were derived from survey-based open-ended responses rather than in-depth interviews or focus groups, which may have limited thematic depth and nuance. In addition, the modest sample size and uneven distribution across subgroups limited the feasibility of conducting more complex or non-parametric statistical analyses (e.g., chi-square tests across multiple categorical variables), and findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. The use of a survey-based instrument may also have constrained the depth of qualitative responses, which should be considered when interpreting thematic findings. These limitations highlight the need for further research to validate and extend these findings across broader and more diverse populations. Future research could address these limitations by including a larger, more diverse sample and employing longitudinal designs.

Conclusion

This study explored the views and practices of South African undergraduate SLT and audiology students regarding linguistic and cultural integration within their training. Findings from this cross-sectional, non-probability sample should be interpreted cautiously, yet they consistently indicate persistent gaps in current SLT and audiology education, where cultural competence and linguistic diversity remain insufficiently addressed. While students value linguistic and cultural integration and report attempting to implement inclusive practices, they face significant challenges due to limited curriculum content, scarce institutional resources and support, and a lack of diverse representation among staff. These constraints may limit, rather than definitively determine, students’ readiness to provide culturally competent care, particularly in South Africa’s multicultural, multilingual healthcare context. These findings extend previous work on curriculum and clinical service transformation by providing a more focused examination of how students experience and navigate linguistic and cultural integration within their training.

Rather than representing evidence-based prescriptions, the findings of this study highlight several considerations for future research, curriculum development, and policy dialogue. Firstly, future research and curriculum review processes may explore the incorporation of cultural competence, indigenous language exposure, and awareness of linguistic privilege into SLT and audiology training. Such curricular enhancements should be developed and evaluated iteratively, including the use of case studies and assessments that reflect diverse clinical realities. Secondly, future institutional initiatives could examine the feasibility and effectiveness of formal interpreter services and expanded access to bilingual and culturally relevant teaching materials. However, these considerations should be viewed as aspirational and context-dependent, given resource constraints across institutions. Thirdly, further research could investigate the relationship between staff diversity, mentorship experiences, and student preparedness, while institutions may consider strategies to broaden representation and cultural competence among academic and clinical educators. Lastly, future policy dialogue and empirical inquiry may explore approaches to promoting cross-cultural engagement and structured student support systems that contribute to more inclusive training environments aligned with South Africa’s commitment to equitable healthcare. The potential impact of such interventions requires further empirical investigation.

The integration of linguistic and cultural competence within SLT and audiology programmes remains an important component of broader transformation efforts in South African higher education. While this study does not provide generalisable evidence, it offers empirically grounded insights from student perspectives that can inform future research, curriculum development, and policy dialogue. Continued context-sensitive efforts are required to ensure that SLT and audiology education aligns with the needs of the diverse populations it serves.
