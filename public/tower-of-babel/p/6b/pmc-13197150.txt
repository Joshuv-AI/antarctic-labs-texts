Prebiotic Diet Enhances Suppression of Tumor Necrosis Factor-Alpha Production in Response to Lipopolysaccharide after Exhaustive Exercise
Authors: Hiromi Yano, Ryo Fujii, Rikuho Tahara, Chihiro Watanabe, Hiroki Hamada, Takumi Kono, Takafumi Aoki, Michael J. Kremenik, Eri Oyanagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough it is well known that severe exercise induces temporary immunosuppression, it remains unclear the effect of gut microbiota on this phenomenon.MethodsIn this study, to investigate the effect of gut microbiota on the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α production immediately after the exhaustive exercise, C3H/HeN male mice were (1) treated with or without antibiotic (AB), (2) transplanted cecal content from a 5% partially hydrolyzed guar-gum (PHGG) diet, which is classified as low viscous in dietary fiber, fed mice, and (3) intake PHGG diet for 6 weeks. The mice were stimulated by LPS administration immediately after loaded forced exhaustive exercise.ResultsAfter the exhaustive exercise, plasma TNF-α concentration in AB mice prior to response from LPS was significantly higher than that in control mice (p < 0.01). On the other hand, differences in transplanted cecal contents did not affect TNF-α production by exercise prior to LPS administration. Direct intake of PHGG, however, attenuated the increase in plasma TNF-α concentration in response to LPS, compared with both non-fiber and control diet groups (p < 0.01).ConclusionThese results suggest that LPS-induced TNF-α production immediately after intense exercise is regulated depending on both the composition of the gut microbiota and the material being fermented.

Introduction

Temporary immunosuppression occurs immediately after high-intensity exercise, increasing the risk of upper respiratory tract infections and other infectious diseases [1]. It should be noted that evidence from both human [2–4] and animal [5–7] studies indicates that intense exercise may lead to an increased risk of infectious diseases. We have already shown that several pathogens associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)-induced innate immune responses were attenuated by intense exercise [8–11]. Furthermore, exhaustive exercise-induced innate immune suppression is greatly inhibited by specifically inhibiting the β-adrenergic receptor. And also, the exhaustive exercise suppresses cytokine production without downregulating its mRNA expression, suggesting a catecholamine-mediated, independent post-transcriptional mechanism underlying exercise-induced immune suppression [8, 9]. A recent study has suggested the role of gut microbiota-derived metabolites as critical regulators of the gut-immune axis, contributing to maintaining intestinal homeostasis [12]. It has, however, never been clear how gut microbiota respond to immune responses after intense exercise; the ability of the gut microbiota to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) derived from dietary fiber intake as a survival strategy of symbiotic bacteria. Additionally, immune activation, including anti-inflammation in the intestinal tract, is regulated by gut microbiota via SCFAs production [13, 14]. Especially, a partially hydrolyzed guar-gum (PHGG) diet, which is classified as low viscous in dietary fibers, induces a high fermentation in gut microbiota [13].

On the other hand, it is easy to understand that many mammals must hunt for food through strenuous exercise. Additionally, after severe exercise, there is a need for highly active gut immunity against food exposed pathogens [15]. Thus, in the natural world, exercise and nutrients are linked. It remains, however, unclear the responses in gut microbiota on immune responses after intense exercise.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is widely recognized as a potent inducer of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the PAMPs. LPS, gram-negative bacterial component, binds to toll-like receptor (TLR) 4, and then induces nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) to translocate into the nuclei. As a result of strong innate immune responses, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α are induced [16]. In fact, we have already shown that LPS-induced increases in the level of plasma TNF-α were inhibited by intense exercise [8, 9]. The aim of this study was to investigate the effect of gut microbiota on LPS-induced TNF-α production immediately after the exhaustive exercise.

Materials and Methods

Eight-week-old C3H/HeN male mice were used for the experiments (Clea, Tokyo, Japan). Mice were kept in a group housing environment at a room temperature of 22 ± 1°C with a 12-h light-dark cycle from 8:00–20:00. Drinking water and food were provided ad libitum. Sample sizes were determined based on the existing literature of a similar study [13]. This study was conducted with the approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kawasaki University of Medical Welfare (No. 23–007).

Experiment 1: Antibiotic (AB) Treatment

Animals and AB Treatment

The mice were randomly divided into 4 groups: control and sedentary (CS, n = 10), C and exercise (CEx, n = 10), AB treatment and S (ABS, n = 10), and ABEx (n = 12). The AB cocktail consisted of ampicillin 1 g/L (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), neomycin 1 g/L (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), metronidazole 1 g/L (Merck), vancomycin 0.5 g/L (Merck), and artificial sweetener Splendor 2.5 g/L (Tokyo Kasei Kogyo, Tokyo, Japan). The cocktail was given ad libitum for 7 days.

Exercise Load

The treadmill running protocol was as follows: the starting speed was 9 m/min. This speed was increased 2 m/min every 3 min until it reached 17 m/min. Thereafter, we continued to increase the speed by 1 m/min every 3 min until exhaustion. Exhaustion was defined as the point at which a mouse refused to run despite being given mild touches. Electric shock was not used during the treadmill run [9].

LPS Stimulation

After the AB treatment, C3H/HeN mice were stimulated by LPS (Escherichia coli 055:B5, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) 1 mg/kg (i.v.), immediately after the exhaustive exercise. Blood samples were collected from the eye vessel before exercise and at 1, 3, and 6 h after LPS injection in mice under isoflurane anesthesia, and plasma TNF-α concentrations were analyzed using an ELISA kit [13].

Experiment 2: Gut Microbiota Transplantation

Animals and Gut Microbial Transplantation

In the next experiment, recipient mice were transplanted with feces from PHGG-fed donor mice and were stimulated with LPS after exhaustive exercise in order to investigate the direct effect of gut microbiota on exercise-induced TNF-α response to LPS. Donor mice were fed with a non-fiber diet (NF, n = 22), control diet (CD, n = 36), and 5% PHGG diet (PHGG, n = 6) for 6–8 weeks. The cecal contents were collected from NF, CD, and PHGG mice matched for age and sex and pooled. Cecal contents were promptly transferred into 2-mL round-bottom tubes containing twice the volume of autoclaved physiological saline and homogenized for 1 min at 200 Hz using a microtube homogenizer BC-G10 (BMC, Tokyo, Japan). The homogenate was immediately centrifuged to remove large particulate and fibrous material. Fresh fecal slurry was then pooled, diluted to a concentration of 100 mg/mL [17, 18]. The recipient mice were randomly divided into 4 groups: oral administration of only vehicle group (non-transplantation: N-TP, n = 10), transplantation of cecal microbiota after non-fiber diet (NF-TP, n = 10), transplantation of cecal microbiota after control diet (CD-TP, n = 10), and transplantation of cecal microbiota after 5% PHGG diet (PHGG-TP, n = 10). All mice were given the same AB cocktail (100 μL/day) used in experiment 1 for 7 days. The cecal microbiota transplantations were performed via gastric gavage of freshly prepared material on three times over a 2-week period, beginning 48 h after discontinuation of ABs and placement on untreated drinking water. Cecal microbiota transplantations were initiated by placing 200 μL of the donor cecal contents directly into the oral cavity of mice [17, 18]. Transplanted mice were maintained in sterile cages until treadmill running load.

Exercise Load and LPS Stimulation

The protocol for treadmill running was the same as in the experiment 1. Also, as well as the experiment 1, each mouse was stimulated by LPS (1 mg/kg, i.v.) immediately after the exhaustive exercise, and then blood samples were collected before exercise and at 1, 3, and 6 h after LPS injection.

Experiment 3: PHGG Intake

PHGG-Fed Mice

In the final experiment, PHGG (online suppl. Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551426) were fed to mice for 6 weeks. They were stimulated with LPS after exhaustive exercise in order to investigate the effect of PHGG-containing diet intake under the presence of gut microbiota on exercise-induced TNF-α response to LPS. The mice were randomly divided into 3 groups: control diet (CD, n = 11), non-fiber diet (NF, n = 12), and 5% PHGG diet (PHGG, n = 14).

Exercise Load and LPS Stimulation

The protocol for treadmill running, LPS stimulation, and blood sampling were the same as in the experiments 1 and 2.

ELISA

The TNF-α concentration in plasma was analyzed using an ELISA with commercially available kits (Mouse TNF-alpha Quantikine ELISA Kit, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN). The minimum detectable dose of the TNF-α range was 1.88 pg/mL. All sample determinations were measured in duplicate and mean values were calculated.

Statistical Analysis

Data were expressed as beeswarm plots and boxplots, but data of the food intake were expressed as bar plots, showing means or means ± SEM. Statistical calculations were carried out with free software R (ver. 4.5.0) and R studio (ver. 2025.05.0). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using the Levene test to guide the choice between parametric and non-parametric analyses. Since parametric analysis was employed on all datasets, either analysis of variance (ANOVA) or t tests were used. Data of body weight and food intake in experiment 1 were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. We conducted an unpaired t test with and without AB drinking for the values of exhaustive running time. Also, exhaustive exercise rates of the mice were determined using Kaplan-Meier curves [19], and comparisons between groups were analyzed using the log-rank test. In experiments 2 and 3, data of body weight, food intake, and exhaustive running time were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. The values of changes in plasma TNF-α concentrations before and after exhaustive exercise were analyzed using two-way repeated measure ANOVA. Post hoc Bonferroni tests were conducted. The p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, AB-treated mice did not attenuate the LPS-induced TNF-α production even if the mice were running until exhaustion. On the other hand, gut microbiota transplantation from PHGG-fed mice was no affected by the response to LPS inducible TNF-α production after exhaustive exercise. However, directly PHGG dietary intake for 6 weeks significantly suppressed LPS-induced TNF-α production after fatigue exercise in mice. Therefore, it was suggested that LPS-induced TNF-α production immediately after intense exercise is regulated depending on the dietary composition, meaning different dietary fibers.

It was never reported that there was innate immune responses to LPS in germ-free and/or AB treated mice after severe exercise. AB treatment-induced initial microbiome is destroyed and all mice grow in similar conditions [20, 21]. In fact, AB treatment in mice greatly modified the gut microbiota, reducing levels of Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, and overall bacterial count [22]. Accordingly, in mice treated with ABs, which led to an almost complete depletion of gut microbiota, LPS-induced TNF-α production was increased, even after exhaustive exercise. This result suggests that temporary immunosuppression occurs immediately after high-intensity exercise is partially regulated by gut microbiota in mice.

AB treatment decreases strain retention by reducing colonization resistance in the recipient and induces enhanced donor strain engraftment [20]. Therefore, in order to promote the establishment of donor strain retention, we performed gut microbiota transplantation from PHGG-fed donor mice into AB-treated recipient mice. Subsequently, we evaluated the direct effects of the microbiota itself on the innate immune system. However, we did not find the impact of LPS-induced TNF-α production. At the least, it suggests that changes in the gut microbiota alone do not affect the exercise-induced suppression of the LPS/TLR4/NF-κB pathway. Therefore, this phenomenon might indicate the necessity of fermentable dietary fiber with changes in gut microbiota. In the experiment 3, to examine the direct effect, we evaluated the response to LPS stimulation after exercise using mice fed with PHGG for 6 weeks. As a result, the increase in plasma TNF-α concentration in response to LPS was significantly attenuated in the PHGG-fed mice compared to the NF and CD mice. Hence, it was suggested that, in conjunction with changes in the gut microbiota, the intake of fermentable dietary fiber, such as PHGG, may possess a function to suppress the increase in plasma TNF-α concentration in response to LPS.

In general, gut microbiota regulate immune function in the host [23]. Microbial communities and the metabolites they produce are increasingly recognized as essential elements that profoundly influence immune function. Especially, it is well known that SCFAs (acetate, butyrate, and propionate), which are the main metabolites of gut microbiota, influence immune cell subsets and their functions [23]. Many regulatory properties of SCFAs require signaling through GPCRs, including GPR43, GPR41, and GPR109A, which are expressed by numerous cell types, including immune cells [24]. And then, the SCFAs driven inhibition of HDACs tends to promote a tolerogenic, anti-inflammatory cell phenotype that is crucial for maintaining immune homeostasis. In fact, exposure to not only peripheral blood mononuclear cells and neutrophils [25, 26] but also macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs) [27, 28] to SCFAs inactivated NF-κB and downregulated production of the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α. Thus, it appears that SCFAs-induced HDAC inhibition acts as a crucial regulator of NF-κB activity and innate immune responses. Furthermore, SCFAs also influence peripheral T cells, particularly regulatory T (Treg) cells, through HDAC inhibition [29]. T-cell differentiation to Treg cells are accelerated by SCFAs (butyrate), which are produced from PHGG via fermentation by gut microbiota. And also, it is well known that Treg cells greatly induce anti-inflammation. In fact, Watanabe et al. [30] reported that high levels of Treg cells in the intestine were observed by PHGG-treated mice for 6 weeks. Moreover, SCFAs contents in the cecum of these mice were higher than that in CD-fed mice. Yokogawa et al. [13] showed that PHGG treatment attenuated plasma TNF-α concentration after LPS stimulation, compared with CD treatment. Moreover, this phenomenon was deleted by AB treatment before PHGG intake. Even if that study was performed on sedentary animals, it appears that our results are strongly supported. Furthermore, Takagi et al. [31] reported that chronic ingestion of PHGG prevents the development of colitis in mice by modulating the gut microbiota and SCFAs. In particular, the abundances of Clostridium and Bacteroides had increased in PHGG-fed mice. Additionally, Aoki et al. [14] showed that decreasing Firmicutes abundance was observed by PHGG treatment in mice. Namely, PHGG intake can stimulate changes to the bacterial community and/or function in the gut. Based on previous studies that PHGG intake induces alterations in the gut microbiota of mice [13, 14, 30], we have initially hypothesized that gut microbiota transplantation may modify cytokine production prior to the LPS response. Alterations in the gut microbiota alone, however, introduced via gut microbiota transplantation, did not exert significant effects. The results showing suppressed TNF-α production in prior to LPS response following PHGG intake indicate that consumption of highly fermentable dietary fiber, which promotes sustained short-chain fatty acid production by the gut microbiota, is essential for suppressing immune responses.

In this study, we evaluated innate immune responses by intravenously administering LPS. However, in physiological situations, it is necessary to assume the invasion of endotoxin from the intestinal tract. We have already reported increased mucosal lesion [32] and LPS concentration in the portal vein [33] after acute forced exercise. Additionally, it has been reported that strenuous exercise causes gastrointestinal bleeding and slightly systemic endotoxemia in humans [34–39]. These findings might be suggested an increased permeability of intestinal wall function by exercise. On the other hand, gut microbiota-produced SCFAs support intestinal barrier function by stimulating intestinal epithelial cells to secrete IL-18, antimicrobial peptides, and mucins and by increasing the expression of tight junction proteins. SCFAs are also thought to promote intestinal IgA production, which would enhance sIgA levels [40]. In fact, we showed that the levels of mucin and sIgA in the feces were affected by deity fiber [41]. Although we did not overstate any mechanistic links to intestinal permeability or barrier integrity, those previous findings suggest that exercise and/or dietary fiber intake on intestinal barrier function must also be taken into consideration.

Taken together, the TNF-α production 1 h after LPS stimulation at sedentary condition (in experiment 1) are used as the 100% baseline, and then the LPS-induced TNF-α production after exhaustive exercise among control-, AB- and PHGG diet-fed mice (Fig. 7). TNF-α suppression in the control diet group (62%) is significantly lower than that in the AB diet group (77%) (p < 0.05) and significantly higher than that in the PHGG diet group (39%) (p < 0.001). In conclusion, our findings indicate that the suppressive effect of dietary fiber intake on LPS-induced TNF-α production immediately after intense exercise is likely to involve the presence of the gut microbiota and its metabolites, highlighting their potential role in modulating the innate immune response.

However, this study has several limitations. The fractions of donor-derived and retained strains after FMT are influenced by AB administration, which are one factor to reduce microbiome colonization resistance [42]. Also, future studies will evaluate Zo-1, Occludin and Claudin expression and portal LPS to determine whether PHGG attenuates exercise- or LPS-induced endotoxemia through effects on the intestinal barrier. Additionally, our results only include the cases of PHGG intake and LPS stimulation. Therefore, the effects of other fermentable dietary fibers, such as inulin, oligosaccharides, and other fermentable dietary fibers, and other PAMPs stimulation remain unknown. Moreover, we could not clarify the impact of PHGG-induced immunosuppression after exercise on the risk of upper respiratory tract infection or other infections.
