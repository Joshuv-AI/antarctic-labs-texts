Superior spinal accessory nerve function after robotic versus open selective neck dissection in papillary thyroid carcinoma: a retrospective and prospective cohort study
Authors: Yan Fang, Kai Yue, Hao Li, Yuxuan Wang, Jin Liu, Xinhua Li, Weifeng Shi, Shaoshi Chen, Ailin Yang, Yi Ding, Jin Zhang, Ruoyu Shi, Yansheng Wu, Yuansheng Duan, Xudong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Selective neck dissection (SND) involving levels II–IV for N1b papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) may compromise spinal accessory nerve (SAN) function. Robotic thyroidectomy via the bilateral axillo-breast approach (BABA RT) offers technical advantages for SAN preservation. We compared SAN functional outcomes following BABA RT versus open thyroid surgery (OTS) with SND for N1b PTC in a prospective cohort (50 patients per group; July 2024 to December 2024) and a 1:1 propensity score–matched (PSM) retrospective cohort (121 per group; December 2020 to June 2024). SAN function was assessed via electromyography (EMG), active shoulder abduction, trapezius muscle strength, and the Shoulder Pain and Disability Index (SPADI). Oncologic outcomes were evaluated. The prospective cohort showed BABA RT outperformed OTS at 3 months postoperatively: greater SPADI reduction (median difference [MD], -5.5 [95% CI, -8.0 to -3.0]; p < 0.001), greater shoulder abduction (MD, 9.5° [95% CI, 6.8 to 12.1]; p < 0.001), and better EMG scores and trapezius muscle strength (p < 0.001). 76.0% of BABA RT patients achieved shoulder abduction ≥ 151° within 3 months versus 14.0% with OTS (p < 0.001). This advantage persisted at 6 months, with a higher rate of SPADI ≤ 2 (56.0% vs. 28.0%; p = 0.005). A retrospective PSM-matched cohort confirmed the SPADI advantage at 6 months. Both cohorts showed BABA RT retrieved more level IIb nodes with comparable oncologic safety. BABA RT provides superior SAN functional outcomes and higher level IIb nodal yield than OTS, supporting its use in selected patients with N1b PTC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11701-026-03263-2.

Introduction

Total thyroidectomy (TT) with therapeutic lateral neck compartment dissection is recommended for papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) with clinically evident lateral neck nodal metastases [1]. However, dissection of level II is technically challenging due to its confined space and proximity to the spinal accessory nerve (SAN) [2, 3]. In open thyroid surgery (OTS), inadequate exposure increases the risk of iatrogenic SAN injury via direct transection, excessive traction, vascular compromise, or thermal damage [4–6]. The SAN is particularly vulnerable during level IIb dissection, and its dysfunction is a well-documented cause of postoperative shoulder morbidity, including pain, restricted abduction, and scapular winging, collectively termed “shoulder syndrome” [7, 8]. The reported incidence of shoulder syndrome after selective neck dissection (SND) ranges from 0% to 2.7% [4]. Importantly, transient functional shoulder morbidity may still be observed early after surgery despite macroscopic SAN preservation. Such morbidity can profoundly compromise patients’ quality of life [9]. Given this impact, the prevention of SAN injury remains a key surgical concern that warrants further investigation.

Robotic thyroidectomy via the bilateral axillo-breast approach (BABA RT) offers technical advantages that may mitigate SAN injury during level IIb dissection. These advantages include 3D magnified visualization, enhanced instrument dexterity [10], and a symmetrical midline view, enabling precise identification and preservation of the SAN. Moreover, the BABA approach utilizes a caudal-to-cephalad, antegrade dissection trajectory that aligns with the natural course of the SAN, allowing for earlier and more complete exposure of the nerve before lymph node removal. However, to date, no study has directly compared SAN functional outcomes between BABA RT and OTS in patients with N1b PTC. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective and prospective cohort study to compare postoperative SAN function between the two surgical approaches.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective and prospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Head and Neck Oncology, Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute & Hospital, with the clinic’s institutional review board approval (Approval No. E20240766). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

All patients with N1b PTC underwent TT combined with ipsilateral SND (levels II–IV), which was performed by a single surgical team. Preoperative evaluation included neck ultrasonography (US), US-guided fine needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB), and computed tomography (CT). Inclusion criteria were: (1) PTC ≤ 2 cm confirmed by US-FNAB; (2) Unilateral lateral neck node metastasis confirmed by US-FNAB and thyroglobulin (Tg) measurement in FNAB washout fluid; (3) No preoperative shoulder syndrome. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Prior neck surgery or radiotherapy; (2) Tumor invasion of local structures, such as recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN); (3) Metastasis in level I, V, or bilaterally; (4) Fusion, fixation, or perinodal infiltration of metastatic lymph nodes (LNs); (5) Inadequate follow-up (< 6 months) or incomplete clinical data.

The retrospective cohort initially assessed 622 consecutive patients treated between December 2020 and June 2024. Following the exclusion of 172 patients who did not meet the inclusion criteria, a total of 450 patients were included, comprising 142 who underwent BABA RT and 308 who underwent OTS. A 1:1 propensity score match (PSM) using radius matching (caliper = 0.05) was performed to balance age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and bilaterality, creating a retrospective PSM-matched cohort (the matched cohort) of 121 patients per group.

Sample size calculation and power analysis for the prospective cohort

The primary outcome used for sample size considerations was the between-group difference in postoperative SPADI change at 3-month follow-up. Assuming a clinically relevant median between-group difference corresponding to a moderate standardized effect size, a two-sided alpha error of 0.05 and a beta error of 0.20 (80% power), the required sample size was estimated to be approximately 40 patients per group.

The prospective cohort assessed 128 patients for eligibility between July 2024 and December 2024. After exclusions (n = 12), 116 participants were enrolled and consecutively assigned to each surgical approach (BABA RT: n = 56; OTS: n = 60). Following enrollment, 6 patients in the BABA RT group and 10 in the OTS group were lost to follow-up, resulting in 50 patients per group available for the final prospective analysis. SAN function assessment

SAN function was assessed by blinded physiotherapists using a standardized protocol. The retrospective cohort was evaluated at 6 months postoperatively; the prospective cohort was assessed preoperatively (baseline) and at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively.

The following measures were assessed: (1) Needle electromyography (EMG) of the trapezius muscle on the operated side, graded 1–5 (1 = most severe, 5 = normal) [11, 12]. The EMG costs were covered by departmental operating funds, and participants incurred no additional out-of-pocket expense; (2) Active shoulder abduction on the affected side, measured in degrees using a gravity-referenced digital inclinometer [13, 14], with attainment of ≥ 151° pre-specified as a binary endpoint [15]; (3) Trapezius muscle strength during shoulder abduction graded 0–5 per the Daniels and Worthingham scale (0 = no muscle contraction, 5 = normal strength) [16]; and (4) The Shoulder Pain and Disability Index (SPADI), a validated patient-reported outcome (13 items, 0-100) where higher scores indicate worse status [13, 17].

Preoperative shoulder pain was assessed with a visual analog scale (VAS) scored 0–10 [18] in both cohorts. Postoperative pain and disability were captured using the SPADI instrument at the timepoints specified above.

Surgical technique

Robotic SND via BABA

The robotic procedure was performed following a previously described technique [19]. Standard steps for dissection of levels III and IV are detailed in the Supplemental Sect. 1 and Supplemental Fig. 1. The following description focuses on SAN identification and preservation during level II dissection, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The main trunk of the SAN was identified at the junction of the middle and upper thirds of the medial border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle (SCM). Under magnified 3D visualization, the course of the trapezius branch was meticulously traced. Specific anatomical variations were documented, including patterns where the trapezius branch runs within the SCM (Fig. 2a), as well as the variant in which the trapezius branch arises before the SAN penetrates deep to the SCM (Fig. 2b). After clearing level IIa tissue medial to the SAN (Fig. 2c), the nerve was gently elevated using fine robotic instruments, allowing for direct visual confirmation and complete clearance of level IIb (Fig. 2d).

Open SND

The open procedure was performed through a conventional transcervical incision. The anatomical boundaries and oncological principles for levels II, III, and IV dissection were identical to those of the robotic procedure. The SAN was identified using the same landmark. Level II dissection proceeded under direct vision, with exposure of level IIb typically requiring manual elevation of the SAN.

Postoperative follow-up

Patients were followed postoperatively via outpatient clinic or telephone. SAN function was assessed as previously described. Transient hypoparathyroidism was defined as parathyroid hormone < 15 ng/mL and/or serum calcium < 2.35 mmol/L, if resolved within 6 months postoperatively. All patients underwent preoperative video laryngoscopy. RLN injury was diagnosed when postoperative hoarseness occurred and vocal cord palsy was observed on video laryngoscopy. Transient RLN injury was confirmed if it recovered within 6 months. Oncologic surveillance with neck US and Tg measurement showed no tumor recurrence throughout follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables and as median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables. Between-group comparisons were performed using the χ² tests, Fisher’s exact tests or the Wilcoxon rank sum tests as appropriate. For longitudinal functional outcomes, the median change from baseline to each postoperative time point was calculated for each group. Between-group comparisons of these changes were also performed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test, and the effect size was quantified using Cohen’s d with 95% confidence interval (CI). An analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model was used to compare 3-month postoperative SPADI between groups, with treatment group as the main effect and baseline SPADI as a covariate. Time-to-event analysis for SPADI recovery (using thresholds of ≤ 2 and ≤ 5 points) utilized Kaplan-Meier curves with the log-rank test. The binary outcome of shoulder abduction (≥ 151°) within 3 months postoperatively was expressed as risk difference (RD) with 95% CI. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

The prospective cohort included 100 patients (50 per group; 88.0% females; median age, 29.0 [IQR, 24.3–35.0] years), with a median active shoulder abduction of 164.1° (IQR, 158.7-167.9) and a median shoulder pain VAS of 0 (IQR, 0–0). None of the robotic procedures were converted to open surgery. The matched cohort comprised 242 patients (121 per group). Before matching, baseline characteristics including age, sex, BMI, and bilaterality differed significantly between the BABA RT (n = 142) and OTS (n = 308) groups (Supplemental Table 1). After matching in the retrospective cohort and assignment in the prospective cohort, patient characteristics were comparable between the BABA RT and OTS groups (Table 1).

SAN function outcomes between the two groups over time

At the 1-month follow-up in the prospective cohort, the BABA RT group outperformed the OTS group across all measured outcomes. The median SPADI was 10.0 (IQR, 4.0–17.0) in the BABA RT group versus 13.5 (IQR, 6.8–21.3) in the OTS group (p = 0.041). The active shoulder abduction was also better in the BABA RT group (median [IQR], 143.3° [138.6-148.4] vs. 140.5° [136.0-143.7]; p = 0.008). The EMG scores (median [IQR], 4.0 [4.0–5.0] vs. 4.0 [3.8-4.0]; p = 0.009) and trapezius muscle strength (median [IQR], 4.0 [4.0–5.0] vs. 4.0 [4.0–4.0]; p = 0.008) likewise showed statistically significant advantages for the BABA RT group (Supplemental Table 2).

At the 3-month follow-up, the BABA RT group demonstrated a greater reduction in the SPADI (median change [IQR], 7.5 [3.0–13.0]) compared with the OTS (median change [IQR], 13.0 [7.8–19.2]) group (median difference [MD], -5.5 [95%CI, -8.0 to -3.0]; p < 0.001). Similarly, active shoulder abduction showed greater recovery in the BABA RT group (median change [IQR], -8.1° [-12.4 to -4.5]) than in the OTS (median change [IQR], -18.0° [-21.6 to -12.3]) group (MD, 9.5° [95% CI, 6.8 to 12.1]; p < 0.001). Effect sizes for these outcomes were calculated as Cohen’s d = -0.9 for the SPADI and Cohen’s d = 1.3 for active shoulder abduction. A baseline-adjusted ANCOVA showed that the BABA RT group had a significantly lower 3-month SPADI than the OTS group (adjusted mean difference, -6.1 [95% CI, -9.0 to -3.3]; p < 0.001; Supplemental Table 3). EMG scores (median change [IQR], 0.0 [0.0 to 0.0] vs. -1.0 [-2.0 to -1.0]; MD, 1.0 [95% CI, 1.0 to 1.0]; p < 0.001) and trapezius muscle strength (median change [IQR], 0.0 [0.0 to 0.0] vs. -1.0 [-1.0 to 0.0]; MD, 1.0 [95% CI, 1.0 to 1.0]; p < 0.001) also showed greater improvement in the BABA RT group (Fig. 3 and Supplemental Table 4).

By 6 months, the SPADI remained lower in the BABA RT group than the OTS group (median [IQR], 3.5 [0.0-7.3] vs. 5.5 [1.8–12.0], p = 0.040), with no significant between-group differences in active shoulder abduction, EMG score, or trapezius muscle strength (Supplemental Table 2).

Timing and attainment of SAN functional recovery between the two groups

The BABA RT group was superior in both the rate and speed of SAN functional recovery.

Analysis using different SPADI thresholds consistently showed a faster recovery in the BABA RT group. Under the stringent criterion of SPADI ≤ 2 (Fig. 4a and Supplemental Table 5), the median recovery time was 6.0 months for the BABA RT group compared with > 6.0 months for the OTS group, with recovery rates at 6 months of 56.0% versus 28.0%, respectively (log-rank χ² = 8.0, p = 0.005; Hazard ratio [HR], 0.4 [95% CI, 0.2 to 0.8]). Using the sensitivity threshold of SPADI ≤ 5 (Supplemental Table 5), the median recovery time was 6.0 months in both groups, yet recovery rates remained higher in the BABA RT group at 3 months (44.0% vs. 22.0%) and 6 months (66.0% vs. 50.0%) (log-rank χ² = 4.1, p = 0.044; HR, 0.6 [95% CI, 0.4 to 1.0]).

This advantage was also observed in active shoulder abduction (Fig. 4b and Supplemental Table 6). While the rate of achieving active shoulder abduction ≥ 151° at 1 month did not differ significantly between groups (16.0% vs. 8.0%; RD, 8.0% [95% CI, -4.6 to 20.6]; p = 0.357), a statistically significant difference emerged by 3 months, with the BABA RT group attaining a higher rate (74.0% vs. 12.0%; RD, 62.0% [95% CI, 46.9 to 77.1]; p < 0.001). Consequently, the composite attainment rate within the first 3 postoperative months was also higher in the BABA RT group (76.0% vs. 14.0%; RD, 62.0% [95% CI, 46.7 to 77.3]; p < 0.001).

Validation of 6-month SAN function outcomes in the retrospective PSM-matched cohort

The matched cohort validated the prospective findings: SPADI remained lower in the BABA RT group (median [IQR], 4.0 [1.0-7.2] vs. 6.0 [2.0–11.0]; p = 0.015). A similar trend favoring the BABA RT group was observed for active shoulder abduction, although it did not reach statistical significance (median [IQR], 164.2° [157.8-169.4] vs. 163.3° [156.2-166.7]; p = 0.063). As in the prospective cohort, there were no significant between-group differences in EMG scores or trapezius muscle strength at 6 months (Supplemental Table 7).

Surgical outcomes and postoperative complications between the two groups by cohorts

The number of retrieved level IIb LNs was higher in the BABA RT group in both the matched (median [IQR], 6 [3-9] vs. 5 [3-8], p = 0.026) and prospective (median [IQR], 6 [4-9] vs. 4 [2-7], p = 0.031) cohorts. However, total, central, lateral (excluding level IIb), and metastatic LNs were comparable between the two groups in both cohorts. The operative time was longer for the BABA RT group in both the matched (median [IQR], 262.0 min [219.5-327.5] vs. 100.0 min [89.5–120.0], p < 0.001) and prospective (median [IQR], 212.0 min [168.0-249.8] vs. 91.5 min [80.0-109.3], p < 0.001) cohorts. Drain removal time, postoperative hospital stay, and postoperative Tg levels did not differ.

Postoperative complication rates were comparable between groups. In the matched cohort, the incidence of transient hypoparathyroidism (14.0% vs. 17.4%, p = 0.480), transient RLN injury (3.3% vs. 2.5%, p = 1.000), and chyle leakage (0.8% vs. 1.7%, p = 1.000) showed no differences. Similarly, in the prospective cohort, the rate of transient hypoparathyroidism was also comparable (14.0% vs. 10.0%, p = 0.538). No permanent complications occurred in either cohort (Supplemental Table 8).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to specifically evaluate SAN functional outcomes in patients with N1b PTC undergoing TT and SND via BABA RT. Employing both the prospective and matched cohorts, we demonstrate that BABA RT achieves superior SAN functional recovery while maintaining oncologic completeness and safety compared to OTS. These findings establish BABA RT as a SAN functionally superior and oncologically non-inferior alternative for selected patients with N1b PTC.

The principal challenge in the surgical management of patients with N1b PTC is balancing oncologic completeness with SAN preservation [9, 20]. This balance is most delicate in level II, where the SAN forms the boundary between sublevels IIa and IIb [21], creating a surgical hotspot for iatrogenic injury [22]. Level IIb dissection is particularly hazardous. Its deep, narrow anatomy and intimate relationship with the SAN mean that even careful retraction can cause shoulder dysfunction [23]. Postoperative shoulder impairment spans a spectrum, and clinically evident shoulder syndrome represents one end of this spectrum. Even with macroscopic SAN preservation, postoperative pain and transient functional shoulder morbidity may occur in the early postoperative period [24]. OTS may increase SAN injury risk due to suboptimal exposure of level IIb. Although transoral and transaxillary robotic approaches offer enhanced visualization and maneuverability, their access to level IIb is often limited by constrained viewing angles or working space [25–27]. Notably, the existing literature on robotic lateral neck compartment dissection has focused primarily on oncologic and cosmetic outcomes [27–30], leaving a significant gap in systematic, quantitative evidence regarding SAN function. Against this backdrop, BABA RT emerges as a promising alternative by virtue of its midline surgical view, which provides optimal, direct access to level IIb and facilitates SAN preservation. While SAN functional outcomes after neck dissection are well-documented in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [13, 31–33], our study provides the first comprehensive assessment of this critical functional measure in thyroid cancer, addressing this evidence gap.

Our results demonstrated a clear, time-dependent functional advantage for BABA RT. At 1 and 3 months, the BABA RT group showed superior outcomes across all objective (EMG scores, active shoulder abduction, trapezius muscle strength) and subjective (SPADI) measures, establishing a substantial functional advantage during the early postoperative phase. Critically, this benefit enabled faster recovery of daily independence. Within 3 months, 76.0% of BABA RT patients achieved shoulder abduction ≥ 151°, compared to only 14.0% in the OTS group. A pivotal and sustained patient-reported benefit was observed at 6 months. Despite equivalent recovery in all objective parameters by this time, the BABA RT group maintained lower SPADI in both the matched and prospective cohorts. This dissociation between subjective and objective outcomes aligns with prior research, where similar discrepancies have been attributed to factors like chronic myofascial pain [11]. The novel insight from our study is the attribution of this sustained benefit to the enhanced precision of BABA RT, which likely mitigates collateral non-neural trauma. This interpretation is strongly supported by our time-to-event analysis, which showed a shorter median time to return to preoperative SPADI baseline in the BABA RT group (6 months) compared to the OTS group (not reached within follow-up, log-rank test χ² =8.0, p = 0.005).

The superior functional outcomes observed with BABA RT are likely mediated by its technical advantages. A key technical cornerstone is establishing a stable three-point tension plane via coordinated traction and counter-traction, a technique applied throughout the procedure (Fig. 5). The platform’s symmetrical midline view provides the foundation for this technique and facilitates access to critical areas such as level IIb. Key robotic capabilities, such as 3D magnification, seven degrees of freedom, tremor filtration, and fine instrument control, further facilitate meticulous dissection within the confined spaces of the neck [10]. In level IIb, the synergy between macroscopic stability (from the tension plane) and microscopic robotic precision enables our tailored “small-scope, slow-progression” dissection strategy. We hypothesize that this minimizes trauma to the SAN, thereby preserving its functional integrity.

The oncologic completeness of BABA RT was not only non-inferior but superior in the dissection of level IIb, as evidenced by its consistently higher lymph node yield across both cohorts. The technical superiority, afforded by enhanced visualization and instrumental dexterity, facilitates meticulous perineural dissection without compromising other oncologic outcomes. Specifically, the two groups had comparable yields of total LNs, metastatic LNs, and postoperative serum Tg levels.

Importantly, these superior functional outcomes were achieved without sacrificing surgical safety. Complication rates were comparable between the two groups, consistent with previous studies [28, 34, 35]. Specifically, rates of transient hypoparathyroidism, transient RLN injury, and chyle leakage did not differ. Furthermore, no permanent parathyroid dysfunction or RLN paralysis occurred in either cohort.

This study has several limitations. First, despite employing PSM and a prospective design, the non-randomized nature of the study retains a risk of selection bias. Second, the single-center setting constrains the sample size and generalizability of the findings. Third, SPADI was analyzed primarily as change from baseline (ΔSPADI) to capture postoperative recovery dynamics. However, ΔSPADI may introduce increased variance and baseline dependency, potentially complicating clinical interpretation. We therefore conducted a baseline-adjusted ANCOVA sensitivity analysis for 3-month postoperative SPADI as a robustness check (Supplemental Table 3). Finally, although SAN function was assessed up to 6 months, the follow-up duration remains insufficient to characterize long-term (≥ 12–24 months) neurological recovery and the durability of SAN-related functional benefits, as well as long-term oncologic outcomes. Consequently, future prospective, multicenter studies with extended follow-up are warranted to validate the long-term durability of oncologic and SAN functional outcomes following BABA RT in the N1b PTC population.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that BABA RT provides superior preservation of SAN function alongside non-inferior oncologic outcomes. These findings position BABA RT as a safe and effective surgical option for selected patients with N1b PTC, offering the specific advantages of enhanced SAN functional outcomes and precise level IIb dissection. Although the functional superiority of BABA RT was consistently observed in both the prospective and retrospective PSM-matched cohorts, these findings originate from a high-volume center with extensive robotic experience. Therefore, reproducibility across centers with different levels of expertise may vary, and multicenter validation is warranted.
