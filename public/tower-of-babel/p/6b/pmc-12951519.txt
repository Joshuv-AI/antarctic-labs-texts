APP ubiquitination by VHL protein is essential for MVB sorting and lysosomal degradation
Authors: Chunyan Shan, Rixu Cong, Xiangyu Xu, Yuanjun Li, Yonglu Tian, Wenyuan Cao, Wei Zhao, Linqing Miao, Junlin Teng, Ning Huang, Jianguo Chen, Zhen-Ge Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmyloid precursor protein (APP), a type I transmembrane protein, is closely related to the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Amyloid beta (Aβ) is generated by sequential processing of APP in the Golgi apparatus and endosomes, and its toxicity leads to neuron dysfunction and neurodegeneration. APP is selectively shuttled between intracellular membrane compartments and ultimately transported into lysosomes. However, the mechanisms underlying APP sorting signals and lysosomal degradation are largely unclear. In this study, we show that the von Hippel‒Lindau (VHL) protein, a subunit of an E3 ligase, recognizes the cytoplasmic domain of APP and mediates its ubiquitination. VHL-mediated ubiquitination facilitates the sorting of membrane APP into intraluminal vesicles of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) and subsequent degradation in lysosomes. Therefore, the loss of VHL accelerates Aβ plaque deposition and memory deficits in AD model mice. Our findings reveal the role of VHL in restricting AD pathogenesis through ubiquitination-dependent MVB sorting and lysosomal degradation of APP.Keywords: amyloid precursor protein, von Hippel–Lindau protein, ubiquitination, lysosomal degradation, MVB sorting

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a chronic neurodegenerative disease that is the main cause of dementia and can gradually lead to death. According to the Aβ cascade hypothesis, the accumulation of Aβ is the main cause of AD pathogenesis, which triggers a series of downstream effects, including changes in synapses, the activation of glial cells, the hyperphosphorylation of tau, the formation of neurofibrillary tangles, neuron death, and cognitive disorders (Selkoe, 2011; Selkoe and Hardy, 2016). Aβ is generated from the amyloidogenic pathway processing of amyloid precursor protein (APP), in which APP is processed sequentially by β-secretase and γ-secretase, while the nonamyloidogenic pathway regulated by α-secretase cuts inside Aβ and does not generate Aβ peptides (Thinakaran and Koo, 2008; O’Brien and Wong, 2011). The different cleavage sites at the C-terminus of Aβ usually generate two kinds of Aβ: Aβ40 and Aβ42 (Haass et al., 2012). The processing of APP is regulated by its membrane trafficking. APP is likely processed by β-secretase at the Golgi apparatus and trans-Golgi network upon transport from the endoplasmic reticulum (Thinakaran and Koo, 2008; Haass et al., 2012). After being transported to the cell membrane, APP undergoes α-secretase processing, while unprocessed full-length APP on the plasma membrane is internalized and transferred to endolysosomes via clathrin-coated vesicles, where the acidic environment is suitable for β-secretase processing (Nordstedt et al., 1993; Yamazaki et al., 1996). Finally, a majority of APP is transported to lysosomes (Koo et al., 1996; Lorenzen et al., 2010; Xu et al., 2021). However, whether APP metabolism is regulated through lysosomal degradation is still poorly understood.

Ubiquitination is a crucial signal for regulating the endocytosis of many membrane proteins (Shih et al., 2002; Raiborg and Stenmark, 2009), especially membrane receptors such as G protein-coupled receptors (Shenoy et al., 2001; Toshima and Toshima, 2010) and receptor tyrosine kinases (Shimokawa and Koibuchi, 2015; Critchley et al., 2018), and for sorting membrane proteins such as glutamate receptors into intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) and regulating their degradation in lysosomes (Schwarz et al., 2010; Kowalski et al., 2011; Goo et al., 2015). APP is ubiquitinated by several E3 ligases. FBL2 and HRD1 both bind to APP and promote APP K48-linked ubiquitination, which is an essential signal for APP degradation in proteasomes but not for degradation through lysosomes (Kaneko et al., 2010; Watanabe et al., 2012). The E3 ligase responsible for APP sorting into ILVs and further lysosomal destruction is still unknown.

The von Hippel‒Lindau (VHL) tumor suppressor protein is the substrate recognition subunit of the VHL–Elongin C–Elongin B (VCB) ubiquitin complex, whose α domain binds to Elongin C to form the VCB complex and the β domain binds to specific substrates for ubiquitination (Okuda et al., 2001; Hansen et al., 2002). Dishevelled, an important component in the Wnt signaling pathway, is ubiquitinated by VHL under starvation conditions and sorted to the autophagosome–lysosome system for degradation (Gao et al., 2010). Here, we report that VHL is a key regulator of APP metabolism and Aβ production both in vitro and in vivo. VHL-mediated ubiquitination is crucial for APP sorting into ILVs and lysosomal degradation. VHL depletion restricts APP on the limiting membrane of MVBs and blocks its lysosome-dependent degradation, which ultimately accelerates pathogenesis and memory loss in AD model mice.

Results

VHL deprivation promotes amyloid plaque deposition in an AD mouse model

To investigate whether VHL affects AD pathology in vivo, we generated VHL conditional knockout (cKO) mice by crossing tamoxifen-induced Camk2a-Cre (Camk2a-CreERT2) mice (Madisen et al., 2010) with VHL2-lox mice (Supplementary Figure S1A). Next, VHL cKO mice were crossed with 5×FAD mice, a model of AD (Oakley et al., 2006), to examine whether AD pathology in 5×FAD mice was affected by VHL depletion (Supplementary Figure S1A). Cre-negative 5×FAD;VHL2-lox littermate mice (further referred to as 5×FAD mice) were used as controls (Supplementary Figure S1A). In the mouse brain, the VHL antibody produced a specific band at ∼20 kDa, and this signal gradually increased during postnatal development (Supplementary Figure S1B). Injection of tamoxifen led to a notable decrease in VHL expression in Camk2a-expressing neural tissues, including the hippocampus and cortex (Figure 1A), further confirming the efficacy of VHL depletion.

Immunofluorescence staining of brain sections via monoclonal antibodies specifically recognizing amyloid β (Aβ) peptides revealed that the area of amyloid plaques was significantly greater in both the hippocampus and cortex regions of the 5-month-old VHL cKO/5×FAD mice than in those of the control 5×FAD mice (Figure 1B and C). Consistent with these findings, increased soluble Aβ42 levels were observed in both the hippocampus and cortex of the VHL cKO/5×FAD mice at the same stage (Figure 1D). In addition, we examined different forms of APP in the mouse brain and identified that the levels of full-length APP, sAPPβ, and C-terminal fragment (CTF) of APP were higher in 5-month-old VHL cKO/5×FAD mice (Supplementary Figure S1C), further revealing the link between VHL and AD pathology. Therefore, VHL downregulates Aβ production and plaque accumulation in an AD mouse model.

VHL depletion accelerates cognitive deficits in an AD mouse model

The 5×FAD mice display cognitive and behavioral impairments as early as 4 months after birth (Forner et al., 2021). To determine whether VHL is also involved in this process, we performed three behavioral tests of both the control and VHL cKO/5×FAD mice at the age of 5 months. First, a Y-maze spontaneous alternation test was performed to assess short-term spatial working memory. Compared with that of the control 5×FAD mice, the spontaneous alternation rate of the VHL cKO/5×FAD mice was markedly lower (Figure 1E), whereas locomotion was not altered after VHL depletion, as no significant change in the number of total arm entries was observed (Supplementary Figure S1D). Next, we carried out a novel object recognition (NOR) test to assess memory and cognition. Mice are more inclined to explore new, unknown objects than familiar objects. In the NOR test, no preference difference was found between the two groups for the same objects at the initial step (0 h) (Figure 1F; Supplementary Figure S1E). However, the percentage of time spent on exploring new objects, the cognitive index, was significantly lower in the VHL cKO/5×FAD mice than in the 5×FAD mice (1 h, Figure 1F and G). Finally, we conducted a Morris water maze test to examine spatial learning and memory. Both the control and VHL cKO/5×FAD mice showed similar total swimming distances during the probe test (Supplementary Figure S1F), which excludes locomotor deficits after VHL depletion. Compared with the control 5×FAD mice, the VHL cKO/5×FAD mice crossed the platform quadrant fewer times (Figure 1H and I). In summary, VHL depletion accelerates cognitive deficits in an AD mouse model.

VHL regulates APP sorting into ILVs

APP membrane trafficking is required for its metabolism (Haass et al., 2012). To investigate whether VHL regulates APP intracellular trafficking, we first constructed a stable VHL-reversed expression cell line (VHL rev), which is based on the 786-O renal cancer cell line that lacks VHL protein expression (VHL null) (Supplementary Figure S2A; Gnarra et al., 1994). Then, APP-YFP was transfected into 786-O VHL null or 786-O VHL rev cells. APP-YFP-containing vesicles colocalized with the late endosome and lysosome (LEL) marker LAMP1 in both 786-O VHL null and 786-O VHL rev cells (Supplementary Figure S2B). However, the APP vesicles were significantly larger in VHL null cells (vesicle diameter: 1.07 ± 0.04 μm) than in VHL rev cells (vesicle diameter: 0.42 ± 0.01 μm) according to 3D structured illumination microscopy (3D-SIM) analysis (Figure 2A and B). Interestingly, treatment with bafilomycin A1 (BFA1), a compound that specifically inhibits lysosomal degradation, also increased the APP vesicle size and APP protein level in 786-O VHL rev cells (Supplementary Figure S2C–E), suggesting that VHL-mediated APP metabolism may correlate with lysosomal function.

MVB sorting is critical for protein degradation through the lysosomal pathway (Gruenberg and Stenmark, 2004). To further investigate whether VHL facilitates APP sorting into ILVs, we used Rab5-Q79L, a Rab5 constant activation mutant, to enlarge endosome vesicles (Hislop et al., 2011). APP-GFP and Rab5-Q79L-RFP were cotransfected into 786-O VHL null or VHL rev cells. Fluorescence images revealed that APP accumulated on the endosomal limiting membrane rather than being sorted into ILVs after VHL depletion (Figure 2C). Statistical data revealed that the fluorescence signal inside endosome vesicles significantly decreased in VHL null cells (Figure 2D), suggesting that the APP sorting process from the limiting membrane into ILVs was suppressed. We then performed immune-electron microscopy to investigate APP sorting after endocytosis. In electron microscopy images, MVBs are characterized by containing multiple ILVs resulting from invagination of the limiting membranes. In 786-O VHL rev cells, internalized APP was normally sorted into different ILVs inside the MVBs (Figure 2E and F). In contrast, APP mainly resided on the limiting membrane of endosomes in 786-O VHL null cells (Figure 2E and F). In addition, the increased volume of MVBs after VHL depletion was observed (Figure 2E and F). Collectively, these results indicate that VHL is required for APP sorting into ILVs.

VHL binds to the APP intracellular domain

Ubiquitination is an important sorting signal in the MVB pathway (Katzmann et al., 2001). As VHL is a subunit of an E3 ligase, we next investigated whether VHL-mediated ubiquitination regulates APP sorting into MVBs. Immunoprecipitation (IP) assays using an anti-APP antibody revealed that VHL coimmunoprecipitated with endogenous APP in mouse brain lysates (Figure 3A). The human VHL gene has two isoforms, VHL30 and VHL19 (Supplementary Figure S3A; Iliopoulos et al., 1998; Blankenship et al., 1999). Although both VHL isoforms could interact with APP (Supplementary Figure S3B), VHL19 showed more interaction with APP-residing membrane compartment (Supplementary Figure S3C and D). Live-cell imaging revealed that VHL19 colocalized with APP in trafficking vesicles (Figure 3B and C). VHL is the substrate recognition subunit of the E3 ligase—the VCB complex, which recruits Elongin B, Elongin C, and Cullin-2 to substrates (Stebbins et al., 1999; Hon et al., 2002). The IP results revealed that, in addition to VHL, Elongin C and Cullin-2 also coimmunoprecipitated with APP (Figure 3D), indicating that VHL binds to APP through the substrate recognition domain and recruits the VCB E3 ligase complex.

To determine the domains responsible for APP binding, we constructed GFP-tagged VHL30 and its different mutants (Figure 3E) and then cotransfected them with Flag-tagged APP into HEK293T cells. Co-IP assays using an anti-Flag antibody revealed that the deletion of amino acids 63–99 or 100–155 of VHL, but not the deletion of the α domain, inhibited the binding of VHL to APP (Figure 3F), indicating that the first β domain, which serves as the substrate recognition domain of VCB E3 ligase, is required for binding to APP.

To determine the VHL-binding region of APP, we performed yeast two-hybrid experiments using full-length and truncated mutants of the APP intracellular domain (AICD-647–695) (Supplementary Figure S3E). The results showed that AICD-667–695 and AICD-647–692 did not affect the interaction, while AICD-647–687 and AICD-647–676 could barely bind to VHL19 (Supplementary Figure S3F). We further performed co-IP using C-terminal deletion mutants of APP (Figure 3G). The lack of amino acids 682–695 but not 693–395 of APP abolished the interaction with VHL (Figure 3H), confirming that the YENPTYKFFEQ motif at amino acids 682–692 of APP is necessary for recognition by VHL.

We conclude that the interaction between VHL and APP is dependent on the binding of the first β domain of VHL and the YENPTYKFFEQ motif of AICD.

VHL promotes the ubiquitination and lysosomal degradation of APP

As VHL depletion blocks APP sorting into ILVs (Figure 2), we assessed whether VHL affects APP protein levels via ubiquitination. Overexpressing VHL decreased the endogenous level of APP, whereas treatment with the lysosome inhibitor BFA1, rather than the proteasome inhibitor MG132, markedly reversed the decrease in APP levels induced by VHL (Figure 4A), suggesting the promotion of APP degradation by VHL through the lysosomal pathway. Consistent with these findings, VHL depletion inhibited APP protein degradation in a cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay (Figure 4B).

Next, we examined the ubiquitination level of APP. In HEK293T cells, VHL overexpression significantly increased the ubiquitination level of APP-GFP (Figure 4C). To confirm that VHL directly promotes APP ubiquitination, we performed an in vitro ubiquitination assay. GST-tagged AICD, HA-Ub, and VHL-3×Flag were expressed in HEK293T cells, which were then purified and incubated with 786-O cell lysates. The results revealed that VHL increased the ubiquitination level of AICD (Figure 4D). Taken together, these data suggest that VHL is an E3 ligase that regulates APP ubiquitination.

Next, we tried to identify the ubiquitination sites of APP that are mediated by VHL. There are five lysine residues (K649, K650, K651, K676, and K688) within the AICD. To validate the function of these lysine residues, we substituted them with arginine residues, generating single lysine mutants, a three-lysine mutant (K649R, K650R, K651R; K3R), and the K5R mutant with all five lysine residues mutated, and performed an in vivo ubiquitination assay. The ubiquitination level of the APP-K5R mutant, but not single lysine mutants or the APP-K3R mutant, strongly decreased (Figure 4E). In contrast, compared with wild-type (WT) APP, the APP-K5R mutant maintained its protein level in the context of VHL overexpression (Figure 4F). Finally, we focused on the subcellular localization of the APP-K5R mutant. APP-K5R-expressing LELs were enlarged in size (Figure 4G and H). Furthermore, APP-K5R was barely observed in ILVs in cells coexpressing Rab5-Q79L-RFP to increase the endosome size (Figure 4I), which was similar to the phenotypes of APP-containing vesicles from VHL-depleted cells (Figure 2).

Taken together, these data indicate that VHL mediates APP ubiquitination within the intracellular domain, promotes its sorting into ILVs, and ultimately leads to lysosomal degradation.

VHL blocks APP processing and Aβ secretion

APP processing through the amyloidogenic pathway is tightly controlled and closely related to AD pathogenesis (Halim et al., 2011; Lin et al., 2014). As VHL promotes the degradation of APP via the lysosomal pathway (Figure 4), we next examined whether VHL ultimately affects APP processing. We found that the levels of full-length APP, the nonamyloidogenic pathway products sAPPα and CTFα, and the amyloidogenic pathway product CTFβ were elevated after VHL knockdown in APPswe/HEK293 cells (Figure 5A and B). Aβ secretion was measured by ELISA. Both Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels increased by 16% and 40%, respectively, after VHL depletion (Figure 5C). In addition, no significant changes in the APP mRNA level or secretase activity were detected after VHL depletion (Supplementary Figure S4A and B).

We next examined APP processing in 786-O VHL null and VHL rev cells. Conversely, the expression of VHL in 786-O cells reduced the level of full-length APP (Figure 5D). Importantly, the mature form of APP, which undergoes O-linked glycosylation and exhibits slower migration compared to the immature form (Haass et al., 2012), showed a more pronounced decline in its protein level (Figure 5D and E). ELISA results revealed that Aβ40 secretion was also restricted in these VHL rev cells (Figure 5F). We further examined surface APP by biotin-labeled surface protein isolation experiments. The surface APP concentration decreased significantly in 786-O VHL rev cells (Figure 5G and H).

To further investigate the role of VHL in APP processing in vivo, we injected an adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9) carrying a control (AAV-GFP) or VHL19-P2A-GFP (AAV-VHL19-GFP) into the hippocampus of 5×FAD mice at the age of 2 months (Supplementary Figure S4C and D), and Aβ plaque accumulation was characterized 3 months later. The results revealed that the area of amyloid plaques in the hippocampus of the 5×FAD mice injected with AAV-VHL19-GFP was significantly smaller than that of the group injected with AAV-GFP (Figure 5I and J), whereas no changes in the area of amyloid plaques in the uninjected cortical region were observed (Figure 5I and J). Together, these data indicate that VHL negatively regulates APP processing and Aβ secretion.

Discussion

The transport of endocytic APP to the endolysosomal network is the major pathway for its processing and destruction (Yamazaki et al., 1996; Haass et al., 2012). In this study, our data provide further evidence that ubiquitination is an essential signal for APP sorting in the endolysosomal network (Figure 6). Our results show that VHL is the substrate recognition E3 ligase for the endolysosomal degradation of APP. VHL-mediated ubiquitination serves as a signal sorting APP into ILVs. APP was reported to be sorted and trafficked via the ESCRT machinery, and mutations in cytoplasmic lysine residues abolished this sorting (Morel et al., 2013). FBL2 ubiquitinates APP for proteasome degradation; however, FBL2-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination is unlikely to be the signal for ESCRT recognition. Here, we show that VHL mediates the ubiquitination of APP, which results in the targeting of APP to ILVs and, ultimately, its degradation in lysosomes.

Working model of VHL-mediated regulation of APP metabolism. In wild-type cells, VHL mediates APP ubiquitination at early endosomes, which is essential for APP sorting into ILVs of MVBs and degradation in lysosomes. After VHL depletion, APP is restricted to the limiting membrane of the MVB, which blocks APP degradation through the lysosomal pathway and ultimately accelerates Aβ secretion and Aβ plaque deposition.

Most APP undergoes amyloidogenic processing in endosomal systems (O’Brien and Wong, 2011; Haass et al., 2012). Multivesicular endosomes are organelles for transmembrane protein sorting (Raiborg and Stenmark, 2009). Previous reports have shown that inhibiting MVB sorting of APP promotes Aβ secretion (Edgar et al., 2015). Consistently, our data show that the depletion of VHL causes the missorting of APP, the accumulation of APP on the limiting membrane, and the subsequent promotion of sequential amyloidogenic processing. These results are also consistent with previous reports that Aβ generation predominantly occurs on the limiting membrane of MVBs, where the active pools of BACE1 and/or presenilin exist (Morel et al., 2013). Our results indicate that VHL promotes the lysosomal degradation of APP, which leads to a decrease in many types of intracellular processing products and Aβ secretion. However, depletion of VHL dramatically increased amyloid plaque formation in AD model mice. As VHL induces a decrease in surface APP, while VHL depletion results in APP accumulation on the cell surface, APP might undergo default recycling to the plasma membrane, which remains to be further investigated. Besides, a recent study reported that the activated HSC70 mediates APP degradation through chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) (Xu et al., 2022). Different from other types of autophagy, CMA does not engulf substrates via the invagination of the lysosomal membrane (Kaushik and Cuervo, 2018). Since our electron microscopy studies have observed that some internalized APP is sorted into the ILVs of MVBs, we speculate that APP degradation may occur through parallel mechanisms involving both CMA and the VHL-mediated pathway.

Defects in endosome sorting and lysosomal function play a pathogenic role in AD (Nixon et al., 2000). Endolysosomal dysfunction, which is related to APP accumulation and Aβ production in lysosomes, is an early-stage pathogenic symptom of AD (Nixon, 2017; Whyte et al., 2017). Endosome size is increased in the human AD brain, which appears before local Aβ or tau pathology (Nixon, 2017). The appearance of enlarged endosomes coincides with initial increases in soluble Aβ peptides. There is also evidence that endosomal pathology contributes to the increased β-processing of APP (Torres et al., 2012). Our results show that APP missorting and accumulation could be a reason for dysfunction of the endolysosomal system. Ablation of VHL leads to APP accumulation on the limiting membrane of MVBs, limiting membrane stacking, and the increased endosome volume, similar to early pathogenic defects in AD neurons (Torres et al., 2012). The production of Aβ upon APP processing on the limiting membrane could further contribute to endosomal abnormalities. Disturbance of endosomal sorting inhibits the targeting of APP to ILVs and subsequent delivery to lysosomes, leading to intracellular Aβ accumulation, which further contributes to endosomal dysfunction (Edgar et al., 2015). APP accumulation induced by VHL depletion leads to the enlargement of endosomal vesicles and stacks of limiting membranes. Since ubiquitination serves as a critical regulatory signal for the endocytosis of numerous membrane proteins, it would be of significant interest to investigate whether VHL-mediated ubiquitination participates in this process. Thus, dysfunction of endolysosomal degradation and Aβ toxicity might play synergetic roles in AD-related progression, and promotion of APP degradation and lysosomal proteolysis should inhibit this pathogenic progression.

VHL syndrome is a rare familial disease characterized by the development of tumors in multiple organs, such as hemangioblastomas of the retina and central nervous system (Haase, 2009). VHL-dependent hypoxia inducible factor (HIF) degradation acts as an oxygen sensor, and the transcription of BACE1 and the substrate of γ-secretase are regulated by HIF (Sun et al., 2006; Zhang et al., 2007; Li et al., 2009). Here, we prove that APP is a substrate of VCB ligase through direct recognition of VHL. Therefore, VHL may play an important role in stroke- or ischemia-related AD. However, further research is needed to explore the mechanism by which VHL regulates APP recognition under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. In addition, based on our findings that Aβ and VHL are negatively correlated and that VHL depletion promotes amyloid pathogenesis in AD model mice, VHL may be an important target for AD therapy. Our data also suggest that drugs promoting VHL activity could be used for early-stage AD treatment.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

MG132 and BafA1 were purchased from Sigma; mouse anti-HA (clone HA-7), anti-GAPDH (clone GAPDH-71.1), and anti-FLAG (clone M2) antibodies were also purchased from Sigma. The mouse anti-GFP antibody (clone RQ-2) was from MBL; the rabbit anti-APP antibody (clone Y188) was from Epitomics; the mouse anti-APP antibody (clone 22C11) was from Millipore; the mouse anti-Aβ antibodies (clone 4G8 and clone 6E10) were from Covance; the mouse anti-VHL antibody was from BD; the anti-Cullin-2 antibody was from ABGENT; and the anti-Elongin C antibody was from Proteintech.

Constructs

cDNAs of VHL and APP were amplified from the mouse brain via RT‒PCR. VHL and APP mutants were produced via PCR from their cDNAs, cloned, and inserted into the vectors pEGFP-N3 (Clontech) and p3×FLAG-CMV-14 (Sigma), respectively. Vectors expressing HA-Ub-K48 or HA-Ub-K63 were a kind gift from Dr Zhengfan Jiang (Peking University).

Cell lines

Neuro-2a (N2a) cells, HEK293T cells, and APPswe/HEK293 cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco). 786-O cells were grown in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Transient cell transfection was carried out using jetPEI (Polyplus-transfection). The 786-O cell line was transfected with VHL30-IRES-RFP and screened with puromycin to generate 786-O VHL rev stable cell lines.

Mice

VHL2-lox mice (Jackson Laboratory stock number: 004081; Haase et al., 2001), CamK2a-CreERT2 mice (Jackson Laboratory stock number: 012362; Madisen et al., 2010), and 5×FAD mice (from Dr Chen Zhang’s laboratory, Jackson Laboratory stock number: 034840; Oakley et al., 2006) were purchased from the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University. All experimental procedures were performed in compliance with animal protocols approved by the IACUC at Peking University (No. LS-ChenJG-3). Camk2a-CreERT2 and 5×FAD mice were separately crossed with VHL2-lox mice, and the offspring were further crossed with VHL2-lox;Camk2a-CreERT2;5×FAD mice. The siblings of the VHL2-lox;5×FAD mice were treated as the control mice. Tamoxifen (T5648, Sigma) was injected (200 mg/kg, i.p.) into the mice at the age of ∼2 months for five consecutive days. At the age of 5 months, the mice were subjected to behavioral tests and then sacrificed, and frozen sections of the brain were prepared for Aβ immunostaining.

Behavioral tests

Five-month-old mice of a random sex were subjected to behavioral tests, including NOR tests. Habituation was performed in the testing room for >30 min at the beginning of each test day. All tests were carried out in compliance with animal protocols approved by the IACUC at Peking University. The data were recorded and analyzed using a SuperMaze video tracking system (Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd) and a Morris water maze video analysis system (ZS-Morris, Zhongshi Science & Technology).

For the NOR test, each mouse was placed in an empty rectangular container [40 cm (L) × 40 cm (W) × 40 cm (H)] and habituated to the arena for 30 min. The chambers and objects were cleaned before each mouse test with a 70% (v/v) ethanol solution to effectively exclude the possible interference of odors on mouse behavior. The next day, the test mouse was allowed to freely explore two identical objects that were symmetrically placed from the arena center for 10 min. One hour later, one of the familiar objects was replaced with a novel one, and each mouse was allowed to explore the two objects again. The time spent exploring both objects was recorded. The recognition index was calculated as the ratio of the time spent exploring the novel object to the total time spent exploring both objects.

For the Y-maze test, each mouse was placed into one arm of a ‘Y’-shaped chamber [30 cm (L) × 5 cm (W) × 15 cm (H)] and allowed to enter each arm freely for 10 min. The total number of arm entries and alternations by each mouse was recorded. The percentage of spontaneous alternations was calculated as the ratio of the number of arm alternations to the number of total arm entries.

The Morris water maze test was performed in a large circular pool (120 cm in diameter) filled with opaque water (containing titanium dioxide, 19°C ± 1°C), in which a platform (12 cm) was hidden 1 cm below the water surface. Four starting points were set at the edges for the mice to enter the water. Four reference objects of different shapes were placed along the wall surrounding the water tank to provide visual cues for the spatial localization of the mice. The mice were subjected to four training trials per day for five consecutive days and then placed into the water facing the sidewalls of the pool from different start positions across trials. On Day 6, the platform was removed, and a probe test was performed for the mice for 60 sec. The total swimming path and the number of platform region crossings were recorded.

Stereotactic injection of AAV

For VHL19 overexpression in the hippocampus, 2-month-old 5×FAD mice were anesthetized (1.5%−2% isoflurane in oxygen at a flow rate of 0.8 LPM), placed on a stereotaxic frame, and injected bilaterally with AAV9 carrying the EGFP control or VHL19-P2A-EGFP (1 × 1013 v.g./ml, WZ Biosciences, Inc.) into the hippocampal region via an automated stereotaxic injection apparatus (RWD Life Science) at the following parameters: anteroposterior (AP): −2.0 mm; mediolateral (ML): ±1.5 mm; and dorsoventral (DV): −1.7 mm. Three microliters of AAV were delivered at 0.30 μl/min to each lateral. After each injection, the syringe was left for 10 min and then withdrawn slowly.

Co-IP assay and immunoblotting

The cells and mouse brains were lysed with ice-cold extraction buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM dithiothreitol, 10% glycerol, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and proteinase inhibitor mixture). The lysates were centrifuged at 12000× g for 20 min, and the supernatants were then premixed with either 5 μg of antibody or control IgG at 4°C for 2 h. The immunocomplexes were incubated with protein A-Sepharose beads (Pharmacia) at 4°C overnight. After extensive washes with extraction buffer, the bound proteins were collected with 2× SDS sample buffer and analyzed by SDS–PAGE and immunoblotting. After gel electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking with 5% low-fat milk in 1× TBST (0.2% Tween-20), the membrane was probed with primary antibodies and detected with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies.

Immunofluorescence

The cells grown on coverslips were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min at room temperature. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After washing with PBS five times (10 min each), the cells were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Flour 488 or Alexa Flour 568 for 1 h at room temperature. After washing with PBS five times (10 min each), the cells were subjected to imaging. Fluorescence images were taken under an LSM-710 confocal microscope (Zeiss). For super-resolution imaging, APP-EGFP was transfected into 786-O null or VHL rev cells for 24 h, and images were taken after cell fixation under an N-SIM microscope (Nikon) equipped with a 100× 1.49 NA objective. Images were reconstructed via NIS-Elements AR software (Nikon).

The mice were anesthetized and intracardially perfused with cold PBS and 4% PFA. The brains were isolated and postfixed in 4% PFA for 24–72 h at 4°C. The brains were washed in PBS, incubated with 15% or 30% sucrose in 4% PFA, and embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound (4583, Sakura). Sagittal sections (20 μm thick) were collected with a freezing microtome (CM1950, Leica). The sections were washed in PBS, blocked with blocking buffer (2% BSA) for 1 h at room temperature, and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies against β-amyloid (2454, Cell Signaling Technology) overnight at 4°C and Alexa 568-conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo) for 1 h at room temperature and mounted with mounting medium (0100-01, Southern Biotech). Mouse brain slices were imaged with an Axio Scan Z1 microscope (Zeiss) and processed with Zen software (Zeiss).

Biotin labeling of cell surface proteins

786-O VHL null or VHL rev cells were washed with cold PBS, followed by incubation with EZ-linkTM Sulfo-NHS-SS-biotin (0.5 mg/ml, Pierce) for 1 h at 4°C. The reaction was stopped by washing three times with quenching buffer (50 mM NH4Cl, 1 mM MgCl2, and 0.1 mM CaCl2) in PBS and subsequently three times with PBS. The cells were then lysed and precipitated with streptavidin Sepharose (GE) at 4°C for 2 h. Biotinylated proteins in the precipitated fraction were subjected to immunoblotting analysis.

Immunoelectron microscopy

The 3×HA-APP-GFP vector was transfected into 786-O VHL null or VHL rev cells for 24 h. The cells were incubated with anti-HA antibodies (1:500) on ice for 30 min, followed by incubation with anti-mouse IgG conjugated with 10 nm gold (1:10) for another 30 min. The cells were kept in 37°C medium and allowed to endocytose for 15 min. Then, the cells were fixed, and GFP-positive cells were relocated, prepared for transmission electron microscopy, and observed as described previously (Huang et al., 2017). Immunogold-stained samples were imaged with a transmission electron microscope (Tecnai G2 20 Twin, FEI).

Yeast two-hybrid assays

VHL19 was cloned and inserted into the prey vector pGBKT7 (Clontech Laboratories), and fragments of AICD were cloned and inserted into the bait vector pGADT7. Yeast two-hybrid experiments were performed as previously reported (Wang et al., 2013). Briefly, the prey and bait vectors were cotransformed into the AH109 yeast strain, and then the yeasts were plated onto double (Trp-, Leu-) and quadruple (Trp-, Leu-, His-, Ade-) selective media for 2‒4 days at 30°C.

Ubiquitination analysis

APP-3×FLAG, VHL-RFP, and HA-Ub were transfected into HEK293T cells. After 36 h, the cells were lysed, and anti-Flag antibodies were used for co-IP. The precipitated protein was subjected to immunoblotting with anti-HA antibodies for ubiquitination analysis.

For in vitro ubiquitination analysis, 786-O cells were washed two times with PBS, and hypotonic solution (20 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 5 mM MgCl2, 8 mM KCl, 1 mM dithiothreitol, 0.5 mM PMSF, and protease inhibitor cocktail) was added to lyse the cells on ice. The cells were collected, frozen and thawed three times, and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant. The supernatant was then subjected to high-speed centrifugation (100000× g) for 4 h to obtain 786-O cell lysates. For protein purification, VHL30-3×FLAG and HA-Ub were transfected into HEK293T cells. After 48 h, anti-Flag and anti-HA antibodies were used for IP separately, and Flag polypeptide and HA polypeptide were used to elute the bound proteins. The expression of AICD fused with GST (GST-AICD) was induced in Escherichia coli, and the protein bound to the GST beads. After five washes with hypotonic buffer, the GST-AICD beads were incubated with VHL30-3×FLAG and HA-Ub proteins and 786-O cell lysates in ATP buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 2 mM ATP, and 5 mM MgCl2) for 1–2 h at 30°C. After extensive washes with hypotonic buffer, the bound proteins were collected with 2× SDS sample buffer and subjected to immunoblotting.

Analyses of APP processing

APPswe/HEK293 or APPwt/N2a cells were transfected as described earlier. Serum-free culture medium was added to the cells after transfection, and the conditioned medium was collected. For analysis of full-length APP and CTFs, the cells were lysed on ice after three washes with PBS. The media and cell lysates were subjected to SDS–PAGE on 8% or 12% Tris–Tricine gels and immunoblotting using specific antibodies. Densitometric analysis of the bands from immunoblotting was performed using ImageJ (NIH). For quantification of the Aβ levels in the media, ELISA kits with capture and detection antibodies specific for Aβ1–40 or Aβ1–42 (IBL) were used. For quantification of the Aβ levels in vivo, a sandwich-ELISA method was used as previously described (Illouz et al., 2017). Briefly, the mouse cortex and hippocampus were dissected and homogenized in RIPA tissue buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, and 1% NP-40) supplemented with PMSF (M145, Amresco) and a protease inhibitor cocktail (M307, Amresco), and the supernatant was collected after 12000 rpm centrifugation at 4°C. The 96-well microplate was covered with anti-β-amyloid, 1-16 antibodies (803015, BioLegend) diluted in coating buffer (C1055, Solarbio) overnight at 4°C. The plate was washed with PBST (0.05% Tween-20) three times and blocked in 3% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. After four washes with PBST, the tissue supernatant was added to each well and incubated overnight at 4°C. After four washes with PBST, anti-β-amyloid (1-42) antibody (14974S, Cell Signaling Technology) was added as the detection antibody, and the mixture was incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After four washes with PBST, goat-anti-rabbit IgG HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (111-035-003, Jackson ImmunoResearch) was added. After four washes with PBST, 50 μl of 3,3′,5′,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (PR1200, Solarbio) was added. The color reaction was allowed to develop for 60 min and stopped by adding stop solution (C1058, Solarbio). The optical density was measured at 450 nm on a spectrophotometer (Thermo).

Statistical analyses

All the experiments were performed three times. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. *P < 0.05; ***P < 0.001; n.s., not significant.
