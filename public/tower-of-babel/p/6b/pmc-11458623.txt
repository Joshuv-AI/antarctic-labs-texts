Stability and suitability of housekeeping genes in phlebotomine sand flies
Authors: Fabiana Sassù, Barbora Vomáčková Kykalová, Cecilia Stahl Vieira, Petr Volf, Erich Loza Telleria
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe investigated gene expression patterns in Lutzomyia and Phlebotomus sand fly vectors of leishmaniases. Using quantitative PCR, we assessed the expression stability of potential endogenous control genes commonly used in dipterans. We analyzed Lutzomyia longipalpis and Phlebotomus papatasi samples from L3 and L4 larval stages, adult sand flies of different sexes, diets, dsRNA injection, and Leishmania infection. Six genes were evaluated: actin, α-tubulin, GAPDH, 60 S ribosomal proteins L8 and L32 (RiboL8 and RiboL32), and elongation factor 1-α (EF1-α). EF1-α was among the most stably expressed along with RiboL8 in L. longipalpis larvae and RiboL32 in adults. In P. papatasi, EF1-α and RiboL32 were the top in larvae, while EF1-α and actin were the most stable in adults. RiboL8 and actin were the most stable genes in dissected tissues and infected guts. Additionally, five primer pairs designed for L. longipalpis or P. papatasi were effective in PCR with Lutzomyia migonei, Phlebotomus duboscqi, Phlebotomus perniciosus, and Sergentomyia schwetzi cDNA. Furthermore, L. longipalpis RiboL32 and P. papatasi α-tubulin primers were suitable for qPCR with cDNA from the other four species. Introduction

Phlebotomine sand flies (Diptera: Psychodidae), particularly those in Phlebotomus genus (in the Old World) and Lutzomyia genus (in the New World), are proven vectors of human diseases caused by Leishmania parasites (Trypanosomatidae) and phleboviruses. Additionally, certain species within the Lutzomyia genus have been incriminated as vectors of Bartonella bacteria, which cause bartonellosis1,2.

Phlebotomus papatasi (a vector of Leishmania major that causes cutaneous leishmaniasis) and Lutzomyia longipalpis (a vector of Leishmania infantum that causes visceral leishmaniasis)3 were the sand fly species to have recent versions of their transcriptome4,5 and genome6 available in the NCBI and VectorBase public databases7,8. These studies widened the possibilities for investigating the molecular basis of sand fly biology and their interactions with pathogens.

Gene expression studies play a crucial role in understanding the functions of various molecules. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) is a reliable technique for quantifying gene expression levels and PCR-based pathogen detection. It is commonly used in clinical and biological research worldwide9. Additionally, a high-throughput qPCR (HT-qPCR) system based on the microfluidic technique considerably increased the number of reactions per experiment while reducing operational time and reagents. Identifying stably expressed genes across different tissues, developmental stages, and experimental conditions is essential for such studies to serve as internal controls10.

Genes coding for tubulins and ribosomal proteins are commonly used as endogenous controls for semi-quantitative and quantitative gene expression studies in insects11. Nevertheless, analyses of putative reference genes, considering diverse samples, such as developmental stages, feeding regimens, and experimental conditions, have not been conducted in sand flies. In the present study, we selected six genes involved in various cellular functions commonly used as endogenous controls for gene expression analyses in insects11,12. These genes include actin and alpha-tubulin (α-tubulin) involved in the cell structure13,14; glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) involved in the cell metabolism15; 60 S ribosomal proteins L8 and L32 (RiboL8 and RiboL32)16,17, and elongation factor 1-alpha (EF1-α)18 involved in protein synthesis. We assessed their gene expression stability and suitability as reference genes in qPCR-based gene expression analyses in L. longipalpis and P. papatasi.

In addition, we tested the primers initially designed for L. longipalpis and P. papatasi for use in PCR and qPCR with cDNA from four other sand fly species that are proven or suspected Leishmania vectors. These species include Lutzomyia migonei (New World vector of L. infantum)19, Phlebotomus perniciosus and Phlebotomus duboscqi (Old World vectors of L. infantum and L. major, respectively)20, and Sergentomyia schwetzi, putative vector of Leishmania in Sub-Saharan Africa transmission to animals21.

Results

Gene identification

We selected the muscle-type actin identified in the L. longipalpis (LLOJ004800) and P. papatasi (PPAI004850) sequence databases. The phylogenetic analysis showed that the sand fly actin amino acid sequences shared similarities with the Drosophila melanogaster actin 87E and formed a sister group with muscle-type actins from mosquitoes and moths. These muscle-type actin sequences formed a separate clade from the cytoplasmic actin, found in vertebrates and invertebrates, and insect flight muscle actin (Figure S1).

The α-tubulin sequences were identified from L. longipalpis (LLOJ000130) and P. papatasi (PPAI001843) databases. In our phylogenetic analysis, the sand fly deduced amino acid sequences were closely related to α-tubulins from mosquitoes and moths. They formed a sister clade with the beta-tubulins (β-tubulins) while more distantly related to gamma-tubulins (γ-tubulins) from insects and vertebrates (Figure S2).

The L. longipalpis GAPDH sequence (ABV60323) was previously identified22, and PPAI009479 is the corresponding P. papatasi GAPDH gene. The sand fly GAPDH sequences grouped with Bactrocera spp., Drosophila spp., and Glossina spp. in a phylogenetic clade formed with other flies and separated from mosquito GAPDH sequences (Figure S3).

We also selected two genes coding for proteins that belong to the 60 S large subunit, the ribosomal proteins L8 and L32. The L. longipalpis (EW992436) and P. papatasi (PPAI008202) RiboL8 amino acid sequences, as well as the L. longipalpis (ABB58742) and P. papatasi (PPAI003040) RiboL32 sequences were identified from the publicly available databases. In both ribosomal protein phylogenetic analyses, the sand fly sequences formed a unique group closely related to the Drosophila sequences and separated from the mosquito group (Figures S4a and b).

The sand fly EF1-α sequences available from the databases were L. longipalpis (LLOJ008981) and P. papatasi (EY207611). The sand fly sequences formed a branch closely related to the mosquito EF1-α sequences in the cladograms. They formed a clade with other invertebrate sequences and separated from the vertebrate clade (Figure S5).

Expression levels and analysis for a consensus ranking of the candidate reference genes

We used the measured crossing point (Cp) results of the qPCR amplification to evaluate the gene stability of the sand fly samples. L. longipalpis and P. papatasi larvae samples of the third instar (L3), early fourth instar (e-L4), and late fourth instar l-L4) were defined as group 1 (G1). Pupae, sugar-fed males (M), sugar-fed females (F), females collected at 24 h (blood24h) and 72 h (blood72h) post blood feeding, females at 24 h post dsRNA injection (dsRNA24h), and females collected at 24 h (Li24h or Lm24h) and 144 h (Li144h or Lm144h) post-Leishmania infection were defined as a second group (G2).

The expression stability of candidate reference genes was assessed using RefFinder23, a user-friendly online tool that comprehensively evaluates through four distinct algorithms: ΔCt24, BestKeeper25, NormFinder26, and GeNorm27. RefFinder generated a consensus ranking by geometrically averaging the stability rankings produced by these four algorithms.

In L. longipalpis larval G1 (LlG1) samples, the gene with the lowest Cp value was actin, with a Cp mean below 20. In L. longipalpis G2 (LlG2), actin displayed a slightly higher Cp value, with a Cp mean above 22 (Fig. 1a, dashed red lines). For α-tubulin and GAPDH, the Cp mean in G1 and G2 was above 23 (Fig. 1b and c, dashed red lines). RiboL8 and RiboL32 showed a Cp mean above 24 in G1 and above 20 in G2 (Fig. 1d and e, dashed red lines). Finally, EF1-α presented a Cp mean above 18 in G1 and G2 (Fig. 1f, dashed red lines).

The other L. longipalpis and P. papatasi primers did not amplify cDNA from at least one of the six sand fly species (Figures S6, S7, and S8).

Discussion

Although molecular biology studies on sand flies have been conducted for decades28–30, no data was previously available on the expression of potential reference genes across different biotic and abiotic conditions. Our study provides evidence that helps the choosing of endogenous controls for PCR- and qPCR-based studies in sand flies.

The sand fly samples selected for our study were carefully chosen to represent key variables, including developmental stages, sex, feeding regimens, dsRNA injections, and Leishmania infection. The inclusion of L3 and e-L4 larval stages (G1) is particularly significant, as these voracious stages31 can be targeted in control strategies aimed at disrupting development32, such as through larvicide baits. Additionally, the progression from larva to pupa and adult (G1 and G2) involves extensive growth and tissue remodeling, driven by processes like cell replication and differentiation33. Adult forms (G2) are particularly relevant for reproduction-based control strategies34. Furthermore, variations in adult feeding status reflect the nutrient acquisition dynamics, which are important for strategies that disrupting the insect digestive physiology35. Feeding also introduces diverse microbes into the sand fly system36.

Female samples in this study included those subjected to dsRNA microinjections, a common approach for gene silencing studies37. It is important to highlight that dsRNA injections can mimic a dsRNA virus infection, activating the RNAi-mediated immune response38. Lastly, female sand flies infected by Leishmania represent a critical public health concern3,39. Overall, the G1 and G2 groups represent commonly studied samples in both field and laboratory research, encompassing natural and experimental conditions for L. longipalpis and P. papatasi, two species frequently used in sand fly molecular studies.

The G3 and G4 sample groups comprise dissected tissues, representing more detailed approaches; therefore, we focused on one species, P. papatasi. The G3 group includes dissected tissues and body parts of females collected towards the end of blood digestion while ovaries are developing40. The G4 group represents stages of blood digestion and L. major development in the P. papatasi gut, including parasite multiplication, differentiation, and attachment to the sand fly gut39. The sand fly gut samples represent one of the critical organs to study Leishmania’s interaction with its vector.

The gene stability ranking between the two sand fly species, LlG1 and PpG1 larval samples, were generally consistent. EF1-α consistently ranked among the two most stably expressed genes, alongside a ribosomal protein (RiboL8 in LlG1 and RiboL32 in PpG1). Genes coding for actin, tubulins, ribosomal proteins, and EF1-α are often reported among insects’ most stably expressed genes across different developmental stages. For example, the most stable reference genes are actin and tubulin for the black soldier fly Hermetia illucens41; actin, 60 S ribosomal protein L13a (RiboL13a), β-tubulin, 40 S ribosomal protein S3a (RiboS3a), and EF1-α for the jackfruit borer moth Diaphania caesalis42; and β-tubulin, eukaryotic elongation factor 2 (eEF2) and 40 S ribosomal protein S3 (RiboS3) for the six-toothed bark beetle Ips sexdentatus43. The consistent stability of EF1-α and ribosomal proteins across both sand fly species underscores their reliability as endogenous controls for gene expression studies. Similar findings in other insects further validate the use of these genes in analysing gene expression across various developmental stages in sand flies.

A slightly different ranking was observed in the LlG2 and PpG2 samples, where EF1-α remained one of the two most stably expressed genes, alongside with RiboL32 in LlG2 and actin in PpG2. For insects under different feeding regimens, examples of the most stably expressed genes included tubulin, RiboL32 and EF1-α for the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster44; actin and 40 S ribosomal proteins S15 and S18 (RiboS18) for the dark-winged fungus gnat Bradysia odoriphaga45; actin and RiboS18 for the greenfly aphid Aphis gossypii46; and actin and GAPDH for the brown planthopper Nilaparvata lugens47. When comparing different sexes, among the recommended reference genes are EF1-α and GAPDH for the gramineous stem borer Sesamia inferens48; actin, EF1-α, and RiboS3a for D. caesalis42; β-tubulin, eEF2 and RiboS3 for I. sexdentatus43; 40 S ribosomal protein S26 and 60 S ribosomal protein L13 (RiboL13) for the webworm moth Hyphantria cunea49; and LaminCa and β-tubulin 97EF for D. melanogaster50. Our results are consistent with these insect studies, where EF1-α, ribosomal proteins, and occasionally actin or GAPDH are identified as stable reference genes in adult insects. These findings emphasize the reliable stability of EF1-α across diverse conditions, including feeding regimens and sex differences.

Under experimental conditions, such as dsRNA ingestion or injection that activates the RNAi-mediated antiviral response, the best reference genes are EF1-α for the spotted stalk borer moth Chilo partellus51; RiboS18 and EF1-α for the southern pine beetle Dendroctonus frontalis52; α-tubulin and RPL12 for the pepper weevil Anthonomus eugenii53. Under viral infection, the stably expressed genes are GAPDH, 60 S ribosomal protein L27, and β-tubulin for the cotton bollworm Helicoverpa armigera infected by the nuclear polyhedrosis virus54; annexin B and actin for Trichoplusia nicells infected by Nucleopolyhedrovirus infection, while the S18 and S28 ribosomal RNA genes were not suitable55; RiboS18 and elongation factor 1-beta for the biting midge Culicoides sonorensis infected by bluetongue virus or vesicular stomatitis virus56. Despite variations in infectious conditions, EF1-α is consistently shown as a highly stable reference gene across diverse insect species. While EF1-α levels may fluctuate across different cells and tissues57, whole-body samples average these variations, leading to more consistent detection, such as in G1 and G2 sand fly samples. In conclusion, it should be used along with a ribosomal protein or actin for accurate gene expression analyses in pathogen-related experimental challenges in sand flies.

It is unsurprising that actin and ribosomal proteins ranked differently between L. longipalpis and P. papatasi G1 and G2 samples. Similar discrepancies in reference gene stability have been observed in closely related species. For instance, in the mosquito Aedes aegypti, EF1-α and GAPDH were deemed less stable and unsuitable as reference genes58. Conversely, in Aedes albopictus, RiboL32 ranked among the most stable genes in larvae, pupae, and sugar-fed females, but was less stable in males and blood-fed females59. Discrepancies were also noted between the Mediterranean fruit fly Ceratitis capitata and the olive fruit fly Bactrocera oleae, where β-tubulin was one of the top three most stable genes in C. capitata, while α-tubulin was among the least stable in B. oleae60. These differences highlight the importance of validating reference genes for each specific species and experimental context, including tissue sampling, rather than assuming stability based on findings from other organisms.

In the female sand fly dissected tissues (PpG3), the gene ranking diverged slightly from that observed in whole-body samples (PpG1 and PpG2), highlighting the importance of accounting for sampling differences. Actin, for instance, is expected to have stable expression in adult insects compared to developing stages61 due to the less variable growth rates of the tissues. However, tissues like ovaries, which undergo extensive cellular modification62, present unique challenges when selecting a stably expressed gene. Gene stability analyses across different tissues and body parts showed that the most stably expressed genes are EF1-α, 40 S ribosomal proteins S13 and S20 for S. inferens48; actin and eukaryotic initiation factor 4 A for D. caesalis42; 40 S ribosomal proteins S15 and S26 for H. cunea49; and RiboS3a for the spruce bark beetle Ips typographus63. While ribosomal proteins are broadly applied, tissue-specific factors should be considered. In P. papatasi, actin is an additional reliable choice across these various tissues.

The sand fly gut samples (PpG4) are particularly important, as the gut is a key organ for studying Leishmania interactions with its vector. While few studies have examined the stable expression of candidate reference genes in insects infected by parasites, expression stability may vary depending on the insect host and the specific type of protozoan infection. The most stably expressed genes are EF1-α for the kissing bug Rhodnius prolixus infected by Trypanosoma cruzi64 and GAPDH and α-tubulin for Trypanosoma rangeli infection65; EF1-α, actin 1, and 40 S ribosomal protein S7 for the mosquito Anopheles dirus infected by Plasmodium berghei66; and arginine kinase and RiboL13 for the bumblebee Bombus terrestris infected by Crithidia bombi67. T. rangeli and P. berghei can cross the gut epithelium and invade the hemocoel65,66, while C. bombi, T. cruzi develop exclusively in the digestive tract64,67. In sand flies, Leishmania develops exclusively in the digestive tract3,39. However, we did not observe a direct correlation between the stably expressed genes in PpG4 samples and those found in insects infected by C. bombi and T. cruzi. These differences emphasize the importance of considering the specific host-parasite interactions when choosing reference genes.

Due to the availability of genome and transcriptome sequences for only a few sand fly species, we evaluated the applicability of the designed primers across other colonized sand flies. Consequently, we tested the efficacy of L. longipalpis and P. papatasi reference gene primers on amplifying the cDNA of L. migonei, P. duboscqi, P. perniciosus, and S. schwetzi. We identified that three L. longipalpis and two P. papatasi primer pairs successfully amplified cDNA samples from the other four species in PCR and qPCR. Employing cross-species PCR is not a novel concept and eliminates the need to design new primers for each species68. Considering the crucial role of sand flies as disease vectors, the primers designed for reference genes in this study can be applied as a normalization tool in the PCR-based detection of pathogens carried by sand fly species.

Our study showed that actin, EF1-α, RiboL8, and RiboL32 are the most stably expressed genes among six commonly used reference genes across diverse groups of L. longipalpis and P. papatasi samples. These primers can be also used in PCR to amplify the cDNA of other sand fly species important for public health.

Methods

Gene selection, primer design, and primer validation

Genes were selected among other insect studies’ most frequently used reference genes11. For instance, actin genes encode highly conserved multi-functional globular proteins that form microfilaments in the cytoskeleton and thin filaments in the contractile apparatus of muscle cells13. Similarly, the second gene, α-tubulin encodes a globular protein that is a key component of the cell microtubules14. The third selected gene was GAPDH that encodes a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD)-binding enzyme involved in glycolytic and gluconeogenic pathways15. In addition to these, two ribosomal proteins were selected for their critical role in maintaining ribosome structural integrity and ensuring efficient mRNA translation69. Lastly, EF1-α was included for its essential role in delivering aminoacyl tRNAs to the ribosome during protein synthesis18.

A few of these genes had primers previously designed for sand flies, such as RiboL32 (also named as RP49)70, GAPDH71 for L. longipalpis, and actin and RiboL8 for P. papatasi72 (Table 8). Other genes, such as α-tubulin and EF1-α, were identified by similarity using the Aedes spp. gene sequences58,59 as a query to search on the L. longipalpis and P. papatasi databases publicly available on VectorBase8 and NCBI websites7 using blast search tools.

To validate the identity of the newly selected genes, we used their corresponding deduced amino acid sequences. We searched for conserved domains against the InterPro73 and the NCBI Conserved Domain Database74 databases to support their identity. We also assessed the similarities with other organism sequences using the MUSCLE multiple sequence alignment tool75 built-in Geneious 7.1.9 software (Biomatters, Auckland, New Zealand). In addition, a phylogram analysis was performed using the Maximum Likelihood method76. According to the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) score, the best substitution model was estimated using MEGA X 10.0.5 software77 with a bootstrap value of 400 repetitions. Additional information is provided in the phylogram figure legends.

The primers originated for the present study (Table 8) were designed on Geneious Prime® 2022.2.2 software (Biomatters, Auckland, New Zealand) using the following parameters: melting temperatures between 59.0 °C and 61.0 °C, GC content between 40 and 60%, nucleotide length between 18 and 24, and amplicon length of between 150 and 225 bases. Additionally, a serial dilution of pooled cDNA of L. longipalpis and P. papatasi samples was made to determine the primer efficiencies on the qPCR assays. Only the primers with efficiency between 90% and 110% (Supplementary Table 1) and producing a single peak in the derivative melting curve were selected. The gene name, accession numbers, and primer sequences are listed in Table 8. Partial gene coding sequences amplified by PCR were sequenced for identity confirmation.

Insect rearing

The sand flies used in this study were obtained from colonies reared in the Department of Parasitology, Faculty of Science, Charles University. They include L. longipalpis (origin Jacobina, Bahia, Brazil, established in Prague in 1995), P. papatasi (origin Adana, Turkey, established in 2005), L. migonei (origin Pernambuco, Brazil, established in Prague in 2014), P. duboscqi (origin Senegal, established in Prague in 1994), P. perniciosus (origin Murcia, Spain, established in Prague 1998) and S. schwetzi (origin Ethiopia, established in 2008). All colonies were kept at 25 ± 1 °C and photoperiod 14:10 (light: dark) under the rearing conditions described in previously78.

Sand fly samples

To evaluate gene expression stability on P. papatasi and L. longipalpis sand flies, we tested several developmental stages (larvae, pupae, and adults), feeding regimens, and experimental conditions (dsRNA injection and Leishmania infection) (Table 9). Whole bodies from larvae and adult sand flies were collected in three pools of 10 individuals (Groups 1, 2, and 5). Dissected tissues were collected in three pools from 15 individuals (Groups 3 and 4).

Feeding regimens

We selected two feeding larvae stages, third (L3) and early-fourth (e-L4) instars, and one non-feeding stage late-fourth (l-L4) instars. Larvae and pupae were collected directly from the rearing pots in three replicate batches of ten individuals. Sucrose-fed male and female adults (three days old) were collected after feeding ad libitum on cotton wool soaked in a sucrose solution (30%) offered in small Petri dishes. Food dye was added to the sucrose solution to distinguish sugar-fed from non-fed insects. In addition, females were blood-fed on defibrinated sheep blood (LabMediaServis, Jaromer, Czech Republic). Fully engorged females were transferred to a new cage and were collected at different time points (24, 48, 72, and 144 h) post-blood feeding. These adult sand fly samples were also collected in three replicate batches of ten individuals.

Double-strand RNA injection

Colony-reared sugar-fed female sand flies were injected intrathoracically with 150 ng of a dsRNA non-related to the sand fly commonly used as mock dsRNA. An Escherichia coli LacZ β-galactosidase79 template for dsRNA synthesis was amplified from the p-GEM-T Easy plasmid (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) by PCR using dsLacZ primers coupled to a T7-promoter sequence (5’ TAATACGACTCACTATAGGGAGATATCCGCTCACAATTCCACA 3’, and 5’ TAATACGACTCACTATAGGGAGAGAGTCAGTGAGCGAGGAAGC 3’)80,81. The dsRNA in vitro synthesis was done using the MEGAscript RNAi Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the fabricant instructions. The dsRNA was microinjected in the female sand fly thorax following previously established methods in L. longipalpis82 and collected 24 h after dsRNA injection.

Leishmania infection

Infected female sand flies were collected in various intervals after feeding (Table 9) a blood meal containing 106 L. infantum (MHOM/TR/2000/OG-VL) for L. longipalpis or L. major promastigotes (FV1 MHOH/IL/80/Friedlin) for P. papatasi. Both parasite species were cultivated in Medium199 (Sigma–Aldrich, Saint Louis, MI, USA) at 23 °C, supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA), 1% BME vitamins (Sigma–Aldrich), and 250 µg/mL amikacin (Medopharm, Pozorice, Czech Republic).

Tissue dissection

For the gene expression analysis in different tissues, blood-fed P. papatasi females were dissected after blood feeding to collect the following tissues: heads, thoraxes, guts (midgut and hindgut), Malpighian tubules, developed ovaries with eggs, and posterior end of abdomen (last three segments of abdomen and accessory glands). For the dissections, adults were rinsed in a sterile saline solution (NaCl 0.9%) and carefully dissected using forceps under a 40× magnification objective lens. Each sample was made of three batches of fifteen individuals.

Additional sand fly species

We also used other sand fly species from the three genera available in our colony collection. We collected batches of 10 adult females from L. migonei, P. duboscqi, P. perniciosus, and S. schwetzi colonies to test the applicability of the selected gene primers across different sand fly genera.

All sand fly samples were immediately stored at -80 °C after collection until RNA extraction. All the samples are listed in Table 9.

RNA extraction, DNA synthesis, and PCR testing

Total RNA was extracted from samples using the E.Z.N.A.® TOTAL RNA KIT I (Omega, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions within five days after sample collection. The RNase-free DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific) digestion was used to clear possible genomic DNA traces. Synthesis of cDNA was carried out in the same day using the Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche, Pleasanton, CA, USA) using up to 1 µg of total RNA and anchored-oligo dT(18) primers in a 20 µL reaction following the manufacturer’s instructions. A conventional PCR test targeting a ribosomal protein gene was used to test successful cDNA synthesis. Reactions were done according to the EmeraldAmp GT PCR Master Mix (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan) instructions and visualized in a 2% agarose gel. Cycling conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 3 min; 34 amplification cycles (95 °C for 30 s; 60 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 1 min); and 72 °C for 5 min.

Gene expression

The concentration of cDNA in each sample was measured using the Quantus fluorometer (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). qPCR analyses were performed using a 384-well plate in a LightCycler 480 thermocycler (Roche) with SYBR Green I Master (Roche) in a final volume of 10 µL, 0.15 ng of cDNA, and gene-specific primers following fabricant’s instructions. The cycling conditions were composed of initial enzyme denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 45 cycles of amplification at 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C of extension for 45 s. All samples were tested in technical duplicates.

Data analysis for selecting reference gene candidates

Four distinct statistical methods, namely ΔCt24, BestKeeper25, NormFinder26, and GeNorm27 built-in RefFinder online analysis tool23, were applied to analyze the candidate reference genes’ expression levels in the three data groups.

ΔCt analysis utilizes relative Cp values assessing the variations in the Cp’s mean standard deviation when compared in pairs among other genes within each sample. When introducing additional genes in the analysis, it evaluates which pairs are less variable24. BestKeeper uses raw Cp values in pairwise correlation and calculates each gene’s standard deviation and Pearson correlation values against all the other genes25. NormFinder uses a model-based strategy to estimate the expression variation of a group of candidate genes across several samples. It estimates inter- and intra-group variations and converts these indexes into a stability value; the lower value indicates higher stability26. GeNorm provides gene expression stability values for individual genes by evaluating the geometric mean of Cp data of a specific gene compared to all other genes27. Each method generates a final rank based on the gene with the least variation among samples.

RefFinder23 (accessible at https://www.ciidirsinaloa.com.mx/RefFinder-master/) compares the genes’ ranks to create a consensus among the four methods. It determines the optimal and overall ranking of the candidate reference genes across the samples, thus granting an easy reproductivity of the data thanks to its simple-to-use interface. To confirm the results generated by RefFinder, independent analyses using BestKeeper, NormFinder, or GeNorm tools were performed with matching outcomes. Nevertheless, we have opted to present the results obtained by this web-based application exclusively to avoid unnecessary redundancy in this context.

Applicability of selected reference genes to other sand fly genera

Considering that the selected genes may share conserved sequence regions across sand fly species, we tested L. longipalpis and P. papatasi reference gene primers (Table 8) on the cDNA of four additional sand fly species from the three genera available in our insectary (Table 9) using the PCR methods described above and visualized in a 2% agarose gel. Furthermore, a serial dilution of cDNA from L. migonei, P. duboscqi, P. perniciosus, and S. schwetzi was done to determine the primer efficiencies on the qPCR assays.
