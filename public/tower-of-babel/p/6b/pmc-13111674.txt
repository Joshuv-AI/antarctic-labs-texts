Gut microbiota-derived lactate is associated with disrupted cardiac circadian rhythms in alcoholic heart disease
Authors: Wei Siang, Lin Wenji, Zhao Yiji, Feng Yan, Lai Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlcoholic Heart Disease (AHD) involves gut microbiota dysbiosis, metabolic disturbances, and circadian disruption, yet their interconnections remain unclear. Using a murine AHD model, we integrated echocardiography, metabolomics, cardiac transcriptomics, and 16S rRNA sequencing to investigate alcohol-induced pathology. It evaluated dietary fiber and acetate interventions for their potential to restore gut microbiota balance, lactate homeostasis, and circadian gene expression. Statistical analyses included correlation networks, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and pathway enrichment. Chronic alcohol consumption led to gut dysbiosis characterized by an overgrowth of Akkermansia muciniphila and a depletion of Lactobacillus intestinalisand and Bacteroides acidifaciens. This condition was associated with hyperlactatemia fraction, myocardial dysfunction, evidenced by a reduced revealed fraction and cardiac fibrosis. Transcriptomic analysis revealed strong dysregulation of circadian-related genes, including BHLHE41, NFIL3, and PER2. Interventions improved microbial diversity, reduced lactate levels, and successfully regulated cardiac related indicators through the lactate-circadian rhythm pathway. ROC analysis validated BHLHE41, NFIL3, and PER2 as high-accuracy biomarkers (AUC > 0.85). Our study reveals a gut‑heart axis in AHD where microbiota‑derived lactate links to circadian disruption, worsening disease. Dietary fiber and acetate are promising therapies that rebalance metabolites and modulate circadian networks, offering novel biomarkers and strategies for alcohol‑related cardiovascular disease.Subject terms: Cardiology, Diseases, Microbiology, Physiology

Introduction

Chronic excessive alcohol consumption is a well-established risk factor for the development of alcohol-induced heart disease (AHD), characterized by progressive cardiac dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis, and metabolic dysregulation1. Despite extensive research on the cardiotoxic effects of ethanol, the molecular mechanisms linking alcohol-induced metabolic perturbations to cardiac pathology remain incompletely understood. Emerging evidence2,3 suggests that systemic metabolic abnormalities, particularly lactate accumulation, may serve as critical mediators of cardiac damage in AHD. Furthermore, recent studies4,5 have highlighted the bidirectional interplay between gut microbiota-derived metabolites and host circadian rhythms in regulating cardiovascular homeostasis. However, whether alcohol disrupts this gut-heart axis by altering microbial communities and downstream circadian signaling pathways—thereby exacerbating cardiac dysfunction—remains unexplored.

Preliminary investigations have demonstrated that chronic alcohol exposure induces hyperlactatemia, a condition associated with impaired mitochondrial function and oxidative stress in cardiomyocytes6. Elevated lactate levels correlate with worsened cardiac output and pathological remodeling7,8, yet the causal relationship between metabolic dysregulation and structural cardiac deterioration in AHD lacks mechanistic clarity. Intriguingly, dietary interventions such as high-fiber diets and acetate supplementation have shown cardioprotective potential9,10, possibly by modulating gut microbiota composition and restoring metabolic balance. Nevertheless, the extent to which these interventions rescue alcohol-induced circadian gene dysregulation in the heart, or interact with microbial metabolites to improve cardiac function, remains unknown.

The circadian clock is a molecular oscillator composed of core clock genes and their regulatory networks that govern daily (~24-h) rhythms in organ, tissue, and cell function, including the myocardium11,12. Various studies13–15 have confirmed the existence of peripheral clocks in cardiovascular cells. Disrupting the synchronization or imbalance of circadian rhythms is associated with cardiovascular metabolic diseases16. Many cardiovascular variables, including heart rate, myocardial contractility, stroke volume, electrocardiogram waveforms (RR, PR, QRS, and QT intervals), endothelial function, and blood pressure, exhibit strong circadian rhythms14,17. Heart disease is multifactorial, and disruption of circadian rhythms has been shown to increase the risk by 40–63%, especially in diseases such as arrhythmia and acute myocardial infarction14. The role of circadian clock genes such as CLOCK, BMAL1, PERs, CRYs, NFIL3, and BHLHE41 in regulating circadian rhythm and cardiovascular disease function has been extensively studied18,19. However, the specific mechanisms by which excessive alcohol consumption disrupt these genes and lead to impaired myocardial function in AHD were still poorly understood.

In this study, it was hypothesized that chronic alcohol consumption disrupts cardiac circadian rhythms and gut microbial homeostasis, driving lactate accumulation and myocardial dysfunction. By integrating echocardiographic assessments, metabolomic profiling, cardiac transcriptomics, and microbiome analysis, this study systematically investigated the interplay between alcohol-induced metabolic perturbations, gut microbiota shifts, and circadian gene dysregulation in a murine AHD model. The therapeutic efficacy of dietary fiber and acetate in restoring cardiac function was further evaluated, with a focus on their ability to modulate lactate levels, microbial communities, and circadian signaling pathways. The findings reveal novel associations between gut-derived metabolites, myocardial circadian gene expression, and metabolic reprogramming in AHD, thereby offering potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for alcohol-related cardiovascular pathology.

Results

Excessive alcohol consumption leads to decreased heart function and increased lactate levels

We first evaluated the effects of chronic alcohol intake on the overall physiological status of mice, The timeline was shown in Fig. 1A. The results showed that compared to the AHD group, both high fiber diet and acetate significantly improved the body weight and HW/BW of AHD mice (Fig. 1B, C, Supplementary Table 1). Subsequently, we measured cardiac hemodynamic parameters using echocardiography to evaluate cardiac pumping function, and the results showed that alcohol induced myocardial function decline in AHD mice. We analyzed the EF (%), FS (%), LVIDd (mm), LVIDs (mm), IVSd (mm), and LVPWd (mm) of anesthetized mice. After long-term chronic alcohol induction, EF (%) and FS (%) in mice were significantly decreased (P < 0.01), while LVIDd (mm) and LVIDs (mm) were significantly increased (P < 0.01). IVSd (mm) and LVPWd (mm) also showed a decreasing trend or significant decrease (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01) (Fig. 1D–J). The differences in cardiac pathological phenotypes showed that under the action of alcohol, myocardial fibrosis worsened in AHD mice (Fig. 1K), and BNP, LDH, and lactate levels significantly increased (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01) (Fig. 1L–O). After administering alcohol to different groups of mice, the lactate content in alcohol diseased mice changed significantly higher than that in normal mice. These findings indicate that abnormal blood metabolism may play an important role in the occurrence and development of AHD.

Improving alcoholic heart disease function can improve changes in blood metabolites

To further confirm the changes in blood metabolic species from normal mice to alcoholic heart disease mice, based on our previous research, we intervened with dietary fiber and acetate in alcoholic heart disease mice (Fig. 2A). The results showed that dietary fiber and acetate improved mouse Body weight, HW/BW, pathology, EF and FS, serum BNP and LDH levels (Fig. 2B–H, Supplementary Table 2). Lactic acid and blood gas indicators (pH, HCO3-, pCO2, and BE) in mice with AHD also improved after treatment (Fig. 2J–M). These results indicate that under the action of high fiber diet and acetic acid, not only did the heart function of AHD mice improve, but also the blood metabolites of AHD mice showed significant changes. This suggests that lactate and other blood metabolites play an important role in the occurrence and development of AHD.

Cardiac transcriptomics

The cardiac transcriptomics of the dietary fiber group or acetate group were different from those of the control group (Fig. 3A, B). In the heart, 257 genes were differentially expressed in the dietary fiber group (FDR < 0.05), and 357 genes were differentially expressed in the acetate group. 128 genes were differentially expressed in both dietary fiber and acetate diets (Fig. 3C). The overlap was analyzed to identify common pathways modulated by both interventions, despite their different mechanisms. Enrichment analysis shows that differentially intersecting genes were mainly involved in biological processes such as regulation of circadian rhythms, circadian rhythms, regulation of circadian behavior, and the regulation of muscle organ development by circadian rhythms (Fig. 3D). The molecular functional level is mainly involved in transcription cofactor binding, sulfur compound binding, proteoglycan binding, FK506 binding, DNA binding transcriptional activator activity, RNA polymerase II specificity, etc (Fig. 3E). Cellular component analysis indicated that differentially expressed genes were enriched in extracellular matrix structures, transcriptional complexes (including the CLOCK-BMAL complex), and cell-cell junctions (Fig. 3F). KEGG analysis showed that differentially expressed genes were mainly involved in the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway and circadian rhythms signaling pathway (Fig. 3G). These analyses were performed on the overlapping gene set from both treatment groups to highlight common therapeutic pathways. These results indicate that a high fiber diet and acetate play important roles in promoting heart health and metabolic regulation.

Verification of differentially expressed genes in myocardial circadian rhythm and ROC curve

Through cardiac transcriptomics, we found that the circadian signaling pathway was altered during the improvement process of AHD in mice. The relative expression results of BHLHE41, NR1D1, NR1D2, PER2, PER3, NPAS2, NFIL3, DBP mRNA in myocardial tissue of the control, AHD, AHD+Fiber, AHD+Acetate groups were validated by qPCR (Fig. 4A–H). To further screen potential targets of AHD and identify differential biomarkers for detecting AHD, we performed ROC analysis on BHLHE41, NR1D1, NR1D2, PER2, PER3, NPAS2, NFIL3, DBP mRNA. The AUC values of BHLHE41, NR1D1, NR1D2, PER2, PER3, NPAS2, NFIL3, DBP AUC are 0.85, 0.78, 0.55, 0.88, 0.72, 0.65, 0.91, and 0.8, respectively (Fig.4I–P). These results indicate that BHLHE41, NFIL3, and PER2 may serve as potential biomarkers for AHD.

Effects of dietary fiber and acetic acid on gut microbiota in AHD

To further investigate the effects of dietary fiber and acetate on gut microbiota changes in AHD, we evaluated the Chao1 and Shannon indices of control, AHD, AHD+Fiber, and AHD+Acetate. Compared with the Control group, the Chao1 and Shannon indices in the AHD group were significantly reduced (P < 0.01), while after intervention with dietary fiber and acetate, the Chao1 and Shannon indices were significantly increased (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5A, B). Based on our previous research, we specifically observed the expression of Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus intestinalis and Bacteroides acidifaciens in different groups. Compared with the Control group, the AHD group showed Akkermansia muciniphila significantly increased (P < 0.01), while Lactobacillus intestinalis and Bacteroides acidifiers significantly decreased. After intervention with dietary fiber and acetate, Akkermansia muciniphila was significantly reduced (P < 0.01), while Lactobacillus intestinalis and Bacteroides acidifiers were significantly increased (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5C–E). These results indicate that during the treatment of AHD, Akkermansia muciniphil, Lactobacillus intestinalis and Bacteroides acidifiers play an important role in the pathological progression of AHD.

The impact of myocardial circadian rhythm genes on blood metabolites and their relationship with gut microbiota

Based on the above research findings, we further explored the correlational network of myocardial circadian rhythm genes with blood metabolites and their association with the gut microbiome. Among these associations, lactate, blood metabolites, and microbiota may be factors correlated with changes in myocardial circadian rhythm genes (Fig. 6A). It is worth noting that in this study, significant associations were found between Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus intestinalis, Bacteroides acidifiers, lactate, and myocardial circadian rhythm genes (NFIL3, PER2, BHLHE41) (Fig. 6B–E). The co-occurrence of changes in blood metabolites and myocardial metabolic disorders observed in the AHD mouse model suggests that microbiota-associated changes in blood metabolites may be an important correlate of changes in heart circadian rhythm genes and systemic metabolism.

Analysis of the interaction between gut microbiota Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus intestinalis, and Bacteroides acidifiers with lactate and circadian rhythm signals

After determining that the differentially expressed genes associated with circadian rhythm were mainly NFIL3, PER2, and BHLHE41, in order to explore their associations, we analyzed the correlation between these genes and lactic acid, and the bacteria Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus intestinalis, and Bacteroides acidifiers. Therefore, we investigated the correlation between myocardial genes and microbiota in these phenotypes. The results showed a correlation network with two components: the first involving the gut microbiota, which includes Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus intestinalis, and Bacteroides acidifiers. Akkermansia muciniphila is positively correlated with lactate, while Lactobacillus intestinalis and Bacteroides acidifiers are negatively correlated with lactate. In the second component,lactate is positively correlated with NFIL3 and negatively correlated with PER2 and BHLHE41 (Fig. 7A). Subsequently, we investigated the correlation between NFIL3, PER2, BHLHE41 and lactate, Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus intestinalis, and Bacteroides acidifiers. Lactic acid is positively correlated with NFIL3 (r = 0.48) and negatively correlated with PER2 and BHLHE41 (r = –0.21 and –0.24) (Fig. 7B); Akkermansia muciniphila is positively correlated with NFIL3 (r = 0.48) and negatively correlated with PER2 and BHLHE41 (r = –0.22 and –0.25) (Fig. 7C); Bacteroides acidifaciens was positively correlated with PER2 and BHLHE41 (r = 0.23 and 0.25), while negatively correlated with NFIL3 (r = –0.52) (Fig. 7D); Lactobacillus intestinalis was positively correlated with PER2, and BHLHE41 (r = 0.22 and 0.25), while negatively correlated with NFIL3 (r = -0.48) (Fig. 7E);. These results suggest a correlational network in which the gut microbiota Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus intestinalis, and Bacteroides acidifiers are associated with the expression of genes related to the cardiac circadian signaling pathway (NFIL3, PER2, BHLHE41) in a manner that correlates with lactate levels, potentially linking microbial changes to cardiac function in AHD.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the therapeutic potential of dietary fiber and acetate in improving these pathological changes, and explored new mechanisms potentially involved in the cross-talk between circadian genes and microbial metabolism. The observed changes in cardiac gene expression pattern in AHD were correlated with lactic acid, blood metabolites and intestinal flora. The main purpose of this study is to investigate the complex associations of chronic alcohol intake, cardiac dysfunction, blood metabolite imbalance and intestinal microbiota imbalance in AHD. We identified several gene sets altered in mice with alcohol induced heart disease under the influence of high fiber and acetate diet. The differentially expressed genes were mainly involved in PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and circadian rhythms signaling pathway. In addition, the microbial composition of AHD mice fed with high fiber diet and acetate diet was significantly changed. The key bacteria whose abundance correlated with the improvement of AHD by fiber and acetate were Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus intestinalis, and Bacteroides acidifiers (P < 0.05). The results of the correlation analysis between cardiac genes and microbiota suggested that the intestinal microflora of Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus intestinalis, and Bacteroides acidifiers might be associated with changes in gene expression of cardiac circadian rhythm that correlate with the level of lactic acid, and with the improvement of myocardial function and save the myocardial function damaged by AHD. Of course, we found that Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus intestinalis, and Bacteroides acidifiers were significantly associated with lactic acid and circadian rhythm signal genes, especially NFIL3, PER2, and BHLHE41.

Alcohol and its metabolites (such as acetaldehyde) can directly damage myocardial cells, resulting in decreased myocardial contractility, abnormal electrical activity, and ultimately lead to AHD20. Some studies21,22 also showed that alcohol drinking may indirectly regulate the occurrence of cardiovascular events by regulating intestinal microbiota or inhibiting the pressure signal of brain amygdala (reducing sympathetic excitability). The increase of blood lactate is a common metabolic alteration in chronic alcoholism and an important factor associated with the development of AHD23–25. Our findings confirm that chronic alcohol exposure significantly impairs cardiac function, as evidenced by reduced EF, FS, and exacerbated myocardial fibrosis. These structural and functional deteriorations align with elevated serum biomarkers of cardiac stress (BNP, LDH) and metabolic imbalance (lactate), consistent with prior reports linking alcohol abuse to cardiomyopathy and hypoxia-driven anaerobic metabolism26,27. Notably, the marked increase in blood lactate levels suggests a shift toward glycolytic metabolism, potentially exacerbating myocardial injury through acidotoxicity and mitochondrial dysfunction28. The concurrent expansion of left ventricular dimensions (LVIDd, LVIDs) further underscores alcohol’s role in promoting maladaptive cardiac remodeling, a hallmark of progressive heart failure.

Dietary fiber fermentation produces SCFAs, which have different physiological effects as important signal molecules. The most abundant short chain fatty acids include propionate (C3), acetate (C2) and butyrate (C4), accounting for 95% of the total short chain fatty acids in the body29. The concentrations of these short chain fatty acids in the gut vary from 10 mm to 100 mm, and have a variety of effects in the gastrointestinal tract, such as activating ileal motility, mucus synthesis, and epithelial health protection (by up regulating the expression of tight junction proteins and serving as the main fuel source for epithelial cells in the colon)30,31. Although the large intestine metabolizes most of the short chain fatty acids, a small part is still absorbed into the systemic circulation29. A large number of experiments have shown that SCFAs reaching systemic circulation regulate cardiovascular function. For example, in a hypertension model, butyrate or acetate supplementation can reduce blood pressure32. It was recently found that butyrate supplementation for 45 days can reduce systolic and diastolic blood pressure in diabetic patients33. Intervention with dietary fiber or acetate not only restored cardiac function but also normalized blood lactate and acid-base parameters, implicating metabolic reprogramming as a key mechanism in AHD mitigation. The reversal of myocardial fibrosis and downregulation of BNP/LDH further support the cardioprotective efficacy of these interventions. Intriguingly, transcriptomic analysis revealed that both treatments modulated overlapping pathways, including PI3K-Akt, and Circadian rhythm signaling pathways central to cell survival, inflammation, and oxidative stress responses. The shared enrichment of genes regulating circadian rhythms (e.g., PER2, BHLHE41, NFIL3) suggests that alcohol-induced disruption of cardiac circadian clocks may contribute to AHD pathogenesis, while dietary interventions may help restore metabolic homeostasis.

PER2 is a member of the period gene family, which encodes a core component of the circadian clock regulating exercise activity, metabolism, and behavior34. The gene was up-regulated by CLOCK/ARNT1 heterodimer, but then inhibited in the feedback loop by PER/CRY heterodimer interacting with CLOCK/BMAL135. NFIL3 is a transcription regulator that binds to the activating transcription factor (ATF) site as a homodimer in many cell and viral promoters36. The encoded proteins inhibit the expression of PER1 and PER2, so they play a role in the regulation of circadian rhythm37. BHLHE41 encodes a basic helix loop helix protein expressed in various tissues38. This gene is considered to be related to the control of circadian rhythm and cell differentiation39. The robust differential expression of these circadian modulators (BHLHE41, NFIL3, PER2) in AHD hearts, validated by qPCR and ROC analysis (AUC > 0.85), positions these genes as promising diagnostic biomarkers for AHD. Their involvement in circadian behavior and muscle organ development aligns with emerging evidence that disrupted circadian rhythms exacerbate cardiovascular diseases by impairing metabolic flexibility and stress adaptation40. The strong correlation between these genes and lactate levels further implies a potential feedback loop wherein metabolic byproducts may influence circadian transcriptional activity, which could perpetuate cardiac dysfunction.

In cardiovascular diseases, the gut often affected by ischemia during low cardiac output states41. During this period, the microvilli and villi of the colon become vulnerable to cell hypoxia and anaerobic metabolism42. All these factors ultimately lead to the instability of intestinal microbial composition (ecological imbalance), which is mainly manifested by changes in Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides (Gram-positive bacteria), and an increase in Firmicutes Gram-negative bacteria43,44. Our data unveil a critical correlative role for gut microbiota in AHD. The depletion of beneficial taxa (Lactobacillus intestinalis, Bacteroides acidifaciens) and overgrowth of Akkermansia muciniphila in AHD mice correlate with hyperlactatemia and circadian gene dysregulation. Strikingly, dietary fiber and acetate reversed these microbial shifts, concurrently lowering lactate and modulating circadian gene expression. The observed correlations between Akkermansia muciniphila (pro-inflammatory), Lactobacillus intestinalis (anti-inflammatory), and lactate levels suggest that microbial-derived metabolites may be linked to cardiac circadian pathways. For instance, lactate, a product of bacterial fermentation, could act as a signaling molecule to influence NFIL3 and PER2 expression, thereby potentially linking gut dysbiosis to myocardial metabolic-circadian crosstalk.

A correlative model is proposed in which alcohol-induced gut dysbiosis is associated with altered microbial metabolite profiles (e.g., elevated lactate), which in turn correlates with dysregulation of cardiac circadian genes, and with cardiac dysfunction and fibrosis. Dietary interventions restore microbial balance, reduce lactate overload, and modulate circadian gene expression, thereby ameliorating this pathological condition. The high diagnostic accuracy of BHLHE41, NFIL3, and PER2 (AUC > 0.85) underscores their potential as non-invasive biomarkers for AHD progression.

While this study reveals important associations, it has limitations. The single timepoint measurement limits our ability to assess true circadian rhythmicity. Future studies with multiple Zeitgeber time points are needed to confirm circadian disruption. Additionally, the roles of NFIL3 and BHLHE41, while correlated with circadian function, are not exclusive to the core clock machinery. Future work should include analysis of core clock genes (e.g., CLOCK, BMAL1, CRYs) at both mRNA and protein levels to strengthen the circadian aspect. While this study provides mechanistic insights, causal relationships require validation via germ-free models or targeted gene knockout studies. The precise role of lactate as a mediator between microbiota and circadian genes warrants further investigation, including metabolomic profiling of microbial-derived metabolites. Additionally, translational studies in human cohorts are needed to confirm the therapeutic relevance of dietary fiber and acetate in clinical AHD management. This work establishes correlative links in a gut-heart axis in AHD pathogenesis, involving microbiota-metabolite-circadian interactions. By identifying potential biomarkers (BHLHE41, NFIL3, PER2) and demonstrating the efficacy of dietary modulation, our findings highlight potential avenues for interventions targeting metabolic-circadian crosstalk in alcohol-related cardiovascular disorders.

Methods

Experimental animals

All animals were housed in SPF grade animal rooms with a 12-h / 12-h light / dark cycle, a relative humidity of 40–70%, and an average temperature of 25 °C.The animal research and studies proposed here have been reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanxi Agricultural University [Approval No. SXAU-EAW-2024M0504-04].

Animal models and treatments

This study used male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) to establish an alcohol intervention model. During the experiment, a gradient ethanol exposure protocol was implemented using Lieber DeCarli liquid feed: 10% ethanol containing feed was given on days 1–3, increased to 20% ethanol on days 4–6, and adjusted to 30% ethanol on days 7–9. The ethanol concentration was maintained at 36% from day 10 to the end of the experiment. Refer to our previous research for modeling method45,46. The experimental feed system was based on the Lieber-DeCarli liquid diet formulation. The detailed composition of the diets is summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Control diet: The normal control liquid diet (Trophic Animal Feed High-tech Co., Ltd., Nantong, China, Product Code: TP2020B) contained 47.6% of total energy from carbohydrates. The total dietary fiber content was 5.0 g/L, provided primarily by cellulose (Insoluble Fiber, Trophic, Cat#: CEL-100). High-fiber diet: The high-fiber intervention liquid diet was custom-produced by the same supplier (Trophic, Product Code: TP2020B-HF). The total dietary fiber content was increased to 15.2 g/L by supplementing the base diet with a blend of soluble and insoluble fibers, including: Inulin (10.0 g/L, Soluble Fiber, Shanghai Hongye Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Cat#: HY-INU-90), Cellulose (5.0 g/L, Insoluble Fiber, Trophic, Cat#: CEL-100), Pectin (0.2 g/L, Soluble Fiber, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#: P9135). All feeds were prepared fresh and replaced three times a week to ensure stability and prevent spoilage.

Three weeks before alcohol intervention, a pre-treatment plan was initiated: the experimental group animals received high fiber feed or drinking water containing 200 mmol/L magnesium acetate (Merck, 63052) (supplemented three times a week), and a cage feeding strategy was used to prevent cross contamination of gut microbiota between groups. Four intervention plans were set up for the experiment (n = 10/group): Control group, AHD group, AHD+Fiber group, AHD+Acetate group. The intervention frequency and dose were based on our previous study45. After 8 weeks of systematic intervention, all animals were euthanized. To minimize confounding effects of diurnal variation, all animals were sacrificed within a 2-h window starting at Zeitgeber Time (ZT) 4–6 (4–6 h after lights on) under the 12:12 light-dark cycle.

Echocardiography

Anesthetize mice by intraperitoneal injection of 1.5% pentobarbital sodium. When their heart rate was maintained within the range of 400–500 beats per minute, perform echocardiography to obtain parameters such as Left ventricular ejection fraction (EF%), fractional shortening (FS%), left ventricular end diastolic diameter (LVIDd), left ventricular end systolic diameter (LVIDs), interventricular septum end-diastolic thickness (IVSd), and left ventricular posterior wall end-diastolic thickness (LVPWd) in each group of mice to evaluate their cardiac function.

Blood gas analysis

Arterial blood was collected from the abdominal aorta of mice using arterial blood sampler for blood gas analysis, and the levels of pH, pCO2, and base excess (BE) were determined.

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay

Blood samples were collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm/min for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain serum. Then, according to the instructions of kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, the serum levels of B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and lactic acid were measured.

Histopathological staining

After euthanasia of mice, the heart was removed by thoracotomy and rinsed with physiological saline to remove blood. Large blood vessels, atrial and right ventricular tissues were cut off, and the water was dried with sterile filter paper. The tissue was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h and embedded in paraffin to make 4–6 μm thick sections. Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and Masson staining were performed separately, and an inverted optical microscopy was used for observation.

Transcriptomics

Total RNA was extracted from heart tissues of mice in each group and an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer was used to accurately detect RNA integrity and total amount. mRNA containing poly-A tails was enriched using Oligo (dT) magnetic beads, followed by fragmentation with divalent cations at high temperature. Then the first and second strand cDNA were synthesized sequentially. The cDNA was purified using an AM Pure XP system and underwent subsequent PCR amplification and purification to obtain the library. After quality inspection of the library fragments, sequencing was performed on the Illumina Nova seq platform, and 150 bp paired end reads were generated. Bioinformatic analysis was performed on the sequencing data, including data quality control, sequence alignment, detection of new transcripts, quantification of gene expression levels, differential gene analysis, Gene Ontology (GO), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis.

Intestinal microbiome

Fecal samples were collected aseptically from the colon during euthanasia and stored at –80 °C. Using the Power Soil DNA Isolation method, DNA was extracted from 5 samples in each group. The V3-V4 region of bacterial 16S rDNA was amplified using 341 F and 806 R primers and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq sequencer (300 bp paired-end reads) by the Australian Genome Research Institute. PCR products were verified by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. Quantitative detection was performed using the QuantiFluor^TM^ ST blue fluorescence quantitative system (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Sequencing reads were processed and analyzed using the MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from heart tissues using TRIzol reagent, and the RNA concentration was measured using Nano Drop 3000. Hiscript III RT SuperMix was used for reverse transcription to synthesize cDNA. ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix was used for qPCR under the following conditions: 95 °C for 1 min, then 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. β-actin was used as the internal control. The relative expression levels of BHLHE41, NR1D1, NR1D2, PER2, PER3, NPAS2, NFIL3, DBP were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and graphing were performed using Graph Pad Prism (version 9). The normal distribution of the data was validated using the Shapiro Wilk normality test. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons was used to compare data between disease models (Control and AHD) and diets (Control, Fiber, and Acetate). One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test was used to compare differences among dietary groups within the same disease model. The values are expressed as mean ± SEM, with P < 0.05 indicating significance. The correlation between gut microbiota and transcriptome genes was analyzed using GENEMANIA (https://genemania.org/) and the R ggplot package. A stepwise redundancy analysis was performed on the correlation between gut microbiota variation (Bray Curtis dissimilarity) and other data using the ordiR2step function in the vegan package. A permutation analysis of variance was conducted on the correlation between gut microbiota and metadata using the adonis function. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was used to correct the Pvalue. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to test the predictive performance of gene expression levels for AHD, and to determine model sensitivity, and specificity. P < 0.05 indicates a statistically significant difference. Lasso regression analysis was performed using the glmnet R package (v.4.1.6) to investigate associations between mucosal microbiota, lactate, and circadian genes.
