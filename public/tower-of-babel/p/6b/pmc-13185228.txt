Reply to commentary on “The influence of thirty-degree leg elevation on noradrenaline requirements administered as a prophylactic variable infusion during cesarean delivery, an open-label randomized controlled trial.”
Authors: Mina Adolf Helmy, Lydia Magdy Milad
Published: 2026

We want to thank doctors sincerely. Piscopo, Cascella, and Piazza for their thoughtful and comprehensive commentary on our study, “The influence of thirty-degree leg elevation on noradrenaline requirements administered as a prophylactic variable infusion during cesarean delivery, an open-label randomized controlled trial.” [1] Their observations highlight important physiological and methodological considerations that enrich the ongoing discussion on strategies to mitigate spinal anesthesia-induced hypotension (SAIH) during cesarean delivery.

SAIH is one of the most common complications during cesarean delivery, and if it is not anticipated and prevented, it can lead to significant maternal and neonatal adverse events [2].

Physiological basis of leg elevation

We agree that aortocaval compression is a major contributor to SAIH. In our trial, all patients were positioned with a standardized 15° left lateral tilt, confirmed using a digital inclinometer, to minimize vena cava compression [1]. Thus, the differences observed between groups reflect the additional effect of leg elevation rather than the absence of tilt. Mechanistically, leg elevation enhances venous return and increases preload, which may be particularly effective when combined with noradrenaline. Unlike pure α-agonists such as phenylephrine, noradrenaline possesses β-adrenergic activity that helps preserve cardiac output [3]. This pharmacodynamic profile, when paired with improved central blood volume from leg elevation, may synergistically reduce the incidence of SAIH more effectively than either intervention alone.

Choice of vasopressor

We acknowledge the ASA guidelines recommending phenylephrine as first-line therapy [4]. However, emerging evidence supports noradrenaline as an effective alternative, with the advantage of preserving heart rate and cardiac output [5–7]. Our study was designed to evaluate whether a mechanical adjunct (leg elevation) could further reduce noradrenaline requirements, thereby enhancing safety and minimizing potential adverse effects. Neonatal outcomes in our cohort remained within normal ranges, supporting the safety of this approach.

Relation to prior studies

We appreciate the reference to Hasanin et al. (2017) [8], which demonstrated reduced hypotension with leg elevation in the absence of prophylactic vasopressors. Our study builds upon that work by combining leg elevation with noradrenaline infusion [1], thereby addressing the commentators’ suggestion of integrating pharmacologic and mechanical interventions.

We also note their mention of our subsequent caffeine trial. In that study, caffeine administration was evaluated as a pharmacologic adjunct to reduce SAIH. Importantly, we likewise adopted a standardized 15° left lateral tilt in all patients, underscoring our commitment to minimizing aortocaval compression across protocols. While caffeine demonstrated promising hemodynamic effects, we did not encounter any neonatal adverse outcomes. As shown in Table 3 of that study, 5-min APGAR scores, umbilical artery pH, base excess, PCO₂, and NICU admissions were comparable between groups, with all values remaining within normal physiological ranges. We agree, however, that further evaluation of neonatal safety is warranted before widespread adoption, given caffeine’s ability to cross the placenta [9].

Local anesthetic concentration

We acknowledge the point regarding bupivacaine concentration. In our setting, 0.5% hyperbaric bupivacaine is standard practice, and the dose administered (10 mg) was consistent with international recommendations for cesarean delivery [7]. We appreciate the commentators’ highlighting of long-standing practices in other regions, which further enriches the discussion on optimizing anesthetic safety.

Conclusion

We share the view of Piscopo et al. that both maternal and neonatal outcomes must guide best practice in obstetric anesthesia. Our findings suggest that leg elevation is a simple, safe, and cost-effective adjunct that may reduce vasopressor requirements without compromising safety. Similarly, our caffeine trial demonstrated encouraging results without neonatal adverse outcomes, though further research is needed to confirm long-term safety. We hope that future multicenter studies will further clarify the role of these interventions across diverse patient populations and in combination with established positioning strategies.

We thank Dr Piscopo et al. again for their valuable insights, which contribute meaningfully to advancing clinical practice in this important area.
