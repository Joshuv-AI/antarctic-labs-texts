Exploring social support for eating disorders in Indigenous contexts
Authors: Maureen Plante, Amelia Austin, Suha Damag, Melissa Tremblay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEating disorders can affect anyone, regardless of cultural and ethnic background. However, culturally situated understandings of eating disorders are important for optimizing care. Moreover, social support is widely recognized as a key component in the recovery of eating disorders. Established Euro-Western treatment models generally conceptualize social support as the role family and close friends play in providing emotional and practical support during a loved one’s recovery. Indigenous perspectives of social support, which may include connection to ancestors, community, non-human relatives, and the land, are largely absent from such models, which limits understanding of the culturally relevant social networks that can help support Indigenous peoples with eating disorders. The present research addresses this gap exploring social support through Indigenous worldviews.MethodsThis qualitative project was completed with an Indigenous research approach. Two Indigenous therapists participated in a semi-structured sharing circle (focus group) and one Indigenous therapist participated in a semi-structured individual conversation (interview). Both the sharing circle and the interview were facilitated by the lead author, who is an Indigenous researcher. The data were analysed using qualitative description.ResultsQualitative analysis produced two main themes that reflect interrelated conceptualizations of social support from Indigenous perspectives, relevant to working with Indigenous peoples struggling with eating disorders. The interrelated themes were Wahkotowin (relationships between all things), and 2) land as support and (re)connection.ConclusionsThis research presents novel insights into Indigenous perspectives on social support as relevant to eating disorder treatment. It expands the understanding of social support beyond immediate family networks to include relationships between all things, including ancestors, community, all living things, and the importance of land-based healing. The study addresses the need for Indigenous perspectives in the field of eating disorders research and treatment as well as the need to understand and incorporate culturally specific forms of social support.Keywords: Eating disorders, Indigenous mental health, Indigenous worldviews, Social support

Introduction

Eating disorders (EDs) impact both the body and mind, including social functioning and overall quality of life [1]. The worldwide prevalence of EDs is up to 17.9% in young women and 2.4% in young men [2]. There is emerging evidence that EDs are more prevalent among Indigenous1 peoples than non-Indigenous people in settler-colonial states [3, 4]. Many EDs go undiagnosed and untreated [5], but even individuals with access to treatment have modest rates of recovery [6]. There is a growing understanding that personalized care for EDs, including consideration of culture, can help support more positive and equitable outcomes [7].

In Euro-Western contexts, the historical approach to the treatment of EDs was to isolate the individual from their family via an extended hospital stay [8]. This approach began to shift in the 1970 s with the development of a variety of family therapies to treat EDs [9, 10]. It is currently understood that support from family, including parents, caregivers, partners, and close friends can be a vital component of recovery from an ED [11–13]. This understanding is reflected in current treatment models. For example, ED focused family therapy for adolescents and emerging adults focuses on empowering parents/caregivers to renourish their young person to restore weight and/or normalize eating patterns [14–16]. In addition, social support is seen via the involvement of close others, such as parents or spouses, in individual treatment for adults [17], as well as adjunct skills training for parents and caregivers through well-known models such as the New Maudsley Method [18]. Most recently, the ED field has seen the emergence of lived experience peer support alongside conventional treatment [19].

While these therapeutic approaches represent a leap forward in harnessing the power of social support in the treatment of EDs, these interventions are still limited to Euro-Western conceptualizations of family systems, without consideration for other forms of connectedness. Current treatment approaches may not resonate with individuals from cultural backgrounds where forms of relationality beyond Euro-Western family systems also underpin good health, including Indigenous cultures. There is a growing body of published literature exploring the concept of EDs from Indigenous worldviews, including Anishinabek [20], Shipibo, Mestizo, Yagé [21], and Māori [22, 23]; however, we still have much to learn. In a Canadian context, clinical practice guidelines for the treatment of EDs in young people do not provide considerations specific to Indigenous individuals and families [24]. Further, the Canadian Eating Disorders Strategy [25] acknowledges that many gaps exist in treatment approaches for minority groups, including Indigenous Peoples2, and recommends developing culturally appropriate services and improving treatment match to patient needs [25].

Indigenous worldviews

From Indigenous perspectives, mental health is understood holistically to be inseparable from the physical, cognitive, emotional, and spiritual aspects of being, known as the four quadrants of health [26]. There is growing awareness of the importance of interventions that are culturally relevant and meaningful [22, 25–27], and that take into account spiritual traditions and knowledge, as well as the central role that community and land play in healing for Indigenous peoples [28, 29]. Health philosophy from a First Nations perspective is strength-based and can be understood as concentric circles. A centermost circle represents an individual held within layers of surrounding circles that represent multifaceted aspects of the four quadrants. This extends to individual values (responsibility, respect) that are surrounded by social support (family, community). Broader social, cultural, environmental and economic factors represent determinants of health that are essential to the individual and communities. The outermost circle represents people coming together to support healthy communities [26]. According to Indigenous conceptions of health, when one of the physical, mental, spiritual, or emotional domain is unbalanced, illness can emerge until all dimensions are restored, thereby resulting in balance [30].

Centering relationships with ancestors is another component of Indigenous health and wellbeing. Healing acknowledges that we have ongoing relationships with ancestors who came before us and those yet to come into life [30–32], and that those relationships may be considered medicine in the sense that they can contribute to healing. Another concept that is integral to Indigenous-centered understandings of social support is Wahkotowin, or interconnectedness. Wahkotowin is one of several Cree3 Natural Laws that translates to relationships between all things including human, plant, animals, minerals, and the cosmos [31]. Wahkotowin helps us understand that we are never alone given that there are many relationships surrounding us [31], including views of the animal, plant, mineral, and cosmos as relatives and helpers. In addition to Wahkohtowin, land-based healing has been cited as an effective approach to healing among Indigenous peoples [28, 33, 34]. Land-based healing includes activities that connect to the land and nature in a culturally and spiritually meaningful way [35], and there is acknowledgement of being in relationship to our non-human relatives. Wahkohtowin (interconnectedness) has practical implications when working with Indigenous clients and is utilized within Indigenous therapies, such as Indigenous Focusing-Oriented Therapy (IFOT).

Therapeutic approaches

Indigenous focusing oriented therapy (IFOT)

IFOT is a unique, culturally relevant and holistic approach to working with Indigenous peoples, centered on Indigenous perspectives grounded in a collective lens, relationality and interconnectedness [36]. In IFOT, grounding exercises are somatic and based on Eugene Gendlin’s focusing-oriented therapy [37]. The client is encouraged to connect with the felt sense, wherein the client selects a problem and senses how it feels in their body. Once the problem is identified, the client captures the felt sense with a word, phrase, or image (the handle)[28]. Throughout the session, the client goes back and forth between the felt sense and the handle. The client is then asked to get curious about the problem and receive any medicines or shifts that they may experience. In IFOT, medicines in this context refer to a holistic understanding that comes from within the person [28]. During a session, a therapist may ask the client if what they are experiencing is all theirs or if ancestors are also holding the experience with the client. Essentially, IFOT teaches clients that they are not alone in their experience as it is shared with others. IFOT draws upon the felt sense, which helps clients understand that they are within a web of relations across time, past and present ancestors, in community, and future generations. Clients' felt sense also connects them to intergenerational wisdom and strength, exemplifying a strength-based approach to healing, also considered medicine from within. In general, Indigenous approaches to healing take a strengths based focus, which helps to depathologize client’s symptoms and experiences by looking at the symptoms as messages holding wisdom and healing [36]. Moreover, Indigenous approaches are holistic, which may be particularly important when working with Indigenous clients who experience EDs. A strengths-based approach allows the client to deconstruct the pathologizing nature of their ED and help connect to a relational sense of being. In other words, clients with EDs may feel less alone in their experiences and find meaning within their symptoms.

Developmental theory of embodiment

Embodiment is a relevant framework for understanding EDs [38, 39]. Embodiment refers to the lived experience of being in one’s body and understands the body as a source of knowledge through how individuals experience the world and relate to others [40, 41]. This bodily knowing can occur both consciously and unconsciously [42]. When individuals become disconnected from this process, they may lose awareness of the body and experiences, such that they are less attuned to their bodies [42]. The Developmental Theory of Embodiment posits that embodied experiences are shaped by social contexts, including dominant social labels, expectations, and power relations [40]. Sociopolitical influences, such as patriarchal norms and cultural messages about women’s bodies, prioritize appearance-based values, contributing to the objectification, devaluation, and sexualization of women. These processes can foster a disembodied sense of self, which has been linked to the development and maintenance of EDs [42].

Together, IFOT and the Theory of Embodiment provide a nuanced understanding of the disconnect women experience from their bodies. Both approaches see the body as a source of knowledge and recognize the impact of restricted agency is shaped by patriarchal and colonial contexts can constrain embodied experiences. A holistic understanding of self-struggles and embodied existence is therefore essential for theory development, treatment, and research [41]. Emerging research suggests that ayahuasca, traditionally used by the Amazonian peoples, is reported to support spiritual connection and contribute to movement toward a more embodied sense of self [43, 44]. The importance of embodied connection is further illustrated in McHugh and colleagues’ [45] study of urban Indigenous youth in Canada, where participants described positive relationships with their bodies expressed through cultural practices such as dance and wearing regalia. Consistent with Piran’s Developmental Theory of Embodiment, girls’ experiences are reflected either with agency or restriction of their bodies within a physical sphere, shaping their embodied sense of self over time.

At present, social support in ED treatment is generally understood as emotional and practical support from family, caregivers, partners and close friends [11–13]. Social support from an Indigenous perspective is the connection to family, including ancestors, community, and all living beings, including animals, plants, and the cosmos and spiritual world. Given the importance of social support and in response to limited cultural translation in the treatment of EDs, this research aimed to reconceptualize the ways in which social support can be understood and applied in ways that capture Indigenous worldviews.

Methods

Qualitative description

Data analysis aligned with the qualitative description (QD) method, a flexible approach that allows researchers to describe experiences and complex phenomena in a way that provides a rich in-depth understanding while staying close to participants’ words [46]. QD is particularly well suited for inquiry of rich insights into health related matters, for pragmatic research, and for gaining first hand knowledge from healthcare professionals [46]. Gaining first-hand knowledge from Indigenous healthcare professionals is especially important for including Indigenous perspectives on social support and identifying cultural elements that may be relevant for treatment. While a key strength of QD is its capacity to capture first-hand knowledge from Indigenous healthcare professionals practicing IFOT, findings may not be generalizable beyond the specific contexts studied [46, 47].

Further, the QD method was integrated with an Indigenous approach to research in that this work was done in a community-based way that emphasized reciprocity, respect, and relationality [48]. Elder guidance was incorporated throughout the research process that included a sharing circle, participant recruitment, and creating a grounding exercise and educational resources. The Elder developed educational resources for IFOT practitioners. Educational resources included the recording of the sharing circle posted to a private Facebook group, and two teaching videos that are widely accessible on YouTube. The educational resources that were developed by the Elder were used to support learning and understanding, enhance knowledge of IFOT, and ensure that knowledge was accessible and relevant to IFOT practitioners.

This study did not aim to prove or disprove a hypothesis [49]; instead, this study aimed to build understanding of what social support means from the perspectives of Indigenous therapists when supporting an Indigenous person with an ED.

Ethics

MP initiated the project by consulting with her Master’s supervisor, a Cree/Métis IFOT practitioner, who then sought guidance and approval from a respected Métis Elder regarding the research question and direction of the study. Upon receiving the Elder’s approval and securing ethics clearance from the University of Calgary’s Human Research Ethics Board, which adheres to the Tri-Council Policy Statement—2 guidelines for research involving Indigenous Peoples [50], the study commenced.

Inclusion criteria

Eligible participants were required to be IFOT practitioners who self-identified as First Nations, Métis, or Inuit. Inclusion criteria required participants to have strong knowledge of Indigenous perspectives. Participants were not eligible to participate if they lacked training and experience with IFOT or were non-Indigenous.

Recruitment

Recruitment was carried out through MP’s Master’s supervisor reaching out to IFOT practitioners and three individuals expressed interest in participating. Participants who were interested in participating received an email from MP to arrange for a mutually agreeable time for a sharing circle.

Consent

All participants were emailed an informed consent document and a predetermined agreed upon date for the sharing circle. One participant was unable to participate in the sharing circle, therefore an individual conversation was arranged over Zoom. During the interview and sharing circle, formal introductions were made and MP reviewed the informed consent form. Participants were aware that the sharing circle and conversation would be audio and video recorded, and the conversations would be transcribed verbatim. Participants were informed that they would be provided a copy of their respective transcript to ensure the accuracy of their words and have an opportunity to modify it. No participants modified their portion of the sharing circle or individual conversation due to participant burden of other commitments. All participants expressed their consent verbally. Participation was voluntary, and no financial compensation was provided. The participants consented to using their first names. For the purpose of this study, all participants will be referred to as Sisters due to the significance of the Three Sisters (corn, beans, and squash) in relation to food, and to emphasize relationships among participants and the aims of the research.

Participants

Three Sisters who identified as women and of Cree/Métis (n = 1), Métis (n = 1), and Stl’atl’imx (n = 1) ancestry agreed to take part. All participants had training and expertise in IFOT. All women were currently practicing therapists specializing in Indigenous therapy with over two years experience.

Procedure

To support the research, the Métis Elder developed a grounding exercise based on IFOT techniques, which consisted of an 8-min video posted on the IFOT community resource page as an educational resource for IFOT practitioners. MP completed the grounding exercise after the sharing circle. The Three Sisters were invited to speak about EDs from the lens of their practice and cultural knowledge.

Following the Elder’s guidance, data collection consisted of one sharing circle (n = 2) and one individual conversation (n = 1). MP facilitated the sharing circle and individual conversation virtually over Zoom. Each session was approximately 60 min in duration. A smudge ceremony opened the sharing circle, consistent with Indigenous protocols [51], creating a culturally grounded space for connection and discussion. MP began the sharing circle by telling a story of a young Cree/Métis girl’s experience with an ED, setting the tone for the conversation. A broad question was posed to the participants: how are EDs conceptualized among IFOT therapists?

Postionality

The current paper was led by a Cree/Métis doctoral candidate (MP), with guidance from a senior Métis researcher (MT), one non Indigenous early career researcher in ED (AA), and one non Indigenous doctoral candidate (SD). All authors bring their own backgrounds, perspectives, and cultural knowledge. MP is of Cree/Métis ancestry and has grown up with cultural and spiritual knowledge. MP is a member of the Métis Nation of Alberta, and from a small Cree/Métis community called Marlboro, Alberta. Her approach to data analysis was informed by her cultural knowledge. MP aims to bring cultural relevancy to her work with clients and often thinks about tensions between Indigenous and Euro-Western knowledge and how to integrate them. MP, at the time of writing this, is a Canadian Certified Counsellor and has worked with clients, Indigenous and non Indigenous with a range of mental health concerns. She approaches her counselling work through a Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) lens, Compassion focused approaches, and incorporates Indigenous knowledge and therapeutic techniques when appropriate.

AA is an early career researcher in young people’s mental health, with a specific focus on EDs. She is a White settler born and raised in Canada. She received her training in ED research at King’s College London. Bringing together personal and professional experiences, she spends much time thinking about the tension produced when knowledge from renowned urban institutions encounters rural, northern, and Indigenous contexts. \Within the current work, she supported the lead author to articulate and contextualize the dominant Euro-Western ED understandings and treatment approaches.

SD is a doctoral candidate and health services researcher with a focus on health equity, access to care, and the relational dimensions of health. She is an Arab Middle Eastern immigrant living and working in Canada, and her perspectives have been shaped by experiences of immigration and academic work examining how social and structural conditions influence care experiences. Bringing together personal and professional experiences, she often reflects on the tensions that arise when dominant Euro-Western health concepts are applied across distinct cultural, rural, and Indigenous contexts without sufficient attention to history, place, and relationships. Her work aims to support research that is contextually grounded and that brings diverse ways of knowing into relationship with care in respectful, non-extractive ways.

Melissa Tremblay (MT) is a citizen of the Métis Nation of Alberta and a member of the Lac Ste. Anne Métis Community Association. She is an Associate Professor in the Faculty of Education at the University of Alberta, and a Registered Psychologist. MT has clinical and scholarly expertise in Indigenous mental health and culturally responsive practice, with particular attention to how colonial histories and ongoing structural inequities shape wellbeing and access to care for Indigenous peoples. As a Métis scholar and practitioner, MT approached this work from a relational and strengths-based orientation. She supported methodological and interpretive decision-making by offering mentorship to the lead author, and attending to cultural integrity and accountability in the research process in ways that aim to honour participants’ perspectives and responsibilities to Indigenous communities.

Data analysis

After transcribing the sharing circle and individual conversation and completing member checking, MP began to notice and look for patterns of meaning in the data by coding the transcripts by hand. During this part of the data analysis, MP began to write down ideas and preliminary codes. MP began to generalize initial codes after becoming familiar with the data and developing a preliminary list of codes; coding was therefore data-driven. MP then organized the codes into potential themes based on the research question and began to consider how the identified codes formed an overarching theme pertaining to social support. MP then refined the themes to identify clear distinctions between the themes. MP created a thematic map to help differentiate and connect relationships among the codes. Themes were then defined.

Results

In response to the aim of exploring the meaning of social support from Indigenous therapists when working with Indigenous clients with EDs, what became central for participants were clustered around two themes: (1) Wahkotowin: relationships between all things and (2) Land as support and (re)connection. Findings from the sharing circle and individual conversation provide insight into cultural elements that may be considered within the context of EDs.

Wahkotowin: relationships between all things and Land as support and (re)connection support each other, showing a strong interconnected relationship between many facets of social support grounded in Indigenous knowledge. Theme 1 centered on Wahkotowin: relationships between all things. Wahkotowin translates to the relationship between all things and is a Cree concept of kinship and interconnection across human, ancestral, land-based, and cosmic relationships. Two subthemes of Wahkotowin centered on interconnectedness and body wisdom. The Sisters emphasized it as an embodied, lived practice rather than an abstract philosophy in therapeutic settings. Theme 2 focused on land as support and (re)connection, and three subthemes centered on the idea that land is an important consideration for social support. All Sisters recognized the importance of land as a support, connection, and reconnection. Land was described as a constant presence and a source of grounding, healing, and cultural continuity (Fig 1).

Theme 1: Wahkotowin: relationships between all things

Sister 1 indicated that Indigenous approaches to healing and wellness are lacking in work with clients presenting with EDs. The lack of culturally relevant approaches reinforces the individual working through the ED is alone.

Interconnectedness

The Sisters discussed the importance of Interconnectedness, which translates to Wahkotowin. Interconnectedness was seen as an integral component to ED treatment as it encompasses the relationship to all things demonstrating clients are not alone in their suffering.

​Sister 1: They’re [i.e., non-Indigenous approaches] missing the whole interconnectedness there, and that’s so important to wellness. It’s not individualized, it’s not all yours.

​Sister 1 challenged current ED support and therapy by situating the disorder as a collective experience, and there are interconnected relationships that are important to wellness. Situating the experience of EDs as a collective experience begins to connect the client to deeper relationships reflected in the theme of Wahkotowin. Wahkotowin includes the relationships among humans, animals, plants, the cosmos, and spiritual relationships accessible to individuals. The Sisters view an individual in a web of relationships rather than in isolation. The web of relationships an individual is part of includes family and ancestors. The inclusion of ancestral families becomes a distinctive feature of IFOT and Indigenous approaches to healing. The generational knowledge strengthens the notion of the importance of connecting to a collective experience. Sister 3 noted this:

Sister 3: [If the client says] it’s two generations, and you go wow, that’s a lot of knowing. That’s many generations of knowing about food and habits of eating has changed. Then it’s almost like learning or getting through the layers and noticing the wellness has always been there because they came in feeling gross and disgusting about their weight and how they were but then it’s noticing that “no, this is a beautiful medicine to know and learn that this has been generational and it came out of surviving and needing to protect yourself and then getting under those layers of your own critic and getting to the medicine of needing to wow, this has been shared and your blood memory knows this and it’s looking at it in a way that’s not a disorder, or an eating disorder. What’s she holding there about eating disorders? How many generations had this before her? Is this all hers? Is this something she’s sharing with all the other little girls?”

While Sister 3 expressed the relationship among all things, including generational knowledge, compassion was a unique finding. Sister 3 took a strengths-based approach, reframing experiences of EDs as a compassionate intergenerational conversation. A strengths-based and compassionate approach acknowledges the hardships of past generations, which can act as a powerful acknowledgement that individuals are not alone in their suffering. Sister 3 referred to this as beautiful medicine. Beautiful medicine represents a holistic, strengths-based approach to healing, as knowledge and wisdom are found within the client. Beautiful medicine also honours healing as a process that includes intergenerational relationships, such as the wisdom of ancestors, and as a compassionate way for individuals to know they are not alone in their suffering. Compassion was extended to mean the intergenerational wisdom from past ancestors.

Body wisdom

​Sister 1 emphasizes the importance of body wisdom. Body wisdom appears to be strongly connected to spiritual or ancestral knowing. This approach encourages individuals to connect to their bodies in an attempt to decipher why signs and symptoms of EDs are present.

Sister 1: You realize what your body is holding onto. Your body will do things and show up in certain ways. What is the message there? What’s it trying to tell you or show you, and there you are saying how intergenerational that was, including all the other survivors. As you put that in the room there saying that also happened here. The other survivors, when you put them in the room like that, it shows how interconnected we are and how vicarious it is.

​Sister 3 supports the importance of body wisdom. Body wisdom and intergenerational knowledge are closely connected to Wahkotowin. The Sisters describe this relationship as one in which we are all connected to our ancestors, who are seen as having wisdom. As a result, their wisdom comes out in signs and symptoms which try to speak to clients.

Sister 3: “We ask those questions about how collective it is. When [the client] sat there and realized this ‘isn’t just my mom, this goes further back’. Sometimes you can notice it just by the way they’re looking. When you have the questions to ask and notice, you can say ‘I notice you’re looking out this way and it can seem ancestral or older when a client is looking out that way. Sometimes you can get it wrong, and they will say, "No, I’m just looking at the wall or something." A lot of the time, it will be ‘no, I’m noticing it’s generational’.

​Body wisdom is unique from an Indigenous perspective as it includes a felt sense of collective understanding and ancestral knowing that can be connected to spirituality. Ancestors are understood as supportive collective entities and are in spiritual relationships. The importance of all the relationships an individual is part of is a unique form of social support that can facilitate a strengths-based approach to healing.

​Theme 2: land as support and (re)connection

​In addition to Wahkotowin, a common theme among all Sisters was the importance of land as a support, a connection, and a means of reconnection. The land connects to the animal, plant, cosmos, and human nation through the belief that all things are connected. Land as support, connection, and reconnection was described as a constant presence and a source of grounding, healing, and cultural continuity. Subthemes of Community/Social Connections, Land/Environment Relationships, and Economic Restraint.

​Community/social connections

Sister 1: [Events in the community, on the land] bring people together. I’m not sure when the seals come in, [but] you can just feel everybody coming together. It’s just so healthy, beautiful, and delicious. You can see wellness go up in communities. The Goose Hunt time in James Bay. You see entire families beaming. The colour of their skin changes. Have you ever sat in the kitchen with four or five women shelling peas? You feel the mood in the room when you’re all shelling peas or canning together. When that’s missing, and you’re all alone, it’s pretty hard to live.

Sister 1 talked about the importance of connection with human relatives and community on the land. The land offers beautiful, unique opportunities to observe, connect with, or reconnect with others in a culturally grounded way. Opportunities in the community offer a unique way to reduce isolation and enhance connection to others. In addition, community and social connections bring about rich opportunities to learn about traditional and spiritual teachings around harvesting or hunting practices.

Land/environment relationships

For some people, access to culturally grounded community activities may help Indigenous peoples reconnect with their culture. Sister 3 expressed the importance of land in therapy settings.

Sister 3: We put it out on the land. Like, even in this space, we always have land around us. No matter where we are, whether in a concrete jungle or a city, land is always there for you. What’s going on here is you can make the inside out by making space with it by using pebble stones like grandfathers [rocks] and setting it out like one thing at a time, what’s showing up in your body right now that’s causing trouble or an issue in your life. You set that out, and you get a breath there, and you’re like, ‘Okay, now I have a moment to just notice that’s there’.

​In contrast to community events, Sister 3 notes that land is accessible at any time and is a meaningful support for clients. Culturally relevant therapeutic techniques were described as using the land. Sister 3 spoke about the ways that they found helpful to explicitly incorporate the land into sessions with clients. IFOT therapists make land accessible in sessions in various ways (e.g. using rocks). Using land is a means of social support, as it helps clients create space between themselves and the ED.

Economic restraint

​Although Sister 3 discussed land in the context of drawing on support to address concerns with problems, Sister 2 discussed the land in terms of restricted food sovereignty, which impacts the means of social support for Indigenous peoples.

Sister 2: I am a Métis person without land. I am in a situation where I live in a condo. I cannot have a garden. I don’t have access to it. There are a lot of Indigenous people wherever they are living, whatever’s going on in their lives, they don’t have access to traditional food. They can’t hunt, they don’t have the right to hunt, or if they have rights to hunt, they’re very restricted. These colonial policies that are happening today are very interconnected to this conversation we’re having on eating disorders, and I think just again the importance of acknowledging that you know what gets labelled as anorexia or bulimia.

​Sister 2 talked about the consequences of colonial practices that impact Indigenous peoples’ access to traditional food and the disruption of social support, particularly in urban settings. Access to land is particularly important for some Indigenous people and is understood as what sustains people by supporting and sustaining the four quadrants of health and wellness for individuals. Sister 2 notes that the restricted access to land as a place to gather food impedes Indigenous peoples' food sovereignty and connection to medicine and tradition. The lack of access to land can impact Indigenous people with EDs, as they may not have access to traditional foods. In summary, the Sisters touch on important components of Indigenous perspectives and the various forms of social support that may help Indigenous peoples with EDs.

Discussion

This study has outlined cultural elements that may be important to consider when providing social support for Indigenous peoples with EDs; Wahkotowin (relationships with all things) and Land as Support and (re)Connection. Important treatment considerations for expanding social support include community and social connections, which can harness (re)connection to culture and spiritual components. Figure 2 depicts important considerations for social support when working with Indigenous peoples with EDs. Figure 2 is depicted in a circular way to demonstrate that the relevant domains to supporting an Indigenous person with an ED are inseparable and blend into each other. In addition, each domain can affect the next because they are interconnected. The economic domain affects access to land and the environment, which, in turn, shapes cultural practices, which are directly connected to spiritual, community, and social domains.

Key cultural considerations for relevant social support for indiginous peoples with EDs

Subsistence-based living is represented by the economic domain of Fig 2. Subsistence-based living encompasses activities such as obtaining, processing, producing, and consuming food; preserving culture; and promoting physical activity [52]. Subsistence-based living is directly connected to the land, environment, and cultural practices, as well as spirituality, but also involves the promotion of communal relationships [52]. One Sister discussed the limitations of existing policies that negatively affect access to land and, in turn, subsistence practices. Subsistence-based practices can offset economic marginalizations through resourcefulness and promote well-being [52]. Subsistence-based practices and land are closely connected, as they offer opportunities to learn traditional food teachings that enhance social and spiritual connections [53], which was reflected in one Sister’s comment in community events. The land is seen as an integral component of Indigenous well-being due to the cultural, spiritual, and ancestral connections [53]. Access to land has been a salient protective factor for Indigenous youths’ mental health and well-being [54]. Connection to the land opens opportunities to connect with culture, Elders, community members, ancestors, and helps build strong communities [54]. Community and social connections are an important component of Indigenous well-being and include opportunities to attend social events regularly [53]. Regular opportunities to connect with the community have positive impacts on reducing isolation and enhancing relationship building [52]. Spirituality is another important component of Indigenous well-being [53], and can be integrated through cultural practices. Although Canadian research on spirituality in ED treatment is limited, emerging literature from studies with Amazonian Indigenous peoples with EDs suggests that incorporating ceremonial components into treatment enhances spiritual healing and is associated with improved well-being [43, 44]. With a strong connection to ancestors, spirituality was particularly salient among the Sisters. Spirituality is connected to all facets of life and can help with managing stress and difficult emotions [43, 53]. Closely connected to spirituality is culture. Culture is woven throughout other forms of social support, connecting an individual to spirituality, community, and the land [53]. The key cultural considerations relevant to social support for Indigenous peoples with EDs include elements supported by existing literature for enhancing well-being. Understanding Indigenous well-being as relational and spiritual may offer a unique alignment with the Theory of Embodiment, with opportunities to integrate cultural elements.

Drawing on the Theory of Embodiment, the discussion examines the themes to better integrate cultural elements in the treatment of EDs. Wahkotowin (relationships with all things) connects to the idea that our bodies are a source of knowledge, and that knowledge acquisition occurs through how we relate to others and through our experiences of the world. The knowledge resides in the body, allowing individuals to get a sense of others and the world [42]. According to the Theory of Embodiment, we can become consciously aware of the knowledge from others [42]. The knowledge of others was reflected in the Sisters' descriptions of knowledge as a collective knowing that multiple generations have contributed to. The idea of collective knowing may reflect intergenerational journeys. Intergenerational journeys are reflected in scholarship aimed at understanding healthy embodiment and the impact of the relationship between daughters and mothers [41]. Mothers can and do play an important role in growing their daughters’ sense of embodiment, and mothers are seen as an intergenerational resource [41]. This study extends the meaning of intergenerational resources to include ancestral knowledge as a way of knowing and a means of coming to know. Wakotowin (relationships with all things) emphasizes intergenerational knowledge, where Land as Support and (re)Connection addresses the movement of individuals.

Piran [39]distinguishes between physical engagement and blocked physical engagement in the Theory of Embodiment. When girls and women engage in non-objectifying, non-sexualized, and non-weight-and-shape-focused physical activities, increased physical agency, body awareness, and connection can occur [39]. The idea of freedom in physical engagement was reflected in the activities the Sisters discussed with others in community cultural events and in a shared sense of togetherness. Barriers to joyful physical movement that impact Indigenous girls and women include colonial policies that restrict access to traditional foods and medicines. Historical accounts of blocked physical engagement include the nutrition experiments during the residential school period. In Canada, nutrition experiments occurred between 1942 and 1952, during which research on children in residential schools examined the effects of nutritional interventions that eventually informed dietary knowledge [55]. The historical context of the residential school period invites critical reflection on how embodied movement has been restricted through policies and practices, and on how this knowledge can be applied to other treatment approaches.

While this study may deepen understanding of embodiment, other approaches may benefit from incorporating cultural perspectives into ED treatment. Many treatment approaches to EDs may have cross-cultural applicability. Existing treatments with a strong evidence base show high attrition rates and, in some cases, a chronic and severe course, suggesting that different treatment approaches are needed [56]. Narrative therapy is a promising approach to treating EDs [57] that positions the client as the expert of their life and frames problems, such as EDs, as external to the individual [57]. Narrative therapy aims to help clients unpack the meaning of their problem, identify, and reconstruct hidden identity narratives [58]. Internal family systems (IFS) is another emerging and promising approach to the treatment of EDs that sees the psyche having distinct parts that conflict with each other [59, 60]. Clients tend to personify their ED [61], which aligns with narrative therapists position that the ED over takes the client [62]and IFS would posit that clients talk about their ED as different parts; for example, a part of the client being scared of the ED and another part as doing what the ED says [60].

From a theoretical perspective, IFOT is parallel to approaches in narrative therapy and IFS. The shared emphasis on distinct others may serve as approaches to incorporate cross-cultural applicability. Across these approaches, aspects of the client are integrated. Narrative therapy and IFS parts work address aspects of an individual, whereas IFOT expands the focus to include other relations, such as ancestors, and draws upon the interconnectedness of all things across time [63].

Clinical implications

The wisdom of the Sisters indicates a need to incorporate cultural knowledge into ED treatment. Based on the themes from this study, there is rich potential to incorporate Indigenous perspectives into ED treatment that focuses on social support. McBride and Kwee [41] note that spirituality can play an important role in helping clients focus on non-appearance domains. The focus on non-appearance domains for Indigenous clients is reflected in the Sisters’ wisdom by connecting with the earth and teaching clients about intergenerational wisdom. Teaching clients may help shift their process to focus on the messages symptoms might be conveying and may help them enhance compassion through connection. Incorporating land-based strategies or activities that connect an individual with ED to culture and community may have salient clinical implications. Community events or activities that focus on building cultural knowledge and connecting with others may enhance an Indigenous client’s wellbeing. This aligns with previous research with Wet’suwet’en peoples who participated in IFOT with Wet’suwet’en worldviews [29]. Participants reported a shift in their healing, expanding from individual to ancestral healing to enhance collective resiliency [29].

Organizations that advocate and support individuals and families with experiences of EDs have an opportunity to engage with the Canadian Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) Calls to Action through a meaningful, non-performative way to support and work with Indigenous peoples to ensure access to culturally meaningful and relevant support [64]. Meaningful community engagement is encouraged for researchers and treatment providers who work with Indigenous peoples with EDs. Meaningful community engagement ensures that research and knowledge sharing are conducted in a respectful, reciprocal way and honour the diverse worldviews of each Nation. Indigenous Tools for Living is a condensed version of IFOT developed for frontline workers supporting clients experiencing complex trauma [63], although it has not been clinically tested with ED populations. Clinical implications include incorporating land into sessions, registering for Indigenous Tools for Living, integrating community events led by culture, learning about intergenerational wisdom, engaging the community, and connecting interested clients with EDs to Elders and knowledge keepers to learn about the cultural significance of food. All clinical implications are rich areas to explore in ED treatment.

Study limitations and future directions

​This study’s sample consisted of three First Nations and/or Métis practitioners and may not be generalizable to all First Nations, Métis, or Inuit practitioners, nor may their perspectives reflect those of other Nations. Given that this study speaks to the perspectives of therapists and practitioners, it does not give insight into what Indigenous peoples struggling with EDs view as social support. Another limitation of this study was that only one author analyzed and interpreted the data. This study would have benefited from two or more authors analyzing and interpreting the data to add breadth to the results [65]. A third limitation was the small sample size and lack of follow up sharing circles and individual conversations to ensure data saturation was achieved. A final limitation is that Panofsky et al. [29] note that more research is needed to substantiate the effectiveness of IFOT when working with Indigenous peoples. To our knowledge, there is no research that explores the effectiveness of IFOT when working with Indigenous peoples with EDs. Despite limited research on IFOT's treatment of EDs, there are many avenues for exploration. The results of this study suggest that further research is needed for; 1) Understanding the role of Indigenous worldviews in the treatment of EDs , including what social supports are salient to Indigenous women and girls with EDs ; 2) Adapting and testing family-based therapy for EDs to include Indigenous worldviews; 3) The outcomes and impact of Indigenous worldviews in the treatment of EDs for Indigenous women and girls; 4) The impact on the lack food sovereignty and subsistence based living for Indigenous peoples with EDs, and 5) The effectiveness and meaning of integrating cultural community events in ED treatment. In general, it would be prudent to broaden the scope of the study to include different genders, ages, and other Nations which can be overlooked or pan-Indigenized. In Canada, there is an urgent need for epidemiological research, including but not limited to prevalence rates, determinants of health, and health outcomes of Indigenous people with EDs living in urban and rural spaces.

Conclusions

This study contributes novel insights to the field of EDs regarding the reconceptualization of social support when working with Indigenous peoples. Findings may provide a culturally relevant and meaningful framework warranting further investigation, as well as practical implications for treatment. Practitioners should remain open to discussing and encouraging other forms of social support and connectedness that resonate with the individual in front of them. Increasing understanding of what social support can entail brings the field closer to including diverse perspectives, specifically when working with Indigenous peoples struggling with EDs.

Social support can be broadened to include ancestral ties, community-hosted cultural events, connection to the land, and cultural engagement. Practitioners are encouraged to learn about Indigenous worldviews, their clients’ connection to culture and community, and explore culturally relevant means of social support available to them. Within this context, it is important for clinicians to build relationships with Indigenous community members to learn about their worldviews. Drawing on other forms of social support beyond family, partners, and close friends can expand an individual's support network. These connections are from ancestors, non-human relations, land, and include unique economic considerations and community and social connections grounded in culture. This aligns well with efforts to address culture in ED care and treatment. These results can help advance the field of EDs and integrate culturally relevant care for Indigenous peoples struggling with EDs. In Canada, there is still much work to be done in this area, and future work may build on our findings.
