High-performance stretchable conductive film on complex microscale structures via spontaneous fusion of liquid metal
Authors: Sen Zhou, Bo Pang, Ganguang Yang, Zhixin Wang, Siting Wei, Yuqi Qiu, Qingyang Zheng, Zhouping Yin, Changsheng Wu, Tianzhao Bu, Hao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conformal fabrication of stretchable conductive film on micro-structured surfaces is important for flexible electronics. Existing methods based on material design and structural engineering suffer from limitations in stretchability, conductivity, and geometrical compatibility. Here, we propose a universal strategy for fabricating high-performance stretchable conductive film on complex micro-structured surfaces. By precisely controlling the interparticle liquid bridge force and the critical rupture strength of liquid metal (LM) particle shells, the spontaneous fusion of LM particles is realized to form high-performance stretchable conductive film without requiring extra activation processes. Driven by the Laplace pressure difference, the stretchable conductive film can be conformally deposited on arbitrary 3D microstructures such as high-aspect-ratio vertical interconnect accesses. This capability enables the development of stretchable multilayer electronic systems with high stability even under dynamic strain. Moreover, the thin film allows the exposure of sweat pores and hair when forming seamless coupling with human skin, enabling highly effective electrical stimulation and high-fidelity electrophysiological monitoring with sweat permeability and imperceptibility. This work establishes a universal approach for integrating high-performance stretchable conductors onto complex 3D architectures, bridging material-level innovation with system-level implementation for wearable electronics and bio-integrated systems.

Introduction

With the rapid expansion of flexible electronics into emerging fields such as wearable health monitoring, soft robotics, human-machine interactions, the fabrication of stretchable conductors on complex microstructures that can serve as both electrical interconnects in flexible electronic systems and bioelectrodes for physiological monitoring has become a key technology1–4. Recent advances in stretchable conductors have been achieved through innovations in material design and structural engineering5. Approaches based on structural engineering, such as serpentine patterns or origami-inspired architectures, facilitate the integration of rigid electronic devices with stretchable polymer substrates6,7. However, these methods are unsuitable for multilayer interconnects. In contrast, although intrinsically stretchable polymer conductors allow the fabrication of three-dimensional (3D) interconnections, their electrical and mechanical performances are limited8,9. Although existing stretchable conductors can form complex geometric patterns on planar substrates or curved surfaces with large radius, achieving conformal coverage over complex micro-structured 3D surfaces such as human skin is still challenging.

Gallium-based liquid metals (LMs) have attracted great attention owing to their unique combination of metal-like electrical conductivity and liquid-like deformability10–21. However, in the ambient environment, LM particles (LMPs) are enveloped by spontaneously formed oxide layers22,23. To fabricate continuous stretchable conductive film, additional conductivity activation processes for LMPs fusion through mechanical force, laser irradiation, acoustic excitation, thermal or chemical treatment are necessary24. However, mechanical force is susceptible to cause undesired leakage of LM. Laser irradiation and acoustic excitation suffer from low material utilization efficiency. Thermal and chemical methods require exposure to elevated temperatures and reactive chemicals, leading to poor substrate compatibility. Moreover, these extra activation processes are particularly prone to introducing defects when LMPs are applied to 3D microstructures, thereby limiting the applicability to substrates with complex topographies. In recent years, researchers attempted to develop activation-free strategies, although progresses had been made, these approaches remained largely confined to planar substrates, fibers or surfaces with large radii of curvature25–28. Consequently, a broadly applicable strategy for fabricating stretchable electronics on complex microscale structures remains a challenge.

In this work, we synthesize a material, primarily composed of Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), LM, Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), and Citric acid (CA), which is referred to as PLMAC, and develop a universal strategy for fabricating stretchable conductive film on 3D complex microstructures based on PLMAC. By engineering a thin, hydrophilic PVA layer on the surface of the LMP shells, the liquid bridge system is established between adjacent particles. During solvent evaporation, the liquid bridge force (LBF) increases dramatically and ultimately exceeds the critical rupture strength (CRS) of the LMP shells, thereby triggering the spontaneous fusion of LMPs. When atomized via spray coating, PLMAC ink forms a highly mobile aqueous layer that can infiltrate into the deep regions of micro-structured surfaces. As the water solvent evaporates, the high-performance stretchable conductive film is conformally formed on the surface of complex microstructures due to the spontaneous fusion of LMPs. Thus, the PLMAC film can be deposited on the surfaces of high-aspect-ratio vertical interconnect access (VIA) serving as 3D interconnections, which enables the development of multilayer stretchable electronic systems. Moreover, the PLMAC film can be directly deposited on complex biological surfaces, such as human hairy skin and micro-wrinkled tissues, to form an imperceptible and conformal epidermal electrode, which enables highly effective electrical stimulation and high-fidelity monitoring of microvolt-level electrophysiological signals.

Results

Stretchable conductive film based on the spontaneous fusion of LMPs

This PLMAC composite can be dispersed in water to form an ink for spray coating (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1). The spontaneous fusion of LMPs is facilitated by controlling the interparticle LBF and the CRS of particle shells. The surface modification by PVA introduces polar functional groups to impart the LMPs with excellent wettability towards water. After the PLMAC ink is sprayed onto substrate, the enhanced wettability promotes strong cohesive interactions between LMPs (Fig. 1b). As the water evaporates, surface tension induces the formation of concave meniscus liquid bridge between the hydrophilic surfaces of adjacent LMPs, generating an LBF that pulls adjacent particles together, which facilitates the fusion of LMPs. In addition, CA can reduce the thickness of oxide layer, resulting in a decrease in the CRS (Fig. 1b). When the LBF exceeds the CRS, the particle shells start to crack, causing the interconnection between LMPs. After water evaporation, a conformally coated PLMAC film remains on the micro-structured surface. Therefore, the PLMAC film can be deposited onto an arbitrary substrate with complex 3D microstructures (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 3). Moreover, the PLMAC film can also be directly deposited onto the complicated surfaces of skin, serving as imperceptible epidermal electrodes for electrical stimulation and physiological signals monitoring (Fig. 1h).

During the early and middle stages of solvent evaporation, the interparticle space remains rich in moisture, and the resulting LBF has not yet reached its maximum. As evaporation proceeds and the liquid bridge volume progressively decreases, the LBF per unit area increases sharply. The LBF reaches its peak value immediately prior to complete solvent evaporation (Supplementary Fig. 4). When this maximum LBF exceeds the CRS of LMP shells, spontaneous fusion of LMPs is successfully triggered. Therefore, our analysis focuses primarily on the terminal stage of evaporation, at which the majority of LMPs are in close-packed contact (H = 0) and the intervening liquid bridges become exceedingly slender (φ → 0). The detailed derivation is provided in Supplementary Note 1. According to Eq. (5), we can control the LBF by regulating the contact angle θθ. Due to the surface modulation of PVA on LMPs, the contact angle θθ is determined by the PVA content in PLMAC. As shown in Fig. 2b, the conductivity of PLMAC film is first enhanced and then reduced with the increasing of PVA content. For bare hydrophobic LMPs, liquid forms a convex meniscus between the adjacent particle shells (Fig. 2c-i), and the contact angle is 142.5° in this condition (Fig. 2d). According to Eq. (5), the LBF is calculated negative as a repulsive force, which prevents the fusion of LMPs (Fig. 2c-i). As the content of PVA increases, the contact angle of the LMPs is reduced to 44.5°. Hydrophilic modification by PVA reduces the interfacial energy between the LMP shell and the water solvent34, which allows the formation of a concave meniscus with a small radius of curvature (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Movie 2). Thus, the positive LBF as an attractive force is generated, which induces the rupture of particle shells and promotes the spontaneous fusion of LMPs (Fig. 2c-ii and Supplementary Movie 3). However, PVA also has the adverse effect of increasing the CRS. Consequently, when the particle surface is excessively modified by PVA, the CRS is greatly enhanced and exceeds the LBF, leading to the inhibition of fusion of LMPs (Fig. 2c-iii).

To evaluate the effect of PVA on the CRS of LMPs, we conducted rupture experiments on individual particles by using atomic force microscopy (AFM). Supplementary Fig. 5 presents the force-displacement curve obtained by the AFM probe and the four stages of a single-particle rupture experiment, in which the third stage corresponds to particle rupture. As shown in Fig. 2e, for particles with a diameter of 3.5 μm, the CRS increases substantially by 2.19 μN upon the addition of excess PVA (5.33 wt%), whereas with an optimal amount of PVA (2.67 wt%), the CRS rises by only 0.35 μN. Therefore, a moderate amount of PVA (2.67 wt%) was selected as the optimal condition, achieving a balance between enhanced wettability (a positive LBF) and a minimal increase in CRS.

In addition, CA is introduced to synthesize PLMAC with a thinner oxide layer. As shown in Fig. 2b, compared with PLM (containing LM and PVA), the conductivity of PLMC (containing LM, PVA, and CA) film is improved after the addition of CA. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, for particles with a diameter of 3.5 µm, the PLMC particle exhibits a CRS of 3.15 µN, which is 0.33 µN lower than that of PLM (3.48 µN). The improvement is attributed to the increased acidity of the liquid bridge during solvent evaporation, which thins the oxide layer of LMPs, thus reducing the CRS. This mechanism is confirmed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which reveals that the thickness of oxide layer of LMPs is reduced from 4.1 nm to 2.7 nm (Fig. 2f). To further elucidate the chemical change, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra indicate that the introduction of CA effectively weakens the dominant role of Ga2O3 in overall Ga composition (Supplementary Fig. 7).

We fabricated PLMAC film with different sizes, and its electrical conductivity remains stable, which indicates that the spontaneous fusion of LMPs occurs uniformly throughout the entire film (Supplementary Fig. 8). Moreover, the PLMAC film is compatible with a wide range of substrate materials. We have successfully deposited PLMAC film onto polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), polyimide (PI), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), very high bond (VHB) tape, and silicon wafers (Supplementary Fig. 9 and 10).

Electrical and mechanical performances of PLMAC film

As shown in Fig. 3a, the carboxyl groups of CA can react with the hydroxyl groups of PVA via esterification, resulting in cross-linking between free PVA in solution and PVA adsorbed on the surface of LMPs. This cross-linking enhances the stretchability of the PLMAC film. When stretched, the cross-linked network entangled on the interconnected particles acts as a stress-dissipating structure, thereby significantly enhancing its mechanical stability. In addition, the introduction of AgNO3 solution leads to the in-situ formation of AgNPs on the shells of the LMPs via a reduction reaction. The excellent wettability between Ag and LM reduces the separation between AgNPs and LMPs during stretching, ensuring the strain-insensitivity of PLMAC film. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analyses confirmed the structure of PLMAC particles. As shown in Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 11, AgNPs grow in situ on the surface of the LMP oxide shell and are further embedded within the cross-linked PVA layer. This structure prevents extensive direct contact between the AgNPs and the LM core, thereby suppressing the large-scale formation of brittle intermetallic compounds. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 12, the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the PLMAC film exhibits strong characteristic diffraction peaks of Ga and In, along with weak but discernible peaks of Ag-Ga intermetallic compounds, confirming that a limited degree of Ag-Ga alloying reaction indeed occurs in the PLMAC film35. We measured the resistance change (ΔR/R0) of PLMAC film under uniaxial tensile strain. When stretched to 120% strain, the PLMAC film exhibits a negligible resistance change (ΔR/R0 = 0.167). This value is much lower than the theoretical prediction based on Poisson’s law for an incompressible conductor (ΔR/R0 = (1 + ε)2 – 1, where ε is the applied strain. For 120% strain, ΔR/R0 = 3.84). Moreover, after 3000 cycles of 100% strain, the PLMAC film maintains stable electrical performance (Supplementary Fig. 15). We also measured the resistance change of PLMAC film under twisting and bending conditions (Fig. 3c). The PLMAC film demonstrates stable resistance (ΔR/R0 < 0.015) against substantial torsional deformation (up to 360°) and bending deformation (with a curvature radius as small as 1 mm).

The influence of material composition on the electrical and mechanical properties was measured, as shown in Fig. 3d. As the strain was increased to 700%, the PLM film presented a sharp increase in resistance, and the ΔR/R0 of the PLMAC film (1.129) was much lower than that of the PLMC film (3.884) at 700% strain. SEM images reveal that, under 100% uniaxial tensile strain, the PLMC film developed microcracks, whereas the PLMAC film remained entirely crack-free under the same conditions. (Supplementary Fig. 16). These results demonstrate that PVA cross-linking significantly improves film stretchability, and the AgNPs effectively preserve the structural integrity of the film, thereby mitigating the impact of large strain on conductivity. We further subjected the three types of film to cyclic tensile strain of 500%. The PLM film has a dramatic resistance shift during the initial cycles (Fig. 3e). While, both PLMC and PLMAC film maintain stable electrical properties after 700 cycles (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 17). The outstanding cyclic stability shown in Fig. 3e, f confirms that PVA cross-linking endows the film with excellent electrical and mechanical stability under repeated tensile strain. Furthermore, the electrical resistance and the ΔR/R0 under 700% tensile strain of the PLMAC film after seven days of aging remain virtually unchanged, demonstrating the excellent long-term stability of the PLMAC film (Supplementary Fig. 18). Compared with the state-of-the-art stretchable conductors3,10,13,37–43, the PLMAC film exhibits an outstanding performance with a quality factor (defined as the reciprocal of the gauge factor at maximum strain) of 6.2, and a conductivity of 1.36 × 106 S m⁻¹, demonstrating superior comprehensive performances (Fig. 3g).

A stretchable multilayer electronic system based on PLMAC film

The high aspect ratio VIA with highly conductive interconnects is important but challenging to achieve for high-density flexible electronic systems. Oxygen plasma treatment renders the VIA sidewalls hydrophilic, promoting the formation of a concave liquid meniscus that draws the PLMAC ink into the channel (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Movie 4). After heating, rapid solvent evaporation at the pinned contact line on the sidewall induces an outward radial capillary flow (coffee-ring effect), which transports LMPs from the center of the VIA toward the sidewall. This process drives selective LMPs deposition and subsequent spontaneous fusion on the sidewalls, ultimately leaving the VIAs unobstructed. This unique hollow VIA structure is demonstrated by SEM and EDS images of the VIA cross-sections (Fig. 1f). Typically, to achieve high-density flexible electronic systems, VIA is solid-filled with conductive materials to serve as 3D conductive interconnects44. However, due to the modulus mismatch between the substrate and the conductive materials, VIA walls become the regions of stress concentration under large strain, leading to mechanical failure. Compared with the conventional solid-filled structure, this unique hollow structure effectively mitigates delamination between conductive materials and VIA walls. Finite element analysis (FEA) reveals that, under 50% tensile strain, the normal contact force between the conductive material and the VIA sidewalls in the hollow structure is uniformly distributed along the circumference and is reduced by nearly two orders of magnitude compared to that in the solid-filled structure, thereby substantially alleviating the adhesion requirements for the conductive material (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 19). Despite this intrinsic advantage, robust adhesion remains essential and is achieved through two synergistic mechanisms enabled by oxygen plasma pretreatment. Plasma-generated silanol groups esterify with PVA hydroxyl groups, forming a covalent network that dissipates interfacial stress45. Additionally, plasma-induced nanoscale roughness allows the ink to infiltrate surface asperities, and upon solvent evaporation and LMPs fusion, the film becomes mechanically interlocked into the PDMS topography. We subjected the hollow VIAs to 2,200 cycles of 50% tensile strain. Properties of the PLMAC epidermal electrode

Most flexible epidermal electrodes are planar patch-type devices, which cannot form conformal contact with the microscopic textures of skin (Supplementary Fig. 30). The resulting gaps at the electrode-skin interface increase the contact impedance and are prone to causing motion artifacts. To address this issue, we sprayed the PLMAC ink directly onto the skin surface. Owing to its high fluidity, the ink infiltrates deep into epidermal folds. Within a few minutes, the PLMAC undergoes in-situ spontaneous fusion on skin surface, forming stretchable conductive film that achieve seamless coupling with the skin microstructure. Due to the very low film thickness (as low as 15.8 μm, Supplementary Fig. 31), the PLMAC electrode deposited on the epidermis exhibits distinct ridge structure, precisely matching the texture of skin and allowing the exposure of sweat pores and hair (Fig. 5a). Meanwhile, the stretchable PLMAC electrode can accommodate significant muscle deformation (Fig. 5b). We measured the skin contact impedance of PLMAC electrode, PDMS-based electrode (PLMAC film deposited on PDMS substrate), and commercial electrode (Ag/AgCl electrode). The results demonstrate that the skin contact impedance of PLMAC electrode is an order of magnitude lower than that of the other electrodes in the frequency range of electrophysiological signals (1-100 Hz) (Fig. 5c). We recorded electromyography (EMG) signals simultaneously with both PLMAC and commercial electrodes under cyclic compression of the muscle. The results show that the PLMAC electrode can stably capture EMG signals during muscle deformation, while the signal noise of commercial electrodes increases progressively (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 32). Demonstrations of electrophysiological monitoring by the PLMAC electrode

To validate that PLMAC film can serve as high-performance epidermal electrode for both recording and stimulation, we conducted a series of electrophysiological tests. Neurophysiological monitoring is an indispensable technique used for assessing functional integrity of neural pathways, employing key modalities such as somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs) and motor evoked potentials (MEPs). However, obtaining reproducible signals requires averaging hundreds of individual potential traces. Consequently, SSEPs and MEPs impose high demands on electrode performance for both stimulation and acquisition to minimize the number of averaging and enhance diagnostic efficiency. We deposited PLMAC electrodes on the scalp of rabbit brain, serving as monitors for evoked potentials and stimulators (Fig. 6a, b and Supplementary Fig. 38). As shown in Fig. 6c, PLMAC electrodes exhibit a lower impedance (at 20 Hz) and a higher SNR compared with the cup electrodes (CE) (Supplementary Fig. 39). To evaluate the performance of PLMAC electrode in recording SSEPs, commercial needle electrodes were used to stimulate the median nerve of rabbits with current intensities of 5 mA, 10 mA, 20 mA, and 25 mA. The N20 evoked potential was detected on the left channel (CH1). The latency of N20 remained stable at approximately 20 ms and was unaffected by the variation in current intensity, while the amplitude of the N20/P25 increased with higher current intensity up to 20 mA (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 40a). Consequently, we applied a current intensity of 20 mA to stimulate the median nerves of both upper limbs and the tibial nerve, and recorded the corresponding evoked potentials from CH1-CH3. The results show that the amplitudes of the N20/P25 and P37/N45 are significantly larger in the channels contralateral to the stimulation site, whereas these potentials are markedly less discernible in the ipsilateral channels, which is consistent with clinical observations (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 40b). PLMAC electrodes were also applied for transcranial electrical stimulation of the rabbit brain to generate MEP. With increasing current intensities (20 mA, 40 mA, 60 mA, 80 mA and 100 mA), the amplitude of MEP increases correspondingly (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 40c). Discussion

The rapid evolution of flexible electronics demands stretchable conductors that can conformally adhere to complex micro-structured surfaces, such as human skin or intricate 3D architectures. Traditional methods relying on material design or structural engineering face limitations in stretchability, conductivity, and geometric compatibility. In this study, we propose a facile and universal strategy for conformally fabricating high-performance stretchable conductors on complex 3D microstructures via the spontaneous fusion of LMPs. The proposed method achieves spontaneous fusion of LMPs by precisely controlling the LBF and the CRS. Through surface modification with PVA, we impart hydrophilic properties to LMPs, enabling the formation of concave menisci between particles. The resulting LBF acts as an internal driving force for aggregation, while CA reduces the oxide layer thickness, reducing the CRS to facilitate shell rupture.

The PLMAC material embodies a holistic design, synergistically integrating multiple functionalities into a single water-processable system. PVA not only hydrophilically modifies the LMP surface to generate the LBF that drives spontaneous fusion of LMPs, but also undergoes esterification cross-linking with CA, improving the stretchability of the PLMAC film. In addition, PVA can cross-link with activated substrate surfaces, enhancing the substrate compatibility of the PLMAC film. The AgNPs reduced in-situ on the LMP surface serve as nanoscale anchors that improve the strain insensitivity of the electrical performance. Furthermore, the sustained and synergistic release of Ag⁺ and Ga³⁺ ions imparts inherent antibacterial properties to the material. We have provided a more comprehensive comparison in Supplementary Table 1 between the PLMAC and the latest advances in LM-based conductors, including activation method, maximum strain, electrical conductivity, substrate compatibility and the capability of conform to microscale 3D structures. This comparison highlights the unique advantages offered by PLMAC: activation-free fabrication, high electrical conductivity, and the ability to conformally coat microscale structures.

A significant advantage of our method is the ability to form conformal coatings on challenging microstructures, such as high-aspect-ratio VIAs. Leveraging Laplace pressure difference, the PLMAC ink seamlessly covers vertical walls, creating hollow conductive structure that minimize stress concentration. This enables the development of stretchable multilayer electronic systems with high stability, as shown by consistent signal output under dynamic strains. The versatility extends to biological surfaces, where PLMAC electrode achieves high-performance stimulation and low-impedance, artifact-free monitoring of microvolt-level electrophysiological signals from animals and human subjects. We also compare previously reported fabrication approaches for conformal electronic systems, as shown in Supplementary Table 2. Those approaches typically suffer from limited material mechanical or electrical performance and substrate compatibility, rely on costly specialized equipment and complex fabrication processes. More importantly, they are generally restricted to surfaces with millimeter-scale radii of curvature. In contrast, this method is not only applicable to micrometer scale surfaces and a wide range of substrate, but also exhibits excellent conductivity and stretchability, demonstrating excellent overall performances.

The spontaneous fusion strategy of LMPs addresses critical challenges in the fabrication of highly conductive and stretchable interconnects on 3D sophisticated microstructures. The demonstrated applications in the multilayer stretchable electronic system and imperceptible epidermal electrodes highlight the translational potential, offering new avenues for wearable technology and biomedical engineering.

Methods

Materials

Unless otherwise specified, all chemicals used in this study were analytical grade and used as received without further purification. The LM alloy was purchased from Dongwan Huatai Metal Materials Technology Co., Ltd. CA and PVA were obtained from Aladdin. AgNO3 solution (1.001 mol/L) was purchased from Guangzhou Hewei Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. PDMS (Sylgard 184, Dow Corning), VHB tape (3 M), PET, PI, PTFE, and silicon wafers were supplied by Suzhou Yancai Micro-Nano Technology Co., Ltd.

Preparation of the PLMAC ink

A 10 wt% PVA solution was prepared by dissolving 5 g PVA in 45 mL deionized (DI) water at 95 °C under magnetic stirring. Next, 1.5 g EGaIn was transferred into a 5 mL sample vial using a syringe, followed by the addition of 1.5 g DI water and 0.5 g prepared PVA solution (the total mass of DI water and PVA solution was maintained at 2 g in all cases). The mixture was then subjected to ultrasonication at 150 w for 2 min using an ultrasonic processor, with the process carried out in an ice bath. Subsequently, 0.1 g 30 wt% CA solution and 0.01 g AgNO₃ solution were added, and the mixture was stirred for 5 min to yield the PLMAC ink.

Characterizations of the PLMAC

To analyze the surface morphology of LMPs, PLMAC ink was diluted with DI water at a volume ratio of 100:1 and deposited onto a steel grid. TEM images were acquired using a Talos F200X (FEI). SEM images of the PLMAC film and VIA were captured using a RISE-CLARA (TESCAN). The CRS of LMPs was evaluated by recording force-displacement curves using the AFM (Jupiter XR, Oxford Instruments Asylum Research). FTIR spectra were obtained using a Nicolet iS50R (Thermo Scientific) to identify cross-linking between PVA and CA. XPS (AXIS SUPRA + , Kratos Analytical, Shimadzu) was conducted to obtain C 1 s, O 1 s, Ga 3 d and Ag 3 d spectra. XRD analysis was performed using a Smart Lab 9 kW (Rigaku). The wettability of the LMP and PLMAC films was evaluated by measuring the contact angle with a contact angle goniometer (OCA35, Dataphysics). To assess ion release from the PLMAC film, the film was immersed in PBS solution for 24 h and 72 h, filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter, and the ion concentrations in the filtrate were determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, NexION 5000, PerkinElmer). Electromechanical testing was performed on PLMAC samples deposited on PDMS and VHB tape substrates using a tensile testing system (FlexTest-S-P2, Nanoupe). The electrical resistance of the PLMAC film during stretching were measured in real time using a benchtop digital multimeter (DMM6500, Keithley).

Fabrication of the stretchable electronic system and VIA array

A double-layer rectangular circuit board (50 mm × 35 mm) was designed using EasyEDA software. The top and bottom layer patterns were then exported to AutoCAD 2024 for subsequent fabrication processes. All electronic components, including the microcontroller, voltage regulator, capacitors, and resistors, were purchased from LCSC (Lichuang Mall). System control was implemented using a microcontroller unit (STM32G030F6P6TR, STMicroelectronics), while power management was provided by a voltage regulator chip (HX9193-33GB-N, HX). For the fabrication of stretchable circuit boards, PDMS (Sylgard 184, 10:1 w/w) was first spin-coated onto a clean, dry glass plate and subsequently cured in an oven at 90 °C for 60 min. After peeling off the PDMS film, adhesive tapes were affixed to both sides of the PDMS substrate. The top-layer circuit patterns and through-holes were defined by a UV laser cutter on the adhesive tape. The PDMS substrate was then flipped along its neutral axis, and the bottom-layer circuit patterns were patterned in the same manner. Following oxygen plasma treatment of both surfaces, PLMAC ink was spray-coated using an airbrush. The pattern was rapidly developed by evaporating the solvent on a hot plate at 90 °C, forming the PLMAC circuit traces. Commercial rigid electronic components were then placed onto the patterned substrate using tweezers. Hard PDMS (Sylgard 184, 4:1 w/w) was applied around these components to secure them and establish strain isolation regions. The same protocol was applied for the fabrication of the VIA array.

Finite element analysis

To investigate interfacial delamination phenomena in solid and hollow through-holes, FEA was conducted using Abaqus software (Abaqus/CAE 2024.HF1, Dassault Systèmes, France) to simulate the normal contact forces between the hole walls and the conductive materials. Three-dimensional models of both types of through-holes (each with a diameter and height of 200 μm) were constructed, with the hollow through-hole modeled as having a 20 μm-thick film on the inner wall. The mechanical behavior of the circuit board incorporating an additional flexible strain isolation structure was also simulated using Abaqus.

Circuit characterizations

To evaluate the signal quality of the output from the stretchable electronic system, the device was mounted on a tensile testing system, with the signal output interface connected to an oscilloscope (TDS 2004C, Tektronix) and the programming interface connected to a laptop. The code, written in Keil, was used to control the duty cycle of the output PWM waveform. To assess the signal acquisition and communication performance of the stretchable electronic system, the system was mounted on a tensile tester, with the signal acquisition interface connected to a function generator (AFG1062, Tektronix) and both the programming and serial interfaces connected to a laptop. To evaluate the effect of dynamic deformation, the system was subjected to cyclic tensile strains of 50% at a uniaxial stretching speed of 5 mm/s while maintaining normal functional operation.

Fabrication of PLMAC epidermal electrode and PDMS-based electrode

A mask was prepared by patterning adhesive tape using a UV laser cutter. Prior to electrode fabrication, skin oil was removed with an abrasive gel (Nuprep, Tenocom), followed by cleaning with anhydrous ethanol. The mask was then applied to the skin, and PLMAC ink was spray-coated onto the masked area. 3 min later, the mask was removed to yield the PLMAC electrode. For PDMS-based electrodes, the mask was applied onto the PDMS substrate, which was subsequently treated with oxygen plasma. PLMAC ink was then spray-coated onto the masked area to form the PDMS-based electrode.

In vitro cytotoxicity assays

The cytotoxicity of PLMAC was evaluated using fibroblasts (iCell Bioscience Inc, RRID: CVCL_C9G0) and the CCK-8 assay. Fibroblasts were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 6 × 103 cells per well. To each well, 100 μL of PLMAC ink (prepared by 10-fold dilution and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane) was added. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ for 24, 72, and 120 h. After incubation, the medium was removed and each well was washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, 100 μL of culture medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the cells were further incubated for 2 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (SPARK 10 M, Tecan, Switzerland). Relative cell viability was calculated as follows: Relative viability (%) = (OD sample–OD background)/(mean OD control–OD background) × 100%, where OD background refers to the absorbance of wells containing only CCK-8 reagent and culture medium. Fibroblasts used were destroyed during or after the analysis. SPSS software (version 25.0, SPSS Inc.) was utilized for analyzing statistics by using two-sided Student’s t-test between two groups and ANOVA with Least Significant Difference (LSD) post hoc test between multiple groups.

Antibacterial activity assessment

Three 12 mL bacterial culture tubes were each filled with 3 mL Luria-Bertani (LB) liquid medium. Single colonies of MRSA and E. coli were picked from solid media and inoculated into two of the tubes, with the third tube serving as a blank control. The tubes were incubated overnight (15 h) in a shaking incubator at 37 °C and 200 rpm. The resulting bacterial suspensions were diluted with sterile PBS to a concentration of 106 CFU/mL. For each test, 2 mL of the diluted bacterial suspension was added to a sterilized sample tube, while the control group received no sample. All tubes were incubated statically at 37 °C for 6 h. After incubation, the bacterial suspensions were serially diluted 10-fold with sterile PBS, and 100 μL of each dilution was spread evenly onto LB agar plates. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 18 h, after which colony images were captured and the number of colonies was recorded.

Animal experiments

All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No.: IACUC 2023-3868). Three male Japanese white rabbits (10 weeks old, 3.5 kg) were purchased from Wuhan Wanqian Jiaxing Co., Ltd. Since the phenomena measured represent natural physiological responses, sex was not a relevant factor and therefore was not considered. Prior to surgery, anesthesia was induced by intravenous injection of sodium pentobarbital. The rabbit’s head was fixed in a standard stereotaxic frame. Hair removal cream (VEET) was applied to the cranial area for 10 min, after which the hair was removed and the skin sterilized with anhydrous ethanol. A patterned adhesive mask was placed on the cranial region, and PLMAC ink was spray-coated to form PLMAC electrodes. For SSEP monitoring, an electrical stimulator (Natus UltraPro S100 EMG/NCS/EP Neurodiagnostic System) was used to stimulate the left/right median nerve of the forelimb and the posterior tibial nerve of the hindlimb via needle electrodes, with SSEP signals collected using the PLMAC electrodes. For MEP monitoring, the stimulator delivered stimulation to the motor scalp via PLMAC electrodes, and MEP signals were collected from the forelimb using needle electrodes. Recorded evoked potentials were digitally band-pass filtered between 30-300 Hz and averaged over 500 trials. The data were analyzed by Viking V20.1, MATLAB (Version R2023b) and OriginPro 2022 (Version 9.9.0.225).

Human subject experiments

All human skin experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No.: IEC 2024-0898-01), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. The research process was harmless to humans. Since the phenomena measured represent natural physiological responses, sex was not a relevant factor and therefore was not considered. The volunteer was a 27-year-old male (170 cm, 71 kg). For the control group, cup electrodes (Greentek) and conductive paste (Ten20, Tenocom) were used.

Statistics and reproducibility

The experiments corresponding to Figs. 1d, e, 3b, and 5m were repeated independently at three times with similar results.
