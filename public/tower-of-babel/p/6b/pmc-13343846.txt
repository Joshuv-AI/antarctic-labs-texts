Photoinduced reduction of nitro(hetero)arenes with earth-abundant FeCl3 promoter
Authors: Lirong Yao, Yue Chen, Tianshu Qin, Hao Wu, Yihong Wang, Chiyi Liu, Rongxiao Ni, Aiqin Liu, Zhushuang Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The reduction of nitroaromatic compounds to aromatic amines is a crucial step in the production of pharmaceuticals, dyes, and other materials. In this study, we used the earth-abundant iron salt FeCl3 as a promoter to efficiently reduce various (hetero)aryl nitro compounds under visible light irradiation at 395 nm and room temperature. This method, with its gentle reaction conditions and wide substrate range, effectively avoids reliance on precious metals and harsh conditions common in traditional methods. Its versatility is demonstrated by its ability to reduce a broad spectrum of nitro compounds, making it a valuable tool for chemists and researchers across multiple fields. Mechanistic studies have confirmed that chlorine radicals formed through photoexcitation are the key active species responsible for reducing nitro groups to amino groups.

Introduction

Aromatic amines are important organic chemical raw materials,1 widely applied in various fields such as the rubber industry, dyes, pharmaceuticals, and pesticides1 (Fig. 1). Industrially, aniline derivatives are commonly synthesized via the reduction of nitroaromatic hydrocarbons,2 with mainstream protocols including metal reduction,3 sulfide reduction,4 transfer hydrogenation,5–7 and catalytic hydrogenation8–13 (Scheme 1). Metal reduction and sulfide reduction rely on stoichiometric reducing agents (e.g., sulfides or metallic reductants such as iron, tin, zinc, etc.). However, these approaches generate massive volumes of wastewater and exhaust gas, thereby triggering serious environmental concerns. Transfer hydrogenation usually utilizes hydrazine hydrate,14 sodium borohydride,15 alcohols,16 formic acid,17–19 and carbon monoxide/water20 as hydrogen donors for the reduction of nitro compounds.20–26 Catalytic hydrogenation, the most prevalent industrial method, encompasses both precious metal catalysts27–31 and non-noble metal catalyst systems,32–35 both of these two categories of methods suffer from poor chemoselectivity and tend to damage vulnerable functional groups in the target molecules, such as halogen atoms, carbon–nitrogen double/triple bonds, and carbonyl groups with hydrogen gas as the reducing agent. Moreover, the transportation and storage of hydrogen sources, along with the high-pressure reaction conditions, give rise to considerable safety risks. Nevertheless, In recent years, biocatalytic,36,37 electrocatalytic,38–50 and photocatalytic protocols51–58 for the reduction of nitro compounds have been continuously developed. Photocatalysis has attracted extensive attention from both academia and industry due to its green and safe characteristics. Conventional photocatalytic55–68 reactions usually require costly and scarce photosensitizers to accomplish the reduction of nitro groups, which limits their large-scale industria applications. To address the inherent limitations of traditional photocatalytic systems, researchers have been continuously exploring more economical and efficient catalytic strategies.63–71 In 2025, the research group led by Bert U. W. Maes72 reported a visible-light/copper co-catalyzed method using isopropanol as the hydrogen source for the selective reduction of nitroaromatic hydrocarbons to arylamines (Scheme 1). In this work, we present a more environmentally benign, sustainable, and cost-effective strategy for the photoinduced reduction of nitro groups to amino groups, using water as the hydrogen source with trimethylsilyl chloride (TMSCl) and ferric chloride (FeCl3) as reaction promoters.

Results and discussion

We performed condition screening using commercially available 2,4-dichloro-5-nitro-trifluoromethylbenzene as the model substrate. Using a solvent mixture of acetonitrile and water (5 : 1, v/v) under 395 nm irradiation and 0.8 equivalents, of FeCl3(theoretically requiring 6.0 equivalents of iron) as the promoter for 18 hours the target product, 2,4-dichloro-5-amino-trifluoromethylbenzene, was obtained with only a 23% yield (Table 1, Entry 1). To further improve the yield, we first studied the effect of acidic additives. Equimolar amounts of HCl, H2SO4, formic acid, acetic acid, oxalyl chloride and TMSCl (Table 1, Entries 2–5) and the results showed that all acidic additives increased the reaction yield, with oxalyl chloride reaching 45% and TMSCl giving the highest at 67%. Therefore, TMSCl was identified as the best additive. Next, we systematically examined the effect of irradiation wavelength (Table 1, Entries 6–9). Under screened wavelengths conditions, the product yields were all lower than the 67% obtained at 395 nm, confirming 395 nm as the optimal wavelength. During solvent screening (Table 1, Entries 10–13), DMF and acetonitrile gave similar yields. Considering the issues of solvent recovery and environmental friendliness for large-scale applications, we selected acetonitrile as the solvent. Based on the literature reports, we supposed hypothesized that trace water in acetonitrile exerts an influence on the reaction, we tested different acetonitrile–water volume ratios (1 : 1, 2 : 1, 10 : 1). When the ratio was v(MeCN) : v(H2O) = 10 : 1, the yield increased to 74%. Further reducing water content to v(MeCN) : v(H2O) = 200 : 1 significantly enhanced the yield to 93%. To evaluate the necessary of Fe3+ and Cl− respectively,we tested other Iron salts and chlorine-containing transition metal salts (Table 1, Entry 14–17). None of these gave satisfactory results. It is worth noting that when Fe2(SO4)3 and NaCl were used as promoters (Table 1, Entry 18), only a low yield was obtained, which showed that reaction efficiency heavily depends on FeCl3. Ultimately, the best ratio was 0.8 equivalents of FeCl3 and 1.0 equivalent of TMSCl.

After successfully constructing the FeCl3-promoted, light-induced nitro reduction system, we systematically investigated the scope of this reaction (Scheme 2). Nitrobenzene was smoothly converted to aniline with a yield of 46% (1a). Notably, the introduction of electron-withdrawing groups onto the aromatic ring significantly enhanced the reaction efficiency. Among monosubstituted nitrobenzenes, electron-withdrawing groups such as bromine and trifluoromethyl (−CF3) exhibited excellent compatibility (1b–1d, up to 67%). When strong electron-withdrawing groups, such as cyano (−CN), were introduced, the reaction activity was further improved, achieving yields of up to 77%. As the number of electron-withdrawing substituents on the aromatic ring increased, the reaction efficiency showed a marked upward trend: dichloro and dibromo-substituted substrates afforded yields ranging from 58% to 72% (1f–h). Substrates containing strong electron-withdrawing groups. Substrates containing strong electron-withdrawing groups, such as cyano and trifluoromethyl (1i–1k), also worked in the reaction, producing the reduced products in yields from 52% 75%. For example, 4-chloro-3-nitrobenzophenone (1l), based on a benzophenone scaffold, achieved a 62% yield in the conversion from nitro to amino groups. Even in multiply substituted nitro substrates bearing multiple strong electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., halogens, cyano, trifluoromethyl), the target amine products were still obtained with high yields (1m–1u). Remarkably, the highly chlorinated pentachloronitrobenzene (1u) achieved a conversion rate of 97%. In heterocyclic systems, nitropyridine substrates demonstrated exceptional reaction performance. Monohalo-substituted nitropyridines yielded the target products with yields of 53–65% (1v–1z), while polyhalo-substituted nitropyridines exhibited further improved yields of 75–88% (1aa–1ag). Notably, nitropyridines bearing electron-donating methyl groups maintained the structural integrity of the substituents while still achieving good yields (1ai, 1aj). This system was also applicable to nitropyrimidine compounds, with mono-, di-, and trihalo-substituted pyrimidine derivatives achieving conversions with yields of 80–95% (1ak–1an). Methyl-substituted pyrimidine substrates remained unaffected under the reaction conditions (1ao, 1ap), maintaining good yields. Meanwhile, substrates containing both halogen and ester groups (1aq) achieved a conversion rate of up to 97%. Furthermore, nitropyrazine substrates also participated smoothly in the reaction, yielding the corresponding amine products with good yields (1ar, 1as). Finally, we further explored the reactivity of dinitro compounds. Halogen-substituted dinitrobenzene substrates 1at, 1au and 1av underwent selective mononitro reduction smoothly, affording the target products in yields of 68%, 55% and 60%. These results demonstrate that the FeCl3-promoted, light-induced reduction system exhibits excellent functional group tolerance and broad substrate applicability, providing a versatile and reliable method for the selective reduction of a wide range of nitro(hetero)arenes, from simple to complex substrates.

To study the mechanism of photoinduced synergistic promotion by iron(iii) chloride and TMSCl in nitro reduction, we conducted a series of validation experiments. Firstly, the radical characteristic of the reaction was confirmed through radical trapping experiments (Scheme 3). When radical scavengers such as TEMPO or DPE were introduced into the system, the reaction was significantly inhibited, and no target reduction product was detected. Further mass spectrometry analysis revealed the presence of adducts like DPE-Cl, indicating the generation and participation of chlorine radicals in the reaction, thereby proving that the reaction proceeds via a radical pathway. Secondly, we investigated the mechanism of action of TMSCl. GC-MS analysis revealed the formation of hexamethyldisiloxane in the reaction mixture, indicating that TMSCl not only serves as a chlorine source to provide chlorine radical but also rapidly reacts with water to generate HCl, which supplies hydrogen and chlorine species and promotes the forward progression of the reaction. Finally, the crucial role of light was verified through light-dependent experiments. In on–off light control experiments (Fig. 3), the reaction completely failed to proceed under dark conditions, whereas it proceeded smoothly under irradiation, further confirming that the reaction is a light-driven radical process. In summary, the experimental results indicate that this reaction is a light-promoted radical process, in which TMSCl serves as a dehydrating agent, and light is a necessary prerequisite for the reaction to occur.

Two potential pathways for nitro reduction have been documented in the literature (Scheme 4).To investigate the detailed pathway of FeCl3-promoted photoinduced nitro reduction, this study performed a series of control experiments with potential intermediates(Scheme 5), including nitrobenzene (I), nitrosobenzene (III), N-phenylhydroxylamine (IV), azobenzene (VI) and hydrazobenzene (VII), to examine their conversion products and yields under standard reaction conditions with and without light irradiation, while systematically recording the residual amounts of starting materials and the formation ratios of the target product aniline (V) and byproducts azoxybenzene (VIII) and benzidine (IX). Experimental results showed that nitrobenzene (I) gave aniline (V) in 46% yield with no azoxybenzene (VIII) observed under standard light irradiation, whereas no aniline (V) was formed and 95% of nitrobenzene (I) remained in the dark; nitrosobenzene (III) afforded aniline (V) in 65% yield and azoxybenzene (VIII) in 35% yield under illumination, but only azoxybenzene (VIII) in 26% yield without aniline (V) in the absence of light; N-phenylhydroxylamine (IV) produced aniline (V) in 70% yield and azoxybenzene (VIII) in 25% yield under light, while giving azoxybenzene (VIII) in 65% yield with no aniline (V) formed in the dark; azobenzene (VI) generated only benzidine (IX) in 56% yield without aniline (V) under light irradiation; hydrazobenzene (VII) furnished aniline (V) in 15% yield and benzidine (IX) in 45% yield under light, but only benzidine (IX) in 70% yield with no aniline (V) in the dark; under optimized light conditions, the yield of aniline (V) from nitrobenzene (I) increased to 75% with no azoxybenzene (VIII) detected, whereas no aniline (V) was formed and azoxybenzene (VIII) was obtained in 99% yield without light. On the basis of these results, the dominant pathway for the reduction of nitrobenzene to aniline in this system was established as a light-dependent stepwise process: nitrobenzene (I) → nitrosobenzene (III) → N-phenylhydroxylamine (IV) → aniline (V), confirming that light irradiation is indispensable for the reaction and that the reduction process ceases in the dark.

Conclusions

This study has successfully developed a green and efficient method for reducing nitroarenes. Utilizing earth-abundant FeCl3 as the promoter under 395 nm visible light irradiation and mild conditions, a wide range of (hetero)aromatic nitro compounds were selectively reduced to the corresponding aryl amines. The method features broad substrate applicability, particularly for complex molecules containing electron-withdrawing groups and halogen substituents, and demonstrates excellent functional group tolerance. Mechanistic studies reveal that the reaction is initiated by chlorine radicals generated via photoexcitation of FeCl3, proceeding through a pathway involving (I → III → IV → V) (Pathway 1). This strategy presents a novel approach to the green synthesis of aryl amines, with significant potential applications.
