Emphysematous cholecystitis in a young male without predisposing factors: case report
Authors: Bedilu Zewdu Asmare, Mulualem Amare Woldemichael, Ibrahim Hussen Kedir, Alemnew Maru, Getachew Sahile Alemu, Dereje Assefa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Emphysematous cholecystitis (EC) is a rare, life-threatening form of acute cholecystitis, usually occurring in older patients with a history of diabetes mellitus and other comorbidities.Case presentation:We present a 36-year-old male patient with no predisposing factors who presented with right upper quadrant pain, fever, nausea, vomiting, and loss of appetite, which was clinically indistinguishable from acute non-EC. Multimodality imaging revealed gas within the lumen and wall of the gallbladder, confirming the diagnosis.Clinical discussion:This case is unusual due to the absence of advanced age and diabetes mellitus, highlighting that EC can occur outside the classic risk profile. The patient was treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics, and an open cholecystectomy was performed.Conclusion:Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for EC, even in younger patients without predisposing factors, presenting with cholecystitis symptoms. Timely initiation of broad-spectrum antibiotics and prompt surgical management remain essential for reducing morbidity and mortality.

Introduction

Emphysematous cholecystitis is a rare necrotizing infection of the gallbladder caused by gas-forming organisms[1,2]. It carries high morbidity and mortality rates, 50% and 25%, respectively[3,4]. The condition typically affects patients aged 50–70 years and is strongly associated with diabetes mellitus, vascular diseases, and immunosuppression[5,6]. Clinically, emphysematous cholecystitis often mimics non-emphysematous acute cholecystitis, presenting with right upper quadrant pain, fever, nausea, and vomiting. Because the symptoms are nonspecific, imaging plays a vital role in diagnosis. Ultrasound may demonstrate intraluminal gas with a reverberation artifact, while plain abdominal radiographs typically show gas outlining the gallbladder and air-fluid levels. Computed tomography (CT) remains the most sensitive and specific imaging modality, clearly identifying gas within the lumen and wall of the gallbladder. While typically seen in older patients with comorbidities, its occurrence in young, healthy patients is rare.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old male patient presented with right upper quadrant pain of 2 days’ duration. The pain had progressively worsened over the past 24 hours and was associated with nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, and high-grade fever. The patient had no history of diabetes mellitus, peripheral vascular disease, immunosuppression (including HIV), prior gallbladder disease, malignancy, or systemic disease.

On arrival, vital signs were: BP 120/70 mmHg, pulse 120 bpm, and temperature 38.3°C. Physical examination revealed right upper quadrant tenderness and a positive ultrasound Murphy's sign; however, no rebound tenderness, guarding, or jaundice was noted. Laboratory evaluation revealed marked leukocytosis of 29 000 with 85% neutrophil predominance. Liver function tests were normal (AST 26 IU/L, ALT 20 IU/L, ALP 119 IU/L). Renal function tests were normal; creatinine was 1.05 mg/dL. Total bilirubin was 1.47 mg/dL, and direct bilirubin was 0.45 mg/dL. A random blood glucose level was 100 mg/dL, and HIV serology was negative. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated a distended gallbladder containing multiple echogenic foci with positive reverberation artifact, suggestive of intraluminal gas, along with a positive sonographic Murphy's sign (Fig. 1). Based on these findings, emphysematous cholecystitis was suspected. A plain abdominal radiograph further showed air within the gallbladder fossa forming an air-fluid level (Fig. 2). A contrast-enhanced CT scan confirmed the diagnosis, revealing a thickened, enhancing gallbladder wall with gas collection in both the lumen and the wall (Fig. 3). The patient was admitted to the ward and started on intravenous ceftriaxone 1 g twice daily and metronidazole 500 mg three times daily. After 4 hours of stabilization, he underwent open cholecystectomy as laparoscopic interventions were unavailable. Intraoperative findings included a distended, gangrenous gallbladder with multiple areas of palpable crepitus of air in the gallbladder wall. Further dissection and inspection of the excised gallbladder showed 50 mL of pus content but no palpable stones (Supplemental Digital Content Figure S1, available at: http://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A88). Dense inflammatory adhesions were noted between the gallbladder and adjacent structures, including the omentum, colon, and duodenum. The intraoperative course was uncomplicated, with no significant anatomic variation or technical difficulties encountered intraperitoneally. Postoperatively, the patient continued intravenous ceftriaxone and metronidazole for 7 days. His vital signs stabilized, feeding was tolerated, and he was discharged on postoperative day 7. He was followed for 1 year and remained stable with no complications.

Case discussion

EC was first described by Stoltz in 1901[1,2]. It represents an acute necrotizing infection of the gallbladder wall caused by gas-forming microorganisms[1,3,4]. It is uncommon and accounts for fewer than 1% of all acute cholecystitis cases[5]. The inflammatory process causes edema, which initially compromises venous and lymphatic drainage. Progressive edema eventually leads to stenosis and occlusion of the arterial supply, resulting in ischemia and necrosis of the gallbladder wall[3].

The microorganisms most frequently implicated include Clostridium welchii (Clostridium perfringens), Escherichia coli, Klebsiella, and Streptococcus species[4,6]. Clostridium welchii and E. coli are considered the two most common causative organisms[8,9].

EC requires immediate surgical intervention[6]. Due to the rapid ischemic changes and necrosis, the risk of gangrene and gallbladder perforation is high, leading to significant morbidity and mortality[3,4,6]. It predominantly affects males, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 7:3, and generally occurs in patients between 50 and 70 years of age[1,5,10,11]. About 40% of patients have associated diabetes mellitus[2,6]. Compared with classic acute cholecystitis, where gallstones are present in nearly 90% of cases, EC shows a lower association, with gallstones found in roughly 40% of cases[10,11]. Additional predisposing factors include immunosuppression, peripheral vascular disease, previous surgery, and recent trauma[4,6].

Clinically, the symptoms of EC often mimic those of classic acute cholecystitis, including right upper quadrant pain, a positive Murphy’s sign, nausea, vomiting, and fever. However, EC typically presents with a more aggressive clinical course, characterized by rapid progression of symptoms, more severe abdominal pain, and a higher likelihood of sepsis[2–4,6,11].

Imaging is crucial for diagnosis, as the clinical presentation is nonspecific[2,3]. The hallmark radiological feature is the presence of gas within the gallbladder lumen, wall, or pericholecystic tissues, without any communication to the gastrointestinal tract[12]. While plain abdominal radiographs are not the primary diagnostic tool, they can reveal abnormal gas outlining the gallbladder wall or air–fluid levels in the lumen[6,10]. In our patient, we observed gas outlining the gallbladder wall along with air-fluid level formation.

Ultrasound is the first-line imaging modality for gallbladder pathology, boasting a diagnostic accuracy of approximately 77% for emphysematous cholecystitis[5]. On ultrasound, gas appears as echogenic foci that produce reverberation artifacts or broadband acoustic shadowing[6,11], which was also evident in our case. Multiple tiny echogenic bubbles rising within the bile have been described as the “champagne” or “effervescent” sign[5,11]. Differentiating EC from a porcelain gallbladder or a gallbladder filled with multiple stones (wall-echo-shadow complex) can be challenging[5,6].

CT is the most sensitive and specific modality for diagnosis[2,3,6,9]. It clearly illustrates gas in the gallbladder wall and lumen and detects pericholecystic inflammatory changes, edema, and abscess formation, while ruling out alternative diagnoses[1,2,4]. EC can be radiologically classified based on the distribution of gas: Grade 1 indicates gas confined to the gallbladder lumen, Grade 2 signifies intramural gas within the gallbladder wall, and Grade 3 indicates gas extension into the pericholecystic tissues or adjacent structures[10]. In our patient, CT imaging revealed gas in both the lumen and the gallbladder wall, consistent with Grade 2 EC.

Following diagnosis, intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics and surgical management should be initiated promptly[4,5]. Cholecystectomy remains the definitive treatment for EC[1,9]. Although laparoscopic cholecystectomy has been safely performed in selected patients[4], it can be challenging because of a high conversion rate to open surgery[10,13]. For critically ill patients who are not surgical candidates, temporary percutaneous cholecystostomy may be considered[5,12].

This case report has several limitations. Bacteriological culture and histopathological examination were not performed because these services were unavailable in our resource-limited setting, limiting the microbiological and pathological characterization of the disease. In addition, laparoscopic management was not available, and the patient underwent open cholecystectomy from the outset. Although a few similar cases have been reported in young patients without traditional risk factors, this condition remains relatively uncommon in this age group.

Conclusion

Emphysematous cholecystitis can occur even in otherwise healthy individuals. Clinicians should maintain a high level of suspicion when evaluating rapidly progressive right upper quadrant pain. Early imaging and urgent surgical intervention are crucial for preventing morbidity and mortality.
