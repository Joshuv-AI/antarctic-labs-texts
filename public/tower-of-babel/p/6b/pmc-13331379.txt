Optimizing Lung Transplant Logistics Using Controlled Hypothermic Preservation to Extend Ischemic Time
Authors: Mena M. Botros, Alhusain Alsaghayer, Christopher Tanabe, Karla McElroy, Ahmad Goodarzi, Simon K. Yau, J. Georges Youssef, George Naufal, Dewei Ren, Ray Chihara, Erik E. Suarez, Yuncan A. He, Howard J. Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background.Effective organ preservation before transplantation is pivotal for successful outcomes but can be limited by logistics. Organs are primarily preserved by flushing with a cold solution and storing on ice, then transplanted within 8 h of procurement. Controlled hypothermic preservation (CHP) at 4–8 °C has increasingly been used to extend ischemic times. Here we compared early outcomes of transplants using CHP (n = 41) and traditional preservation on ice (n = 41).Methods.Our institution used LUNGguard, a CHP device by Paragonix, for the extension of ischemic time to optimize operating room (OR), transplant team, and recipient logistics. We evaluated 1-y mortality, primary graft dysfunction, length of stay, time to liberation from mechanical ventilation, and time on oxygen support.Results.There were no differences in patient outcomes between storage on ice and CHP. In selected cases, extending ischemic times using CHP allowed for additional organ procurement as well as improved OR logistics.Conclusions.In our experience, CHP has been shown to be a safe and effective method of extending ischemic times in donor lungs, allowing for increased donor acceptance and improved OR efficiency. This supports a growing body of evidence favoring a shift from ice to CHP as the standard for donor lung storage in cases of extended ischemic time.

INTRODUCTION

Presently most donor lungs are preserved by flushing with a cold solution, storing on ice, and transplanting within 8 h.1-5 With the introduction of the continuous allocation system and subsequent removal of geographic barriers, increasingly longer ischemic times are being observed. The use of extended preservation times has helped overcome geographic constraints and increase donor utilization. Despite this, the need to minimize ischemic time limits donor availability for any given recipient.6 Traditionally, lung transplantation is performed at any time throughout the day, with many procedures occurring overnight to minimize organ ischemic time. Overnight transplantation has been associated with decreased long-term survival, a higher risk of postoperative pneumonia, and higher center costs.7 The extension of ischemic time can also allow for semi-elective transplantation to avoid overnight transplants with the goal of improving operative scheduling, decreasing center costs, and, most importantly, improving outcomes.

Prior studies had suggested 10 °C as an optimal temperature for static lung preservation.8,9 Interest in controlled hypothermic preservation (CHP) at temperatures above the 4 °C range was renewed after additional research identified improved mitochondrial health and lower concentrations of inflammatory markers in porcine lungs preserved at 10 °C.10 In that same report, they described a pilot study with donor lungs preserved using CHP and demonstrated the safety of prolonged ischemic times when donor lungs are stored at 10 °C before transplant. Since then, further extension of ischemic times has been reported using both on-site controlled temperature incubators and portable CHP devices.11-13 A multicenter study looking at storage in on-site incubators with ischemic times reaching beyond 18 h showed primary graft dysfunction (PGD) rates similar to reported benchmarks.14 A separate study on prolonging ischemic times in donor lungs preserved with portable CHP devices saw similar rates of grade 3 PGD and 1-y survival with ischemic times >15 h.15 Ongoing studies continue to show promising outcomes for lungs preserved using CHP.16-19 While the evidence on graft outcomes and cold static preservation is rapidly evolving, important questions remain, including the ideal temperature range and its full impact on near- and long-term graft function and clinical outcomes.20,21,22

We hypothesized that using CHP for the safe extension of ischemic time would optimize operating room (OR) and transplant team logistics. Transplants were shifted to morning to improve operative staffing, decrease operative costs, and improve recipient outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This is a single-center retrospective, nonindustry funded review of 82 adult single and bilateral lung recipients transplanted between April 2022 and May 2023 at Houston Methodist Hospital. The study was conducted under institutional review board protocol PRO00023549. The need for written consent was waived by the institutional review board, so no written consent was obtained. Patients were assigned to CHP versus ice preservation based primarily on 2 main logistical factors: donor distance and OR availability at the time of organ procurement. Recipients of donor lungs transported on ice were referred to as the ICE group. Donor lungs from the CHP group were transported using the Paragonix LUNGguard. The LUNGguard system maintains donor lungs at a temperature between 4 °C and 8 °C. As shown in Table 1, baseline characteristics between the 2 groups are statistically similar across demographic, clinical, and donor variables. Given the nature of the allocation mechanism and the balance in observed covariates, we did not expect selection to correlate with unobserved patient factors or underlying clinical conditions that might confound the estimated treatment effects. Multiorgan and retransplant recipients were excluded. Procurement and postoperative care followed the standard of care at Houston Methodist Hospital. The operative strategy involved thoracotomy with or without sternal transection depending on the approach for single or double lung transplant. The primary outcome was 1-y survival. Secondary outcomes included PGD at 24, 48, and 72 h, total length of stay (LOS), intensive care unit LOS, time to extubation, and time to room air.23

RESULTS

Donor Pao2/Fio2 ratios for the CHP group were lower than those of the ICE group, with an average of 416.65 versus 479.08 (P = 0.003). There were also more donors of unknown race in the CHP group; otherwise, donor and recipient characteristics were well matched (Table 1).

As expected, the CHP cohort had significantly longer ischemic times when compared with ice preservation, with an average total ischemic time of 453.4 min (SD, 139.1 min) versus 306.2 min (SD, 72.7 min; P = 0.00; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.243-0.505). The range of ischemic times for our CHP cohort was 182–775 min, compared with 175–589 min in the ICE cohort.

PGD rates (grades 1, 2, and 3) were comparable between CHP and ICE groups at 24 h (95% CI, –1.865 to 0.116; P = 0.083), 48 h (95% CI, –1.116 to 0.808; P = 0.754), and 72 h (95% CI, –1.028 to 0.793; P = 0.801). At 72 h, the incidence of PGD of any grade in the CHP group was 17, whereas the incidence in the ICE group was 20. Four patients in the CHP group had PGD grade 3 at 72 h compared with 1 patient in the ICE group; however, there was no significant difference between the 2 groups (95% CI, –1.317 to 2.287; P = 0.598; Figure 1). Figure 1 reflects the absolute value for each category. Although the results were not significant, we had a higher incidence of any grade of PGD in the ICE group. There were 37 patients (90.2%) in the CHP group and 38 patients (92.6%) in the ICE group who survived to 1 y. No statistically significant difference in 1-y survival was found between the 2 groups (95% CI, –7.082 to 2.064; P = 0.282). Moreover, there was no difference in intensive care unit LOS or total index LOS (95% CI, –0.260 to 0.391; P = 0.690) and (–0.169 to 0.266; P = 0.659), respectively. Additionally, time to extubation (95% CI, –3.653 to 1.391; P = 0.374) and time until supplemental oxygen was weaned (95% CI, –0.238 to 0.649; P = 0.359) were not significantly different (Figure 1).

Additionally, we did not observe any association between donor Pao2/Fio2 ratio and either grade 3 PGD (P = 0.520) or 1-y survival (P = 0.358). CIs were (–0.124 to 0.006) and (–0.019 to 0.007), respectively.

DISCUSSION

In this nonindustry funded single-center retrospective cohort study, we found no significant difference in key early posttransplant outcomes between CHP and ice preservation, despite significantly longer total ischemic times in the CHP cases. Importantly, there were no differences in early postoperative mortality, PGD, LOS, time to extubation, or weaning to ambient air. This study represents our initial experience with CHP starting in April 2022. At the time, published data on extending ischemic times were limited, so we were cautious in pushing toward longer ischemic times. While the average ischemic time of our CHP group was 450 min, 19 of 41 patients had ischemic times >8 h. We did not extend up to 24 h, with our longest ischemic time lasting 775 min, given the acuity of illness of our recipients and donors. Our initial experience aligns with that seen at other centers and suggests CHP can be safely used to extend ischemic time and facilitate transplant logistics. Given our results and the current published literature, donor lung preservation with extended ischemic times via CHP is slowly becoming the standard of care.

Additionally, extending ischemic time with CHP (time-shifting) resulted in significant improvements in logistics and resource utilization at our center. Before April 2022, approximately 50% of lung transplant cases occurred outside of OR prime time, defined as 07:00–15:00. Despite an increase in volume, lung transplants occurring outside of prime time decreased to 29% after we began using CHP. This practice allowed more transplants to occur when the greatest number of resources were available and significantly reduced call team utilization and cost.

This study is limited by its retrospective design, relatively small cohort size, and single-center experience. A growing body of evidence suggests CHP is a safe and effective means of extending ischemic time. In our experience, with increased organ acceptance, improved OR efficiency, decreased operational expenditures, and comparable patient safety, CHP at 4–8 °C has been demonstrated to be an effective instrument in shifting transplantation to the morning hours.
