Overcoming Space and System Limitations: A Novel Lamina Spreader Technique for Optimal Reduction via External Fixation
Authors: Jourdan H Meltzer, Carol Lin
Published: 2026

Introduction

Restoring length and alignment during provisional external fixation of complex distal tibia fractures is a persistent technical challenge. Centralizing the talus beneath the tibial plafond and restoring fibular length are critical to long-term outcomes, 1 yet achieving precise multiplanar control within standard fixation systems remains difficult. 2 Manual traction facilitates gross reduction but offers limited coronal and sagittal control and requires a dedicated assistant. Compressor-distractors provide incremental adjustment but are not universally available, are poorly suited to the hindfoot because of space constraints, and cannot directly manipulate fixation pins. Proprietary compressor devices, which clamp to the carbon rod and distract against a fixed point via a threaded bolt, offer controlled distraction with no skin risk from instrument slippage but are limited to their specific fixation system and do not function in small spaces nor can they work directly on a pin.

A high-quality provisional reduction is not merely a temporizing measure: it protects the soft tissue envelope during the staged interval and substantially facilitates articular reduction at the time of definitive fixation by preserving length, alignment, and ligamentotaxis. We present a technique using standard external fixation components and a lamina spreader to achieve controlled, reproducible multiplanar reduction of ankle-spanning constructs. Applicable to any Schanz pin–based system, this approach has facilitated fine reduction control across pilon, ankle, and calcaneal fractures in our practice where standard distraction tools were unavailable or infeasible. The use of lamina spreaders to assist with reduction in ankle-spanning external fixation has been briefly described previously. 3 The present article provides a detailed, step-by-step description of a systematic multiplanar correction sequence with an accompanying Pearls and Pitfalls framework.

Indications and Contraindications

This technique is indicated for intra- and extra-articular distal tibia fractures with soft tissue compromise necessitating staged fixation, particularly when a compressor-distractor is unavailable, system-incompatible, or spatially infeasible. Relative contraindications include calcaneal tuberosity fractures precluding safe transcalcaneal pin placement, presence of hardware interfering with pin placement, or soft tissue compromise precluding pin placement in the lower limb.

Technique

The patient is positioned supine with the c-arm from the contralateral side. Standard radiographs—AP/lateral/mortise of ankle and AP/lateral of tibia—are obtained preoperatively. Preoperative CT is not required and may be more informative obtained after external fixation. Sterile prep is carried proximal to the knee.

Step 1: Initial Pin Placement

A proximal tibial 5-mm bicortical pin is placed ≥8 cm from the tubercle, anterior to posterior 5 mm medial to the crest. A medial to lateral transcalcaneal pin is placed through the posteroinferior tuberosity under fluoroscopy.

Step 2: Delta Frame

The tibial and transcalcaneal pins are connected medially and laterally with 2 bars and 4 pin-to-bar clamps. In universal clamp systems, orient the clamp body toward the fracture to maintain a pushing vector against the pin even when loosened.

Step 3: Gross Length

Longitudinal traction through transcalcaneal pin, applied slowly, restores gross length while avoiding permanent pin deformation. Rotational alignment of the foot relative to the limb axis is concurrently corrected.

Step 4: Coronal Correction

All delta frame clamps are tightened. Provisional clamps placed just proximal to the calcaneal pin-to-bar clamps serve as fixed backstops. A lamina spreader is placed between the provisional and permanent clamp to preferentially lengthen one side or the other (Figure 1, A-B): lateral for valgus, medial for varus. To create length, the permanent clamp of the desired side (the target clamp) is loosened while the lamina spreader is opened.

(A) A provisional fixed universal clamp, connected only to the bar, is placed just proximal to the permanent pin-to-bar clamp securing the transcalcaneal pin, allowing a lamina spreader to be positioned between the 2 clamp interfaces. (B) AP fluoroscopy prior to coronal correction demonstrating valgus malalignment and loss of talar centralization. (C) With the permanent pin-to-bar clamp loosened to permit sliding and the provisional clamp fully tightened as a backstop, the lamina spreader applies controlled asymmetric distraction for coronal correction. The permanent clamp is oriented toward the fracture to maintain a pushing vector against the pin even when loosened. T-handle chucks applied to one or both ends of the transcalcaneal pin can improve surgeon comfort, keeping their hands out of fluoroscopic imaging, and allow for greater force generation. (D) AP fluoroscopy after lamina spreader–assisted coronal correction demonstrating restoration of coronal alignment and talar centralization. AP, anteroposterior.

Step 5: Deformity Assessment

Coronal alignment is assessed on the AP and mortise views. Residual sagittal deformity and talar translation are assessed on lateral view.

Step 6: A-Frame Conversion

A distal tibial anterior-to-posterior pin is placed, as close to the fracture site as definitive fixation will permit, via a horizontal bar bridging the medial and lateral tibia-calcaneal bars, converting the delta to an A-frame. This pin serves as a joystick for residual correction.

Step 7: Iterative Sagittal and Residual Coronal Correction

Distraction forces from the lamina spreader are combined with fixed clamp stabilization to achieve satisfactory alignment. Typically, the tibial shaft must be driven posteriorly to meet the talar dome as the orientation of the bars creates a posteriorly-directed vector with distraction. With bar-to-bar clamps locked and only the distal tibial pin-to-bar clamp loosened, a sponge-protected lamina spreader between the clamp and skin drives the tibial shaft posteriorly (Figure 2). A provisional pin clamp provides purchase if the skin-to-clamp space is too large for the lamina spreader (Figure 2B). For residual coronal correction, the lamina spreader can be positioned against the distal tibia pin-to-bar clamp and the respective medial or lateral bar-to-bar clamp (Figure 3). For valgus, a medial lamina spreader pushing against the medial permanent bar-to-bar clamp translates the tibia laterally. For residual varus, the opposite is done.

(B) Should there be too much space between the protected skin surface and the distal tibial pin-to-bar clamp to facilitate lamina spreader distraction, a provisional clamp may be added to the distal tibial pin. With this independent clamp as a backstop, the laminar spreader can be used for distraction to correct sagittal plane deformity. (C) Lateral fluoroscopy demonstrating residual sagittal plane deformity prior to lamina spreader distraction. Note that length has been restored at this stage through the coronal correction step (Figure 1); Figure 2 illustrates sagittal correction only. Step 8: Foot Stabilization

To maintain neutral dorsiflexion and prevent equinus contracture during the staged interval, a midfoot pin is placed and connected to the medial bar. We rely on a trans-cuneiform pin capturing 5 cortices (medial cuneiform ×2, middle cuneiform ×2, lateral cuneiform ×1); a first metatarsal pin is an acceptable alternative. All clamps are fully tightened, alignment confirmed fluoroscopically, and provisional clamps removed. The final external fixator construct is illustrated in Figure 4C.

(B) Postoperative AP, mortise, and lateral radiographs demonstrating restoration of fibular length, centralization of the talus beneath the tibial plafond, and neutral sagittal alignment. (C) Clinical photograph of the final external fixator construct demonstrating the completed A-frame configuration with midfoot pin connected to the medial bar maintaining neutral ankle dorsiflexion. AP, anteroposterior.

Pearls and Pitfalls

Clamp discipline is the single most critical detail: all nontarget clamps must be fully tightened before each spreader maneuver or prior correction is lost. Transcalcaneal traction must be slow and incremental to avoid permanent pin deformation, which restricts clamp travel. The lamina spreader must never contact unprotected skin; always interpose a sponge. Plan adequate distal bar length at frame construction to permit clamp travel during distraction. Frame bars can encroach on swollen or compromised soft tissue over the staged interval: interposing a blue towel between the bar and skin at the time of frame construction helps maintain safe distance and facilitates monitoring of soft tissue status.

Complications

Pin site infection is mitigated by careful drilling technique and routine pin care. Transcalcaneal pin deformation or breakage results from rapid excessive traction; slow incremental loading is essential. Iatrogenic soft tissue injury is prevented by sponge interposition at spreader-skin interfaces. Loss of reduction is preventable by maintaining strict clamp discipline. Neurovascular injury is avoided by fluoroscopic confirmation before each pin is fully seated.

Case Illustration

A 78-year-old male sustained an open Gustilo-Anderson type IIIA right pilon fracture after an 8-foot fall. Radiographs demonstrated comminuted, shortened tibial plafond and fibula fractures in valgus and recurvatum (Figure 4A). He underwent urgent irrigation and debridement with ankle-spanning external fixation. Sequential lamina spreader distraction corrected length, coronal alignment, and sagittal deformity. Postoperative radiographs confirmed restoration of fibular length, talar centralization, and neutral sagittal alignment (Figure 4B). Soft tissue integrity was maintained at the open medial wound, which was associated with an uncomplicated definitive fusion at 11 days. The technique has been applied across additional pilon, ankle, and calcaneal fractures in our practice, supporting its adoption as a routine component of our staged fixation protocol.

Discussion

This technique converts a static delta frame into an active reduction device using only standard clamps and a lamina spreader. The fixed provisional clamp provides directional, controlled force, and iterative improvements in alignment are preserved through careful clamp management for an optimal reduction. This system-independent approach is particularly valuable when proprietary compressor distraction tools are unavailable, system-incompatible, and unable to be accommodated in the space available, or when direct controlled manipulation of the Schanz pin is desired. This technique is intended to offer comparable fine control over the vector and magnitude of applied force with the added benefit of being capable of manipulating a single pin at a time.
