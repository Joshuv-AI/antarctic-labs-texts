Long‐Term Estimated Physical Activity Patterns From Youth to Middle Age and Left Ventricular Structure and Function: A 30‐Year Longitudinal Study
Authors: Shunyi Li, Jianpin Luo, Shen Zhao, Haobin Zhou, Rongyang Xi, Jun Chen, Shichao Li, Kaiwei Li, Lin Song, Xiaolin Duan, Minhui Xu, Dingli Xu, Qingchun Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe long‐term effects of physical activity (PA) variability and cumulative PA (CPA) from young adulthood on cardiac remodeling remain controversial, and it is unclear whether these effects resemble those observed in elite athletes.MethodsA total of 1465 participants with recorded PA spanning a 30‐year period were included in this study. PA levels were assessed at 9 follow‐up visits using the CARDIA (Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults) questionnaire. At year 30, left ventricular structure and function were evaluated using standardized echocardiography. CPA was calculated as the sum of average PA levels between consecutive visits multiplied by the corresponding intervisit intervals (in years). PA variability was quantified using average real variability (ARVPA). Additionally, participants were categorized into 4 groups: sustained low CPA and ARVPA group, low CPA and high ARVPA group, high CPA and low ARVPA group, and high CPA and high ARVPA group.ResultsThis study included 849 women (57.1%), 883 White participants (60.3%), and had a mean age of 55.3 years. Participants in the sustained high CPA group exhibited lower incidence of hypertension and diabetes. Multiple linear regression analyses showed that, compared with the sustained‐low‐CPA group, the sustained‐high‐CPA group and high‐CPA and high‐ARVPA group exhibited increased left ventricular end‐diastolic dimension index, and the high‐CPA and high‐ARVPA group revealed an increased left ventricular mass index and left atrial volume index.ConclusionsOur study suggests that high CPA, even when accompanied with high variability in PA levels, is associated with alterations in left ventricular structure in midlife.Keywords: cardiac remodeling, echocardiography, left ventricular myocardial function, left ventricular myocardial structure, physical activitySubject Categories: Heart Failure, Remodeling, Exercise

Physical activity (PA) is recognized for its capacity to induce alterations in cardiac structure and function. 1 Previous studies have predominantly examined the role of structured exercise on myocardial structure and function. Increased left ventricular (LV) wall thickness is observed in elite adolescent athletes relative to their nonathletic peers. 2 Moreover, long‐term endurance training in healthy young adults has been shown to lead to an augmentation in LV mass and volume. 3 These changes, collectively termed exercise‐induced cardiac remodeling, are primarily characterized by an enlargement of ventricular mass and volume, and potentially accompanied by alterations in systolic and diastolic function. 4 , 5 , 6 However, professional athletes represent only a small segment of the general population, limiting the generalizability of these findings. Furthermore, there is a paucity of studies on how daily PA affects LV structure and function in the general population. Therefore, it is crucial to evaluate alterations in cardiac structure and function through examination of PA volume and variability in a population.

PA exerts a wide range of physiological effects on the cardiovascular system, 7 , 8 benefiting cardiovascular health by mitigating the risks of hypertension, dyslipidemia, obesity, and coronary heart disease. 9 , 10 Prior studies found that the average PA levels over a 24 ‐year span were negatively associated with LV mass in older adults. 11 Conversely, another finding from the MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) study reported that higher mean PA over a decade was linked to increased LV mass and LV end‐diastolic volume in middle‐aged and older adults. 12 However, the impacts of long‐term cumulative PA (CPA) and PA variability in young adults on cardiac remodeling remain a debated topic, and particularly regarding whether such effects resemble the exercise‐induced cardiac remodeling observed in elite athletes. Additionally, PA levels fluctuate considerably throughout life, with individuals undergoing reduced periods of inactivity, increased activity, or sustained high/low PA. Therefore, it is essential to investigate the impact of long‐term longitudinal changes in PA on cardiac structure and function, with the aim of informing cardiovascular disease prevention in broader populations. Here, we hypothesize that CPA and the average real variability of PA (ARVPA) from young adulthood are associated with alterations in LV structure and function at midlife. Our study sought to examine the 30‐year longitudinal associations between PA patterns and LV structure and function in the CARDIA (Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults) cohort.

METHODS

Information about the CARDIA study can be found at https://www.cardia.dopm.uab.edu. Data also can be made available upon reasonable request to the CARDIA coordinator center.

Study Population

CARDIA is a multicenter prospective cohort study designed to investigate cardiovascular risk and disease risk factors in young adulthood. At baseline (year 0: 1985–1986), 5115 Black and White women and men aged 18 to 30 years were recruited from 4 US sites. 13 Site examinations were conducted at years 2, 5, 7, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 after baseline, with retention rates of 91% (year 2), 86% (year 5), 81% (year 7), 79% (year 10), 74% (year 15), 72% (year 20), 72% (year 25), and 71% (year 30), respectively. All participants have provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the institutional review board at each site location. More details about the CARDIA study's design and recruitment procedures have been published previously. 14

The present study included individuals who underwent echocardiography at year 30 and had 9 consecutive PA measures in the 30‐year follow‐up period (years 0–30). Among 2172 eligible participants with 9 consecutive PA data, 1483 participants had available echocardiograms at year 30. After excluding 18 individuals with missing other covariates, the final analytical cohort comprised 1465 participants (Figure S1).

PA Measurements

Self‐reported PA was evaluated using the CARDIA PA history questionnaire across 9 assessments. 15 Participants provided information regarding their frequency of engagement over the past year in 13 specific activity categories (5 moderate intensity: nonvigorous exercise, home exercise or aerobics, walking and hiking, golfing and bowling, and home maintenance or gardening; 8 vigorous intensity: running or jogging; biking; job lifting, carrying, or digging; racquet sports; swimming; exercise or dance class; shoveling or lifting during leisure; and strenuous sports). These activities spanned recreational exercise, sports, occupational activities, and home domains. Each PA was assigned an intensity score (ranging from 3 to 8 metabolic equivalents) and a duration threshold (ranging from 2 to 5 hours per week), above which the activity was classified as frequent. The PA score was calculated by multiplying the frequency (number of months) by the intensity score and a weighting factor. The total PA score, expressed in exercise units, is calculated as the sum of all PA scores. The CARDIA PA questionnaire demonstrated a retest reliability (0.77––0.84), with questionnaire scores correlating with external validation criteria. More details have been published previously. 16

The study collected PA data at 9 examinations (from year 0 to year 30) over 30 years in all participants. The ARVPA was used to assess change in PA levels by taking the absolute difference between consecutive PA measurements and dividing it by the time interval between those measurements. CPA was determined by calculating the mean PA between consecutive visits and multiplying it by the corresponding time interval of years, thereby assessing CPA from young adulthood to middle life (Figure S2). Based on the median CPA and ARVPA, participants were divided into high and low groups. To investigate the combined effects of CPA and ARVPA on LV structure and function, PA categories were divided into 4 groups, including the sustained‐low‐activity group (low CPA and low ARVPA), the low‐CPA and high‐ARVPA group, the sustained‐high‐activity group (high CPA and low ARVPA), and the high‐CPA and high‐ARVPA group.

Outcome

At year 30, trained sonographers at all on‐site centers performed echocardiographic examinations of participants using an Artida 2‐dimensional system (Apilo Artida scanner; Toshiba Medical Systems). All acquired images were digitally transferred to the Echocardiography Core Reading Center (Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, MD) for subsequent analysis based on a standard offline image analysis system (Digisonics, Houston, TX). Additionally, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) were measured using an apical 4‐chamber view to assess LV systolic function. LV mass was calculated using the Devereux formula. 17 LV mass and LV diastolic dimensions were indexed with body surface area. 18 LV end‐diastolic dimensions were measured from a parasternal long‐axis view. Moreover, tissue Doppler at the lateral and septal mitral annulus was used to quantify early peak diastolic mitral annular velocities (e′). Pulsed‐wave Doppler echocardiography was used to determine the peak velocities of the early phase (E) and late phase (A) of mitral inflow velocities as well as their ratios (E/A ratio). The E/e′ ratio served as a surrogate for LV filling pressures, was calculated by dividing the average E wave by the interval and transverse e′ velocities. Speckle‐tracking echocardiography was performed using Toshiba's semi‐automated 2‐dimensional wall‐motion analysis software to assess LV strain and strain rate. According to guidelines, 3 cardiac cycles were acquired at 46.2 frames per second and heart rate <70 beats/min in apical and short‐axis views. 19 Following manual endocardial border tracing, Lagrangian strain was calculated as [(L(t)−L0)/L0]×100%, where L0 represents the initial segment length at QRS onset and L(t) is the length at t time. Longitudinal strain was derived from 4‐chamber views while circumferential strain was obtained from midventricular short‐axis images, with both analyzed using a 6‐segment model incorporating endocardial, midwall, and epicardial layers. Global strain values were computed as the average of segmental peak measurements, following established guidelines with vendor‐specific algorithm adjustments. More details of echocardiography protocol were described in previous studies. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23

Covariates

All covariates were obtained from the year 30 examination. The demographic information, education level, smoking status, disease history, and medication use (antihypertension, antidiabetic, lipid‐lowering therapies) were collected by self‐report or review of medical records. In addition, blood pressure and body mass index were measured according to a standardized protocol. Triglycerides, total cholesterol, and plasma concentrations were assessed using enzymatic methods. Moreover, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol concentrations were measured using a dextran–magnesium precipitation method. Serum low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol was calculated using the Friedewald equation. 24 Hyperlipidemia was defined as a self‐reported diagnosis of Hyperlipidemia, total cholesterol level of ≥200 mg/dL, or use of lipid‐lowering medications. Hypertension was defined as a self‐reported diagnosis of hypertension, systolic blood pressure ≥130 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥80 mm Hg, or taking antihypertensive medications. 25 Diabetes was defined as self‐reported history of diabetes or fasting glucose level of ≥126 mg/dL or taking antidiabetes medications. 26 Additional details have been previously reported. 14 , 27

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were described by mean±SD, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Sample characteristics were compared across CPA groups, ARVPA groups, and PA categories using the χ2 test for categorical variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables. First, we used multivariable linear regression to evaluate the relationship between the CPA group, ARVPA group, and PA categories and the LV structure and function. Model 3 was adjusted for the following covariates: race, sex, age, education level, body mass index, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, hypercholesteremia, diabetes, and antihypertensive drugs. 28 According to the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines, and low LVEF was defined as LVEF <52% for men or <54% for women. 18 We subsequently used multivariable logistic regression to evaluate the association between PA categories and the prevalence of poor LVEF. Stratified analyses were conducted by sex, race, and hypertension status in the study population. Finally, after excluding patients with non–myocardial infarction acute coronary syndrome (ACS), atrial fibrillation, and congestive heart failure at the 30‐year follow‐up, we conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the relationship between PA category and LV structure and function. All statistical analyses were performed with R statistical software version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS 24 (IBM, Armonk, NY). A P value <0.05 was statistically significant.

RESULTS

Sample Characteristics of the Study Population

Among the 1465 participants after 30‐year follow‐up in this study, 849 (58.0%) were women, with a mean (SD) age of 55.2±3.51 years, and 883 (60.3%) were White adults. Compared with participants in the low‐CPA and low‐ARVPA group, those in the high‐CPA and high‐ARVPA group were more often men and less likely to be of Black race, with a higher education level. Additionally, the high‐CPA and high‐ARVPA group exhibited reduced levels of body mass index, diabetes prevalence, hypertension, and antihypertensive medication (Table 1). Those in the sustained CPA group exhibited lower prevalence of diabetes and hypertension. Similar demographic characteristics for the high CPA group were observed compared with low CPA group (Table S1). Furthermore, male sex, younger age, current smoking, and higher systolic blood pressure were associated with the high‐ARVPA group (Table S2).

Subgroup Analysis

The longitudinal relationship between different PA categories and LV structure and function stratified by sex is presented in Table S4 and Figure S3. No significant interactions were observed between different PA categories and echocardiographic metrics. In addition, subgroup analyses stratified by race revealed no significant interactions between various PA categories and LAVI, LVEF, LV longitudinal strain, average e′, E/A, and E/e′ ratio (Figure 1 and Table S5). Notably, White participants had higher LVEDVI (β=3.48 [95% CI, 1.96–5.00]) in the high‐CPA and high‐ARVPA group, and higher LVEDVI (β=2.92 [95% CI, 1.05–4.79]) in the high‐CPA and low‐ARVPA group, compared with those with low CPA and low ARVCPA. Among Black participants, significantly higher LVEDVI was observed in both the high‐CPA and low‐ARVPA group (β=5.87 [95% CI, 2.06–9.68]), and the high‐CPA and high‐ARVPA group (β=2.28 [95% CI, 0.09–4.47]). In addition, we performed analyses stratified by hypertension status. Significant interaction was observed exclusively for LVEDVI. The association was more significant in hypertensive participants than in normotensive participants (Figure 2 and Table S6).

Sensitivity Analysis

Table S7 presents the relationship between PA categories and LV structure and function after excluding individuals with acute coronary syndrome, atrial fibrillation, and congestive heart failure. Similar relationships were observed between PA categories and LV structure/function. Compared with sustained low CPA, participants with high CPA had higher LVEDVI (β=3.70 [95% CI, 2.01–5.40]) in the sustained‐high‐CPA group (β=2.98 [95% CI, 1.73–4.24]) in the high‐CPA and high‐ARVPA group, participants in the high‐CPA and high‐ARVPA group exhibited higher LVMI (β=4.96 [95% CI, 2.52–7.40]), and LAVI (β=1.30 [95% CI, 0.39–2.22]).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the CPA levels and the variability of PA levels to elucidate their impacts on cardiovascular diseases. First, in this large, community‐based cohort study, higher CPA was associated with LV structural remodeling (mass/chamber volumes), while no such association was observed for diastolic function and load‐dependent measures of systolic function (expressed as the LVEF). Second, individuals with higher variability of PA from young adulthood to midlife showed increased LV mass and LV end‐diastolic volume, whereas no correlation was observed with left atrial volume. Third, the joint analysis revealed noticeable alterations in the cardiac structure, whereas no significant changes were observed in LV systolic and diastolic function among populations with high CPA levels, even those with high PA variability. Importantly, these associations remain independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Finally, in patients with hypertension, high variability, even low cumulative amounts of PA, was found to induce ventricular remodeling.

Previous studies demonstrated that higher PA levels have been shown to lower cardiovascular illness risk, including heart failure. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 However, excessive PA potentially elevates cardiovascular risk and contributes to cardiac remodeling. 33 , 34 , 35 The role of diverse PA patterns in cardiac structure and function during the young‐to‐midlife transition remains controversial. Hegde et al reported that greater average PA levels were linked to decreased LVMI and better diastolic and systolic function. 11 However, a key limitation of such studies lies in the infrequent and long‐interval assessment of PA levels, which may substantially compromise the accuracy of estimates regarding participants' true PA exposure. On the contrary, other studies have found that middle‐aged adults with higher PA levels exhibited increased LVMI and left atrial size. 36 Our findings indicate that increased CPA independently correlates with increases in LVMI, LVEDVI, and LAVI after adjusting for all covariates. Furthermore, a key strength of this study is the longitudinal assessment of PA over 30 years, providing a more accurate assessment of the PA–cardiac structure/function relationship than single‐time‐point measurements. In addition, we found that higher CPA were associated with a decreased incidence of diabetes and hypertension. There are statistically significant differences in the incidence of hypertension and diabetes among people with different PA categories. These findings indicate that sustained high PA levels in youth may contribute to reduced incidence of cardiovascular risk factors. Further studies are essential to examine the association between PA patterns and cardiovascular disease incidence.

Another notable finding in this study is that individuals with higher variability of PA from young adulthood to midlife had increased LV mass and LVEDVI. Furthermore, given that a substantial proportion of the population (n=540 [≈40% of our cohort]) alternates between periods of lower and higher PA levels, we innovatively conducted a combined analysis of PA variability and cumulative exposure. Our results reveal that sustained elevated PA levels are associated with LV mass and LVEDVI, but not with LAVI. In addition, the LAVI, LVMI, and LVEDVI increased in those with higher PA levels and higher PA variability. These findings differ from those of analyses examining only PA levels or variability in isolation, suggesting the importance of analyzing both the cumulation and variability of PA in conjunction.

Previous studies have reported sex and race disparities in LV structure and function within the US population. 12 , 37 Our analysis revealed no significant sex‐based differences in LV structure. However, racial disparities were observed. Specifically, our findings suggest that White participants with higher CPA and higher variability in PA exhibited increased LVMI and LVEDVI. Among Black participants, sustained CPA and higher CPA and higher‐variability PA were associated with increases in LVEDVI. Notably, the difference between PA categories and LVMI is marginal. This attenuated association may reflect the relatively limited sample size resulting from our stringent inclusion criteria and extended 30‐year follow‐up period. In addition, our analysis reveals a significant interaction between PA categories and LVEDVI that is probably modified by hypertension status. Notably, we found that high variability, even low cumulative amounts of PA, was associated with ventricular remodeling in patients with hypertension. However, a similar result was not observed in normotensive individuals. This may be explained by the inherent presence of LV remodeling in participants with hypertension. 38 , 39

Several limitations are presented in this study. First, none of the participants in the CARDIA study had clinically significant cardiovascular disease at baseline, and the sample comprised relatively healthy individuals, excluding young adults with preexisting cardiac conditions. This may limit the generalizability of the results. Second, load‐dependent metrics, such as LVEF, were used to assess systolic function in this study, which may not accurately reflect LV function under specific conditions. We incorporated multidimensional strain analysis, including global longitudinal strain and LV circumferential strain, to overcome the inherent limitations of conventional LVEF. 40 Additionally, recall bias may affected the self‐reported PA, whereas a reliable and validated questionnaire was used in the CARDIA to assess PA levels. 41 , 42

CONCLUSIONS

High CPA and high variability in PA levels from adolescence to midlife are associated with alterations in LVMI, LVEDVI, and LAVI. However, the clinical significance of these structural changes remains to be elucidated and warrants further investigation.
