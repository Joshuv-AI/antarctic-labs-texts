Biological aging partly mediates the association between physical activity and overactive bladder: evidence from NHANES 2007–2020
Authors: Junlong Huang, Pingting Chen, Jiayi Zhuang, Ziqiao Wang, Zheng Liu, Wenshuang Li, Chi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOveractive bladder (OAB) impairs quality of life and may increase mortality risk. Physical activity (PA) is a modifiable behavior that could protect against OAB, but the role of biological aging as a potential mechanistic link remains unclear. We examined associations among PA, biological aging, OAB, and all-cause mortality and tested mediation by biological aging.MethodsData from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2007–2020) were used. Weighted Cox regression assessed OAB and all-cause mortality associations. Weighted logistic regression examined associations among PA, biological aging (PhenoAge and KDMAge acceleration), and OAB. Finally, Mediation analysis explored the mediating effect of biological aging on the PA-OAB relationship.ResultsThis study included 18,664 participants, of whom 3,989 (21.37%) reported OAB symptoms. In the mortality-linked cohort (2,553 participants with OAB), 539 (21.11%) deaths were observed. Cox regression showed OAB significantly increased all-cause mortality risk (HR 1.430 [95% CI 1.275–1.604]; P < 0.001), while PA reduced mortality risk in OAB patients (HR 0.572 [95% CI 0.468–0.698]; P < 0.001). Fully adjusted models showed PA reduced OAB risk (OR 0.856 [95% CI 0.775–0.946]; P = 0.003), while each 1-unit increase in PhenoAge and KDMAge acceleration raised OAB risk by 2.7% (P < 0.001) and 2.9% (P = 0.018). Mediation analysis revealed PA delayed PhenoAge and KDMAge acceleration (mediation proportions: 13.47% and 2.71%, respectively), reducing OAB risk.ConclusionsPA could reduce the risk of developing OAB partly by slowing biological aging and decrease all-cause mortality in patients with OAB. This study emphasizes the role of PA in preventing and managing OAB and encourages further evaluation of the mechanisms and effects of behavioral interventions for OAB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-026-02100-5.Keywords: Overactive bladder, Physical activity, Phenotypic age, Klemera-Doubal Age, Mortality

Background

According to the definition by the International Continence Society, overactive bladder (OAB) is a syndrome characterized by urgency, often accompanied by increased frequency and nocturia, with or without urgent urinary incontinence (UUI), in the absence of urinary tract infections or other obvious pathological conditions [1, 2]. Previous studies have reported that the prevalence of OAB across different countries ranges from 9.6% to 31.7% [3–6]. A recent study has shown that OAB is positively associated with higher mortality [7]. The symptoms of OAB not only severely impact patients’ daily lives and social functioning, but are also closely associated with psychological issues such as anxiety and depression, and aging-related diseases such as cerebrovascular disease [8, 9]. However, its etiology is unclear and its treatment is limited.

Numerous studies have shown that the incidence of OAB significantly increases with age [10–12], suggesting that OAB may be closely linked to the physiological processes of aging. Aging is considered a key factor in the deterioration of bladder function. The changes in neural and bladder epithelial function with age may lead to increased bladder hypersensitivity [13]. Moreover, aging is often accompanied by chronic low-grade inflammation and elevated oxidative stress levels [14]. These studies highlighted the potential role of aging in the progression of OAB. In recent years, biological age markers, such as Phenotypic Age (PhenoAge) and Klemera-Doubal Age (KDMAge), have further elucidated the relationship between the aging process and risk of developing disease. Unlike chronological age, biological age integrates multiple physiological indicators—such as inflammatory markers, metabolic state, and immune function—providing a more accurate reflection of an individual’s true aging status [15]. Accelerated biological aging refers to a biological age that exceeds chronological age and is often associated with a higher risk of chronic diseases [16].

Meanwhile, physical activity (PA) as a well-recognized health intervention, is widely believed to delay the aging process through various mechanisms [17–19]. Studies have shown that regular PA not only slows aging by improving cardiovascular health, enhancing muscle strength and flexibility, but also effectively modulates immune function, reduces chronic low-grade inflammation, and lowers oxidative stress levels in the body, thereby decelerating biological aging [20]. These mechanisms provided a potential role for PA in mitigating bladder function deterioration and reducing the risk of developing OAB.

However, current research on the associations between PA, biological aging, and OAB remains limited. In this study, we hypothesized that PA might delay aging to reduce the risk of OAB. Therefore, the primary aim of this research is to investigate the associations between PA, biological aging, and OAB based on a nationally representative population from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database, providing new evidence on the role of behavioral interventions in patients with OAB.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This study used data from the NHANES, a U.S. national survey assessing health and nutritional status through biennial data collection from a representative sample of non-institutionalized adults and children. Participants were interviewed at home, then invited for assessments and blood samples at a mobile examination center. The NHANES data are publicly available on the CDC website (http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.htm), and the protocol was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Institutional Review Board with informed consent [21]. For the present analysis, we included data from four NHANES cycles: 2007–2008, 2009–2010, 2015–2016, and 2017–March 2020. The 2011–2012 and 2013–2014 cycles were excluded because they lacked key laboratory biomarkers required to calculate the biological aging indicators KDMAge and PhenoAge. The 2019–2020 cycle was not completed due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, we used the combined 2017–March 2020 pre-pandemic cycle provided by NHANES. Additional exclusion criteria included: (1) individuals under 20 years, (2) pregnant women, and (3) those with missing data on OAB, PA, or covariates. This study finally included 18,664 participants (Fig S1 in Supplementary materials).

OAB assessment

Clinically, OAB is typically diagnosed based on symptoms of UUI, frequency, and nocturia. Many researchers define OAB in the NHANES using two key symptoms: UUI and nocturia [22–24]. Similarly, OAB was assessed using the NHANES Kidney Disease-Urology Questionnaire in this study. UUI score was evaluated with two questions: (a) “In the past 12 months, have you leaked or lost control of even a small amount of urine with an urge to urinate, and couldn’t reach the toilet in time?” and (b) “How frequently does this occur?” Nocturia score was assessed with: “How many times do you urinate at night?” The overactive bladder symptom score (OABSS) was calculated by summing the UUI and nocturia scores (Table S1 in Supplementary materials). Participants with a total score of ≥ 3 were diagnosed with OAB [22–24].

Measurements of biological aging and biological aging acceleration

We assessed biological aging using KDMAge and PhenoAge. KDMAge is based on biomarkers like forced expiratory volume in first second, systolic blood pressure, albumin, creatinine, C-reactive protein (CRP), glycated hemoglobin, alkaline phosphatase, blood urea nitrogen, and total cholesterol [25, 26], while PhenoAge incorporates factors such as chronological age, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, creatinine, glucose, CRP, lymphocyte percentage, mean corpuscular volume, red cell distribution width, and white blood cell count [16, 26]. To further evaluate individual biological aging status, we calculated KDMAge acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration as the residual from regressing biological age on chronological age [26]. A residual > 0 indicates accelerated aging, while ≤ 0 indicates non-accelerated aging.

Assessment of PA

PA refers to any movement that increases energy expenditure, regardless of its purpose or intensity [27]. PA data were collected using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ), developed by the World Health Organization (WHO). The GPAQ collects information on PA in three domains: work-related activity, travel, and recreational activities (For detailed PA assessments, refer to the GPAQ analysis guide: https://www.who.int/ncds/surveillance/steps/resources/GPAQ_Analysis_Guide.pdf). Following WHO guidelines, each participant’s PA was converted into metabolic equivalent (MET) minutes of moderate-to-vigorous PA per week. MET values vary by activity type, with the NHANES providing reference MET values for different PA types. PA scores were calculated using the formula: MET × weekly frequency × duration per session [28, 29]. According to WHO recommendations, adults should engage in 150 min of moderate-intensity or 75 min of vigorous-intensity PA per week, or a combination equivalent to at least 600 MET-minutes [30]. Therefore, participants in this study were classified into insufficient PA (No-PA) and PA groups (< 600 or ≥ 600 MET-minutes per week, respectively).

Measurements of covariates

The covariates included in this study were age, gender (male, female), race (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, other race), BMI (< 30 kg/m², ≥ 30 kg/m²), poverty-income ratio (PIR, < 2, ≥ 2), marital status (married/living with partner, living alone), health insurance (yes, no), smoking status (never, former, current), alcohol use (yes, no), hypertension (yes, no), diabetes (yes, no), coronary heart disease (CHD, yes, no), stroke (yes, no), and cancer/malignancy (yes, no). Detailed data collection procedures are available on the NHANES website (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes). Additionally, data release cycle, kidney stones (yes, no), depression (yes, no), sleep trouble (yes, no), and stress urinary incontinence (SUI, yes, no) were included in the sensitivity analyses. All covariates were selected a priori based on known or suspected confounders in the relationships between PA, biological aging, and OAB [3, 31–34].

Mortality ascertainment

Mortality data in the NHANES are obtained by linking to the National Death Index (NDI), covering participants from 1999 to 2018 with follow-up through December 31, 2019. The follow-up time for mortality is calculated from the date of the first interview to either the date of death or December 31, 2019, whichever comes first. This study used data from the 2007–2020 cycles. Due to missing parameters related to biological aging for 2011–2014 participants, the analysis of OAB and all-cause mortality is limited to those from 2007 to 2010 and 2015–2016.

Statistical analysis

The NHANES uses a complex cluster design, leading to oversampling and demographic differences across groups. To account for this and ensure representativeness, all analyses applied appropriate sampling weights. Continuous variables are presented as means (SD) or medians (IQR), depending on distribution, while categorical variables are shown as frequencies and percentages. Differences between groups were assessed using weighted ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables, and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Trends in OAB were evaluated using survey-weighted prevalence estimates. The statistical significance of linear trends was assessed by treating the NHANES 2-year period as a continuous variable in survey-weighted generalized linear regression models. Further analysis of OAB prevalence trends was conducted across population subgroups defined by age (20–39 years, 40–59 years, and 60–80 years), gender (male and female), and race (non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Mexican American, other Hispanics, and other races). To explore potential differences in OAB prevalence among these subgroups, the Wald F tests were employed to assess interaction terms between the 2-year survey period and each subgroup.

Stepwise weighted Cox regressions and Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to examine the association between OAB and all-cause mortality. Model 1 was unadjusted, model 2 adjusted for age, gender, and race, while Model 3 further adjusted for BMI, PIR, marital status, health insurance, smoking status, alcohol use, hypertension, diabetes, CHD, stroke, and cancer/malignancy, ensuring comprehensive control of potential confounders. The associations between PA, biological aging, and OAB were assessed using stepwise weighted logistic regression models. In addition, restricted cubic splines (with 3 knots and the 5th percentile as the reference point) were used to assess the nonlinear relationship between PhenoAge /KDMAge acceleration (R package “BioAge”) and OAB. Moreover, subgroup and interaction analyses were performed based on age, gender, BMI, PIR, alcohol use and diabetes. After comprehensive adjustments, sensitivity analyses were conducted to verify the robustness of the results, taking into account factors such as depression, data release cycle, kidney stones, sleep trouble and SUI.

Finally, this study further explored the mediating role of biological aging in the association between PA and OAB using a parallel mediation model (R package “mediation”). The mediation effect analysis was performed using quasi-Bayesian Monte Carlo methods, with 1,000 simulations based on normal approximation. The direct effect (DE) represents the effect of PA on OAB without the mediator, while the indirect effect (IE) reflects the effect of PA on OAB through biological aging. The mediation proportion was calculated as the ratio of IE to total effect (TE). All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.3, http://www.R-project.org), with two-sided P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic characteristics of study participants

A total of 18,664 participants were included in our study (weighted population: 174,622,034), of whom 3,989 (21.37%) were diagnosed with OAB (Table S2 and S3 in Supplementary materials). Compared to the non-OAB group, OAB participants were older, and had higher mortality and shorter survival times. Additionally, they were more likely to be female and had higher rates of BMI ≥ 30 kg/m², PIR < 2, being married/ living with partner, health insurance coverage, smoking (past and current), hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, stroke, and cancer/malignancy. Further analysis revealed that individuals with No-PA, accelerated PhenoAge, and accelerated KDMAge had significantly higher prevalence of OAB (P < 0.05, Fig. S2 in Supplementary materials).

Trends in OAB prevalence

We explored the trends in OAB prevalence across the overall population and within subgroups based on gender, age, and race (Fig. 1 and Table S4 in Supplementary materials). The data show that the overall prevalence of OAB increased steadily from 15.3% in 2007–2008 to 18.4% in 2017–2020 (difference 2.93% [95% CI: 0.83% to 5.03%]; P < 0.001 for trend, Fig. 1A), with no statistically significant differences in this upward trend across gender, age, or racial subgroups (P > 0.05 for interaction). Additionally, OAB prevalence was consistently higher in females than in males (Fig. 1B). The 60–80 age group had a significantly higher prevalence of OAB compared to younger age groups (Fig. 1C). Among racial groups, Non-Hispanic Black individuals had consistently higher OAB prevalence than the other four racial groups (Fig. 1D).

Association of OAB with all-cause mortality

As of December 31, 2019, 1419 deaths occurred. Participants with OAB had higher mortality and shorter survival times than those without OAB (Table S2 in Supplementary materials). Weighted Cox regression model indicated that OAB was an independent risk factor for all-cause mortality (HR 1.430 [95% CI 1.275–1.604]; P < 0.001, Table S5 in Supplementary materials) after full adjustment. Weighted Kaplan-Meier survival curve further demonstrated that participants in the OAB group had lower survival probability compared to thenon-OAB group, with this trend becoming more pronounced over time (Fig. 2A). In addition, subgroup analysis revealed that PA partially reduced the higher risk of mortality associated with OAB (Fig. S3 in Supplementary materials).

Association between PA and OAB

We firstly confirmed that PA significantly reduced the hazard ratio of all-cause mortality in the whole cohort (HR 0.722 [95% CI 0.626–0.833]; P < 0.001, Table S5 in Supplementary materials). Then, the weighted Kaplan-Meier survival curves revealed that No-PA reduced survival probability in individuals with OAB (Fig. 2B). PA reduced the hazard ratio of all-cause mortality in patients with OAB (HR 0.572 [95% CI 0.468–0.698]; P < 0.001). Subsequently, a stepwise weighted multivariate logistic regression model was applied to further investigate the association between PA status and OAB. The results demonstrated that PA significantly reduced the risk of OAB (OR 0.493 [95% CI 0.450–0.540]; P < 0.001). This association remained consistent after both mildly (OR 0.745 [95% CI 0.676–0.822]; P < 0.001) and fully adjusted analyses (OR 0.856 [95% CI 0.775–0.946], P = 0.003) (Table 1). Moreover, participants in the No-PA group had increased frequency of urgency urinary incontinence, nocturia, and moderate to severe OAB compared to those in the PA group (Table S6 in Supplementary materials).

Association between biological aging and OAB

The association between age and OAB was assessed using restricted cubic spline analysis, which showed a significant increase in OAB risk with advancing age. The risk rose sharply after the age of 50, and the relationship between age and OAB was linear (Fig. S4 in Supplementary materials). In addition, both PhenoAge and KDMAge acceleration had a significantly positive association with OAB risk (Fig. S5 in Supplementary materials).

The fully adjusted logistic regression models showed that each 1-unit increase in PhenoAge and KDMAge acceleration raised OAB risk by 2.7% [95% CI 1.018–1.037, P < 0.001] and 2.9% [95% CI 1.006–1.053, P = 0.018], respectively (Table 1). This trend persisted across both binary and quartile-based analyses, especially for PhenoAge acceleration. Individuals with PhenoAge acceleration had a 35.4% [95% CI 1.203–1.523, P < 0.001] higher OAB risk than those without acceleration, with the fourth quartile showing a 74.9% [95% CI 1.465–2.087, P < 0.001] higher risk compared to the first quartile. KDMAge acceleration showed similar trends, with a 21.9% [95% CI 1.013–1.467, P = 0.038] higher risk for those with acceleration and a 30.5% [95% CI 1.003–1.697, P = 0.048] increase in the fourth quartile compared to the first.

Further analysis showed that participants with accelerated biological aging had higher frequencies of UUI, nocturia, and severe OAB (Table S8 and Table S9 in Supplementary materials). Figure 3 presents the subgroup analysis of the associations between PhenoAge/KDMAge accelerations and OAB. The results indicated that PhenoAge acceleration consistently increased OAB risk across all subgroups, with the effect being particularly pronounced in individuals aged < 50 years, females, alcohol users, and non-diabetics, with significant interactions observed within these groups (P < 0.05). In contrast, the association between KDMAge acceleration and OAB was not significantly influenced by age, gender, BMI, PIR, alcohol use, or diabetes status. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that the associations remained robust after adjusting for various factors, including kidney stones, SUI, depression, sleep trouble, and data release cycle (Table S10–S14 in Supplementary materials).

Association between PA and biological aging

The stepwise weighted regression models indicated that PA significantly slowed PhenoAge acceleration (OR 0.285 [95% CI 0.230–0.353]; P < 0.001) (Table S15 in Supplementary materials). This association remained robust after both mild (OR 0.346 [95% CI 0.277–0.431]; P < 0.001) and fully adjusted models (OR 0.605 [95% CI 0.498–0.735]; P < 0.001). Similarly, in the fully adjusted model, participants engaging in PA had a lower KDMAge acceleration (OR 0.740 [95% CI 0.588–0.932]; P = 0.001).

Mediation analyses

Parallel mediation analysis showed that both PhenoAge and KDMAge acceleration significantly mediated the relationship between PA and OAB risk (Fig. 4). PhenoAge acceleration explained 13.47% of PA’s effect on OAB, with an indirect effect of -0.21% [95% CI -0.28% – 0.00%, P < 0.001], while KDMAge acceleration explained 2.71% of the effect, with an indirect effect of -0.08% [95% CI -0.19% – 0.00%, P = 0.028]. The direct effect of PA on OAB was − 1.32% [95% CI -2.47% – 0.00%, P < 0.001] for PhenoAge and − 2.78% [95% CI -4.62% – -1.00%, P < 0.001] for KDMAge.

Discussion

This study found a steady increase in the overall prevalence of OAB from 2007 to 2020 and examined the complex relationships between PA, biological aging, OAB, and all-cause mortality. The findings not only confirmed a significant association between OAB and the increased risk of mortality, but also demonstrated that PA could significantly reduce all-cause mortality in patients with OAB. In addition, biological aging acceleration significantly increased the risk of OAB, while PA could reduce the risk. Mediation analysis further elucidated that PA could reduce the risk of developing OAB partially through delaying biological aging.

First, we observed an overall increase in the prevalence of OAB, underscoring the need for heightened awareness of this condition. Subgroup analysis showed that the upward trend in prevalence did not vary significantly across gender, age, or race, indicating that public health interventions may be more effective when focusing on universal strategies rather than targeting specific populations. Consistent with previous studies [2, 4, 6, 12], our findings identify older adults and women as high-risk groups, highlighting the necessity of strengthening prevention and treatment efforts within these populations to address the rising prevalence of OAB. Meanwhile, this study confirmed the significant association between OAB and all-cause mortality, similarly to the previous study [7]. OAB is not merely a dysfunction of the urinary system but is closely linked to various aging-related diseases. And these diseases lead to a higher risk of mortality. For instance, previous studies have found that OAB might increase the risk of developing metabolic syndrome [35], cerebrovascular diseases [9], and depression [23]. These comorbidities may contribute to the elevated risk of mortality observed in patients with OAB. In addition, OAB is associated with elevated levels of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress [36, 37], both of which are closely tied to a higher risk of mortality [38, 39]. This indicates that OAB might have a direct effect on overall health outcomes. Furthermore, our study demonstrated that not only could PA reduce the risk of developing OAB, but also it could decrease the hazard ratio of all-cause mortality in patients with OAB. Previous studies have demonstrated that PA can lower all-cause mortality by improving cardiovascular function, strengthening the immune system, and alleviating the risk of metabolic syndrome and systemic immune-inflammation response [20, 40]. These mechanisms might explain the role of PA in reducing mortality in patients with OAB. Although our study did not explore the underlying mechanisms of this association, these findings support PA as a promising behavioral intervention in patients with OAB, potentially enhancing their long-term outcomes.

Second, our study identified PhenoAge and KDMAge acceleration as independent risk factors for OAB. Oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation are key mechanisms that accelerate biological aging [41]. Because inflammation and oxidative stress are involved in the pathogenesis of OAB, these factors may explain the potential mechanisms of biological aging in the progression of OAB. Previous study found that PhenoAge was related to the decline in bladder smooth muscle function and weakened autonomic regulation of the bladder [16]. And KDMAge primarily reflects biological age through changes in DNA methylation patterns, representing epigenetic alterations. Yamashita et al. found that chronic inflammation could accelerate aging-related DNA methylation, affecting the function of multiple organs, including the bladder [42]. Murata et al. further explained that inflammation generates reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, leading to DNA damage and epigenetic changes in gene expression, which may impair bladder function [43]. These results may explain the role of PhenoAge and KDMAge acceleration in OAB. Moreover, we found that PA could reduce the risk of OAB by slowing PhenoAge and KDMAge acceleration. Previous studies revealed that PA could reduce oxidative stress [44] and lower systemic inflammation levels [45], thereby delaying cellular aging and tissue damage. Furthermore, PA influences DNA methylation to regulate gene expression, slowing biological aging. Sailani et al. discovered that individuals who engage in long-term PA exhibit lower levels of DNA methylation in certain gene promoter regions, which delays cellular functional decline [46]. The protective effects of PA against OAB are not limited to its modulation of aging speed but also extend to its direct improvements in bladder smooth muscle function, strengthening of the pelvic floor muscles, and enhancement of nervous system regulation. Daniela et al. pointed out that PA enhances parasympathetic activity and reduces excessive sympathetic excitation, improving bladder compliance and control [47]. Therefore, PA might directly (such as improving smooth muscle function and bladder compliance) or indirectly (such as delaying biological aging via reducing oxidative stress and systemic inflammation) protect individuals from developing OAB. Future mechanistic studies should focus on these potential mechanisms to explore the deeper role of PA in OAB.

Currently, non-operative treatments for OAB include lifestyle modifications, behavioral therapy, and physical interventions. As a side-effect-free lifestyle intervention, PA shows potential in delaying biological aging and alleviating OAB symptoms. Clinical studies have demonstrated that regular PA could improve bladder control in elderly patients, alleviating urinary urgency and frequency [48, 49]. Moreover, obesity is a known risk factor for urinary incontinence in OAB. By promoting weight loss and improving metabolic function, PA can further alleviate bladder pressure and autonomic dysfunction, significantly improving lower urinary tract symptoms [50]. Therefore, incorporating regular PA as part of a comprehensive treatment plan for OAB could help mitigate symptoms and enhance patient quality of life. A higher-quality clinical research study, such as randomized controlled trials (RCTs), should be conducted in the future to evaluate the long-term effects of PA on patients with OAB.

Honestly, our study has some limitations. First, the NHANES study design is cross-sectional and observational, which limits our ability to establish a causal relationship between PA and OAB. Future studies with more rigorous designs are needed to further validate their relationship. Second, the measurement of PA and the diagnosis of OAB were primarily based on self-reports, which may introduce recall bias. Finally, although we made efforts to control for known confounding factors, there may still be unmeasured variables that could influence the association between PA, biological aging, and OAB.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that OAB significantly increased the risk of all-cause mortality, while PA can partially offset the risk of all-cause mortality in patients with OAB. Moreover, PA could reduce the risk of developing OAB directly or indirectly by slowing biological aging. This research highlights the importance of PA as an effective strategy for the prevention and management of OAB, encouraging basic researchers and clinical workers to assessing the mechanisms and effect of behavioral therapy in patients with OAB.
