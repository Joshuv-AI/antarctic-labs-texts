Distribution of Neurosurgical Workforce and Infrastructure in Nigeria in 2024: A Cross-Sectional National Overview
Authors: Jude-Kennedy Chinedu Emejulu, Sunday Patrick Uzomaka Nkwerem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Nigerian neurosurgery has remained under the existential threat of poor political decisions of governments and the resultant socio-economic instability. Currently, the country is undergoing its worst form of insecurity and brain drain, as skilled professionals emigrate abroad to more secure and safer countries to improve their income and living standards. The health sector, especially neurosurgery, is one of the hardest hit. The objective of this study is to profile the available neurosurgery infrastructure and manpower in the 6 geopolitical zones of Nigeria.Methodology:This involves an online cross-sectional survey by questionnaire, phone calls, and social media chats of the neurosurgeons working in all 44 neurosurgery facilities listed on the Nigerian Academy of Neurological Science (NANS) website. This was matched with secondary data on the Nigerian regional population figures obtained from internet fact-checks. Descriptive statistics were used to interpret and analyse the collated data.Results:There were 44 neurosurgery facilities under the leadership of 111 out of the 166 certified neurosurgeons; the rest had emigrated due to the economic hardships of the country. The neurosurgeon: population ratio was 1:2.12million. The North-East Zone has the least number of neurosurgeons.Conclusion:The number of neurosurgeons per Nigerian population is poor. Furthermore, the abysmal statistics and the discrepancy in the regional distribution even make access worse in the North East region of the country.

Introduction

Nigeria's population at the end of 2024 was 235million people, with a 2023 growth rate of 2.38%, and previous growth rates of 2.38%, 2.41%, 2.22%, and 2.34% for 2022, 2021, 2020, and 2019, respectively.[1,2] The provision of Universal Health Coverage (UHC), using the 17 parameters of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) of the United Nations Organization (UNO), has remained a major challenge for sub-Saharan African countries, not least Nigeria, and its frequently changing government policies.[3,4,5,6,7]

Modern neurosurgery started in Africa in 1951 with the efforts of Osman Sorour and his two colleagues, Samuel Boctor and Ibrahim Higazi, in different parts of Egypt. It was more than a decade after Egypt when neurosurgery came into Nigeria, with humble beginnings in 1962, by the pioneering efforts of E Latunde Odeku, who was trained in the United States of America.[8]

Some 5years after, Nigeria registered her 2nd neurosurgeon, Adelola Adeloye, in 1967, and subsequently, came a decade of rapid establishment of different facilities by Nigerian neurosurgeons trained in different parts of the world. Colin da Silva (Lagos) in 1968, Nosiru Ojikutu (Lagos) in 1969, Samuel Ohaegbulam (Enugu) in 1974, and Adebayo A Olumide (Ibadan) in 1974 made the list of the first contingent of Nigerian neurosurgeons in its first two decades of existence. After that era, a few more specialists came back to join the development of neurosurgery in the country.

The growth of the specialty, however, suffered significant workforce attrition over the subsequent years, till 2000, when the structured, full-course training eventually became institutionalized at the University College Hospital (UCH) Ibadan, and so began the survival story of Nigerian neurosurgery in the 21st century.

In the various historical times when the profession suffered its setbacks, they were mostly due to reasons bothering on the country's political governance. Consecutive Nigerian governments did not invest much in neurosurgery, nor did they encourage the setting up of new or standard facilities. [9,10]

It got so bad that from 1984 onwards, centres that offered services were reduced from 8 (Ibadan, Lagos, Enugu, Jos, Zaria, Ile-Ife, Owerri, and Port Harcourt) to 2 (Ibadan and Lagos), whereas the training centres decreased from 3 to just 1 (only Ibadan).[11]

In 1999, Nigeria had only 5 active neurosurgeons practising within the country, at Ibadan, Lagos, Owerri, and Port Harcourt, in three public teaching hospitals and one private facility. The rest of the specialists had left the shores of the country for the Gulf States, Europe, North America, and parts of Africa, starting from 1985.[12]

By 2000, the emigrants had gradually begun to return to the country, bringing along their newly acquired skills and equipment. In that same year, the 3rd set of indigenously trained neurosurgeons, Timothy O Odebode and Edward O Komolafe, were certified after being trained by M Temitayo Shokunbi and Adefolarin O Malomo, and the growth and progress became formally established. Some 13years earlier, before the migration of neurosurgeons, Ohaegbulam in Enugu had trained Nigeria's first indigenous neurosurgeon, Wilfred C Mezue in 1987, and then, Shokunbi had followed up with the training of Adefolarin O Malomo, who was certified in 1991 at Ibadan.[11]

After the 1985-1999 era of brain drain, another (2nd) episode of migration started by 2016 (and is still ongoing into 2025), as the poor economic policies of the successive federal governments began to bite harder. The story of the loss of specialised practitioners was the same in all Nigerian tertiary health institutions, with the unabated loss of a critical mass of manpower, leading to a degradation of services, training, and research.

The Abuja Declaration on Healthcare Financing was adopted in 2001 by African Union (AU) governments to set a target of allocating at least 15% of their national budgets to healthcare annually.[13, 14] In her review of the compliance by African nations, the WHO reported a very low level of compliance after 10years of that Declaration. According to the WHO, by 2020, only 7 African countries' budgets met the benchmark of the Abuja Accord, viz. Rwanda, Botswana, Niger, Zambia, Malawi, Burkina Faso, and Togo.[13] Nigeria's annual budgetary allocation to health, in those 10years, barely scratched beyond the 5% mark, at 30-35% of the Abuja recommendation. [15]

In the 2024 national budget, the Nigerian government's health allocation was 5.03%, and the preceding years were no better; 2025 was not any better. [16,17] In addition, the volatility and dwindling naira value have also affected the purchasing power of the health institutions. [15,16,17,18,19,20]

Onak et al, in a cross-sectional survey involving 913 physicians from states across the federation, showed that even among junior and senior-level doctors, married and single professionals, the majority of Nigerian physicians want to emigrate for professional practice.

The top 3 reasons proffered for escaping from the country were: high taxes and salary deductions (98%), low work satisfaction in Nigeria (92%), and poor salaries/emoluments (91%); some also added insecurity.[22] But that was 8years ago. Presently, with the worsening insecurity across the federation, insecurity as a factor will most likely come into the top 3.[23]

Neurosurgery, as a profession, became one of the earliest casualties of the economic policies of the successive governments, just as they affected the healthcare services of Nigeria in general. The Nigerian government had contracted VAMED, a health services company, to upgrade and re-equip the major tertiary health facilities in Nigeria, and the impact was evident. [24, 25] The number of neurosurgery facilities in Nigeria had begun to rise, as several new centres got established, bringing the total to at least 44 centres, by 2024.

Ironically, while the number of centres rose, the neurosurgeons were escaping to foreign countries simultaneously, 10-20 years after VAMED intervention – to Europe, Gulf States, and North America. This put a heavy strain on the depleted available manpower, in the face of progressively increasing national population, in what has become the worst episode of brain drain in Nigeria's history.

As of 2024, only 111 out of 166 neurosurgeons are still available in Nigeria, and the number could get even lower in the coming years. And, with the 111 neurosurgeons left behind to cater for Nigeria's population of 235million, it comes out to 1 neurosurgeon to 2.12million persons. [1,2] Meanwhile, the World Health Organization (WHO) and Global Neurosurgery Committee (GNC) recommend 1 neurosurgeon to 200,000 persons, a 10-time (or 1000%) gap ahead of the Nigerian current status. [26,27,28,29,30]

On account of this grim reality, we set out to profile the national spread of the 44 neurosurgery centres and the distribution of available neurosurgeons in the six North-Central, North-East, North-West, South-East, South-South, and South-West Zones of Nigeria.

We study the distribution of the mass of neurosurgery facilities and services around Nigeria's 6 geopolitical zones, 36 states with a Federal Capital Territory, and a population of 235 million. To evaluate the demographic factors that could explain the pattern of the distribution and discrepancies between different zones.

Methods

This is a national descriptive cross-sectional workforce mapping using a survey of participants and secondary data on the Nigerian population. An online cross-sectional survey amongst the neurosurgery personnel in 44 Nigerian tertiary public and private health facilities that offer neurosurgery services. The list of the centres was got from the website of the Nigerian Academy of Neurological Surgeons.

Offline and online search and cross-referencing of available records, previous studies, and publications on Nigerian neurosurgery, government policies, and the present socioeconomic status of the country.

Online questionnaires, direct individual phone calls, emails, WhatsApp chats, the Nigerian Academy of Neurological Surgeons website and archives, and authentication of retrieved data. The data obtained was matched with the Nigerian population.[1]

Results were presented in tables and figures, and the institutions were divided into six categories, VI–I, in descending order of the available number of neurosurgeons. Descriptive statistics and comparative analysis were utilized in analyzing the collated statistics. Ethical approval was obtained from Nnamdi Azikiwe University with reference number NAUTH/CMAC/01/VOL.2/240

Discussions

By the end of 2024, just about 67% of the 166 certified and registered neurosurgeons in Nigeria were still in practice within the country. The rest, 33%, had already emigrated abroad or had major health challenges. Only 111 full-time certified neurosurgeons were still serving in the 44 facilities available in the country. The situation, inadvertently, provided an extra impetus for medical tourism and capital flight abroad. It has been reported that medical tourism by Nigerians has gulped billions of Naira. The impact of this on the economy is remarkable. [32,33]

Poor funding of the health sector is partly responsible for this unfortunate situation. From 2020 to 2024, the annual budgetary allocations to health were, respectively, 3.83%, 4.18%, 4.56%, 4.7%, and 5.03%, some of the lowest in Africa, and 2020 was the lowest in the past decade, <4%, about 26% of the Abuja Declaration.[14] For 2025, standing at 5.2% (N2.48trillion of the total annual budget of N47.9trillion), there was not much difference from the preceding year's 5.03%. And, with such paltry allocations, the health sector in Nigeria is 300-500% under-funded and bound to run into the myriads of challenges that have encouraged the large exodus of the country's high-level skilled manpower to greener pastures abroad.

In addition, the poor implementation of the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) has also not given the much-needed support in the health sector, unfortunately. The National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) in Nigeria is not achieving its set goals, especially when healthcare services are mostly accessed through out-of-pocket expenditure. Current statistics indicate that less than 5% of Nigerians are enrolled in NHIS, while 70% still finance their care independently.[34] Major issues are inefficient service delivery, inadequate healthcare infrastructure, and poor resource management.[34]

The rise in the number of centres within a time span of 25years till 2024, from 3 to 44 - a 1466.7% increase, and that of neurosurgeons from 5 to 166, a 4150% rise, were very remarkable, even when they fell far short of the WHO and GNC recommendation of one neurosurgeon to 200,000 population. The limited number of slots and centres accredited for neurosurgical training in Nigeria may also be one of the factors responsible for the poor statistics.

The highest number of neurosurgeons and neurosurgery facilities was seen in the South-West (see Table 1). This may be explained by the fact that neurosurgery services and training started in South-West Nigeria and subsequently spread to other zones in Nigeria. [8]

North-East has the least number of neurosurgeons. The number of centres and neurosurgeons generally appeared to have lower neurosurgical density in regions experiencing insecurity in Nigeria, though causality cannot be fully inferred. The more violence-ridden zones of the North-East and North-West were the most marginalized, underdeveloped, and deprived of neurosurgery facilities and manpower, whereas the zones like the South-West and South-East had the highest availability of both the facilities and manpower. Besides Urbanization, rural-urban migration may also have contributed to this pattern of regional distribution of the neurosurgical workforce. [35] In addition, workers, including neurosurgeons, will likely settle down in cities and regions which perceived economic opportunities and a better standard of living. [36] Other determinants may also be the training institution's geography and even incentives put in place by the training institution.

To buttress this fact, Tables 1 and 3 above show that not only is the North-East the least developed in neurosurgical infrastructure, but it also has the least number of neurosurgeons in practice, 6.4% of 111, despite holding a 12% share of the national population. And, this discordance becomes clearer when it is compared with the South-South, with the same population estimates but with 10 facilities and 17 neurosurgeons, representing 23% of the available facilities as against the 9% in the North-East, and 15.3% of neurosurgeons compared to 6.4% in the North-East, respectively. The discordance between the zones suggests that neurosurgery services are less available in the zone with the perceived high level of insecurity. Nigeria's modern-day large-scale insecurity took its roots, becoming a national preoccupation, from the North-East Zone in 1980, through the revolt of Mohammed Marwa Maitatsine.[36,37] That putsch, eventually, birthed the massive violence of the 21st century, from that same zone comprising Adamawa, Bauchi, Borno, Gombe, Taraba, and Yobe States, since 2003.[36,37] It spread stealthily to the other zones of Nigeria, in its most violent mould. Historically, the North-East of Nigeria remains the youngest zone to establish neurosurgery (Maiduguri 2015). The earliest zone in Nigeria was the South-West at Ibadan in 1962, which was followed by the South-East (Enugu 1974), North-Central (Jos 1985), South-South (Port-Harcourt 1985), and North-West (Sokoto 1997).

It is even more worrisome when the North-East is compared with the North-Central, which has 11% of the population compared to the North-East's higher population of 12.5%. The North-Central has 16% of the facilities and 19% of the neurosurgeons, compared to the North-East's 9% and 6.4%, respectively. The fact that Nigeria's Federal Capital Territory, Abuja, is located in the North-Central remains an important determinant, but the relative peace in the North-Central compared to the North-East remains a plausible additional factor to the discordance. Besides, the opportunity Abuja provides as the state capital of the federation is also a pull factor. [35,36]

The case of the North-West is equally worrisome. The zone holds an estimated 23% of Nigeria's population, but has only 11.4% of the facilities and 17% of neurosurgeons, compared to the South-West with 22.7% and 21.6% of facilities and neurosurgeons, a near-200% superiority on both parameters, despite holding the same population as the North-West.[31] Meanwhile, the South-West is the most stable socio-political and pioneering zone for neurosurgery in Nigeria.

For the South-East, with 18% of the national population, 18% of the facilities, and 20% of the manpower, respectively, the performance indices were very comparable to those of the South-West. There has been some confined insurgency in the South-East, which is just a shade higher than in the South-West. but falls far short of the all-out war situation in the North-East. [37,38] These further lend credence to the impact of insecurity, and possibly urbanization on all of its socio-economic indices, and not least, the healthcare services, and the neurosurgery infrastructure. However, the impact of differences in the number of training centres and economic clustering in urban centres should not be underestimated.

The average number of neurosurgeons per centre was highest in the North-West (see Table 2). This is explained by the fact that the zone has relatively fewer centres, with the neurosurgeons concentrated in these facilities. This relationship is, however, not statistically significant. (P = 0.576)

Despite the unfortunate, abysmal statistics on the global Nigerian neurosurgeon/population ratio, North-Central has a relatively higher neurosurgeon/population ratio compared to other zones in Nigeria (see Table 3). North-East has the lowest ratio. The volatile security situation in the North-East may contribute to the poor statistics. This grim picture of abysmal statistics appears to be the lot of most low- and medium-income countries of the world. [39]

In all of those, the most rewarding aspect of the development, however, was the fact that almost all of the 166 certified neurosurgeons, except for four, were locally trained.

Conclusion

The number of neurosurgeons per Nigerian population is poor. Despite the abysmal statistics, the discrepancy in the regional distribution even makes access worse in the North East region of the country. Only two centres have six neurosurgeons, while more than half of the total number of centres have two neurosurgeons or fewer.
