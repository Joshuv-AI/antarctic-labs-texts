Interventions to reduce postoperative mortality in low- and middle-income countries: systematic review
Authors: Liam N Phelan, Anita E Agbeko, Alazar B Aregawi, Aneel A Bhangu, Parvez D Haque, Sivesh K Kamarajah, Maria Picciochi, Sandrine Zola, Dmitri Nepogodiev, James Glasbey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative mortality is the third leading cause of death worldwide and disproportionately affects patients in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Although many deaths are potentially preventable, evidenced-based interventions remain limited. The aim of this systematic review was to consolidate randomized trial evidence on interventions designed to reduce postoperative mortality in LMICs.MethodsMedical databases from database inception to February 2025 were systematically searched for randomized trials evaluating interventions in any aspect of the patient pathway from admission to discharge that were designed to reduce postoperative mortality within 90 days of surgery in LMIC patients. Eligible studies included elective and emergency operations across all surgical specialties. The protocol was preregistered in PROSPERO, the international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD42024604760).ResultsFrom 10 877 search results, some 18 trials involving 95 521 patients were included. Three were multicountry trials and half were conducted in China. Trials spanned multiple specialties, with only 4 of 18 assessed as having a low risk of bias. Four trials showed a significant reduction in mortality, although the interventions were largely context specific (two neurosurgical trials centred on operative technique in China, one Brazilian trial investigating patient blood management, and one study evaluating maternal death reviews and implementation of best practice after caesarean deliveries in Senegal and Mali). Adherence to interventions was generally high and similar between simple and complex interventions (93.6% versus 96.8% respectively), although adherence varied substantially across trials.ConclusionFew RCTs have targeted postoperative mortality in LMICs and no intervention demonstrated consistent effectiveness across multiple contexts. To maximize clinical impact, future research should prioritize the development of interventions in regions with the greatest need, particularly sub-Saharan Africa, and ensure they are co-developed with LMIC interest holders and evaluated across diverse clinical environments.

Introduction

The 2015 Lancet Commission for Global Surgery highlighted the increasing burden of morbidity and mortality of common surgical conditions in the world’s most resource-limited regions.1 Access to safe, timely, and affordable surgical and anaesthetic care must be an integral part of national health systems to reduce premature death1. Postoperative mortality is the third leading cause of death worldwide; however, progress towards the 2015 Lancet Commission’s aims has been slow and unequal, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)2,3. It is recognized that patients in LMICs are disproportionately affected, with a two to three times increased risk of postoperative mortality compared with patients in high-income countries (HICs)4,5. In the last 50 years perioperative mortality has significantly decreased, although the greatest reductions have been in developed countries6. Large international observational studies have supported these findings; the African Surgical Outcomes Study (ASOS) showed patients in Africa undergoing surgery were twice as likely to die compared with patients in the rest of the world, despite being younger, having a lower risk profile, and having lower complication rates, whereas the International Surgical Outcomes Study (ISOS), which included patients from HICs, middle-income countries, and low-income countries (LICs), showed that patients in LMICs had a lower baseline risk, but had similar outcomes to patients in HICs, which may reflect differences in case mixes, hospital capabilities, and postoperative surveillance across LMIC subgroups7,8. ASOS suggested a failure to identify and treat life-threatening complications in the perioperative interval led to a high incidence of potentially avoidable deaths, a phenomenon increasingly described as ‘failure to rescue’8. Some estimates suggest that patients in some LMICs have up to a 50- to 100-fold increased risk of death after caesarean delivery and a 40-fold increased risk of death after inguinal hernia repair or appendicectomy compared with HIC patients and disparate outcomes are consistent across specialties, including obstetrics, paediatric surgery, cancer care, and trauma8–14. The literature consistently shows there is an inverse relationship between risk of perioperative and anaesthetic-related mortality and the Human Development Index (HDI)6. While World Bank income classification is commonly used to group countries for health research, it does not fully capture within-group heterogeneity in health system capacity, workforce, infrastructure, and access to care. The HDI incorporates life expectancy, education, and income, and therefore provides a complementary lens through which to understand variation in surgical outcomes within and across income groups. Countries with similar income classifications may therefore experience markedly different postoperative mortality risks due to differences in broader social and health system development. Postoperative mortality is a complex, poorly understood area, but it is likely that many deaths after surgery in LMICs are avoidable and preventable.

The causal pathway for death is complex and likely multifactorial in origin. Therefore, it seems unlikely that a solitary, simple intervention will significantly reduce postoperative deaths in LMICs and offset the inequity in outcomes seen in LMICs compared with in HICs. Globally it has been recognized that there is a need for further intervention, trials, and action in LMICs to tackle postoperative mortality2,15–18. Although poorer postoperative outcomes and limited trial evidence in LMICs are well recognized, less attention has been paid to the nature of the interventions that have been tested, the contexts in which trials have been conducted, and whether existing randomized evidence meaningfully targets the mechanisms underlying excess postoperative mortality. Therefore, the aim of this review was not simply to quantify an evidence gap, but to characterize the scope, focus, and limitations of randomized trials evaluating interventions that were designed to reduce postoperative mortality in LMICs, to inform future intervention development and trial design.

Methods

The protocol for this review has been published previously19. This manuscript is reported in accordance with PRISMA20. The protocol was preregistered in PROSPERO, the international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD42024604760).

Aims and objectives

The primary aim was to identify interventions that have been assessed in RCTs that were designed to reduce short-term postoperative mortality and to address the following objectives: identify the number of RCTs that have tested interventions designed to reduce short-term postoperative mortality after non-cardiac surgery; assess the geographical location where the trials were conducted; describe the interventions, their components, and their timing in the patient pathway; assess the impact of the interventions on mortality; and assess the adherence to the interventions and compare adherence between simple and complex interventions.

Mortality was selected as the primary endpoint in this review because it represents the most consequential and inequitable surgical outcome globally and has been prioritized by interest holders in LMICs. While postoperative complications are more frequent and easier to power for, mortality captures failures across the entire perioperative pathway, including delayed recognition, limited rescue capacity, and system-level constraints, which are central to outcome disparities in resource-limited settings.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

RCTs that assessed any intervention designed to reduce short-term postoperative mortality after non-cardiac surgery in LMICs were included if they reported short-term postoperative mortality (within 90 days of surgery) as the primary outcome, co-primary outcome, or as part of a composite measure. If studies reported multiple primary outcomes, they were included if there was a power calculation demonstrating that the study was powered to assess mortality. The intervention could be in any part of the patient pathway during a hospital admission as this is the interval in which most people die after surgery. Patients could be of any age, sex, or ethnicity, and the surgery could be either elective or emergency surgery. Both individual patient and cluster RCTs were included. There were no time limitations in the search criteria. Where articles were not written in English, they were translated into English when possible. For this review, the term ‘LMICs’ was used as an umbrella descriptor encompassing LICs, lower-middle-income countries, and upper-middle-income countries, according to the World Bank income classifications. Country income was defined using World Bank classifications applicable at the time each trial was conducted. Only interventions tested in LMICs were assessed, as the reasons for postoperative mortality in LMICs are likely to be context specific and may differ from those in HICs. The list of LMICs used in the search terms can be found in the Supplementary material.. The inclusion criteria, reported according to the Population/Intervention/Comparator/Outcome (PICO) framework, are summarized in Table S1.

Exclusion criteria

Studies that reported overall survival, progression-free survival, or disease-free survival as the primary outcome were excluded. Studies that included both LMICs and HICs were only included if LMIC-specific data were presented separately. HICs were excluded, as the clinical context in which the interventions are introduced and tested is likely to differ significantly from most LMICs. Studies that included both surgical and medical patients were only included if data for the surgical cohort alone were presented. Studies comparing non-operative versus operative approaches were excluded (as were endoscopic and endovascular interventions, which were not considered to be surgical procedures), as patients in one arm of such studies did not undergo surgery. Reviews, study protocols, abstracts, editorials, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and opinion pieces were excluded. Animal studies were also excluded.

Information sources

A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, the Science Citation Index and the Emerging Sources Citation Index (via the Web of Science), EBSCO, the Cochrane Library, and the VHL study database in February 2025. The search date for each database and the results of the searches can be found in Table S2.

Search strategy

The search strategy incorporated a combination of population terms, condition terms, and geographical terms. The search terms for each database are summarized in the Supplementary material. The main population and condition terms included: mortality, death(s), survival, surgery, surgical, surgeries, operation, pre-operative, peri-operative, postoperative, randomised controlled trial, controlled trial, randomised, and placebo. To complete the searches, these terms were combined with country names identified through the World Bank’s list of LMICs, outlined in the Supplementary material.

Selection process

First, articles were included or excluded after screening of their titles and abstracts. Where insufficient information was presented to decide about eligibility, full-text articles were accessed. Where papers were clearly ineligible, according to the published protocol, full texts were excluded. For papers thought to be eligible for final analysis, this was corroborated by two further reviewers (J.G. and A.A.B.). This increased depth of review and discussion was felt necessary as some papers with marginal reasons for exclusion (for example did not specifically state death was the primary outcome measure, but the power calculation was carried out with death as the outcome of interest) required additional validation.

Data items

Data were extracted from the included studies using a data collection sheet in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Office, Redmond, WA, USA). This captured key information, including study characteristics, population details, intervention details, and outcomes. The individual components of included interventions were extracted verbatim and then in the synthesis and analysis stages thematically coded and grouped.

Synthesis methods

All studies were grouped by intervention type, summarizing and classifying trials according to the mechanism of action of the interventions (for example pharmacological or educational). They were then mapped to the patient perioperative pathway, which was considered as preoperative, intraoperative, or postoperative. Intraoperative was defined as the interval in which a patient goes into an operating theatre or equivalent until the time that they leave the operating theatre or equivalent. Therefore, anaesthetic interventions were classified as intraoperative interventions. Syntheses are displayed in figures mapping mechanism of action against phase of patient pathway and statistical significance against patient pathway. A meta-analysis of proportions was conducted on the adherence data and stratified by simple and complex interventions. The analysis was conducted in R 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The complexity of an intervention was decided upon based on the Medical Research Council (UK) guidance on evaluating complex interventions. A simple intervention was defined as single-component exposure delivered at a discrete time point (for example choice of antibiotic or operative technique). In contrast, complex interventions consisted of multiple interacting components delivered across time or organizational levels, such as enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols.

Data synthesis

After extraction of the individual components of the included interventions, they were thematically coded and grouped. Thematic analysis was performed through coding the themes and discussing these in a forum of a subgroup of the authors (L.N.P., J.G., S.K.K., D.N., and A.A.B.) as a form of member checking. The groupings were used to map each intervention’s mechanism of action across the surgical pathway from the preoperative phase and decision for surgery to postoperative rehabilitation. The adherence data for the interventions were extracted and the implementation strategies used for the interventions were assessed. Finally, the impact on mortality of each of the tested interventions was assessed. In addition to the above, data were assessed for subgroups based on urgency of surgery (planned/unplanned), World Bank country income group (upper-middle-income country/lower-middle-income country/LIC), and indication for surgery (cancer/benign/trauma/obstetrics). There was no planned synthesis of the results from different studies, as the patient populations, surgical specialties, and interventions were likely to be too heterogeneous; instead, the studies and their individual outcomes are described.

Assessment of reporting bias

There was no risk of any bias due to missing results in the above synthesis. To meet the inclusion criteria, the trials had to report on interventions used to modify patient postoperative mortality, meaning that there were no missing data. Study risk of bias was assessed using the Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool. For cluster randomized trials, the cluster-specific RoB 2 domain was applied.

Results

Study and patient characteristics

The database search identified 10 877 studies. After screening and full-text review, 18 studies were included (Fig. 1). Most studies were conducted in upper-middle-income countries or lower-middle-income countries, with only two trials including LICs. Fifteen studies were conducted in a single country, most frequently China (9 studies); the remainder were in Brazil (2 studies), India (3 studies), and Mexico (1 study)21–35. There were three multicountry studies: CORONIS was conducted across Argentina, Chile, Ghana, India, Kenya, Pakistan, and Sudan36; ASOS-2 was conducted across 33 African countries37; and the Zongo et al.38 trial was conducted in Senegal and Mali. This is summarized in Fig. S1. Seven studies were single-centre studies23–25,28,29,32,34. The remainder were conducted at between 5 and 594 sites21,22,26,27,30,31,33,35–38. All trials were individual patient randomized, except for ASOS-2 and the Zongo et al.38 trial, which were cluster randomized. The 18 studies included 92 521 patients. The age inclusion criteria were ≥18 years in 16 studies and ≥14 years in 2 studies. Eight trials included elective patients only, five included emergency patients only, and five included both. A range of specialties were included, the most common being general surgery (5 studies) and obstetrics (3 studies). Twelve trials were conducted exclusively in upper-middle-income countries, three in lower-middle-income countries, and zero in LICs. There were three studies that included countries from a mixture of World Bank income categories: CORONIS (upper-middle income countries, lower-middle income countries, and LICs), ASOS-2 (upper-middle income countries, lower-middle income countries, and LICs), and the Zongo et al.38 trial (lower-middle income countries and LICs). Overall, there were 95 521 patients; 7212 were from upper-middle-income countries, 11 699 were from lower-middle-income countries, and 1593 were from LICs (ASOS-2 and the Zongo et al.38 trials did not report the country-level breakdown of participants, so are not included). Of those with country-level data, China accounted for 47.2% (3407 of 7212) of upper-middle income data. Study characteristics are summarized in Table S3. Most trials had a male predominance and ASA grade I–II patients. Only one trial exclusively included minimally invasive surgery28. Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

The risk of bias was assessed using the RoB 2 tool. Across 18 randomized studies, 4 had an overall low risk of bias, 13 had some concerns, and 1 had a high risk of bias. The most frequent issues were lack of blinding in surgical or care-pathway trials where clinician knowledge could influence care outcome assessment, cluster-specific concerns in cluster trials or analyses, and one non-prespecified post-hoc analysis. In contrast, large, blinded drug and anaesthesia trials with objective outcomes were consistently low risk. In cluster randomized trials, including ASOS-2 and the Zongo et al.38 trial, concerns primarily related to deviations from intended intervention fidelity, with additional concerns related to selective reporting and post-hoc subgroup analyses in the Zongo et al.38 trial. Seven trials assessed an operative technique, although only the trials conducted in neurosurgery demonstrated significant differences in outcomes21,23,28,30–32,36. Sun et al.21 compared minimally invasive surgery with craniopuncture combined with urokinase infusion against craniotomy and clearance of haematoma for those with cerebral haemorrhage. The co-primary outcomes were death and dependence, and the trial was designed to determine intervention superiority. Sun et al.21 found no difference in 14-day mortality, but a significantly higher fatality rate in the craniotomy group at day 90 (36 of 145 (24.9%) versus 23 of 145 (15.9%), chi-squared = 5.350, P = 0.020). Chen et al.32 aimed to demonstrate superiority of controlled versus rapid decompression after craniotomy for severe traumatic brain injury; the co-primary outcomes were 30-day mortality and Extended Glasgow Outcome Scale at 6 months. Chen et al.32 showed a significant difference in 30-day mortality, in favour of controlled decompression (OR 0.51 (95% c.i. 0.27 to 0.96), P = 0.035).

Four trials assessed pharmacological interventions22,25,26,35. None of these showed any significant impact on the primary outcome. Two trials were on patient blood management27,34. Both showed some significant findings and, although the parameters of the trial interventions differed slightly, the results contradicted each other. Liao et al.27 randomly assigned anaemic patients to a restrictive, liberal, or individualized blood transfusion strategy. The latter was guided by the West-China-Liu score. The primary outcome was a composite of in-hospital complications and all-cause mortality by day 30, which did not differ between trial groups. However, the individualized (1.2%) and restrictive (0.8%) strategies had lower all-cause mortality at day 60 than the liberal strategy (4.1%, P = 0.003). However, there was no difference in all-cause mortality at day 30, day 180, or 1 year27. de Almeida et al.34 randomized patients undergoing major surgical procedures for abdominal cancer to assess superiority of either a restrictive or liberal blood transfusion strategy during ICU admission. The primary outcome was a composite endpoint of death from all causes and severe clinical complications within 30 days of randomization, with an absolute risk reduction of 16% (95% c.i. 3.8% to 28.2%) (P = 0.012) for the liberal strategy. de Almeida et al.34 also reported reduced 30- and 60-day mortality in the liberal strategy group compared with the restrictive strategy group (30 days: 8.2% versus 22.8%, P = 0.005; 60 days: 11.3% versus 23.8%, P = 0.022).

The other interventions tested included an ERAS protocol versus standard care in patients undergoing elective hepatopancreatobiliary surgery. ASOS-2 implemented a bundle of interventions to enhance postoperative surveillance in 33 African countries across several specialties in both elective and emergency settings. There was a trial on general anaesthesia with a thoracic epidural block versus general anaesthesia alone in emergency open general surgery operations and, finally, there was a trial on brief (<15 min) focused ultrasonography with predefined treatment recommendations based on ultrasonographic findings versus standard care before emergency non-cardiac surgery. None of these interventions showed any significant difference24,29,33,37. Finally, Zongo et al.38 implemented an educational package including a workshop and quarterly educational clinically oriented and evidence-based outreach visits focused on maternal death reviews and best practice implementation to reduce hospital-based maternal mortality. The exploratory post-hoc analysis, with hospital maternal mortality as the primary outcome, showed no difference in women undergoing a vaginal delivery; however, there was a significant reduction in maternal mortality after caesarean delivery (adjusted OR 0.71 (95% c.i. 0.58 to 0.82), P = 0.0034) compared with controls38. The impact on the primary outcome of all trials is summarized in Table 4, Table S5, and Fig. 3. Some studies were powered for non-inferiority and therefore, while assessing mortality as a primary outcome, they may not have been trying to demonstrate a radical improvement in patient outcomes. The only trial with significant findings that included patients from lower-middle-income countries or LICs was the Zongo et al.38 trial; the other three trials were all from upper-middle-income countries.

When comparing the interventions with positive findings with the literature from HICs, the findings are similar. de Almeida et al.34 favoured a liberal transfusion strategy, which was also supported by Docherty et al.39 in a systematic review that compared restrictive versus liberal transfusion strategies with regard to the outcomes of non-cardiac surgery for those with cardiovascular disease. Docherty et al.39 included seven studies, one of which was the study by de Almeida et al.34. All other studies were from HICs. The study supported the use of a liberal transfusion threshold of >80 g/l, but the effects on mortality and other outcomes were uncertain. However, the TOP superiority randomized trial (USA) did not demonstrate a difference in 90-day death or major ischaemic outcomes for patients at high risk of cardiac events after major vascular or general surgery operations when comparing a liberal with a restrictive transfusion strategy40. Huan et al.41 published a systematic review and network meta-analysis of surgical interventions for spontaneous supratentorial intracerebral haemorrhage. Over a third of the included studies, including the study by Sun et al.21, were from China and 61% (19 of 31) of the studies compared a surgical intervention with a medical treatment and thus would have been excluded from the present review. There are no HIC equivalents of the recent study by Huan et al.41 (which was published in 2025) for comparison. No studies of postoperative death reviews in HICs were found that could be comparable to Zongo et al.38.

Adherence

Adherence to the trial interventions and their components are outlined in Table S6. Although four trials in China achieved an adherence of 100% to the intervention, the larger trials involving multiple countries had poorer adherence. When synthesized using a random-effects model, the pooled adherence rate was 94.8% (95% c.i. 90.3% to 98.0%), although the corresponding prediction interval (69.7% to 100%) was much wider. Subgroup analyses showed similar adherence between simple interventions (93.6% (95% c.i. 90.2% to 96.3%)) and complex interventions (96.8% (95% c.i. 76.3% to 100%)). The test for subgroup differences was non-significant (P = 0.52), suggesting that intervention complexity did not meaningfully affect adherence, a finding that may appear counter-intuitive, but suggests that well-designed, complex interventions can be delivered with high fidelity, even in resource-limited settings. However, heterogeneity was high (I2 = 99.8%), reflecting large differences in adherence across the included trials. The findings are summarized in Fig. 4.

Discussion

There have been many publications highlighting the inequities in access and outcomes between HICs and LMICs1,3,4,6,8,10–12,14,15. However, this review of interventions that were designed to reduce postoperative mortality in LMICs demonstrates that there are only a handful of interventions that have been formally tested and most have not demonstrated a measurable benefit to patients. Notably, half of the studies were conducted in China, a country classified as an upper-middle-income country that is approaching high-income status, but that met the World Bank LMIC criteria during the study interval and therefore fulfilled the eligibility criteria. Nevertheless, its healthcare infrastructure, surgical volume, and research capability differ substantially to many lower-middle-income countries and LICs and its predominance must be considered when interpreting generalizability. Additionally, only three trials included an LIC, all of which were multicountry studies, highlighting these patients are under-represented in the literature. Importantly, the absence of demonstrated benefit in many trials should not be interpreted as evidence of true ineffectiveness; instead, it may reflect low baseline mortality and subsequent limited statistical power or challenges in implementation. The overall number of trials included was small. The WHO publishes the number of trials per year by income group, demonstrating that the number of trials positively correlates with the income group. In 2024, HICs conducted 100 times more trials than LICs (33 293 versus 387 respectively)42.

While there were meaningful reductions in some of the studies, particularly those in the neurosurgical field, the interventions are highly context specific and difficult to generalize to other surgical specialties or healthcare settings21,32. Moreover, the mortality after neurosurgical operations is high, which is likely to make it easier to power trials to demonstrate a significant change in mortality because of an intervention, compared with specialties where the mortality rate is comparably low, such as obstetrics. The other trials that showed significant findings were those by Liao et al.27 and de Almeida et al.34. Both assessed patient blood management interventions and, while different in their intervention parameters, they showed conflicting results, with benefit from liberal blood transfusion strategies in de Almeida et al.34 and benefit from individualized and restrictive strategies of blood transfusion compared with a liberal strategy of blood transfusion in Liao et al.27. Furthermore, Liao et al.27 only showed a benefit at 60 days and not 30 or 180 days, questioning the true effect of the intervention on mortality. While potentially transferable to other contexts and specialties, further research is required in view of the conflicting results. Zongo et al.38 was the only study to introduce an educational package focusing on maternal death reviews and best practice implementation to reduce hospital-based maternal mortality. The literature on the impact of morbidity and mortality reviews/meetings on reducing preventable death is limited; however, Motsumi et al.43 showed that morbidity and mortality reviews/meetings reduced preventable deaths in Botswana. Zongo et al.38 is the only intervention that targeted system change, rather than patient-level change. Rather than representing a specialty-specific solution, the study by Zongo et al.38 should be viewed as an example of a conceptual proof-of-principle study, which shows that system-led strategies, including audit, feedback, and structured implementation of best practice, can reduce postoperative mortality in resource-limited settings. This approach targets failures in recognition and response, rather than individual procedures, and seems plausible and pragmatic to implement across surgical specialties and healthcare contexts. As such, this should be a target for future studies or interventions.

At first glance, the finding that few trials demonstrate a measurable mortality benefit may appear unsurprising, given the well-described scarcity of high-quality surgical trial evidence in LMICs. However, one of the main contributions of this review is that it demonstrates that existing randomized trials have largely focused on highly context-specific, specialty-level, or perioperative technical interventions, many of which are poorly positioned to address the dominant drivers of postoperative mortality in low-resource settings. By systematically mapping interventions across the surgical pathway and income groups, this review highlights a misalignment between where excess mortality occurs and where randomized intervention efforts have been concentrated.

It is understood that 80% of postoperative deaths occur in hospital and half of these are the result of sepsis; however, there were only three trials considering sepsis and its control44. Unfortunately, none of them showed a significant improvement in mortality. Vinodhini et al.25 assessed the impact of antibiotic duration on patients with intra-abdominal infection after a source control procedure, but showed no difference. Li et al.33 implemented ultrasound-guided assessment of haemodynamic shock and respiratory failure before emergency surgery and, although there was no change in 30-day mortality, there were frequent changes in diagnosis and patient management. Finally, ASOS-2 implemented a complex bundle of interventions to increase postoperative surveillance of high-risk patients, although it did not show any difference in mortality37. Given the high proportion of in-hospital deaths after sepsis, this may be a target for future trials, with consideration of interventions in the preoperative phase, with early recognition and management of sepsis followed by source control. This may help to prevent the cascade of events that start from the presentation of a critically unwell patient to hospital and end in preventable postoperative death.

The larger studies have focused mainly on intraoperative and postoperative interventions and, while some of them have showed promising results when adhered to as intended, implementing complex interventions in resource-limited environments proved difficult. The ASOS-2 trial exemplified this challenge; despite showing that large, multicentre randomized trials are feasible in LMICs, implementation fidelity was low. The investigators highlighted the importance of co-designing future interventions with local clinical teams to ensure cultural and logistical alignment, ownership, and consideration of implementation strategies in resource-limited environments to improve implementation fidelity. However, this review does demonstrate that the delivery of RCTs is feasible in LMICs.

The WHO Surgical Safety Checklist is one of the most widely used interventions that is proven to reduce postoperative death. Haynes et al.45 introduced a checklist with three components (sign in, time out, and sign out) in the intraoperative phase; this non-randomized study demonstrated significant reductions in complications (from 11.0% to 7.0%, P < 0.001) and mortality (from 1.5% to 0.8%, P = 0.003). As a result it has been adopted throughout the world and is the standard of care in some settings46. A study in 94 countries showed it was used in over 75% of operations47. There were two notable studies that were ultimately excluded. Chaudhary et al.48 introduced a modified WHO checklist in a randomized setting and showed a reduction in mortality in the checklist arm (5.7% versus 10.0%, P = 0.04), although only 85% of patients had a fully completed checklist. It was excluded as the primary outcome did not meet the inclusion criteria. Naidoo et al.49 introduced a modified WHO surgical safety checklist for maternity patients in South Africa in a cluster randomized setting. The intervention arm showed significant improvements in the adverse incident rate per 1000 procedures, postoperative sepsis, and unscheduled return to theatre, but no change in mortality. Interestingly, when Naidoo et al.49 compared good versus poor implementers of the modified checklist, those sites that were good implementers showed a significant reduction in mortality (incidence rate ratio 0.09 (95% c.i. 0.01 to 0.72), P = 0.023). Checklist-type interventions are likely to be transferable between contexts and specialties, practical to follow, and may be a target for future trials to reduce preventable postoperative mortality. These interventions are mostly assessed in pre-intervention/post-intervention-style studies, a valid approach for evaluating impact; however, these studies are not randomized and therefore are not included in the present review.

Despite improvements in mortality after surgery globally, the greatest change has occurred in HICs. A concerted global effort is urgently needed to reduce preventable postoperative death in LMICs. Research should prioritize pragmatic, embedded randomized trials within routine surgical care, supported by regional LMIC-led trial coordination hubs. Capacity building may be strengthened through task-shifting research roles to non-physician clinicians, adoption of simple trial designs, and transferring trial leadership to local investigators. Emergency surgery, particularly emergency laparotomy, may represent a high impact starting point. Its broad global coverage, large eligible patient population, and high baseline mortality make it a promising focus for trials powered to detect a difference in mortality, unlike many of the smaller studies within this review. Successes in this area may catalyse improvements in other surgical specialties through system strengthening and optimization of perioperative care.

Whilst a thorough search of the literature was conducted, it may not have captured some studies in LMICs that are unpublished or that are published in regional journals not included in the databases used. Several databases were used to minimize this risk. Randomized trials were chosen, aiming to capture interventions most likely to affect policy and practice; however, this means that data from observational studies were not included. Additionally, while there are other study designs that may have advantages in some circumstances, RCTs provide the highest level of evidence by reducing confounding and hence were chosen as the focus of this review. HICs were excluded, although there may be successful interventions trialled in HICs that may be transferable; however, it was felt that the context in which an intervention was tested is important and likely different between HICs and LMICs. Despite this, it is recognized that LMICs is a broad term and contains countries with differing socioeconomic climates and likely diverse clinical environments. Only three trials provided any data from LICs, highlighting the difficulty of gaining data or implementing trials in the world’s most resource-limited and vulnerable regions and patients. Edge and Buccimazza50 recently highlighted some of the ethical and professional issues in global surgery. The control of research in LMICs remains within HICs, but, to meet contemporary ethical standards, research studies should be conceptualized and developed within a context that is of benefit to patients in LMICs. Additionally, research in LMICs may involve ethical committees that are less available and may be more time-consuming and costly, hindering research output. Finally data collection may be dependent on patients travelling substantial distances for follow-up, which may reduce engagement, particularly in rural settings50. Additionally, the list of LMICs changes over time, so the list used in the search terms of the present review may not be representative of all time intervals.

The methodology for the thematic analysis is outlined in the Methods section. The themes were coded and discussed within a forum (member checking) to ensure credibility and transferability; however, it is recognized that another team of researchers may come to different conclusions regarding the interpretation of intervention themes. While there are some interventions that show a meaningful reduction in postoperative mortality, some of the interventions are very specific or relevant to a very subspecialized scenario. As such, they will not move the world forward in terms of preventable postoperative mortality, as they are not transferable to other contexts.

Mortality was chosen as the primary outcome for trials, given the inequitable outcomes in postoperative mortality globally. It could be argued that interventions designed to reduce complications should have been looked at, as they are a precursor to postoperative mortality. However, it is unclear whether this relationship exists and it cannot be assumed that reducing overall complications will lead to a mortality benefit. Furthermore, most studies are underpowered to show a difference in mortality, as it is often a secondary outcome that is not formally powered. Therefore, this review focused on studies looking specifically at mortality as a primary outcome. The predominance of null findings reflects a combination of trials with low baseline morality, resulting in them being underpowered, context-specific, or specialty-level interventions, and challenges with implementation fidelity in resource-constrained environments. By systematically characterizing where, how, and on whom mortality-focused trials have been conducted, this review helps delineate the limitations of prior approaches and identifies key design principles for future interventions. In this context, informing the development of a novel intervention refers not to extrapolating efficacy from positive trials, but to learning from the collective shortcomings of existing evidence. Specifically, the findings support a shift away from isolated technical or perioperative interventions towards pragmatic, system-level strategies that target ‘failure to rescue’, are scalable across settings, and are co-designed with local stakeholders to maximize implementation and sustainability.
