Screening hypertension using non-laboratory risk factors with machine learning: a retrospective cross-sectional study in Indonesia
Authors: Reza Ishak Estiko, Bambang Widyantoro, Dafsah Arifa Juzar, Ramdhan Maulana Yusup, Iqbal Fauzi Rakhmat, Estiko Rijanto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to screen for hypertension in a vast Indonesian population using machine learning (ML) and 11 non-laboratory risk factors, validating the results through internal and external validations.Setting and participantsFrom the initial 1 782 365 participants aged 15 and above registered at the Integrated Counseling Post primary care centres across Indonesia from 2014 to 2017, incomplete data and outliers were excluded, and 268 210 participants were included in our analysis. The dataset was split deterministically into a dataset for training using 10-fold internal cross-validation of 204 315 participants and another dataset for external validation of 63 895 participants.DesignThis retrospective cross-sectional study used three ML algorithms, that is, random forest, gradient boosting and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and compared them against logistic regression as a benchmark to screen hypertension based on the WHO and International Society of Hypertension criteria. The importance of the risk factors was ranked. By partly using continuous versus categorical age, waist circumference (WC) and body mass index (BMI) risk factors, we evaluated the screening performance regarding sensitivity and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsThe external validations revealed that the XGBoost model performed the best in hypertension screening. The external validation, which partly uses continuous variables, provides 0.97 sensitivity and 0.75 AUC, indicating excellent screening capability. The importance rank of the risk factors was consecutively family history of hypertension (FH-HTN), age, WC, BMI, occupation, education, sex, smoking, low physical activity, lack of fruit or vegetable intake and alcohol consumption.ConclusionsBy using 11 easy-to-collect non-laboratory risk factors, the ML model successfully screens for hypertension with better performance than the benchmark. Using the numerical variables of age, WC and BMI yields a better discrimination capability than the categorical variables. FH-HTN and age are the two top risk factors for the development of hypertension. This study is a useful academic exercise and shows ML’s importance in handling large data sets.

Introduction

Developing countries often lack access to primary healthcare programmes for the early detection and treatment of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). As a result, detection in people with high-risk factors is usually late, and people die at a productive age from CVDs.1 To reduce the mortality caused by CVDs, early hypertension detection and management should be performed through routine risk factors surveillance and outcome monitoring.2 The Ministry of Health - Republic of Indonesia (MOH-RI) launched a national non-communicable disease (NCD) surveillance programme. The programme was executed by local community-based organisations known as Integrated Counseling Post (POSBINDU) under the supervision of government-owned local primary healthcare centres called PUSKESMAS. Registered, well-trained POSBINDU workers supervised by medical physicians from PUSKESMAS screened local people for early detection; they recorded local people’s hypertension risk factors through interviews, anthropometric measurements and essential medical check-ups.3

Measuring blood pressure (BP) is a straightforward and definitive method for detecting hypertension.4 However, in practice, this is not always feasible in the context of large-scale community-based screening in Indonesia. Several people have trouble reaching health facilities because of long distances,5 low awareness6 or lack of home blood pressure measurement (HBPM) in every house,7 which contributes to a high number of undiagnosed hypertension cases in Indonesia.8 This operational limitation motivated our study. Finding an early screening model via telemedicine before going to health facilities is necessary, especially for people not receiving regular BP measurements.9 Integrating this telemedicine model into mobile applications such as JKN Mobile by Indonesia’s National Health Insurance or SATUSEHAT Mobile by MOH-RI can benefit the NCD’s early detection and prevention programme.10

Several studies have developed hypertension prediction models using population-level data.1116 However, some of these models excluded important modifiable lifestyle risk factors,11 12 relied on prior diagnoses such as diabetes or high cholesterol,13 14 or lacked generalisability to broader populations.15 16 Many applied artificial intelligence (AI)11 13 16 and machine learning (ML)1216 algorithms in comparison with logistic regression (LR), but some included outcome-related variables, which may bias the predictions. Additionally, none of the studies reported external validation, which is crucial for assessing model performance.12 17 These limitations—particularly in risk factor selection and validation—pose challenges to the practical use of ML in predicting health outcomes.

In contrast to previous studies, we performed hypertension screening in a general population with the hypothesis that the screening can be conducted using a group of variables composed of 11 easy-to-collect non-laboratory risk factors. The key questions of this study are as follows: what is the importance rank of the 11 risk factors, and do ML algorithms outperform the gold standard LR in screening hypertension using such risk factors?

Method

Study population

This retrospective cross-sectional study included 1 782 365 participants aged ≥15 who agreed to participate in the POSBINDU screening programme and were in good health, were at risk or already had NCDs. The participants from the general population across Indonesia visited 3670 POSBINDUs in 315 districts (cities/regencies) in all provinces. The dataset contains participants who visited and registered for the first time at those POSBINDUs. After excluding the observations with incomplete data and outliers using the internal lower edge and internal upper edge, we obtained a dataset of 268 210 participants. Then, we split the dataset deterministically into a dataset for training using 10-fold internal cross-validation of 204 315 participants (from January 2014 to the end of June 2017) and another dataset for the external validation of 63 895 participants (from July to December 2017) (figure 1). Hypertension was diagnosed as systolic BP ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic BP ≥90 mm Hg, based on BP measurements in the office as per the WHO and International Society of Hypertension (ISH) criteria.18 19

Flow of participants.

Data collection

The MOH-RI collected the raw data under the NCD surveillance programme from 2014 to 2017. Standardised questionnaires were administered to well-trained workers at POSBINDU to complete the interviews (online supplemental figure 1).20 The participants’ information regarding age, sex, family history (FH) of hypertension, tobacco smoking status, physical activity, fruit or vegetable consumption, alcohol consumption, education level and occupation was collected. Vital signs and anthropometric measurements were also examined. The definition and measurement procedure of the risk factors refer to the NCD risk factor measurement guidebooks released by the MOH-RI.20 21

FH of hypertension (FH-HTN) refers to the hypertension history of the participant’s family, that is, if one of the participant’s parents, uncle/aunt or grandparent had hypertension. Smoking is defined as the routine smoking of tobacco. Alcohol consumption refers to drinking alcohol routinely or if the participant drank at least one glass of alcohol in the most recent month. The consumption of fruits or vegetables (FVs) per day is defined as at least five servings of FVs per day. Physical activity (PA) refers to a minimum of 30 min per day or 150 min per week of exercise.20 Education was categorised into four levels: (1) no formal education, (2) elementary and junior school, (3) senior high school and (4) university. Occupation refers to the participant’s occupation type. Based on physical intensity, we classified occupations into four levels: (1) high, (2) intermediate, (3) low and (4) unspecified.22

Anthropometric information and BP were measured by well-trained workers based on the NCD risk factor measurement guidebook. The anthropometric measurements included height, weight and waist circumference (WC). The participants were required to wear light clothes and maintain a correct standing posture to reduce bias during the measurement. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated as per the standard formula (BMI=weight/height2 (kg/m2)). WC was measured using a tape around the participant for one circle 1 cm above the navel.20 BP was measured using a validated digital BP upper-arm cuff monitor. Before the measurement, the respondents were prepared to avoid PA, smoking or eating for at least 30 min and sit down for 5–15 min. While in the measurement table, respondents were instructed to sit in a back-supported chair with their feet flat on the floor, put their resting bare arm on the table and roll up their sleeves as per ISH guideline.19 21

Data analysis

Preprocessing training data was performed before developing the hypertension screening models. Observations containing noise, inconsistencies or outliers were removed. P values were calculated using χ2 tests and independent Mann-Whitney t-tests. Only the risk factors statistically significantly related to hypertension were considered in the development of the model. We compared the statistics of the training and the external validation datasets in terms of means, SD, percentage and p values association with hypertensive and normotensive participants. We also performed an importance rank analysis of 11 non-laboratory risk factors.

Algorithms

LR remains the most popular traditional method in hypertension prediction models, as reported by Chowdhury et al.17 However, it requires assumptions, such as a linear relationship between continuous independent variables and the outcome, and no collinearity between independent variables.23 In contrast, ML algorithms have advantages over LR because they are free from such stringent assumptions. Studies that employed ML or AI to predict hypertension—using population-based, non-laboratory risk factors resembling ours— reported that random forest (RF), gradient boosting (GB) and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) were among the best-performing algorithms.12 13 15 16 They are all ensemble methods based on decision trees (DTs) which are superior in handling non-linear relationships, complex interactions and high-dimensional data. Accordingly, we selected RF, GB and XGBoost as the ML algorithms in our study. Moreover, those studies consistently compared the performance of their models against LR; therefore, we adopted LR as the benchmark.

RF is a popular ensemble ML algorithm with DTs as the base classifier; it is generated by training multiple DTs using randomly selected observation and feature samples. A subset of the features is randomly selected to search for the best split at each node. This approach reduces the variance in DTs and improves the classification capability of a dataset with complex interactions.24

GB finds an approximation function Fxi that maps instances xi to outcome yi by minimising a given loss function. A boosting algorithm iteratively combines a weak learner, hkxi, into a strong learner, Fkxi, using an additive operation.

γk is the weight of the kth learner function hkx. When simple algebra does not calculate the minimum loss function, gradient descent is employed. It is a first-order iterative optimisation algorithm that is used to search for the minimum value of a function and is calculated for the model parameter.25 The loss function gradient is calculated for the predicted value. The calculation steps of GB can be found in an article on predictive analytics in clinical medicine.26

Similar to GB, XGBoost builds an additive expansion of the approximation to minimise a regularised objective using DTs as base classifiers. The predicted output y^i is given by an approximation function Fxi as a tree ensemble model using additive functions.27

To control the complexity of the trees, XGBoost adapts the following regularised objective Legb.

The regularisation term Ω penalises the complexity of the model and selects a model employing simple functions. T and w are the number of leaves in the tree and the output scores of the leaves, respectively. γ controls the minimum loss reduction gain required to split an internal node. The model training is fast owing to tree complexity reduction.

Model development

Age, BMI and WC were originally continuous numerical variables. For the conventional analysis, these three variables were converted into categorical variables. Age was converted to five categorical variable levels through z-transformation. We categorised BMI into five levels as per the WHO in Southeast Asia: (1) underweight, <18.5; (2) normal, 18.5–22.9; (3) overweight, 23–24.9; (4) obesity class I, 25–29.9; and (5) obesity class II, ≥30.28 We categorised WC into two levels as per the WHO 2006 and the MOH-RI. For WC in males: (1) normal, <90 cm, and (2) central obesity, ≥90 cm, whereas for females: (1) normal, <80 cm, and (2) central obesity, ≥80 cm.21 29

We developed two types of hypertension screening models: the Type One model, which is based on categorical variables, and the Type Two model, which is partly based on continuous variables of age, BMI and WC. We verified hypertension screening performances through internal and external validations to ensure reliability.

Figure 2 shows our model development pipeline, which comprises three primary steps: data preparation, model training and external validation. The model training step is performed via a stratified optimisation protocol, where we optimise the hyperparameter values using a grid search at the high level. In contrast, at the low level, we execute a 10-fold internal cross-validation. At the end of the training step, the optimal hyperparameter values, optimal model parameter values and internal validation performances were recorded. Then, we performed external validation using a new dataset, that is, a dataset not considered in the model training.

We used IBM SPSS Statistics V.29.0 to conduct the analyses and Python V.3.11.7 software to construct the screening models. We used the Scikit-learn package, which provides ML algorithms to non-specialists from which the source code and documentation can be downloaded.30

Performances regarding sensitivity, accuracy, specificity, precision, F1-score and area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) were evaluated. For screening purposes in primary care, the model must have a highly sensitive value to detect a true positive, reflecting the ability to catch as many cases as possible and avoid missed diagnoses.31

Multivariate analysis

The multivariate analysis results in table 3 reveal that hypertension development is significantly associated with all the easy-to-collect non-laboratory risk factors (p≤0.05). The FH-HTN is the most significant risk factor (OR 10.688, 95% CI 10.343 to 11.044). Age increases the risk of developing hypertension (from OR 1.371, 95% CI 1.313 to 1.432 to OR 3.678, 95% CI 3.429 to 3.946). Notably, each age group had a significant difference in OR elevation. The likelihood of hypertension also increased with BMI (from OR 1.053, 95% CI 1.005 to 1.104 to OR 1.591, 95% CI 1.492 to 1.697). Although low PA was found to increase the risk of hypertension, this result shows that hypertension increases with occupation physical intensity (from OR 1.173, 95% CI 1.116 to 1.233 to OR 1.326, 95% CI 1.281 to 1.373). Conversely, all education levels exhibited a significant protective effect against hypertension development (p<0.001), with higher education levels exhibiting a more significant protective effect (from OR 0.865, 95% CI 0.828 to 0.903 to OR 0.661, 95% CI 0.620 to 0.705).

Screening performance

Table 4 shows the internal and external validation performances of the developed models. In the Type One group, LR, GB and XGBoost outperformed each other in several aspects, either in internal or external validation. In the internal validation, XGBoost had the best sensitivity (0.9683), accuracy (0.8307) and AUC (0.7625, 95% CI 0.7544 to 0.7707), while LR had the best specificity (0.3502), precision (0.8423) and F1-score (0.6850). In the external validation, XGBoost had the best sensitivity (0.9736) and AUC (0.7424, 95% CI 0.7381 to 0.7467), GB had the best accuracy (0.8480) and LR had the best specificity (0.3170), precision (0.8611) and F1-score (0.6746).

Risk factor importance

To assess the predictive contribution of risk factors on the models, we analysed the importance rank of each risk factor for hypertension based on the average decrease in AUC following permutation (figure 4). Among the top five ranks, FH-HTN has the most significant reduction in AUC when permuted, suggesting that it is the most influential factor of hypertension, followed by age, WC, BMI and occupation physical intensity (Occupat). The 6th to 11th ranks were education level (Educat), sex, smoking, low PA, lack of fruit or vegetable intake (low FV) and alcohol consumption.

Discussion

Performance comparison of hypertension prediction/screening models

Our study demonstrates that the XGBoost model provides the best sensitivity, accuracy and AUC across all scenarios—both internal and external validations using Type One or Type Two models—except in one case: the accuracy in the external validation of the Type One model, where the GB model slightly outperforms XGBoost. As shown in table 4 and figure 3 (a) and (b), Type Two models consistently yield higher AUC and accuracy values than Type One models. Notably, Type Two models do not require the categorisation of numerical variables, making them more efficient by reducing preprocessing needs.

Online supplemental table 1 compares our best screening model with those from previous studies.1116 Wang et al11 developed an artificial neural network (ANN) model incorporating questionnaire-based risk factors, including a history of diabetes and hyperlipidaemia, and found that ANN outperformed LR. AlKaabi et al15 identified 11 risk factors, including a history of diabetes and cholesterol, using a relatively young, educated and affluent Qatari cohort—which limits generalisability. They concluded that RF performed better than DT and LR. Islam et al13 used data from urban Ethiopia, including 20 risk factors such as a history and presence of diabetes, and found that XGBoost outperformed ANN, RF and LR. Kurniawan et al14 included a history of diabetes and cholesterol and reported better performance for LR over DT, RF and GB. These clinical histories require laboratory tests and high health literacy, which may not be feasible in low-resource settings. Our study population had low education levels—58.19% had no formal education or had only completed elementary or junior high school—highlighting the need for simpler, non-laboratory models.

Islam et al12 used data from India, Nepal and Bangladesh and found GB superior to other ML algorithms, including XGBoost and LR. However, they included outcome-informative features such as ‘ever-measured BP’ and ‘doctor’s perception of high BP’, which may introduce data leakage. Kurniawan et al14 similarly used elevated HBPM, also prone to this bias.32 33

Zhao et al16 used non-laboratory features similar to ours, including psychological pressure in place of education level, and found RF to be the best model. However, their study population was limited to individuals aged 20–70 living near Beijing Local Hospital, China. Their results, based on pseudo-external validation, showed a sensitivity of 0.83. In contrast, our external validation achieved a sensitivity of 0.97, indicating superior screening capability.

Internal validation: the dataset is randomly divided into N equal subsets, each with a balanced proportion of hypertensive cases. N-1 subsets are used for training, and the remaining one for testing. Performance is evaluated using N-fold cross-validation.12

Our study pioneered the use of ML for hypertension screening, which was verified through internal and external validation.

Importance rank of hypertension risk factors in the Indonesian population

Our findings highlight genetics as a key non-modifiable risk factor, consistent with Niiranen et al.34 Age-related vascular stiffening35 and the association between WC and abnormal BP36 further support known clinical evidence. BMI may cause hypertension and other CVDs through renin-aldosterone activation and endothelial dysfunction.37 High occupational PA may increase heart rate, BP and hypertension risk.38

Low education is associated with poor awareness of hypertension and its risk factors.39 Sex differences in incidence may stem from biological and behavioural variations.40 Smoking triggers sympathetic activation and arterial stiffness,41 while low PA42 and insufficient FV intake43 are linked to higher hypertension risk. Although alcohol is a known risk factor,44 only 1.22% of our sample reported alcohol consumption. In line with this, our feature importance analysis ranked drinking alcohol as the least influential risk factor, likely reflecting Indonesia’s religious norms.

Limitations and future outlook

The participants were selected through consecutive sampling, indicating they were already aware of healthcare or counselling services. The low prevalence of smoking and alcohol consumption in our sample also limits generalisability to broader populations. Moreover, only 15% of the original dataset was used, which may restrict the potential of ML models to perform optimally.

Our external validation did not expose differences in ethnicity or geography, and the external validation population was limited to 63 895 participants. Although this study is a valuable academic exercise and an ML experiment, its clinical applicability remains uncertain. Broader external validation involving more diverse populations is needed.

Future studies should address missing data to maximise ML potential as the sample size grows.45 A previous article reported that ANN achieved slightly higher AUC than other ML algorithms.46 ANN, as a non-linear learning algorithm and early form of AI, could be further explored by tuning essential hyperparameters. Our previous review also identified support vector machine (SVM) as a promising algorithm for hypertension prediction.47 As a fundamentally different ML approach from ensemble ML and ANN, SVM warrants further investigation in future studies.

Conclusion

In Indonesia, with the fourth largest population in the world and limited access to BP measurement instruments at home, early hypertension screening is required to prevent the development of hypertension-related catastrophic diseases. From this study, we obtained the following conclusions. The 11 easy-to-collect, non-laboratory risk factors are determinants of hypertension, where the FH-HTN is the most essential, followed by age, WC, BMI, occupation, education level, sex, smoking status, low PA, lack of FV intake and alcohol consumption. When evaluated through internal and external validations, XGBoost performed better in hypertension screening than RF, GB and traditional LR, as indicated by a substantially high sensitivity value. Furthermore, the hypertension screening models trained partly using age, WC and BMI as continuous numerical risk factors possessed better discrimination capability than those trained as categorical ones.

Some topics for future studies include handling missing data and exploring other ML algorithms, such as ANN and SVM, which have different characteristics than the ensemble ML algorithms used in this study.

We recommend building an information system for hypertension screening that enables physicians to further validate the clinical utility and enhance the models’ performance by inputting new data for autonomous continuing model training and validation.
