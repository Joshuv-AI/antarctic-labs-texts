Compound delivery of eVLPs enhances prime editing for targeted genome engineering and high-throughput screening
Authors: Jethro Langley, Lou Baudrier, Jada Curry, Kiran Narta, Hayley M Todesco, Kyle Potts, Sorana Morrissy, Douglas J Mahoney, Pierre Billon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEngineered virus-like particles (eVLPs) enable transgene-free ribonucleoprotein delivery for genome editing, yet optimized strategies for high-throughput applications remain unexplored. Prime editing enables precise genomic modifications but suffers from limited efficiency. Here, we present PRIME-VLP (Progressive Repeated Infections for Maximized Editing via Virus-Like Particles), which exploits eVLP-mediated editing kinetics through multiple sequential sub-saturating transductions at optimal intervals. PRIME-VLP achieves 1.5- to 2.9-fold improvements in prime editing efficiency across diverse genomic targets and cell types without increasing off-target editing, compromising cellular viability or causing transcriptional perturbations. By decoupling pegRNA and editor delivery through pegRNA-free eVLPs, PRIME-VLP enables pooled prime editing screens and circumvents transgene silencing limitations. Using a 6,000-pegRNA library targeting TP53, PRIME-VLP achieved 2.8-fold higher editing and improved reproducibility compared to conventional lentiviral delivery, identifying TP53 loss-of-function variants conferring Nutlin-3 resistance. This work expands the versatility of eVLPs beyond their current in vivo therapeutic applications, demonstrating their promise for high-throughput functional genomics.Keywords: prime editing, engineered virus-like particles, CRISPR, genome editing, ribonucleoprotein delivery, high-throughput screening, functional genomics, variant characterization, variants of uncertain significance

Introduction

The exponential growth in genomic sequencing has identified genetic variants across diverse populations and disease contexts,1,2,3,4 yet most remain variants of uncertain significance due to limited functional interpretation, restricting clinical application.5,6 High-throughput functional genomics screens elucidate gene function at scale in native genomic contexts.7,8 However, characterizing individual variants requires transitioning from gene-level perturbations to single-nucleotide precision. Recent advances in base editing9,10 and prime editing11 have enabled systematic evaluation of genetic variants. Prime editing is particularly promising because it can install base substitutions, insertions, or deletions without double-strand breaks or donor templates.12,13 This versatility offers a unique opportunity to characterize variants not yet linked to human variation or disease. Despite this versatility, the widespread adoption of prime editing in precision genome editing and high-throughput applications has been constrained by low editing efficiency.11,12 Recent strategies to enhance prime editing include pegRNA architecture optimization,14,15,16,17 nicking strategies,11 transient repair pathway inhibition,18,19 editor component engineering,19,20,21,22,23 metabolic modulation,24 and co-selection.25,26 However, achieving reproducible high editing across thousands of targets in large-scale functional screens remains challenging.

A primary reason is the difficulty of delivering large prime editing components in current screening methods. Given low prime editing efficiency, stable high-level editor expression is required to accumulate edits over extended periods of up to a month.27 Existing approaches rely predominantly on lentiviral integration28,29,30,31 of large editor constructs. However, lentiviral delivery has three major limitations: (1) variable transgene expression due to semi-random integration,31,32 (2) progressive silencing through promoter methylation and chromatin repression,33,34 and (3) packaging limits causing low functional titers35,36,37 or requiring complex multi-vector strategies.11 These limitations lead to inconsistent editing and poor reproducibility. Recent prime editing screens26,27,28,29,30,31,38,39,40,41,42 highlight these challenges, often requiring site-specific editor integration,27 single-cell cloning to isolate high-expressing populations,26,30,31,38,39 disrupting mismatch repair,27 or co-selection.26 Thus, the lack of an effective delivery platform remains a critical bottleneck for prime editing in functional genomics.

These limitations highlight the need to bypass transgene integration. Engineered virus-like particles (eVLPs) are an emerging modular ribonucleoprotein (RNP) delivery system for genome editing.43,44,45,46,47,48,49 Recent studies show that eVLPs can deliver Cas9,41,43,46,47,48 base editors,41,43,49 and prime editors42,44,49 to diverse cell types and tissues.43,44,49 Unlike lentiviral vectors, eVLPs package functional RNP complexes by fusing editors to viral structural proteins during particle assembly and delivering pre-assembled complexes that function immediately without transcription or translation. eVLPs also offer scalable production, large-protein-complex packaging, compatibility with diverse viral architectures,43,44,45,46,47,49,50 and flexible tropism for cell-specific targeting.43,46,47,48,51,52 Although therapeutically promising, their use in high-throughput CRISPR-based screens remains unexplored, leaving questions concerning large-scale editor delivery, screening compatibility, and cellular perturbation unresolved.

To overcome these constraints, we developed an eVLP-based delivery method that leverages RNP editing dynamics. We analyzed eVLP-mediated editing kinetics and dose-response relationships, providing the rationale for optimally timed sequential deliveries that maintain editor levels while avoiding saturation. This led to Progressive Repeated Infections for Maximized Editing via Virus-Like Particles (PRIME-VLP), a compound-delivery strategy using multiple temporally spaced, sub-saturating eVLP doses to enhance prime editing. PRIME-VLP achieves 1.5- to 2.9-fold improvements across diverse targets and cell types without increasing off-target effects, compromising viability, or disrupting gene expression. To enable pooled screening, we developed a decoupling strategy that separates editor and pegRNA delivery, circumventing lentiviral limitations. We demonstrate this approach through a 6,000-pegRNA library screen targeting TP53, achieving 2.8-fold superior performance compared to lentiviral delivery and identifyingTP53 loss-of-function variants conferring resistance to Nutlin-3. This work expands eVLP versatility beyond therapeutic applications, demonstrating promise for functional genomics.

Results

Development and characterization of eVLPs for precision genome editing

To evaluate eVLPs as a delivery platform for precision genome editing, we adapted a previously described Moloney murine leukemia virus (MMLV) Gag-fusion approach using an adenine base editor (ABE8e)43 to package additional CRISPR-based genome editors, including a cytosine base editor (tadCBEd) and a prime editor (PE2max) (Figure 1A). eVLPs were produced following established protocols,43,44 yielding a 100 μL preparation from the standard production scale. Characterization across independent batches confirmed consistent particle concentrations, size distributions, and batch-to-batch reproducibility, with volume-based normalization equivalent to particle-normalized dosing (Figures S1A–S1E), consistent with current practice.43,44,53,54 Having established consistent eVLP production and packaging, we next sought to validate their editing performance.

We benchmarked eVLP editing against plasmid transfection using TransIT-293 and Lipofectamine 2000 protocols.55,56 A full 100 μL eVLP preparation (4 × 1011 particles) was used to ensure maximal editing, with editing assessed at days 3 and 7 to account for the sustained transgene from plasmid-based delivery (Figure S1F). At day 7, eVLP delivery achieved significantly higher editing efficiencies (76.6%, 59%, and 39.4%) than plasmid transfection (18.9%, 24.5%, and 28.7%), representing 1.4- to 4.1-fold improvements across all three editors (Figures 1B and 1C) while maintaining high cell viability profiles (Figures 1B–1D). Notably, eVLPs and Lipofectamine 2000 achieved comparable prime editing at day 7, suggesting that efficiency may be constrained by the short window of intracellular RNP availability rather than by intrinsic editor limitations.43,44 To test this, we analyzed PE2max-eVLP editing kinetics across three independent genomic loci over 5 days. All three loci exhibited similar kinetics, with editing increasing rapidly over the first 2 days before plateauing by day 3 (Figure 1E), at which point 91% of total edits had occurred (Figures 1F and S1G). This rapid editing profile, consistent with the short half-life of RNP complexes in cells57 and DNA repair kinetics,58 confirmed that RNP availability is limiting. Given that prime editing can benefit from sustained editor activity through high expression for prolonged periods,27,29,59,60 we hypothesized that sequentially timed, repeated deliveries could replenish intracellular RNP levels and enhance overall editing efficiency.

Compound delivery of eVLPs overcomes editing saturation and enhances prime editing efficiency

To optimize a dosing strategy for sequential PE2max-eVLP transductions, we first characterized the dose-response relationship between eVLP input and editing efficiency. To assess dose-dependent effects, we titrated PE2max-eVLPs from 5 to 100 μL across three targets (Figure 2A). Editing efficiency at 3 days post-transduction showed non-linear dose-dependent responses at all three genomic loci (Figures 2B and S2A–S2C). Editing efficiency increased from 9.4% at the lowest dose to 20.9% at intermediate doses, then plateaued at 24.5% even after increasing to 100 μL. Critically, at 25 μL, editing reached 92.9% of the efficiency observed at 100 μL, indicating that sub-saturating doses achieve near-maximal editing (Figure 2C). This saturation effect was consistent across all targeted loci despite different maximal editing efficiencies.

Since eVLPs lack integrative nucleic acids, they should bypass innate immune mechanisms that prevent viral superinfection.61 We tested this using sequential editing of two genomic loci (Figure 2D). First, HEK293T cells harboring a GFP stop-codon reporter were transduced with ABE-eVLPs containing a gRNA that restored GFP expression, resulting in 23.8% GFP restoration (Figure 2D, left). GFP+ cells were sorted to isolate a population that had successfully undergone primary transduction and retransduced with tadCBEd-eVLPs targeting endogenous RASA2. TadCBEd was used to avoid potential confounding from residual ABE activity. RASA2 editing was comparable between the secondary infection (80%) and primary transduction of naive cells (90%) (Figure 2D, right), demonstrating that cells remain permissive to eVLP retransduction. In primary human T cells, sequential dosing with tadCBEd-eVLPs also improved editing, indicating that primary cells with intact innate immune responses remain permissive to eVLP-mediated editing (Figure S2D). Thus, sequential eVLP transductions are feasible without loss of cellular permissiveness.

Having confirmed feasibility, we optimized the intervals between eVLP transductions to maximize cumulative editing efficiency. Cells underwent four rounds of PE2max-eVLP transduction, varying the transduction interval from 1 to 5 days (Figure 2E). Editing efficiency increased progressively with longer intervals up to 3 days (Figure 2F, green background), beyond which additional days provided little additional benefit (Figure 2F, pink background). Specifically, intervals of 1–3 days between transductions contributed 51.9%, 31.6%, and 13% of the maximal editing, respectively (Figure 2G). In contrast, days 4 and 5 contributed to only 2.7% and 0.8%, indicating a plateau in cumulative editing frequency beyond the 3-day interval (Figure 2G). These results confirmed that peak efficiency occurs with 3-day intervals, providing sufficient time for each round of editing to complete while maintaining optimal efficiency. This timing aligns with our kinetics data, which show that editing reaches maximum levels within 3 days (Figures 1E and 1F), providing a mechanistic foundation for our compound-delivery approach.

PRIME-VLP improves editing efficiency through compound eVLP transductions

To evaluate PRIME-VLP performance, we compared prime editing across six endogenous targets using either a single saturating dose of eVLPs (100 μL, ∼4 × 1011 particles) or the same total particles delivered via the PRIME-VLP strategy (four sequential transductions of 25 μL each). To eliminate technical variability between conditions, eVLPs were pooled from multiple preparations (Figure 3A). Next-generation sequencing (NGS) analysis revealed that PRIME-VLP outperformed the standard approach across all targets by 1.51- to 2.33-fold across five independent biological replicates (Figures 3B and 3C). PRIME-VLP achieved significantly higher editing than single high-dose delivery at all six loci, with improvements observed across targets with widely varying baseline efficiencies.

To assess broader applicability, we compared PRIME-VLP against single-dose delivery in three additional human cell lines (HAP1, A549, and RPE-1). We employed the PCNA +2 AA>CG pegRNA for this analysis, as it demonstrated a modest improvement (1.74-fold) among our initial targets (Figure 3B), thus providing a representative baseline to assess PRIME-VLP performance. PRIME-VLP consistently enhanced editing across all cell lines (HEK293T, 58%–85%; HAP1, 9%–26%; A549, 29%–60%; and RPE-1, 15%–39%) (Figure 3D). Because mismatch repair competency reduces prime editing in primary human T cells,18,19,62 we tested whether PRIME-VLP could be applied to primary cells using base editing. While single high-dose eVLP delivery yielded robust editing across all targets (average: 31.9%), PRIME-VLP further enhanced editing to 44.6%, a 1.4-fold improvement (Figure S3).

Finally, we assessed whether the PRIME-VLP advantage extends to next-generation prime editor variants by comparing PE2max, PE7, and vPE under single-dose and compound-delivery conditions (Figure 3E). PRIME-VLP significantly enhanced editing across all editors, including PE7, which already achieved high baseline editing with a single dose (83.3%), demonstrating broad compatibility with next-generation prime editors.

Altogether, these results demonstrate that PRIME-VLP enhances editing across diverse genomic loci, cell lines, including primary human T cells, and genome editing platforms, establishing its versatility for precision genome editing applications.

Off-target analysis demonstrates enhanced on-target precision of PRIME-VLP

Enhanced editing efficiency could exacerbate off-target activity through prolonged or repeated editor exposure. To address this, we analyzed off-target editing using two promiscuous gRNAs with established off-target profiles11 and compared PRIME-VLP to single high-dose transduction. As a positive control, we delivered Cas9 nuclease via plasmid transfection or eVLPs and measured editing using NGS (Figure 4A). As expected, plasmid delivery of Cas9 generated on-target insertions or deletions (indels; 34.4% at HEK3 and 16% at HEK4) and substantial off-target editing, including one HEK4 off-target site exceeding the on-target site (off-target site 3: 59.4%) (Figure 4A). Cas9-eVLP delivery enhanced on-target editing (up to 98.4% for HEK3 and 99.5% for HEK4) while retaining activity at all off-target sites, confirming both the robust editing capacity of eVLPs and the promiscuous nature of these gRNAs (Figure 4A).

We next compared on- and off-target activities between PRIME-VLP (4 × 25 μL) and the same number of particles in a single high-dose PE2max-eVLP transduction (1 × 100 μL) (Figure 4B). Consistent with our previous observations (Figure 3), PRIME-VLP increased on-target editing with a nearly 2-fold increase for both pegRNAs (45%–81.7% for HEK3 and 5.2%–9.2% for HEK4) (Figure 4B). Critically, this substantial increase in on-target editing was not accompanied by detectable increases in off-target editing at any of the 8 off-target sites examined. Indeed, PRIME-VLP significantly improved (reduced) the off-target-to-on-target editing ratios compared to single high-dose delivery at both HEK3 (Figure 4C) and HEK4 (Figure 4D), demonstrating that compound delivery enhances on-target editing specificity rather than compromising it. These results establish PRIME-VLP as a delivery strategy that simultaneously improves on-target editing efficiency and specificity compared to single high-dose delivery.

Transcriptome and cellular viability analyses demonstrate minimal cellular perturbation

Beyond off-target editing, repeated eVLP transductions could perturb cellular physiology or gene expression. We therefore performed transcriptome and viability assessments. Using bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), we compared transcriptional profiles of primary human T cells from four healthy donors treated with a single high-dose eVLP (100 μL) or with the same number of particles using PRIME-VLP (4 × 25 μL), relative to untreated control cells (Figure 4E). To capture acute and sustained transcriptional responses, cells were harvested 3 days after the initial transduction and after the final transduction, enabling the detection of immediate cellular stress responses and long-term transcriptional dysregulation (Figure 4E).

Differential expression analysis revealed modest transcriptional perturbations on day 3, with single high-dose transduction inducing a stronger response than PRIME-VLP (Figure 4F), likely reflecting the 4-fold higher particle exposure at this early point, further justifying the importance of using optimized sub-saturating doses. By day 12, primary T cells returned to baseline transcriptional state (Figure 4F), and fGSEA analysis63 detected no pathway-level changes in T cell activation, apoptosis, or exhaustion (Figures S4A and S4B). Principal-component analysis confirmed that variance was driven primarily by time point (92%) and inter-donor variability (5%) rather than by delivery method (Figures S4C and S4D).

To assess whether eVLP delivery affected viability, primary human T cells and HEK293T cells were treated with a single high-dose transduction (1 × 100 μL) or PRIME-VLP (4 × 25 μL) (Figure S5A), and cell viability was assessed on day 3, coinciding with the transcriptional response peak. No significant differences in T cell viability were observed compared to untreated controls (Figure S5B). T cell expansion transiently decreased between days 3 and 6 in eVLP-treated samples but was equivalent between PRIME-VLP and high-dose conditions and did not persist beyond day 6 (Figure S5C). Similarly, HEK293T cells monitored at regular intervals showed that neither approach affected cell viability relative to untreated controls (Figure S5C).

Collectively, these analyses demonstrate that PRIME-VLP maintains cellular homeostasis across multiple endpoints, inducing only transient, mild transcriptional responses that resolve without pathway-level disruption or sustained viability effects.

Decoupling editor and (pe)gRNA delivery enables high-throughput prime editing screens using eVLPs

Having demonstrated that PRIME-VLP enhances prime editing efficiency without compromising specificity or cellular fitness, we sought to adapt it to high-throughput prime editing screens, where low editing efficiencies represent a major technical barrier.27,29,30,64 Although previous work identified pegRNA packaging as a key limitation in eVLP-based delivery systems44,49 and developed aptamer-based approaches, such as PP749 and MS2,44 to improve pegRNA packaging per particle, we decided to pursue a fundamentally opposite approach: pegRNA-free particles that decouple the editor and pegRNA delivery. We hypothesized that eVLPs could either deliver pegRNA-free editors or editors complexed with a non-targeting pegRNA that could be functionally exchanged by a lentivirally delivered pegRNA library, as previously demonstrated with gRNAs and Cas9 RNP electroporation.65

To test pegRNA swapping, we produced PE2max-eVLPs containing targeting pegRNAs (positive control), non-targeting pegRNAs, or no pegRNAs. We hypothesized that eVLPs lacking targeting pegRNAs could still achieve editing when targeting pegRNAs were delivered independently via lentivirus (Figure 5A). Using three pegRNAs encoding distinct edit types (HEK3 +1 CTTins, HEK3 +1 T del, and PCNA +2 AA>CG), we observed successful pegRNA exchange across all conditions. Targeting pegRNA-loaded eVLPs achieved 73.6% average editing (range: 55.9%–97.3%) (Figure 5B). Editors delivered with non-targeting pegRNAs successfully swapped them for lentivirally delivered targeting pegRNAs, achieving an average editing of 68.7% (range: 50.8%–91.4%), only a modest reduction compared to the positive control (Figure 5B). Notably, pegRNA-free eVLPs retained substantial activity, indicating that while non-targeting pegRNAs enhance prime editor stability and editing efficiency, they are not strictly required for functional editing.

To assess the generalizability of (pe)gRNA decoupling across CRISPR systems, we used ABE-eVLPs targeting three independent loci (HEK3, RASA2, and FANCF) (Figure S6A). ABE-VLPs showed similar patterns: particles containing targeting gRNAs achieved 95.4% average editing, non-targeting gRNAs reached 76.0%, and gRNA-free particles achieved 68.0% (Figure S6B), demonstrating broad applicability across CRISPR-based editing platforms.

PRIME-VLP enhances high-throughput prime editing screens

To evaluate PRIME-VLP in pooled prime editing screens, we constructed a miniature (mini) pegRNA sensor library that pairs each pegRNA with its surrogate target sequence, enabling high-throughput quantification of editing by NGS (Figure 5C). We optimized non-targeting pegRNA and pegRNA-free eVLP dosing for pooled screening applications across HEK293T, RPE-1, K562, and HCT116 cells transduced with the mini library at a low multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.3. Following library selection, cells were treated with 25–100 μL of pegRNA-free PE2max-eVLPs. Editing slightly increased between 25 and 50 μL before saturating at higher doses across all cell lines (Figure S7A), identifying 50 μL as the optimal dose for decoupled PRIME-VLP. We also compared our PE2max-eVLPs with the eVLPv2.3 system44 across four cell lines and found comparable performance, with v.2.3 achieving a modest 1.09-fold improvement (Figures S7B and S7C). We therefore used PE2max-eVLPv2.3 with 50 μL dosing for pooled screens.

To benchmark PRIME-VLP against established methodologies, we directly compared eVLP-mediated and conventional lentiviral editor delivery in a pooled prime editing sensor screen. HEK293T cells were transduced with the pegRNA library and treated with prime editors delivered via lentivirus (Lenti), eVLPs loaded with a non-targeting pegRNA (NT), or pegRNA-free eVLPs (pegRNA-free). Over a 24-day screening experiment comprising eight PRIME-VLP transductions, we harvested cells at multiple time points (days 6, 15, and 24) to assess temporal editing dynamics. Lentiviral delivery of PE2max exhibited highly variable editing efficiency ranging from 0.1% to 80.1% across different loci, with a mean of 14.8% (Figure 5D). In contrast, PRIME-VLP consistently outperformed lentivirus at nearly every locus, with pegRNA-free and NT conditions achieving average editing efficiencies of 29.1% and 27.3%, respectively, approximately 2-fold above lentiviral delivery (Figure 5D). These results highlight PRIME-VLP as a scalable high-throughput editing platform and demonstrate robust prime editing without pre-loading pegRNAs into eVLPs.

To understand the mechanism underlying PRIME-VLP’s superior performance, we analyzed the temporal dynamics of editing accumulation. Both PRIME-VLP approaches (pegRNA-free and NT) demonstrated robust initial editing (day 6), followed by sustained accumulation throughout the experiment (slope = 0.42 and 0.36) (Figure 5E). In contrast, lentiviral delivery of PE2max exhibited minimal editing accumulation beyond day 6 (slope = 0.04), suggesting a plateau in editing efficiency after the initial week (Figure 5E). This temporal difference suggested that lentiviral PE2max expression might decline over time, potentially due to transgene silencing.33,34,66

To test this hypothesis, we measured PE2max expression over time in multiple cell lines. We transduced HEK293T, A549, HAP1, HCT116, and K562 cells with PE2max lentivirus, selected for stable integration, and quantified PE2max expression relative to GAPDH by RT-qPCR at days 0 (post-selection) and 24 (Figure 5F). The results confirmed substantial transgene silencing across all cell lines, with PE2max transcript levels declining by 74% in HEK293T cells, 82% in A549 cells (Figure 5G), and 27%–53% in HAP1, HCT116, and K562 cells (Figure S7D). These experiments demonstrate that diminished PE2max expression impairs editing efficiency, highlighting transgene silencing as a major limitation of lentiviral systems that is circumvented by RNP-based eVLP delivery.

PRIME-VLP enables scalable high-throughput prime editing screens and enables functional variant discovery

To demonstrate PRIME-VLP scalability in functional genomics applications, we evaluated its performance using a biologically relevant, higher complexity screening platform: a 6,000-pegRNA library that targets TP53, which contains functionally validated variants with known phenotypic effects,29 and employs positive selection to identify pathogenic TP53 mutations that confer resistance to Nutlin-3.67

We first benchmarked editing efficiency without phenotypic selection against conventional lentiviral PE2max delivery in a large-scale pooled format (Figure 6A). HEK293T cells were transduced with the 6,000-pegRNA library at a low MOI (MOI = 0.3), followed by PRIME-VLP treatment using PE2max-eVLPv2.3 loaded with non-targeting pegRNAs over 24 days (Figure 6A). NGS of the pegRNA and surrogate sensor sites recovered 5,784 pegRNAs (96.4% coverage) and revealed that PRIME-VLP achieved consistently high editing across all domains of p53 (Figure S8A). PRIME-VLP achieved >10% editing for 82.1% of pegRNAs, compared to 44.2% with lentiviral delivery (Figure 6B), a 1.9-fold increase in active pegRNAs. PRIME-VLP editing efficiencies were consistently high across pegRNAs, with a mean of 44.1%, 2.8-fold above lentiviral delivery (15.9%) (Figure 6C). At the individual pegRNA level, 4,319 pegRNAs improved by >10% absolute editing, 1,053 showed modest gains (0%–10%), and only 412 (7.1%) performed better with lentivirus (Figure 6D). We next assessed the pegRNA-level correlation between experimental replicates across the library. PRIME-VLP showed strong concordance for individual pegRNAs (R2 = 0.82), indicating consistent editing outcomes for each target, whereas lentiviral delivery exhibited poor pegRNA-level reproducibility (R2 = 0.2) (Figure S8B), consistent with stochastic, mosaic expression and silencing across cell populations. Even when comparing the best-performing lentiviral replicate for each pegRNA against average PRIME-VLP performance, PRIME-VLP outperformed lentivirus by 1.9-fold (Figure S8C). Additionally, PRIME-VLP achieved more uniform editing across the library, particularly benefiting lower-efficiency pegRNAs (Figure S8D). Importantly, reduced lentiviral editing was not due to poor library representation or sequencing quality, as both conditions showed comparable pegRNA coverage and read depth (Figure S8E). Together, these results establish PRIME-VLP as an efficient and reproducible high-throughput prime editing platform.

PRIME-VLP achieves a 2.8-fold higher mean editing efficiency (44.1%) compared to lentiviral delivery (15.9%). Boxplots display median, quartiles, and range across all pegRNAs for n = 2 biological replicates per condition.

Cells are then treated with Nutlin-3 to select for variants that confer resistance by disrupting the p53 pathway.

(F) MAGeCK analysis of pegRNA enrichment from Nutlin-3 selection screen. Volcano plot showing significance (−log10 FDR) vs. effect size (log fold change [LFC]) for pegRNA enrichment after 11 days of Nutlin-3 treatment. 181 significantly enriched pegRNAs were identified (FDR < 0.05). Key variants are labeled, including functionally validated and replicated variants.

Data represent mean ± SEM from independent biological replicates where indicated. Statistical analysis and significance thresholds are described in the STAR Methods.

Having benchmarked PRIME-VLP, we evaluated its utility for functional screening. We used the 6,000-pegRNA TP53 library to perform a positive selection screen in A549, a lung adenocarcinoma cell line with wild-type TP53 and intact p53 signaling. Cells were transduced with the library at a low MOI and subjected to PRIME-VLP over 24 days using PE2max-eVLPv2.3 loaded with a non-targeting pegRNA (Figure 6E). Following editing, cells were treated with Nutlin-3, and genomic DNA was harvested at day 11 post-treatment for NGS to assess pegRNA frequencies and sensor editing. Although editing was lower than in HEK293T cells, consistent with our previous observations (Figure 3D), sensor editing was still robust across p53 domains (Figure S9). Nutlin-3 treatment resulted in a 1.9-fold increase in editing across the library (Figure S9), consistent with the enrichment of cells harboring TP53 loss-of-function mutations conferring resistance to p53-mediated growth arrest. After filtering for active pegRNAs (>10% editing), as done previously,29,68 MAGeCK analysis identified 181 significantly enriched pegRNAs, capturing 62 significant hits from a previous TP53 screen29 and identifying additional unique candidates (Figure 6F). Benchmarking against two published TP53 functional screens29,69 revealed that our dataset correlated more strongly with the prime-editing-based screen29 than with the overexpression-based screen69 (Figure S10), with both prime editing approaches enriching variants in the oligomerization domain that were under-represented in the overexpression screen (Figure S10C).

The 62 replicated candidates had an average editing of 25.3% before treatment, increasing to 41.4% after treatment, highlighting their resistance to Nutlin-3 (Figures 6G and S9B). Importantly, our PRIME-VLP screen recapitulated 11 functionally validated p53 variants,29 including several hotspot mutations targeting R248 (Figure S9C), all of which showed increases in editing post-selection (Figure S9D). The screen also identified variants at residues matching functionally validated hits, such as G244A, I254M, and A347T (Figure 6F), suggesting similar mechanisms of p53 inactivation. The functional relevance of the unique candidates from our screen was supported by their presence in tumor sequencing cohorts (74/76 variants recorded in COSMIC) (Figure S11A) and by AlphaMissense pathogenicity predictions, with 65.1% of unique missense variants classified as high-confidence damaging substitutions (Figure S11B). To validate a subset of unique candidates, we introduced five variants (F109C, W146G, E171G, V173G, and L264I) individually into A549 cells. All five unique candidates showed positive enrichment comparable to functionally validated controls, confirming their ability to confer Nutlin-3 resistance (Figures 6H and S11C). Together, these findings demonstrate that PRIME-VLP enables robust, high-fidelity pooled prime editing screens in a biologically relevant context, identifying functional variants without requiring stable transgenic expression of a prime editor and providing an effective alternative to conventional lentiviral delivery for functional CRISPR screens.

Discussion

Advances in sequencing technologies have led to the identification of an increasing number of genetic variants of unknown significance, necessitating scalable approaches for their functional characterization.1,2,3,4,5 Prime editing offers unparalleled precision for this purpose, yet its adoption has been limited by suboptimal delivery strategies, particularly in high-throughput contexts.11,13 Here, we introduce PRIME-VLP, an eVLP-based delivery strategy that utilizes compound delivery of multiple temporally spaced, sub-saturating particle doses to achieve consistent, high-efficiency prime editing across diverse genomic targets, variants, cell types, and throughput.

Our kinetics analysis revealed that eVLP-mediated prime editing reaches maximum editing within 3 days post-transduction, indicating a narrow window of peak RNP activity. This is consistent with prior studies of DNA repair kinetics showing that Cas9 activity plateaus around 60 h post-induction.70 Dose-response analysis further revealed saturation effects at higher eVLP concentrations, where increasing particle numbers yielded diminishing returns in editing efficiency. Pre-assembled RNPs likely bind available targets rapidly upon entry, while excess RNPs are degraded before engaging new sites, an observation supported by the reported Cas9 half-life of 6–8 h after delivery.70 These kinetic and dose-response insights provided the rationale for PRIME-VLP, establishing that a limiting factor in eVLP-mediated prime editing is the duration of intracellular RNP availability, a constraint that compound delivery directly addresses.

We observed that cells remained permissive to eVLP uptake and editing across multiple rounds, with secondary transductions producing comparable or additional editing between previously transduced and naive cells (Figures 2D and S2D). The absence of viral nucleic acids in eVLPs likely contributes to this sustained transduction capacity by avoiding activation of cytosolic nucleic acid sensors, including cGAS-STING and RIG-I/MDA5.61,71,72 Our results demonstrate that sequential delivery of optimized eVLP doses prevents saturation effects and achieves superior cumulative editing efficiency compared to single high-dose treatments (Figure 3).

Off-target and transcriptome analyses demonstrated that PRIME-VLP maintains high on-target specificity while minimizing cellular perturbation. Off-target analysis using promiscuous gRNAs with established off-target profiles revealed that PRIME-VLP enhanced on-target editing while maintaining or improving specificity ratios compared to single high-dose treatments. Transcriptomic analysis further supported this finding, showing that PRIME-VLP induced fewer and more transient gene expression changes than single high-dose delivery (Figure 4F), most likely reflecting lower particle exposure at each transduction. The upregulation of immediate-early genes including FOS, FOSB, and EGR1 was consistent with retroviral capsid sensing via TRIM5α, which drives downstream MAP kinase and NF-κB signaling.73 These findings suggest that excessive particle delivery can trigger cellular stress responses that are minimized with optimized dosing strategies.

PRIME-VLP offers significant advantages for high-throughput prime editing screens by overcoming delivery constraints inherent to existing lentiviral approaches. Current lentiviral systems suffer from packaging limitations for large prime editor constructs, resulting in low functional titers,35,36 the need for selectable markers,37,59 and variable expression due to integration site effects.31,32 We demonstrated that loss of transgene expression33,34 further impacts sustained prime editor expression in target cell populations in pooled settings, with PE2max levels declining 27%–82% over 24 days across multiple cell lines. This has been somewhat addressed through site-specific transgene integration of prime editors with Cas9-mediated homology-directed repair.27 However, single-cell cloning was required to isolate high-expressing clones, indicating that the integration site is not the only limitation.27 Furthermore, single-cell cloning can generate clone-specific phenotypes unrelated to the intended genetic modification, often due to genomic instability accumulated during prolonged expansion.74 The process is also labor-intensive, poorly scalable across cell lines, and incompatible with many physiologically relevant models not amenable to single-cell cloning. In contrast, by delivering the pegRNA library via lentivirus and supplying prime editors via eVLPs, we showed that PRIME-VLP circumvents limitations of lentiviral-based editor expression. The varying fold improvements across cell lines reflect, at least in part, differences in LDLR surface availability, which positively correlates with prime editing efficiency under both single-dose and PRIME-VLP conditions (Figure S12).

Direct benchmarking against lentiviral delivery demonstrated that PRIME-VLP achieved significantly greater editing, with a 2.8-fold higher average efficiency over a 6,000-pegRNA library (Figure 6). Moreover, editing levels across replicates were highly correlated for PRIME-VLP (R2 = 0.82), while lentiviral replicates were not (R2 = 0.2). This discrepancy reflects the heterogeneous nature of lentiviral expression, in which cells that express high levels of PE2max will likely undergo editing, whereas low-expressing cells will not,32,75 and these cells are unlikely to contain the same pegRNA in both replicates. In contrast, PRIME-VLP delivers the editor as a protein complex, bypassing the need for sustained transcription and resulting in more consistent editing.

In a large-scale functional screening, PRIME-VLP successfully identified TP53 loss-of-function mutations conferring resistance to Nutlin-3. Editing frequencies for enriched variants increased substantially following selection but did not reach saturation, consistent with the cytostatic rather than cytotoxic mechanism of Nutlin-3, which induces cell-cycle arrest rather than cell death.67,76 Consequently, the 11-day selection window likely produced a partial but substantial enrichment rather than complete outgrowth. Editing in A549 cells was lower than in HEK293Ts, most likely reflecting cell-type-specific differences in eVLP uptake, nuclear access, or repair pathway activity.77,78 Nevertheless, the screen retained sufficient resolution to recover known TP53 variants conferring Nutlin-3 resistance and identify unique candidate variants at key p53 residues not previously identified.29

To assess the biological accuracy of our screen, we compared enrichment Z scores between our dataset and two published TP53 functional screens. Enrichment Z scores were more strongly correlated between the two prime-editing-based screens than the overexpression-based screen (Figure S10), consistent with the expected concordance between approaches that introduce variants at endogenous loci under physiological expression levels.29 Among the candidates classified as “unique” in our screen, 83.9% (52/62) showed consistent enrichment with Giacomelli et al.69 (Figure S10D), supporting their functional relevance. Unique candidates were further supported by recurrence across tumor sequencing datasets and by AlphaMissense pathogenicity predictions. Among Gould et al.29 hits that did not reach statistical significance in our dataset, 97% (421 out of 434) assessable pegRNAs showed consistent editing enrichment following Nutlin-3 treatment, suggesting that most were present but fell below the detection threshold due to the shorter selection window in our experiment (11 vs. 32 days) (Figure S13). Importantly, targeted validation of five novel unique candidates (F109C, W146G, E171G, V173G, and L264I) confirmed their ability to confer Nutlin-3 resistance in A549 cells (Figure 6H), demonstrating that PRIME-VLP identifies functionally significant TP53 variants not previously captured in prior screens. Together, these analyses establish the biological accuracy of PRIME-VLP for high-throughput functional variant characterization.

In conclusion, PRIME-VLP introduces a flexible method to overcome key prime editing delivery challenges for targeted genome engineering and high-throughput screening. This work demonstrates how mechanistic understanding of cellular kinetics can guide more effective genome editing strategies. By leveraging temporally optimized RNP delivery via eVLPs, this approach circumvents lentiviral delivery limitations and enables sustained editing. PRIME-VLP expands the utility of eVLP technology beyond its established in vivo therapeutic applications to high-throughput CRISPR-based screening platforms. Consistent with this, recent work has independently demonstrated the utility of VLP-based delivery for pooled CRISPR screening in primary human myeloid cells,79 further supporting the broader applicability of VLP platforms for high-throughput functional genomics applications.

Limitations of the study

Although we demonstrated that PRIME-VLP improves prime editing efficiency without exacerbating off-target and transcriptional perturbations, the off-target analysis relied on a defined panel of eight previously characterized sites associated with two promiscuous gRNAs rather than unbiased genome-wide methods and therefore does not exclude novel off-target activity at unanticipated loci under repeated transduction conditions. Moreover, transcriptional analysis was performed using bulk RNA-seq, which captures the average response across the cell population and may underestimate perturbations in a subset of cells. Editing efficiency in the TP53 screen was quantified at surrogate sensor sequences rather than the endogenous chromosomal TP53 locus, which, while an established approach, represents an indirect proxy that may not capture locus-specific chromatin or sequence context effects. Finally, the Nutlin-3 selection window of 11 days, while sufficient to identify known and novel resistance variants, is shorter than in comparable published screens, which likely reduces our statistical power to detect variants with modest resistance phenotypes that may be present in our dataset but fall below the significance threshold.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture and cell lines

All cell lines were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Lenti-X 293 T (Takara 632180), HEK293T (ATCC CRL-1573), RPE-1 (ATCC CRL-4000) and A549 (ATCC CCL-185) cells were cultured in DMEM (Corning 10-013-CV) supplemented with 10% Fetalgro bovine growth serum (RMBIO FGR-BBT) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Cytiva Hyclone SV30010). HCT116 (ATCC CCL-247) and K562 (ATCC CCL-243) cells were maintained in RPMI (Corning 10-040-CV) supplemented with 10% Fetalgro bovine growth serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. HAP1 cells (Horizon Discovery C859) were cultured in IMDM (Gibco 12440053) supplemented with 10% Fetalgro bovine growth serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Adherent cells were dissociated using 0.05% Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco 25300054). Cell lines were not authenticated for the experiments described in this study. Cell line morphologies were monitored throughout the duration of the study.

Primary human T cells were isolated from four healthy donors under ethics approval from the Health Research Ethics Board of Alberta (HREBA.CC-16-0762). Informed consent was obtained from all donors. T cells were purified using the EasySep Human T cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies 17951) and cultured in TexMACS medium (Miltenyi Biotec 130-097-196) supplemented with 5% Fetalgro bovine growth serum and 200 IU/mL recombinant human IL-2 (Miltenyi Biotec 130-097-743). T cells were activated with T cell TransAct beads (Miltenyi Biotec 130-128-758) one day prior to transduction.

Method details

Plasmid construction and cloning

All gRNAs and pegRNAs were synthesized as complementary oligonucleotides (IDT) and cloned into the appropriately digested plasmid vectors using the Golden Gate cloning, as previously described.80,81 Briefly, oligonucleotides were phosphorylated using T4 polynucleotide kinase (NEB, M0201) and annealed by heating to 95°C for 5 min followed by slow cooling to room temperature. Successful cloning of (pe)gRNAs was verified by Sanger sequencing (Azenta Life Sciences).

Additional plasmids (described below) were constructed using Gibson Assembly and verified by whole plasmid sequencing (Azenta Life Sciences or Plasmidsaurus). The eVLP-TadCBEd construct (BB666) was generated by digesting pCMV-MMLVgag-3xNES-ABE8e (Addgene 181751) with BglII to remove Tad8 and nCas9 components. Mutated Tad8e82 and nCas9 were then reintroduced, the 2xUGIs were amplified from pCMV-ancBE4max (Addgene 112094). The PE2max-eVLP construct (BB499) was assembled by digesting pCMV-MMLVgag-3xNES-Cas9 (Addgene 181752) with BglII and inserting PE2max amplified from pCMV-PEmax (Addgene 174820). The Gag, NES sites, protease cleavage site, FLAG and SV40 NLS sequences were reconstituted by PCR amplification from the original plasmid. The plenti-PE2max-blastR construct (BB236) was created by digesting pLenti PE2-BSD (Addgene 161514) with EcoRI and AgeI, followed by insertion of PE2max amplified from pCMV-PEmax-P2A-BSD (Addgene 174821).

The pLenti MLH1dn dox-inducible plasmid was assembled by cloning Mlh1dn from pCMV-PEmax-P2A-hMLH1dn (Addgene 174828) into pRDA_174 (Addgene 136476) under an inducible promoter from XLone-GFP (Addgene 96930). A hygromycin resistance gene from mini pUC19 hygroR (BB224) was incorporated for selection. The Dox-inducible GFP eSTOP reporter (TAG) construct (BB299), and the gRNA ABE GFP-eSTOP (TAG) (BB298) were generous gifts from Dr. Ciccia (Columbia University). Complete oligonucleotide sequences are provided in Tables S1 and S2, and additional published plasmids used in this study are listed in Table S3.

Lentivirus production and cell transduction

Lentiviruses were produced in Lenti-X 293 T cells seeded at 5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates with 2 mL complete DMEM. After 24 h, cells were transfected with 3 μg total DNA comprising equal molar ratios of psPAX2 (Addgene 12260) and pMD2.G (Addgene 12259), along with the transfer vector. Transfection was performed in a total volume of 250 μL using OptiMEM (Gibco 31985070) and 3 μL of 1 mg/mL polyethylenimine (PEI) (Sigma 408727). The transfection mixture was incubated for 15 min at room temperature before being added dropwise to the cells. Following overnight incubation, media was replaced with 2 mL of fresh complete DMEM, and cells were incubated for an additional 48 to 60 h. Viral supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min, and filtered through 0.45 μm filters to remove cellular debris. Lentiviruses were used immediately or aliquoted and stored at −80°C. For large-scale screening applications, production was scaled up to 15 cm dishes using proportionally increased reagent volumes.

For lentiviral transduction, target cells were seeded at 3 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates with 2 mL of complete media. The following day, lentivirus was added to the cells along with 10 μg/mL polybrene. Cells were spinoculated at 935 × g for 2 h at 32°C followed by a 12-h incubation at 37°C and 5% CO2. Media was then replaced with fresh complete media, and antibiotic selection began on the next morning using puromycin (1 μg/mL), blasticidin (10 μg/mL), or hygromycin (100–400 μg/mL as determined by kill curves for each cell line).

Production and characterization of eVLPs

eVLPs were generated in Lenti-X 293 T producer cells following the established protocol.43 Briefly, cells were seeded at 5 × 106 cells per 75 cm2 flask in 10 mL of complete DMEM, and transfections were performed 24 h later using either JetPRIME (Polyplus 101000046) according to Banskota et al.,43 or PEI.

For Targeting gRNA (T) loaded eVLPs, transfection was performed using JetPRIME with optimized ratios: 400 ng of VSV-g (BB14), 3.375 μg of pBS-CMV-gagpol (Addgene 35614), 1.125 μg of the editor plasmid, and 4.4 μg of the guide plasmid mixed with 18.6 μL of JetPRIME in JetPRIME buffer for a total volume of 500 μL.43 The mixture was incubated for 10 min at room temperature before being added to the cells. Non-Targeting gRNA (NT) eVLPs were produced using PEI transfection with 860 ng of VSV-g, 7.258 μg of pBS-CMV-gagpol, 2.419 μg of the editor plasmid, and 9.462 μg of the (pe)gRNA plasmid (BB106, for gRNAs; or BB523 for pegRNAs). pegRNA-free (−) eVLPs were generated using PEI transfection with 1.634 μg of VSV-g, 13.791 μg of pBS-CMV-gagpol, and 4.597 μg of the editor plasmid. For PEI transfections, plasmid mixtures were combined with 60 μL of PEI (1 mg/mL) in OptiMEM for a total volume of 500 μL and incubated 15 min at room temperature before being added dropwise to the cells. Media was replaced with 10 mL of fresh complete DMEM at 12 h post-transfection. eVLPs-containing supernatants were harvested 96 h after transfection, centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min and filtered. The eVLP supernatants were then concentrated 100-fold in serum-free, antibiotic-free media or PBS by ultracentrifugation at 30,000 × g for 2 h at 4°C. Concentrated eVLPs were used immediately or stored at 4°C for up to one week. Particle concentration and size distribution were measured using TRPS on the IZON qNano TRPS Particle Analyzer (IZON Science Q5013) according to manufacturer’s instructions. eVLP samples were initially diluted 1:50 in PBS, then further diluted (1:10 to 1:1,000) in PBS containing 0.1% TWEEN 20 (Sigma-Aldrich P1379) for quantification. Each sample were analyzed alongside IZON calibration beads (IZON Science CPC100) using a 50–330 nm Nanopore (IZON Science NP100) until 1,000 events were recorded. Data were processed using the IZON Control Suite software (v3.2) to calculate particle concentration and size distribution. Particle characterization was also performed using the Corning VideoDrop (VD1000). eVLP samples were diluted 1:50 in PBS and analyzed using the following acquisition parameters: 100 frames per video (>3 videos per sample), a minimum of 400 tracked particles, 0.73ms exposure time, and a viscosity of 0.928 mPa/s.

Base editing and prime editing via transfection

Editing experiments were performed in HEK293T cells using two distinct transfection protocols. For TransIT-293 (mirusbio MIR 2700) transfections, cells were seeded at 70–80% confluency in a 48-well plates at 5.5 × 104 cells per well in 250 μL complete DMEM according to the manufacturer’s instructions.80,83 For Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen 11668027) transfections, cells were seeded in 96-well plate with 1.8 × 104 per well in 100 μL complete DMEM as previously described.56

Transfections were performed 24 h after cell seeding. For base editing experiments, cells were transfected with 387 ng of TadCBEd plasmid (Addgene 193835) or ABE8e plasmid (Addgene 138489) and 113 ng of gRNA plasmid using 1 μL of TransIT-293 reagent. For prime editing, cells were transfected with 417 ng of PEmax plasmid (Addgene 174828) and 83 ng of pegRNA plasmid. TransIT-293 DNA transfection mixtures were incubated for 20 min at room temperature before dropwise addition to cells. Lipofectamine 2000 transfections consisted of 292 ng of base or prime editor plasmids with 96 ng of the (pe)gRNA plasmid and 0.5 μL of Lipofectamine 2000 reagent. The DNA:lipid complexes were incubated for 10 min at room temperature before being added dropwise to the cells. Cells were maintained in culture for 72 h until collection by centrifugation at 300 × g for 3 min. Cell pellets were stored at −20°C until genomic DNA extraction.

Prime editing via electroporation

A549 cells were detached using trypsin. After neutralizing trypsin with complete culture medium, cells were washed with PBS. For each reaction, 1.2 × 105 cells were pelleted and resuspended in 10 μL of Neon NxT Buffer R (Invitrogen). The cell suspension was then combined with 1 μg of PE7 encoding mRNA and 0.25 μL of Protector RNase Inhibitor (40 U/μL; Sigma-Aldrich, cat: 3335399001). Electroporation was performed using a 10 μL tip on the Neon NxT Transfection System (Invitrogen NEON1S) with the following parameters: 1,230 V, 30 ms, and 2 pulses. Immediately following electroporation, cells were transferred to a 12-well plate containing 1 mL of complete DMEM without antibiotics. Genomic editing was assessed 72 h post-transfection via PCR and Sanger sequencing.

Base editing and prime editing via eVLP transduction

For eVLP-mediated editing, HEK293T cells were seeded in 48-well plates at 3.5 × 104 cells per well in 250 μL of complete DMEM. Cells were transduced 8 h after seeding with 100 μL of concentrated eVLPs. Base editing experiments used ABE8e or TadCBEd eVLPs loaded with appropriate gRNA, while prime editing utilized PE2max eVLPs loaded with the relevant pegRNAs. Cells were collected 72 h post-transduction, and cell pellets were stored at −20°C until genomic DNA extraction.

Multiple eVLP transductions

For compound transduction experiments, HEK293T, HAP1-MLH1dn, RPE-1-MLH1dn or K-562-MLH1dn cells were seeded in a 48-well plate at 3.5 × 104 cells per well with 250 μL of complete media.43 Cells were transduced 8 h later with either 25 or 100 μL of PE2max-eVLP loaded with specific pegRNAs including PCNA +2 AA>CG (BB146); HEK3 +1 CTTins (BB415); HEK3 + 1 T del (BB416); RNF2 +5 G>T (BB732); ADA +9 C>T (BB854) or EMX1 + 1 G>T (BB926). eVLPs were added directly to the culture media.

For time course analysis, cells were collected 1, 2, 3, 4 or 5 days after transduction and were stored at −20°C until genomic DNA extraction. For multiple transduction experiments, cells were trypsinized, counted and replated at 3.5 × 104 cells per well (48-well plates) 8 h before subsequent addition of eVLPs. To investigate the days between transductions, cells that underwent four sequential transductions were collected at the indicated times (days 4, 8, 12, 16, or 20 post-initial transduction).

PRIME-VLP protocol

The optimized PRIME-VLP protocol for HEK293T involves transducing 3.5 × 104 cells with 25 μL (for targeted editing) to 50 μL (for screen) of eVLP (700–1,400 cells/μL), every three days. Cells are collected 3 days after the final transduction, on day 12 or 24 for the screens. eVLP volumes were adjusted according to cell line-specific optimization and scaled proportionally to the number of cells seeded in each experiment.

Genomic DNA extraction, Sanger and NGS

Genomic DNA was extracted from the frozen cell pellets using QuickExtract DNA Extraction Solution (Biosearch Technologies QE09050) as described previously.83 Editing in samples was assessed using either Sanger or NGS (Azenta Life Sciences).

For Sanger sequencing analysis, samples were amplified using locus-specific primers in the Table S4. Chromatogram files (.ab1) were used to quantify editing efficiency using EditR84 v1.1.10.

For NGS analysis, samples underwent dual PCR amplification. The first PCR used Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB M0491L) and locus-specific oligonucleotides listed in the Table S5. A second PCR was performed using 1 μL of the first PCR as template to incorporate Illumina indexes and adapter sequences. Amplicon libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform using paired-end 2 × 150 bp reads with a 5% PhiX spike-in. Sample demultiplexing and FASTQ generation were performed using Illumina’s bcl2fastq tool with standard indexing primers. Editing at endogenous loci was evaluated using CRISPResso2 (v2.3.2),85 with a Phred quality cut-off of ≥20. Reads were aligned to reference amplicons for quantitative analysis. Prime editing efficiency was quantified within an 11-bp window centered on the Cas9 (H840A) nick site, using the pegRNA spacer and 3′-extension sequences. Base editing efficiency was assessed using quantification_window_center of −7 and a quantification_window_size of 20; the resulting Alleles_frequency_table.txt was then used to determine editing efficiency.

The accession numbers for the NGS data are SRA: SUB15455217; BioProject PRJNA1294868.

Cell viability analysis

Each sample was counted in triplicate using the DeNovix CellDrop counter to determine viability. HEK293T were seeded as described previously, while T cells were plated in round-bottom 96-well plates at 5 × 104 per well in 50 μL complete TexMACS media. Cells were either left untreated or transduced 8 h later with 25 μL (PRIME-VLP protocol) or 100 μL of eVLPs. Both HEK293T and T cells were counted in triplicate and replated at initial cell densities on days 3, 6 and 9. PRIME-VLP cells received four sequential transductions of 25 μL eVLPs each, totalling 100 μL eVLPs from a single preparation. Final cell counts were performed on day 12 for all experimental conditions.

Evaluation of sequential eVLP transduction efficiency

HEK293T ABE reporter cells containing a doxycycline-inducible GFP cassette with a premature stop codon were seeded in a 48-well plate at 3.5 × 105 cells per well with 250 μL of complete DMEM. Eight hours post-seeding, cells were transduced with 25 μL of ABE8e eVLPs carrying gRNA targeting the stop codon. Three days after plating, 1 μg/mL of doxycycline was added. After 24 h, cells were sorted for GFP expression to obtain a homogeneous population of GFP-positive cells (Cumming School of Medicine Flow Cytometry Core Facility). Immediately after sorting, GFP-positive cells were replated in 48-well plates at 3.5 × 104 cells per well. Non-edited, GFP-negative HEK293T reporter cells were plated in parallel as secondary transduction controls. When cells were fully attached (1 h), both populations were transduced with 25 μL of TadCBEd-eVLPs carrying RASA2-targeting gRNA. Cells were collected 72 h post-transduction, gDNA was extracted, and RASA2 editing efficiency was quantified by Sanger sequencing.

Off-target editing analysis

Off-target editing analysis was performed using HEK293T cells transduced with eVLP either containing Cas9 or PE2max loaded with HEK3- and HEK4 (pe)gRNAs, following the standard eVLP or PRIME-VLP transduction protocols detailed above.

For comparison with conventional transfection, HEK293T cells were seeded in 48-well plates at 5.5 × 104 cells per well in 250 μL complete DMEM for transfection using TransIT-293 reagent, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, transfection mixtures contained 750 ng of Cas9 (Addgene 42230) and 250 ng of HEK3 (BB867) or HEK4 (BB868) gRNA plasmids mixed with 2 μL of TransIT-293 in 25 μL total volume adjusted with OptiMEM. The DNA:lipids complexes were incubated for 10 min at room temperature before being added to HEK293T cells. All cells were collected on day 12 and stored at −20°C until gDNA extraction. On- and off-target editing frequencies at HEK3 and HEK4 loci were quantified by NGS.

RNA sequencing and transcriptome analysis

Primary human T cells were isolated from four healthy donors and seeded in round-bottom 96-well plates at a density of 5.0 × 105 cells per well in 50 μL of complete TexMACS medium. Cells were either left untreated as controls or subjected to transduction using either the eVLP or PRIME-VLP protocols, as detailed above. Following treatment, T cells were harvested on day 3 for assessment of acute cellular responses and day 12 for long-term responses. All samples were immediately frozen at −80°C prior to RNA extraction and sequencing, both performed by Azenta Life Sciences.

Twenty-four RNA-Seq libraries were generated with high-quality metrics, averaging ∼37 million reads per library (range: 25–50 million reads) using 150-nucleotides paired-end sequencing. Data quality assessment was performed using FastQC, with all samples passing QC with a mean base quality score of >35. Raw FASTQ files were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome using the STAR aligner86 in two-pass mode, with “–quantMode” parameter configured to generate read counts per gene. The alignment yielded >80% uniquely mapped reads across all samples. Gene annotations were performed using the gencode.v44chr_patch_hap1_scaff.annotation.gtf file, with resulting gene counts were utilized for subsequent differential expression analysis.

Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2.87 Comparisons were made between treated samples (1 × 100 μL or PRIME-VLP) and untreated controls, analyzed independently for day 3 and day 12 time points. Genes were considered differentially expressed when meeting criteria of BH-adjusted p-value <0.05 and absolute |Log2 fold change| >1. Pathway enrichment was performed using fGSEA63 with previously reported CAR T cell gene sets that represent distinct aspects of CAR-T cell biology.88

Analysis of LDLR expression using flow cytometry

To quantify cell surface LDLR, culture media was removed and cells were washed once with PBS. Cells were detached by incubating in 5 mM EDTA in PBS for 5 min at 37°C, followed by gentle pipetting to ensure a single-cell suspension. The reaction was neutralized by diluting the cells in cold FACS buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% FBS and 2 mM EDTA). For immunofluorescent staining, cells were incubated at 4°C with a PE-conjugated anti-human LDLR antibody (R&D Systems, AF2148) at a concentration of 5 × 104 cells/μL for 15 min, followed by two washes with FACS buffer to remove unbound antibody, samples were quantified using an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer equipped with a YL1 laser.

Construction and cloning of the miniature sensor pegRNA library

The miniature sensor pegRNA library consisted of 31 pegRNAs, each with manually designed target sequences. These constructs were cloned into a modified lentiGuide-Puro (BB26), that had been pre-modified to remove the original gRNA scaffold sequence and incorporate the epegRNA 3′ extension sequence along with two restriction enzyme sites. Individual pegRNAs were inserted into BsmBI-linearized BB770 using Gibson assembly. Plasmids used to construct the miniature sensor pegRNA library are listed in Table S8. Target sequences were subsequently cloned by sequential digestion with EcoRI and BamHI. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing (Azenta Life Sciences).

Sequences of the pegRNAs and targets are listed in the Tables S2 and S6.

Pooled prime editing mini screen and analysis

Cell lines were infected with the miniature sensor pegRNA library using lentivirus at an MOI = 0.3 with a representation of 1,000× (>3.1 × 104). Cells were selected with 1 μg/mL puromycin for a minimum of 3 days. After recovery, 3.5 × 104 cells were harvested and frozen as an initial screen sample, while an equivalent number of cells were divided into experimental groups. One group received PE2max (BB236) lentivirus at a MOI of 1 followed by selection with 10 μg/mL blasticidin for at least 5 days. A second group was transduced with either Non-Targeting pegRNA (NT) (BB523) loaded PE2max-eVLP or pegRNA-free (−) PE2max-eVLPs following the PRIME-VLP protocol.

The PRIME-VLP transduction protocol involved seeding cells in a 48-well plate at 3.5 × 104 cells per well with 250 μL complete medium, followed by eVLP transduction 8 h post-seeding using optimized eVLP amounts. Cells were subsequently trypsinized, counted and re-transduced every 3 days until day 24, completing a total of 8 transduction cycles. Lentivirus-infected control cells were passaged in parallel with PRIME-VLP treated cells throughout the 24-day experimental period. All MLH1dn cell lines were maintained in 1 μg/mL doxycycline starting from PE2max infection and first PRIME-VLP transduction through the duration of the screens. Cell pellets containing a minimum of 3.5 × 104 cells per condition were collected on days 6, 15 and 24 (final) and stored at −20°C until genomic DNA extraction.

Genomic DNA extraction was performed using standard protocols, followed by a 2-step PCR amplification strategy. First, pegRNAs and targets were amplified using NEBNext Ultra II Q5 Master Mix (NEB M0544X). A second PCR reaction was performed using the first PCR as a template, adding adapters and barcodes sequences, as described previously.89 Oligonucleotides used for the PCR amplification are available in the Table S5. Amplicon libraries were sequenced by Azenta Life Sciences using the Illumina NovaSeq platform with paired-end 2 × 150 bp reads with a 5% PhiX spike-in. Sample demultiplexing and FASTQ file generation were performed using Illumina’s bcl2fastq tool with standard indexing primers. Analysis of miniature sensor library data was conducted using a custom R script that searched sequence reads for target-specific sensor sequences, categorized reads by target, and calculated editing rates by quantifying reads that matched either the wild-type or intended edit sequence. Targets demonstrating <1% editing across all the experimental conditions were excluded from downstream analysis.

PE2max expression and RT-qPCR

Multiple cell lines including HAP1-MLH1dn, HCT116, K562-MLH1dn, A549-MLH1dn, and HEK293T were infected in duplicate with PE2max lentivirus at approximately MOI of 1. Following blasticidin selection at 10 μg/mL, initial cell pellets were collected and frozen at −80°C as baseline samples for RNA extraction. Cells were maintained in continuous culture with regular passaging for 24 days, after which final samples were collected for comparative RNA analysis.

RNA extraction was performed by TRIzol chloroform extraction. Briefly, cell pellets were resuspended in TRIzol at a volume of 1 mL per 1.0 × 107 cells, incubated for 5 min at room temperature, and centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 5 min at room temperature. Chloroform was added at one-fifth the initial TRIzol volume (e.g., 200 μL per mL TRIzol). Samples were vortexed for 15 s, incubated for 3 min at room temperature, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The aqueous phase was carefully collected, and RNA was precipitated by adding 100% isopropanol at half the original volume (e.g., 500 μL per mL TRIzol) followed by incubation at room temperature for 10 min and centrifugation at 7,500 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Pellet was air dried and resuspended in DEPC-treated water. RNA quantification was performed using NanoDrop spectrophotometer.

Reverse transcription was performed with the SuperScript IV First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen 18091050) with random hexamer primers according to manufacturer specifications. Reactions were performed at room temperature for 10 min, 50°C for 1 h, and 80°C for 10 min. The resulting cDNA was diluted 1:20 for reactions using 1 μg RNA input or 1:4 for lower RNA input. Quantitative PCR was conducted using Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 6 with reactions containing 5 μL of 2× Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems 4367659), 0.1 μL each primer (100 μM) (primer sequences are available in the Table S7), and 1 μL diluted cDNA in 10 μL total volume. All samples were analyzed in triplicate. The qPCR program was as follows: Initial hold stage at 50°C for 2 min and 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 30 s, with a final melt curve analysis at 60°C for 1 min ramping to 95°C for 5 s.

Prime editor silencing and editing

HEK293T cells were infected in duplicate with PE2max (BB236) lentivirus at an MOI of 1 and selected with 10 μg/mL blasticidin. One set of cells was immediately frozen while another was maintained in continuous culture for 24 days. Low-passage PE2max HEK293T cells were thawed on day 21, and both low and high-passage cells were transfected on day 24 with pegRNAs (BB520, BB676, and BB677). After 72-h incubation, all cells were pelleted and stored at −20°C prior to genomic DNA extraction. Editing efficiency was assessed through locus-specific PCR amplification followed by Sanger sequencing (Azenta Life Sciences).

Construction and cloning of p53/Nutlin library

The p53 Nutlin sensor library was adapted from Gould et al., 202429 and synthesized by Twist Biosciences as a lyophilized oligonucleotide pool. Library amplification and cloning were performed according to established protocols, using the lyophilized material resuspended in TE buffer (pH 8.0) at 10 ng/μL and subsequently diluted to 1 ng/μL. Ten parallel PCR reactions were performed using 1 ng of oligonucleotide pool per reaction for 14 cycles total (primers available in Table S1). All PCR reactions were pooled and purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter A63881).

The sensor library backbone (BB1131) was derived from lentiGuide-Puro (Addgene 52963) with modifications that included the removal of the gRNA scaffold sequence and the addition of a BsmBI site (oligonucleotides are available in Table S1). Both the amplified library and backbone were digested with BsmBI (NEB R0734S) and EcoRI-HF (NEB R3101S) and gel purified using GenepHlow Gel/PCR kit (Geneaid DFH300) and the AMPure XP beads. Ligation was performed using the High concentration T4 DNA ligase (NEB M0202M) at 25°C overnight, with 7:1 insert-to-vector ratio. Following heat inactivation at 65°C for 10 min, four electroporation reactions were conducted using 25 μL BioSearch Technologies Endura DUOs Electrocompetent cells (60242-2) with 3 μL ligation product per reaction (Bio-Rad MicroPulser electroporator 1652100). Bacteria cultures were grown overnight at 32°C in 2 L of LB medium with 100 μg/mL of carbenicillin.

Library representation was verified by Amplicon-EZ sequencing, and recombination rates were confirmed by Sanger sequencing of 10 individual colonies.

p53/nutlin library screening and analysis

Both HEK293T and A549-MLH1dn screens were performed in duplicate following established protocols.29,89 Briefly, initial library infection involved 1 × 107 cells transduced with TP53 sensor library lentivirus at MOI 0.3, followed by puromycin selection at 1 μg/mL. Five to six days post-transduction, 3.5 × 106 selected cells, representing >500× library coverage, were either collected as initial samples or plated for experimental conditions: no-editor controls plated in 15 cm dishes and PE2max lentivirus infection, and NT pegRNA-loaded PE2max-eVLP transduction conditions seeded at 2.5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates, for a total of 14 wells each.

Lentivirus infections were performed 24 h post-seeding with plenti-PEmax-blastR (BB236) at MOI 1; followed by blasticidin selection at 10 μg/mL for at least 5 days. A549-MLH1dn cells were maintained in 1 μg/mL doxycycline throughout the experiment beginning on day 0. Every three days for 24 days, 3.5 × 106 cells from each condition were replated, with eVLP-transduced cells receiving additional transductions for a total of eight transductions over 24 days. Cell pellets of 3.5 × 106 cells were collected and stored at −20°C for each condition and time point.

The HEK293T screen concluded on day 24 with final sample collection of 3.5 × 106 cells. On day 24, 3.5 × 106 A549-MLH1dn cells were collected for each condition and replated for two additional days of expansion before being separated into untreated and Nutlin-3 treatment groups. Nutlin-3 (Selleckchem S1061) was added to the cells on day 27 at 10 μM and maintained for 11 days until screen completion on day 38. Cells were passaged every 3 days, maintaining >500× representation. Cell pellets were collected and frozen at −20°C for day 5 and day 11 (final) of Nutlin-3 treatment.

Genomic DNA extraction was performed using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood & Tissue kit (Qiagen 69504) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. NGS samples were prepared using dual PCR amplification with NEBNext Ultra II Q5 Master Mix (NEB M0544×). Initial PCR amplification consisted of 19 cycles using total genomic DNA from each sample as a template across 14 reactions with 3 μg of template genomic DNA. The second PCR of 11–16 cycles added adapters and sample barcodes using pooled PCR1 products across 4 reactions per sample (primers listed in Table S5). Final PCR products were pooled, purified, and sequenced using NovaSeq with paired-end 2 × 150 bp reads and 5% PhiX spike-in.

Sample demultiplexing and FASTQ file generation were performed using Illumina’s bcl2fastq tool with stand indexing primers. Editing frequency analysis employed a custom R script that combined forward and reverse reads, aligned them to specific pegRNAs using 3′ extensions, and calculated editing percentages using the formula (Edited Reads/(Edited Reads + Wild-type Reads)) x 100. To exclude sequencing and recombination errors, only precisely edited and WT-matching reads reads were considered, which accounted for 85-90% of reads. Functional screen analysis utilized the MAGeCK (v0.5.9.5) pipeline90 to estimate statistical significance and fold change for each pegRNA using default parameters. Statistical comparisons assessed differences between Nutlin-3-treated and control groups on day 11, with results merged with editing efficiency based on pegRNA identifiers.

Unique candidate validation by competitive growth assay

A549 cells were transduced at a high MOI with a lentiviral vector co-encoding the candidate pegRNA and GFP. Following transduction, cells were electroporated with PE7 prime editor-encoding mRNA as described above. To evaluate the relative fitness and enrichment of the edited population, a competitive growth assay was established by mixing the pegRNA-expressing (GFP+) population with WT non-fluorescent A549 cells. The populations were mixed to achieve an initial target of approximately 20% GFP+ cells, and this baseline proportion was verified by flow cytometry using an Attune NxT. Mixed cell populations were then treated with 10 μM Nutlin-3 (Selleckchem S1061) or left untreated as a control for 5 days. Following selection, the proportion of GFP+ cells was quantified in each condition using the Attune Nxt. The change in GFP+ proportion (delta GFP, %) was calculated as the difference between the GFP+ (%) fraction at day 5 and the initial GFP+ (%) at day 0. All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism. Statistical details, including the specific tests used, exact values of n, and what n represents, are reported in each figure legend. For screening analysis, editing frequency at sensor sites was calculated as (Edited Reads/(Edited Reads + Non edited Reads)) X 100. MAGeCK (v0.5.9.5) was used with default parameters to rank pegRNAs by enrichment between Nutlin-3-treated and untreated groups. Prior to MAGeCK analysis, pegRNAs with <10% editing at sensor sites were filtered to remove inactive pegRNAs, as previously described. Replicate concordance was assessed by Pearson correlation (R2). Pairwise enrichment Z score comparisons with published datasets were used to assess screen accuracy, as described in the results section. RNAseq data were analyzed by differential gene expression using DESeq2.87 Genes were considered differentially expressed when meeting criteria of BH-adjusted p-value <0.05 and absolute |Log2 fold change| >1. Pathway enrichment was performed using fGSEA.63
