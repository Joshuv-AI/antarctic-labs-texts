Relationship of frailty and resumption of social participation activities after the COVID‐19 pandemic among community‐dwelling older adults in Japan
Authors: Sachiko Ozone, Ryohei Goto, Shogo Kawada, Shoji Yokoya, Tetsuhiro Maeno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigated social participation trajectories following the Coronavirus Disease 19 (COVID‐19) pandemic and its relationship with frailty among community‐dwelling older adults.MethodsWe selected 5000 older residents (65–84 years) from Kitaibaraki City, Japan, excluding those certified for long‐term care. After the first survey in April 2022, the second survey was conducted with 1931 respondents from the first survey in May 2023. Respondents of the second survey who had participated in social participation activities in January 2020 were eligible for this study. The questionnaire included social participation status in January 2020, April 2022, and May 2023, along with the Kihon Checklist in April 2022 and May 2023. Participants were categorized into four groups according to the participation status: Continued, Resumed, Discontinued in 2023, and Discontinued in 2022. Binary logistic regression analysis assessed the relationship between these groups and frailty risk assessed by Kihon Checklist, adjusting for age, gender, living status, economic conditions, working status, self‐rated health, and 2022 frailty status.ResultsOf 1289 respondents (66.8%), 648 were analyzed. Social participation groups were: Continued (284, 43.8%), Resumed (119, 18.4%), Discontinued in 2023 (92, 14.2%), and Discontinued in 2022 (153, 23.6%). The Discontinued in 2022 group had a significantly higher risk of frailty (OR = 2.26, 95% CI: 1.16–4.42, p = 0.017) compared to the Continued group.ConclusionsSocial participation trajectories are associated with frailty risk in older adults. Sustained discontinuation of social activities, particularly since 2022, is related to increased frailty risk, highlighting the importance of maintaining social engagement.Keywords: COVID‐19, frailty, Kihon Checklist, social participation

BACKGROUND

The definition of social participation varies across literature, but it is generally defined as individuals' involvement in activities that provide interaction with others in community life and important shared spaces. 1 Social participation is reported to be associated with various health outcomes, such as frailty, disability, 2 and mortality. 3

The Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic began in 2020, leading to restrictions on social participation activities for older adults. Physical activity began to decline in April 2020, 4 raising concerns that even those who were not previously frail might face an increased risk of frailty. 5 The progression of frailty remained a concern into the second year of the pandemic.6, 7 In addition to frailty, cognitive decline associated with social isolation among older adults was also a concern. 8 Psychological stress 9 and depression scores 10 also worsened among older adults during the pandemic. Our study found that, as of 2021, those who continued their activities had a lower risk of frailty, 11 and a higher proportion of women than men had discontinued their activities during the pandemic. 12

In Japan, the legal classification of COVID‐19 was changed from Class II to Class V infectious disease, 13 which eased restrictions and contributed to the resumption of many activities. Physical and social activities, which had decreased during the pandemic, had resumed to pre‐pandemic levels by January 2023. 14 However, some individuals have not resumed participation in these activities, and the impact of resuming social participation on frailty after a period of discontinuation remains unclear. Additionally, it is unknown whether the higher proportion of women who discontinued their activities persists.

In the current post‐COVID‐19 era, it is crucial to investigate whether those who were initially able to participate in social activities but have not resumed them remain at high risk of frailty. Understanding the relationship between activity status post‐pandemic and frailty can help identify target populations for focused frailty prevention efforts in the future.

The objective of this study was to compare the frailty status of community‐dwelling older adults in Japan who had participated in social activities before the COVID‐19 pandemic in terms of participation status during and after the pandemic.

METHODS

Setting and participants

We conducted a retrospective longitudinal observational survey using data from two annual surveys conducted in Kitaibaraki City in Ibaraki Prefecture, Japan, which is approximately 160 km north of Tokyo. Kitaibaraki City has a population of 39,898, with 36.8% of citizens aged over 65 as of October 1, 2023. 15 The first survey was conducted in April 2022, followed by a second survey in May 2023.

The target population was older adult residents from age 65 to 85 who were not certified to require long‐term care under Japan's long‐term care insurance system at the time of the first survey in April 2022. At the first survey, we mailed a questionnaire with identification numbers to 5000 people who were randomly selected by the administrative staff of the city, of which 1931 were returned. We mailed the second questionnaire in May 2023 to those who responded to the first survey. The subjects of this study were respondents who answered that they had participated in social activities in January 2020, which was before the COVID‐19 pandemic, in the first survey and completed the second survey. The list of participant identification numbers was managed exclusively by city officials and was used solely for the purpose of mailing the two rounds of questionnaires.

In Japan, the first COVID‐19 case was reported in January 2020, and the government declared a state of emergency from April to May 2020, during which almost all activities were canceled. Intermittent states of emergency and calls for activity restriction continued thereafter. Various activities began to gradually resume around 2022, and following the relaxation of COVID‐19 regulations under the infectious disease control law in May 2023, 13 social activities largely returned to pre‐pandemic levels. In Kitaibaraki City, social participation activities generally followed this national trend.

Measurements

At the first survey in April 2022, we asked the participants' age, gender, social participation status in January 2020 and April 2022, and all items in the Kihon Checklist (KCL) to evaluate the frailty status of the participants. At the second survey in May 2023, we asked the social participation status in May 2023 and KCL. We also asked participants' living status, working status, satisfaction with economic conditions, regular hospital visits, hospitalization within the past year, and self‐rated health status.

The KCL is a self‐administered questionnaire consisting of 25 items, validated as a tool for assessing long‐term care needs16, 17, 18 and frailty status. 19 The KCL items are categorized into seven categories: physical strength, nutrition, eating, socialization, memory, mood, and lifestyle. A higher total score indicates a greater risk of decline in functional capacity. A cutoff score of 8 or higher is generally adopted as a cutoff value.3, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22 This threshold has demonstrated good predictive validity in identifying frailty as defined by other established models. For example, Satake et al. reported that a KCL score of 8 or more had a sensitivity of 89.5% and specificity of 80.7% compared with Fried's frailty phenotype. 19

Given the diverse definition of social participation in the literature, and considering the characteristics of Kitaibaraki City, we defined social participation as taking part in either local festivals or events, community activities in the neighborhood, voluntary groups, or volunteer activities. We categorized the participants into four groups according to their social participation status in 2022 and 2023; (1) “Continued group” were those who continued to participate in social participation activities in April 2022 and May 2023; (2) “Resumed group” were those who discontinued to participate in 2022 but resumed in 2023; (3) “Discontinued in 2023 group” were those who participated in 2022 but discontinued to participate in 2023; and (4) “Discontinued in 2022 group” were those who discontinued to participate in both 2022 and 2023. Participants with incomplete responses to the KCL items were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

We compared age, gender, living status, working status, satisfaction with economic conditions, presence of regular medical visits, hospitalization within the past year, self‐rated health status, and the proportion of individuals corresponding to impairment in each KCL category among the four groups using either ANOVA or chi‐squared tests. The primary outcome, frailty status in 2023, was defined by a total score of 8 or higher in the KCL in 2023. Social participation patterns between 2020 and 2023 were categorized into four groups. To examine the association between social participation patterns and the risk of frailty in 2023, binary logistic regression analysis was conducted. The primary outcome was frailty in 2023, defined by a total KCL score of 8 or higher. This was treated as a binary dependent variable in the logistic regression model. The main independent variable was social participation status from 2022 to 2023. The four social participation groups (Continued, Resumed, Discontinued in 2023, Discontinued in 2022) were treated as a categorical variable and included in the model as dummy variables, with the Continued group used as the reference category. Based on previous studies,18, 19, 23 Age, gender, living status (living alone or not), satisfaction with economic conditions, working status, self‐rated health status, and frailty status in 2022 (based on a KCL cutoff of 8 in 2022) were included as covariates. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, and a p‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additionally, a supplemental analysis was conducted to examine the potential circularity between KCL items and social participation. The total KCL score was recalculated after excluding two items directly related to social behavior, and the modified scores were compared among the four social participation groups using Welch's ANOVA. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 30 was used for all statistical analyses. This study was approved by the University of Tsukuba Medical Ethics Board (number 1602‐2).

RESULTS

A total of 1289 participants (66.8%) responded to the survey. In January 2020, 669 (51.2%) of these respondents had engaged in social participation activities. We excluded 629 who had not participated in social participation activities in January 2020 and 12 who did not answer all items of KCL, which left 648 participants eligible for analysis (Figure 1).

Flow chart of study participants.

The mean age of the participants was 74.1 ± 4.9 years; 318 (50.1%) were women, 96 (14.8%) lived alone, and 181 (27.9%) had a job. Of the participants analyzed, 284 (43.8%) continued to participate in social participation activities in 2022 and 2023 (Continued group), 119 (18.4%) discontinued participating in 2022 but resumed in 2023 (Resumed group), 92 (14.2%) continued in 2022 but discontinued in 2023 (Discontinued in 2023 group), and 153 (23.6%) discontinued participating in both 2022 and 2023 (Discontinued in 2022 group). There was a significant difference in gender among the groups, with the proportion of women being 44.3% in the Continued group, 47.4% in the Resumed group, 54.5% in the Discontinued in 2023 group, and 60.3% in the Discontinued in 2022 group (p = 0.011). The participants' perceived economic status also showed significant variation between the groups (p = 0.049). In the Discontinued in 2022 group, the proportion of participants who reported being satisfied with their economic status was lower (47.7%) compared to the other groups, while the proportion of those who reported being unsatisfied was higher (50.3%). There were no significant differences in residential status, working status, percentage of those making regular visits to medical facilities, and hospitalization within a year among groups (Table 1). In a supplemental analysis using the 23‐item KCL score (excluding two social behavior items), similar patterns were observed across the four groups. The results of Welch's ANOVA indicated a significant difference among the groups, supporting the robustness of the association between social participation and frailty.

Multivariable logistic regression results are presented in Table 2. After adjusting for covariates, participants in the Discontinued in 2022 group were more likely to have a KCL score of 8 or higher in 2023 compared to those in the Continued group (OR = 2.26, 95% CI: 1.16–4.42, p = 0.017). In addition, having a KCL score of 8 or higher in 2022 was strongly associated with frailty status in 2023 (OR = 7.47, 95% CI: 4.26–13.13, p < 0.001). Poor self‐rated health (OR = 5.52, 95% CI: 2.96–10.29, p < 0.001) and being economically unsatisfied had higher odds of frailty in 2023 (OR = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.05–3.03, p = 0.033). No significant associations were found for age, gender, living alone, employment status, or other categories of social participation. The model showed good fit (Nagelkerke R 2 = 0.346; Hosmer‐Lemeshow test: p = 0.977).

DISCUSSION

In this study, those who had discontinued and not resumed social participation activities were found to be at a higher risk of frailty compared to those who continued their activities. In addition, participants who were classified as frail based on the KCL in 2022, those with poor self‐rated health, and those dissatisfied with their economic status were also more likely to be frail in 2023. Although women were more likely to have not resumed their social participation during the COVID‐19 pandemic, gender was not independently associated with frailty status in the adjusted analysis.

Among participants who had engaged in social participation before the COVID‐19 pandemic in 2020, those who had discontinued social participation from 2022 onward were found to have a significantly higher risk of frailty in 2023 (OR 2.263, 95% CI 1.158–4.422) compared to those who continued their participation. This result suggests the potential for prolonged social isolation following the pandemic to increase frailty risk in older adults. While previous research has shown a significant decline in social activities among older adults during the early stages of the pandemic, 4 followed by a trend of recovery, 24 other studies have reported the potential for even intermittent social participation to inhibit frailty progression. 25 Our finding that frailty risk was elevated in the group who discontinued participation despite having participated in 2020 underscores the importance of maintaining social connections or resuming them after a period of interruption.

Participants who were at risk of frailty based on the KCL in 2022 or reported poor self‐rated health were more likely to be classified as frail in 2023. This finding is consistent with previous studies. Prior frailty status has been shown to be an important predictor of future changes in frailty and functional decline,17, 26, 27 emphasizing the need for early screening and intervention. Although frailty is considered to be a reversible condition, it also tends to progress over time, making early identification particularly important. In addition, self‐rated health is known to reflect a comprehensive assessment of physical, mental, and social well‐being. 28 It has been associated with depressive symptoms, functional decline, and social isolation, and is recognized as a predictor of future frailty, long‐term care needs, and mortality.29, 30 Among older adults with prior experience of social participation, self‐rated health along with the KCL may serve as a practical screening indicator for frailty prevention.

Dissatisfaction with economic conditions was associated with frailty in 2023, suggesting a link between economic circumstances and frailty. While previous research has indicated an association between economic conditions and social participation in older adults,23, 31 studies directly examining the relationship between economic circumstances and KCL scores are limited. It is possible that various aspects of life assessed by the KCL are influenced by economic conditions, which, in turn, may explain the association with frailty as evaluated by the KCL. However, further research is needed to elucidate the detailed mechanisms.

Even though gender was not associated with frailty in 2023, the proportion of individuals who discontinued social participation activities continued to be higher among women, a trend consistent with past studies.12, 32 For instance, Goto et al. 12 reported that the proportion of social participation among older men decreased from 37.5% pre‐pandemic to 25.4% during the pandemic, while for women, it decreased from 45.5% to 23.8%. Similarly, Hirose et al. 32 found that women had five times higher odds of discontinuing outpatient rehabilitation due to COVID‐19 preventive measures compared to men. From the beginning of the COVID‐19 pandemic, women were reported to be more proactive in taking preventive measures and had a higher rate of discontinuing social participation activities.32, 33 Lower participation rates in social activities among women have been observed in other countries as well. 34 Moreover, it has been suggested that social participation opportunities available for modern older women may not meet their needs. 35 These findings may indicate that there are factors beyond the COVID‐19 pandemic that have contributed to women's inability to resume activities after the pandemic.

There are some limitations to this study. First, this study was conducted in a single suburban city in Japan, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. However, given their consistency with previous studies on frailty among older adults, the results may still offer valuable insights for other contexts. Second, the respondents of the survey of this study may have had a relatively high interest in social participation activities and frailty, which may have underestimated the risk associated with discontinuation of social participation activities. However, efforts were made to mitigate this bias by targeting a large and diverse population for the survey, and the consistency of the findings with previous research supports their reliability. Furthermore, this study did not collect specific data on the proportion of participants engaging in each type of social participation activity (e.g., local festivals, community activities, volunteer groups) at baseline (2020) or within the subsequent participation trajectory groups, which limits our ability to provide a detailed demographic breakdown of social engagement patterns. Finally, the assessment of frailty was based on the KCL, a validated and widely used self‐administered screening tool in Japan. While the KCL is suitable for large‐scale, community‐based postal surveys, it does not fully capture physical frailty and may be influenced by aspects such as social participation itself. Although alternative tools like Fried's Frailty Phenotype 36 or the Edmonton Frail Scale 37 provide more detailed physical assessments, these were not feasible in the context of this study because of the self‐administered questionnaire design. The choice of the KCL with a cutoff of 8 points was based on previous studies demonstrating its predictive validity for long‐term care need and mortality risk.

CONCLUSIONS

This study highlights the significant association between social participation trajectories and frailty risk in older adults. Our findings suggest that patterns of social participation are related to subsequent frailty risk, even after considering baseline frailty, self‐rated health, and economic circumstances. Further research is needed to determine the relationship between resuming social participation activities and subsequent frailty risk.
