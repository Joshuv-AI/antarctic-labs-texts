Organic–Inorganic Hybrid Hole‐Selective Layer Interface Engineering for Enhanced Photoelectrochemical Water Oxidation on Bismuth Vanadate Photoanodes
Authors: Kaige Tian, Dingyanyan Zhou, Jiafan Zhang, Zhennan Wang, Yujin Ji, Youyong Li, Hui Li, Zhong‐Yong Yuan, Shuit‐Tong Lee, Tianyi Ma, Junqing Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDue to poor carrier migration and sluggish water oxidation kinetics, bismuth vanadate (BiVO4) photoanodes show limited photoelectrochemical (PEC) performance and stability. Coupling a hole‐selective layer (HSL) can mitigate these issues. Here, we construct an organic‐inorganic hybrid HSL by combining a self‐assembled monolayer (Me‐4PACz) with NiOx on BiVO4. Through systematic control experiments that decouple the contributions of the individual layers, we quantitatively reveal a genuine synergy between NiOx and Me‐4PACz. NiOx rapidly extracts holes and provides catalytic sites. The Me‐4PACz adopts a vertical, non‐dense orientation, generating a molecular dipole that forms a continuous SAM‐NiOx‐BiVO4 energy cascade and lowers the hole transfer barrier. More importantly, we uncover a “local anchoring and remote activation” mechanism: a single Me‐4PACz binds via P–O–Ni to a surface Ni site (passivating a harmful defect), while its electron‐withdrawing effect propagates through the Ni–O–Ni lattice to convert adjacent Ni2+ into active, defect‑free Ni3+ sites. The phosphonate group further stabilizes these Ni3+ centers. The resulting SAM‐NiOx‐BiVO4 photoanode achieves a photocurrent density of 6.90 mA cm−2 at 1.23 V versus reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) and retains stable performance for over 60 h. This work expands SAM applicability in PEC water oxidation and offers a rational design principle for organic–inorganic hybrid photoanodes.

Introduction

Photoelectrochemical (PEC) water splitting offers a promising route for converting solar energy into hydrogen fuel, addressing ongoing energy and environmental challenges [1, 2, 3]. The development of efficient photoanodes represents a central challenge in developing high‐performance PEC water‐splitting systems, as the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) involves a four‐electron process that typically limits overall efficiency [4, 5, 6]. Among the many n‐type semiconductors used as photoanodes, bismuth vanadate (BiVO4) has attracted considerable attention due to its suitable bandgap (≈ 2.4 eV), non‐toxic and inexpensive raw materials, and favorable energy band positions. Unfortunately, the inherent defects of BiVO4, particularly its poor interfacial carrier transport and sluggish surface water oxidation reaction kinetics, result in PEC water oxidation performance and stability that fall significantly short of expectations [7].

In recent years, the power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with an inverted (p–i–n) architecture have achieved marked progress, driven by the development of hole‐selective layers (HSLs) and defect passivation strategies [8, 9]. Notably, the strategy of coupling HSLs also applies to BiVO4 photoanodes and has been shown to enhance carrier separation and facilitate interfacial carrier transfer [10]. The effectiveness of this approach critically depends on the choice of HSL. An excellent HSL must possess a suitable energy band structure, high conductivity, good selectivity, and robust stability [11]. Inorganic HSL materials offer stability and economic advantages but often suffer from interface energy level mismatch and high defect density. Organic HSL materials feature tunable energy levels, high hole mobility, and low‐defect interfaces, yet their environmental stability is insufficient. Therefore, hybrid interface engineering that combines the strengths of both classes is emerging as a promising strategy to achieve synergistic effects.

Among many organic materials suitable for HSLs, self‐assembled monolayer (SAM) materials stand out as a research hotspot due to their efficient carrier extraction capability and low nonradiative recombination losses [12, 13]. However, their weak bonding to the BiVO4 photoanode surface and poor wettability hinder the formation of a uniform film, resulting in undesired interfacial carrier recombination at the semiconductor/HSL interface. In the field of perovskite solar cells, this limitation has been explored in greater depth. Studies have shown that the p‑type semiconductor NiOx serves as an excellent anchoring interlayer, forming robust chemical bonds (e.g., P–O–Ni) with SAMs and enabling more complex interfacial effects. For example, SAM modification can modulate the concentration and distribution of Ni3+ in NiOx, passivate surface defects, and create a more favorable energy‑level alignment [14, 15]. Based on these findings, we propose that combining NiOx with Me‑4PACz will not only improve SAM coverage and ordering through chemical anchoring but also synergistically tune the electronic structure of NiOx via the electron‑withdrawing and coordination effects of the SAM. Moreover, as a classical inorganic HSL, NiOx has energy levels well aligned with both BiVO4 and the SAM. Hence, an organic‐inorganic hybrid HSL consisting of NiOx and Me‐4PACz is expected to enhance interfacial carrier transport.

Inspired by the above ideas, we propose a strategy to modify BiVO4 photoanodes by constructing organic‐inorganic hybrid HSLs using NiOx and SAM. By judiciously selecting [4‐(3,6‐dimethyl‐9H‐carbazol‐9‐yl)butyl]phosphonic acid (Me‐4PACz) to bond with NiOx, we develop a BiVO4‐based composite photoanode. Benefiting from this novel configuration, the new composite SAM‐NiOx‐BiVO4 photoanode achieves a high photocurrent density of 6.90 mA cm−2 at a bias of 1.23 V versus RHE and retains stable performance for over 60 h. A series of detailed physicochemical characterizations and density‐functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal the underlying mechanism by which the coupled NiOx‐SAM hybrid HSL enhances the PEC oxidation performance of the BiVO4 photoanode: NiOx extracts holes and provides catalytic sites; more importantly, we uncover a “local anchoring and remote activation” mechanism. Specifically, a single Me‐4PACz molecule binds via a P–O–Ni bond to a surface Ni site (passivating a harmful defect), while its electron‐withdrawing effect propagates through the Ni–O–Ni lattice to convert adjacent Ni2+ ions into active, defect‑free Ni3+ sites. Additionally, the Me‐4PACz establishes a favorable BiVO4‐NiOx‐SAM energy level cascade and stabilizes high‑valent Ni centers through strong P–O–Ni coordination. Unlike conventional systems such as NiOOH‑based materials that offer only catalytic activity, our hybrid interface uniquely combines energy level tuning, defect passivation, and enhanced OER activity. This work not only broadens the applicability of SAMs in PEC water oxidation but also provides a rational design principle for composite photoanodes.

Results and Discussion

In this work, the SAM‐NiOx‐BiVO4 photoanode was prepared via a sequential process. As shown in Figure 1a, BiVO4 was prepared by the classical electrodeposition‐calcination method, while NiOx and Me‐4PACz were deposited on the BiVO4 photoanode surface by the spin coating‐calcination method. More specific information about the material and synthesis methods can be found in the Supporting Information.

The Me‑4PACz molecule (Figure S1a) consists of a 3,6‑dimethyl‑9H‑carbazole group linked to a phosphonic acid group by a butyl chain. DFT calculations (Figure S1b) reveal an inhomogeneous electron cloud distribution: the carbazole group is electron‑deficient (dark blue, electrophilic) and the phosphonic acid group is electron‑rich (bright red, nucleophilic), creating a significant intrinsic dipole moment directed from the carbazole ring toward the phosphonic acid group. As shown in Figure 1b the XRD patterns of the three photoanodes. All diffraction peaks match the standard card (JCPDS PDF #14‑0688), confirming that BiVO4 is monoclinic and that the coupling of NiOx and Me‑4PACz does not alter its crystal structure. No peaks corresponding to NiOx or Me‑4PACz are discernible in the main pattern, owing to the low NiOx loading, the amorphous nature of the SAM, and the lattice mismatch between BiVO4 and NiOx, which introduces microstrain that broadens and attenuates the NiOx peaks. An enlarged view (Figure S2c) reveals weak NiOx features, confirming its presence. To further demonstrate whether Me‐4PACz is present on the surface of the SAM‐NiOx‐BiVO4 photoanodes, we compared the Raman and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) of the three photoanodes. Raman spectroscopy (Figure 1c) did not detect any signals from Me‑4PACz, likely because the conjugated organic molecule produces strong fluorescence under visible/near‑infrared excitation, which masks the Raman signal. The NiOx Raman peaks are also very weak, which can be attributed to its symmetric crystal structure and low polarizability. In contrast, FTIR (Figure 1d) clearly shows characteristic peaks corresponding to Ni─O, P─O, C─N, and C─C bonds. Comparison with the IR spectrum of pure Me‑4PACz (Figure S2d) confirms that the P─O bond originates from the ─H2PO3 group, the C─N bond is characteristic of carbazoles, and the C─C bond is attributed to Ar─CH3. These data clearly demonstrate that Me‑4PACz is present on the SAM‐NiOx‐BiVO4 photoanode surface.

To investigate the effect of the coupled NiOx‐SAM hybrid HSL on the microscopic morphology and crystal structure of BiVO4 photoanodes, we characterized three types of photoanodes by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As shown in Figure 2a, pristine BiVO4 exhibits a typical “worm‑like” porous structure derived from the “sheet‑like” BiOI precursor (Figure S3). After coupling NiOx nanoparticles, the surface roughness of these “worms” increased, and their diameters became larger (Figure 2b). In Figure 2c, the surface of the “worm” is uniformly covered with a granular “ice‑cream‑like” layer, which corresponds to the NiOx‑SAM hybrid HSL. High‐resolution TEM (HR‐TEM) analysis (Figure 2d) confirms that pristine BiVO4 exhibits lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.456 nm, characteristic of the (011) crystal plane [16]. Figure 2e clearly shows NiOx nanoparticles firmly anchored to the BiVO4 surface, displaying lattice fringes corresponding to the NiOx (200) plane. There is a significant lattice mismatch between BiVO4 (0.456 nm) and NiOx (0.209 nm), which is consistent with our earlier analysis of why the diffraction peak signal for NiOx is weaker. Notably, the TEM image in Figure 2f of the SAM‐NiOx‐BiVO4 photoanode reveals a 2–6 nm thick amorphous layer enveloping the NiOx nanoparticles, confirming the successful integration of the NiOx‐SAM hybrid HSL. Cross‑sectional SEM images (Figure 2g) provide morphological evidence for the successful coupling of the hybrid HSL on BiVO4, consistent with the structural findings from other characterizations. Energy‑dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping (Figure 2h) shows a uniform spatial distribution of Bi, V, O, Ni, C, N, and P providing atomic‑level evidence of the composite photoanode's structural integrity.

The chemical compositions and electronic states of the three photoanode surfaces were analyzed separately using x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). After coupling the NiOx‐SAM hybrid HSL (Figures S4a and S4b), the characteristic peak positions of the elements Bi and V are significantly shifted as a result of electron migration, proving the existence of a strong interaction between BiVO4 and the NiOx‐SAM hybrid HSL. The XPS spectra of the O 1s energy level (Figure S4c) reveal three distinct peaks corresponding to lattice oxygen (OL), defect‐related oxygen (commonly attributed to oxygen vacancies, OV), and chemisorbed oxygen (OC) [17, 18]. Notably, after Me‐4PACz molecules were coupled onto the surface of the NiOx‐BiVO4 photoanode, the signal intensity of the OV peak significantly decreased. This can be attributed to the passivation of OVs by the phosphonic acid groups of Me‐4PACz. The Ni 2p XPS spectra (Figure S4d) reveal the coexistence of Ni2+ and Ni3+ in the NiOx‑BiVO4 photoanode. Following the coupling of Me‐4PACz, the Ni2+/Ni3+ ratio decreased significantly, confirming that Me‐4PACz promotes the oxidation of Ni2+ to Ni3+, which is attributed to the electrophilic nature of the carbazole groups. Figures S4e and S4f show the C 1s and N 1s XPS spectra, respectively. The C 1s spectra consist of three peaks, corresponding to C─N, sp3‑hybridized C─C, and sp2‑hybridized C─C bonds. Similarly, the P 2p spectra (Figure S4g) resolve into four components, corresponding to P═O, P─O, P─O─M, and P─C bonds. The clear presence of the P─O─Ni peak provides direct evidence for the chemical anchoring of Me‐4PACz onto the NiOx surface.

To probe the influence of Me‐4PACz on NiOx, we performed x‐ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectroscopy to examine changes in the electronic structure and local coordination of the Ni element pre‐ and post‐modification. Figure 3a presents the Ni K‐edge X‐ray absorption near‐edge structure (XANES) spectra for Ni‐foil, NiOx‐BiVO4, and SAM–NiOx–BiVO4. The absorption spectra of NiOx–BiVO4 and SAM–NiOx–BiVO4 exhibit nearly identical profiles, but the average valence state of Ni in SAM–NiOx–BiVO4 is slightly higher, consistent with the partial oxidation of Ni2+ to Ni3+ observed by XPS. In the extended x‑ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra (Figure 3b) and the corresponding fit (Figure 3c), a slight decrease in the Ni─O bond length is observed, indicating a modifying effect of Me‑4PACz on NiOx. Notably, the EXAFS spectra of both NiOx–BiVO4 and SAM‐‑NiOx‑‐BiVO4 in Figure 3b show a Ni─Ni bond length longer than that in Ni foil, which is attributed to altered electronic interactions from wavefunction overlap within the Ni─O─Ni bridging structures that share common vertices. Moreover, Figure S5 and Table S1 reveal that after Me‑4PACz modification, the Ni─O bond length decreases from 2.09 Å to 2.06 Å, and the coordination number drops from 5.6 to 5.4. The bond shortening is attributed to the conversion of Ni2+ (larger ionic radius) to Ni3+ (smaller radius). Both observations are consistent with the formation of a strong P─O─Ni bond and the stabilization of Ni3+ via a strong ligand field.

A series of tests were performed to evaluate the PEC water oxidation performance of the related photoanodes. These tests were performed in a three‐electrode system with 1 M potassium borate (K─Bi) buffer saline (pH = 9.2) as the electrolyte and a PLS‐FX300HU 300 W xenon lamp (purchased from Beijing Perfectlight Technology Co., Ltd.) fitted with an AM 1.5G filter as the light source. The photocurrent density, measured by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) under illumination, serves as the principal metric. As shown in Figure 3d, the photocurrent density of the SAM–NiOx–BiVO4 photoanode reaches 6.90 mA cm−2 at a bias voltage of 1.23 V versus RHE, which is 3.3 times that of the unmodified BiVO4 photoanode. Notably, the LSV measurements were conducted at a scan rate of 10 mV s−1 without iR compensation. The forward and backward scans are nearly coincident (Figure S6a), confirming that the photocurrent arises from steady‑state water oxidation rather than capacitive or displacement currents. To decouple the contributions of the individual layers, we prepared two control samples: Me‑4PACz‐BiVO4 (without NiOx) and Me‑4PACz‐NiOx‐FTO (without BiVO4). As shown in Figure S6b, Me‑4PACz‐BiVO4 exhibits a photocurrent density of 2.84 mA cm−2, whereas Me‑4PACz‐NiOx‐FTO shows negligible current under both dark and illuminated conditions (Figure S6c), ruling out direct electrocatalytic activity of the SAM–NiOx stack. The photocurrent density of bare BiVO4 at 1.23 V versus RHE is higher than those typically reported for unmodified BiVO4, which is attributed to extended KOH immersion time after calcination that optimizes the film thickness (Figure S7) and increases the number of oxygen vacancies (Figure S8), thereby enhancing carrier separation and light absorption. The photoelectric conversion efficiency of the photoanode is also an important criterion for evaluating the performance of photoanode PEC water oxidation, which is usually composed of two parameters: the incident photon‐to‐current efficiency (IPCE) and the applied bias photon‐to‐current conversion efficiency (ABPE). As shown in Figure 3e, the NiOx‑BiVO4 photoanode achieves a maximum IPCE of 58.7% at 405 nm, compared with 24.9% for unmodified BiVO4. Notably, after further coupling with Me‑4PACz, the IPCE reaches 94.8%, confirming the beneficial impact of the NiOx‑SAM hybrid HSL on photoelectric conversion efficiency. ABPE represents the efficiency of the photoanode in converting incident photons into current under applied bias, as derived from the LSV curves under illumination using Equation S1. As expected, the SAM‐NiOx‐BiVO4 photoanode in Figure 3f achieved the highest ABPE value (2.31%) at the lowest bias voltage (0.68 V vs. RHE).

Stability is a crucial metric that must not be overlooked when assessing the PEC water oxidation performance of photoanodes, as it fundamentally determines their practical application potential. Figure 3g illustrates the chronoamperometry curves for the three photoanodes, showing that the SAM‐NiOx‐BiVO4 photoanode exhibited a mere 11.7% reduction in photocurrent density after 60 h of continuous irradiation. This decline is significantly less than that observed for the unmodified BiVO4 photoanode. To further evaluate the stability of the SAM–NiOx–BiVO4 photoanode, we conducted a 100 h durability test. As shown in Figure S9a, the photocurrent density decreased by 18.9% after the test, demonstrating good operational stability. Post‐test characterization of the sample was carried out using XRD, SEM, and XPS. Figure S9b,c indicate that both the crystal structure and microscopic morphology remained largely unchanged. XPS analysis following stability testing (Figure S10) revealed several chemical changes. The Bi 4f peak shifted toward higher binding energies, while the V 2p peak shifted slightly toward lower binding energies, consistent with the partial oxidation of Bi3+ to Bi5+ and the reduction of V5+ to V4+ caused by photocorrosion. The area of the OV peak increased (Figure S10c), indicating enhanced electronic localization at the defect sites. The area of the Ni3+ peak also increased significantly (Figure S10d), attributed to the partial oxidation of Ni2+ to Ni3+ by photogenerated holes. A C─O peak appeared in the C 1s spectra (Figure S10e), while the N 1s and P 2p signals weakened (Figure S10f,g), indicating slight hydroxylation of the benzene ring and protonation of the carbazole nitrogen and phosphonic acid groups. Despite these changes, the photocurrent remained at a high level (6.70 mA cm−2 after 100 h, Figure S10h). This long‑term stability arises from the synergistic protection of the NiOx‐SAM bilayer. NiOx rapidly extracts photogenerated holes from BiVO4, minimizing hole accumulation at the surface and suppressing photocorrosion. Meanwhile, Me‑4PACz chemically anchors to NiOx via P─O─Ni bonds, occupying coordinately unsaturated sites that are otherwise prone to dissolution. The SAM also stabilizes high‑valent Ni3+ active sites through a strong ligand field and, even after partial hydroxylation, retains its ability to passivate surface defects and maintain a favorable energy level cascade. Consequently, the hybrid bilayer preserves the structural integrity of BiVO4 while sustaining efficient hole extraction and water oxidation kinetics over extended operation. To evaluate the performance of the PEC water‐splitting cell with the SAM–NiOx–BiVO4 photoanode, we monitored the production of hydrogen and oxygen over time by gas chromatography and calculated the Faradaic efficiency using Equation S2. Figure 3h shows that following 2.5 h of illumination, the photoanode and cathode yielded 112.96 µmol of O2 and 230.18 µmol of H2, respectively. The molar ratios of these products closely align with the stoichiometric number of H and O atoms in water (2:1). Surprisingly, the average Faradaic efficiencies reached impressive values of 95.1% and 94.9%, signifying that an overwhelming majority of photogenerated carriers participated in the water‐splitting reaction. A comparison with recently reported BiVO4‑based photoanodes (Table S2) further highlights the excellent PEC performance of the SAM‑NiOx‑BiVO4 photoanode.

To investigate the origin of the enhanced PEC performance, we examined the three sequential steps of PEC water oxidation: photogenerated carrier excitation, migration, and surface reaction. Light is the driving force for electron transitions, and ultraviolet‑visible (UV‑vis) absorption spectroscopy was used to study carrier excitation [19, 20]. As shown in Figure 4a, coupling the NiOx‑SAM hybrid HSL does not substantially shift the absorption edge of BiVO4 but increases the light absorption intensity. This increase is attributed to the intrinsic light‑absorbing properties of NiOx, which is a dark‑colored material. The bandgap of a semiconductor is another crucial factor in determining the excitation of photogenerated carriers. Here, the bandgap values for the three photoanodes can be calculated using Equation S3. As presented in Figure S11a, the bandgap value of the BiVO4 photoanode exhibits minimal alteration upon coupling with the NiOx‐SAM hybrid HSL. This finding refutes the hypothesis that the enhanced water oxidation performance of the BiVO4 photoanode stems from bandgap modifications. Furthermore, the UV‐vis spectra of NiOx and Me‐4PACz, along with the calculated bandgaps (Figure S11b‐e), indicate that both NiOx and Me‐4PACz have wide bandgaps, consistent with literature reports.

The migration capacity of carriers primarily depends on the interaction between driving forces and migration resistance. In this context, the driving force for migration is primarily the built‐in electric field, while the migration resistance is mainly influenced by the resistivity of the photoanode [21, 22]. The effect of the coupled NiOx‐SAM hybrid HSL on the migration resistance of the carriers can be analyzed by the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) obtained at a bias voltage of 1.23 VRHE. As illustrated in Figure 4b, the EIS of all three photoanodes reveals two adjacent semicircles, indicating two capacitive processes. Consequently, they can be fitted using the dual R–C (resistance‐capacitance) equivalent circuit diagram depicted in Figure S12a. It is worth clarifying that the equivalent circuit in Figure S12a comprises five parameters: R s, R bulk, C bulk, R ct, and CPE. Here, R s represents the series resistance at the interface between the FTO and the semiconductor. R bulk denotes the bulk‐phase resistance of the semiconductor, corresponding to the smaller semicircle in the high‐frequency region of the Nyquist plot. This parameter reflects the resistance encountered by carriers during migration within the semiconductor bulk phase. R ct represents the charge transfer resistance at the semiconductor‐electrolyte interface, corresponding to the larger semicircle in the low‐frequency region, which reflects the resistance encountered by carriers during interfacial transfer [23, 24]. In addition, C bulk and CPE cannot be overlooked, as they represent the space charge layer capacitance and surface state capacitance, respectively. Table S3 summarizes the fitted parameters for the three photoanodes, showing that the R s values are essentially identical, indicating that the NiOx‐SAM hybrid HSL modification does not alter the ohmic contact at the FTO/photoanode interface. In contrast, the R ct decreases progressively from bare BiVO4 to NiOx‐BiVO4 and further to SAM‑NiOx‑BiVO4. Importantly, the Me‐4PACz‐BiVO4 control sample (without NiOx) in Figure S12b exhibits an R ct value lower than that of bare BiVO4 but higher than that of NiOx‐BiVO4, confirming that the SAM alone does not impede interfacial charge transfer; its dipole effect actually facilitates hole transport. It is worth noting that the significant enhancement of interfacial capacitance after modification may arise from the combined effects of pseudo‐capacitance introduced by the NiOx layer itself and chemical capacitance contributed by surface states modulated by the Me‐4PACz molecular layer. The reduced R ct together with the increased capacitance promotes hole transfer at the semiconductor‐electrolyte interface.

The strength of the built‐in electric field force is determined by the extent of energy band bending [25, 26]. The structure of the SAM‐NiOx‐BiVO4 photoanodes (Figure 4c) requires that the energy bands of the constituent materials match. We therefore measured the energy band structures of BiVO4, NiOx, and Me‑4PACz by ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS, Figure S13a–c) in combination with the known bandgaps. We also performed UPS measurements on SAM‑NiOx to evaluate how the Me‑4PACz modification influences the energy band structure of NiOx. The energy level alignment shown in Figure 4d is stepwise, forming a continuous cascade that facilitates carrier migration. Comparison of the UPS spectra of NiOx and SAM‑NiOx (Figure S13d) reveals that the Fermi level of NiOx shifts downward after SAM attachment, a result of the strong intrinsic dipole moment of Me‑4PACz. This dipole generates an interfacial electric field that drives holes in NiOx toward the interface, creating an electron depletion layer and upward energy band bending. Consequently, the valence band maximum (VBM) of NiOx shifts downward, reducing the energy barrier for hole transfer from BiVO4 to NiOx. The degree of energy band bending depends on the difference between the hole quasi‑Fermi level (E fn) and the electrolyte redox potential and can be quantified by open‑circuit photovoltage (OCP) measurements. Figure 4e shows the voltage change (ΔV OC) before and after illumination for the three photoanodes. The SAM‑‐NiOx‑‐BiVO4 photoanode exhibits a significantly larger ΔV OC than the others. The built‑in electric field strength can be quantitatively assessed using Equation S4. As shown in Figure 4f, the built‑in field of the SAM‑NiOx‑BiVO4 photoanode is 1.16 times that of bare BiVO4, a consequence of the continuous energy level cascade. Mott–Schottky (M‑‐S) measurements in the dark also probe the built‑in field strength because the extent of energy band bending is proportional to the difference between the flat‑band potential (E fb) and the operating potential [27, 28]. As depicted in Figure 4g, the successive coupling of the two HSLs induces a substantial negative shift in the flat‐band potential of the BiVO4 photoanode, thereby confirming the enhancement of the built‐in electric field. The enhanced built‐in electric field promotes carrier separation and transport, as supported by the increase in “apparent carrier density” (N d) derived from EIS data using Equation S5.

The bulk phase charge separation efficiency (η bulk), defined as the fraction of photogenerated holes that successfully traverse the semiconductor bulk phase to the semiconductor/electrolyte interface (SEI) without recombination, is a key indicator for evaluating carrier separation and transport efficiency [29, 30]. To quantify this parameter, we performed photocurrent density measurements on the three photoanodes in a 1 M K–Bi buffer solution containing 0.5 M Na2SO3 as a hole sacrificial agent (Figure S14a). In addition, we determined the light‐harvesting efficiency (LHE) (Figure S14b) and theoretical photocurrent densities (J abs) (Figure S14c) for each photoanode using Equation S6. These parameters are crucial for the calculation of η bulk. Figure 4h illustrates the η bulk curves of the three photoanodes calculated using Equation S7. It is evident that the coupled NiOx‐SAM hybrid HSL significantly enhances the η bulk value of the BiVO4 photoanode from 86.9% to 98.8% at a bias voltage of 1.23 VRHE. This improvement indicates a substantial enhancement in carrier separation and transport capabilities. To assess any photothermal contribution, we monitored the surface temperature of BiVO4 and SAM‑NiOx‑BiVO4 photoanodes under continuous illumination using an infrared thermal imager (Figure S15a‐m). The temperature remained essentially constant for both, ruling out the possibility that the SAM elevates the surface temperature and thereby enhances carrier separation via a thermal effect. Intensity‐modulated photovoltage spectroscopy (IMVS) was conducted under open‐circuit conditions, where no water oxidation reaction occurs on the photoanode surface [31, 32]. Consequently, IMVS can also be utilized to evaluate carrier mobility ability. The photogenerated carrier lifetime (τ rec) can be derived from the IMVS response curves (Figure S16a‐c) using Equation S8. Evidently, under equivalent light intensity conditions, the SAM‐NiOx‐BiVO4 photoanode demonstrates a markedly higher τ rec value than the other two photoanodes (Figure 4i). This observation suggests that the carriers within the SAM–NiOx–BiVO4 photoanode possess prolonged lifetimes, a phenomenon that is a result of enhanced carrier migration ability.

Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) is an advanced characterization technique based on atomic force microscopy (AFM) that can be used to examine the potential distribution on the surface of a photoanode. Since no redox reactions occur on the semiconductor surface during the test, KPFM under in situ irradiation can be used to visually determine the carrier separation efficiency inside the semiconductor. As shown in Figure 5a‐c, the surface potential of the SAM–NiOx–BiVO4 photoanode changed by 239.82 mV before and after the illumination, which is much larger than that of the NiOx–BiVO4 and BiVO4 photoanodes. Clearly, the carrier separation efficiency within the SAM–NiOx–BiVO4 photoanode is the highest. The chopper illumination profile in Figure S17a also confirms the above conclusion. Photoluminescence spectroscopy (PL) is effective at detecting fluorescence signals from shallow photoanodes and has therefore been a method used to study carrier radiative recombination [33, 34, 35]. Figure 5d shows the PL signals detected by three photoanodes at the excitation wavelength of 375 nm. Bare BiVO4 exhibits a strong emission peak at 507 nm. This peak is slightly blue‑shifted relative to the typical band‑edge emission of BiVO4 (≈ 517 nm, bandgap 2.4 eV), which we attribute to a widened bandgap of approximately 2.43 eV caused by oxygen vacancies introduced during the KOH treatment. Therefore, the 507 nm emission is assigned to band‑edge recombination rather than deep‑level defect recombination. Upon NiOx deposition, the PL intensity is markedly quenched, indicating suppressed bulk phase recombination. Modification with Me‐4PACz further reduces the PL signal, which is consistent with energy band structure optimization.

Photoelectrochemical (PEC) water splitting is an electrochemical process at semiconductor electrodes under illumination that converts solar energy into chemical energy (oxygen or hydrogen). The kinetics of water oxidation can be investigated using electrochemical characterization. Overpotential is the additional potential required to drive the reaction beyond the thermodynamic equilibrium potential; a lower overpotential indicates a more effective catalyst at lowering the activation energy barrier and accelerating charge transfer. Figure S17b and Table S4 show the dark‑state LSV curves for the three photoanodes and the overpotentials at 1 mA cm−2. The SAM‑NiOx‑BiVO4 photoanode exhibits the smallest overpotential, indicating that the NiOx‑SAM hybrid HSL effectively reduces the activation energy barrier for the surface water oxidation reaction. The Tafel slope reveals the sensitivity of the reaction rate to the overpotential, serving as a core parameter for quantifying the kinetic properties of electrode reactions. A smaller Tafel slope indicates faster reaction kinetics and higher catalyst activity. In Figure S17c, the Tafel slope of the SAM‐NiOx‐BiVO4 photoanode is much smaller than that of the BiVO4 photoanode, confirming faster reaction kinetics. The electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) is the fraction of the electrode surface that participates in electrochemical reactions. A larger ECSA typically exposes more active sites, enhancing the water oxidation rate. Figures S18a‐c and 5e show the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves for the three photoanodes and the double‑layer capacitance (C dl) derived from Equation S9. The SAM‑NiOx‑BiVO4 photoanode has the highest C dl value, which facilitates the rapid oxidation reaction between photogenerated holes and water molecules at the interface. In addition, we measured the ECSA and C dl of the Me‑4PACz‐BiVO4 photoanode (Figure S18d and e). Its C dl is slightly lower than that of bare BiVO4, reflecting incomplete coverage of the BiVO4 surface by Me‑4PACz molecules.

The wettability of the electrolyte on the photoanode surface plays a critical role in modulating the efficiency of the surface water oxidation reaction. Enhanced wettability facilitates closer and more extensive contact with water molecules, thereby accelerating the reaction kinetics at the solid‐liquid interface. As shown in Figure S19a‐c, the unmodified BiVO4 photoanode exhibits poor wettability with water molecules. After coupling with NiOx, the contact angle between water molecules and the BiVO4 photoanode is significantly reduced, owing to the exceptional water absorption capability of NiOx. A slight increase in the water contact angle on the NiOx–BiVO4 photoanode follows its further coupling with Me‐4PACz. This phenomenon can be attributed to the molecular orientation of Me‐4PACz on the NiOx surface. In contrast, Me‐4PACz deposited directly onto BiVO4 (without NiOx) yields a smaller contact angle (Figure S19d). The lower contact angle on BiVO4 indicates that the SAM orientation is disordered in the absence of the NiOx anchoring layer, whereas the higher contact angle on NiOx suggests that the carbazole groups are arranged more perpendicularly, under the linkage of the P‐O‐Ni bond. Specifically, the Me‑4PACz molecule has a phosphonic acid group and a large π‑conjugated 3,6‑dimethyl‑9H‑carbazole group. During spin coating, it attaches to NiOx via P─O─Ni bonds (Figure S20). The carbazole group tends to align parallel to the NiOx surface to maximize contact area and minimize steric hindrance, while the butyl chain extends perpendicular to the surface. This configuration results in a predominantly vertical molecular orientation, with the outermost carbazole group imparting hydrophobic character and thus reduced wettability. The slight decrease in wettability does not mean that Me‑4PACz blocks the electrolyte entirely. The water contact angle of SAM‑NiOx‑BiVO4 remains well below the hydrophobic threshold (>90°), and the Me‑4PACz‐NiOx‐FTO electrode shows negligible dark current (Figure S6c), confirming that the SAM does not form a dense barrier. Owing to steric hindrance, Me‑4PACz molecules do not fully cover the NiOx nanoparticles. Electrolytes with high ionic strength can still permeate the gaps between Me‑4PACz molecules and reach the NiOx surface. Moreover, the strong hydrophilicity of the phosphonic acid group ensures that hydroxyl ions in the electrolyte can access NiOx. Thus, assisted by the hydrophilic phosphonic acid groups, electrolyte ions penetrate the molecular gaps to reach NiOx active sites. When photogenerated holes migrate to this interface, highly active Ni sites drive efficient alkaline water oxidation, mitigating any negative impact of the modestly reduced wettability.

Transient photocurrent curves at 1.23 V versus RHE provide insight into the kinetics of surface water oxidation. Under these conditions, bulk phase recombination is minimized, so the transient response mainly reflects surface reaction dynamics. As shown in Figure 5f, a distinct spike appears immediately upon illumination, then gradually decays to a steady‑state value. This behavior captures the transient accumulation of photogenerated holes at the interface, followed by their depletion as the system equilibrates [36]. To compare decay kinetics, the transient curves were normalized (Equation S10). The normalized decay curves (Figure S21) show that bare BiVO4 has the shortest relative carrier lifetime, consistent with dominant surface hole recombination and low extraction efficiency. After NiOx deposition, surface recombination is suppressed. NiOx acts as a hole transport layer, providing an efficient extraction pathway and shifting the dominant process from surface recombination to transport; consequently, the normalized decay lifetime increases substantially. After further modification with Me‑4PACz, the relative carrier lifetime of SAM‑NiOx‑BiVO4 increases further. This is attributed to suppression of surface recombination by the SAM through energy level modulation and surface passivation. Notably, the transient spike amplitude of SAM‐NiOx‐BiVO4 is very small. This is because the introduction of the SAM effectively suppresses surface hole accumulation, allowing holes to be extracted or to participate in the water oxidation reaction almost immediately upon reaching the surface, thereby reducing the transient spike amplitude. The surface charge injection efficiency (η surface) serves as a quantitative metric, representing the proportion of photogenerated holes that participate in the water oxidation reaction compared to the total population of holes arriving at the semiconductor‐electrolyte interface [37]. This parameter can be directly derived from Equation S11, providing a critical metric for evaluating the kinetics of the surface water oxidation reaction. As anticipated, the η surface value of the unmodified BiVO4 photoanode is merely 33.1% (Figure 5g), whereas the η surface value of the SAM‐NiOx‐BiVO4 photoanode reaches as high as 94.3%, indicating enhanced surface water oxidation reaction kinetics due to the coupled NiOx‐SAM hybrid HSL.

Time‐resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectroscopy provides an indirect yet powerful means to probe the kinetics of water oxidation reactions on photoanode surfaces by examining the intricate processes of photogenerated carriers [38, 39]. To this end, we conducted TRPL measurements on three distinct photoanodes, with the resulting decay profiles analyzed and fitted using a double exponential function model to extract relevant kinetic parameters. The kinetic profile is characterized by two distinct components: a fast decay component (τ 1) and a slow decay component (τ 2). Among these, τ 1 is primarily associated with non‐radiative recombination, while τ 2 is mainly related to radiative recombination [40]. Additionally, the average carrier lifetime (τ avg) can be determined using Equation S12, offering a comprehensive metric for evaluating carrier dynamics. As shown in Figure 5h and Table S5, coupling with the NiOx‐SAM hybrid HSL increases both τ 1 and τ 2, as well as the average carrier lifetime τ avg. The increase in τ 1 indicates passivation of nonradiative recombination centers, such as oxygen vacancies on NiOx, consistent with the decreased OV XPS signal (Figure S4c). The decrease in the Ni2+/Ni3+ ratio (Figure S4d) shows that the density of active Ni3+ sites is not reduced but increased. This is explained by our proposed “local anchoring and long‑range activation” mechanism: a single Me‑4PACz molecule anchors via a P─O─Ni bond to a surface Ni site (deactivating that site and passivating adjacent oxygen vacancies), while its electron‑withdrawing effect propagates through the Ni─O─Ni lattice to convert multiple adjacent Ni2+ into defect‑free Ni3+ active sites. Thus, the SAM selectively passivates harmful defects while remotely increasing the density of nearby active Ni3+ sites. The increase in τ 2 reflects a longer apparent radiative recombination lifetime, consistent with the suppression of nonradiative pathways. The extended τ avg confirms an overall reduction in carrier recombination losses. It is worth noting that the steady‑state PL intensity decreases (Figure 5d), whereas the TRPL lifetime increases. This observation is explained by two distinct effects: the reduction in steady‑state PL intensity arises from efficient hole extraction by NiOx and the SAM (reducing the concentration of carriers available for radiative recombination), while the prolonged TRPL lifetime originates from passivation of surface defects by the SAM (suppressing nonradiative recombination). These synergistic effects stem from the NiOx‑SAM hybrid HSL, which enhances the built‑in electric field, accelerates surface water oxidation kinetics, and enables selective passivation of harmful defects.

Intensity‑modulated photocurrent spectroscopy (IMPS) probes the kinetics of photogenerated charge transport, recombination, and surface reactions. Its low‑frequency semicircle reflects the surface reaction process, with a radius inversely proportional to the surface reaction resistance (R surface). A smaller R surface indicates a lower energy barrier and faster water oxidation kinetics. Among the IMPS curves of the three photoanodes (Figure S22a‐c), the low‑frequency semicircle of SAM‑NiOx‑BiVO4 has the largest radius, meaning the smallest R surface and the fastest kinetics. Additionally, the carrier migration time (τ trans), an essential parameter for evaluating carrier migration kinetics, can be derived from Equation S13. As shown in Figure 5i, τ trans significantly decreases with increasing applied bias voltage, indicating a substantial improvement in the migration efficiency of photogenerated holes. Moreover, at the same bias voltages, the SAM–NiOx–BiVO4 photoanode demonstrates a considerably lower τ trans compared to the pristine BiVO4 photoanode, directly attributed to the built‐in electric field generated by the coupled NiOx‐SAM hybrid HSL, which promotes interfacial carrier transport.

Subsequently, we used density‐functional theory (DFT) calculations to theoretically explore the modification of NiOx by Me‐4PACz. The NiO (100) surface was chosen as the model because it is atomically equivalent to the experimentally observed (200) facet (TEM, Figure 2e). Figure 6a shows the OER activity diagrams of NiOx and SAM‐NiOx, respectively, as well as the adsorption configurations of the intermediates at each stage of OER. The free energy of SAM‐NiOx is lower than that of NiOx in the *OH–*O stage, indicating that coupling with Me‐4PACz effectively reduces the energy barrier for O─H bond cleavage. This facilitates the rapid formation of *O intermediates. In addition, we calculated the charge density differences of SAM‐NiOx. As shown in Figure 6b,c, the Ni atoms in NiOx are in the electron‐deficient region (blue shading) under the modification of Me‐4PACz. This observation suggests that Ni2+ loses electrons and is thus partially oxidized to Ni3+, which is consistent with previous experimental results. Figure 6d illustrates the projected density of states (PDOS) for the d‐band centers of Ni atoms functioning as active sites for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) in both NiOx and SAM‐NiOx. The analysis uncovers that the d‐band centers in SAM‐NiOx are situated closer to the Fermi energy level (E F) compared to NiOx. This proximity signifies a higher density of d‐electrons near E F, thereby enhancing electrical conductivity and facilitating more efficient carrier migration. The position of the d‐band center also acts as a key indicator for assessing the adsorption strength of OER intermediates on the catalyst surface. A reduced distance between the d‐band center and the E F signifies an increased degree of orbital hybridization between the d electrons and adsorbed species, leading to a stronger adsorption affinity. Obviously, the Ni atom in SAM‐NiOx has a greater adsorption strength for OER intermediates, which is attributed to the increase in the proportion of Ni3+ in NiOx due to the modification of Me‐4PACz. The unique d7 electronic configuration of Ni3+, characterized by a greater number of unpaired electrons in its outer orbital, facilitates stronger interactions with *O intermediates. This enhanced adsorption promotes the formation of O–O bonds. Notably, the calculations employ an ideal, anhydrous NiO (100) surface, whereas the actual NiOx surface is hydroxylated and contains defects, and the OER occurs in an aqueous environment. Surface hydroxylation and solvation are known to lower the adsorption energies of OER intermediates and reduce reaction barriers. Therefore, the absolute barrier values may be overestimated, but the relative trend, a barrier reduction after SAM modification, remains reliable.

Integrating the aforementioned characterization and analysis, we propose a plausible mechanism by which the NiOx‑SAM hybrid HSL modifies the BiVO4 photoanode. The BiVO4 photoanode suffers from severe bulk phase and surface recombination due to its low carrier mobility and sluggish water oxidation kinetics, which limit its PEC performance. After coupling the NiOx‑SAM hybrid HSL (Figure 6e,f), the enhanced performance of the SAM‑NiOx‑BiVO4 photoanode arises from the synergistic interplay between NiOx and Me‑4PACz. First, NiOx acts as a hole‑selective layer that efficiently extracts photogenerated holes from BiVO4 and provides the primary catalytic sites for water oxidation. The Me‑4PACz, anchored via P─O─Ni bonds, adopts a vertical molecular orientation that creates a dipole layer, thereby establishing a continuous BiVO4‐NiOx‐Me‑4PACz energy level cascade. This orientation increases the degree of energy band bending, strengthens the built‑in electric field, and promotes interfacial carrier migration. Second, when a Me‑4PACz molecule is anchored to a surface Ni site via a P─O─Ni bond, that anchored site loses its catalytic activity due to coordination saturation and simultaneously passivates adjacent oxygen vacancies (local anchoring effect), effectively reducing carrier recombination at the surface. Concurrently, the strong electron‐withdrawing effect of the carbazole group in Me‑4PACz propagates through the Ni─O─Ni lattice network, converting multiple adjacent Ni2+ sites into defect‐free Ni3+ active sites (long‐range activation effect), thereby creating more OER active sites. These newly formed Ni3+ sites possess a d7 electronic configuration, which optimizes the adsorption of OER intermediates and lowers the energy barrier of the *OH‐*O step, significantly accelerating the kinetics of the surface water oxidation reaction. Finally, the phosphonic acid groups in Me‑4PACz act as strong‐field ligands, stabilizing these high‐valent Ni3+ centers via P─O─Ni coordination bonds and preventing their reduction or deactivation during the reaction. Notably, Me‑4PACz does not impede electrolyte access. The nanoscale gaps between molecules allow the electrolyte to penetrate to the NiOx surface, where the newly formed Ni3+ sites rapidly catalyze the water oxidation reaction. Consequently, the combined effects of energy level alignment, defect passivation, and Ni2+ activation/Ni3+ stabilization lead to a marked increase in the photocurrent density of the BiVO4 photoanode and enhance its long‑term stability.

Conclusion

In summary, we propose a generalized strategy for modifying the BiVO4 photoanodes using NiOx‐SAM hybrid HSL. Detailed physicochemical characterization and density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal the contributions of the individual layers and their synergistic effect. The NiOx layer rapidly extracts holes and provides active catalytic sites for the water oxidation reaction. Meanwhile, the vertically oriented Me‐4PACz generates a molecular dipole, establishing a continuous energy level cascade (BiVO4–NiOx–Me–4PACz) that lowers the hole transfer barrier. Importantly, we uncover a “local anchoring and remote activation” mechanism: Me‑4PACz anchors to a surface Ni site via a P─O─Ni bond, passivating a harmful defect (local anchoring), while its electron‑withdrawing effect propagates through the Ni─O─Ni lattice to convert adjacent Ni2+ into defect‑free Ni3+ active sites (remote activation). The phosphonic acid group further stabilizes these high‑valent Ni3+ centers through a strong ligand field. The non‑dense SAM leaves nanoscale gaps that allow electrolyte access, ensuring that the active Ni3+ sites remain accessible. Benefiting from this synergistic mechanism, the SAM–NiOx–BiVO4 photoanode achieves a high photocurrent density of 6.90 mA cm−2 at a bias voltage of 1.23 V versus RHE and maintains stable operation for more than 60 h. The results of this work are expected to greatly expand the application of SAM materials in the field of PEC water oxidation, and the proposed novel configuration will also provide a design principle of future integrated photoanodes for efficient solar energy conversion.
