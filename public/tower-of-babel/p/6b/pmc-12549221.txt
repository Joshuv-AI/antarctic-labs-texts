Differential impact of manganese on glutamate clearance, electroencephalography, and sleep in Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Rebecca A. Buchanan, Alec T. Kramer, Erin S. Calipari, Aaron B. Bowman, William P. Nobis, Fiona E. Harrison
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONUnderlying glutamate dysregulation in Alzheimer's disease can be worsened by environmental factors like manganese (Mn) exposure. This study examined how excess Mn affects glutamatergic signaling and neurotransmission in a beta‐amyloid mouse model.METHODSAPP/PSEN1 and control mice were exposed to systemic Mn subcutaneously. Gene expression, glutamate clearance dynamics, electroencephalography (EEG) activity, and sleep architecture were analyzed using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), Western blot, ex vivo hippocampal slices, and EEG recordings.RESULTSMn exposure elevated brain Mn levels and altered glutamate dynamics in both WT and APP/PSEN1 mice. Wild‐type (WT) mice showed faster glutamate clearance, increased spiking, disrupted sleep, and brain wave changes. APP/PSEN1 mice exhibited slower glutamate clearance, altered gene expression, increased glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and changes in non–rapid eye movement (NREM) delta and rapid eye movement (REM) alpha power.DISCUSSIONMn exposure altered glutamate clearance and brain activity, particularly in WT mice. APP/PSEN1 mice showed impaired clearance, limited gene expression changes, and altered EEG patterns, suggesting distinct or pre‐existing compensatory mechanisms.Highlights Manganese exposure significantly alters glutamate clearance dynamics differentially in wild‐type and APP/PSEN1 mouse models of Alzheimer's disease.Acute manganese treatment disrupts sleep architecture, evidenced by changes in electroencephalography (EEG) patterns and vigilance states.APP/PSEN1 mice exhibit slower glutamate clearance and altered gene expression compared to wild‐type mice following manganese exposure.Distinct brain wave frequency shifts were observed in response to manganese treatment, particularly affecting delta, theta, and alpha rhythms.Findings suggest a potential exacerbation of excitatory/inhibitory imbalances due to environmental manganese exposure in Alzheimer's pathology.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common form of dementia worldwide, with a complex etiology that is influenced by age, genetics, and environmental factors. Risk conferred from environmental factors is modifiable, but is the least understood, including those relating to neurotoxic metal exposure. Manganese (Mn) is an essential trace metal that serves as a cofactor for metalloenzymes involved in moderating oxidative stress and contributing to the glutamine/glutamate cycle through enzymes such as mitochondrial superoxide dismutase and astrocytic glutamine synthetase, respectively. 1 , 2 , 3 Overexposure to Mn occurs through different occupational and environmental settings, with inhalation and consumption of Mn‐enriched drinking water posing the greatest exposure risks in both occupational and domestic settings, although the relative exposure in each case varies. Chronic high exposure can result in the development of manganism, a Parkinsonian neurological disorder characterized by bradykinesia, tremor, and rigidity, 4 in which the greatest Mn accumulation is found in the basal ganglia. 5 In contrast, the influence of chronic but more moderate Mn brain accumulation on the development and progression of other neurodegenerative diseases, such as AD, is less understood.

AD and Mn neurotoxicity share pathophysiological mechanisms, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, apoptosis, and neuroinflammation. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Efficient glutamate clearance is necessary to prevent excitotoxicity, and excitation–inhibition imbalance is a major contributor to neuronal cell death that underlies behavioral phenotypes in both AD and Mn neurotoxicity. Glutamate clearance occurs predominantly by action of astrocytes via GLAST and GLT‐1 in rodents (EAAT1 and EAAT2, respectively, in humans). Lower GLT‐1 expression and activity in brain tissue correlate with neurodegeneration and clinical signs of patients with AD, 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 with similar decreased expression reported in murine models of AD. 14 Mn exposure can decrease expression of GLAST and GLT‐1 in astrocytes, both in vivo in rodent studies and in vitro, 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 and directly drives excitotoxicity from disruption of glutamatergic signaling through mechanisms such as impaired energy metabolism. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22

Disruptions in the excitatory/inhibitory balance within neuronal circuits lead to aberrant epileptiform activity. 23 Such abnormal activity and seizures are more prevalent in familial and sporadic AD patient populations compared to cognitively healthy populations of the same age. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 Further, the presence of this activity in AD patient subpopulations is associated with earlier and accelerated cognitive decline compared to AD patients without epileptiform activity. 28 , 29 , 30 Spontaneous interictal spiking and seizures are also reported in multiple mouse lines that carry mutations in genes associated with AD. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 This irregular neuronal activity could further drive excitotoxicity and therefore be a facilitator of the neuronal degeneration observed in AD. 36 , 37 , 38 Critically, case studies of humans exposed to either chronic or acute high doses of Mn report accumulation in the brain resulting in seizures in these individuals. 39 , 40 We have previously demonstrated a direct link between sub‐acute Mn exposure and increased susceptibility to induced seizures in an animal model of AD. 41

Changes in sleep architecture and electroencephalography (EEG) spectral data, reflecting shifts in power within different frequency bands (i.e., Delta (0.5–4 Hz), Theta (4–8 Hz), Alpha (8–12 Hz), etc.), are also a defining feature between AD and cognitively healthy populations. 42 , 43 , 44 Although only a relatively small number of studies have quantified shifts of these different brain waves in mouse models of AD, the changes that have been demonstrated are consistent with alterations seen in prodromal AD. 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49

Given the critical role of efficient glutamate clearance and impairments that are observed both in AD and with Mn exposure independently, and the known EEG‐related changes within AD populations, we sought to determine if there is a combined impact of Mn exposure in the context of rodent AD pathology model systems. We used the APP/PSEN1 mouse model of amyloidopathy to observe glutamate clearance in ex vivo hippocampal slices, and further interrogated EEG activity in response to Mn exposure. Through this work, our goal was to address the gaps in knowledge relating to the potential role of Mn exposure on glutamatergic signaling and neurotransmission within vulnerable populations.

METHODS

Experimental animals

The current study used APPSWE/PSEN1ΔE9 transgenic mice (APP/PSEN1; APP: amyloid precursor protein, PSEN1: presenilin 1) 50 (Jackson Laboratories, RRID:MMRRC_034832‐JAX) on the C57BL/6J background (Jackson Laboratories, RRID:IMSR_JAX:000664) and their non‐transgenic wild‐type (WT) littermates. APP/PSEN1 mice were first obtained from The Jackson Laboratory and were then subsequently bred in‐house. Genotyping was performed by Transnetyx using promoter sequences provided by The Jackson Laboratory (5′ à 3′ GTGTGATCCATTCCATCAGC, GGATCTCTGAGGGGTCCAGT, ATGGTAGAGTAAGCGAGAACACG). Mice were weaned at 21–28 days and housed in groups of 2–5 of a single sex in a temperature‐ and humidity‐controlled housing room on a 12:12 light:dark cycle with food and water available ad libitum. Mice used for cell culture studies were euthanized on post‐natal day 4. For all other in vivo experiments, mice were aged to 6 months. APP/PSEN1 mice produce amyloid beta oligomers throughout their life but exhibit sparse or no amyloid plaque formation until approximately 6 months, and do not exhibit robust cognitive decline until approximately 8 months of age. 51 , 52 Approximately equal numbers of male and female mice were used in each genotype‐treatment group. Genotypes were established through DNA from a tail snip at weaning and confirmed following euthanasia. All protocols were approved by the Vanderbilt University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Acute manganese injections

Acute Mn injections were performed as previously described. 53 In brief, manganese chloride tetrahydrate (MnCl2 • 4(H2O), Fisher Scientific) was dissolved in deionized water as a 1% stock solution, filtered through a 0.2 mm membrane, and administered at 50 mg/kg body weight. Saline or sterile water was used as a control injection. Treatment was administered at a volume of 5 mL/kg injected subcutaneously in the left or right (alternating) inguinal area using an insulin syringe (27 G, ½ inch). The 50 mg/kg MnCl2 • 4(H2O) dose corresponds to a dose of 13.8 mg/kg Mn2+. Mice were exposed acutely (1‐week exposure) at 6 months of age using a previously adapted exposure paradigm demonstrating that repeated subcutaneous doses at 50 mg/kg resulted in significantly more brain Mn accumulation compared to a single dose. 54 We have previously confirmed that repeated exposure of 50 mg/kg over 1 week results in significantly increased brain Mn. 41 , 53 Mice were given Mn on days 1, 4, and 7 prior to euthanasia and tissue acquisition for biochemical studies or prior to kainic acid (KA) exposure experiments on day 8 (24 h after the final injection).

Tissue collection

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane prior to cervical dislocation. Cardiac perfusion was then performed with 12 mL of 1× phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) solution. Following perfusion, the liver and brain were removed. Using a scalpel and small dissecting spatula, a portion of the right and left cerebral cortex was removed, and the entire right and left hippocampi were separately removed. 55 The tissue was frozen on dry ice and stored at −80°C until used in biochemical and molecular analyses.

Tissue manganese levels

Mn concentrations were measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS) from a protein lysate (described below in the Western blot section) using the methods previously described. 53 Briefly, 50 µL protein lysate was digested at a 1:1 dilution in ultrapure nitric acid (70%) for 24–48 h at 70°C (water bath); 100 µL of digested tissue was further diluted to 2% nitric acid with deionized water and Mn subsequently analyzed. Sample concentrations were calculated relative to a standard curve generated with values from 0–1000 µg/L.

Adeno‐associated virus injections

Under 2%–5% isoflurane anesthesia and using sterile technique, mice received bilateral intrahippocampal injections in the CA1 region (from bregma: ± 1.8 mm lateral‐medial, −2.0 mm anterior–posterior, and −1.5 mm dorsal–ventral) of pENN.AAV9.GFAP.iGluSnFr.WPRE.SV40 (AAV9.GFAP.iGluSnFr; Addgene, #98930‐AVV9) of ∼400 µL per injection. Mice received analgesic (ketoprofen, 10 mg/kg, subcutaneously) prior to and following surgery to ensure at least 48 h coverage. Approximately 2.5 weeks were allowed for viral incorporation prior to imaging experiments.

Ex vivo hippocampal slice collection

Acute brain slices were prepared from 6‐month‐old WT and APP/PSEN1 mice that had received bilateral intrahippocampal injections of AAV9.GFAP.iGluSnFr. Mice were decapitated under isoflurane, and their brains were removed quickly and placed in an ice‐cold N‐methyl‐D‐glucamine (NMDG) slicing solution containing 92 nM NMDG, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 30 mm NaHCO3, 20 mM HEPES, 25 mM glucose, 2 mM thiourea, 5 mM Na‐ascorbate, 3 mM Na‐pyruvate, 0.5 mM CaCl2‐2H2O, and 10 mM MgSO4‐7H2O. NMDG solution was oxygenated and equilibrated with 95% O2/5% CO2. Hemisected sagittal slices (300 µm) were prepared using a vibratome (model VT1200S, Leica Biosystems). Slices were transferred to a holding chamber containing NMDG solution warmed to 37°C and slowly returned to room temperature over the course of 30 min. Slices were then transferred to oxygenated ACSF at room temperature containing 125 mm NaCl, 2.4 mm KCl, 1.2 mm NaH2PO4, 25 mm NaHCO3, 25 mm glucose, 2 mm CaCl2, and 1 mm MgCl2, and were maintained under these incubation conditions until recording.

Ex vivo hippocampal slice stimulation and data acquisition

Slices were transferred to a submerged recording chamber continuously perfused at 2.0 mL/min with oxygenated ACSF maintained at 30 ± 2°C. The CA1 region of the hippocampus was anatomically identified on a microscope (Slicescope II, Scientifica). Slices were stimulated with a monopolar electrode in an ACSF‐containing borosilicate glass micropipette with the grounding wire placed in the ACSF bath controlled by a SIU91 constant current isolator (Cygnus Tech) and triggered by TTL (pClamp 11, Molecular Devices). iGluSnFr excitation and emission wavelengths are filtered using a standard green fluorescent protein (GFP) filter cube and collected through a 10× objective. iGluSnFr responses were evoked by a 20‐ms pulse of 100 Hz at a stimulus intensity of 75 µA. The iGluSnFr transients were recorded using MicroManager, with 4 × 4 pixel binning and an exposure time of 1 ms and an acquisition rate of approximately 40 frames per second. Each slice was stimulated three separate times with 2 min between stimulations. Images were analyzed using ImageJ. Fluorescent intensity per frame was acquired in a 100 pixel area circle five pixels from the electrode tip. Baseline fluorescence prior to stimulation was collected and subtracted from the change in fluorescence following stimulation (ΔF/F0).

Primary astrocyte culture

Primary astrocytes were isolated from day 4 postnatal mouse brain as previously described. 56 Mouse pups were sacrificed by decapitation after being sprayed with 70% ethanol. The brain was removed and placed into a dissecting dish filled with Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS) (Corning, cat # 21‐21‐CV) on ice, and additional dissection procedures were performed under a dissection microscope. Tail tissue was collected, and samples were genotyped for APP/PSEN1 and sex. Cortical tissue was isolated, and the meninges were carefully removed. Cortical tissue was cut into small pieces and transferred into a 5 mL microcentrifuge tube and digested with 0.5% trypsin–ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (trypsin–EDTA) (Gibco, cat # 15‐400‐054) in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 30 min, shaking every 5–10 min and then neutralized with astrocyte medium (DMEM (Gibco, cat # 11995‐065) supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma, cat # F2442) and 1× penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, cat # 15140‐122) at 37°C for 5 min. Cells were then centrifuged at 100x g for 5 min, and the supernatant was decanted. The dissociated tissue was resuspended using the same astrocyte medium described above, and the remaining tissue was triturated using a P1000 pipette and then a P200 pipette to dissociate cells into a single cell suspension. Cells were plated on poly‐L‐lysine (Sigma, cat # 25988‐63‐0) coated T25 flasks (Thermo Scientific, cat # 156367) and housed in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% carbon dioxide (CO2) until ∼80% confluent. The medium was changed every 2–3 days, and dishes were shaken at 180 rpm for a minimum of 30 min after reaching ∼50% confluence to remove microglia and generate an astrocyte‐enriched culture.

RNA Isolation and quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Cells

Primary astrocytes were cultured as described above in T25 flasks (two flasks per pup) until ∼80% confluency and were then treated with either 100 µM MnCl2 • 4(H2O) or vehicle in media so that one flask of cells from each pup received each treatment. Following 24 h incubation with Mn or vehicle‐containing media, cells were washed with Dulbecco's PBS (DPBS), and RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, cat # 74134) according to the manufacturer's protocol. cDNA was made using High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit according to the manufacturer's protocol (Applied Biosystems, cat # 4368814). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using a BioRad CFX96 thermocycler using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermofisher, cat # A25742) expression. Relative expression of genes (Table S1) was estimated using the delta–delta Ct method and was normalized to the geomean of glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as a reference gene. All samples were run in triplicate.

Brain tissue

RNA was extracted and purified from cortical tissues using an RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini Kit (Qiagen, cat # 1023539) following the manufacturer's protocol, and RNA concentration was quantified using the Nanodrop 2000 system (Thermo Scientific, cat # ND‐2000). RNA samples were confirmed for purity (A260:A230 ratio > 1.7 and A260:A280 ratio > 1.8), and 0.5 µg of total RNA was used for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis using the RT2 First Strand Kit (Qiagen, cat # 330404) following the manufacturer protocol. Each sample was combined with the RT2 SYBR Green qPCR Mastermix (Qiagen, cat # 330503) and was loaded in a RT2 Profiler PCR Array plate containing primers for Mouse GABA and Glutamate related genes (Qiagen, cat # PAMM‐152ZD‐24) and run on Bio‐Rad CFX96 thermocyclers following the manufacturer protocol. Data were analyzed using the Qiagen GeneGlobe Design & Analysis Hub (geneglobe.qiagen.com), and changes in gene expression were quantified based on the average geometric means of five different housekeeping genes (Actb, B2m, Gapdh, Gusb, and Hsp90ab1). p‐Value was calculated based on a Student's t‐test of the replicate 2^(‐ΔCT) (gene expression) for values for each gene in the control group (WT treated with vehicle) and experimental groups. Fold change (2^(‐ΔΔCT)) is the normalized gene expression in the experimental groups divided by the normalized gene expression in the control group.

Western blot

Protein lysates were prepared by homogenizing frozen tissue using a Bullet Blender STORM with appropriate beads (Next Advance, cat # ZROB05) in 250 µL Pierce RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Scientific, cat # 89900) with protease and phosphatase inhibitors [cOmplete™ EDTA‐free phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 04693132001), 1:100 phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 3 (MilliporeSigma, cat. # P0044), and 1 mM sodium orthovanadate per 10 mL of RIPA buffer. Protein lysate was collected following centrifugation at 12,000 g for 5 min. Protein concentration was measured using the standard bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay protocol (Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Scientific, cat # 23225). Samples were denatured with NuPAGE lithium dodecyl sulfate (LDS) sample buffer (Thermo Scientific, cat # NP0007) and NuPAGE reducing agent (Thermo Scientific, cat # NP0009), and samples were loaded into Bolt 4%–12% Bis‐Tris Plus gels (Thermo Scientific, cat # NW04120BOX) at 10 µg protein per well. Samples were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using the iBlot2 system (Thermo Scientific, cat # IB23001). Following transfer, membranes were blocked for 1 h with 5% nonfat milk or 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma cat # A3059) in Tris‐buffered saline with 0.1% Tween‐20 (TBST). Blots were incubated in one of the following antibodies in 5% milk in TBST [guinea pig anti‐GLT‐1 [1:4000] (glutamate transporter 1), EMD Millipore Corp cat # AB1783; rabbit anti‐GLAST [1:1000] (glutamate aspartate transporter 1), Novus cat # NB100‐1869; or mouse anti‐actin [1:1000], Santa Cruz Biotechnology cat# sc‐47778] at 4°C on a rotator overnight. Following primary antibody incubation, blots were washed with TBST and then incubated for 2 h shaking at room temperature in appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated secondary antibody [HRP‐anti‐rabbit [1:2000], Promega cat # W401B; HRP–anti‐guinea pig [1:5000], Sigma cat # A7289; HRP‐anti‐mouse [1:2000], Promega cat # W402B]. Protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescence (Western Lighting Plus ECL, Perkin Elmer, cat # 103E001EA). Membranes were stripped (Restore Stripping Buffer, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat # 21059) before probing for subsequent proteins. Mice representing each sex, genotype, and treatment group were present on each blot. Protein bands were quantified using ImageJ (imagej.nih.gov). Each protein band was normalized to its own actin internal loading control and then to the average WT vehicle‐treated mouse for that blot. Each reported sample value represents the average value from two to three technical replicates (blots) for each biological replicate (mouse).

Surgical implantation of telemetry devices

EEG activity in mice was recorded using a wireless EEG telemetry system (PhysioTel HD‐X02; Data Sciences International, DSI, St. Paul, MN) as previously described. 48 , 49 These devices allow for the collection of data from two bipotential channels that each require two wires (one positive and one negative wire) and an internalized reference electrode connected to the negative lead of the EEG channel. In brief, under 2%–5% isoflurane anesthesia and sterile technique, recording and reference EEG wires were inserted into two 1‐mm burr holes (from bregma +1.5 mm anterior‐posterior and −1.5 mm medial‐lateral for reference; −1.5 mm anterior‐posterior and +1.5 mm medial‐lateral for recording) using a hand drill, and wires were placed on the surface of the dura. Electromyograph (EMG) wires were implanted in direct contact with the dorsal muscles of the neck. The wireless telemetry device connected to the four wires (0.3 mm diameter helix of stainless‐steel coils protected with silastic coating 0.63 mm in diameter) was implanted subcutaneously according to the manufacturer's protocol. Following EEG lead placement, the skull was covered with dental cement, and the incision site was sutured. Mice received analgesic (ketoprofen, 10 mg/kg, subcutaneously) prior to and following surgery to ensure at least 48 h coverage. Mice were allowed at least 4 days to recover prior to data acquisition.

EEG data acquisition

Mice were individually housed in standard home cages and placed on PhysioTel receiver plates (model RPC‐1). Data transmission occurred in real‐time through the MX2 data exchange matrix and Dataquest ART software (DSI, St. Paul, MN) and was recorded through Ponemah (5.20‐SP11). Single‐channel EEG and EMG were continuously sampled at a rate of 500 Hz, and activity was sampled at 50 Hz. Video recordings of each mouse were synchronized to its physiologic measurements using Axis cameras (M1145‐L) and MediaRecorder 2.6 (Noldus).

EEG data analysis

Telemetry data were analyzed using NeuroScore (version 3.3.0, DSI, St. Paul, MN). EEG signal was separated into independent frequency powerbands (delta 0.5–4 Hz, theta 4–8 Hz, alpha 8–12 Hz, sigma 12–16 Hz, beta 16–24 Hz, and gamma 24–40 Hz) and these were analyzed for power in independent vigilance states. Vigilance states were determined and characterized as slow‐wave sleep (non–rapid eye movement; NREM), paradoxical sleep (rapid eye movement [REM]), or wake, which were determined by delta power, theta power, muscle tone, and movement. Vigilance states were determined based on the following parameters: NREM: mid‐high delta power, low‐mid theta power, low‐mid muscle tone, low movement; REM: low delta power, high theta power, low muscle tone, low movement; wake: low‐mid delta power, low‐high theta power, high muscle tone, low‐high movement. To determine low versus high delta and theta powers for defining vigilance states, theta was divided by delta to generate a ratio and threshold, which was between 1.2 and 1.5 for each animal. Values above the threshold were considered high theta, and values below the threshold were considered high delta. Spikes were determined as being at least 200 µV in amplitude (no greater than 1000 µV) and at least 20 ms long (no greater than 70 ms). Spike trains had a minimum spike interval of 0.05 s and maximum spike interval of 1 s; minimum train duration 0.5 s, train join interval of 1 s and minimum number of spikes per train was 3. 57 All spikes and spike trains were determined following EEG data processing with a 1 Hz high pass filter and a 49 Hz low pass filter and reported for a 4 h window. For powerbands, relative power percentages are reported, which normalize the output by representing what percentage of the total power in the signal arises from that power band. Activity levels were collected as sums over 10‐s epochs, and a total sum was reported per mouse during 4 h light and dark periods.

Statistics

Data are reported as mean ± SEM unless otherwise noted. Analyses were completed in GraphPad Prism version 9 or higher. We did not anticipate sex differences in response to subcutaneous Mn exposure, but data were first analyzed with sex as an independent variable to confirm the lack of sex differences using a three‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (2 sexes × 2 treatments × 2 genotypes). Where sex was not a significant contributor to differences, then the sexes were combined within groups and analyzed together. For all dependent variables, unless otherwise noted, two‐way univariate ANOVA (2 genotypes × 2 treatments) with or without the additional repeated measure of time was conducted with appropriate post‐hoc follow‐up tests. In ex vivo hippocampal slice imaging experiments, fluorescent decay tau was calculated from the one‐phase decay of a non‐linear regression model from the time of stimulation. The decay tau and SEM of groups were then compared using a two‐way ANOVA. All microarray RNA expression changes were quantified using Qiagen GeneGlobe Analysis Center (https://geneglobe.qiagen.com/us/analyze). Data relating to EEG experiments were first analyzed as a repeated measures (RM) three‐way ANOVA with genotype, treatment, and dose as variables, and then analyzed within genotype (WT and APP/PSEN1) for the dependent variables of genotype and treatment using a repeated measures two‐way univariate ANOVA with a Sidak's post hoc to quantify changes due to dose and treatment within genotype, unless otherwise stated. Repeated measures tests where one or more values were missing were analyzed using a mixed effects analysis. The threshold for statistical significance for all tests was p < 0.05. Statistical outliers were identified by the ROUT method with a Q coefficient of 1% and subsequently removed from analyses. Analyses were performed to determine normality and equal variances of the data. When equal variances or normality were not met, a Geisser–Greenhouse correction or log‐transformation of data was performed, respectively.

RESULTS

Subcutaneous exposure to Mn increases brain Mn levels

We exposed 6‐month‐old mice to an acute high dose of Mn provided as three subcutaneous doses of 50 mg/kg manganese chloride tetrahydrate (MnCl2‐4H2O) or vehicle over the course of 1 week (Figure 1A). This exposure paradigm results in a significant increase in elemental Mn levels in the cortex (Treatment F1,40 = 129.5, p < 0.0001, Figure 1B), hippocampus (Treatment F1,39 = 315.1, p < 0.0001, Figure 1C), and liver (Treatment F1,41 = 55.7, p < 0.0001, Figure 1D) in both WT and APP/PSEN1 mice. Import of Mn occurs through transporters, including divalent metal transporter‐1 (DMT1), which also transports other divalent ions such as iron (Fe). Evidence of Fe accumulation has been observed in AD and has been implicated in the pathophysiology of AD. 58 , 59 Therefore, the amount of elemental Fe was quantified in addition to Mn. There were no differences in Fe levels in the cortex of mice regardless of genotype or treatment (Ps > 0.12, Figure 1E). Mn‐treated mice had a modest decrease in Fe in the hippocampus compared to vehicle‐treated mice (Treatment F1,42 = 4.39, p = 0.042, Figure 1F). There were no significant differences in Fe levels in the liver (Ps > 0.12, Figure 1G).

Mn exposure alters glutamate clearance dynamics in ex vivo hippocampal slices

Following confirmation of Mn accumulation in the hippocampus following this exposure regimen, we then sought to observe glutamate clearance dynamics in response to Mn exposure in ex vivo hippocampal slices from a separate cohort of 6‐month‐old mice (Figure 2A). Glutamate release was evoked by the application of an electrical current through a stimulating electrode held at 0.75 µA and with a 100 Hz burst over 20 ms, which was repeated three times along the Schaffer Collaterals from the hippocampal CA3 to CA1 regions. Glutamate clearance was quantified as the decay rate of fluorescent signal of the iGluSnFr probe (delivered by pENN.AAV9.GFAP.iGluSnFr.WPRE.SV40), which contains a circularly‐permutated GFP that emits in the 488 wavelength channel upon excitation following binding of glutamate. 60 Change in fluorescence (ΔF/F0) from baseline was recorded over a total of 1500 ms in WT (Figure 2B‐i) and APP/PSEN1 (Figure 2B‐ii) animals treated with vehicle or Mn. Peak response (ΔF/F0) following the first stimulation was no different between genotype or treatment groups (Ps > 0.29, Figure 2C), suggesting no differences in presynaptic release of glutamate. Mn‐treated WT mice had a significantly faster fluorescent signal decay rate compared to vehicle‐treated WT mice (Treatment F1,39 = 2.11, p = 0.15, Genotype F1,39 = 0.997, p = 0.32, Interaction F1,39 = 5.29, p = 0.027; WT Mn‐treated versus WT vehicle‐treated p = 0.021, Figure 2D). APP/PSEN1 mice had a marginally slower fluorescent decay rate ( p = 0.052). Following the second and third stimulations, there were significant decreases in peak response (Stimulation F2,116 = 16.0, p < 0.0001) and decay tau (Stimulation F2,117 = 429.5, p < 0.0001), as anticipated (Figure 2E). There were no differences in peak response between groups over the three stimulations, which again suggests no differences in presynaptic glutamate release. However, in addition to a significant decrease in decay tau over the three stimulations, APP/PSEN1 mice showed a significant increase in decay tau time (Genotype F1,117 = 15.34, p = 0.0002) and a significant interaction between stimulation, treatment, and genotype (Interaction F2,117 = 9.49, p = 0.0002) (Figure 2F). These results suggest that while glutamate clearance after repeated stimulation increases in both WT and APP/PSEN1 mice, it is slower in APP/PSEN1 mice compared to WT, and Mn exposure has differing effects in WT or APP/PSEN1 mice.

Expression of glutamatergic proteins and RNA is changed following Mn exposure

Changes in the mRNA and protein expression of glutamatergic genes, particularly astrocytic glutamate clearance genes (Glt‐1 and Glast), could explain a potential compensatory mechanism resulting in the more rapid clearance of glutamate in Mn‐treated WT mice. Previously, we have shown altered glutamate clearance dynamics in primary mouse astrocytes following 24‐h incubation with 100 µM MnCl2‐4H2O or vehicle. 41 Following a similar exposure paradigm, but without subsequent addition of glutamate (Figure 3A ), cells were collected following the 24‐h incubation period. Downregulation following Mn‐exposure was observed in the mRNA expression of Gfap (Treatment F1,17 = 59.2, p < 0.0001, Genotype F1,18 = 0.17, p = 0.68, Interaction F1,17 = 0.004, p = 0.95, Figure 3B), Glast (Treatment F1,17 = 135.2, p < 0.0001, Genotype F1,18 = 3.6, p = 0.07, Interaction F1,17 = 3.1, p = 0.097, Figure 3C), and Glt‐1 (Treatment F1,17 = 68.2, p < 0.0001, Genotype F1,18 = 4.7, p = 0.04, Interaction F1,17 = 1.2, p = 0.29, Figure 3D). In addition to quantifying the expression of genes related to glutamate clearance, we were interested in changes relating to glutamate release by astrocytes. Non‐vesicular release of glutamate has been shown through a glutamate‐permeable volume‐regulated anion channel (VRAC; SWELL1). 61 This swelling can be mediated through aquaporin‐4 (AQP4) trafficking to the membrane, and abnormal localization has been observed both in AD models 62 and following Mn exposure in vitro. 63 Mn exposure also resulted in decreased mRNA expression of Swell1 (Treatment F1,17 = 5.7, p = 0.029, Genotype F1,19 = 2.3, p = 0.14, Interaction F1,17 = 0.06, p = 0.81, Figure 3E), and Aqp4 (Treatment F1,17 = 74.02, p < 0.0001, Genotype F1,18 = 0.42, p = 0.53, Interaction F1,17 = 0.36, p = 0.56, Figure 3F). Only Glt‐1 expression was decreased in APP/PSEN1 mice compared to wild‐type mice. To determine if the decreased expression of these transcripts was due to an upregulation in apoptotic mechanisms expression of Casp1 was quantified. There were no significant changes observed due to Mn exposure, but there was significantly more Casp1 expression in astrocytes from APP/PSEN1 pups (Treatment F1,34 = 1.1, p = 0.3, Genotype F1,34 = 7.9, p = 0.008, Interaction F1,34 = 2.2, p = 0.15, Figure 3G). Although there was more Casp1 expression in APP/PSEN1 pups, this increase does not explain the changes in expression of these glutamate‐modulating astrocytic transcripts.

Expression of genes relating to glutamatergic and GABAergic neurotransmission was also quantified using a commercially available microarray plate on RNA extracted from cortical tissue from 6‐month‐old mice exposed to the Mn exposure paradigm described in Figure 1A. We documented five differentially expressed genes when comparing vehicle‐ and Mn‐treated WT mice (Figure 3H, Table S2). When comparing vehicle‐treated WT and APP/PSEN1 mice, there were six differentially expressed genes, where APP was the only upregulated gene in APP/PSEN1 mice, and all other genes were downregulated compared to WTs (Figure 3I, Table S2). Interestingly, there were no genes that both had a fold change greater than 1.5 and a significant change in expression between vehicle‐ and Mn‐treated APP/PSEN1 mice (Figure 3J, Table S2). In these same mice, the protein expression of glutamate clearance transporters, GLT‐1 and GLAST, and GFAP was quantified in cortical and hippocampal tissue. In cortical tissue, there were marginal increases in Mn‐treated WT mice in both GLT‐1 (Figure S1A) and GLAST (Figure S1B) protein expression that were not observed in APP/PSEN1 mice, although the difference was not statistically significant. There was an increase in cortical GFAP expression in APP/PSEN1 mice and significantly more hippocampal GFAP expression in vehicle‐treated APP/PSEN1 mice (Figure S1C,F). It is possible these modest changes in GLT‐1 and GLAST protein expression, although non‐significant, combined with RNA expression changes in Mn‐treated WT mice, and the lack of differentially expressed genes in Mn‐treated APP/PSEN1 mice compared to vehicle‐treated, are indicative of a compensatory response to Mn exposure by WT mice.

WT mice have increased spiking activity following Mn exposure

Subpopulations of patients with AD experience subclinical epileptiform activity recorded by EEG, and this has been associated with earlier and accelerated cognitive decline compared to AD without this epileptiform activity. 29 , 30 Evidence of this pathology in AD, coupled with altered glutamatergic handling following Mn exposure in the AD mouse model, could exacerbate an excitatory/inhibitory imbalance and result in increased epileptiform activity. WT and APP/PSEN1 mice underwent the intracranial implantation of wireless EEG telemetry devices at 6 months of age, and baseline data were collected prior to any vehicle or Mn exposure injections (“Dose: 0”). Data were also collected following the first injection (“Dose: 1”; ∼16 h post injection), prior to the third injection of 50 mg/kg MnCl2 • 4H2O or vehicle (“Dose: Pre3”; ∼68 h post second injection), and following Mn or vehicle administration “Dose: 3”; 16 h post injection), respectively (Figure 4A). Spiking activity increased significantly in both WT and APP/PSEN1 mice over the recording period, regardless of Mn or vehicle treatment, and APP/PSEN1 mice exhibited significantly more spikes than WT mice (Time F1.46,60.9 = 13.26, p = 0.0001, Treatment F1,42 = 1.7, p = 0.2, Genotype F1,42 = 8.1, p = 0.0068, Figure 4B). Mn‐exposed mice also had more spikes than vehicle‐treated littermates (Time × Treatment F3,63 = 2.73, p = 0.051). To assess whether Mn exposure sensitized the glutamatergic system, mice were given a single intraperitoneal injection of a low dose of KA (10 mg/kg), an excitatory agonist, 24 h following the final injection of Mn or vehicle. The number of spikes changed over a 6 h recording window post KA, but the pattern of change depended on treatment (Time × Treatment F5,155 = 2.44, p = 0.037, Figure 4C, E), with different spiking responses in WT mice depending on whether they were given Mn or vehicle (Time × Treatment F5,70 = 2.7, p = 0.028). In the first hour, following Mn exposure, Mn‐treated mice had significantly fewer spikes than vehicle‐treated mice with no differences between WT or APP/PSEN1 mice (Treatment F1,29 = 8.22, p = 0.007, Genotype F1,29 = 0.91, p = 0.35, Interaction F1,29 = 0.3, p = 0.59, Figure 4D). However, there was no difference in the total number of spikes over a 6 h period (Ps > 0.25, Figure 4E). The initial decrease in spiking following Mn‐exposure could be due to asynchronous firing rather than a decrease in activity. These data show a differential response in spiking following Mn exposure between WT and APP/PSEN1 mice, with WT showing more sensitivity in subclinical spiking activity. Further, these results show that Mn exposure results in WT mice exhibiting spiking activity and a response to KA that is similar to APP/PSEN1 mice.

Mn exposure disrupts sleep in WT mice

Sleep disruption is a common pathology in patients with AD, but there is currently no literature on the effect of Mn exposure on sleep or in combination with AD pathology. Using data acquired from the EEG telemetry devices, different vigilance states of wake, NREM sleep, and REM sleep were assigned based on brain wave activity and movement data through concurrent EMG recordings. Mice were assigned one of the above vigilance states over 10‐s epochs over the course of 4 h during a light period, where mice are predominantly inactive. These 4 h recording windows occurred at baseline, approximately 12 h, following the first Mn injection, prior to the third Mn dose, and approximately 12 h after the third dose (labeled 0, 1, Pre3, and 3, respectively, Figure 5A). There were no differences in the amount of wake time between WT and APP/PSEN1 mice (Ps > 0.16, Figure 5B). However, there were differences in wake time in WT mice depending on treatment (Time × Treatment F3,57 = 4.93, p = 0.004). Vehicle‐treated mice spent less time awake after the second Mn injection (pre‐dose 3 ( p = 0.04) and after dose 3 ( p = 0.02)) compared to baseline, and Mn‐treated mice spent more time awake following the first dose ( p = 0.007) and third dose of Mn ( p = 0.04) when compared to pre‐dose 3. Similar to wake time, there were no differences in the amount of NREM sleep time between WT and APP/PSEN1 mice (Ps > 0.32, Figure 5C). There were, however, differences in NREM sleep time in WT mice depending on treatment (Time × Treatment F3,57 = 5.97, p = 0.0013). Vehicle‐treated WT mice spent more time in NREM sleep pre‐dose 3 ( p = 0.016) and following dose 3 ( p = 0.0038) compared to baseline, whereas Mn‐treated WT mice spent less time in NREM following dose 1 ( P = 0.0035) and dose 3 ( p = 0.015) when compared to pre‐dose 3. There were no significant changes in REM sleep between WT or APP/PSEN1 mice (Ps > 0.49, Figure 5D). In addition to quantifying the amount of time spent in different vigilance states, the number of wake bouts and transitions from sleep into periods of wake were quantified to provide information on sleep fragmentation. There were no significant changes in the number of wake bouts between WT and APP/PSEN1 mice regardless of treatment (Ps > 0.32, Figure 5E). We further quantified whether Mn exposure would alter activity in WT or APP/PSEN1 mice either during light periods, when the mice are more inactive, or in dark periods, when mice are most active. We found no difference in activity during the light period between genotypes or treatment (Ps > 0.083, Figure 5F). Greater activity was recorded in APP/PSEN1 mice during the dark period, but Mn‐treated mice were less active (Treatment F1,39 = 11.04, p = 0.002, Genotype F1,39 = 5.42, p = 0.025, Figure 5G). WT Mn‐treated mice had the greatest changes in activity during a dark period, with lower activity following dose 1 ( p = 0.004) and dose 3 ( p = 0.0035) compared to baseline. These data support the hypothesis that WT mice are more sensitive to Mn exposure, as indicated by their experiencing more shifts in time spent in different vigilance states and activity levels in response to Mn exposure compared to APP/PSEN1 mice.

AD genotype and Mn exposure both result in shifts in brain wave frequency powerbands

Different brain wave frequency bands are associated with different levels of cognitive activity, with slower waves (Delta 0.5–4 Hz, Theta 4–8 Hz) associated with sleep and drowsiness and faster waves (Beta 16–24 Hz, Gamma 25–40 Hz) associated with active engagement in thinking and deep focus. Shifts towards lower frequency brain waves are seen in AD patient populations. 43 Currently, there are no data on Mn exposure and brain wave spectral power, so we sought to determine if the shifts we saw in sleep time were reflected in the different brain waves, particularly delta, theta, and alpha rhythms. Within the experimental mice, we quantified the relative power of each frequency band within 10 s epochs during each vigilance state, as different powerbands have higher power during different vigilance states (i.e., high delta power during NREM sleep). The same 4 h periods analyzed for the determination of vigilance states (Figure 5A) were used to compare the spectral power of different brain wave frequencies. We first performed statistical comparisons of the relative percentage of power of each powerband within each vigilance state between groups and have summarized the main effects of genotype and treatments over the course of exposure (Table S3). APP/PSEN1 mice had a significant increase in relative power of sigma (12–16 Hz) in both wake (Genotype F1,38 = 4.11, p = 0.0498) and REM sleep (Genotype F1,38 = 5.68, p = 0.022), increases in beta (16–24 Hz) in wake (Genotype F1,38 = 4.48, p = 0.041), NREM sleep (Genotype F1,38 = 5.81, p = 0.021), and REM sleep (Genotype F1,38 = 10.74, p = 0.0022), and increases in gamma (25–40 Hz) in NREM sleep (Genotype F1,38 = 7.02, p = 0.012) compared to WT mice (Table S3). We also observed an increase in theta (4–8 Hz) in Mn‐treated mice during wake (Treatment F1,38 = 5.25, p = 0.028, Table S3). No brain wave powerbands changed significantly due to both APP/PSEN1 genotype and Mn exposure. In addition to quantifying differences in the main effects of genotype and treatment in brain wave powerbands, we also quantified changes from baseline in both WT and APP/PSEN1 mice over the course of vehicle or Mn exposure. During wake periods in Mn‐treated WT mice, there was a significant decrease in delta power over the course of Mn exposure (Treatment F1,19 = 14.66, p = 0.0011, Time × Treatment F3,57 = 9.57, p < 0.0001, Figure 6A), no significant changes in theta power (Ps > 0.08, Figure 6B), and significant differences in alpha power (Treatment F1,19 = 12.92, p = 0.0019, Time × Treatment F3,57 = 6.22, p = 0.001, Figure 6C), specifically between pre‐dose 3 and baseline ( p = 0.03), following the first dose ( p = 0.001), and following the third dose ( p = 0.016). No effect of Mn‐treatment was observed in APP/PSEN1 mice. During NREM sleep, delta power decreased in Mn‐treated APP/PSEN1 mice (Treatment F1,19 = 4.92, p = 0.039, Figure 6D) while no significant changes were seen in WT mice. Theta power in NREM sleep was significantly higher in Mn‐treated WT mice (Treatment F1,19 = 9.6, p = 0.0059, Figure 6E) with a specific increase in theta power following dose 1 compared to baseline ( p = 0.008, Time × Treatment F3,57 = 3.31, p = 0.027). There were no significant changes observed in alpha power during NREM sleep (Figure 6F). During REM sleep, there was a significant increase in delta in Mn‐treated WT mice between dose 1 and pre‐dose 3 ( p = 0.041, Time × Treatment F3,57 = 2.97, p = 0.039, Figure 6G) with no significant changes observed in APP/PSEN1 mice. There were no significant changes observed in theta power during REM sleep (Figure 6H). Alpha power in REM sleep was significantly altered in Mn‐treated WT mice (Time × Treatment F3,57 = 3.65, p = 0.018, Figure 6I), with significant differences between pre‐dose 3 and baseline ( p = 0.031), following the first dose ( p = 0.012), and following the third dose ( p = 0.0008). Additionally, alpha power was significantly changed in Mn‐treated APP/PSEN1 mice (Time × Treatment F3,57 = 4.03, p = 0.012), with higher alpha power following dose 1 compared to baseline ( p = 0.04) and pre‐dose 3 ( p = 0.023). Differences in the percentage of relative power from delta, theta, and alpha during wake, NREM, and REM in WT and APP/PSEN1 animals were also analyzed with similar results to those reported as change from baseline in Figure 6 (see also Figure S2). Together, these changes in brain wave frequencies in both WT and APP/PSEN1 mice in response to Mn‐treatment show an interesting pattern where continued exposure results in changes that are potentially reversed to baseline between acute exposures, as evidenced when looking at a time point immediately prior to the final dose (pre‐dose 3). Further, most changes in response to Mn exposure occur in WT mice, which is consistent with the other data presented here that WT mice appear consistently more sensitive to Mn exposure compared to APP/PSEN1 mice.

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we tested the impact of exposure to the neurotoxicant Mn on the glutamatergic system in the context of AD‐associated amyloidopathy. We demonstrated that Mn accumulation in the cortex and hippocampus altered hippocampal glutamate clearance. Mn exposure also altered spiking and sleep architecture as measured using EEG. This study provides a greater temporal resolution on the impacts of Mn exposure on glutamatergic signaling from previous studies of Mn exposure in the early stages of AD pathophysiology. 11 , 17 , 64 Of particular interest was that WT mice were more sensitive to many of the effects of Mn exposure than were APP/PSEN1 mice.

We previously reported Mn‐induced differences in glutamate clearance using cultured cortical astrocytes 41 within the first 10 min (faster clearance), and over 1 h (diminished total clearance). However, those data reflect clearance on the order of minutes, whereas physiological clearance occurs on the order of milliseconds. We addressed this challenge and increased temporal resolution by recording glutamate clearance in ex vivo hippocampal slices in mice transfected with an adeno‐associated virus (AAV) carrying iGluSnFR to probe glutamate binding. This permitted quantification of the fluorescent decay within milliseconds following electrical stimulation within the CA1 region of the hippocampus, allowing observation of clearance in a neural circuit and brain region associated with cognitive decline in AD. Mn exposure resulted in different glutamate clearance dynamics in WT and APP/PSEN1 mice. Mn‐exposed WT mice showed faster glutamate clearance than vehicle‐treated mice, and moderately faster clearance than Mn‐treated APP/PSEN1 mice. Peak response was unchanged between groups, suggesting that the changes in fluorescent decay tau were due to glutamate clearance differences between groups and not presynaptic release. Although the data from primary astrocyte culture more strongly suggest a difference in glutamate cycling by astrocytes, these findings in ex vivo hippocampal slices are suggestive of a failure to attenuate glutamate clearance in APP/PSEN1 mice, or that the AD pathology in these mice acts on similar mechanisms as Mn exposure, since there was no cumulative effect. Glutamate clearance in APP/PSEN1 mice proximal and distal to amyloid plaques has been quantified using iGluSnFr fluorescent decay. 14 In that study, glutamate clearance in cortical regions was only impaired proximal to amyloid plaques and was improved with the treatment of ceftriaxone, a cephalosporin antibiotic that increases GLT‐1 expression. Their report of no differences in clearance distal to plaques supports our findings of no differences between vehicle‐treated WT and APP/PSEN1 mice at an age when very few plaques are present. However, we did observe altered glutamate clearance in Mn‐treated WT mice that was not observed in Mn‐treated APP/PSEN1, suggesting an independent impact of Mn exposure on glutamate clearance in the hippocampus.

We identified only modest changes in cortical GLT‐1 and GLAST protein among groups, and hippocampal expression of proteins was similar, so expression level alone cannot explain the differential hippocampal glutamate clearance. RNA expression experiments identified changes that occurred in WT mice in response to Mn exposure that were not observed in APP/PSEN1 mice, supporting the greater impact of functional differences in glutamate clearance rate in the hippocampus. Similarly, genetic models of Huntington's disease, which also have known changes in glutamate handling, 53 were less sensitive to Mn than WTs, and it is likely that there are functional differences not directly measured here, including sensitivity to the oxidative environment 65 , 66 , 67 or differences in cellular trafficking.

A more precise study of gene transcript expression relating to glutamate clearance, astrocyte reactivity, glutamate release, and apoptosis was performed in primary astrocyte culture. In addition to glutamate clearance, astrocytes can also release glutamate, including through the non‐vesicular release by glutamate‐permeable volume‐regulated anion channel (SWELL1) under pathological conditions. 61 This swelling can be mediated through aquaporin‐4 (AQP4), and in rat primary astrocytes, Mn exposure induced astrocytic swelling through increased trafficking of AQP4 to the cell membrane. 63 Mn exposure significantly decreased expression of Gfap, Glast, Glt‐1, Swell1, and Aqp4. Glt‐1 was the only transcript with decreased expression in APP/PSEN1 astrocytes. Decreased expression of Glast and Glt‐1 in Mn‐treated cells could indicate an attenuated response to Mn‐exposure and supports our previous findings of lower glutamate clearance in cultured astrocytes. 41 We report a strong decrease in Aqp4 mRNA expression in primary mouse astrocytes following Mn exposure, which may be at least partially driven by an altered oxidative environment. Lack of change in Casp1 level suggests observed differences were not due to apoptosis in Mn‐treated cells; however, it is possible that other pro‐apoptotic molecular mechanisms were activated.

Shifts in excitatory/inhibitory balance in AD can result in observable seizures and subclinical epileptiform activity, and are linked to earlier and accelerated cognitive decline. 23 , 29 , 30 Those with pre‐existing epileptiform activity are also potentially more vulnerable to neurotoxic damage caused by Mn exposure. We previously showed that Mn‐treated APP/PSEN1 mice are more sensitive to KA than controls at 3 months. 41 We, therefore, sought to determine if Mn exposure itself could increase subclinical epileptiform activity and seizure severity following KA challenge. For a change in EEG voltage to be reported as a spike, a population of neurons must fire synchronously to result in a large deflection of voltage above threshold or significantly above average background activity. Rather than the decreased spiking activity in the first hour post‐KA in Mn‐treated mice representing lower activity, we hypothesize this difference is due to asynchronous firing of neurons that is re‐synchronized over the 6‐h recording period. Additional studies are necessary to further clarify these changes.

Sleep disruption is well documented in AD patients with alterations in sleep duration, sleep fragmentation, and increased diurnal napping 68 . Causes of sleep disturbances in AD include degeneration of neural pathways regulating sleep, confounding psychiatric symptoms, environmental factors, and medications. 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 Sleep–wake cycle disruption has been reported in APP/PSEN1 mice beginning as early as 4 months, 74 with most studies reporting differences by 6–9 months, 75 , 76 suggesting a direct link to neuropathological damage. In contrast with recent literature, we did not observe significant differences in sleep time between WT and APP/PSEN1 mice. One potential contributor to the lack of consistency across reports could be due to the genetic background of the mice harboring the APP and PSEN1 mutations. The mice used in the present study were on a C57Bl/6J (B6) congenic background, compared to other lines maintained on a mixed B6;C3H genetic background (#034829‐JAX). 74 , 75 , 76 There are important physiological differences between these genetic backgrounds, including epileptiform activity and amyloid aggregation 33 that contribute to neuronal hyperexcitability and signaling, regulating sleep, and account for differences between our mice and these studies conducted in mice of a similar age. We observed changes in wake and NREM sleep time over the course of Mn exposure in WT mice. These changes suggest that Mn exposure results in more wake time and less NREM sleep time, with no significant changes to REM sleep in WT mice. Interestingly, the changes following the first and third doses of Mn are significantly different compared to pre‐dose 3, suggesting that changes in behaviors such as sleep may revert to normal with decreasing Mn bioavailability.

Human studies have shown shifts toward increased power in lower frequency bands such as delta and theta waves in the frontocortical regions of patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) compared with cognitively healthy subjects and in later neurodegenerative stages of the AD continuum associated with greater amyloid burden. 43 Conversely, preclinical AD patients with subthreshold to intermediate amyloid burden had shifts toward higher frequency powerbands, such as increases in frontal alpha power 43 and in beta and gamma bands. 42 These early shifts to higher frequencies in AD patients with sub‐critical amyloid burden are posited to be an early compensatory mechanism. 42 EEG studies in APP/PSEN1 mice report altered power frequency distributions such as shifts toward higher frequencies, similar to observed shifts in preclinical AD with lower amyloid burden. 45 , 46 , 47 In the present study, we observed shifts in relative power toward higher frequencies in APP/PSEN1 mice compared to WT mice (Table S3), which was consistent with our previous findings in 16‐week‐old APP/PSEN1 mice 48 . However, complementary decreases in lower frequency delta bands were not seen in the young mice used in the present study, although baseline comparisons between genotypes were trending toward decreases during NREM (p = 0.07, treatment groups combined at baseline and Welch's t‐test; data not shown). In quantifying differences between Mn or vehicle exposure within WT or APP/PSEN1 mice, we observed changes from baseline in delta, theta, and alpha power resulting from Mn exposure. Changes were predominantly present in Mn‐treated WT mice, whereas Mn‐treated APP/PSEN1 mice had changes in alpha over Mn exposure in REM sleep. WT mice exposed to Mn again demonstrated higher sensitivity to Mn exposure than APP/PSEN1 mice.

Glutamate handling in AD is known to be impaired at many levels, including clearance, and Mn exposure may further disrupt clearance. Those with AD are more susceptible to stressors that can exacerbate an excitatory/inhibitory imbalance. Together, our data lend evidence to Mn exposure contributing to excitatory/inhibitory imbalance in both AD and cognitively healthy, or amyloid‐pathology‐free populations. Further studies to better understand mechanistically how Mn exposure impacts glutamate clearance and its role in AD development and prognosis are needed to protect this growing and vulnerable population from neurotoxic environmental exposures.
