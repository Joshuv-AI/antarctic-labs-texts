Direct-in-NOD genetic ablation of Bcl3 leads to complete type 1 diabetes protection
Authors: Jeremy J Racine, Jennifer R Dwyer, Harold D Chapman, Amy Bell, Raymond F Robledo, David V Serreze
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt was previously reported that genetic ablation of the NF-κB atypical inhibitor Bcl3 through congenic introduction of a 129P2-embryo derived knockout allele (Bcl3tm1Ver) accelerated autoimmune diabetes in the NOD mouse model. Conversely, we found that direct CRISPR-mediated ablation of this gene in the NOD/ShiLtDvs substrain completely inhibited diabetes development. Our CRISPR approach excised exons 3–7 within the NOD Bcl3 gene. These new NOD-Bcl3−/− mice had very low levels of insulitis, indicating protective mechanisms elicited early in the disease process. Dissimilar to reports of Bcl3 ablation in nonautoimmune C57BL/6-background mice, we found that splenic and lymph node B cells were not reduced. However, splenic T2 and MZ cells were increased with a disruption of B-cell follicle formation. Diabetes protection was associated with elevated splenic and lymph node regulatory T cells, and increases in CD4 effector and CD8 central memory T cells in pancreatic lymph nodes. Diabetes protection was overridden by anti-PD-1 administration. Previous studies suggested that Bcl3 may influence diabetes development downstream of Nfkbid, another atypical NF-κB inhibitor. Indeed, co-introduction of this Bcl3 knockout allele also completely blocked diabetes in NOD-Nfkbid−/− mice normally characterized by accelerated disease. Collectively these findings support the possibility that prior findings may have been driven by congenic introduction of linked modifier genes from non-NOD background strains and initiate a critical reevaluation of the role of Bcl3 in type 1 diabetes pathogenesis.Keywords: autoimmunity, B cells, diabetes, rodent, T cells

Introduction

Our group previously reported how Nfkbid, an atypical modulator of NF-κB activity, affects autoimmune type 1 diabetes (T1D) outcomes in NOD-based mouse models.1,2 CRISPR/Cas9-mediated ablation of Nfkbid or expression elevation by introduction of a C57BL/6 (B6)–derived genomic transgene sequence, respectively, results in either accelerated T1D or complete disease resistance in the NOD/ShiLtDvs substrain.1,2 Transgenic overexpression mediated disease protection in part through improved thymic negative selection of pathogenic T cells, and peripheral expansion of regulatory T (Treg) cells with increased suppressive capacity. We found Bcl3 to be the single NF-κB target gene characterized by respectively decreased and increased RNA transcript levels in CD4/CD8 double-positive thymocytes and Tregs from NOD stocks in which Nfkbid expression was ablated or elevated.1 Similar to Nfkbid, Bcl3 functions as a transcriptional modifier by binding to NF-κB subunits via its ankyrin repeats.3–5 We hypothesized that the T1D-protective effects of Nfkbid overexpression may be mediated through modulation of Bcl3.1

The role of Bcl3 itself in autoimmune diseases is unclear. Genetic knockout of Bcl3 in mouse models inhibits autoimmune experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis and gut inflammation6–8 but exacerbates lupus symptoms.9 In clinical settings, contradictory findings have been reported for association of Bcl3 expression levels with Crohn’s disease and/or colitis.7,10 Finally, a previous study reported Bcl3 may exert a protective effect in NOD mice11 based on an observation that a stock carrying a 129/B6-derived congenic segment including an ablated version of Bcl3 (allele designated Bcl3tm1Ver) was characterized by accelerated disease onset.11 These findings mirrored the accelerated T1D in our Nfkbid knockout NOD model,2 co-characterized by reduced Bcl3 expression.1 Whether the above contrasting effects are due to genetic background or disease-specific processes is unknown.

To disrupt Bcl3 without introduction of passenger genome from another mouse strain, we utilized CRISPR/Cas9-directed targeting to excise exons 3 through 7 of this gene directly in our T1D-susceptible NOD/ShiLtDvs substrain. DNA and mRNA sequences were truncated and full-length Bcl3 protein was undetectable as expected. However, to our surprise, we found that NOD-Bcl3−/− mice were completely protected from T1D development. NOD-Bcl3−/− mice had very low levels of insulitis progression, indicating protective mechanisms likely occurring early in the disease process. Contrary to reports in nonautoimmune B6-background animals,12,13 splenic and lymph node B cells were not reduced. However, B-cell development in the spleen was altered, with an increase in T2 and marginal zone (MZ) cells, and disruption of B-cell follicle formation. Pancreatic lymph node (PancLN) B cells had elevated MHC-II, CD86, and CD40 expression. T1D protection was associated with elevated Treg levels in spleens, lymph nodes, and pancreatic islets. In addition, there was an accumulation of CD4 effector and CD8 central memory T cells in the PancLNs of NOD-Bcl3−/− mice. T1D protection was abrogated by anti-PD-1 administration. Finally, introduction of this new Bcl3 knockout allele into the NOD-Nfkbid−/− strain completely blocks their normal accelerated T1D development. This study reevaluates the role of Bcl3 in T1D development and emphasizes the need for caution when congenically introducing mutations derived from other mouse background strains.

Materials and methods

Mice

The NOD/ShiLtDvs (hereafter NOD),14 NOD.Cg-Emv30b Prkdcscid/Dvs (hereafter NOD.scid),15 and NOD.Cg-Tg(TcraBDC2.5, TcrbBDC2.5)1Doi/DoiJ (hereafter NOD.BDC2.5)16 substrains have been previously described. NOD.BDC2.5 breeders were originally obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. Mice lacking (NOD/ShiLtDvs-Nfkbidem3Dvs/Dvs; hereafter NOD-Nfkbid−/−) or overexpressing (NOD/ShiLtDvs-Nfkbidem3Dvs Tg[Nfkbid]60-2Dvs/Dvs; hereafter NOD-TgNfkbid60A) Nfkbid have been previously described.1,2 All mice used in this study were maintained in a specific pathogen–free barrier room (RAF-B5) at The Jackson Laboratory, which is additionally monitored for segmented filamentous bacteria.17 Incidence and flow cytometry studies were performed using female mice only. Cellular proliferation culture assays were performed using male mice. All procedures involving mice have been approved by The Jackson Laboratory’s Animal Care and Use Committee.

Bcl3 mutant mouse development

To generate NOD-Bcl3−/− mice, 500 ng/µL Cas9 protein (Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 V3 protein; IDT, Newark, NJ) and 600 ng/µL of each guide RNA (upstream: ATGTCCAAGAAAGCGAATTT; downstream: CACCGGGACACTTTGGTCGA) hybridized with Alt-R tracrRNA (IDT) were delivered to NOD zygotes via electroporation,18 and ∼100 zygotes were transferred to 5 pseudopregnant females. Off-target analysis19 (Table 1) was performed using Benchling cloud software package (Benchling, San Francisco, CA). Twenty-three founders with mutations ablating the full-length Bcl3 transcript were identified by DNA sequencing of tail tips (wild-type [WT] allele sequencing: Fwd: CAATTCAAACCCTGGTTGCT and Rev: GATGCTCTCTTTTGGCCTCA; mutant allele sequencing: Fwd: GGACCTGTGATCAAGTTAGCAG and Rev: AGTTGGTGGCACTTTGGTCT). Due to a previous report of T1D acceleration in Bcl3−/− NOD,11 4 founders were initially crossed to disease-free1 Nfkbid-overexpressing NOD-TgNfkbid60A mice. Lines 2 and 3 had superior breeding and were thus used for all subsequent studies. Lines 2 and 3 were separately intercrossed to fix the Bcl3 mutant alleles to homozygosity. This was assessed by PCR of tail tip DNA to detect the presence or absence of the WT and mutant Bcl3 alleles. WT PCR primers were: forward primer A: CCCCTGCTTAGCCAGTAGAA (upstream of exon 3) and reverse primer B: GCTCCCAAGCTTGAAAAGG (within exon 3) (Fig. 1A). Mutant allele PCR primers were: forward primer A and reverse primer C: CCAGTTGCAGACTCTCACCA (exon 8) (Fig. 1A). After 3 generations, mice in each line carrying the Bcl3 mutation and also the endogenous Nfkbid allele, but not the 60A transgene, were identified (NOD-Nfkbid+/+ Bcl3−/−-TgNfkbid60A–), and used to establish independent NOD-Bcl3−/− strains (formal strain designations for lines 2 and 3 are NOD/ShiLtDvs-Bcl3em1Dvs/Dvs and NOD/ShiLtDvs-Bcl3em2Dvs/Dvs). Nfkbid and Bcl3 are separated by only ∼10.6 MB on chromosome 7. Thus, to combine the Nfkbid−/− and Bcl3−/− mutations, mice were screened for a recombination between these 2 loci. Six mice in line 2 and 5 in line 3 demonstrated a recombination between the Nfkbid and Bcl3 genes and were intercrossed to fix both mutant deletion alleles to homozygosity in the absence of the 60A transgene (formal strain designations for lines 2 and 3 are NOD/ShiLtDvs-Bcl3em1Dvs Nfkbidem3Dvs/Dvs and NOD/ShiLtDvs-Bcl3em2Dvs Nfkbidem3Dvs/Dvs).

Sequence analyses

Sequencing analysis was performed on tail tip DNA from NOD-Bcl3−/− line 2 and line 3 mice. In brief, the mutant allele was PCR amplified using primers A and C, run on an agarose gel, extracted and quantified. Five nanograms of purified PCR product was sequenced utilizing the above primers by the Sanger method. The PCR amplicons were aligned to Bcl3 genomic sequence using the National Center for Biotechnology Information Blast online tool (Fig. 1A).

mRNA analysis by RT-PCR

Spleens were harvested from NOD and NOD-Bcl3−/− mice into HBSS supplemented with 2% FBS on ice. Single cell suspensions were obtained using surgical forceps to disrupt the tissue through 70-µm nytex nylon mesh bags. Following centrifugation, samples were resuspended in 5 mL of Gey’s buffer20 to lyse red blood cells. Cells were counted on a Beckman Coulter Z2 Cell and Particle Counter and 15 million were frozen in 1 mL TRIzol (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA) at −80 °C. RNA extraction was performed by phenol chloroform phase separation according to the manufacturer’s instructions and 2 µg of purified RNA was reverse transcribed using SuperScript IV VILO reverse transcriptase master mix. One hundred nanograms of cDNA was amplified with the following RT-PCR primer sets: Bcl3: Fwd: GCCCTTTACTACCAGGGACC and Rev: AGGCCATTGGAGCTGAGACG and Actb: Fwd: ACCCGCCACCAGTTCGC and Rev: TAGACGTCTTTACGGATGTCAACG. PCRs were amplified within a linear range, resolved on agarose gels with ethidium bromide and imaged using a Syngene (Cambridge, UK) G: Box Chemi XRQ gel documentation system. Exported *.tiff files of full blot images were imported into Inkscape 1.4 (Inkscape Project) for figure preparation, and relevant sections were clipped for presentation.

Western blotting of Bcl3

Splenocytes were harvested as above into single cell suspensions and frozen in cold lysis buffer (1XRIPA buffer [Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA] containing 1× HALT protease inhibitors [MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA]). B cells from NOD or NOD-Bcl3−/− mice were enriched by negative selection from single cell spleen preparations prepared as above, using anti-CD43-biotin labeled antibody (Tonbo/Cytek Biosciences, Fremont, CA) and streptavidin bead–based magnetic separation (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). B cells from 5 mice were pooled prior to freezing in cold lysis buffer. Lysates were thawed, sonicated, spun to remove debris, and quantified by Bradford Assay. Protein lysates (50 µg from splenocytes or 16 µg from enriched B cells) were loaded onto duplicate gels, separated by Tris-glycine SDS gel electrophoresis, and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. Membranes were blocked in 5% milk in TBS with 0.1% Tween-20, and incubated with Bcl3 (1:200 of 23959-1-AP) (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL) and β-actin (1:5,000) (MilliporeSigma) primary antibodies followed by HRP-labeled secondary antibodies. Bcl3 and β-actin were detected using SuperSignal West Pico Plus (ThermoFisher Scientific) and images were captured using the Syngene G: Box Chemi XRQ imager. Exported *.tiff files of full blot images were directly imported to Inkscape 1.4 for figure preparation, and relevant sections were clipped for presentation.

Flow cytometry

Spleens and pancreatic lymph nodes (PancLN) were harvested as above to obtain single cell suspensions. Pancreases were inflated in place via bile duct infusion of 2.5 mL HBSS containing 5 units/mL collagenase P (Boehringer Mannheim, Mannheim, Germany) and 10 µg/mL DNAase I (MilliporeSigma) prior to removal and digestion at 37 °C for 30 min. Tissue was dissociated via vigorous shaking, then washed 2 times with HBSS and poured onto sterile glass dishes. Islets were visualized using a Leica MZ6 (Leica Camera, Wetzlar, Germany) and handpicked into fresh HBSS. Following a second round of handpicking into RPMI supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated HyClone low IgG FBS (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin (both from MilliporeSigma), 100 μM 2-mercaptoethanol, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 1× MEM nonessential amino acids, and 1× GlutaMAX (all three from ThermoFisher Scientific), islets were cultured overnight at 37 °C. For analyses of B cells, up to 3 × 106 cells were resuspended in 24 µL FACS-buffer (calcium and magnesium-free PBS, 0.1% sodium azide, 2% FBS) plus 1 µL Fc-shield (anti-CD16/32; 2.4G2; Tonbo/Cytex Biosciences) and incubated at 4 °C for 5 min. Twenty-five microliters of a 2× antibody cocktail diluted in Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) was then added, and cells were incubated for another 30 min rocking at 4 °C. Cells were washed with 2 mL FACS buffer and resuspended in 250 µL FACS buffer.

For analysis of T-cell subpopulation phenotyping, 5 × 106 cells were stained for surface antibodies along with Fixable Viability Dye (ThermoFisher Scientific), then washed and fixed using BioLegend (San Diego, CA) Foxp3 staining kit per the manufacturer’s instruction. Fixed samples were stored overnight at 4 °C, permeabilized the next day, and stained for Foxp3. For cytokine analysis, PancLN single cell suspensions were plated in RPMI supplemented as above and stimulated with 25 ng/mL PMA and 1 μg/mL ionomycin in the presence of 1× GolgiPlug (Brefeldin A, BD Biosciences), for 4 h. Samples were harvested, stained for surface antibodies, and fixed using BD Cytofix/Cytoperm. Cells were washed in FACS buffer, stored at 4 °C, then permeabilized with BD Perm/Wash Buffer prior to cytokine staining.

Antibodies (clone, fluorochrome) used in this study were obtained from the following vendors: BD Biosciences: CD19 (1D3, BB700), B220 (RA3-6B2, PE-CF594), CD4 (RM4-5, BB700), CD8a (53-6.7, BV480), CD62L (MEL-14, BV650), CD44 (IM7, AF700), Ly77 (GL7, FITC), I-Ad (AMS-32.1, cross-reactive with I-Ag7, PE), CD11b (M1/70, BV650), CD40 (3/23, BUV661), CD19 (1D3, BV786), IgDb (217-170, PE), CD23 (B3B4, PE-Cy7), IgM (II/41, Allophycocyanin), CD21 (7G6, FITC), IL-17A (TC11-18J10, BV786), IL-5 (TRFK6, PE), IL-4 (11B11, PE); BioLegend: CD45.1 (A20, PE), CD4 (RM4-5, Allophycocyanin), ICOS (C398.4A, BV605), PD-1 (29F.1A12, BV711), CD86 (GL-1, PE-Cy5), IFN-γ (XMG1.2, Allophycocyanin), TNF-α (MP6-XT22, PE-Cy7); Tonbo/Cytex Biosciences: CD11c (N418; AF700); ThermoFisher Scientific: CD90.2 (53-2.1, eFluor 450).

All flow cytometry data were captured using either an Attune NxT (ThermoFisher Scientific) or FACS-Symphony A5 (BD Biosciences) cytometer. Data were viewed and analyzed using FlowJo 10 (BD Biosciences). All data plotted from flow cytometry analyses were combined from a minimum of 3 separate experiments unless otherwise noted. Gating strategies are presented in detail in Fig. S1. In brief, singlets were obtained by diagonal gating on FSC-A vs FSC-H followed by diagonal gating on SSC-A vs SSC-H. Live cell gating was performed using either propidium iodine (for nonfixed cells) or eFluor 780 fixable viability dye (ThermoFisher Scientific) exclusion prior to specific cellular subset gating.

Proliferation studies

B cells from NOD or NOD-Bcl3−/− mice and TCR transgenic T cells from NOD.BDC2.5 mice were processed into single cell suspensions as described above. B cells from individual mice were enriched by negative selection using anti-CD43-biotin–labeled antibody (Tonbo/Cytek Biosciences) and streptavidin bead–based magnetic separation over LD columns (Miltenyi Biotec). BDC2.5 transgenic T cells were enriched by negative selection using biotin-labeled antibodies against B220, CD8, CD11b, CD11c, and Ter119 (TONBO, Cytek Biosciences) with streptavidin bead–based magnetic separation over LD columns (Miltenyi Biotec) and then labeled with cellular proliferation dye (ThermoFisher Scientific). Purified populations (100,000 B cells and 50,000 T cells) were plated into 96-well round-bottom plates in RPMI media (supplemented as above) with various concentrations of BDC2.5 mimotope (AHHPIWARMDA). Three days later, dilution of proliferation dye in labeled BDC.5 T cells was measured by flow cytometry.

T1D determination

Mice in incidence studies were checked once a week for glycosuria using Ames Diastix (Bayer, Leverkusen, Germany). T1D was determined after 2 readings of >0.25% (corresponding to 300 mg/dL in blood) on 2 separate days.

Anti-PD-1 administration

Anti-PD-1 (clone 29F.1A12; Bio X Cell; Lebanon, NH) was diluted in Dulbecco’s PBS and mice received either 200 µg (200 µL) or Dulbecco’s PBS (200 µL) twice weekly via i.p. injection for a planned 6 injections. Only one anti-PD-1 treated mouse received all planned 6 injections of anti-PD-1. The remaining mice developed T1D prior to completion of the injection schedule.

Splenocyte transfer studies

Splenocytes were prepared from NOD control and NOD-Bcl3−/− mice into single cell suspensions as described above and resuspended in sterile Dulbecco’s PBS. Five million splenocytes in 200 µL were i.v. injected into NOD.scid recipients and mice were followed for T1D development.

Histology

Pancreas samples were fixed in Bouin’s solution (Rowley Biochemical, Danvers, MA) for at least 24 h prior to embedding in paraffin blocks. Three levels (100 µm apart) were sectioned and placed on a single slide per mouse. Slides were then stained with H&E and aldehyde fuchsin prior to scoring for insulitis. Mean insulitis scores were obtained by a blinded observer (D.V.S.) using a previously described method.21–23 Each islet is scored as follows: 0, no lesions; 1, peri-insular aggregates; 2, <25% islet destruction; 3, >25% islet destruction; 4, >75% islet destruction. The summed score for each pancreas is then divided by the total number of islets observed across all 3 sections to obtain the mean insulitis score for that mouse. Spleen preparations were fixed overnight in 10% neutral buffered formalin prior to embedding in paraffin blocks. Three levels (100 µm apart) were sectioned and stained with H&E. Slide scans were captured using a Hamamatsu NanoZoomer C13239-01 (Hamamatsu, Shizuoka, Japan) and analyzed using NDP.view2 software package. Images were viewed with the 5× lens, then exported setting the width dimension at 3 in wide at 600 DPI.

Statistics

All statistics were calculated using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software; Boston, MA). Incidence study P values were obtained using Mantel–Cox analysis. P values for comparisons of a single parameter between 2 strains were calculated using 2-tailed Mann–Whitney analysis. P values for comparisons of 2 or more parameters between 2 strains were calculated using 2-way ANOVA with Šidák multiple comparisons test. Additional statistics outside of these scenarios are described in the respective figure legends.

Results

Creation of direct-in-NOD Bcl3−/− mice

Our prior work on Nfkbid1 found Bcl3 to be the single NF-κB target gene with RNA transcript levels correlating with Nfkbid expression (when ablated or overexpressed) in both double-positive thymocytes and peripheral Tregs from NOD mice. There was a prior report stating that Bcl3 ablation in NOD mice exacerbated T1D.11 Thus, we hypothesized the robust T1D protection we observed following Nfkbid overexpression in NOD mice was mediated through Bcl3.1 The prior study assessing the role of Bcl3 in NOD T1D development utilized a congenically introduced deletion allele of 129P2/OlaHsd strain origin (Bcl3tm1Ver)24 that was subsequently crossed into B6.11 Thus, we made a clean CRISPR/Cas9-generated Bcl3 knockout allele directly into the NOD/ShiLtDvs substrain to test our hypothesis.

We designed guides targeting exons 3–7 of the NOD Bcl3 gene (harboring 6 of 7 ankyrin repeat domains) with low probability19 of off-target potential (Fig. 1A and Table 1). Upstream guide ATGTCCAAGAAAGCGAATTT was situated in the intron preceding exon 3. Downstream guide CACCGGGACACTTTGGTCGA was situated in the intron preceding exon 8. Twenty-three potential founder mice were obtained, and 4 were chosen for continued crossing based on initial sequencing (mosaicism is possible at this generation). Breeding considerations led us to focus on line 2 and line 3 and, once the new mutations were fixed to homozygosity, tail samples were resequenced for both lines to determine the specific mutation in now nonmosaic animals. Both lines had the same 3287 bp dropout mutation (Fig. 1A). RT-PCR analysis using primers designed to amplify a 940 bp region showed a truncated cDNA product consistent with the deletion of exons 3–7 (Fig. 1B). Western analysis revealed a loss of full-length Bcl3 protein in NOD-Bcl3−/− mice in whole splenocyte preparations and enriched splenic B cells (Fig. 1C).

NOD-Bcl3−/− mice are completely protected from T1D development

Prior to performing studies with our new mutation paired with the Nfkbid-overexpressing transgene, we ran an incidence study on our 2 NOD-Bcl3−/− lines to test the previous report of T1D exacerbation.11 To our surprise, both line 2 and line 3 were completely protected from T1D development (Fig. 2A). Histological examination of a subset of pancreases from each line at the end of the incidence study (30 wk of age) showed very low levels of insulitis (<2) (Fig. 2B). We then assessed insulitis at an earlier time point for line 2, which went through more in-depth subsequent analyses of immune cell populations. We found significantly reduced insulitis at 10 to 12 wk of age compared to age-matched NOD control mice (Fig. 2C, open circles). Comparison of the level of insulitis in NOD-Bcl3−/− line 2 mice at 10 to 12 wk (Fig. 2B, closed circles) versus 30 wk (Fig. 2C, closed circles) showed that no progression occurred beyond the earlier examined age point (P = 0.4023, Mann–Whitney test).

NOD-Bcl3−/− mice have elevated levels of lymphocyte populations

Since insulitis progression at 10 wk of age already showed signs that NOD and NOD-Bcl3−/− mice were diverging in the disease process, we chose this time point to compare immune cell populations (see gating examples in Fig. S1). Unless otherwise noted, ensuing data are from line 2 mice. NOD-Bcl3−/− mice had an increased yield of splenic CD45+ leukocytes (Fig. 3A). Previous reports of Bcl3 ablation indicated a drop in B-cell levels.12 While the frequency of CD19+ B220+ B cells was reduced in our model (Fig. 3B), their total number was not (Fig. 3C). The frequency of B cells was likely reduced due to the increased yield of T cells, which was elevated by both frequency (among CD45+) and yield (Fig. 3D, E). Overall T-cell yield increase was driven by a large increase in CD4+ T cells, and a smaller one in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3F). The frequency of splenic Tregs was reduced (Fig. 3G), most likely due to the yield increase of Foxp3– CD4+ cells. However, the Treg yield was elevated in NOD-Bcl3−/− mice (Fig. 3H).

In the PancLN, the yield of CD45+ leukocytes was marginally increased (Fig. 3I). The same was observed for CD19+ B220+ B cells (Fig. 3J), again in contrast to what has been reported for lymph nodes in another Bcl3−/− model.13 The yield of PancLN CD4+ but not CD8+ T cells was elevated in NOD-Bcl3−/− mice (Fig. 3K). Finally, the yield of Tregs was also elevated in the PancLN (Fig. 3L).

Within the pancreatic islet infiltrate, NOD-Bcl3−/− mice had a much lower frequency of B cells and a higher frequency of T cells compared to NOD controls (Fig. 3M, N). The proportion of T cells within the CD4+ subset was increased in Bcl3-deficient islets, with a concomitant decrease in the CD8+ subset (Fig. 3O). Within the increased CD4 population seen in NOD-Bcl3−/− mice, the frequency of Tregs was also elevated compared to NOD (Fig. 3P). Together these data indicate that in the NOD background, Bcl3 ablation does not lead to the same splenic and lymph node yield reductions of B cells observed in B6 mice, but does change the yield and/or frequencies of several lymphocyte populations prior to and after islet infiltration.

B-cell developmental features are altered in NOD-Bcl3−/− mice

Next, we examined if splenic B-cell subpopulations potentially differed between NOD and NOD-Bcl3−/− mice. Splenic B cells develop from bone marrow precursors passing through several stages of transitional (immature; ex T1/2/3) states prior to developing into mature follicular (Fo) or MZ subsets.25,26 There was a marginal reduction of CD23+ (Fo & T2) and respective increase of CD23− (MZ and T1) B cells (Fig. 4A, B). Among the CD23+ B cells, we observed a massive reduction in the IgDHi IgMLo subset, and a shift into a IgMHi phenotype (Fig. 4A, C). Absent additional markers, these 2 populations typically respectively define Fo and T2 B cells. T3 B cells, which may not be true transitional B cells,27,28 would likely be included in the IgMLo subset, but this was not further resolved through traditional CD93/AA4.1 staining26 due to a polymorphism preventing this in NOD mice.29 Thus, we further tested whether there was an expansion of T2 B cells (indicating a potential block in development) or simply a change in the phenotype of the mature Fo subset. We utilized an alternative gating strategy to identify B-cell subsets. Gated CD19+ cells can be split into T1, T2, Fo, or MZ based on CD23 versus CD21/35 staining (Fig. 4D, left). Gating in this manner, we find mature Fo B cells are reduced, with an expansion of CD23− CD21/35+ MZ and CD23+ CD21/35+ immature T2 B cells (Fig. 4D). This phenotype was also confirmed in line 3 mice (Fig. S2A). Prior studies with B6 Bcl3−/− mice indicated a disruption of B-cell follicles and germinal centers.13,24,30 We did not observe a reduction of steady-state Ly77+ B cells, a traditional marker of germinal center B cells (Fig. 4F, G). An observer blinded to the genotype analyzed 5 NOD and 6 NOD-Bcl3−/− spleens by histology. Three of 5 NOD were described as having “normal” spleen follicles (in size and structure), while the remaining 2 had large follicles with normal structure. In contrast, in 6 of 6 Bcl3−/− samples, the splenic follicles were described as large and disorganized (Fig. 4H). Together, these data confirm that Bcl3 ablation in NOD mice disrupts splenic B-cell development.

APC co-stimulatory changes following Bcl3 ablation

T2 B cells are less prone to apoptosis after antigen encounter than the more immature T1 subset.31,32 NOD B cells are known to bypass negative selection to soluble self-antigens at the T1 stage of development, leading to an accumulation of the T2 subset.33 This accumulation has been hypothesized to seed the Fo and Mz subsets with increased levels of autoreactive B cells.33 This accumulation is exacerbated even further in NOD-Bcl3−/− mice, but with an opposite effect on T1D development. Due to the decreased T1D incidence in NOD-Bcl3−/− mice, we therefore hypothesized that perhaps these splenic B cells would show a decrease in co-stimulatory markers. Surprisingly, the opposite was true. Splenic and PancLN Bcl3−/− B cells had elevated expression of MHC-II (Fig. 5A, B), CD86 (Fig. 5C, D), and CD40 (Fig. 5E, F) compared to those from NOD controls. These features were also observed on line 3 B cells (Fig. S2B–G). Finally, we examined these co-stimulatory markers on islet-infiltrating B cells. Similar to splenic and PancLN B cells, NOD-Bcl3−/− islet B cells had elevated levels of MHC-II, CD86, and CD40 compared to NOD B cells (Fig. 5G–I).

Increase of effector and memory T cells in PancLN following Bcl3 ablation

To determine if changes to T-cell activation in the PancLN may explain T1D protection in Bcl3−/− mice, we tested for a potential paucity of CD44+ CD62L− T-effector (TEff) or CD44+ CD62L+ central memory (TCM) cells in Bcl3−/− mice. Surprisingly, in both Foxp3- and Foxp3+ CD4+ lineages, we observed a drop in the frequency of CD44− CD62L+ naïve T cells (TN), with an increase in the frequency of TEff subset (Fig. 6A, B). For CD8+ T cells, while TEff cells remained the same between both strains, the observed drop in TN cells was coupled with an increase in the frequency of TCM cells (Fig. 6C). A smaller frequency increase of TCM was also observed for Foxp3− CD4+ T cells (Fig. 6A). In a small experiment with NOD-Bcl3−/− line 3 mice, the frequency expansion of TEff Foxp3− and Foxp3+ CD4+ T cells, as well as TCM CD8+ T cells, was repeatable (Fig. S2H–J). For each of CD4+ Foxp3−, CD4+ Foxp3+, and CD8+ T cells, no yield changes were detected in the TN population (Fig. 6D). However, for both CD4+ T-cell subsets, the number of TEff cells were increased in NOD-Bcl3−/− mice (Fig. 6E). The TCM yield was also increased for CD4+ Foxp3− and CD8+ T cells in NOD-Bcl3−/− mice (Fig. 6F). We further examined CD4+ T-cell subpopulations and observed that for the Foxp3− subset, the median fluorescent intensity (MFI) of PD-1 and ICOS was elevated in NOD-Bcl3−/− mice (Fig. 6G, H). For Foxp3+ CD4+ T cells, only the MFI of ICOS was elevated (Fig. 6G, H). No differences were observed in PD-1 expression on CD8+ TCM or TEff cells (Fig. 6I). Again, we confirmed these phenotypes in a small experiment with line 3 mice (Fig. S2K–M). Next, we compared the inflammatory cytokine profile of T cells from the PancLN of NOD and NOD-Bcl3−/− mice. PancLN cells were stimulated ex vivo then analyzed by flow cytometry for cytokine production. NOD-Bcl3−/− CD4+ and CD8+ T cells had a higher frequency of TNF-producing cells, but only CD8+ T cells from NOD-Bcl3−/− mice showed a higher frequency of IFN-γ production (Fig. 6J, K). Finally, while we did not observe any difference in the frequency of GITR+ Tregs, we did detect an increased frequency of CD73+ Tregs in NOD-Bcl3−/− mice (Fig. 6L).

Phenotypic T-cell alterations in NOD-Bcl3−/− islets

Changes to T-cell subset makeup and markers of activation within the PancLN combined with a paradoxical failure of insulitis progression (Fig. 2B, C) prompted us to examine T-cell status within the pancreatic islet infiltrate. Islets from NOD-Bcl3−/− mice had increased frequencies of TEff cells within both the CD4+ Foxp3− (15.1% mean increase) and CD4+ Foxp3+ (19.5% mean increase) subsets compared to NOD controls (Fig. 7A). The percentage of CD8+ TEff cells did not differ between strains (Fig. 7A). The frequency of TCM cells among Tregs was halved in Bcl3−/− mice compared to control NOD, but TCM fractions did not differ among CD4+ Foxp3− and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 7B). A marked elevation in PD-1 expression was noted among TEff within CD4+ Foxp3+ T cells (2.5 mean fold increase) (Fig. 7C). Additionally, in contrast to PancLN CD8+ T cells, islet CD8+ TEff cells had a 2.6 mean fold increased PD-1 expression, while CD8+ TCM cells were unchanged from NOD levels (Fig. 7D). ICOS expression increased within the Treg TEff subset but not within CD4+ Foxp3− TEff (Fig. 7E). ICOS expression was also elevated among CD8+ TEff, but not CD8+ TCM cells (Fig. 7F). Finally, we tested whether the PD-1 pathway contributed to T1D protection in NOD-Bcl3−/− mice. Starting at 10 wk of age, anti-PD-1 (200 µg) or PBS was injected into NOD-Bcl3−/− mice twice a week for a planned 6 total treatments. Eight of 9 anti-PD-1–injected mice became diabetic prior to the completion of the injection schedule, compared to no PBS-treated animals (Fig. 7G).

T1D-protection is mostly maintained in secondary transfers

Transfer of immune cells to a lymphopenic environment can break tolerance mechanisms and cause activation of pathogenic T cells. Therefore, we wondered whether splenocytes transferred from NOD-Bcl3−/− mice would exhibit a protective effect in secondary Bcl3-sufficient NOD.scid recipients. 5 × 106 whole splenocytes were injected i.v. into NOD.scid recipients then monitored for T1D development. As expected, a majority of the recipients receiving NOD splenocytes developed T1D by 20 wk posttransfer (Fig. 8A). While a few recipients of NOD-Bcl3−/− splenocytes developed T1D, a majority remained disease-free (Fig. 8A). We examined one of 2 cohorts of recipients receiving NOD-Bcl3−/− splenocytes and found 3 of 10 with high levels of insulitis (scores of 3 or 4) with the remainder having low levels (7/10 mice <3; Fig. 8B). Together, these data indicate hematopoietic changes in the transferred cells account for part of the protection imbued by Bcl3 ablation.

Bcl3 ablation retains robust T1D protection when paired with Nfkbid−/− mutation

Finally, we asked whether Bcl3 ablation could protect from the accelerated T1D observed in NOD-Nfkbid−/− mice. Compared to NOD controls, NOD-Nfkbid−/−-Bcl3−/− mice were completely protected from T1D (Fig. 9A). Survivors examined at the end of the study also had low levels of insulitis development (Fig. 9B). At 10 wk of age, there was already a substantial difference in insulitis between NOD-Nfkbid−/− and NOD-Nfkbid−/−-Bcl3−/− mice (Fig. 9C). Together, these data indicate that Bcl3 ablation remains highly protective, even when introduced within a genetic background prone to accelerated T1D development.

Discussion

Our results directly contradict those reported by Ruan et al,11 which found exacerbated T1D in NOD mice carrying an ablated Bcl3 gene, B6 (low-dose streptozotocin induced), and B6 × NOD.BDC2.5 (cyclophosphamide induced) models. All 3 models carried the Bcl3−/− mutation Bcl3tm1Ver, which was originally generated in 129P2/OlaHsd embryos.24 Congenic introduction of mutations introduces linked genetic material from the donor strain, which has the possibility of eliciting phenotypes not caused by the targeted gene in question. These genetic regions can be substantial, as has been observed for CD45-congenic mice.34 To further complicate the genetics in question, this 129-derived allele was introduced into NOD mice after blastocyst admixture with B6 genomic material.11 While the construct in question was backcrossed for 9 generations (and typed for NOD MHC alleles and the knockout Bcl3 allele), there is potential for both 129- and B6-derived passenger sequence either directly causing or interacting with the Bcl3tm1Ver allele to cause the previously reported T1D exacerbation.11 Compounding matters, the Mouse Genome Informatics database entry for this allele (1861796; last accessed 12/16/2024) and a Jackson Laboratory public repository mouse listing with this allele (FVB; 129P2-Bcl3tm1Ver/J RRID: IMSR_JAX: 003127; last accessed 3/19/2025) indicate that “The Jackson Laboratory Repository has been provided with sufficient evidence that the mutation is not targeted to the Bcl3 gene in the manner described in the primary publication.”24 While this allele has been independently shown to induce a loss of Bcl3 protein,35 possible off-target effects cannot be evaluated when interpreting findings obtained with this allele if the targeting event is not as originally reported.24

Despite the above, the Bcl3tm1Ver mutation did lead to disruption of spleen follicles and loss of germinal centers in B6 mice,24 a phenotype also observed with the Bcl3tm1Sbn and Bcl3tm1Rie alleles.13,30 Our mutation also disrupted splenic follicle formation, reproducing this phenotype. Lack of proper follicle formation may contribute greatly to the T1D protective phenotype we are observing as B cells are a critical APC in this disease.36,37 Though we observe increases in MHC-II, CD86, and CD40 on Bcl3−/− B cells, this did not increase their ability to stimulate BDC2.5 T cells in vitro. In vivo, the abnormal organization of NOD-Bcl3−/− B-cell follicles may spatially limit correct antigen presentation to T cells and thus contribute to T1D protection. Therefore, it is surprising that accelerated T1D was observed in the prior NOD-based study.11 Part of B-cell follicle and germinal center disruption has been linked to faulty follicular dendritic cell development and network incorporation required for antigen responsiveness.38 While not tested within that Bcl3−/− model, the more severely affected p52−/− mouse model failed to restore normal B-cell follicle/germinal center formation following WT bone marrow reconstitution (follicular DCs are nonhematopoietic in origin).38 However, in our NOD-study, NOD-Bcl3−/− spleen cells still failed to transfer T1D at the same rate as those from NOD controls to NOD.scid recipients, indicating that other potential disease protective mechanisms are in play beyond potentially disrupted follicular DC development.

For the Bcl3tm1Sbn allele,13 a large reduction in spleen and lymph node B cells was reported.12,13 While we observe a reduction in spleen and islet B-cell frequency, the yields of splenic and lymph node B cells were not numerically reduced in this study. Islet cells were not enumerated following handpicking and overnight culture, so cannot be evaluated. However, we do observe a similar drop in splenic Fo B cells to those observed for the Bcl3tm1Sbn allele in B6 mice.12 In contrast, we do not observe a decrease in T1/T2 B cells12 but rather an expansion of T2 immature B cells, and only a small increase in the MZ subset. NOD mice are known to have a defect in splenic B-cell development, specifically enhanced progression from the T1→T2 subsets, due to decreased antigen induced negative selection at the T1 stage.33 Despite the increased frequency of immature B cells, spleen, PancLN, and islet B cells have increased expression levels of MHC-II, CD86, and CD40, but do not have an increased capacity to act as APC ex vivo. T2 B cells themselves are traditionally less susceptible to apoptosis upon antigen encounter.31,32 It is currently unknown whether the B-cell changes documented here indicate that Bcl3−/− T2 B cells could be more susceptible to apoptosis after antigen encounter. This possibility can be studied by crossing this mutation to the various mouse models previously used to dissect soluble and membrane-bound antigen contributions to NOD B-cell selection deficiencies.33 Immature B cells have been shown to be less efficient in presenting antigen to naïve (but not preactivated T cells), but this was linked to decreased CD86:CD28 interactions and not peptide:MHC complex formation.39 However, it is possible Bcl3−/− B cells physically process and load antigen differently than Bcl3-sufficient B cells, a phenotype that has been documented for classical DCs.40 Further dissection of these pathways in Bcl3−/− B cells is warranted.

Examination of insulitis infiltrates in NOD-Bcl3−/− mice at both 30 wk (end of incidence) and at a younger age when incidence curves begin to diverge (10-12 wk) showed similar unchanged low levels. Comparison of PancLN at 10–12 wk showed evidence of increased T-cell activation in the PancLN, as TEff and TCM cells are expanded at this site in NOD-Bcl3−/− mice. The exact mechanism of these yield increases within PancLN (decreased egress, increased immigration, increased proliferation, etc) is currently unknown. Similar increases in various measures of T-cell activation were observed in pancreatic islets of NOD-Bcl3−/− mice, including increased frequencies of CD4+ Foxp3− and CD4+ Foxp3+ TEff cells. Additionally, we detected elevated expression of ICOS on CD4+Foxp3+ TEff and increased PD-1 within Treg and CD8+ TEff subsets within pancreatic islets of Bcl3 NOD-Bcl3−/− mice. We also observed an increased frequency of CD73+ but not GITR+ Tregs in the PancLN. CD73-mediated conversion of extracellular AMP to immunosuppressive adenosine is one mechanism of T- and B-regulatory–mediated suppression.22,41,42 Whether the increased frequency of CD73+ NOD-Bcl3−/− Tregs in the PancLN leads to an increase in adenosine-mediated suppression is currently unknown. Future studies crossing our new Bcl3−/− mutation with a CD73 knockout allele will test the possible role of this pathway in Bcl3−/− T1D protection.

Interestingly, administration of anti-PD-1 overcomes the robust T1D protection imbued to NOD mice by Bcl3 ablation. The speed of diabetes onset following immune checkpoint blockade may be explained by activation and expansion of a small number of islet-infiltrating cells and/or the more abundant TEff and TCM cells from the PancLN migrating into the islets. Future work using antigen-specific tetramer analysis and pairing our mutation with TCR-transgenic mice (BDC2.516 and NOD-AI4 mice43) will elucidate changes in diabetogenic T cells lacking Bcl3. While less efficient than those from NOD control donors, NOD-Bcl3−/− splenocytes did transfer T1D to a small number of NOD.scid recipients. This indicated that PancLN egress in these recipients is not as compromised as in the donor mice. Whether this is due to a role for nonhematopoietic cells in the PancLN is unknown but can be addressed in future studies by moving our new mutation to NOD.scid mice or through reciprocal bone marrow transplant studies.

Interestingly, PancLN T cells from NOD-Bcl3−/− mice had an increased capacity to produce the inflammatory cytokines IFN-γ and TNF following ex vivo stimulation. In Ruan et al,11 an increase in IL-17 was reported following ex vivo stimulation with antigen, and CD4+ T cells had enhanced capacity to differentiate into IL-17–producing cells under polarizing conditions. In contrast, we did not detect a difference in IL-17A+ CD4+ T cells following PMA and ionomycin-induced activation. It is unclear whether TNF production was increased in mice from Ruan et al,11 but IFN-γ was not.11 In a B6/lpr model of lupus, genetic ablation of Bcl3 (Bcl3tm1Sbn) worsened disease symptoms in a manner associated with increased TNF production.9 We previously identified scenarios in which IFN-γ can paradoxically act to suppress diabetogenic CD8+ T-cell responses,44 but it is unclear if that mechanism contributes to disease protection in our NOD-Bcl3−/− model. It is also unclear whether TNF blockade in NOD mice carrying the Bcl3tm1Ver mutation would attenuate the exacerbated disease reported in that model11 as observed for the Bcl3−/− (Bcl3tm1Sbn) B6/lpr model of lupus.

Our present results further suggest the previously reported findings of decreased and increased Bcl3 transcription respectively observed in Nfkbid deficient and overexpressing NOD mice1 may have been compensatory in nature. More specifically, Nfkbid may exert an inhibitory effect on Bcl3 protein function. For example, when there is increased Nfkbid protein, to counterbalance this potential Nfkbid-mediated inhibition, there is an increased demand for elevated Bcl3 expression. Conversely, Bcl3 functionality may be enhanced when Nfkbid is absent, thus decreasing the need for Bcl3 transcription. This hypothesis is based on the finding transgenic overexpression of Nfkbid has the same profound T1D protective effect as ablation of Bcl3 in NOD mice. Further, ablation of Bcl3 within our NOD-Nfkbid−/− strain completely abrogated the aggressive T1D characterizing this strain. Future studies are needed to deconvolute the interplay between these 2 modulators of NF-κB.
