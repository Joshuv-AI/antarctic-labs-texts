A Brief History of Canola Genetic Gains: From Classical Breeding to Genome Editing
Authors: Maryam Azhar, David M Cahill, Ghazanfar Abbas Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCanola exemplifies the transformation of a crop from industrial use to a globally significant edible oilseed through sustained genetic and biotechnological innovation. Historically, rapeseed was characterized by high erucic acid and glucosinolate contents, restricting its use primarily to industrial applications. However, classical breeding efforts in the 1970s successfully developed ‘double‐low’ canola varieties, significantly reducing erucic acid and glucosinolate levels, thus establishing canola as a safe and nutritious food‐grade oil. Subsequent advancements, including the introduction of hybrid cultivars, markedly enhanced seed yields, while mutation breeding and marker‐assisted selection refined key agronomic traits such as lodging tolerance and disease resistance. Later, biotechnology breakthroughs expanded canola's versatility, leading to specialty oils with tailored fatty‐acid profiles, for example, high‐laurate oils for industrial applications and omega‐3 enriched oils for nutritional purposes, as well as herbicide‐tolerant cultivars that simplified weed management. More recently, genome‐editing technologies, notably CRISPR/Cas9, have accelerated trait improvement by precisely modifying oil composition and significantly enhancing pod shatter resistance. This review synthesizes major genetic and breeding milestones that collectively shaped modern canola, highlights ongoing challenges associated with its complex polyploid genome, and discusses how emerging approaches, including multi‐omics integration, precision genome editing, and artificial intelligence, offer promising strategies to further enhance canola productivity and sustainability.Keywords: breeding, canola, genetic gains, oil

Introduction

Canola, derived from Brassica napus ( B. napus , rapeseed), is one of the world's most important oilseed crops. After soybean, B. napus is the second most extensively cultivated oilseed globally, accounting for roughly 13%–16% of edible vegetable oil production worldwide (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations 2023). It is an allotetraploid that was formed by ancient hybridization, interspecific crosses between B. rapa and B. oleracea (Wang et al. 2011; An et al. 2019; Lu, Fadlalla, et al. 2019). This interspecific hybridization resulted in a complex genome with extensive gene duplications and repetitive sequences (Chalhoub et al. 2014). Canola was traditionally high in erucic acid and glucosinolates, bitter compounds in the meal. Early breeding efforts from the 1940s through the 1970s were classical selection programs, crossing and selecting plants with improved agronomic and quality traits. Breeders initially focused on adapting rapeseed to new regions, improving seed yield and oil content, and reducing anti‐nutritional factors. By the 1970s, these programs achieved a landmark breakthrough in oil quality: by creating ‘double‐low’ canola. The first double‐low cultivar, ‘Tower’ (1974), had < 2% erucic acid in its oil and greatly reduced glucosinolates in the meal. This represented a > 95% reduction in erucic acid compared to traditional rapeseed oil, transforming canola into a safe edible oilseed crop. Likewise, glucosinolate content was cut by roughly 70%–90%, vastly improving the meal suitable for livestock feed. These quality gains gave canola its name (from ‘Canadian oil, low acid’) and opened the door to widespread use in foods. This innovation transformed rapeseed from an industrial lubricant plant into a healthy edible oil crop. Yield improvement was also a concurrent goal of canola breeding. Through conventional crossing and selection, seed yields rose steadily. In Canada, for example, average farm yields increased about 60% from the 1960s to 2007, largely due to genetic improvements in varieties (Brewin and Malla 2014). By the late 1970s, breeders had also begun incorporating disease resistance and improving lodging resistance and maturity. Breeding objectives have expanded in recent years beyond yield improvement. They now include enhanced oil profiles, improved nutrient use efficiency, and traits to facilitate mechanized harvesting (Li et al. 2023; Hu et al. 2022). This review provides a chronological, trait‐wise overview of genetic gains in canola. For each trait category, we highlight key milestones such as major gene discoveries, trait improvements, and representative cultivar releases. We also describe the progression of breeding technologies—from the introduction of doubled haploid techniques in the 1980s, which greatly accelerated line development, to the adoption of transgenic approaches in the 1990s and the latest CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing in the 2010s—and how these technologies facilitated trait improvement. A final section addresses the challenges facing canola breeders and prospects for future advances.

Oil Quality

Improving oil composition was the foundational achievement that gave rise to canola. Oil quality breeding has continued to be a central theme. It encompasses the removal of undesirable compounds and the creation of novel fatty acid profiles. High levels of erucic acid in rapeseed oil were deemed undesirable for human consumption due to potential health effects (Beare‐Rogers et al. 1971; Aherne et al. 1976). Canadian breeders responded by screening germplasm for low‐erucic mutants. A major breakthrough occurred in 1960 when Baldur Stefansson and colleagues identified rapeseed lines with near‐zero erucic acid (Stefansson et al. 1961). The trait was shown to be heritable and later linked to fatty acid elongase genes, subsequently identified as the FAE1 locus (Harvey and Downey 1964; Barret et al. 1998; Fourmann et al. 1998). The first low‐erucic cultivar, ‘Oro’ was released in 1968, followed by others such as ‘Zephyr’ (1971) (Briggs 2023). Another significant advancement in oilseed quality was reducing glucosinolates (GSL) in rapeseed meal, making it more suitable for livestock feed. In 1968, the Polish cultivar ‘Bronowski’ was found to have low glucosinolate levels (Josefsson and Appelqvist 1968). This trait was introduced into existing low‐erucic lines, leading to the release of the first double‐low cultivar, ‘Tower’, in 1974 by the University of Manitoba, followed by ‘Candle’ in 1977 (Stefansson and Kondra 1975; Bell 1982; Briggs 2023). The adoption of these cultivars marked the emergence of canola as a distinct crop type, defined by oil with < 2% erucic acid and meal with < 30 μmol g−1 glucosinolates. In 1978, canola was officially named, and by the early 1980s, this standard had been widely adopted in Canadian and European production systems (Roche 2015).

The gene underlying GSL production in Brassica napus has only recently been mapped, identifying BnaC2.MYB28 as a key determinant of seed glucosinolate content variation (Liu et al. 2020). Another study employing QTL mapping and RNA‐seq analysis in a doubled‐haploid population found 59 QTLs (including hotspots on chromosomes A09, C02, C07, and C09). It also identified key regulatory genes such as BnaC09.MYB28, BnaA09.APK1, BnaC09.SUR1 and BnaC02.GTR2a, which control seed glucosinolate content in rapeseed (Chao et al. 2022). Canola oil contains roughly 8%–10% α‐linolenic acid (ALA). ALA is a polyunsaturated fatty acid whose three double bonds accelerate oxidation and off‐flavour formation (Hu et al. 2006). Breeding programs in the 1980s at the University of Manitoba prioritized the reduction of seed linolenic acid to enhance both shelf‐life and deep‐frying performance (McVetty et al. 2007). Through EMS mutagenesis and selection, the first ultra‐low ALA cultivar, ‘Stellar’, was developed in 1987. It has reduced C18:3 levels to approximately 2%–3% of total fatty acids (McVetty et al. 2007). Subsequent frying trials confirmed that oil from ‘Stellar’ exhibited significantly improved oxidative and sensory stability compared to conventional canola oils (Eskin et al. 1989; Xu et al. 1999). Genetic mapping later localized the low‐ALA phenotype to fatty acid desaturase‐2 (fad2) and fad3 loci with additive effects, enabling marker‐assisted selection for further oil‐profile refinement (Hu et al. 2006). FAD2 catalyzes Δ12 desaturation of oleic acid (C18:1) to linoleic acid (C18:2), while FAD3 introduces a Δ15 double bond into linoleic acid to yield ALA (C18:3) (Ohlrogge and Browse 1995) as shown in Figure 1. Because these sequential desaturations share substrates, variation at the FAD2 and FAD3 loci shifts the balance between oleic acid and ALA. For instance, mutations in FAD2 that reduce its activity limit the conversion of oleic acid to linoleic acid. As a result, oleic acid levels increase from approximately 60% to over 75%, enhancing oil stability and health benefits (Hu et al. 2006). Similarly, downregulation of FAD3 reduces ALA accumulation, further improving oxidative stability (Stoutjesdijk et al. 2000). These genetic modifications have been instrumental in developing canola cultivars with improved oil quality (Figure 2).

Timeline of key genetic and biotechnological milestones in canola seed oil quality improvement. Discovery of a near‐zero‐erucic mutant in 1960 and identification of the low‐glucosinolate line ‘Bronowski’ in 1967 enabled release of the first double‐low cultivar ‘Tower’ in 1974. Introduction of the ultra‐low‐linolenic cultivar ‘Stellar’ in 1987 further improved oxidative stability. In 1996, expression of a thio‐esterase gene from Umbellularia californica generated laurate‐rich specialty oil, representing the first transgenic modification of canola oil composition. Cloning of the erucic‐acid locus as FATTY ACID ELONGATION 1 (FAE1) in 1998, followed by mapping of FATTY ACID DESATURASE 2 and 3 (FAD2, FAD3), provided markers for high‐oleic, low‐linolenic selection. Publication of the Brassica napus reference genome in 2014 accelerated allele discovery, and a multigene construct introduced in 2016 enabled synthesis of docosahexaenoic acid‐rich oil. Genome editing now underpins improvement; CRISPR elimination of all FAD2 homoeologues in 2020 elevated oleic acid concentrations above 80%, and concurrent editing of FAD2 and FAE1 in 2022 achieved canola oil combining ultra‐high oleic acid with zero erucic acid.

Canola has also been a platform for tailored oils through transgenic technology. A landmark achievement was high‐laurate canola, developed by Calgene (USA) in the 1990s. By inserting a bay laurel ( Umbellularia californica ) thioesterase gene, researchers rerouted fatty acid synthesis to produce > 40% lauric acid (C12:0) in canola seed oil (Voelker et al. 1996; Knutzon et al. 1999). More recently, metabolic engineering has delivered omega‐3 canola. Walsh et al. (2016) introduced a seven‐gene microalgal/yeast pathway to convert oleic acid through successive desaturations and elongations, achieving 9%–11% DHA (Petrie et al. 2020; Belide et al. 2022).

The recent application of CRISPR/Cas9 (Figure 2) has rapidly accelerated the integration of key oil‐quality traits into Brassica napus , bypassing years of backcrossing. For example, Shi et al. (2022) simultaneously knocked out BnFAD2 and BnFAE1 in the high‐oil, high‐erucic CY2 background. This mutation drove erucic acid levels toward zero while markedly boosting oleic acid levels, effectively stacking double‐low and high‐oleic traits in a single generation.

Furthermore, CRISPR/Cas9 overcomes the challenges of genetic redundancy in allotetraploid canola by enabling simultaneous editing of redundant homoeologues. In one notable example, Zhang et al. (2019) used multiplex CRISPR/Cas9 to knockout seven homologs of BnLPAT2 and four homologs of BnLPAT5 in Brassica napus , yielding homozygous mutants. The seed oil content of mutants was reduced by 32%–39% relative to wild type, underscoring the critical roles these genes play in oil accumulation. Additionally, Zhai et al. (2020) simultaneously knocked out both BnA09.TT8 and BnC09.TT8b, completely blocking proanthocyanidin deposition in the seed coat and creating the first stable yellow‐seeded mutants in rapeseed (Zhai et al. 2020). These lines exhibited significant increases in seed oil content and shifts in fatty acid composition without compromising yield.

Such yellow‐seeded germplasm had never been found in natural or induced collections of B. napus , reflecting the challenge of mutating multiple, functionally redundant gene copies by conventional means (Zhai et al. 2020). Furthermore, simultaneous editing of all four BnaFAD2 homoeologs in Brassica napus generated novel alleles. It shifted seed fatty acid profiles, where oleic acid rose from approximately 66.4% in the wild type to over 80%, while linoleic and linolenic acids decreased proportionally (Huang et al. 2020). These results underscore CRISPR's efficiency for multiplexed editing in polyploid genomes and highlight its potential for creating entirely new allelic diversity. Emerging CRISPR modalities such as base editing and prime editing have greatly expanded genome engineering precision in canola. Base editing enables precise, template‐free single‐nucleotide conversions without double‐strand breaks (Achary et al. 2025). Prime editing, on the other hand, facilitates versatile search‐and‐replace edits. Together, both offer unprecedented precision for multiplexed targeting of redundant homoeologs in polyploid canola and hold enormous potential to engineer complex oil‐quality traits (Li et al. 2023).

Yield

Improving seed yield in canola has been a continual breeding objective, addressed indirectly through traits like lodging resistance, plant stature, and harvestability (Salisbury and Wratten 1999). Early yield gains were achieved by conventional breeding and heterosis exploitation (Figure 3). The first hybrid canola cultivars in the 1980s–90s showed ~20%–30% yield boosts over open‐pollinated lines (Radoev et al. 2008; Salisbury et al. 2016). Mutation breeding then supplied novel plant‐height alleles in canola. The semi‐dwarf bzh allele, released in the cultivar ‘Alesi‐bzh’ (1995), reduced lodging without penalizing seed set. Subsequently, an EMS‐derived mutant ds‐1 (mapped in 2010), introduced a missense change in the DELLA gene BnRGA, producing a semi‐dominant, gibberellin‐insensitive dwarf architecture. This allele is now widely used in height management (Foisset et al. 1995; Liu et al. 2010; Schierholt et al. 2018).

Timeline of genetic interventions that underpin yield improvement in canola. The first commercial F1 hybrid Hyola 40, released in 1989, exploited heterosis and lifted yields by about 20% compared with open‐pollinated cultivars. In 1995 the semi‐dwarf mutant line ds‐1, later shown to carry a missense allele of REPRESSOR OF GA1‐3, lowered plant height and lodging risk and thereby stabilised yield (Foisset et al. 1995). From 2018 onwards targeted modification of valve‐margin regulators inaugurated a new phase of pod‐secure breeding; CRISPR deletion of both BnaA.ALC and BnaC.ALC delayed dehiscence, and multiplex editing of SHATTERPROOF1, SHATTERPROOF2 and INDEHISCENT in 2019 further reinforced pod walls. The same year simultaneous knockout of five BnaJAGGED homoeologues enlarged the replum‐valve junction. In 2020 loss‐of‐function alleles in four BnaMAX1 copies produced compact semi‐dwarf plants with increased branch number. Precision editing progressed in 2021 when quadruple knockout of BnaEOD3 enhanced seed weight, RNA‐interference suppression of BnMAN7A07 strengthened valve‐margin cell walls and CRISPR editing of BnaBREVIPEDICELLUS narrowed branch angles to permit denser stands. In 2022 disruption of two BnaIDA genes almost eliminated pod shatter and simultaneously impeded entry of Sclerotinia, illustrating how multiplex editing now couples harvestability with disease avoidance.

Another critical component of yield preservation is pod shattering resistance. Traditional rapeseed varieties were highly shatter‐prone, leading to seed loss before or during harvest (Child et al. 1998; Mani et al. 2012; Raman et al. 2014). Breeders historically improved shattering resistance by selecting less brittle siliques and even introducing alleles from related species (e.g., Brassica juncea ) (Raman et al. 2023). The genetic basis of silique shattering has been elucidated through Arabidopsis studies, identifying key transcription factors that govern pod dehiscence zone development. Canola orthologs of these genes became targets for mutagenesis and editing. For instance, in Arabidopsis, the bHLH transcription factor ALCATRAZ (ALC) specifies the non‐lignified separation layer at the valve margins, a prerequisite for silique dehiscence (Rajani and Sundaresan 2001). In B. napus, knockout of both BnaA.ALC and BnaC.ALC homoeologs abolishes this separation layer and markedly delays pod shattering. As a result, the siliques remain intact much longer than those of the wild type (Braatz et al. 2018).

Subsequent genome editing of additional valve‐margin regulators has further fortified pod walls. Targeted disruption of the MADS‐box genes SHATTERPROOF1 and SHATTERPROOF2 (SHP1/2) and the bHLH gene INDEHISCENT (IND) doubled shatter‐resistance indices by thickening the replum and reducing dehiscence‐zone lignification (Liljegren et al. 2000; Zhai et al. 2019; Zaman et al. 2021). Editing of the JAGGED gene expanded the replum–valve joint area, increasing resistance by approximately 2‐fold (Zaman et al. 2019). Parallel strategies targeted cell‐wall modification enzymes. RNAi silencing of the mannanase BnMAN7A07 reinforced valve‐margin cells and improved shatter tolerance (Li et al. 2021). CRISPR knockout of IDA family genes eliminated dehiscence entirely, yielding virtually non‐shattering pods akin to indehiscent cereals (Geng et al. 2022).

Apart from pod shatter resistance, other translations of gene function knowledge have occurred from work with Arabidopsis. For instance, ENHANCER OF DA1‐3 (EOD3) in canola: BnaEOD3, a homolog of Arabidopsis EOD3 (CYP78A6), was shown to negatively regulate seed growth. CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated knockouts of all BnaEOD3 copies thereby increased average seed weight per plant by 13.9% (Khan et al. 2021). Another key gene complex influencing seed number is the CLAVATA (CLV) signaling pathway. Canola typically has bilocular siliques, but editing CLV3 homeologs produced heritable multilocular siliques with extra carpels, a trait that could boost seed number per pod if pod fill remains robust (Yang et al. 2018). Plant architecture has also been refined. CRISPR knockouts of BnaMAX1 homologs generated semi‐dwarf, bushy lines with 30%–40% more branches (Zheng et al. 2020), while editing BnaBREVIPEDICELLUS reduced branch angles, enabling denser planting and reduced shading (Fan et al. 2021). Thus, over the past four decades, canola breeding has transitioned from traditional methods to advanced genomic techniques. Initial yield improvements were achieved through the development of hybrid cultivars and the selection of semi‐dwarf traits to reduce lodging. Recent innovations focus on precise genome editing to enhance traits such as pod shatter resistance and plant architecture. For instance, CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated edits of genes like ALC, IND, SHP, JAGGED, and IDA have significantly improved pod integrity, reducing seed loss during harvest. Simultaneously, modifications in genes such as CLV3, EOD3, and MAX1 have optimized seed development and plant structure, leading to increased yield potential. These integrated approaches exemplify how modern biotechnology complements traditional breeding, collectively advancing canola productivity and resilience (Figure 3).

Herbicide Tolerance

Herbicide tolerance was one of the earliest transformative traits engineered in canola, as weed control is critical for maximizing yield. In the 1970s, breeders at the University of Guelph introgressed a chloroplast Ser^264 → Gly substitution in psbA, originally found in a triazine‐resistant bird‐rape mustard biotype, into double‐low Brassica napus lines. This effort produced the first triazine‐tolerant (TT) canola (Beversdorf and Kott 1987). The variety OAC Triton was registered in Canada in 1984, giving farmers a tool to control wild mustard and stinkweed without harming the crop (Beversdorf and Hume 1984). The psbA mutation reduces electron transport efficiency, leading to slower photosynthesis and lower biomass accumulation. Agronomic trials also showed Triton yielded only ~80% of susceptible varieties under equivalent management (Beversdorf et al. 1988). Despite the yield drag, TT canola was widely adopted because of its weed‐control advantage. The clear molecular basis of TT canola, single‐site herbicide action, and a well‐characterized point mutation established a template for subsequent target‐site tolerance strategies (Table 1; McNally et al. 1987; Dekker 2023).

To expand herbicide options, in 1989, researchers employed EMS‐induced mutagenesis on isolated microspores of Brassica napus , followed by doubled‐haploid technology, to develop canola lines exhibiting field‐level tolerance to imidazolinone (IMI) herbicides (Swanson et al. 1989). Subsequent molecular analyses identified that this tolerance was due to specific point mutations in the catalytic subunit of the acetohydroxy‐acid synthase (AHAS) enzyme (Hattori et al. 1995). Commercial interest by BASF soon led to introgressing these AHAS alleles into elite germplasm to create Clearfield canola. It was commercialized in 1998 and rapidly adopted because it delivered herbicide tolerance without the yield drag of triazine tolerance (Stanton et al. 2010).

The first commercial release of transgenic herbicide‐tolerant canola in March 1995 became a watershed in crop biotechnology, introducing two complementary weed‐control systems. AgrEvo's Liberty Link cultivars, built around the bacterial bar gene, withstood in‐crop applications of glufosinate‐ammonium. They were also cleared for cultivation and herbicide use in Canada the same year (Canadian Food Inspection Agency 1995a). In parallel, Monsanto's Roundup Ready line, expressing the CP4‐EPSPS enzyme, brought glyphosate tolerance after regulatory approval in 1995 (Table 1; Canadian Food Inspection Agency 1995b).

By 2000, a bromoxynil‐tolerant transgenic canola was also introduced. This was engineered by inserting the oxy gene from the soil bacterium Klebsiella ozaenae into Brassica napus using Agrobacterium tumefaciens ‐mediated transformation. The oxy gene encodes a nitrilase enzyme that detoxifies bromoxynil herbicide, allowing the canola to survive post‐emergence applications (Table 1; Cuthbert et al. 2001; Food Standards Australia New Zealand 2003). Despite its innovative approach, bromoxynil‐tolerant canola saw limited adoption. By 2000–2001, it occupied less than 1% of Canadian canola acreage, primarily due to the narrow weed spectrum controlled by bromoxynil compared to other herbicides like glyphosate and glufosinate (Jhala et al. 2021). Consequently, bromoxynil‐tolerant canola was eventually phased out in favor of more broadly effective herbicide‐tolerant systems (Figure 4).

Timeline of herbicide‐tolerant canola development. Work on chemical tolerance began with the introgression of a chloroplast Ser264 to Gly substitution in psbA, which furnished triazine‐tolerant cultivars released in 1984. Microspore mutagenesis in 1989 produced the first imidazolinone‐tolerant lines, and subsequent mapping identified point mutations in ACETOHYDROXY ACID SYNTHASE (AHAS) as the causal locus. In 1995 two independent transgenic events brought glufosinate and glyphosate tolerance to market, establishing the first genetically modified herbicide‐tolerant canolas. Clearfield canola, carrying dominant AHAS alleles for imidazolinone tolerance, entered commercial production in 1998. A bromoxynil‐detoxifying nitrilase gene yielded BXN canola in 2000, expanding chemical options. Genome editing now refines these traits; CRISPR base editing recreated the key AHAS mutation for tribenuron‐methyl tolerance in 2020, and gene replacement of 5‐ENOLPYRUVYLSHIKIMATE‐3‐PHOSPHATE SYNTHASE (EPSPS) conferred glyphosate tolerance without foreign DNA in 2021. Ongoing precision editing throughout the 2020s enables assembly of multiple target‐site and metabolic‐detoxification alleles in a single step, providing flexible, stackable herbicide regimes for modern cropping systems.

The success of these genetically engineered traits was evident. Farmers could effectively manage weeds with broad‐spectrum herbicides without crop damage, enabling practices like no‐till farming and reducing yield losses to competition. Over the past decades, stacking of herbicide tolerance genes has been accomplished to broaden weed management options (Beckie et al. 2004).

New genome editing techniques are now enabling precise modifications to herbicide target genes in canola. For instance, CRISPR/Cas‐mediated base editing was applied to recreate a well‐known mutation in BnAHAS1 that confers resistance to IMI tolerance (Wu et al. 2020). This generated canola plants tolerant to the ALS‐inhibitor herbicide tribenuron‐methyl (Wu, Chen, et al. 2020). Unlike prior mutagenesis, CRISPR‐based editing can specifically introduce such advantageous point mutations without the random background mutations of EMS or radiation. Similarly, gene replacement with CRISPR has shown that the native BnaEPSPS gene can be replaced with a herbicide‐tolerant variant, resulting in glyphosate‐resistant canola (Wang et al. 2021). Moving forward, the continued evolution of herbicide tolerance in canola will likely involve pyramiding multiple tolerance genes to keep pace with changing weed populations and to mitigate herbicide resistance in weeds. Gene stacking through crosses or molecular stacking via multi‐gene editing could combine, for example, AHAS (imidazolinone), EPSPS (glyphosate), and even detoxification genes for other herbicides, giving farmers flexibility in herbicide choice. Additionally, a ferredoxin NADP oxidoreductase‐like protein was recently shown to confer isoxaben tolerance, revealing a metabolic, non‐target‐site mechanism that could broaden future herbicide strategies (Broad et al. 2024).

Disease Resistance

Blackleg Resistance

Blackleg, caused by the fungus Leptosphaeria maculans, has historically been a devastating disease in canola ( Brassica napus ), leading to stem cankers and significant yield losses. During the 1970s and 1980s, as canola cultivation expanded in Canada and Australia, blackleg epidemics caused dramatic yield reductions, with losses reaching up to 50% in severely affected fields. In Canada, the widely grown cultivar ‘Westar’ was notably susceptible, and its extensive use contributed to widespread blackleg outbreaks in the 1980s (Salisbury et al. 1995; Juska et al. 1997; West et al. 2001). European winter rapeseed programs identified resistant germplasm, notably the French variety ‘Major’ and the older cultivar ‘Sylvia’, as carrying major resistance loci against Leptosphaeria maculans (Delourme et al. 2004). Detailed genetic studies then revealed a classic gene‐for‐gene relationship: pathogen avirulence genes AvrLm1 through AvrLm11 corresponded exactly to Brassica resistance genes Rlm1 through Rlm11, governing race‐specific stem‐canker resistance (Yu et al. 2005).

Canadian efforts in the late 1980s and early 1990s focused on introducing major R genes into spring canola. A turning point was the release of cultivar ‘Quantum’ in 1993, bred by Dr. Gary Stringam in Alberta, Canada's first canola with high blackleg resistance (Stringam, Bansal, et al. 1995; Stringam, Degenhardt, et al. 1995). Quantum carried a single dominant R gene, later identified as Rlm3, and provided excellent control for about a decade (Larkan et al. 2016). By 1995, numerous blackleg‐resistant varieties, most unknowingly sharing Rlm3, were available (Vasquez‐Teuber et al. 2024). In Australia, cultivars like ‘Surpass 400’ carried LepR3 (Rlm2) introgressed from B. rapa subspecies sylvestris, offering strong resistance (Li and Cowling 2003). To enhance durability, breeders pyramided multiple R genes and rotated them. Through the 2000s, additional major R genes were identified, including Rlm1 and Rlm2 from European winter rapeseed ‘Glacier’, LepR1, LepR2, LepR3 from B. rapa sylvestris, and Rlm4, Rlm7 from French lines. Marker‐assisted selection then became instrumental in stacking these genes (Vasquez‐Teuber et al. 2024). LepR3 was cloned in 2013, and encodes a receptor‐like protein recognizing AvrLm1 (Larkan et al. 2013). Subsequently, Rlm2 was cloned, also encoding a receptor‐like protein (Larkan et al. 2015). These discoveries enabled gene‐specific markers and a deeper understanding of resistance mechanisms. Furthermore, transgenic canola plants expressing a pea defensin gene (DRR206) showed improved resistance to the fungal disease blackleg (Leptosphaeria maculans) in greenhouse trials (Table 2, Figure 5) (Wang et al. 1999).

Timeline of key milestones in genetic disease resistance of canola. Severe blackleg epidemics in the 1980s exposed the vulnerability of prevailing cultivars and prompted systematic resistance breeding. In 1995 the first spring varieties carrying major blackleg resistance genes reached farmers and a Bacillus thuringiensis Cry1Ac event demonstrated genetic control of diamondback moth (DBM). The year 2001 marked deployment of the first cultivar with single‐gene clubroot resistance. Transgenic oxalate‐oxidase lines reported in 2008 provided proof of concept for enzymatic suppression of Sclerotinia stem rot. In 2013 the receptor‐like gene LepR3 was cloned, the first R gene isolated in canola, while the emergence of clubroot pathotype 5X that same year compromised initial resistance sources. Genome editing began to reshape disease management in 2022 when CRISPR‐Cas9 disruption of two BnaIDA homoeologues reduced Sclerotinia infection; that season also saw commercial dominance of hybrids pyramiding blackleg and clubroot R genes. Discovery in 2023 of the WeiTsing calcium channel revealed a broad‐spectrum clubroot defence mechanism. In 2024 intragenic CRISPR insertion and rapid introgression of the Rcr1 allele into elite germplasm established a marker‐free path to second‐generation clubroot resistance.

Sclerotinia Stem Rot

Sclerotinia stem rot (white mold), caused by Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, is a fungal disease. It affects canola, especially under humid conditions during flowering. Unlike blackleg, no major gene immunity to sclerotinia is available in B. napus . Resistance is quantitative and influenced by the environment, making breeding progress slower (Li et al. 1999; Wu et al. 2016). Traditional breeding for sclerotinia resistance involved recurrent selection in infested nurseries and identifying partial resistance sources. For instance, some Chinese germplasm showed less lesion growth (Li et al. 2009). QTL mapping in the 2000s identified several loci associated with reduced disease, but each has a small effect (Wei et al. 2016; Wu et al. 2016).

Biotechnology offered a promising solution because S. sclerotiorum relies on oxalic acid as a virulence factor, secreting oxalate to kill plant tissue. Researchers hypothesized that expressing an oxalate‐degrading enzyme could enhance resistance. In the late 1990s and 2000s, transgenic canola lines were created that express a wheat or barley oxalate oxidase (OXO) gene, which conferred resistance to Sclerotinia stem rot (Table 2) (Thompson et al. 1995; Dong et al. 2008; Liu et al. 2015). Despite these positive outcomes, no OXO‐transgenic canola has been commercialized, due in part to regulatory hurdles and shifting breeding priorities. Another transgenic approach attempted was expressing antifungal proteins or host‐derived antibodies (targeting Sclerotinia), which led to enhanced tolerance (Yajima et al. 2010). Furthermore, overexpression of the defense regulators BnMPK4 and BnWRKY33 has been shown to enhance resistance to Sclerotinia sclerotiorum in Brassica napus (Wang et al. 2009). Transgenic canola lines overexpressing BnMPK4 exhibited increased resistance by activating jasmonic acid‐mediated defense pathways and suppressing reactive oxygen species accumulation. Similarly, overexpression of BnWRKY33 led to reduced lesion development and elevated expression of defense‐related genes such as PR1 and PDF1.2 (Wang et al. 2014).

A very recent breakthrough has leveraged CRISPR/Cas9 to target flower‐abscission genes, interrupting the typical infection route via detached petals. Geng et al. (2022) edited both BnaIDA homeologs, producing mutants that retain petals after flowering. When S. sclerotiorum ascospores landed on these attached petals, infection remained localized and did not spread to stems, reducing stem‐rot development (Figure 5). This strategy initially aimed at reducing silique shattering also confers petal retention and represents a novel, single‐gene solution combining agronomic and disease‐management benefits.

Clubroot Resistance

Clubroot, caused by the obligate protist Plasmodiophora brassicae , became a major constraint on canola in the early 2000s (Gossen et al. 2015). Clubroot causes gall formation on roots, stunting and wilting the plants. It was historically a problem in Brassica vegetable crops and was first identified in canola in Europe (Ikegami et al. 1981; Robak 1991; Dixon 2009).

Clubroot has progressed from a sporadic disease of European Brassica vegetables to a major constraint on global canola. The first verified infection of oilseed rape occurred in Poland in the late 1980s and was formally reported by Robak in 1991, signaling the pathogen's shift from vegetable hosts to oilseed crops (Ikegami et al. 1981; Robak 1991; Dixon 2009). Severe outbreaks across northern Europe during the 1990s prompted German breeders to resynthesize Brassica napus from resistant B. rapa and B. oleracea donors. The resulting winter cultivar ‘Mendel’ was released in the 2000s, providing the first commercial example of single‐gene clubroot resistance in oilseed rape (Frauen 1999), which was followed by many other resistant cultivars (Drury et al. 2021). These field successes rested on three decades of fundamental genetics. Classical mapping in Chinese cabbage and turnip had already defined major resistance loci Crr1, Crr2, Crr3, CRa, CRb, CRc, and CRd, in B. rapa and multiple QTL in B. oleracea (Table 2) (Piao et al. 2009). Fine‐mapping these genes enabled marker‐assisted introgression into canola backgrounds (Matsumoto et al. 2012).

The high evolutionary potential of P. brassicae quickly challenged single‐gene protection. By 2013, a highly virulent pathotype designated 5× overcame the resistance of first‐generation hybrids, underscoring the need for broader genetic bases (Strelkov et al. 2018). Breeding programs, therefore, pivoted to gene pyramiding. For instance, stacking Rcr1 or CRaM with Crr1rutb yielded more durable field control than individual genes alone (Tonu et al. 2023). Transcriptome studies confirm additive defense activation in stacked hybrids, validating the strategy at a molecular level (Wen et al. 2024).

Because natural resistance in B. napus is limited, germplasm exploration continues. A chromosome‐scale assembly of the European differential line ECD04 revealed 62 candidate resistance genes and delivered new markers for breeding (Zhou et al. 2024). Functional genomics has also uncovered novel mechanisms. The WeiTsing channel, cloned in 2023, forms an endoplasmic‐reticulum calcium conduit. This channel protects vascular tissues from infection and confers broad‐spectrum resistance when introduced into canola (Wang et al. 2023). More recently, CRISPR/Cas9 was used to introduce the widely effective Rcr1 allele into elite cultivars without selectable markers. This approach produced commercial‐grade germplasm within 2 years, illustrating a rapid path for trait deployment (Table 2, Figure 5) (Hu et al. 2024).

Insect Resistance

Unlike some other crops, canola has seen limited deployment of insect‐resistant traits. Insect pests of canola include flea beetles, aphids, and the diamondback moth (DBM). There was a GM Bt canola developed in the 1990s targeting DBM, which expressed a Bacillus thuringiensis toxin (Cry1Ac). It received regulatory approval in Canada in 1995, contemporaneously with herbicide‐tolerant events (Ramachandran et al. 1998). However, Bt canola was never widely commercialized, partly because DBM can be episodic and manageable with insecticides, and concerns existed about resistance development. Nonetheless, the development showed that canola could be engineered for insect protection similarly to Bt cotton or corn.

Abiotic Stress Tolerance

Canola was originally bred for oil quality and disease resistance under cool temperate, well‐watered conditions. However, as its production expanded into drought‐prone Canadian prairies, the heat‐stressed Australian wheatbelt and salt‐affected soils in parts of China and India, breeders began to prioritize abiotic stress tolerance alongside quality and disease traits. In the early 2000s, interspecific breeding delivered the first canola‐quality Brassica juncea cultivars adapted to low‐rainfall zones. In Canada, at the Saskatoon Research Centre, large‐scale backcrossing introduced low‐erucic, low‐glucosinolate alleles into B. juncea . It produced the contract‐grown varieties ‘Arid’ and ‘Amulet’ in 2002, which combined canola‐quality oil and meal with shatter resistance and improved drought resilience (Oram et al. 1999; Burton et al. 2004). Australian researchers soon assessed B. juncea germplasm for hot, dry environments, and by 2007, the conventional cultivar ‘Dune’ had been released to exploit juncea's superior heat and drought tolerance (Birchip Cropping Group 2008).

Genes involved in drought responses in Brassica napus are increasingly being characterized, highlighting their roles in enhancing tolerance through various physiological mechanisms. For example, BnMAPK1 enhances drought tolerance by increasing root activity, leaf water content, and proline accumulation (Weng et al. 2014). Several members of the LATE EMBRYOGENESIS ABUNDANT (LEA) gene family, previously recognized for drought resistance in the model plant Arabidopsis, have been validated in canola (Lohani et al. 2020). Specifically, the overexpression of BnLEA4‐1 improves drought and salt tolerance in transgenic Arabidopsis (Dalal et al. 2009), whereas BnLEA3, BnVOC (VICINAL OXYGEN CHELATE), and BnSGI (STRESS AND GROWTH INTERCONNECTOR) mitigate reactive oxygen species damage by enhancing photosynthetic efficiency under drought stress (Liang et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2023). Furthermore, PHOSPHOLIPASE D (PLDα1), a key enzyme producing phosphatidic acid, positively modulates drought tolerance when overexpressed (Lu, Wei, et al. 2019). In addition, DELLA proteins, such as REPRESSOR OF GA (RGA) genes (BnA6.RGA and BnC7.RGA), improve drought tolerance through interactions with abscisic acid signaling pathways (Wu, Chen, et al. 2020). The LATERAL SUPPRESSOR (BnLAS), belonging to the GRAS transcription factor family, confers drought resistance by reducing stomatal aperture and enhancing wax deposition (Yang et al. 2011). Likewise, genes involved in wax biosynthesis, notably 3‐KETOACYL‐COA SYNTHASE 1 (BnKCS1) and ECERIFERUM 1 (BnCER1), promote cuticular wax accumulation, thus enhancing water retention (Wang et al. 2020). Furthermore, recent transcriptomic profiling under severe drought stress identified over 1800 differentially expressed genes, including BnA01.CIPK6, a CBL‐interacting protein kinase, which markedly improves drought resilience upon overexpression (Fang et al. 2022).

Salinity tolerance remains a significant breeding challenge in Brassica napus . Although B. napus displays moderate salinity sensitivity, related species such as B. juncea and B. carinata exhibit superior seedling vigor under saline conditions (Purty et al. 2008). Despite the absence of commercially available salt‐tolerant canola, targeted molecular approaches have shown promising results. Notably, overexpression of the Arabidopsis vacuolar sodium antiporter gene, AtNHX1, in B. napus significantly enhances ionic compartmentation and improves salt tolerance in experimental lines (Doranie Uliaie et al. 2012). Furthermore, GWAS identified genes such as CYTOKININ OXIDASE 5 (BnCKX5) and ETHYLENE RESPONSIVE ELEMENT BINDING FACTOR 3 (BnERF3). BnCKX5 regulates cytokinin homeostasis, and its overexpression was found to increase sensitivity to salt stress. Conversely, BnERF3, belonging to the AP2/ERF family known for roles in various abiotic stress responses, improved seedling‐stage salt resistance when overexpressed (Zhang et al. 2022). Additionally, the transcription factor BnABF2, which binds to abscisic acid‐responsive elements, has been demonstrated to significantly enhance salt tolerance when overexpressed in Arabidopsis (Zhao et al. 2016). Several GWAS studies have identified loci associated with cold stress, providing practical markers for cold tolerance breeding (Wrucke et al. 2019, 2020; Chao et al. 2021). Techniques like genomic selection now leverage hundreds of small‐effect loci identified by GWAS, and multi‐omics strategies integrate transcriptomic, metabolomic, and phenomic data to refine candidate gene selection. Breeders are increasingly combining polygenic resilience with targeted trait deployment, positioning future canola cultivars to effectively withstand abiotic stress with minimal yield penalties, crucial for adaptation to climate change.

Nutrient Use Efficiency

Improving nutrient‐use efficiency, particularly nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P), remains a priority for sustainable canola production, though progress at the gene level has been modest. Canola is inherently a high‐input crop, typically requiring substantial N fertilization (Rathke et al. 2006). Historically, canola breeding did not explicitly select for NUE, but indirectly, some progress was made as modern cultivars tend to have better NUE as compared to the older varieties (Stahl et al. 2019). A notable success involves the overexpression of a barley alanine aminotransferase (AlaAT) gene in canola. Researchers at the University of Alberta engineered transgenic canola lines with root‐specific AlaAT expression, leading to increased N uptake and biomass production under low N conditions. Field trials demonstrated that these lines could achieve equivalent yields with approximately 40% less N fertilizer, marking a significant step in NUE (Good et al. 2007).

Phosphorus utilization is equally vital, with P deficiency in canola leading to symptoms like reduced leaf size and purplish‐red petioles. The transcription factor PHOSPHATE STARVATION RESPONSE1 (BnPHR1) is the central regulator of phosphate homeostasis, and its overexpression promotes phosphate uptake under P‐limited conditions (Ren et al. 2012). Additionally, several other genes involved in phosphate transport and signaling have been characterized; however, they are yet to be deployed into the field for genetic gains (Han et al. 2022; Du et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2023). Advances in machine learning and multi‐omics data integration also provide promising strategies for identifying robust genetic markers and candidate genes linked to NUE. For instance, a machine learning framework called ‘POCKET’, which was previously successful in discovering genes associated with seed oil content, could similarly analyze large datasets from canola grown under varying nutrient conditions to pinpoint NUE‐related genetic signatures (Zhang, Liu, et al. 2025). Ultimately, breeders will need to pyramid multiple small‐effect NUE alleles and manage potential trade‐offs with yield‐related traits, such as growth rate and root architecture, highlighting the complexity of breeding for nutrient‐use traits.

Current Challenges and Future Directions

Despite the remarkable progress in canola improvement, several challenges continue to limit the crop's genetic enhancement. One fundamental difficulty is the genetic complexity of B. napus . As an allotetraploid, canola carries two related sub‐genomes with many genes present in multiple copies (Chalhoub et al. 2014). This redundancy makes it hard to obtain clear phenotypic effects from traditional breeding or mutagenesis, as knocking out a deleterious allele may have little effect if other copies of the gene can compensate. Breeders using random mutagenesis or segregating populations must often combine multiple independent mutations or alleles from both sub‐genomes to see a desired trait change, which is a time‐consuming endeavor. Modern genome editing addresses this to an extent by enabling multi‐site gene knockouts (Cong et al. 2013; Lawrenson et al. 2015), but achieving high‐efficiency edits of all homologs is still non‐trivial. Another challenge is the translation of genomic knowledge into breeding gains. Researchers have identified a large number of QTL and molecular markers associated with yield, quality, and stress tolerance in canola over the years, yet relatively few of these discoveries have been fully exploited in cultivar development (Gu et al. 2024).

Breeding programs tend to focus on a core set of major traits (seed yield, oil content, disease resistance) and major genes, while many QTL of small effect or for less emphasized traits remain unused. Part of the issue is that complex traits like drought tolerance, nutrient use efficiency, or seed yield involve many genes and gene interactions, so marker‐assisted selection for these polygenic traits has had limited payoff (Tan et al. 2024). Additionally, the regulatory networks underlying some important characteristics, such as waterlogging tolerance, shatter resistance, or certain quality traits, are not well understood, and the key genes have yet to be pinpointed. This knowledge gap means breeders lack clear targets for selection or editing for those traits. Pleiotropy and tight linkage between genes can also hinder progress by causing undesirable trade‐offs when one trait is improved (Raman et al. 2019; Xiang et al. 2023). Overcoming these hurdles will likely require more advanced use of genomics and biotechnology in tandem with conventional breeding. Moreover, generating improved canola lines via genetic engineering or editing still relies on effective plant transformation techniques, which in B. napus are notoriously genotype‐dependent and have relatively low efficiency (Calabuig‐Serna et al. 2023). This means that introducing a new trait gene or CRISPR construct and recovering fertile, edited plants can be a bottleneck, especially for recalcitrant elite germplasm. Off‐target effects of CRISPR are another concern (Ali and Zhang 2023) that researchers must mitigate by careful guide RNA design and validation, to ensure that only the target trait is altered. Current advances in bioinformatics tools and innovative strategies are helping in this regard by allowing the selection of highly specific guide sequences (Stemmer et al. 2015; Concordet and Haeussler 2018; Lawrenson et al. 2019; Jacinto et al. 2020).

Regulatory and practical considerations also present challenges. The deployment of transgenic canola, while successful in some countries, is still prohibited or restricted in others due to regulatory frameworks and public acceptance issues surrounding GM crops (Winter 2024). Genome‐edited plants created by CRISPR/Cas9 currently occupy a gray area in regulation—some jurisdictions treat certain edits (those without foreign DNA) as non‐GMO, but others subject them to the same strict regulations as transgenics (Shew et al. 2018; Ahmad et al. 2023). Uncertainty in regulatory approval for new biotech‐derived varieties can slow down the introduction of beneficial traits.

Looking ahead, a number of strategies and emerging technologies hold promise to address these challenges. One important direction is the continued integration of multi‐omics data with powerful computational approaches to better decipher complex traits. Large‐scale projects are underway to catalog the canola pan‐genome and to link genetic variants with phenotypic traits (Song et al. 2020; Tang et al. 2021). Coupling these data with machine learning algorithms can help predict which genes or gene combinations are most influential for a trait, prioritizing candidates for breeding or editing. For instance, machine learning frameworks have been used to successfully identify novel genes affecting seed oil content in B. napus (Tang et al. 2021). As more trait datasets and environmental data are fed into such models, they could significantly speed up the discovery of key regulators of yield and stress tolerance.

Another area of rapid progress is the development of new breeding technologies that circumvent current limitations. Nanotechnology provides novel tools for delivering genetic material into plant cells without the need for conventional tissue culture. For instance, precisely engineered polymeric nanocarriers autonomously traverse the plant cell wall to deliver functional proteins across multiple species (Zhang et al. 2024). Furthermore, high‐aspect‐ratio carriers engineered to fit the wall's pore dimensions can enable efficient DNA entry and robust gene expression (Zhang, Shin, et al. 2025). Lysozyme‐coated layered double hydroxide nanosheets loosen the cell wall matrix and stimulate endocytic uptake. They enable broad‐spectrum delivery of RNA and plasmid DNA into plant cells (Yong et al. 2025). Moreover, carbon nanotube carriers have enabled the introduction of DNA or RNA into plant tissues, leading to gene‐edited seedlings without stable transgene integration (Demirer et al. 2019; Kwak et al. 2019; Landry and Mitter 2019). Complementing these advances, direct delivery of CRISPR–Cas9 ribonucleoprotein complexes into canola cells achieves precise genome editing without leaving a transgene footprint (Sidorov et al. 2022; Moss et al. 2024). Though still in early stages, such approaches could accelerate the development of non‐transgenic edited lines and ease regulatory constraints for canola and other crops.

Lastly, future research will also expand the scope of traits targeted in canola improvement. Beyond the traditional priorities of oil yield and quality, traits such as improved protein content in the meal, enhanced micronutrient uptake, greater resiliency to climate stresses, and even ornamental or industrial uses of B. napus may gain attention. The versatility of canola, which serves as a source of oil and feed as well as a cover crop, biodiesel feedstock, and occasionally a leafy vegetable, allows breeding efforts to be tailored to diverse end‐uses (Zhang et al. 2018). Identifying superior alleles and regulatory elements for these diverse traits will be important. As our understanding of the canola genome's regulation deepens, breeders will be better equipped to modify complex traits without compromising others. The trajectory of advancements from classical breeding to CRISPR‐based editing in canola exemplifies how integrating multiple approaches can drive crop improvement, and it sets the stage for further gains in productivity, sustainability, and quality in the years to come.
