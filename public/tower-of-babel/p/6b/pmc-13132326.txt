Small bowel volvulus revealing advanced jejunal gastrointestinal stromal tumor with diffuse metastases: a case report
Authors: Sujal Paudel, Homendra Sah, Sunam Chhatkuli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common mesenchymal tumors of the gastrointestinal tract, with 25–30% arising from the small intestine. While GISTs commonly present with abdominal pain or gastrointestinal bleeding, they may rarely manifest as acute surgical emergencies such as small bowel volvulus (SBV). Introduction

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common mesenchymal neoplasms of the gastrointestinal tract, arising from interstitial cells of Cajal. They account for approximately 0.1–3% of all gastrointestinal malignancies, with an annual incidence of 7–20 per million population[1,2]. The stomach is the most frequent site of occurrence (40–60%), followed by the small bowel (25–30%), with jejunum being the most common small bowel site[3,4]. Most GISTs present with nonspecific symptoms such as abdominal pain, gastrointestinal bleeding, or are discovered incidentally during imaging[3].

Small bowel volvulus (SBV) is defined as the torsion of a segment of the small intestine around its mesentery, leading to mechanical obstruction and possible vascular compromise. It is a rare surgical emergency[5]. Although SBV is most commonly associated with postoperative adhesions, congenital anomalies, and internal hernias, GISTs represent a relatively rare etiology[1,5,6].

This report presents a rare case of jejunal GIST presenting with SBV and concurrent diffuse peritoneal, hepatic, and umbilical metastases, highlighting the diagnostic and management challenges in complex GIST presentations.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist[7].

Case presentation

Patient information

A 60-year-old female presented to the emergency department with a 2-day history of central and upper abdominal pain associated with two episodes of non-bilious vomiting and inability to pass stool or flatus. She reported no fever, weight loss, or urinary symptoms. Her past medical and surgical history was unremarkable.

Clinical findings

On presentation, the patient was alert, conscious, and oriented. She was afebrile, with a blood pressure of 110/80 mmHg, heart rate of 100 beats per minute, and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. The patient did not have pallor, icterus, cyanosis, clubbing, lymphadenopathy, or pedal edema. Clinical signs of moderate dehydration were observed.

Abdominal examination revealed a distended, soft, and non-tender abdomen. No abdominal mass was palpable. The umbilicus appeared centrally inverted with a subtle nodular mass on palpation. Bowel sounds were preserved. Digital rectal examination revealed an empty rectum. Respiratory, cardiovascular, and neurological examinations were unremarkable.

Diagnostic assessment

Laboratory investigations were significant for hemoconcentration (hemoglobin: 16.8 g/dL) and mild hyponatremia (Na+: 130 mmol/L), likely secondary to third-space sequestration and vomiting. The total leukocyte count was 9540/mm3, with neutrophil predominance (80%). Serum potassium (K+: 4.3 mmol/L), renal function test, and coagulation profile were within normal limits.

Plain abdominal X-ray showed multiple air-fluid levels and dilated small bowel loops, consistent with intestinal obstruction. Abdominal ultrasound revealed a right adnexal complex mass and a hypoechoic lesion in the liver. An initial non-contrast CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis showed dilated small bowel loops with a characteristic whirlpool sign, suggestive of SBV (Fig. 1). Subsequent contrast-enhanced imaging demonstrated a nodular deposit at the umbilicus (Fig. 2) and a large, well-defined, heterogeneously enhancing soft tissue mass in the pelvis (Fig. 3). Additionally, a well-defined heterogeneously enhancing mass with central necrotic/cystic areas was noted in segment 7 of the liver (Fig. 4), suggestive of liver metastasis. Intervention

The patient was resuscitated with intravenous fluids, nasogastric decompression, and broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics (piperacillin–tazobactam). Emergency exploratory laparotomy was performed under general anesthesia by a team of consultant surgeons, assisted by a resident.

Intraoperatively, a large mass (13.0 × 7.5 × 6.5 cm) with cystic degenerative changes was found arising from the jejunal mesentery, approximately 110 cm from the duodenojejunal flexure and 240 cm from the ileocecal junction (Fig. 5). The mass acted as the lead point for a 180° anticlockwise rotation of the mesentery, causing complete SBV with marked proximal dilation and impending vascular compromise (Fig. 6). Multiple tumor deposits were present in the peritoneum, mesentery, omentum, gallbladder, and umbilicus, while the liver appeared grossly normal (Figs 5–7). Segmental resection of the involved jejunal loop, including the tumor, was performed with palliative intent, followed by primary end-to-end jejunojejunal anastomosis. Complete tumor removal was not possible because of widespread peritoneal involvement and metastatic spread. Postoperative diagnostic assessment

Histopathological examination of the resected specimen confirmed a GIST of the spindle cell type. Grossly, the largest mass arose from the submucosa, with ulcerated mucosa and intact serosa. Microscopically, spindle-shaped cells were arranged in fascicles, with mild nuclear pleomorphism, necrosis, and a low mitotic rate (1/5 mm2). No lymphovascular or perineural invasion was seen, and three examined lymph nodes were free of tumor.

Immunohistochemistry revealed strong positivity for DOG1 and CD117 (c-KIT), with focal positivity for CD34 and SMA. S-100 and desmin were negative. The Ki-67 proliferation index was 3%. Due to local resource constraints, molecular analysis of KIT or PDGFRA mutations was not feasible.

According to the AJCC 8th edition, the tumor was staged as pT4NxM1 (stage IV) based on tumor size >10 cm, lack of regional lymph node assessment as routine lymphadenectomy is not recommended in GIST[8], and the radiologic and intraoperative evidence of hepatic, peritoneal, and umbilical metastases. Based on the modified NIH criteria, the tumor was categorized as high risk due to a size >10 cm, irrespective of the mitotic index or primary site[9].

Given the high-risk nature of the tumor, the patient was started on palliative systemic therapy with imatinib mesylate. The patient had an uneventful postoperative course and was discharged after 8 days of hospital stay, pain-free and in a stable clinical condition. At 3-month follow-up, she remained asymptomatic, compliant with imatinib therapy, and had no new complications.

Discussion

SBV is a rare but life-threatening acute surgical emergency, accounting for only 1% of all small bowel obstruction cases[10]. Its annual incidence is 24–60 cases per 100 000 in Asia and Africa and 1.7–5.7 cases per 100 000 in North America and Western Europe[5,10]. SBV can be broadly categorized as primary or secondary based on the underlying etiology.

Primary SBV occurs in an otherwise normal abdominal cavity without any evident pathology. It is thought to result from physiological or dietary triggers, such as ingestion of a bulky high-fiber meal following prolonged fasting, which acutely overloads the bowel and induces vigorous peristalsis, precipitating volvulus. Anatomical predispositions, such as an abnormally long or narrow mesenteric attachment and decreased mesenteric fat, allow excessive bowel mobility and further increase susceptibility to volvulus[5,6].

Secondary SBV develops in the presence of predisposing conditions that create a fixed point for the torsion. Common causes include postoperative adhesions (the most frequent etiology), congenital malrotation, anomalous peritoneal bands (e.g., Ladd’s bands), Meckel’s diverticulum, internal hernias, and intestinal tumors, including GISTs[5,6]. Although GISTs are a relatively uncommon cause of SBV[5,6], large extramural GISTs with limited mobility within the abdominal cavity are more likely to act as lead points for intestinal rotation, causing volvulus. The degree of bowel rotation typically ranges from 120° to 720°[1,11]. In our case, a 13.0 × 7.5 × 6.5 cm jejunal GIST resulted in a 180° anticlockwise volvulus.

The presentation of GIST varies significantly with tumor location and size. Small GISTs are often asymptomatic and detected incidentally, while larger tumors commonly present with gastrointestinal bleeding, abdominal distension, pain, or intestinal obstruction[2,12]. Small bowel GISTs show a higher rate of acute symptoms (>50%) and emergency surgery (15%) compared to gastric GISTs, which present acutely in 31% of cases[11]. Our case reflects this aggressive clinical behavior, presenting as an acute intestinal obstruction requiring emergency surgical intervention. Despite extensive disease, the absence of a palpable abdominal mass or obvious umbilical nodule in this case may be explained by marked abdominal distension and the subtle nature of early umbilical metastasis. Preserved bowel sounds further suggest early mechanical obstruction without progression to paralytic ileus.

Only about 10–20% of GIST patients present with overt metastasis at diagnosis[11]. The liver (65%) and peritoneum (21%) are common sites of metastasis in malignant GISTs[13]. However, umbilical metastasis (Sister Mary Joseph’s nodule) is exceedingly rare in GIST, with only isolated case reports[14]. Such metastases occur in only 1–3% of patients with intra-abdominal malignancies and typically indicate advanced disease with poor prognosis[15]. The presence of Sister Mary Joseph’s nodule along with diffuse peritoneal and hepatic metastases at initial presentation makes this case exceptional in the GIST literature.

The histopathological and immunohistochemical findings in this case were consistent with the typical features of GIST. Spindle cell morphology with strong CD117 and DOG1 positivity confirmed the diagnosis of GIST. S-100 and desmin negativity ruled out neural and smooth muscle tumors, respectively. About 95% of GISTs express CD117, while DOG1 serves as a complementary marker, particularly in CD117-negative cases[16,17].

Despite the large tumor size and metastatic presentation, the low mitotic count (1 per 5 mm2) and low Ki-67 index (3%) reflect a low proliferative activity[17,18]. GISTs are known for their insidious growth and often remain asymptomatic for prolonged periods. In this context, the advanced metastatic burden and acute presentation are better explained by prolonged silent tumor growth and its secondary mechanical complications rather than high intrinsic proliferative activity.

The large tumor size and presence of diffuse metastases indicate advanced GIST with poor prognostic implications. Complete surgical resection was not feasible due to widespread disease, and the intervention was therefore performed with palliative intent. Incomplete resection is only indicated for the palliation of symptoms due to bleeding, pain, or mass effects[13]. In this case, surgery aimed to relieve the obstruction, restore bowel continuity, and improve the patient’s quality of life. Postoperatively, the patient was started on systemic therapy with imatinib mesylate to slow disease progression and prolong survival. Imatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, is the standard first-line therapy for unresectable, metastatic, or recurrent GIST and has significantly improved the median overall survival from 19 months in the pre-imatinib era to approximately 73 months, achieving partial response or disease stabilization in approximately 84% of patients[11,19,20].
