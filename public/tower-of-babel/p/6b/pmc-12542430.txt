Diagnostic value of serum DR-70 levels, a new fibrin-related biomarker, in bladder cancer patients
Authors: Musab Umeyir Karakanlı, Eren Görkem Kutlutürk, Emre Can Polat, Alper Ötünçtemur, Erkan Merder, Murat Dursun, Levent Özcan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBladder cancer has high morbidity and mortality rates when it is diagnosed at an advanced stage. The aim of our study is to determine the effect of the DR-70 protein in the diagnosis of bladder cancer and to evaluate its relationship with the grade of the disease.MethodsSerum samples from 60 patients with bladder cancer and 24 control patients with macroscopic hematuria of benign etiology were included in the study. DR-70 levels were compared between the bladder cancer group and the control group. Additionally, a comparison was made between patients with non-muscle invasive bladder cancer and those with muscle invasive disease.ResultsThe median DR-70 level in the bladder cancer group was 1.08 U/mL (0.48–8.5), whereas it was 0.89 U/mL (0.29–1.76) in the control group. The DR-70 level was significantly greater in the bladder cancer group than in the control group (p = 0.012). DR-70 had a sensitivity of 73.3% and a specificity of 66.7% in detecting bladder cancer. On the other hand, the median DR-70 level was 1.06 U/mL (0.48–4.09) in patients with non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (Ta, T1) and 2.66 U/mL (0.57–8.5) in patients with invasive bladder cancer (T2). DR-70 levels were significantly greater in patients with invasive bladder cancer than in those with non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (p = 0.021). Additionally, DR-70 had 72.7% sensitivity and 63.3% specificity in detecting muscle invasive bladder cancer.ConclusionsCompared with that in the control group, the protein level of DR-70 significantly increased in patients with bladder cancer and was greater in the group with muscle invasion.Keywords: Bladder cancer, Fibrin degradation products, Hematuria, DR-70

Background

Bladder cancer is the sixth most common cancer in the male population, whereas it is the ninth most common cancer in both populations [1]. The age-standardized incidence (per 100.00 person/years) worldwide is 9.5 in men and 2.4 in women [1]. At the time of diagnosis, 75% of bladder cancer cases are limited to the mucosa (Ta, T1, Carcinoma in situ = CIS) [2]. Compared with muscle-invasive bladder tumor patients, the non-muscle invasive patients have higher survival rates and lower cancer-specific mortality rates [3]. As in other cancer types, hyperactivation of the coagulation system is present in bladder cancer. This effect is based on the ability of tumor cells to activate the coagulation system [4]. Procoagulan and fibrinolytic mediators produced by tumor cells and released into the circulation play a role in activating the coagulation and fibrinolytic system. It has also been shown that tumor invasion damages the endothelial layer and releases subendothelial procoagulative factors [5]. An angiogenic mediator called vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is produced in bladder cancer cells. When large case series are examined, studies have shown that VEGF levels increase in bladder cancer patients and this increase is related to tumor grade [6–8]. VEGF increases endothelial passage and allows the passage of proteins such as tissue factor and coagulation factor into the extravascular space. As a result, lysine fragments that activate plasminogen are released with the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin. When plasminogen binds to the released lysine fragments, its active state becomes plasmin. Plasmin converts fibrin into fibrin degradation products, and these fibrin degradation products can be detected in blood and urine [9]. DR-70 is a type of enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) test that can measure all monomers of circulating fibrin degradation products and the sum of initial plasmin degradation products (IPDPs) released in the fibrinolysis cascade. The advantage of the DR-70 test is that it can measure all subtypes of fibrin and fibrin degradation products in human serum at one time. It is based on the principle of detecting increased fibrin degradation products as a result of the hypercoagulability of cancer cells. In this way, it has been used as a biomarker in various cancers [10–12]. It is not specific for cancer type, but its elevated levels may be a warning sign for suspicion of malignancy. It can also be used to evaluate the response to treatment and to predict recurrence after treatment [11]– [12]. The aim of this study was to determine the effect of DR-70 protein in the diagnosis of bladder cancer and to evaluate the relationship with the grade of disease.

Methods

Study population

In this prospective study, patients between the ages of 45 and 70 years who presented to the urology outpatient clinic of our hospital with macroscopic hematuria were enrolled between January and July 2024. It was decided that 84 patients would be sufficient with 0,95 power, 0,05 error and 0,8 effect size. The study was conducted only after the relevant informed consent forms had been obtained from the participants. In all patients, hematuria was assessed by cystoscopy, urinary ultrasonography and contrast-enhanced abdominal tomography. Among these patients, those with bladder cancer (BC) were included in the study group, and patients who developed hematuria due to benign causes other than tumors (urinary calculi, benign prostatic hyperplasia, etc.) and for whom surgery was planned for the cause were included in the control group. Patients with BC were treated with transurethral resection. All patients had sterile urine cultures.

Patients with a previous diagnosis of bladder cancer, patients with active infection at the time of admission, patients with a history of hereditary or acquired thrombosis or existing thrombosis, patients who had recently received antiaggregant, antithrombotic and anticoagulant therapy, patients with diseases that may affect the fibrinolytic system, patients who were currently smoking and patients with other malignancies elsewhere were excluded. In studies comparing active smoker and non-smoker populations, it has been shown that active smoking generally activates coagulation and increases plasma fibrinogen, D-dimer, and tissue plasminogen activator antigen levels [13]– [14].

DR-70 examination

In addition to routine preoperative blood analyses, 5 cc of blood was collected to yellow biochemistry tube from all patients included in the study, and the blood was centrifuged at 500 x g for 10 min after being kept at room temperature for approximately half an hour. All the serum samples were frozen and stored in a deep freezer at −80 °C until the DR-70 level was analyzed. The serum concentration of DR-70 (U/mL) was determined with Sunlong brand DR-70 ELISA kits (catalog number SL2676HU; originating from China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The USA origin BIO-TEK brand; EL x 50 model washer was used as the microplate washer. Readings were performed on USA origin BIO-TEK brand ELx800 model microplate reader. All the parameters were read at a wavelength of 450 nm, and the results of the samples were obtained by substituting the absorbance values obtained from the device in the calibration graph. The unit of the values obtained as U/mL. The measurement range of the test was 0.25 U/mL − 8.5 U/mL, with a sensitivity of 0.05 U/mL.

The diagnosis of bladder cancer was confirmed histopathologically by transurethral tumor resection (TUR-B) and the results were recorded. After TUR-B, patients were grouped into non-muscle invasive and muscle invasive groups according to their pathological features. The difference in the DR-70 level between the two groups and the relationship between the T stage and the DR-70 levels were evaluated.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Mann‒Whitney U test was used for statistical analysis. The chi-square test was used to determine negative and positive predictive values. The ideal cutoff value of the serum DR-70 levels was determined by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, according to bladder cancer and control patients, as the value with the highest Youden’s index. A p value < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference between the case and control groups.

Results

Eighty-four patients with macroscopic hematuria were included in the study. While 60 of the patients were diagnosed with bladder cancer, 24 had hematuria due to benign causes. In the bladder cancer group, 52 (86.6%) patients were male and 8 (13.3%) patients were female. In the control group, 18 (75%) of the patients were male and 6 (25%) were female. The mean age of the patients in the bladder cancer group was 59.3 ± 8.74 years, while the mean age of those in the benign hematuria group was 60.5 ± 7.93 years (Table 1). There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of age characteristics (p = 0,58). According to the results of the ELISA analysis of the serum samples, the median DR-70 level was 1.08 U/mL (0.48–8.5) in the bladder cancer group and 0.89 U/mL (0.29–1.76) in the benign macroscopic hematuria group (Table 1). These results revealed that the DR-70 level was significantly greater in the bladder cancer group than in the benign macroscopic hematuria group (p = 0.012).

In addition, the capacity of DR-70 to predict the T stage was investigated. The median DR-70 level for NMIBC (Ta, T1) patients was 1.06 U/mL (0.48–4.09), whereas the median DR-70 level for MIBC (T2) patients was 2.66 U/mL (0.57–8.5). These results revealed that the DR-70 level of invasive bladder cancer patients was significantly greater than the DR-70 level of non-muscle invasive bladder cancer patients (p = 0.021) (Table 4). ROC curve was drawn in order to determine the optimal cutoff value of DR 70 for discriminating between MIBC and NMIBC (Fig. 2). The AUC was found to be 0.724 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.527–0.920), and the curve was determined to be statistically significant (p = 0.021). in the context of MIBC discrimination, the cutoff value was determined to be 1.1 U/mL, while the sensitivity and specificity values were determined to be 72.7% and 63.3%, respectively. The PPV and NPV were 30.8% and 91.2%, respectively (Table 5).

Discussion

The characteristics of an ideal tumor marker for bladder cancer have been previously described by Huben [15]. The marker should be present in urine, serum, or tumor tissue in decreasing order of preference. The marker should accurately predict the presence of a tumor as well as its extent. Marker levels should correlate with disease severity. High sensitivity and specificity are critical criteria for the utility of a test, but several other essential characteristics must also be met for a marker to be clinically applicable and useful for routine practice.

Urinary cytology, which is considered a non-invasive diagnostic method for the diagnosis of bladder cancer, has low sensitivity (16%) in low-grade disease [16]. However, urinary cytology has a sensitivity of up to 46–84% in high-grade disease [16, 17]. The reasons for these different sensitivity rates may be that cytology preparation is user dependent, varies in conditions such as urinary stones and infection, and is affected by intravesical instillation treatments. When the cut-off value is taken as 1 U/mL, DR-70 has 73.3% sensitivity and 66.7% specificity in determining bladder cancer. According to these results, DR-70 could be promising compared with urinary cytology, and better results can be obtained with studies evaluating the combination of these tests. In addition to urinary cytology, a number of biomarkers that can be measured in urine, serum or both have been studied. One of these tests, UroVysion™, offers a sensitivity of 13–100% and a specificity of 63–100% in the presence of symptoms that raise suspicion of bladder cancer [18]. None of these are recommended as routine tests for diagnosis or follow-up. It is preferred to evaluate these non-invasive tests together with cystoscopy and imaging methods.

There are several studies investigating coagulation system parameters in urogenital system malignancies [5, 9]. These studies have shown increased serum coagulation parameters (D-dimer, fibrinogen, partial thromboplastin time) in bladder cancer patients. However, there are no studies comparing DR-70 levels with conventional coagulation parameters. DR-70, a test that can measure fibrinogen and its degradation products, which are the end products of the coagulation and fibrinolytic system activated by cancer cells at an earlier stage, via ELISA, is a validated test with high sensitivity/specificity in cancer patients.

DR-70 is an easy-to-apply, non-invasive blood test, and studies have shown that it has high specificity and sensitivity rates for detecting colorectal, lung, breast and stomach cancers [19]. In this study, we measured serum DR-70 levels for the first time in patients admitted to the hospital with macroscopic hematuria in groups with and without bladder cancer. In our study, which is the first in the literature, DR-70 can be considered as a potential biomarker in the diagnosis of bladder cancer, but since the sensitivity and specificity are not high enough, it may be recommended to use it as an additional laboratory technique to other imaging methods rather than as a diagnostic tool alone.

The first study in the literature on DR-70 was conducted in China in 1998, in which the serum levels of DR-70 were measured in a healthy patient population and a patient population with various types of cancer, and differences were evaluated [19]. With a better understanding of the effects of the coagulation and fibrinolytic systems on cancer pathophysiology and as a result of a study conducted in this area in 1998, studies on DR-70 have gained momentum. A study comparing DR-70 with carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), which is used in the follow-up of patients with colorectal cancer, revealed that DR-70 has greater sensitivity than CEA and that DR-70 has been approved by the American Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for use in the diagnosis and follow-up of patients with colorectal cancer [20]. In addition to its role in the colorectal system, DR-70 has also been studied in lung cancer. Similar to our study, So et al. compared the DR-70 level in lung cancer patients with that in healthy controls and reported that it was significantly greater [21]. They were able to predict lung cancer with 86% sensitivity and 75% specificity at a specifically determined cutoff value.

The only study on DR-70 in the urogenital system has been conducted by Ediz C. et al. [22], who evaluated the ability of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and DR-70 to predict cancer via prostate biopsy. The aim of this study was to determine the contribution of DR-70 in patients with high PSA levels and which threshold value of DR-70 should be taken into account in the decision of biopsy. As a result of this study, no statistically significant difference was found in DR-70 between the groups of patients with and without prostate cancer detected in patients who underwent biopsy due to high PSA. Therefore, the use of DR-70 was not recommended for additional evaluation before prostate biopsy.

According to these results, DR-70 is promising because it has high sensitivity and specificity compared with urinary cytology and biomarkers and can be used to predict tumor grade. DR-70 levels can be studied in serum obtained from routine blood tests to evaluate the possibility of bladder tumors in patients presenting with macroscopic hematuria, and the possibility of bladder cancer can be predicted. In addition, the DR-70 level determined with serum samples taken before surgery in bladder tumor patients can provide information about whether the current tumor is invasive or not. In this study, increased DR-70 levels in bladder cancer patients were shown, and further studies can be planned regarding its use as a biomarker in the follow-up of bladder cancer patients and in addition to interventional procedures for predicting recurrence. It may reduce the frequency of cystoscopy in bladder tumor follow-up. In addition to cancer detection, DR-70 has also been studied to assess the response of cancer cells to adjuvant chemotherapy [11]. This study demonstrated a statistically significant DR-70 response in the treatment-responsive group. Future studies could evaluate the effectiveness of DR-70 in predicting treatment response in bladder cancer patients requiring neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy.

In future studies, the detectability of the DR-70 level in urine can be investigated and it can find a place among non-invasive tests.

The limitations of our study include the relatively small number of patients. The reason for the number of patients is that we excluded patients with possible conditions that might affect the DR-70 level. Additionally, advanced-stage bladder tumor patients who underwent cystectomy were not included, and the patients were not evaluated in terms of recurrence and progression.
