SCML1 overexpression promotes DNA damage and improves the therapeutic efficacy of cisplatin for ovarian cancer
Authors: Ping Zhang, Yongqi Zhang, Min Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOvarian cancer (OVCA) is one of the most common gynecological tumor and is the eighth most common cause of cancer death in women. Sex comb on midleg-like 1 (SCML1) plays an important role in the regulation of embryonic development, but its role in OVCA has not been detected. SCML1 was identified to be associated with cisplatin resistance in OVCA. The expression of SCML1 in OVCA tissues was verified by testing clinical samples. The effect of SCML1 on the proliferation and drug resistance of OVCA cells was investigated in vivo and in vitro. The expression of SCML1 is significantly lower in OVCA cisplatin-resistant tissues. After SCML1 knockdown, the proliferation ability and cisplatin resistance of OVCA cells were significantly enhanced. The overexpression of SCML1 can inhibit the expression of E2F transcription factor 1 (E2F1) and promote the expression of O-linked N-acetylglucosamine transferase (OGT).

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OVCA) is one of the most common gynecological tumor and is the eighth most common cause of cancer death in women.1 Over the past few decades, the 5-year survival rate for most tumors has improved by approximately 20%, while the prognosis for OVCA patients has not improved significantly.2 Studies have shown that approximately 70% of patients with OVCA are diagnosed at an advanced stage due to a lack of early symptoms, with extensive abdominal and pelvic metastases.3,4 The 5-year survival rate is 92% for patients with early stage OVCA but only 29% for those with advanced OVCA.5 Currently, the preferred first-line treatment for OVCA is cytoreductive surgery combined with platinum-based chemotherapy.6 However, with the increasing number of treatments, almost all patients will progress to platinum resistance, which eventually leads to the death of patients, and approximately 20% of patients with OVCA develop congenital platinum resistance.7 Therefore, finding new markers for the early diagnosis of OVCA and exploring the mechanism of platinum resistance in OVCA is highly important for improving the prognosis of patients with OVCA in the future.

Sex comb on midleg-like 1 (SCML1) is a novel gene located in Xp22 in humans. In the human genome, SCML1 spans 18 kb of genomic DNA and consists of six exons.8 In the sex comb on midleg (SCM) family, there are two other genes, SCML2 and SCMH1. SCML2 plays an important role in the pathogenesis of acute myeloid leukemia, indicating that it can regulate cell differentiation and the cell cycle.9 SCMH1 regulates homologous genes during embryogenesis.10 In lung cancer, SCML1 was shown to regulate the transcription of the HN family of peptides.11 The HN polypeptide family has a protective function in normal cells.12,13 These results indicated that SCML1 may play an important role in the occurrence and development of tumors. SCML1 may be a novel tumor biological and prognostic marker and a potential therapeutic target.

In this study, we found that SCML1 expression was lower in cisplatin-resistant OVCA specimens than in cisplatin-sensitive OVCA specimens. High expression of SCML1 has a good effect on patient prognosis. SCML1 affects the expression level of O-linked N-acetylglucosamine transferase (OGT) in OVCA cells through E2F transcription factor 1 (E2F1), leading to changes in the phosphorylated histone H2A.X (γ-H2AX) content and ultimately affecting the resistance of OVCA cells to cisplatin.

Results

Identification of DEGs affecting OVCA sensitivity to cisplatin treatment and prognosis of OVCA patients

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the GSE189717 and GSE15372 datasets were analyzed using the “limma” package in R 3.6.1 software. A total of 2,077 upregulated genes and 1,919 downregulated genes were identified in the GSE189717 dataset. A total of 805 upregulated and 317 downregulated genes were detected in the GSE15372 dataset (Figure 1A). The heatmap showed the top 50 genes with the most pronounced expression differences in the GSE189717 and GSE15372 datasets to show the expression levels of these DEGs in cisplatin-sensitive and cisplatin-resistant OVCA cells (Figures S1A and S1B). Venn diagram demonstrated that 27 DEGs were downregulated and 89 genes were upregulated both in the GSE189717 and GSE15372 datasets, respectively (Figure 1B). The DEGs were employed in the construction of the lasso model. The optimal model was attained at λ = 0.0715959, resulting in the identification of 11 DEGs (Figures 1C and 1D). A correlation heatmap was produced to analyze the relationships among 11 genes, with blue representing a positive correlation and orange indicating a negative correlation (Figure S1C). The prognostic significance of the 11 DEGs in OVCA was assessed using univariate Cox regression analysis in the GSE140082 dataset, indicating that all 11 genes had a significant impact on prognosis (Figure 1E). Further multivariate Cox analysis was performed, in which 3 genes impacted the prognosis of patients with OVCA, namely, SCML1, RNF144B, and SGCE (Figure 1F). Through the implementation of genomic enrichment analysis (GSEA) to investigate the molecular functions (MFs) and pathways correlated with the DEGs, a total of 11 pathways of particular importance were identified that exhibit close associations with the DEGs (Figure S1D). To further investigate the biological capabilities of the DEGs, an analysis of gene ontology (GO) terms about biological processes (BPs), MFs, and cellular components (CCs) was conducted (Figures S1E and S1F). High expression of SCML1 promotes cisplatin-induced DNA damage in OVCA cells

To assess the influence of SCML1 expression on the efficacy of cisplatin therapy in OVCA, a subcutaneous tumor model was established in nude mice. The findings indicated a deceleration in tumor growth and a reduction in tumor weight within the OE-SCML1 group, implying enhanced responsiveness to cisplatin therapy (Figure 3A). The sh-SCML1 group exhibited accelerated tumor growth and increased tumor weight, indicating resistance to cisplatin therapy (Figure 3B). Cisplatin, a platinum compound, is extensively employed as a primary chemotherapeutic agent for OVCA, primarily functioning through the induction of DNA damage within tumor cells to elicit its antitumor effects.14,15 Alkaline comet assay indicated that low expression of SCML1 facilitated the process of DNA damage repair. Conversely, high expression of SCML1 impeded the repair mechanism (Figure 3C). Upon DNA damage, the phosphorylation of histone H2AX at S139, known as γ-H2AX, is observed, indicating that γ-H2AX is a recognized indicator of DNA damage. Under cisplatin stimulation, western blot analysis revealed a significant association between SCML1 and γ-H2AX expression, indicating that low expression of SCML1 facilitated the initiation of DNA damage repair in OVCA cells. Conversely, high expression of SCML1 had the opposite effect. These findings align with the results obtained from the alkaline comet assay (Figure 3D). To assess the extent of the DNA damage response following cisplatin treatment in OVCA cells with the expression of SCML1, immunofluorescence was employed to detect the formation of γ-H2AX foci. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in the quantity of γ-H2AX foci within the sh-SCML1 group when compared to the NC group. SCML1 regulated the expression level of OGT through E2F1

DEGs were screened in cisplatin-resistant OVCA tissues with downregulated SCML1 compared to cisplatin-sensitive tissues with upregulated SCML1. Heatmaps and volcano plots were utilized to visualize the expression patterns of these DEGs (Figures 4A and 4B). These results suggest that OGT may be a potential regulatory target of SCML1. Western blot demonstrated a significant positive correlation between the expression levels of SCML1 and OGT (Figure 4C). The literature preceding this study has documented the regulatory role of E2F1 in modulating the expression level of OGT.16 Western blot analysis demonstrated a negative regulatory interaction between E2F1 and OGT (Figure 4D). To provide additional confirmation of the regulatory interactions between SCML1, E2F1, and OGT, a rescue experiment involving E2F1 was conducted. Based on the knockdown and overexpression of SCML1, we simultaneously knocked down and overexpressed E2F1 and detected the expression levels of SCML1, E2F1, OGT, and γ-H2AX by western blotting. The findings indicated that the modulation of OGT expression by SCML1 was contingent upon E2F1 and subsequently influenced the expression of γ-H2AX in OVCA cells (Figures 4E and 4F). Subsequently, an OGT rescue experiment was performed to investigate the expression levels of SCML1, OGT, and γ-H2AX. The findings revealed that SCML1 influenced the expression of γ-H2AX in OVCA cells by modulating the expression of OGT (Figures 4G and 4H). SCML1 binding to TRIM21 affects E2F1 protein levels by regulating E2F1 ubiquitination levels

To ascertain the impact of SCML1 on E2F1 protein levels, OVCA cells with NC and knocking down SCML1 were treated with cycloheximide (CHX). The results revealed that SCML1 knockdown enhanced the stability of E2F1 and inhibited its degradation (Figure 5A). Upon overexpression of SCML1 in OVCA cells, treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 restored E2F1 protein levels, indicating that SCML1 may modulate E2F1 protein expression through regulation of E2F1 ubiquitination (Figure 5B). To investigate the regulation of E2F1 protein expression levels by SCML1, mass spectrometry analysis was performed. Mass spectrometry findings suggest that tripartite motif containing 21 (TRIM21) is a potential interacting molecule with SCML1. Results from immunoprecipitation assays indicate that the interaction between TRIM21 and E2F1 in OVCA cells is contingent upon the presence of SCML1 (Figure 5C). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and western blot analyses revealed that the knockdown of SCML1 led to a reduction in E2F1 ubiquitination and an elevation in its protein levels. Conversely, overexpression of SCML1 reversed these effects (Figure 5D). The TRIM21 protein structure comprises a RING-finger domain, a zinc finger domain (B-box), a coiled-coil (CC) region, as well as SPRY and PRY domains.17 Various truncated forms of TRIM21 were generated to identify the structural domain mediating its interaction, and Co-IP results revealed that the CC domain is essential for TRIM21 binding to E2F1 (Figure 5E). The impact of SCML1 on the susceptibility of OVCA to cisplatin is mediated by the regulatory influence of E2F1 on OGT

Using knockdown and overexpression of SCML1 as the foundation, we conducted E2F1 and OGT rescue experiments to evaluate the cellular activity of distinct subgroups of OVCA cells after exposure to varying concentrations of cisplatin. The results of the CCK8 assay suggested that the reintroduction of E2F1 or OGT in the rescue experiment partially altered the sensitivity of OVCA cells to cisplatin, although it was unable to fully negate the effects of SCML1, which implied that the modulation of OVCA cell responsiveness to cisplatin by SCML1 is additionally impacted by the levels of expression of E2F1 and OGT, suggesting that SCML1 affects the sensitivity of OVCA cells to cisplatin through the regulation of OGT by E2F1 (Figure 6A). After rescuing E2F1 and OGT, OVCA cells were exposed to cisplatin treatment. Subsequent crystal violet staining of the surviving OVCA cells demonstrated that SCML1 plays a significant role in modulating the sensitivity of OVCA cells to cisplatin through its regulation of E2F1 and OGT (Figure 6B). Overexpression of SCML1 in ovarian cells stimulated with cisplatin-facilitated DNA damage. Additionally, the rescue of E2F1 and OGT in response to this stimulation partially facilitated DNA damage repair. However, E2F1 and OGT did not counteract the promotion of DNA damage caused by SCML1. The results of the SCML1 knockdown experiments were contrary to those of the SCML1 overexpression experiments (Figure 6C). Immunofluorescence was employed to detect the formation of γ-H2AX foci. Stimulation of ovarian cells with cisplatin, in conjunction with the overexpression of SCML1, leads to an increase in the number of γ-H2AX foci. Conversely, the rescue of E2F1 and OGT resulted in a partial reduction in the number of γ-H2AX foci (Figure 6D). Stimulation of ovarian cells with cisplatin, in conjunction with knockdown of SCML1, leads to a reduction in the number of γ-H2AX foci. Conversely, the rescue of E2F1 and OGT resulted in a partial increase in the number of γ-H2AX foci (Figure 6E). The relationship between SCML1 and OGT expression in OVCA was examined through analysis of the gene expression profiling interactive analysis (GEPIA) database, which unveiled a notable positive correlation in the mRNA level between the SCML1 and OGT (Figure 6F). When SCML1 is lowly expressed, the experimental results are the opposite (Figures S2A and S2B).

Discussion OVCA is a common malignant tumor of the female reproductive system, and its mortality rate ranks first among all gynecological tumors. It is a public health problem that seriously affects women’s health.19 Each histological subtype of ovarian cancer has a different clinical behavior and molecular pattern, but the current standard treatment for all types of OVCA is still combined with tumor cell reduction and platinum chemotherapy. Although some new treatment methods have been recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, the 5-year survival rate of patients has not significantly improved. All of these approved drugs have significant toxicity.20,21,22,23 Therefore, researchers still need to further study the mechanism of OVCA occurrence and development, overcome the serious dilemma of cisplatin resistance in OVCA, extend the survival period of patients, and improve the quality of life of patients.

Moreover, platinum resistance is an important cause of the poor prognosis of OVCA patients. Platinum drugs are cell cycle-nonspecific antitumor drugs that bind to DNA and inhibit the replication of tumor cell DNA, thus inhibiting tumor growth.24 Recent studies have revealed that there are many factors leading to the resistance of tumors to platinum drugs, and the more recognized mechanisms of platinum drug resistance are roughly the following four aspects: first, a decrease in the transmembrane transport ability of platinum drugs, leading to a decrease in platinum drug accumulation in tumor cells25; second, increased expression of intracellular detoxification substances such as glutathione (GSH) and metallothionein (MT), which leads to the inactivation of platinum drugs26,27; third, the DNA damage repair function is enhanced, resulting in insufficient DNA damage to cause apoptosis of tumor cells28; Fourth, the apoptotic function of tumor cells is defective, and tumor cells cannot normally initiate the apoptotic signaling pathway. Among them, abnormal activation of DNA damage repair is the main cause of tumor resistance to platinum drugs.29

In our study, we analyzed differences in gene expression between cisplatin-resistant and cisplatin-sensitive individuals concerning OVCA based on a public database. The results showed that SCML1 may be a potential factor affecting the resistance of OVCA to cisplatin. Subsequently, we tested clinical samples. The results showed that SCML1 expression was decreased in cisplatin-resistant OVCA samples. In addition, SCML1 promoted the therapeutic effect of cisplatin on OVCA cells both in vivo and in vitro. These results confirmed that SCML1 could affect the resistance of OVCA to cisplatin.

The adenovirus E2 gene involved in the activation of cellular transcription factor (E2F) was discovered in 1986, and the interaction between nuclear extracts of adenovirus-infected cells and the E2 promoter region of adenovirus was studied.30 E2F1 was the first identified and most studied member of the E2F family.31 E2F1 is a pivotal transcription factor integral to multiple BPs, including the cell cycle, apoptosis, and the response to DNA damage.32,33 Many studies have shown that E2F1 can promote the occurrence and development of lung cancer,34 gastric cancer,35 prostate cancer,36 liver cancer,37 colorectal cancer,38 and other tumors. Furthermore, high expression levels of E2F1 have been correlated with a poor prognosis in a diverse range of tumor types. Recent studies have demonstrated that the upregulation of E2F1 expression in gastric cancer cells is associated with the increased expression of MDR1 and MRP proteins, reducing the accumulation of anti-cancer drugs within the cells, and thereby contributing to the development of cisplatin resistance in gastric cancer.39 In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), E2F1 promotes the expression of AUF1, which in turn stabilizes the AKR1B10 mRNA, thereby exacerbating the development of HCC and contributing to chemoresistance.40 Existing research has documented that the overexpression of E2F1 contributes to the development and progression of OVCA.41 The expression level of E2F1 in OVCA cells has been observed to significantly influence both the proliferation and sensitivity to chemotherapy.42 However, the underlying regulatory mechanisms governing these effects remain inadequately explored, necessitating further in-depth investigation. In this study, E2F1 affects DNA damage in OVCA cells by modulating OGT, thereby affecting the sensitivity of these cells to cisplatin treatment. This finding is consistent with the results of a previous study and reinforces the role of E2F1 in regulating chemotherapy.

Glycosylation is an important post translational modification of proteins that plays an important role in many BPs. O-glycosylation is the most widely studied modification.43 The N-acetyl-β-D-glucosamine (GlcNAc) monosaccharide unit (O-GlcNAc) linked to intracellular proteins by O-glycosidic linkages are a special form of glycosylation. This modification is directly involved in regulating many BPs.44 O-GlcNAc glycosylation (O-GlcNAcylation) is regulated only by OGT and O-linked N-acetylglucosamine to control the action of transferase (OGA).45 OGT uses a variety of substrates to regulate cellular BPs, such as protein synthesis, protein degradation, and protein-protein interactions.46,47,48 The dysregulation of OGT expression is implicated in a wide array of diseases, such as malignant tumors, cardiovascular disorders, and diabetes.49,50 Consequently, OGT may be a promising candidate for tumor therapy. In lung cancer, oxidative stress enhances the binding of OGT with NRF2, and the elevated levels of O-GlcNAcylation at Ser103 of NRF2 promote resistance to cisplatin.51 In colorectal cancer, OGT engages with thymidylate synthase to facilitate its O-GlcNAcylation, enhancing the enzyme’s stability and contributing to resistance against 5-FU therapy.52 Cisplatin is a widely employed chemotherapeutic agent for the treatment of OVCA; however, the emergence of drug resistance remains a significant hurdle, frequently leading to treatment failure. The expression of OGT was observed to be higher in chemoresistant OVCA tissues compared to chemosensitive ones. A reduced level of OGT expression led to a decrease in the O-GlcNAc modification of SNAP-29, thereby enhancing autophagy in OVCA cells, which contributed to the development of resistance against cisplatin.53 By lowering the OGT-induced O-GlcNAcylation of SNAP-23, exosome secretion is triggered in OVCA cells, thereby fostering resistance to cisplatin.54 In the study, OGT expression was reduced in resistant OVCA tissues. Upon analyzing public databases, it was found that the upregulation of SCML1 is linked to an enhanced expression of OGT. Conversely, a downregulation of SCML1 results in the suppression of OGT expression. Previous studies have identified E2F1 as a negative regulator of OGT expression.16 Combined with our data, we hypothesized that SCML1 could upregulate OGT and promote the accumulation of γ-H2AX in OVCA cells by inhibiting E2F1 expression.

In vivo and in vitro experiments showed that SCML1 inhibited the expression of E2F1, and overexpression of E2F1 reduced the increase in OGT and γ-H2AX levels caused by SCML1 overexpression. After E2F1 knockdown, the resistance of OVCA cells to cisplatin decreased. OGT knockdown can enhance the resistance of OVCA cells to cisplatin. In addition, the expression of γ-H2AX, a sensitive DNA damage marker, was increased in OVCA cells after OGT overexpression, suggesting that the overexpression of SCML1 promotes OGT expression, promotes DNA damage in OVCA cells, and inhibits cisplatin resistance in OVCA cells. Meanwhile, restoring E2F1 and OGT, respectively showed that E2F1 and OGT can influence the effect of SCML1 on the proliferation of OVCA cells and subcutaneous tumors to a certain extent. This further indicates that SCML1 regulates OGT through E2F1, thereby affecting the progression of OVCA.

In conclusion, our study suggested that SCML1 plays an important role in cisplatin resistance in OVCA. SCML1 promotes the expression of OGT in OVCA cells by inhibiting the expression of E2F1, thereby promoting the accumulation of γ-2AX in cells, promoting DNA damage in OVCA cells, and inhibiting the resistance of OVCA cells to cisplatin. This study provides a new target for screening for cisplatin resistance in OVCA patients in the future and provides a new approach to the treatment of OVCA patients with cisplatin resistance.

Limitations of the study

1.The underlying mechanisms have not been fully elucidated. Although this study proposes a mechanism, where SCML1 regulates OGT expression through E2F1, thereby influencing DNA damage and cisplatin sensitivity, the specific molecular pathway by which the SCML1-TRIM21 interaction regulates E2F1 ubiquitination requires further validation. This is particularly important for confirming its generalizability across different ovarian cancer cell lines and in vivo environments.2.The clinical sample size was limited: The number of human ovarian cancer tissue samples used in the study was relatively small. This may affect the robustness and representativeness of the statistical results. Future studies with large-scale, multi-center clinical samples are needed to validate the reliability of SCML1 as a biomarker.3.The complexity of the in vivo model was not fully explored: Although the study utilized a nude mouse subcutaneous xenograft model, this model does not fully recapitulate the tumor microenvironment or metastatic characteristics of human ovarian cancer. It also does not account for the potential influence of the immune system on the response to cisplatin treatment. Future research could employ models that more closely mimic the clinical scenario (e.g., orthotopic transplantation or humanized models) for further validation.

STAR★Methods

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal subjects

The subcutaneous tumor model was established using female nude mice (BALB/c background, 6–8 weeks old) purchased from Vital River Laboratories (Beijing, China). All nude mice were housed in a Specific Pathogen Free (SPF) animal laboratory. The left axilla of the nude mice was used to inoculate the stably transfected SKOV3 and stably transfected CAOV3 cell lines with 5×106 cells. Beginning on the fourth day after subcutaneous injection of OVCA cells, administer intraperitoneal injections of cisplatin (3 mg/kg, three times per week). Subsequently, distinct experimental treatments were administered to each group, following the specific requirements of the experiment. The volume and weight of each group of tumors were measured, and growth curves and histograms of tumor weights were generated. All experiments were meticulously conducted under the instruction from the Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University Research Ethics Committee. Approval number: CMU20241228.

Human subjects

All OVCA tissue samples were sourced from patients in Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University, China (Ethical Approval Number for the human participant: 2025PS311K). Before their inclusion in the study, each OVCA patient provided informed consent by signing a detailed consent form. The fundamental information of the enrolled patients is comprehensively presented in Table S1.

Cell lines and cell culture

The human OVCA cell lines CAOV3, HEYA8, SW626, SKOV3, and A2780 were obtained from BeNa Technology (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China). These cells were cultured in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C using 1640 medium (C3010-0500, Vivacell, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (26010074, Gibco, Paisley, UK) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P1400, Solarbio, Beijing, China). None of the cells in this study were passaged more than 20 times. All cell lines were STR validated and were verified as mycoplasma negative.

Method details

Gene expression profile data and prognosis data

Gene expression profiling data (GSE189717 and GSE15372) of cisplatin-sensitive and cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancers were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/). The GSE189717 dataset is used for differential gene expression analysis utilizing platinum-sensitive and platinum-resistant ovarian cancer cell lines. The GSE15372 dataset utilizes cisplatin-sensitive and cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer cell lines for differential gene expression analysis. The present investigation generated two datasets using the Affymetrix HG-U133 plus 2.0 platform. The Entrez Gene ID was subsequently transformed into the corresponding gene symbol based on the annotation platform. Differential gene screening was based on a log2-fold change >1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05, where downregulated genes are indicated by blue and upregulated genes are indicated by red. Univariate and Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between the expression level of candidate genes and prognosis in the GSE140082 cohort.

Western blotting

Whole-cell lysates were prepared in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with PMFS and phosphatase inhibitors. Whole-cell lysates were used to extract ovarian cancer tissue and ovarian cancer cell proteins. Equal amounts of total protein samples were subjected to separation on 10% SDS‒PAGE gels and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. The primary antibody was diluted with the primary antibody dilution buffer at a ratio of 1:1000, and the corresponding PVDF membrane was incubated overnight at 4°C. Then the PVDF membrane was blocked using a 5% skim milk solution for 1 h at room temperature. The proteins immobilized on the PVDF membrane were incubated with either goat-rabbit secondary antibodies or goat anti-mouse rabbit antibodies for 1 h at ambient temperature. The protein content was subsequently identified by employing Western Bright ECL. The primary antibodies used in the experiments were against SCML1 (Proteintech, No: 15093-1-AP, 1:500), E2F1 (Abiowell, No: AWA02699, 1:1000 and Cell Signaling Technology, No: 3742, 1:1000), OGT (Cell Signaling Technology, No: 24083, 1:1000), γ-H2AX (Abcam, No: ab81299, 1:1000), TRIM21 (Proteintech, No: 12108-1-AP, 1:5000), DYKDDDDK tag (Proteintech, No: 66008-4-Ig, 1:20000), GFP tag (Proteintech, No: 50430-2-AP, 1:2000), Ubiquitin (Proteintech, No: 10201-2-AP, 1:5000), and β-actin (Abcam, No: ab8226, 1:1000).

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from ovarian cancer tissues and paracancerous tissues using an RNAfast200 kit (Fastagen, China). Total tissue RNA was extracted and reverse transcribed into cDNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time qPCR was performed with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The paraffin sections of ovarian cancer tissues and paracarcinoma tissues were subjected to baking in a temperature oven set at 65°C for 2 h, followed by dewaxing and hydration. The tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval by being placed in a repair box containing citric acid antigen repair buffer (pH 6.0) within a microwave oven, followed by subsequent cooling to ambient temperature. The tissue sections were incubated in a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution at room temperature for 10 min, followed by three subsequent washes with PBS 3 times. The tissue was blocked with 5% Compaq goat serum and subjected to incubation at 37°C for 30 min. The tissues were subjected to primary antibody incubation at 4°C overnight. The primary antibody working solution was removed by washing with PBS, followed by the dropwise addition of the secondary antibody working solution. The incubation was carried out at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, a DAB color development solution was added, and the duration of color development was modified while the samples were observed under a microscope. Subsequently, dehydration and sealing were performed, and the staining intensity was examined through microscopic observation. The primary antibodies used in the experiments were against SCML1 (Proteintech, No: 15093-1-AP; RRID: AB_2153243,1:200), E2F1 (Proteintech, No: 12171-1-AP; RRID: AB_2096958, 1:100), OGT (Proteintech, No: 11576-2-AP; RRID: AB_2156943, 1:200), and γ-H2AX (Abcam, No: ab81299, 1:100).

For positive controls, intestinal crypt epithelial cells were employed, as they are known to express the SCML1 consistently, ensuring the validity of the detection system. For negative controls, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was used to replace the specific primary antibody, which helps rule out non-specific binding of secondary antibodies and background signals.

The formula for calculating the H-score is:H−score=∑(pi×i)Here, pᵢ represents the percentage of positively stained cells relative to the total number of cells on the slide, andidenotes the staining intensity (0 for negative, 1 for weak positive, 2 for moderate positive, and 3 for strong positive).

The final score ranges from 0 to 300, with a higher score indicating a stronger overall positive intensity.

We define the scoring categories as follows:

Score 1: 0–75 points, Score 2: 76–150 points, Score 3: 151–225 points, Score 4: 226–300 points.

Immunofluorescence assays

Ovarian cancer cells that had undergone pretreatment were subjected to fixation using a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 15 min at room temperature. A primary antibody working solution of γ-H2AX was added after blocking with 5% goat serum, and the slides were incubated overnight at 4°C. Subsequently, a fluorescent secondary antibody working solution was added and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. To visualize the nuclei, the cells were incubated with a DAPI working solution at room temperature for 10 min. The fluorescence intensity of γ-H2AX and the number of γ-H2AX foci were observed under a laser scanning confocal microscope. The primary antibody used in the experiments was against γ-H2AX (Abcam, No: ab81299, 1:250).

Cell viability assay

Ovarian cancer cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3000 cells per well and subsequently subjected to treatment based on the assigned experimental group. Ovarian cancer cells were stimulated for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of assay reagent was added and incubated at 37°C for 2.5 h. The resulting absorbance at 450 nm was quantified utilizing an enzyme marker.

Construction of stabilized ovarian cancer cell lines

The construction of the lentiviral vector was accomplished at Nanjing Corues Biotechnology. Stable ovarian cancer cell lines overexpressing SCML1 (referred to as SCML1 cells) and control cell lines (referred to as Con-SCML1 cells) were established, along with SCML1 knockdown cell lines (referred to as sh1 cells) and control cell lines (referred to as NC cells).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays

Collect the ovarian cancer cells to be tested and lyse them thoroughly on ice with Lysis/Wash Buffer for 20 min. After centrifuging at 4°C and 12000 rpm for 5 min, collect the supernatant. Add corresponding primary antibody and isotype IgG to separate EP tubes, respectively. Incubate the EP tubes at room temperature on a vertical rotator for 30 min. Add the antigen-antibody complex to the pre-treated magnetic beads and incubate at room temperature on a vertical rotator for 30 min. Following antigen elution and the collection of the supernatant, further analysis will be carried out using mass spectrometry or western blot techniques for detailed detection.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using the IBM SPSS Statistics 26 and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software programs. Three independent replicate experiments were performed, and the mean ± SD was calculated. An unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used to analyze the differences between the two groups. ANOVA was conducted to determine the differences among the various groups.
