Outcomes after specialized rehabilitation for patients with critical COVID-19 and prolonged intensive care unit stays
Authors: Jenny Petersson, Liza Bergström, Anna Cedborg, Johan Uusijärvi, Marianne Kristiansson, Eddie Weitzberg, Fredrik Lennmyr, Eva Sundman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAdvances in intensive care have improved survival in patients with prolonged critical illness, including those with critical COVID-19. However, prolonged stays in intensive care units are associated with impairments in physical function, mental health, and health-related quality of life.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed outcomes for 30 patients admitted to a specialized intensive care rehabilitation center between August 2020 and April 2022 after critical COVID-19.ResultsPatients had a median age of 62 years and had spent a median of 52 days in an intensive care unit prior to admission, followed by 32 days at the center. At the center, weaning from invasive mechanical ventilation via a tracheostomy tube was achieved in 12 of 13 patients within a median of 17 days, and 23 of 25 patients with a tracheostomy were decannulated after a median of 18 days. Functional outcomes improved significantly, as measured by the Functional Oral Intake Scale, the Chelsea Critical Care Physical Assessment Tool, and the Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living (p < 0.001). One- and two-year survival rates were 90% and 86%, respectively. Assessments of health-related quality of life showed physical functioning as the most affected domain.ConclusionThese findings suggest that specialized rehabilitation may support functional recovery and favorable long-term outcomes in patients recovering from critical COVID-19, consistent with reports from other populations with prolonged critical illness.

Introduction

Advances in technology and methods have improved intensive care and supported the survival of patients requiring prolonged treatment [1]. Awareness of the long-term challenges faced by survivors of prolonged critical illness has also increased [2]. The COVID-19 pandemic posed unprecedented challenges, with many patients requiring longer intensive care unit (ICU) stays due to severe respiratory complications and prolonged mechanical ventilation (PMV) [3–6].

There are several terms used to describe patients in need of intensive care treatment for longer time periods, such as ‘prolonged intensive care,’ ‘persistent critical illness,’ ‘chronic critical illness,’ and ‘PMV,’ reflecting variation in patient characteristics and differences in care practices [6]. Historically, mortality rates in this patient group have ranged from 50–70% [7–12] and the [13] majority of individuals dependent on invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) beyond 60 days were unlikely to be weaned [14,15].

More recent studies indicate a shift in outcomes. Across specialized weaning units and intensive care rehabilitation centers, mixed ICU populations requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation have demonstrated substantially improved results, with 1-year survival rates of approximately 65–75% and higher rates of successful weaning and decannulation [16–19]. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests that extended ICU stays and PMV do not appear to increase the risk of depression, anxiety, or Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) compared to ICU survivors in general [16–18].

A broad spectrum of disease severity characterizes COVID-19, ranging from asymptomatic or mild infection to critical illness with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and multi-organ failure. Patients with critical COVID-19 often require prolonged intensive care and experience severe physical and respiratory deconditioning [15]. Persistent symptoms after COVID-19, commonly referred to as ‘long COVID’, can occur in both non-hospitalized and hospitalized individuals, but represent a clinical entity distinct from the functional impairments that arise after prolonged ICU treatment for critical COVID-19 [20].

Despite increasing knowledge regarding outcomes in mixed ICU populations with PMV, evidence on recovery in patients with critical COVID-19 and prolonged intensive care treatment remain limited. The aim of this study was therefore to examine rehabilitation-related outcomes in patients with prolonged ICU stays due to critical COVID-19 by evaluating weaning, decannulation, physical function, mental health, health-related quality of life, and two-year survival following specialized intensive care rehabilitation in Sweden.

Materials and methods

All consecutive patients with critical COVID-19 who were referred to and admitted to a specialized intensive care rehabilitation center in Sweden between August 2020 and April 2022 were included. The center, established in 2013, provides specialized rehabilitation for patients recovering after prolonged intensive care, independent of underlying diagnoses. The study was approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board in Stockholm (reference numbers 2021–05746-01 and 2024–01572-02). As this was a retrospective study based on anonymized data, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

All patients admitted to the specialized intensive care rehabilitation center were either: (i) dependent on IMV via a tracheostomy tube, (ii) weaned from IMV but still dependent on a tracheostomy, or (iii) in need of advanced medical treatment in combination with rehabilitation that could not be met elsewhere. Unstable patients remained in their ICUs until stable for transfer. Rehabilitation was initiated once patients were medically stable and continued throughout the stay alongside ongoing intensive care. Medical rounds involving physicians, nurses, and assistant nurses were held three times per week and as needed. A weekly rehabilitation round gathered in addition physiotherapists, occupational and speech-language therapists, dietitians, social workers, medical engineers and other specialists when required, for a full interprofessional team to guide individualized programs. Therapy was provided six days per week and included respiratory management, progressive mobilization, strength and endurance training, activities of daily living (ADL) practice, swallowing and communication therapy, and psychosocial support provided by the unit’s social worker, with referral to a psychologist when more complex needs were identified. Pharmacological management of psychological symptoms was overseen by the attending physicians in collaboration with a consulting psychiatrist.

Patients who needed IMV via the tracheostomy tube for part of the day were not considered weaned from IMV. Patients who required respiratory support after decannulation were treated intermittently with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) or non-invasive ventilation (NIV) and considered weaned from IMV. Continuous IMV was not exchanged for continuous non-invasive ventilation. Patients with a tracheostomy were deemed decannulated upon removal of the tracheal cannula. If the cannula was reinserted temporarily, the time for decannulation was determined by final removal of the cannula.

Cardiopulmonary sleep study (CPSS) provides diagnostic accuracy and information for CPAP titration comparable to polysomnography [21,22] and was used to diagnose obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). A portable sleep recording device, Nox T3 (Nox Medical, Reykjavik, Iceland) was used to record airflow via a nasal cannula, oxygen saturation, chest and abdominal movement by respiratory inductance plethysmography, sleep time and body position.

Scoring instruments and questionnaires

Validated instruments were used to measure swallowing ability, physical function, ADL, mental health and HRQoL. Swallowing ability was measured using the Functional Oral Intake Scale (FOIS) [23–25]. Physical function was evaluated using the Chelsea Critical Care Physical Assessment Tool (CPAx) [26,27] and aerobic capacity and endurance were measured with the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT) [28–31]. Daily activity independence was rated with the Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living (Katz ADL). The EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level Questionnaire (EQ-5D-5 L) and its visual analogue scale (EQ VAS) together with RAND 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (RAND-36) were used to evaluate HRQoL [32,33]. Mental health outcomes, including anxiety and depression, were assessed using the Swedish versions of the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 Questionnaire (GAD-7) [34] and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [35–37].

Statistical analysis

Changes in FOIS, CPAx and Katz ADL between rehabilitation center admission and discharge were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test due to non-normal distribution. Results are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR).

Results

Thirty patients with critical COVID-19, admitted to a specialized intensive care rehabilitation center in Sweden after prolonged hospital and ICU treatment, were included in the study. The median age was 62 years (range 31–80 years, IQR 54–68 years), with 22 (73%) males. On admission, 13 patients required IMV (median duration pre-admission: 51 days, IQR 29–74) via tracheostomy. In total, 25 patients had a tracheostomy on admission (median duration pre-admission: 42 days, IQR 22–56). Tracheostomy date was missing for one patient. Sixteen patients were weaned off IMV pre-admission (median time 39 days, IQR 35–53), four were decannulated (median time 25 days, IQR 15–40) and one patient had been treated with NIV but not IMV. Weaning time data was missing for one patient. Five patients were admitted for more advanced medical treatment than a standard, non-ICU, rehabilitation facility could provide, due to high oxygen demands, advanced NIV treatment, or both, but did not have a tracheostomy. A total of 12 out of 13 patients dependent on IMV on admission to the center were successfully weaned after a median time of 17 days (range 0–65, IQR 7–40), with a total median IMV duration of 74 days (IQR 32–113). Among the 25 patients with a tracheostomy on admission to the center, 23 were decannulated after a median time of 18 days (range 4–124, IQR 9–31) at the center, with a total median time with a tracheostomy of 56 days (IQR 50–82). One patient with IMV and tracheostomy deteriorated before weaning attempts and died at the center following a decision to limit treatment escalation due to poor prognosis. One patient was readmitted to the ICU because of repeated pneumothorax likely related to COVID-19.

Before discharge, 18 of the 29 surviving patients underwent at least one CPSS. Two patients with pre-existing CPAP-treated obstructive sleep apnea could continue their prescribed therapy without further CPSS. One patient was readmitted to the ICU before a CPSS could be conducted and one patient declined. Two patients were unable to perform the CPSS due to newly developed cognitive impairment likely related to the critical COVID-19 and prolonged ICU stay. The remaining five patients were clinically assessed as not needing a CPSS.

Based on the CPSS results, nine patients were considered likely to benefit from CPAP or NIV treatment, three could not tolerate the mask, and were instead managed with oxygen therapy and/or bed positioning. Six patients were treated with CPAP and one with NIV at discharge.

While none of the patients had had supplemental oxygen prior to the COVID-19 infection, all patients required oxygen therapy on admission to the center. For 11 patients, oxygen therapy could be discontinued after intensive care rehabilitation. Seventeen patients were still dependent on oxygen supplementation either during physical activity, during sleep, or continuously at the time of discharge from the center.

Most patients (26/28) suffered from dysphagia on admission with a median FOIS level of 1 (IQR 1–3), indicating feeding-tube dependent enteral nutrition with minimal or inconsistent oral intake. A total of 24 patients (80%) relied solely on non-oral feeding. Dysphagia significantly improved until discharge reaching a median FOIS level of 5 (IQR 2–6) (p = 0.0001) and 15 patients (54%) were able to meet their nutritional needs orally with FOIS levels ranging from 4 to 7 (Figure 2). On admission, 22 patients were dependent on assistance for their ADL, and none were completely independent according to Katz ADL. At discharge, 23 patients exhibited improvement, and 7 patients achieved complete independence, attaining the highest possible score. The median Katz ADL improved from 0 (IQR 0–2) on admission to 2 (IQR 1–4) at discharge (p = 0.0001).

The median length of stay at the intensive care rehabilitation center was 32 days (IQR 24–50). Of the 30 admitted patients, 29 survived until discharged (97%), and of these, 28 were discharged to a lower level of care. The majority, 24 patients, were discharged for further rehabilitation, or planning of rehabilitation. Three patients were discharged home. One patient needed an evaluation at an acute care hospital, and one patient was readmitted to the ICU.

The post-discharge assessment of depression, anxiety, and HRQoL was performed a median of 13 months (IQR 12–26) after discharge from the center. At that time, four patients had died and three were lost to follow-up, resulting in data available from 23 patients.

Using the PHQ-9, 12 patients showed minimal depression (scores 0–4), six mild depression (scores 5–9), four moderate depression (scores 10–14), and one moderately severe depression (scores 15–19). No patient showed severe depression (scores 20–27). The GAD-7 assessment indicated that 17 patients showed minimal anxiety (scores 0–4), five mild (scores 5–9), and one moderate generalized anxiety disorder (scores 10–14). No patient displayed signs of severe anxiety (scores 15–21).

Within the first 30 days after discharge from the center, two patients died, with one of them being the patient readmitted to the intensive care unit. One additional patient died within the first year after discharge, resulting in a 90% one-year survival rate. Yet another patient died within two years after discharge, leading to an overall two-year survival rate of 86%. The causes of death were not specified in the available records.

Discussion

In this study, patients with critical COVID-19 requiring prolonged intensive care demonstrated substantial recovery following specialized interprofessional rehabilitation. All survivors were successfully weaned from invasive mechanical ventilation and tracheostomy despite extended ICU stays and prolonged ventilation prior to admission. At two-year follow-up, survival remained high, and patients reported better-than-expected health-related quality of life, along with low levels of anxiety and depression.

Evidence on rehabilitation outcomes in patients with prolonged critical COVID-19 requiring PMV remain limited. One study reported successful weaning, decannulation, and functional improvement when rehabilitation was initiated during invasive mechanical ventilation, however, the included patients had shorter ICU stays and higher baseline function than our cohort [41]. Another study of patients with ICU durations comparable to our cohort, but with rehabilitation initiated later, after weaning from mechanical ventilation, demonstrated improvements in physical function and ADL but to a more limited extent [42].

Similarly, non-COVID post-ICU rehabilitation programs delivered after hospital discharge have shown limited effects on physical recovery and survival, although some mental health benefits have been reported [43,44–47]. These studies primarily included shorter stay ICU survivors (typically 1–2 weeks), representing a clinically distinct population with substantially different illness severity and likely differing recovery trajectories.

Reports from specialized ICU-level rehabilitation and weaning services for patients with prolonged ICU stays and PMV indicate higher weaning success, functional improvement, and more favorable long-term outcomes [48–50]. Although this study was not designed to compare outcomes across diagnoses, the rehabilitation model predates the COVID-19 pandemic and is used for patients with prolonged ICU stays regardless of etiology. Previously published outcomes from non-COVID groups treated within the same model report broadly similar recovery patterns [48,51], suggesting that even though COVID-19 is a disease with unique pathophysiology and presentation, prolonged critical illness share many challenges that may be possible to overcome with similar principles of individualized rehabilitation.

The high rate of successful weaning and decannulation in our cohort highlights the importance of structured interprofessional management during prolonged ICU-level rehabilitation. These findings are consistent with recommendations emphasizing individualized assessment, evaluation of breathing function and secretion management, and coordinated decision-making across clinical disciplines [52–54]. Dysphagia was common at admission, consistent with known risks associated with tracheostomy and PMV in critical COVID-19 [55–57]. Although FOIS scores improved significantly, some patients were decannulated despite minimal oral intake, indicating that swallowing scales alone do not capture all factors relevant to decannulation readiness. Most patients also had low to moderate CPAx cough scores on admission, and the combination of subjective grading and the absence of objective measures of cough strength limits the usefulness of this domain for evaluating secretion management [58,59].

Functional outcome measures improved during rehabilitation, with gains in physical capacity and basic ADL. While the improvements of median results may seem limited, 7 of 29 patients were fully independent in ADL by discharge and the number of patients able to complete the 6MWT increased from one to 16. Follow-up after discharge suggested continued progress, as several patients reported minimal difficulty walking longer distances according to the RAND-36 physical functioning domain. Also grip strength as part of CPAx improved while no patient exceeded 60% of age- and sex-adjusted reference values [60]. Taken together, even though physical function was not fully recovered, the documented improvements were clinically relevant and supporting patients’ functional capacity and independence.

Mental health outcomes are a central component of long-term recovery after critical illness. Pre-COVID research shows that approximately one-third of ICU survivors experience anxiety and depression one year after discharge [16,17] and studies of COVID-19–related critical illness report similar or slightly higher rates of psychological distress [61,62]. In contrast, our cohort showed notably lower levels of anxiety and depression, which may be associated with the structured interdisciplinary rehabilitation and early psychological support provided.

To better understand how patients perceived their health after discharge, we assessed HRQoL using RAND-36, EQ-5D-5 L, and EQ VAS. In RAND-36 (Figure 4), results were broadly comparable to those reported in previous Nordic ICU COVID-19 cohorts despite the longer ICU stays in the present study [5] with the greatest impairments observed in the physical domains. Similarly, the EQ-5D-5 L (Figure 5) showed that differences compared with the Swedish reference population were mainly observed in the physical dimensions, whereas self-care and anxiety/depression were more similar to the reference population [39].

Despite these remaining physical limitations, patients reported EQ VAS scores that were comparable to those of the Swedish general population during the pandemic [40] (Figure 6) and similar values have been reported in other cohorts of patients recovering from critical COVID-19 [62,63].

Taken together, these findings suggest that even though certain physical limitations may remain after critical COVID-19 and prolonged intensive care, patients’ perception of global health may be fair, underscoring the importance of providing treatment and rehabilitation according to individual need.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides a broad description of recovery during specialized intensive care rehabilitation, using several established measures of physical function, swallowing ability, and ADL. All eligible patients admitted during the study period were included, and follow-up response rates were high, which strengthens the reliability of the findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a single-center study with a relatively small cohort, which limits generalizability. As no control group was available, improvements cannot be attributed solely to rehabilitation, and natural recovery likely contributed. Economic data were not collected; therefore, cost-effectiveness cannot be assessed. However, almost all survivors were discharged to a lower level of care, which may indicate that this model supports an efficient care trajectory, although formal analyses are required.

Only one follow-up assessment of HRQoL and mental health was performed, limiting insights into longer-term trajectories, and information on psychological or social support after discharge was not available. Larger, controlled studies with repeated follow-up are needed to clarify the contribution of specialized ICU rehabilitation to long-term outcomes.

Conclusion

Despite the challenges associated with prolonged intensive care and invasive mechanical ventilation, this cohort of critically ill COVID-19 patients demonstrated clinically meaningful improvements in swallowing ability, physical function, and ADL during structured interprofessional intensive care rehabilitation. Survival remained high at two years, and patients reported comparatively low levels of anxiety and depression.

These findings suggest that specialized, interprofessional intensive care rehabilitation may facilitate recovery in patients requiring prolonged intensive care due to COVID-19, consistent with observations in other populations with prolonged critical illness. Future studies should examine which components of the rehabilitation model are most important for recovery and how programs can be tailored to different patient groups.
