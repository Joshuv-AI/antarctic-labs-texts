Patient–Doctor Communication in Migraine: Results from the OVERCOME (Japan) 2nd Study
Authors: Shiho Suzuki, Ryotaro Ishii, Daisuke Danno, Tsubasa Takizawa, Masayuki Hamakawa, Yoshinori Tanizawa, Satoshi Osaga, Nao Sasaki, Chie Hashimoto, Mika Komori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDespite the availability of effective treatments, some patients with migraine in Japan do not receive appropriate medical management and treatment for migraine, potentially due to the lack of effective patient–doctor communication. This analysis of the ObserVational survey of the Epidemiology, tReatment, and Care Of MigrainE (OVERCOME [Japan)] 2nd study described the current state and challenges of patient–doctor communication in migraine treatment in Japan.MethodsThis analysis included adults with migraine who consulted doctors for headaches/migraine and reported details of their first consultation: their experiences with doctors regarding migraine diagnosis and treatments, migraine-related topics, and treatment goals discussed, and challenges in communication.ResultsAmong the 6762 included respondents, 55.1% (3729/6762) consulted a general practitioner (GP) first for headaches. Of the 3192 respondents diagnosed with migraine and reported the diagnosing doctor’s specialty (as GP, specialist, or non-headache specialist), specialists (64.0%; 2044/3192), followed by GPs (31.4%; 1001/3192), mostly commonly diagnosed migraine. In the first consultation, doctors told 48.0% (3249/6762) of respondents they had migraine. In the past year, 3854 respondents discussed migraine-related topics with doctors; most commonly, the use and effectiveness of prescription drugs, and headache symptoms, severity, and frequency [48.9% (1885/3854) to 67.8% (2612/3854)]. Specialists most commonly discussed migraine-related topics with respondents, but non-headache specialists, i.e., obstetricians and gynecologists, most commonly discussed menstrual-related migraine. A total of 1978 respondents had migraine-related topics they wanted to but could not discuss with doctors in the past year. Among them, 32.9% (651/1978) of respondents did not discuss the topics because the doctor did not ask. Although 96.6% (3724/3854) of respondents had treatment goals in mind, 36.0% (1388/3854) never discussed it with doctors.ConclusionOpen and considerate patient–doctor communication and appropriate medical judgment are needed for timely and adequate medical management of migraine. Training doctors in effective communication techniques and considering a migraine diagnosis during consultations may be associated with better patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-025-00839-2.

Introduction

The prevalence of migraine, a chronic neurologic disease, is 7.3–8.4% in Japan [1, 2]. Additionally, multiple real-world Japanese studies have associated migraine with a significant burden in terms of quality of life, work productivity, and activity impairment, healthcare resource utilization, and costs [3–8].

The Japanese Headache Guidelines 2021 recommend acute treatments for migraine, including over-the-counter (OTC) medications, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, or prophylactic therapy [9]. New migraine medications approved since 2021 [10], such as calcitonin gene-related peptide monoclonal antibodies (CGRP mAbs; galcanezumab, fremanezumab, and erenumab) and selective 5-HT1F receptor agonists (lasmiditan), are also recommended [9]. Real-world studies have also reported the favorable effectiveness of these medications [11–24]. However, among Japanese people who met the International Classification of Headache Disorders—3rd edition criteria for migraine [25], 42.6% never sought medical help [6]. Moreover, the use of migraine-specific drugs such as triptans, CGRP mAbs, and lasmiditan was low (up to 14.8%) [6, 26]. The lack of appropriate medical attention or treatment could be partly attributed to the patient's tendency to either consider headache pain as ‘not sufficiently severe,’ endure the pain, or use OTC analgesics [27, 28].

The American Migraine Communication Study II, a patient–doctor communication study, trained healthcare professionals (HCP) on patient-centered interviewing techniques such as ask–tell–ask sequences and open-ended questions. These led to a better understanding of migraine impairment and interictal burden, reduced HCP-patient misalignment, and increased HCP and patient satisfaction [29]. In Japan, Hasegawa et al. [30] reported that patients with migraine were satisfied with treatment when doctors asked about the impact of migraine on their daily lives, sympathized with their headache pain, and had an empathetic attitude. Due to existing psycho-cultural–social tendencies in Japan [31], patients with migraine tend to share their symptoms and treatment goals only if the physician asks. However, Japanese physicians state that patients do not proactively share their symptoms and treatment goals and are not proactively involved in their treatment [32]. This misalignment in expectations can only be solved via more effective communication. Currently, patient-centered interviewing techniques are not commonly used in routine clinical practice [33], probably due to a lack of appropriate communication skills or time [34].

The OVERCOME (Japan) 2nd study was conducted in 2023 to describe the clinical history, migraine burden, patient-reported outcomes (PRO), and medical management of patients with migraine throughout their life course [26]. The current analysis of the OVERCOME (Japan) 2nd study assessed the current state and challenges of patient–doctor communication in migraine treatment and sought to explore methods to improve it, thus leading to better patient outcomes.

Methods

Study Design

Danno et al. [26] described the study design, participant recruitment, and sample construction of the OVERCOME (Japan) 2nd study. Briefly, this observational, cross-sectional, nationwide, population-based online survey recruited Japanese adults with or without migraine between June 2023 and August 2023. Participants were recruited using online survey panels from Cross Marketing, Inc., GMO Research, Inc., and Rakuten Insight, Inc.. All participants were screened, and a sample demographically matched in age, gender, and geographic region with the 2020 Japanese census data [35] was constructed. After screening, eligible participants were divided into migraine and non-migraine groups.

The Medical Corporation TOUKEIKAI Kitamachi Clinic ethics committee approved this study (number BGQ09531) on April 19, 2023. The study was conducted per ethical principles originating from the Declaration of Helsinki and was consistent with Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices. All applicable Japanese laws and regulations were followed. All survey respondents provided electronic informed consent and agreed to participate in the study. All data were anonymized before analysis. The datasets generated and/or analyzed during the current study are available at Eli Lilly Japan K.K on reasonable request.

Study Population and Selection Criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria for the OVERCOME (Japan) 2nd study were described in the primary publication [26]. The current analysis was performed on a subset of participants with migraine who consulted doctors for headaches/migraine and reported details of the first consultation. These participants self-reported having a migraine diagnosis by a physician, or met modified International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition migraine criteria [25]. Some analyses were only performed on a subpopulation of participants who met the above criteria and also consulted doctors in the past year.

Variables and Outcomes

Participants self-reported demographic data such as age and sex and clinical characteristics including the average number of monthly headache days (MHD), and OTC and prescription drug use. The impact of migraine was assessed using PRO instruments, including the Japanese Migraine Disability Assessment Scale (MIDAS) questionnaire [36], the Japanese version of the Headache Impact Test-6 (HIT-6) [37], Migraine Interictal Burden Scale-4 (MIBS-4) [38, 39], and Impact of Migraine on Partners and Adolescent Children Scale (IMPAC) [40] (electronic supplementary material Table S1). Necessary permissions to use MIDAS, HIT-6, MIBS-4, and IMPAC were obtained.

Participants also reported their experiences with doctors regarding migraine diagnosis and treatments, migraine-related topics, and treatment goals discussed with doctors, and challenges in communicating with them.

Statistical Analysis

All data were summarized descriptively using mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequency and percentage for categorical variables. Subgroup analyses were performed to describe the differences in (1) migraine severity, frequency, and medical management based on whether they were told that they had migraine at the first consultation, and (2) migraine-related discussion topics with doctors based on the doctor’s specialty, and patient satisfaction with preventive drugs and prescribed acute drugs. The doctors were categorized as general practitioners (GP), specialists, and non-headache specialists. GPs included GPs or doctors at an internal medicine hospital or clinic; specialists included neurologists, neurosurgeons, psychiatry/psychosomatic medicine specialists, headache specialists, or pain specialists; and non-headache specialists included obstetrician–gynecologists, otolaryngologists, ophthalmologists, pediatricians, doctors at a hospital emergency room/night clinic, company clinic, or other specialty doctors.

The results were not adjusted. Formal bivariate comparisons were performed for some subgroups using the t test for continuous variables, Mann–Whitney U test for ordered categorical variables, and the two-proportion z test for categorical variables. Two-tailed p values were reported. Most of the questions in this survey were designed to prevent missing responses, and missing data were not expected. Therefore, we did not define a strategy for handling missing data. However, some MIDAS responses were excluded due to being infeasible, detailed in footnotes of Table 1. Patient Characteristics and Patient-Reported Outcomes

In the overall sample (N = 6762), the mean (SD) respondent age was 42.5 (13.5) years, 68.2% (4611/6762) were female, and 69.5% (4698/6762) were employed (Table 1). Most respondents self-reported a migraine diagnosis by a physician (66.3%). Mean (SD) age at first physician visit was 25.9 (11.6) years. Respondents experienced a mean (SD) of 4.4 (6.0) MHDs at the time of the survey.

Among females with menstruation, 52.2% (1715/3286) experienced menstrual-related migraine (Table 1). Mean (SD) pain severity, MIDAS, HIT-6, and MIBS-4 scores were 6.1 (2.0), 15.1 (25.8), 61.4 (7.4), and 3.9 (3.8), respectively. Per the IMPAC grade, 42.3% (1961/4639) of respondents reported severe-to-very severe impact of migraine on their family. Most respondents had used OTC (87.9%; 5942/6762) or prescribed acute drugs (87.9%; 5937/6762), but preventive drug use was low (48.4%; 3271/6762).

Treatment Journey

From the overall sample (N = 6762), 55.1% (3729/6762) of the respondents first consulted a GP for their headaches and 30.3% (2047/6762) first consulted a specialist for their headaches (Fig. 2). Among the respondents who reported the specialty of the doctor who formally diagnosed their migraine (n = 3192), migraine was most commonly diagnosed by specialists (64.0%; 2044/3192). Among respondents who consulted a doctor in the past year (n = 4610), 46.9% (2164/4610) of respondents visited a GP and 50.5% (2328/4610) visited a specialist. Among the respondents who first consulted GPs (n = 3729), some respondents transitioned to specialists (26.7%; 994/3729) or non-headache specialists (4.6%; 170/3729) to continue migraine management in the past year. Communication and Treatment in the First Consultation

In the first consultation, doctors told 48.0% (3249/6762) of the respondents that they had migraine, while 53.9% (3643/6762) were told inappropriate or disappointing words from the perspective of a respondent with migraine, such as ‘wait and see,’ ‘headache other than migraine,’ or ‘just a headache’ (Fig. 3). The proportion of respondents who were told such inappropriate or disappointing words was significantly higher among respondents not told they had migraine in the first consultation (69.7%; 2449/3513) versus respondents told they had migraine in the first consultation (36.7%; 1194/3249) (p < 0.001). Headache severity (p < 0.001) and frequency (p = 0.003) were slightly greater in respondents told they had migraine at the time of the first consultation versus those not told they had migraine at the time of the first consultation (electronic supplementary material Table S2).Fig. 3Patient–doctor communication in the first consultation (N = 6762). In the first consultation, 83.2% of the total population (5627/6762) had at least some interference with work or daily activities due to headaches (electronic supplementary material Table S2). Similar results were observed among respondents who were told they had migraine in the first consultation (n = 3249). While 84.8% (2756/3249) had at least some interference with work or daily activities due to headaches in the first consultation (electronic supplementary material Table S2), 75.9% (2467/3249) were only prescribed acute treatments [analgesic (59.6%; 1935/3249) and triptan or lasmiditan (26.1%; 847/3249)] (electronic supplementary material Figure S1). Only 21.9% (712/3249) received preventive oral medication in the first consultation (electronic supplementary material Figure S1) despite 36.2% (1176/3249) being eligible (electronic supplementary material Table S2). Doctors told 11.1% (362/3249) of respondents to take OTC headache medicine and 14.9% (483/3249) to ‘wait and see’ (electronic supplementary material Figure S1).

Communication During Consultations in the Past Year

Migraine-related communication topics with doctors in the past year were assessed among respondents who discussed such topics with doctors during this period (n = 3854; Fig. 1). These respondents majorly discussed the use and effectiveness of prescription drugs and headache symptoms, severity, and frequency with doctors in the past year [48.9% (1885/3854) to 67.8% (2612/3854); Fig. 5a]. When the consulting doctors were classified by their specialty for migraine management (GPs, specialists, and non-headache specialists), we found that specialists most commonly discussed migraine-related topics, especially topics related to headaches [up to 59.1% (1041/1762)], preventive drugs [up to 30.4% (536/1762)], and new treatments [18.0% (317/1762)] (Fig. 5b). Further classifying by the consulting doctor’s medical department showed that some topics were most frequently discussed by non-headache specialists, while specialists most frequently discussed other topics. In fact, 48.8% (39/80) of respondents who consulted doctors in the obstetrics and gynecology department (i.e., non-headache specialists) discussed menstrual-related migraine with the doctors (data not shown). On the other hand, 37.0% (127/343) of respondents who consulted psychiatry/psychosomatic medicine specialists (i.e., specialists) discussed mental distress due to migraine (data not shown). Discussion

This analysis of the OVERCOME (Japan) 2nd study described the current state and challenges of patient–doctor communication in migraine treatment in Japan. It revealed that people with migraine receive management and treatment from various medical departments throughout their lives. While specialists play a central role in the diagnosis and treatment of migraine, GPs and non-headache specialists also play essential roles. However, suboptimal communication between doctors and people with migraine may lead to delays in treatment and lower patient satisfaction. This study suggests that doctors should improve patient–doctor communication and consider a diagnosis of migraine during their consultations. This may help to improve outcomes for people with migraine.

In the current analysis, 55.1% of respondents first consulted a GP for their headache, and 46.9% of those who consulted doctors in the past year consulted GPs. This is consistent with the proportion of respondents who consulted GPs for migraine/headache in other OVERCOME studies from Japan, Europe, and the United States (34.4–70.3%) [6, 41, 42]. This makes GPs a key healthcare provider in a patient’s migraine treatment journey [43].

In this analysis, respondents who were told that they had migraine at the first consultation had significantly greater headache severity and frequency than respondents not told they had migraine. They had also more commonly previously used triptans and had faster triptan or oral preventive drug initiation. This suggests that the initial response and decisions made by doctors in the first consultation may influence the subsequent access to appropriate migraine treatment. Vaghi et al. [44] reported this occurrence in a multi-national European survey. Here, 34% of respondents consulted ≥ 4 specialists before being diagnosed with migraine, and 40% did not receive prescription migraine medications for > 5 years after diagnosis. Moreover, in the current analysis, doctors dismissed 53.9% of the respondents’ headaches in the first consultation by saying ‘wait and see,’ ‘headache other than migraine,’ or ‘just a headache.’ Such conversations could be disappointing to people with migraine, seeking medical support for their burden of disease. These observations support the importance of doctors considering a potential migraine diagnosis when managing patients with headaches. However, doctors, especially GPs, may find migraine diagnosis and treatment challenging in initial consultations due to symptom variation, patients not describing/disclosing all the symptoms, time constraints, and/or lack of specialized training in migraine management [42, 43, 45]. As GPs, and non-headache specialists in some cases, are the primary healthcare provider for patients, they should be aware of migraine diagnostic criteria and management strategies [46, 47]. They should also consider referring the patient to a specialist if they suspect a migraine diagnosis or if the treatment strategy is not controlling the symptoms [46, 47]. These actions may reduce the time to diagnosis and access to treatment, thus reducing patient burden [44]. However, even specialists may not diagnose migraine at the first consultation, as they may prioritize ruling out secondary headaches [45, 48] and ask patients to record information such as headache severity and medication usage during attacks [48]. While these steps are crucial for an accurate diagnosis, it is also important to prevent any unnecessary delays in confirming a migraine diagnosis and/or initiating appropriate treatment.

In the current analysis, among GPs, specialists, and non-headache specialists; specialists most frequently discussed a wide variety of migraine-related topics. These topics were related to headache frequency, severity, or symptoms; preventive drugs; and new treatments. This could be attributed to improved awareness among specialists about the pathophysiology of migraine, optimal treatment methods, and aspects important for patient satisfaction, relative to GPs and non-headache specialists [49]. Indeed, in this analysis, the treatment satisfaction of respondents on preventive drug treatments was significantly associated with the number of migraine-related topics discussed. Treatment satisfaction of respondents was also significantly associated with communication with doctors (i.e., greater satisfaction among respondents reporting “There is nothing that I really wanted to talk about but could not”). This suggests that some doctors may be carefully considering each patient’s situation, preference, and needs, to personalize their migraine management.

Non-headache specialists and GPs play a key role in the management of migraine. In the case of menstrual-related migraine, this was most commonly discussed by non-headache specialists such as obstetricians and gynecologists. Also, of those who had consulted doctors in the past year, 46.9% had consulted GPs These findings reiterate the importance the need for awareness of migraine diagnosis, management and treatment goals in these healthcare settings.

The respondents' most common treatment goals in the current analysis were: none or fewer headache attacks, sleeping without a headache, and fast relief after acute medication. These and other goals have been previously reported as common treatment goals among patients with migraine [50, 51]; achievement of these treatment goals has been associated with increased patient satisfaction [52]. Among respondents who discussed migraine-related topics with doctors during consultations in the past year, 96.6% had treatment goals in mind, but as many as 36.0% of them had never discussed their treatment goals with doctors. Up to 10.8% of respondents also reported they wanted to discuss the current prescribed medication’s effectiveness, future treatments, new treatments, and their treatment goals in consultations. However, they felt they could not discuss it, mainly because the doctor did not prompt them. Up to 30% of respondents felt the treatment would not change after discussion. Moreover, 30–40% of respondents reported their doctor never or sometimes listened carefully to them, understood the symptoms, gave an easily understandable explanation, and spent adequate time with them. These behavioral characteristics could be attributed to the perceived power balance between doctors and patients in Japanese clinical settings [32] due to the psycho-cultural–social tendencies in Japan [31]. Overall, these results demonstrate that doctors did not always communicate effectively with respondents and that patients did not always feel they could openly communicate with their doctor.

Addressing such a communication gap between doctors and patients with migraine requires a multi-faceted approach. Doctors need to be trained in effective communication techniques [29], which involves physician empathy, using open-ended questions, active listening, explaining the diagnosis and treatment in detail, and spending adequate time with the patient [32, 34, 43, 53–55]. These actions may facilitate treatment adherence, improve outcomes, and increase doctor and patient satisfaction [55, 56]. In fact, Glassner et al. [53] reported that ≥ 95% of patients were very satisfied when doctors gave them time to talk in the consultation and thoroughly answered their questions, compared with only 29% when the doctors asked yes/no or short-response questions. Doctors also need to develop a co-operative relationship [34, 54] and communicate with patients according to their health literacy levels [57]. Incorporating the aforementioned techniques may help doctors enhance patient–doctor communication and subsequently patient satisfaction.

Strengths and Limitations

Danno et al. [26] describe the strengths and limitations of the OVERCOME (Japan) 2nd study. The current analysis described multiple aspects of patient–doctor communication. This is a major strength of this analysis as such data have not been previously reported in Japan. However, this analysis also has some limitations. Since communication is heavily influenced by cultural tendencies, some results of this analysis may not be generalizable to people with migraine outside Japan. Also, respondents self-reported the data, so are subject to recall bias, especially data regarding experiences such as the respondents’ first consultation for headaches. The self-reported data could have been validated by triangulating with the respondents' medical records, but this is outside the scope of the study. The current statistical analyses also did not adjust for the covariates. This study only considered the respondents’ perspectives, so future research on the doctors’ perspectives may shed more light on the various aspects of patient–doctor communication. Finally, patient sex may influence some aspects of patient–doctor communication [58–61], but such assessments are outside the scope of the current study. Future research could evaluate the impact of patient sex on patient–doctor communication.

Conclusion

Communication between patients and doctors during medical management for migraine is suboptimal in Japan. Open and mindful patient–doctor communication and appropriate medical judgment are needed for timely and adequate medical management of migraine. This may be associated with better patient outcomes. To achieve this, a wide range of healthcare professionals, centered around specialists, must be mindful of migraine in their consultations and possess the attitude and skills to carefully elicit and understand patients’ daily challenges and wishes.
