Cost disparities between pain and opioid use disorder formulations of buprenorphine in US health care system: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Robert W Hurley, Alexandra M Coffield, Spencer W Faircloth, Jae C Yong, Meredith C B Adams
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Buprenorphine offers unique therapeutic benefits in both pain management and addiction medicine through its partial μ-opioid agonist and κ-opioid antagonist properties, providing effective analgesia with reduced abuse potential and respiratory depression risks compared to full μ-opioid agonists.1 The relationship between inadequately controlled pain and opioid use disorder (OUD) development involves multiple factors including opioid exposure, individual susceptibility, and central sensitization.2,3 Given these correlations and buprenorphine's more favorable safety profile, it presents as a potentially advantageous first-line opioid analgesic option for patients with risk factors for OUD, undiagnosed OUD, or comorbid pain and OUD.1,4 Despite these advantages, buprenorphine remains underutilized.5

In the United States, multiple buprenorphine formulations have Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval with distinct indications for either analgesia or medication treatment of opioid use disorder (MOUD). This study aimed to quantify the financial burden of each buprenorphine formulation for patients with differing insurance coverage plans and, when possible, compare this price to the payer acquisition cost.

Methods

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of buprenorphine formulation costs using multiple data sources (IRB #00122891) following CHEERS guidelines for economic analysis in healthcare (https://bit.ly/41IlV3B). Patient out-of-pocket data were derived from the IQVIA Longitudinal Prescription Claims database (January–December 2023) for commercial insurance, self-pay, Medicaid, and Medicare Advantage Part D coverage. GoodRx (https://www.goodrx.com) was used for discounted self-pay prices (November 2024). Payer acquisition costs, or the cost an insurance company or government entity will pay to acquire a medication, were analyzed using National Average Drug Acquisition Cost data (October 2024), Medicare Part D Pricing Public Use File (Q3 2024), and Federal Supply Schedule for Veterans Affairs pricing (November 2024).

Cost comparisons were standardized using equianalgesic doses from FDA-approved prescribing information.6 We analyzed minimum available costs (out of pocket and acquisition for patients and payers, respectively) for each formulation type and FDA indication, calculating monthly and annual expenditures for each payment category and computing relative cost ratios between pain and OUD formulations (R version 4.2.0).

Results

Analysis revealed consistent patient out-of-pocket cost disparities between pain and OUD formulations across categories (Table 1 and Figure 1). Medicare beneficiaries faced a 6.6-fold difference between the least expensive pain and OUD formulations (monthly range: $5.62–$76.94 for OUD vs. $37.22–$109.45 for pain formulations). Commercial insurance patients showed moderate disparities ($18.63–$57.58 for OUD vs. $39.89–$109.45 for pain formulations). Self-pay patients encountered the highest costs, with monthly OUD formulation expenses ranging from $94.64 to $360.10 and pain formulations from $363.92 to $1324.13. Discount programs provided some relief, but costs remained substantial. Most Medicaid beneficiaries and Veterans with 100% service-connected disability ratings (https://www.pbm.va.gov) have subsidized out-of-pocket costs resulting in near zero and zero, respectively, patient payments at the pharmacy counter.

Generic oxycodone (10 mg TID) had substantially lower costs across all payment categories compared to both OUD and pain buprenorphine formulations, with the disparity particularly severe for pain formulations (Table 1). Medicare beneficiaries paid $4.75 monthly for oxycodone versus $37.22–$109.45 (7.8–23.0 times higher) for buprenorphine pain formulations. Commercial insurance patients paid $13.25 for oxycodone versus $39.89–$109.45 (3.0–8.3 times higher) for buprenorphine pain formulations. Self-pay patients faced the greatest disparity, with oxycodone costing $42.32 compared to $363.92–$1324.13 (8.6–31.3 times higher) for buprenorphine pain formulations.

Payer acquisition costs also showed significant disparities between pain and OUD formulations (Table 1, Medication Acquisition Cost (MAC) column). Under Medicaid, costs ranged from $21.11 to $1066.70 monthly (50.5-fold difference). Medicare showed similar disparities ($20.55–$1029.78 monthly, 50.1-fold difference). Veterans Affairs rates were consistently lower ($10.21–$403.08 monthly, 39.48-fold difference).

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate significant cost disparities between pain and OUD buprenorphine formulations across payment categories, with self-pay patients facing the greatest barriers. Insurance status significantly impacts medication accessibility,4 with uninsured patients paying up to 19 times more than insured patients for identical medications.

The wide variation in payer acquisition costs (up to 100-fold between lowest and highest options) suggests systemic pricing inefficiencies that could affect formulary and coverage decisions. Although VA patients don't experience out-of-pocket cost disparities, VA procurement costs show similar disparities between pain and OUD formulations, albeit at rates up to 50% less than Medicare and Medicaid for identical medications, likely reflecting the Veteran’s Health Administration (VHA) ability to negotiate drug prices.

These disparities have important clinical implications. Higher out-of-pocket costs for pain formulations may discourage use in patients who could benefit from their lower abuse potential.4 To address this barrier within buprenorphine formulations, clinicians could consider increased off-label use OUD formulations for pain treatment, as can be practiced in the VA health system (https://bit.ly/4fYUgBp).

Particularly concerning is the substantial cost advantage of generic oxycodone compared to all buprenorphine formulations. This dramatic price differential (up to 42.5-fold for self-pay patients with discount programs and 85.6-fold for Medicaid acquisition costs) creates a powerful economic incentive for both patients and healthcare insurers to use a full μ-opioid agonist with higher abuse potential rather than the safer partial agonist buprenorphine. This economic disparity directly undermines public health efforts to increase utilization of safer opioid alternatives.

While addressing cost disparities is crucial, it represents only one dimension of the complex barriers to optimal buprenorphine use. Even with improved affordability, significant challenges persist, including clinician-level barriers and societal stigma. Many clinicians remain hesitant to prescribe buprenorphine formulations indicated for OUD in pain treatment due to limited training and comfort.7 This reluctance, combined with persistent stigma surrounding medications traditionally associated with addiction treatment, creates multi-layered access barriers beyond economic considerations.8,9 Implementation of standardized training programs, public health campaigns, and professional advocacy efforts are needed to address stigma and normalize consideration of all buprenorphine formulations when clinically appropriate.7–9

Study limitations include the single, cross-sectional, time point analysis, proprietary restrictions on commercial insurance acquisition costs, and focus solely on cost barriers rather than other access issues such as provider availability or pharmacy stocking practices.

Conclusion

Significant cost disparities exist between pain and OUD formulations of buprenorphine, creating barriers to optimal pain treatment and potentially OUD prevention. Addressing these disparities through policy reforms, payer acquisition cost negotiation, and professional education could improve utilization of buprenorphine and potentially contribute to better public health outcomes
