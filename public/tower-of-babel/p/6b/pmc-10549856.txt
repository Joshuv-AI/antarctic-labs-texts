Congenital Cervical Scoliosis Treated with Concave Side Distraction with Three‐Dimensional Printed Titanium Cage
Authors: Zihe Li, Feifei Zhou, Shuheng Zhai, Tian Xia, Shengfa Pan, Fengshan Zhang, Li Zhang, Jiasheng Chen, Yu Sun
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy DesignSingle‐center, retrospective study.ObjectiveHemivertebra resection is the only treatment option for congenital cervical scoliosis (CCS). However, this procedure is complex and technically demanding. It often requires a considerably long operation, and there is substantial intraoperative bleeding. Therefore, we have attempted to treat CCS with a concave side distraction comprising a three‐dimensional (3D) printed titanium cage. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the safety and efficacy of this technique for the treatment of patients with CCS.MethodsA series of 22 patients with CCS who underwent a concave side distraction technique between 2019 and 2021 were retrospectively reviewed and analyzed. Radiological measurements included the Cobb angle of the distraction segments, the kyphosis angle, the range of movement, and the distraction correction angle. Student's t‐test and Spearman correlation analysis were used for statistical analysis. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe study included 12 males and 10 females whose ages ranged from 6 to 14 years old (9.8 ± 2.1 years old). Follow‐up times ranged from 15 to 30 months (25.8 ± 3.6 months). Among 22 patients, two patients developed a postoperative C5 nerve root palsy and recovered after being treated with conservative treatment for 6 months. The duration of surgery ranged from 229 to 756 min (389 ± 112 min), and the estimated volume of blood loss ranged from 100 to 600mL (235 ± 121 mL). The coronal Cobb angle (p < 0.001), kyphosis angle (p < 0.05), and range of movement (p < 0.001) between the last follow‐up and preoperative period were significantly different. A total of 28 segments were distracted, and the Cobb angle of the distraction segment ranged from 2.4 to 14.1° (8.5 ± 3.0°). There were six upper cervical spines (8.9 ± 1.9°) and 22 lower cervical spines (8.4 ± 3.2°) with no significant difference between them (p = 0.130). In addition, there was no correlation between the angle of the concave side distraction and patients’ age (r = 0.018, p = 0.315). The fusion was solid between the bone and the customized 3D‐printed pore metal cage at the final follow‐up.ConclusionThe concave side distraction comprising a customized 3D‐printed titanium cage implantation can provide satisfactory correction results and is a safe and reliable procedure for treating CCS.Keywords: Concave Side Distraction, Congenital Cervical Scoliosis, Customized 3D‐printed Cage, Surgery

Introduction

Congenital scoliosis (CS) encompasses a heterogenous spectrum of conditions that differ in etiology, severity, and presentation. Such cases are classically described as vertebral formation failure, osseous segmentation failure, or a complex overlapping thereof. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Most commonly, spinal deformity of this nature is associated with the presence of one or multiple hemivertebrae leading to scoliosis or kyphosis deformity. 5 , 6 Formation failure may produce a hemivertebra, which represents complete formation failure of half the vertebra and includes three main types: fully segmented (65%), partially segmented (22%), and nonsegmented/incarcerated (12%). 7 , 8

Posterior spinal fusion was previously a popular procedure for CS. However, due to its inability to correct the scoliosis of in‐situ fusion, hemivertebra resection has gradually become the preferred surgical strategy for CS patients secondary to single hemivertebra, and satisfactory radiographic and clinical outcomes have been demonstrated. 9 , 10 , 11 Hemivertebra resection is the only option for treating congenital cervical scoliosis (CCS). 12 , 13 , 14 Osteotomy and excision of the hemivertebra is a complex and technically demanding procedure due to vertebral artery and nerve root exposure. This procedure often requires a long operation and substantial intraoperative bleeding, which are not conducive to patient safety. For example, in the study of Cao et al., the average operation time and intraoperative bleeding of patients treated with hemivertebra resection were 500 min and 703 mL, respectively. 12 To avoid osteotomy or hemivertebrectomy, our center has been attempting to treat CCS with a concave side distraction technique since 2015. A shaped cage filled with cancellous bone graft is placed in the interfacet and intervertebral disc space after the distraction on the concave side, and short segment fixation is applied. 13 Since 2019, we have used three‐dimensional (3D) printed customized pore metal blocks to fill the gap in the intervertebral and interfacet space on the concave side. This study evaluates (i) the safety and efficacy of this surgical procedure in treating CCS (e.g. considering the operation time, blood loss and correction effect) and (ii) the improvement in the condition of the distraction segment on the concave side to provide guidance for preoperative design.

Methods

This study was approved by Peking University Third Hospital Medical Science Research Ethics Committee (No. 2015269).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria: (i) patients with torticollis caused by congenital cervical scoliosis (defined as a Cobb angle of >10°); (ii) surgery indication in patients with severe disfiguring deformity or with proven deterioration deformities; (iii) both operation and follow‐up were performed in our hospital; and (iv) follow‐up time ≥15 months after surgery.

Exclusion criteria: (i) patients with other spinal deformity or disease (e.g., cervical kyphosis, severe ossification of cervical posterior longitudinal ligament, ankylosing spondylitis, and cervical primary or metastatic tumors); (ii) patients with a history of spinal trauma or surgery; (iii) patients with congenital cervical scoliosis caused by torticollis appearance or deformed vertebra in cranial cervical junction; (iv) patients with an incomplete set of radiographical data; and (v) follow‐up time <15 months after surgery.

Selection of the Participants and Observation Assessment

After obtaining institutional review board approval, a retrospective chart review was undertaken. From December 2019 to February 2021, 22 patients with cervical scoliosis were treated with a concave side distraction technique in the Department of Orthopedics of Peking University Third Hospital. Twenty‐two consecutive patients were included, with a mean follow‐up of 25.8 months.

Demographic data were collected for this study. Radiological measurements of the Cobb angle of the distraction segment were performed using preoperative, immediate postoperative, and follow‐up coronal reconstruction imaging of CT scans (Figure 1A,B). The Cobb angle of the distraction segment was defined as the angle between the superior border of the upper vertebral body and the inferior border of the lower vertebral body of distracted segments. Cervical spine curvature was also measured between the angle formed by the lines parallel to the posterior edge of the C2 and C7 vertebral bodies. Interspinous motion of the fixed segment was used as the criteria to evaluate the fusion status. 15 , 16 The gap between the bone and the 3D‐printed spacer was also observed to assess the fusion status. The range of motion of the cervical spine was calculated, including distracted segments and C2–C7 segments on flexion and extension lateral view radiographs. Preoperative spine MRI was performed in all patients to assess intracanal anomalies. Preoperative evaluation also included meticulous neurological examination. Intraoperative blood loss, operative time, and complications were recorded. All imaging data were measured by two spinal surgeons.

Preoperative Preparation

A preoperative angiography CT scan of the cervical spine was performed. Decom data were collected and entered into the computer. Mimics Medical (version 21.0.0.406, Materialize, Belgium) was used to design the shape and size of the customized 3D‐printed metal cage with appropriate angle and height after a distraction according to the patient's Cobb angle of cervical scoliosis (Figure 1C,D). A preoperative MRI scan of the cervical spine was also performed. (Figure 1P). The customized 3D‐printed metal cages were made following previously established protocols. Melted titanium alloy powder (Ti6Al4V, particle size 45–100 μm) was used to make an artificial vertebral body in a layer‐by‐layer fashion an using the EBM S12 system (Arcam AB, Sweden), according to computer‐aided design models with customization for the specific anatomical features. The unique aspect of the cage lies in the porosity, which made its mechanical properties comparable to cancellous bone, and the stress shielding effect could therefore be minimized. The porous ultra‐structure might also be advantageous for osteo‐induction. To ensure the procedure was repeatable, when we designed the customized distraction block on the concave side, its target height could balance the coronal deformity, and the size was generally not more than the size of the hemivertebra on the protrusion side. At the same time, we designed five models at an interval of 1 mm for possible changes during the surgery (Figure 1E,F). A 3D‐printed model was created to help determine the distraction segments and evaluate the pedicle morphology. During preoperative planning, we empirically estimated the extensibility of the concave side extension segment and determined the number of extension segments according to the corrected target angle.

Surgical Technique

All patients underwent anterior–posterior–anterior surgery with neuroelectrophysiological monitoring performed throughout the operation.

First, the anterior approach was performed to remove the discs of the distraction segment and release the uncovertebral joints on the concave side. Then the incisions were closed. In the posterior approach, the facet joints of the target segments were exposed and released with the excision of joint capsules and the interlaminar ligamentum flavum of the distraction segment. The pedicle screws (diameter of 3.5 mm) were implanted using navigation assistance. Short segmental fixation was required. For the segment of the upper cervical spine, occipitocervical fixation should be applied when necessary. Before connecting the fixation rod, a propping clamp was used by one assistant to slowly distract the pedicle screws while the rotation of the Mayfield frame was maneuvered under the operating table. An appropriately sized, customized 3D‐printed metal supporting cage was placed into the space between the facets (Figure 1G). The fixation rods were connected, and bone grafting was performed. The posterior incisions were closed. Finally, an anterior approach was performed. The intervertebral space was distracted on the concave side. An appropriately sized customized 3D‐printed metal supporting cage was inserted into the concave side of the intervertebral space, and the corresponding vertebral body was fixed with a titanium alloy plate (Figure 1H,I).

All patients were intraoperatively monitored with somatosensory‐evoked potentials and motorevoked potential neuromonitoring. After surgery, an appropriate brace was used for every patient to protect the instrumentation for 3 months. All patients were followed up regularly.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics (version 20.0; IBM, Armonk, NY). Means and standard deviations were used to describe the continuous variables. Student's t‐test and Spearman correlation analysis were used for statistical analysis. The statistical significance was defined as a value of p < 0.05 based on two‐tailed tests.

There were 12 males and 10 females with a mean age of 9.8 ± 2.1 years (range 6–14 years). The mean follow‐up period was 25.8 ± 3.6 months (range 15–30 months). Sixteen patients underwent a single‐segment distraction, while six patients underwent a two‐segment distraction. Radiological Measurement and Analysis

The mean Cobb angle of the distraction segment was 20.7 ± 6.8° before surgery, which was corrected to a mean of 9.4 ± 7.2° during postoperative follow‐up (Figure 1J–M, Table 1). There was no change in the coronal Cobb angle between the immediate postoperative period and the final follow‐up. However, there were significant differences in the coronal Cobb angle (p < 0.001), the Kyphosis angle (p = 0.044), and the range of motion (p < 0.001). There were 22 patients with a total of 28 segments of distraction, and the mean correction angle of each segment was 8.5 ± 3.0° (range 2.4°–14.1°).

There was no significant difference in the distraction correction angle between the upper cervical vertebra (8.9 ± 1.9°) and the lower cervical vertebra (8.4 ± 3.2°) (p = 0.130). Additionally, there was no correlation between the angle of concave side distraction and patients’ age (r = 0.018, p = 0.315).

The gap between bone and the customized 3D‐printed pore metal cage was fully filled with bone formation in all patients on CT scan with coronal plan reconstruction at final follow‐up. The results were also satisfying for the special distracted segments of C0–C2 (Figure 2A–H).

Operation and Complications

The operation time ranged from 229 to 756 min (389 ± 112 min), and the volume of blood loss ranged from 100 to 600 mL (235 ± 121mL). Among 22 patients, two patients developed a postoperative C5 nerve root palsy and recovered after being treated with conservative treatment for 6 months. After 3 months of wearing braces, all patients returned to normal posture (Figure 1N,O). The implants of all patients were not loosened or sunk during the follow‐up.

Discussion

Clinical Outcomes

Few studies have reported on the surgical treatment of cervical scoliosis. In previous studies, the operation time was relatively long, and blood loss was massive in the hemivertebra excision. 12 , 13 , 14 In our study, the operation time and the blood loss of patients with CCS treated with concave side distraction comprising a 3D‐printed titanium cage were 389 ± 112 min and 235 ± 121 mL, respectively. Additionally, there were significant differences in the coronal Cobb angle (p < 0.001), the kyphosis angle (p = 0.044), and the range of motion (p < 0.001) between preoperation and final follow‐up. The distraction segments were improved in the coronal plane, and the improved angles were even 8.5 ± 3.0° (range 2.4°–14.1°) for each segment. Therefore, our study showed obvious correction of cervical scoliosis, with a shorter operation time and less blood loss, confirming the safety and efficacy of this surgical procedure. From the CT scan with coronal plan reconstruction, we found that the gap between the bone and the customized 3D‐printed pore metal cage had disappeared at the final follow‐up, which indicated that the fusion between the bone and the customized 3D‐printed pore metal cage was solid.

Advances in the Treatment of Congenital Cervical Scoliosis

Cervical scoliosis with hemivertebrae represents a challenge for spine surgeons due to its inherent growth potential, which leads to focal primary and secondary deformities during the growth period. 17 There are three main procedures used for the treatment of CS with hemivertebrae: in‐situ fusion, convex side hemiepiphysiodesis, and hemivertebrectomy. Hemivertebrectomy has become popular over the past few decades. 18 Although CS due to hemivertebra may show spontaneous improvement, early surgical intervention for hemivertebra is advocated by most surgeons. 19 Hemivertebrotomy has been widely used to treat scoliosis deformity in recent years. 20 , 21 However, to avoid hemivertebrotomy or osteotomy and reduce possible neurological complications, we attempted to ameliorate the coronal scoliosis deformity using a concave distraction method. The results showed satisfactory outcomes. 12 The gap created by distraction on the concave side was filled with a shaped PEEK cage filled with cancellous bone harvested from the iliac crest. This process has the shortcomings of inaccurate shaping of the PEEK cage on the operating table and extra incision on the iliac crest.

In recent years, more and more studies have reported the application of customized 3D‐printed titanium alloy pore metal artificial vertebra, which gave us more options for cervical stability reconstruction. 22 , 23 , 24 Based on our experience, we designed a spacer for the concave facet joint and the anterior intervertebral space during preoperative planning. The rough surface of the spacer provided immediate stability and effectively reduced the stress borne by the screw, reducing the possibility of internal fixation failure. Adequate anterior release of the Luschka joint and posterior release of the facet joint made the operation of the concave side distraction easier and safer. To minimize screw loosening during the operation, forceps were used to distract the articular surface directly rather than the screw.

The customized 3D‐printed pore metal cage provided the possibility of bone ingrowth. The bone graft around the spacer can facilitate the fusion process. Our study also indicated that concave side distraction comprising a customized 3D‐printed pore metal cage could achieve a solid fusion 2 years after surgery in the treatment of CCS. We could not accurately predict the corrective volume of the segment before surgery. Therefore, we prepared spacers of different sizes to accommodate different distraction angles. According to the study results, the average improvement angle of distraction was 8.5°. Therefore, in the preoperative surgical design, the number of distraction segments could be determined according to the angle to be corrected to avoid insufficient improvement and reduce fusion segments as much as possible.

Strengths and Limitations

In this study, patients with CCS treated with a concave side distraction with a 3D‐printed titanium cage all had satisfactory outcomes. Both the operation time and blood loss were decreased, the cervical scoliosis correction was obvious, and the fusion between the bone and the 3D‐printed titanium cage was solid. However, this study had several limitations. First, the number of patients enrolled was relatively small due to the rare pathology. Second, the follow‐up time was short, and scoliosis might develop in the future. For young children who received surgeries before puberty, long‐term follow‐up is necessary because curve progression may accelerate when patients enter the adolescent growth period of puberty.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the application of a customized 3D‐printed titanium cage with concave side distraction in the treatment of patients with CCS can achieve definite correction with safe and reliable surgical results.
