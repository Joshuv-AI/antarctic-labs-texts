Introducing a win–win strategy for both rice yield and sheath blight resistance
Authors: Wenlong Guo, Qian Qian, Xiaoming Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis commentary on Feng et al. (2025, Nat. Genet) discusses the role of SBRR1‐R in sheath blight resistance, along with its importance for resistance gene mining in germplasm resources and the potential of molecular design breeding to enhance sheath blight resistance.

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is the most important staple crop in China and plays a central role in ensuring national food security. Rice sheath blight (RSB) is considered one of the most damaging and difficult‐to‐control fungal diseases in rice (Pan et al., 1999). Caused by the soil‐borne pathogen Rhizoctonia solani, RSB often along with lodging, has reduced seed‐setting rates, especially under epidemic conditions. As extreme weather events happen more often, RSB has emerged as a key obstacle to high‐yield cultivation and sustainable, eco‐friendly agricultural development.

Unlike rice blast and bacterial blight, for which numerous resistance (R) genes have been cloned and deployed in elite cultivars, the genetic research and resource accumulation for RSB resistance remain relatively limited. Over the past five decades, approximately 60 rice accessions have been identified with moderate resistance, while fewer than 10 accessions have demonstrated high and stable resistance (Senapati et al., 2022).

Since the late 1990s, Professor Pan Xuebiao's team at Yangzhou University has continuously refined the identification methods and integrated the physical map of quantitative trait loci (QTLs) to clarify the confidence intervals. However, the lack of accurate, high‐throughput phenotypic platforms and advanced genomic technologies has hindered the fine mapping and cloning of these QTLs. After more than a decade of research, the coarse localization to fine mapping of qSB‐9 Tq and qSB‐11 LE has been completed through extensive molecular marker‐assisted analysis (Zuo et al., 2013, 2014). The introduction of qSB‐7 TQ and qSB‐9 TQ into varieties in Jiangsu Province through marker‐assisted selection (MAS) has greatly boosted sheath blight resistance with stable yield (Hua et al., 2014).

Building on this pioneering work, Professor Zuo Shimin's team has been conducting research on RSB since 2014. Through continuous improvement in phenotypic identification, integrated genomics and transcriptomics, a series of key regulators of resistance has been successfully identified. Including OsPGIP1, OsOSM1, OsNYC3, OsERF65, OsSGR, and OsERF7 (Xie et al., 2025a, 2025b). These findings enrich our understanding of the molecular network underlying sheath blight resistance (Senapati et al., 2022).

THE BREAKTHROUGH DISCOVERY OF SBRR1‐R

Zuo Shimin's team successfully cloned SBRR1‐R for RSB resistance from natural rice varieties (Feng et al., 2025). It was found that this gene is highly prevalent among indica rice varieties in areas prone to sheath blight, but is present at a very low frequency in high‐latitude japonica rice varieties. This gene can make certain indica rice varieties more resistant, along with most japonica rice varieties. Introducing high‐yield japonica rice varieties that have this gene helped to prevent large reductions in harvest when sheath blight outbreaks occur. Subsequently, thorough genetic and biochemical mechanism analysis, a SBRR1‐R‐mediated molecular regulation module for sheath blight resistance was established, and the molecular mechanism underlying disease resistance was elucidated. To the best of our knowledge, this gene is the first in the world to be cloned from the natural variety resources of rice, marking a major breakthrough in the field of RSB resistance. It changes the previous situation in which no sheath blight resistance genes were available for rice breeding and will accelerate the cultivation process of new RSB‐resistant varieties.

INTEGRATE INTELLIGENT BREEDING STRATEGIES

The identification of SBRR1‐R will provide a clear and actionable target for enhancing resistance to RSB, while offering strong potential for integration with AI (Artificial intelligence)‐enabled breeding strategies (Fu et al., 2025). Initially, UAV (Unmanned aerial vehicles)‐based hyperspectral imaging combined with AI‐driven automatic recognition will enable the rapid and accurate acquisition of large‐scale field phenotypic data, facilitating the quantification of the relationship between disease severity and the SBRR1‐R genotype. Subsequently, comprehensive pan‐genome and haplotype analyses will be employed to systematically characterize SBRR1‐R and establish a resistance allele repository. These allelic and haplotypic variants, along with their expression profiles, will be incorporated as key features into genomic selection and AI‐based prediction models, allowing multi‐trait prediction that integrates yield, grain quality, and disease resistance. This pipeline will ultimately support data‐driven, predictive breeding, enabling the rational design of parental combinations and accelerating the development of high‐performance cultivars with improved RSB resistance and favorable agronomic traits (Figure 1).

Review of the history and future prospects of sheath blight breeding

(A) Current status of the molecular network of resistance genes to mosaic disease. In the process of resistance quantitative trait loci (QTLs) localization, the fine mapping of qSB‐9 Tq , qSB‐11 LE , and qSBR11‐1 was achieved. In the molecular network of sheath blight resistance, OsPGIP1 and OsRLCK5 were found to play an important role in maintaining cell wall and plasma membrane homeostasis. OsOSM1, Osa‐miR444b.2 and OsEIL2 enhanced host resistance through the regulation of various hormone signal transduction pathways and the expression of WRKY transcription factors in the face of bacterial infection. OsSGR, OsERF7, and OsRSR1 responded to the proliferation of bacterial infection by affecting the accumulation of reactive oxides. The bHLH57‐SBRR1‐R‐SIP1‐Chit3/4 immunoregulatory module led by SBRR1‐R has been shown to be effective in dealing with pathogenic infection. (B) Artificial Intelligence (AI) accelerates breeding. Future resistance breeding can mine the alleles of resistance genes in more germplasm resources, build corresponding analysis models to improve the accuracy of data and predict and evaluate complex traits, and finally achieve a balance between resistance and yield and quality.
