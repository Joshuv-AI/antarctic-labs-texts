Cold sintering of hybrid copper-iodides in transparent ceramics using dolomitization-inspired densification
Authors: Yu Ren, Fu-zhi Dai, Sijia Lu, Yunfeng Hu, Qi Ding, Mingming Si, Yongsheng Sun, Zhiguo Xia, Yuchi Fan, Wan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe non-toxic hybrid luminescent copper(I) iodides are competitive solution for environmentally sustainable applications in solar cells, lighting, and detectors. However, their susceptibility to elevated temperature and oxidation creates great hurdles for technological integration due to the lack of suitable encapsulation. Inspired by the mechanism and kinetics of dolomitization in geology, we propose a cold sintering process that enables the densification of highly transparent SrF2 ceramic at 150 oC, which can serve as a matrix for hybrid copper iodides. The introduction of cation-rich (Sr2+ rich) solution accelerates the rate-determining precipitation of solvated ions, which largely promotes the cold sintering process at low temperature. During the proposed cold sintering process, the synergistic effect of uniaxial pressure and temperature generates a continuous dispersion of melted hybrid copper iodide ([Cu4I4(pph2Et)4]) throughout the thermally conductive matrix, leading to an external quantum efficiency of 50%, enhanced resistance to thermal quenching, and superior stability after 100 h of damp-heat test. By encapsulating various hybrid copper iodides, the strategy is applicable for not only full-spectrum white lighting, but also X-ray imaging.Subject terms: Lasers, LEDs and light sources; Lasers, LEDs and light sources

Introduction

Hybrid copper(I)-iodides have emerged as a class of versatile phosphors with near-unity photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQY > 95%), broad spectral tunability (450-750 nm), and environmental sustainability1–3. These characteristics make the hybrid clusters ideal candidates for optoelectronic devices4–11. However, their practical application is fundamentally limited by thermal and chemical instability. The decomposition typically initiates below 200 oC and can be further deteriorated by ambient humidity, leading to ligand dissociation and emission quenching12. Therefore, without a suitable packaging matrix to protect the vulnerable hybrid copper iodide, their practical application in luminescent devices is difficult, especially when long-term service is concerned. For instance, although polymeric matrices such as epoxies and silicones are widely used in lighting devices due to their processability and high initial transparency in the visible region, their long-term reliability is often constrained by heat and UV irradiation13,14. Compared with a conventional polymeric matrix, a transparent ceramic matrix with high stability and thermal conductivity offers a promising platform to display the optical performance of hybrid clusters. In particular, the fluorite-type fluoride ceramics such as SrF2 exhibit advantages as a transparent and robust matrix, due to the lower phonon energy and wider optical transmittance (0.125–11 μm)15–18. However, the densification of transparent fluoride ceramics by conventional sintering can only be achieved through grain boundary diffusion at temperatures as high as 2/3 of the melting point (Tm in Kelvin)19, which would inevitably destroy the structural integrity of the hybrid clusters.

To reduce the sintering temperature of fluoride ceramics for compatibility with hybrid copper iodide, an alternative strategy for low-temperature densification needs to be explored. In fact, the densification phenomenon of inorganic particles at low temperatures is not rare in nature. In geology, the diagenesis of sedimentary rock usually takes place at temperatures lower than 200 oC with the assistance of underground fluid and pressure. Inspired by the geological process, vaterite nanoparticles were compacted with additive water at room temperature to reach a relatively density of 87%20. More recently, great efforts have also been devoted to fabricating transparent ceramics by the cold-sintering process (CSP)21–29, in which the densification mechanisms are considered to be closely related to pressure solution creep in geology. However, the typical densification temperature around 1/3 Tm via CSP is still too harsh for most hybrid copper iodides12. Even though some rock-salt-type transparent ceramics with low Tm (such as NaCl and LiF) can be prepared by CSP at temperatures lower than 100 oC29,30, their hygroscopic nature has prevented the practical application as packaging materials.

Among diagenesis processes, we noticed that a typical low-temperature fluid-rock interaction process known as dolomitization can occur at temperatures below 150 oC, at which calcite is converted to dolomite through Mg2+/Ca2+ ion substitution31,32. Geological studies on dolomitization indicate that dolomite formation requires a sufficiently high supply of Mg2+ in underground fluids to overcome the strong Mg2+ solvation-related kinetic barriers33–36, underscoring the critical role of cation-rich fluids in fluid-mediated diagenetic processes. Moreover, although well-ordered dolomite formation is strongly kinetically hindered under near-ambient conditions, dolomitization can be greatly accelerated under hydrothermal conditions37–39. Therefore, we developed a dolomitization-inspired cold sintering process (D-CSP), in which a Sr2+-rich transient liquid phase increases the local chemical potential of Sr2+ and accelerates the precipitation process of cold sintering at relatively low temperatures.

It has been proved that the dolomitization phenomenon can hardly take place when the Mg2+/Ca2+ ratio is lower than 0.72, suggesting that the cations in the solution play a critical role in the low-temperature precipitation process40. Inspired by the dolomitization process, we propose that the precipitation can be greatly promoted by introducing extra cations. However, unlike the alien cation (Mg2+) required for dolomitization, the cation for D-CSP should be the same as that in the target ceramic to preserve the composition. Here, we take the SrF2 as a typical transparent ceramic to demonstrate the strategy of D-CSP, which is illustrated in Fig. 1b. Without adding Sr2+ in the transient liquid, the densification can be considered as classic CSP, in which mass transport is driven by the pressure-assisted dissolution28. However, when the dissolved Sr²⁺ and F⁻ diffuse from high-pressure region to low-pressure pores, the reprecipitation is blocked by the energy barrier from both the hydration shell and nucleation of solvated ions, leading to a sluggish precipitation in kinetics. In contrast, the precipitation could be greatly accelerated with Sr²+ rich liquid, simply because of the enhanced chemical reaction rate by increasing the concentration of reactant, see Eq. (2).

To validate the proposed sintering strategy, we densified the SrF2 nanoparticles (Supplementary Fig. 1) by using Sr(CH3COO)2 solution as the cation rich liquid. Under a constant pressure of 350 MPa, the relative density of SrF2 ceramic reached 99% at a sintering temperature as low as 150 oC, which is below the decomposition temperature for not only the blue-emitting [Cu2I2(tppp)2(bpp)] and yellow-emitting [Cu4I4(pph2Et)4] hybrids, but also the very vulnerable red-emitting [Cu2I2(tppp)2pz] hybrid cluster (Fig. 1c). More importantly, the SrF2 ceramic obtained by D-CSP exhibits high optical transmittance from 50% to 79% in visible light spectrum, which is even superior to the transparency of SrF2 ceramic prepared by hot-press sintering at 800 oC (Fig. 1d)19. Since the optical transmittance of ceramic is very sensitive to the scattering centers like residual pores and amorphous grain boundaries, the high transparency in obtained SrF2 ceramic reflects the high quality of densification achieved by D-CSP. Notably, the proposed strategy reduced the sintering temperature to the level of 1/4 Tm (where Tm = 1733 K for SrF2), which represents a practical advance in terms of fabricating transparent ceramics compared to both high-temperature sintering (HTS) and classic CSP (Fig. 1e).

The low sintering temperature achieved under 350 MPa pressure suggests a highly efficient densification process via adding a cation-rich solution. As shown in the binarization of TEM images (Fig. 2a), the compacted green body without heating (30 oC) has a relative density of around 73%. The residual pores are connected to form channels for mass transport by liquid phase, which is similar to the environment of dolomitization. Although these continuous pore channels decrease gradually with reduced porosity, the binarization image shows that the connected channels can be preserved until 150 oC, which is important for reaching high density at the final stage. Despite the low temperature, the grain boundary formed is homogeneous in composition (Supplementary Fig. 3) and highly crystallized, as revealed by atomic-scale high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) (Fig. 2b).

The high activity of cation-rich solution can be further understood through comparative studies using various liquid phases (Fig. 2c, d). While adding Sr(CH3COO)2 solution achieves near-theoretical density, dry-pressed powder and the bulk sintered with pure H2O only show relative density below 90% under the same conditions. Moreover, adding CH3COOH solution also exhibits a much inferior effect on densification with respect to Sr(CH3COO)2 solution. Since the acid nature of CH3COOH could facilitate the densification by promoting the dissolution process41, the key distinction is that the Sr(CH3COO)2 solution directly increases the chemical potential of Sr2+ near the particle interface around the neck region (Fig. 1b), which accelerates the interfacial precipitation step, whereas acetic acid mainly modulates dissolution without providing enough high cation enhancement. Therefore, this phenomenon clearly indicates precipitation as the rate-determining step in D-CSP. Analysis of shrinkage kinetics reveals that the D-CSP prepared bulk has a shrinkage peak at the lowest temperature compared with the other samples, suggesting enhanced densification activity by using a cation-rich solution. Calculating the activation energy of grain growth (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Figs. 6–12) shows a value of 30 kJ mol−1 for the D-CSP, which is not only substantially lower than that of SrF2 ceramic prepared by adding pure H2O (166 kJ mol−1)42, but also lower than the value (70 kJ mol−1) for SrF2 ceramic densified by adding CH3COOH solution. The reduction in activation energy of grain growth indicates a lowering of the precipitation barrier43, which facilitates faster SrF2 precipitation at particle interfaces around neck regions, leading to fully densified bulk at lower temperature. Therefore, the reduced activation energy of grain growth can be seen as circumstantial evidence that the precipitation process is the rate-limiting step in the densification. In contrast, although the presence of CH3COOH solution can accelerate the dissolution rate, it is yet much less effective compared to cation-rich Sr(CH3COO)2 solution because dissolution is not the rate-determining step in kinetics. In addition, cation-rich solutions were also applied to the preparation of transparent calcium fluoride, reducing the sintering temperature by 50 °C compared with that reported using HCl as the transient liquid phase (Supplementary Fig. 13), and were further extended to the fabrication of higher-refractive-index (Sr0.19Pb0.81)F2 transparent ceramics (Supplementary Fig. 14), further demonstrating the effectiveness of this strategy.

Due to the decreased energy barrier for crystallization, the grain boundaries in D-CSP prepared SrF2 are well crystallized with very little amorphous phase, which can be proved through broadband dielectric spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 4). The Nyquist plot analysis (Supplementary Table 1) shows that ceramic densified with Sr(CH3COO)2 solution exhibits grain boundary resistance (4.57 × 1010 Ω cm) orders of magnitude greater than samples densified with H2O (2.96 × 108 Ω cm) or CH3COOH (1.73 × 108 Ω cm). This high grain boundary resistance could be attributed to low defect density at grain boundaries and the absence of residual liquid that effectively blocks ionic migration. For the ceramics densified via conventional CSP, the pressure driven densification of inorganic particles could lead to highly defective grain boundaries or amorphous interphase due to the insufficient recrystallization, which is detrimental to the optical transmittance in the visible light spectrum44. Notably, FTIR shows no temperature-dependent change in acetate bands (Supplementary Fig. 5), which is consistent with acetate serving as a trace transient transport species rather than amorphous interphase. As revealed by Fig. 2b, the grain boundaries have a typical structure of terrace-ledge-kink (TLK) configurations with extensive low-energy terraces and atomic-scale ledges45, indicating that the densification is controlled by surface precipitation. With the addition of Sr(CH3COO)2 solution, the Sr2+ cations would be preferentially adsorbed on ledges and diffuse along these channels to complete the lattice growth once they encounter the dissolved F- anions, which is more efficient compared with the situation without excess cations.

However, one may argue that the anions could also promote the densification like cations, since chemical kinetics suggests a similar effect of excess anions, see Eq. (2). To further demonstrate the effect of cations on precipitation, we performed simulations for SrF2 in the environment of water molecules using density functional theory (DFT). Depending on the terminating atoms, the surface of SrF2 can exist in two possible configurations of a Sr-terminated surface or an F-terminated surface, respectively. It can be seen that the F- anions on the F-terminated surface gradually deviate from their lattice positions and eventually stabilize in an amorphous-like state. The unstable F- anions tend to diffuse through interstitial sites of Sr atoms until segregation to the Sr-terminated surface (Fig. 2f), which implies that introducing excess anions here could not promote the precipitation. In comparison, the simulations indicate that the Sr-terminated surface is the energetically favored configuration of SrF2 in an aqueous environment. If precipitation follows a TLK model (Fig. 2f), the rate-limiting step is typically the formation of a two-dimensional nucleus on the growth surface. Since the formation of a stable Sr-terminated two-dimensional nucleus is favored, the addition of Sr-rich solution could significantly promote the two-dimensional nucleation stage of {100} surface, leading to enhanced densification efficiency.

Hybrid copper iodide in transparent ceramic

Thanks to the low processing temperature, it is possible to incorporate the hybrid clusters into the transparent ceramic matrix for constructing a hybrid copper iodide in ceramic (HIC) phosphor converter. However, as a composite, the luminescent properties are restricted by the dispersion of phosphor in the matrix, which is especially difficult for hybrid clusters due to the limitation of mechanical mixing of soft powder into rigid particles. We found that the D-CSP process can promote the homogeneous dispersion of hybrid copper iodide while achieving densification. As revealed by 3D laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) (Fig. 3a), the hybrid [Cu4I4(pph2Et)4] shows a wide distribution of particle size in the compacted green body at room temperature, suggesting agglomerated hybrid clusters after mechanical mixing. With increasing temperature for D-CSP, the hybrid clusters became softer and melted at temperatures above 100 oC. Under uniaxial pressure during D-CSP, the liquid-phase hybrid clusters could be compressed into the pores and channels in the ceramic matrix, which largely improves the homogeneity of dispersion. After complete densification, it can be seen that the hybrid clusters penetrated throughout the ceramic matrix to form a continuous phase (Fig. 3a). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Fig. 3b) confirmed that an intimate interface between the hybrid cluster and SrF2 matrix has been formed with slight penetration of the hybrids into ceramic matrix.

The quantum efficiency first decreased continuously for the bulk samples prepared at 70 and 100 oC, which can be ascribed to the recrystallization phenomenon of glass-state hybrid [Cu4I4(pph2Et)4]46. What is more, the internal quantum efficiency (IQE) was largely restored to 82%, and the external quantum efficiency (EQE) was enhanced to 50% after complete densification at 150 oC (Fig. 3c). The recovery should be attributed to the glass transition that occurs under the influence of temperature and pressure, as well as the improved transparency of SrF2 matrix after densification, which is confirmed by the PL lifetime measurements (Supplementary Figs. 14–18). The continuous dispersion of hybrid copper iodide that can hardly be achieved by mechanical mixing greatly increases the absorption efficiency, leading to greatly recovered IQE and EQE in the phosphor converter. In addition, despite the influence in quantum efficiency, the D-CSP process did not change the PL spectra as long as the sintering temperature was not above 150 oC (Fig. 3d). However, when the temperature for D-CSP increased to 200 oC, all the luminescent properties deteriorated due to the irreversible thermal quenching of hybrid [Cu4I4(pph2Et)4], highlighting the advantage of low-temperature densification via D-CSP.

While the high luminescent properties of the hybrid cluster are preserved after the D-CSP process, the transparent ceramic matrix can effectively protect the performance of the vulnerable phosphor under complex environmental conditions. As indicated by the in-situ PL measurement (Fig. 3e), the HIC exhibits a 40% reduction in thermal quenching at 150 oC with respect to the unprotected hybrid cluster. Moreover, the intensity of the emitting peak of HIC can fully recover to the room-temperature state, suggesting that the ceramic matrix can effectively prevent the structural damage of hybrid copper iodide from heat and oxidation at elevated temperatures. Indeed, the bi-continuous structured HIC achieves a thermal conductivity of 2.5 W m−1 K−1 at 300 K (Fig. 3f), which is an order-of-magnitude over the pure hybrid [Cu4I4(pph2Et)4] (0.1 W m−1 K−1). In addition, the uniform continuous dispersion of hybrid copper iodide can facilitate the heat transfer through the intimate interface between the hybrid cluster and ceramic matrix. As a result, the HIC have significantly superior resistance to high temperature and humidity (85 oC, 85% RH), retaining ≈ 80% of its initial PL intensity after a damp-heat test for 100 h (Fig. 3g). In contrast, the pure hybrid cluster almost lost the luminescent function after 3 min under the identical conditions. Therefore, this bi-continuous structure formed by D-CSP is beneficial not only for the high quantum yield, but also the thermal and environmental stability for hybrid copper iodides.

Applications in luminescent devices

To further address the universality of this strategy, various hybrid copper iodides were incorporated into the SrF2 ceramic matrix via the D-CSP process. Exploiting systematic ligand engineering of the hybrid clusters by varying phosphine and nitrogen-donor ligands, we achieved precise emission control across 450–640 nm throughout the visible-light spectrum (Fig. 4a, b). In particular, the blue- [Cu2I2(tppp)2(bpp)] and red-emitting [Cu2I2(tppp)2pz] hybrid clusters were incorporated with the yellow hybrid [Cu4I4(pph2Et)4] to construct a white LED device (Supplementary Fig. 19). To meet the industrial standards for display backlight units47, large-area LED modules was fabricated by using a HIC phosphor convertor with a diameter of 26 mm (Fig. 4c), indicating the potential of scalability. As demonstrated in Fig. 4d, the white LEDs fabricated with these heterogeneously encapsulated emitters exhibit full-spectrum emission spanning from 380 to 750 nm, achieving a correlated color temperature of 3925 ± 150 K with a high color-rendering index (CRI) of 93 (Supplementary Fig. 21). Without the excitation by a blue chip, there is much less intensity in blue emission in the HIC-based phosphor converter, which is highly desirable as a lighting source in the sense of protecting human eyes. Benefiting from the high CRI, the photograph taken under the full-spectrum white light LED shows much vivid color compared with the one using commercial YAG:Ce as phosphor (Fig. 4e). To highlight the performance of the LED device, we compared the HIC-based white LED with reported advanced inorganic and hybrid phosphor systems. As shown in Fig. 4f, the white-LED in this work achieves a balanced combination of CRI and IQE, indicating that the D-CSP based packaging can meet the requirements of device-level integration without sacrificing the optical performance of hybrid clusters.

In addition, since many hybrid copper iodides are very promising scintillators48–50, we demonstrated the possibility of X-ray imaging by using the HIC with hybrid [Cu4I4(pph2Et)4]. It is observed that the HIC-based scintillator shows a typical yellow luminescence under X-ray irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 23a). To evaluate sensitivity under X-ray irradiation, the RL response curve was generated by plotting RL intensity as a function of radiation dose rate. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 23b, HIC shows a clear linear RL dependence on X-ray dose rate over 0.81-13.56 μGy s−1, and the detection limit is estimated to be 599.9 nGy s−1 at a signal-to-noise ratio of 3. The X-ray imaging by using a standard line-pair card (Fig. 4g) shows that the HIC ceramic (10 wt.% [Cu4I4(pph2Et)4], d = 0.5 mm) resolves fine features up to 17.6 line pairs (lp) per mm, demonstrating good imaging capability compared to reported organic-inorganic hybrid scintillating materials (Supplementary Table 2). More importantly, a comparative aging test was conducted for both the pristine hybrid CuI and the HIC ceramic (45 °C, 24 h) to assess environmental stability (Fig. 4h). It can be seen that pronounced image degradation is observed for the pristine hybrid CuI after aging, whereas the HIC retains good resolution after the same test. The modulation transfer function (MTF) determined by the slanted-edge method further provides quantitative results for this observation. Compared with pristine hybrid CuI (from 21.7 to 6.6 lp mm−1), the HIC (from 17.6 to 15.7 lp mm−1) exhibits very subtle reduction in MTF after aging (Fig. 4i). Therefore, although the scintillation properties of HIC are compromised somewhat with respect to the pristine hybrid CuI, the transparent SrF2 matrix improves their optical stability against the environment, thereby supporting reliable application of hybrid copper–iodides in high-resolution X-ray imaging devices.

Discussion

In conclusion, we demonstrated a dolomitization-inspired densification that effectively reduced the densification temperature of cold-sintered transparent SrF2 ceramics to 150 °C, which is compatible with most hybrid copper iodides as a robust matrix for luminescence. By mimicking the hydrothermal dolomitization process in nature, we introduced a cation-rich liquid phase into nanoparticles during the densification, which accelerated the rate-determining precipitation step under uniaxial pressure, thereby achieving near-theoretical density with high optical transmittance. The resulting composite phosphor convertor exhibits a continuous bi-continuous structure, where the hybrid copper iodide can be uniformly distributed in the transparent ceramic matrix without undermining the structure of thermally sensitive hybrid clusters. The continuous bi-continuous structure enables the efficient conversion of ultraviolet light or X-rays into visible light, thereby maintaining its luminescent properties. (Supplementary Fig. 24) Moreover, the ceramic matrix itself acts as protection that isolates the hybrid copper iodides from water, chemical solvents and oxygen in the environment. Finally, the high thermal conductivity nature of SrF2 with an intimate interface formed by D-CSP guarantees the effective heat dissipation in luminescent devices, which is difficult to be matched by other organic or glass matrices. Therefore, we believe this work offers a pathway for deploying environmentally vulnerable phosphors in a variety of photonic applications.

Methods

Materials

High-purity (98%) starting materials included Sr(NO3)2 (99%), KF·2H2O (98.5%), Sr(CH3COO)2 (99%), CuI (99.5%), ethyldiphenylphosphine (PPh2Et, C14H15P, 98%), triphenylphosphine (tpp, C18H15P, 99%), 1,3-bis(4-pyridyl)propane (bpp, C13H14N2, 97%), 3-picoline (3-pc, C6H7N, 99%), and pyrazine (pz, C4H4N2, 99%) were obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.Raw reagents.

Synthesis of SrF2 nanomaterials and D-CSP ceramics

Nanoscale SrF2 powder was synthesized via co-precipitation using Sr(NO3)2 and KF 2H2O precursors. Prepare a 0.5 M solution of Sr(NO3)2 and a 0.05 M aqueous solution of KF. Mix them slowly and evenly, and continue to stir for 2 h. After aging for 12 h, the precipitate was collected by centrifugation (8000 × g), washed five times with deionized water, and then dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 24 h to obtain strontium fluoride powder. Densification employed a stainless-steel die assembly (maximum 300 oC) within a heated press. For the preparation of SrF2 ceramic, 0.5 g powder was homogenized with 10 wt.% 1.5 M Sr(CH3COO)2 solution via mortar grinding (5 min). Wet mixtures underwent prepressing (80 MPa) followed by constant pressure (350 MPa) in the die. Thermal processing featured a 10 oC min−1 ramp to 150 oC with 60 min isothermal hold. Dense products were polished to disks of 10 mm diameter and 0.8 mm thickness.

Synthesis of hybrid copper iodides

The synthesis of Cu4I4(PPh2Et), Cu2I2(tpp)2(bpp) and Cu2I2(tpp)2(pz) was carried out according to reported methods.46,51 Specifically, CuI (1 mmol) and PPh2Et (1 mmol) in CH2Cl2 (2-4 mL) gave crystals via diethyl ether vapor diffusion; heating to 210 oC then rapid cooling formed a transparent [Cu4I4(pph2Et)4]. Separately, CuI (0.19 g, 1.0 mmol) in acetone (10 mL) with 3-pc (0.37 g, 4.0 mmol) stirred 10 min to give A. B was obtained by mixing A (0.15 g, 0.2 mmol) in acetone (10 mL) with tpp (0.13 g, 0.5 mmol) in toluene (2 mL). [Cu2I2(tppp)2(bpp)] came from B (0.11 g, 0.1 mmol) and excess bpp (0.10 g, 0.5 mmol) in toluene (10 mL) at 80 oC for 6 h, while [Cu2I2(tppp)2pz] from B (0.11 g, 0.1 mmol) with excess pz (0.04 g, 0.5 mmol) in toluene (10 mL) at 80 oC for 1 day.

Preparation of HIC

To prepare the HIC (hybrid CuI in SrF2 ceramic), 0.5 g of SrF2 powder and 10 wt.% of [Cu4I4(pph2Et)4] were weighed and mixed with 10 wt.% of 1.5 M strontium acetate solution in a mortar. And place the prepared powder into a steel mold with a diameter of 10 mm. Then, under a pressure of 350 MPa, heat the steel mold at a heating rate of 10 oC min−1 ramp to 150 oC, and keep it at this temperature for 1 h.

To construct a white LED device, the SrF2 powder was mixed with [Cu2I2(tppp)2(bpp)], [Cu2I2(tppp)2pz], and [Cu4I4(pph2Et)4] in a certain mass ratio evenly. Then add 10 wt.% of 1.5 mol L−1 Sr(CH3COO)2 solution, and the subsequent sintering process is the same as D-CSP ceramics.

To obtain an HIC that emits different colors of light, various hybrid copper iodides were incorporated into the SrF2 ceramic matrix via the D-CSP process.

Characterization methodology

Crystalline structures were determined via X-ray diffraction (Rigaku D/max-2500 PC, Cu-Kα₁ radiation λ = 1.54056 Å; 40 kV, 200 mA). Bulk sample densities were quantified using Archimedes’ principle. Microstructural analysis employed transmission electron microscopy (JEOL JEM-2100F). Optical transmission spectra (200–1400 nm) were acquired with a PerkinElmer Lambda 950 spectrophotometer. Phosphor spatial distributions within composites were mapped by laser confocal microscopy (Leica, TCS SP5 II). Photoluminescence properties, including excitation / emission spectra — were characterized using a Horiba Fluorolog-3 fluorimeter. Photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQYs) were measured with a Hamamatsu Quantaurus-QY spectrometer (Model C11347-11). The values of absolute quantum yield (AE) and internal quantum yield (IQE) were derived from the formula ηQE = IS/(ER − ES), where IS is the luminescence emission spectral area of the sample, ER denotes the excitation light spectral area of the null integrating sphere, and ES corresponds to that of the excitation light with the sample present, and EQE = AE × IQE. LED device performance was evaluated using an integrating sphere coupled to an HCPOO spectrograph system. The PL decay spectra were recorded using an FLS1000 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Edinburgh Instruments Ltd., UK).

Radioluminescence spectra

The spectrometer (QE Pro, Ocean Optics) to measure the RL spectra equipped with an X-ray tube (Moxtek, Silver target,12 W, and the tube voltage was 50 kV) and an integrating sphere.

Detection limits

The X-ray detection limit was derived from the linear relationship between the X-ray dose and the corresponding SNR (signal-to-noise ratio). Background noise levels were recorded in the absence of any sample. A dose yielding a signal-to-noise ratio of 3 was then taken as the detection limit.

X-ray imaging

X-ray imaging experiments were performed using a custom-built X-ray imaging system, which includes an X-ray source (Moxtek, Silver target,12 W), reflectors, and a commercial camera. The X-ray beam passes through the object, being converted into visible light by the scintillator. A CMOS camera (KnightCam S455, Canon) then captures an image of the object. The voltage/current of the X-ray tube was set to 50 kV/90 μA. To assess the spatial resolution of the imaging system, MTF measurements are utilized. The spatial resolution is determined by the spatial frequency corresponding to an MTF value of 0.2. The MTF curve was derived using the slanted-edge technique.
