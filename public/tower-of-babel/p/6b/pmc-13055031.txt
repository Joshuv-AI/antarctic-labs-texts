The impact of pedicle screw transitional segment and thread distribution on postoperative rod-screw system failure
Authors: Wen Peng, Weichao Wang, Jie Zhang, Yami Liu, Peiliang Yu, Haoling Huo, Jianzeng Ren, Zhongfa Mao, Xiaojian Wang, Yiguo Yan, Cheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study aimed to evaluate the differences in postoperative internal fixation system failure between two distinct pedicle screw designs, and to examine how variations in the transitional segment and thread distribution of these screws influence the bending mechanical properties of the rod-screw system.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 81 cases of thoracolumbar posterior internal fixation surgery from January 2020 to December 2023, focusing on comparing the postoperative fracture and loosening rates associated with the two pedicle screw designs. Finite element analysis was employed to assess the impact of different transitional segment and thread distribution designs of pedicle screws on stress distribution within the rod-screw system under physiological stress conditions in a T10-L2 fracture model. Dynamic and static bending performance tests were conducted on pedicle screws and rod-screw systems featuring various transition segment and thread distribution designs.ResultsThe clinical analysis revealed significant variations in postoperative fracture rates among rod-screw systems with different pedicle screw designs (p < 0.05), while the rates of loosening remained largely consistent postoperatively. Finite element analysis and mechanical testing demonstrated that extending the transitional segment can mitigate the maximum stress across the entire rod-screw system and enhance the uniformity of stress distribution. This modification improves the bending performance of the pedicle screw and its rod-screw system, shifting the failure point from the pedicle screw to the connecting rod. Conversely, variations in thread distribution design exhibited minimal impact on the stress distribution and bending performance of the entire rod-screw system.ConclusionPedicle screws with optimally extended transitional segments can effectively alleviate local stress concentrations and enhance the bending performance of the screw and its rod-screw system, thereby reducing the risk of postoperative screw fracture.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(4):383–396.Keywords: Thoracolumbar fracture, Pedicle screws, Postoperative complications, Biomechanics, internal fixation, Finite element analysis, mechanical properties, postoperative fractures, retrospective analysis, fatigue, t-tests, bone mineral density (BMD), thoracolumbar fractures

Introduction

In recent years, there has been a global increase in the prevalence of thoracolumbar disorders resulting from degenerative changes and accidental injuries, leading to a corresponding rise in the number of posterior thoracolumbar fusion fixation surgeries.1,2 This procedure involves nerve decompression and bone graft fusion, while preserving nerve function, to restore the spine’s normal structure and function. Subsequently, the pedicle screw-rod fixation system is used to restore spinal stability.3,4 This system employs a three-column fixation technique on vertebral bodies to re-establish the biomechanical stability of the thoracolumbar structure, facilitating fracture healing or intervertebral fusion.5 Despite the widespread adoption of the pedicle screw-rod fixation system due to its advantages, the incidence of postoperative complications arising from screw-rod system failures necessitates urgent technological advancements. The literature indicates that screw-rod system failures manifest in various forms, such as loosening, back-out, and fracture, with an incidence rate ranging from 15% to 60%.6-8

In clinical work, the research team found that one of the pedicle screw products (Group A) had multiple postoperative complications of screw breakage in the short term, while the other pedicle screw product (Group B) did not have similar instances. Through the analysis of the design characteristics of the two products, it was initially found that both screw products conformed to the same design of thread form, depth, and pitch. However, notable differences were observed in the transitional segment and thread distribution, and Group A and Group B (Figures 1a and 1b) had transitional segment lengths of 5 mm and 16.5 mm, respectively. According to relevant references, when the transitional segment length is equivalent to the screw’s total length, the screws take on conical shapes, and this conical screw design can enhance the mechanical properties of the screw.9-11 In addition, Group A screws are characterized by cortical bone threads designed for engagement with high-density bone, such as the pedicle. Existing literature, through pull-out resistance tests on cadaver specimens, suggests that cortical bone threads enhance pull-out resistance and fixation strength.12,13 However, the impact of these design parameters on the overall performance and clinical outcomes of the screw-rod system remains ambiguous.

Therefore, to clarify the underlying causes of pedicle screw fracture following thoracolumbar surgery, this study conducted a retrospective analysis of clinical data from surgeries employing two distinct screw types (Groups A and B). The study thoroughly evaluated and analyzed factors such as fracture and loosening, which contribute to screw-rod system failure in postoperative patients. Subsequent to this, in vivo finite element analysis was employed to assess screws and their screw-rod systems with varying designs within a thoracolumbar fracture model. The primary objective was to identify potential causes for stress alterations in the screws following the implantation of the screw-rod system. Subsequently, both dynamic and static bending resistance performance analyses were performed on the screws and their screw-rod systems with diverse designs.

Methods

Clinical retrospective study

The study cohort consisted of patients who underwent thoracolumbar posterior internal fixation surgeries using two distinct types of pedicle screws, categorized as Group A and Group B, from January 2020 to December 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) a diagnosis of thoracolumbar degenerative diseases or fractures; 2) the surgical intervention consisted of thoracolumbar posterior open fusion fixation or fixation surgery; 3) a postoperative follow-up period of at least one year, or the detection of screw fracture or loosening within one year postoperatively; and 4) patient age of 18 years or older. The exclusion criteria consisted of: 1) a definitive diagnosis of spinal deformities or space-occupying lesions, such as tumours; 2) pre-existing severe spinal cord dysfunction prior to surgery; and 3) secondary or multiple revision surgeries.

In this study, patient demographics data were collected including age, sex, disease types, BMI, and bone mineral density (BMD) (BMD was assessed only for individuals aged over 50 years, with a T-score of 0 assumed for those under 50 years). Additional data on surgical methods, spinal segments, and screw selection were also gathered. Clinical efficacy was evaluated using the visual analogue scale (VAS)14 for pain and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI)15 at three timepoints: preoperative, three months postoperatively, and at final follow-up. To assess internal fixation failure, radiographs were employed for qualitative evaluation of screw-rod fracture, while CT scans were used for quantitative measurement of the screw’s apex vertebral body’s anterior cortex mean gap (SAAC) and the screw’s apex vertebral body’s superior endplate mean gap (SASE), as detailed in Supplementary Figure a.16

In vivo finite element analysis

To account for the variations in the transitional segment and thread distribution between Group A and Group B, two design variables were defined based on common pedicle screw specs in thoracolumbar posterior surgeries (45 mm length, 6.5 mm thread diameter). The transitional segment is defined as the length of the transitional area at the junction between the screw head and the screw tail, with lengths set at 5 mm, 15 mm, 25 mm, and 35 mm. The thread distribution was categorized into cancellous bone thread (Cath) and cortical bone thread (Coth), with the Coth characterized by a length of 13 mm and a thread pitch that is half that of the Cath. The specific designs are illustrated in Figure 1c.

A 26-year-old male volunteer underwent thoracolumbar thin-layer CT scanning. The CT data were imported into Mimics 20.0 (Materialise, Belgium) for the segmentation and extraction of 3D contour models of the T10-L2 vertebrae. The images were further optimized using Geomagic Studio 12 (3D Systems, USA). Subsequently, SolidWorks 2015 (Dassault Systèmes, France) was used to reconstruct the intervertebral discs and facet joint surfaces (Figure 2a). ANSYS Workbench 18.0 (ANSYS, USA) was used to establish seven ligament structure models with Link180 rod elements. A frictional contact with a friction coefficient of 0.1 was defined between the articular surfaces of the spine model, while all other components were set to bonded contact. Finally, we assigned properties to all materials in the model (Supplementary Table i).

Hexahedral elements were used for modelling the discs and joints, while tetrahedral elements were employed for other structures. Workbench 18.0 was used to set multigradient mesh sizes, determine the mesh convergence with a stress change within 5%, and finally confirm that the unit size was set to 1 mm (Figure 2b, Supplementary Table ii). To verify the validity of the finite element analysis model established in this study, a fixed constraint was applied on L2’s lower surface, and an axial compressive load of 400 N and a torque force of 7.5 N·m in the X, Y, and Z axes were applied on T10’s upper surface to simulate flexion-extension, lateral flexion, and rotation. The range of motion of each segment in the finite element model under these six states was calculated and compared with previous studies.17,18 Results showed that the model’s verification results were within a reasonable range, indicating the model’s validity (Figure 2c).

To assess the ultimate stress experienced by pedicle screws following implantation, this study examined the reduction and internal fixation of thoracolumbar fractures. A finite element model of the T10-L2 thoracolumbar spine was employed. A line was drawn parallel to the upper endplate from a point located 15% along the anteroposterior axis of T12. Subsequently, a diagonal line extended from this intersection with the posterior edge to a point at 55% along the anterior upper edge. The region between these lines was resected in a wedge shape to simulate a fracture model.19,20 Spinal pedicle screws were inserted into T11 and L1, aligned parallel to the upper endplate and reaching a depth of 80%. These were connected using a 6.0 mm diameter rod and screw plugs to achieve rigid fixation, thereby creating a T12 fracture screw-rod internal fixation system model (Figures 2d and 2e).

Based on the screw design schemes described in Figure 1c, the spinal pedicle screw-rod system model was modified into eight distinct groups: Cath 5, 15, 25, 35, and Coth 5, 15, 25, 35. A fixed constraint was applied on L2’s lower surface, and an axial compressive load of 400 N and a torque force of 7.5 N·m in the X, Y, and Z axes were applied on T10’s upper surface to simulate flexion-extension, lateral flexion, and rotation (Figure 2f). The range of motion for each group’s finite element model was calculated under six conditions and compared to the normal model’s range of motion to evaluate the impact of screw design schemes on the stability of the T12 fracture model. Additionally, the stresses on the spinal pedicle screws and connecting rods were calculated for each group under six working conditions. Peak stress values and their locations were analyzed to assess the influence of screw design schemes on the stress distribution within the screw-rod system.

Evaluation of bending resistance performance

Based on the pedicle screw design schemes outlined in Section 2.2.1, a qualified medical device company specializing in the pedicle screw-rod fixation system was selected. Pedicle screws with various designs, as well as their connecting rods and screw plugs, were manufactured using TC4 titanium alloy. Concurrently, quality control measures were implemented in accordance with the medical device production quality management system.

A stress model for pedicle screws was developed in accordance with the ASTM F1717 standard, followed by an assessment of the screws’ static and dynamic mechanical performance.21 Static compression bending tests were executed utilizing a microcomputer-controlled electronic universal testing machine (E45.105; MTS Systems, USA) (Figure 2g). The loading rate was maintained at 5 mm/min, and the tests were concluded upon either the failure of the sample or the detection of a 50 mm displacement of the jig. From the load-displacement curve, parameters such as bending stiffness, ultimate strength, and fracture displacement of the screws were derived. Dynamic compression bending tests were conducted using a fatigue testing machine (Electroforce 3510; TA Instruments, USA) (Figure 2h). To optimize testing duration and costs, and informed by prior testing experience and preliminary results, the fatigue performance of the pedicle screws was evaluated through cycle counts under a fixed load. Sinusoidal cyclic loading was applied using a load-controlled method, with a load ratio of 0.1 and a load magnitude of 600 N. The frequency of the fatigue tests was set at 5 Hz, with testing terminating upon specimen fracture or upon reaching one million cycles.

A stress model of the pedicle screw-rod system was developed in accordance with the ASTM F1798 standard, followed by an assessment of its static and dynamic mechanical performance.22 Static compression-bending tests were conducted using a microcomputer-controlled electronic universal testing machine (E45.105) (Figure 2i). The loading rate was maintained at 5 mm/min, and the tests were concluded upon either fracture of the sample or a fixture descent of 50 mm. From the load-displacement curve, the bending stiffness, ultimate strength, and fracture displacement of the screws were evaluated. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were applied to summarize all statistical data in this study. For normally distributed data, the mean (SD) was calculated; otherwise, frequencies and percentages were reported. For categorical data, the chi-squared test was employed when sample size requirements were met, and Fisher’s exact test was used for small samples or when expected frequencies were low. For continuous data, appropriate t-tests were selected based on the comparison type for normally distributed data: the independent-samples t-test was employed for inter-group comparisons, whereas the paired t-test was utilized for intra-group comparisons. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann-Whitney U test was adopted. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were done with SPSS 19.0 (IBM, USA).

Results

Evaluation of clinical efficacy

Following screening and analysis, 81 cases were included in the study, with 35 cases (186 screws) in Group A and 46 cases (246 screws) in Group B. Clinical follow-up data are presented in Table I. A comparative analysis of patient demographics data revealed no significant differences between the two groups for variables such as age, sex, BMI, bone density, and disease types. Additionally, there were no significant differences in treatment plans, surgical segments, and screw models and specifications. Analysis of VAS and ODI scores preoperatively, three months postoperatively, and at the final follow-up indicated that postoperative symptoms in both groups improved significantly compared to preoperative symptoms, with no statistically significant differences between the two groups. Overall, both pedicle screw-rod systems demonstrated effectiveness in treatment, with no significant differences in surgical efficacy.

In vivo finite element analysis

In the analysis of the clinical research, it was observed that Group A uniaxial screws exhibited a higher likelihood of screw fracture with thoracolumbar fractures and fixation surgery. The possible reason could be the differences in the design of the screw transitional segment and thread distribution between Groups A and B. Consequently, this study employs a T10-L2 thoracolumbar finite element model to conduct a stress analysis on screw-rod configurations with varying design parameters following a T12 fracture. When comparing the range of motion between the intact T10-L2 model and the fixed thoracolumbar fracture model, it was found that the range of motion for each group’s models was reduced compared to the intact model under various conditions, with no significant differences observed between the groups (Figure 4a). These findings indicate that pedicle screw-rod internal fixation systems with differing designs satisfy the initial stability requirements in clinical procedures, thereby supporting that the two pedicle screw design schemes in Groups A and B yield essentially equivalent postoperative clinical outcomes.

Overall segmental range of motion (ROM) and stress conditions in an in vivo finite element analysis of a screw-rod system. a) T10-L2 segmental range of motion of each group of models. b) Typical stress distribution in screws. c) Comparison of stress peaks in screws across groups. d) Typical stress distribution in connecting rods. e) Comparison of stress peaks in connecting rods across groups.

The screw stress distribution cloud diagram indicates that across all groups and conditions (Supplementary Figures b and c), stress concentration consistently occurs between the first and fourth threads of the screw head in various segments (Figure 4b), mirroring the fracture locations observed in clinical follow-ups. Alterations to the screw’s transitional segment and thread distribution do not affect the area of stress concentration. Examination of stress peaks across eight different screw types reveals that increasing the length of the transitional segment reduces the peak stress experienced by the screw (Figure 4c) and ameliorates the stress concentration pattern. However, a comparison of Coth and Cath screws through finite element analysis demonstrates that, although the stress peak in Coth screws is marginally lower, the overall trend is not significantly pronounced. This indicates that the thread distribution of the pedicle screw scarcely has any influence on the stress distribution of the screw within the screw-rod system.

The stress distribution cloud diagram for the connecting rod (Supplementary Figures d and e) indicates that, unlike pedicle screws, the connecting rod does not exhibit significant stress concentration (Figure 4d). An examination of the stress peaks in the connecting rod (Figure 4e) reveals no significant variation trend across different groups. However, during forward flexion, the stress peak of the connecting rod increases with the elongation of the transitional segment. There is a notable increase between 5 mm and 15 mm, with less pronounced changes observed between 15 mm and 35 mm. Furthermore, a comparison of the stress distribution and peaks between Coth and Cath screws shows negligible differences, indicating that the thread distribution of pedicle screws does not significantly impact the stress distribution of the rod within the screw-rod system.

Evaluation of bending resistance performance

The results from the pedicle screw bending tests (Figures 5a and 5b) indicated that an increase in the length of the transitional segment can increase the screw’s stiffness (Figure 5c), yield load (Figure 5d), ultimate load (Figure 5e), and fatigue cycle (Figure 5f). Specifically, as the transitional segment length increased from 5 mm to 25 mm, there was a linear increase in stiffness, yield load, and fatigue cycle count. However, at a length of 35 mm, no significant improvement in stiffness and yield load was observed compared to the 25 mm length, with both achieving maximum fatigue cycles. The study determined that the variations in the Coth and Cath screw designs did not have significant differences in terms of screw stiffness (Figure 5c), yield load (Figure 5d), ultimate load (Figure 5e), and fatigue cycle (Figure 5f). An analysis of screw failure modes (Figures 5g and 5h) revealed that screws with 5 mm and 15 mm transitional segments fractured during dynamic and static tests, whereas those with 25 mm and 35 mm segments exhibited bending in static tests and no deformation in dynamic tests. However, there is no difference in the screw failure modes among different thread distributions.

The analysis of inter-group differences in the static bending tests of screw-rod systems (Figures 6a and 6b) revealed that variations in the transitional segment and thread distribution did not result in significant differences in stiffness (Figure 6c), yield load (Figure 6d), or ultimate load (Figure 6e). This lack of significant variation may be attributed to the bearing mode and failure mechanism observed under static bending conditions (Figure 6f). The failure mechanism is predominantly characterized by the bending deformation of the connecting rod, while the deformation of the screws embedded in the test block remains relatively minimal. The possible reason is that in the screw-rod system, the static bending load is predominantly supported by the connecting rod.

In dynamic bending tests, the analysis of the dynamic fatigue cycles (Figure 6g) revealed that the 5 mm transitional segment system exhibited the poorest bending resistance performance, with screw fracture identified as the primary failure mode (Figure 6h), similar to the cases observed in group A. Extending the transitional segment to 15 mm resulted in an increase in fatigue cycles and a shift in the failure mode to connecting rod fracture. Further extensions to 25 mm and 35 mm did not significantly enhance the fatigue cycles or alter the failure mode.

Discussion

Clinical follow-up in this study revealed that the patients with thoracolumbar fractures and those undergoing fixation surgeries are particularly prone to screw fracture and loosening, corroborating existing literature.25 Further analysis showed that in Group A, the overall incidence of screw fracture was 11.43% (4/35). The screw fracture rate reached 50.00% (4/8) in patients with thoracolumbar fractures and 44.44% (4/9) in patients undergoing internal fixation surgery. The rate of fracture of this pedicle screw in thoracolumbar fractures and internal fixation surgery is significantly higher than the reported occurrence rates in similar studies.6,26 This phenomenon may be attributed to the damage inflicted on support structures by thoracolumbar fractures and the inadequate restoration of mechanical stability during surgery, which places additional stress on the posterior column pedicle screw-rod system, thereby increasing the risk of failure.26,27 Meanwhile, this study found that all six fractured screws were uniaxial screws, and the fracture positions occurred at the screw heads. Meanwhile, all the loosened screws were also uniaxial screws. The primary failure mode of multiaxial screws under stress is ball-head rotation, rather than fracture or loosening, whereas uniaxial screws, which lack rotational capability, predominantly fail through fracture or loosening.28,29 Furthermore, uniaxial and multiaxial screws serve distinct functions. The team primarily employed uniaxial screws for distraction, compression, and fixation of surgical segments, while multiaxial screws were mainly used for fixation. Consequently, uniaxial screws are subjected to greater stress post-implantation, thereby increasing the risks of fracture and loosening.

Further in vivo stress analysis of the pedicle screws and connecting rods demonstrated that the primary factor influencing stress variations in screw-rod system design schemes is the modification of the pedicle screw’s transitional segment, rather than differences in thread distribution. In the finite element stress analysis, extending the transitional segment from 5 mm to 15 mm increased the diameter at the screw head, thereby improving local load-bearing capacity and significantly reducing screw stress.10 This observation partially accounts for the lower incidence of postoperative screw fracture in Group B, which used 16.5 mm transitional segment screws, compared to Group A’s 5 mm transitional segment screws. However, it was also found that when the transitional segment is further extended from 15 mm to 25 mm and 35 mm, both the decreasing trend of screw stress and the increasing trend of connecting rod stress become insignificant. This finding suggests that the stress distribution of the entire screw-rod system tends to stabilize once the transitional segment of the pedicle screw reaches 15 mm, and a further increase in the length of the transitional segment will not substantially optimize the stress distribution trend of the screw-rod system.

Further bending performance tests of the screws confirmed that the screw transitional segment can significantly change the dynamic and static bending resistant performance of pedicle screws, but the thread distribution has no impact, which further verifies the results obtained in the finite element analysis. Moreover, as the transitional segment lengthened, the improvement in screw bending resistance also exhibits an inflection point. In the finite element analysis, this inflection point occurred at 15 mm, whereas in the screw bending test it was observed at 25 mm. This discrepancy is because the screw bending test only evaluates the bending resistance of a single screw under local axial loading at the screw tail, while finite element analysis mainly reflects the overall stress state of the screw-rod system. On this basis, combined with the bending performance tests of the screw-rod system, it was found that modifying the transitional segment to 15 mm can transfer the weakest point of the system from the screw to the connecting rod, thereby changing the failure mode and improving the performance of the screw-rod systems. This shift in failure mode is clinically significant, as the removal of a connecting rod post-fatigue fracture is more straightforward than that of a screw.11 In addition, a comprehensive analysis of the influence of Coth and Cath on dynamic bending resistance performance revealed that both types of screws exhibited comparable fatigue performance and failure mode across all transitional segments. However, at lengths of 25 mm and 35 mm, Cath screws demonstrated a higher number of cycles, potentially attributable to variations in stress distribution. Theoretically, a reduction in screw strength could lead to increased stress on the pedicle screw, decreased stress on the connecting rod, and an enhancement in the system’s fatigue performance. Consequently, when the screw strength surpasses the threshold that alters the system’s failure mode, it is advisable to reduce the length of the transitional segments to decrease screw strength and thereby improve the overall fatigue life of the system.

The clinical follow-up in this study was a single-centre study, with all cases recruited from the same medical centre, which may be potentially influenced by the surgical plans and operative techniques of the attending surgeons. Meanwhile, the simplifying assumptions of the T10-L2 fracture finite element model in this study may also limit the generalizability of the results to a certain extent. Future multicentre, large-sample clinical studies, combined with more comprehensive biomechanical evaluations, are needed to provide more reliable evidence for the optimal design and precise clinical application of pedicle screws.

In conclusion, extending the transitional segment of the screw in a judicious manner can mitigate stress concentration within the screw-rod system, thereby enhancing the system’s overall bending resistance and reducing the likelihood of postoperative fracture of the screw-rod system. Furthermore, when the screw’s performance is augmented beyond a specific threshold, the failure mode of the screw-rod system may transition from screw fracture to rod fracture.
