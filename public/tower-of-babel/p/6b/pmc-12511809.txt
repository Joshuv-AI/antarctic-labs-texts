Effects of Roflumilast on Exacerbations and Lung Function in COPD Patients with Bronchiectasis
Authors: Jae-Kyeong Lee, Chang-Seok Yoon, Bo-Gun Kho, Tae-Ok Kim, Hong-Joon Shin, Yong-Soo Kwon, Yu-Il Kim, Sung-Chul Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRoflumilast, a phosphodiesterase 4 (PDE4) inhibitor, reduces neutrophilic airway inflammation and exacerbations in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). However, the effect of coexisting bronchiectasis on its efficacy remains unclear. This study evaluates the long-term impact of roflumilast use in this population. A retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from 2,181 COPD patients. Of them, 180 had bronchiectasis confirmed on CT, with 162 not using roflumilast and 18 receiving it. Baseline data, including demographics, symptom scores, pulmonary function, and biomarkers, were collected. Patients were followed for one year to assess exacerbations and mortality, and survivors were monitored for up to three years for symptom and pulmonary function changes. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to determine predictors of exacerbations. Moderate and severe exacerbations occurred more frequently in the roflumilast group, but mortality did not differ between groups. Roflumilast users showed significant improvements in post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) (p=0.028), as well as in the ratio of forced expiratory volume in one second to the forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC) ratio (p=0.015) compared to no roflumilast group. In multivariate analysis, roflumilast use was independently associated with a higher exacerbation risk (p=0.004, OR=14.643, 95% CI: 2.36–90.848). While roflumilast improved pulmonary function and symptoms in COPD patients with bronchiectasis, it was also linked to a higher risk of exacerbations. These findings suggest caution in its use for this population, and further randomized controlled trials are needed to assess its safety and efficacy.Keywords: Phosphodiesterase 4 Inhibitors, Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease, Bronchiectasis

INTRODUCTION

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive airway disorder characterized by persistent airflow limitation and chronic inflammation.1 Acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) are significant clinical events associated with accelerated lung function decline, impaired quality of life, and increased healthcare utilization.2 Among the pharmacologic interventions targeting exacerbation prevention, roflumilast—a selective phosphodiesterase-4 (PDE4) inhibitor—has been shown to reduce neutrophilic airway inflammation and lower the frequency of moderate to severe exacerbations in patients with severe COPD, particularly those with chronic bronchitis and a history of frequent exacerbations.3

Bronchiectasis, a condition marked by permanent dilation of bronchi and chronic airway infection, often coexists with COPD, a phenotype sometimes referred to as bronchiectasis-COPD overlap syndrome (BCOS).4 This overlap is increasingly recognized in clinical settings and is associated with more severe symptoms, increased bacterial colonization, heightened systemic inflammation, and more frequent exacerbations compared to COPD alone.5 The management of patients with this overlapping phenotype remains a therapeutic challenge due to its complex pathophysiology and limited evidence-based guidance.

Given that roflumilast’s anti-inflammatory effects are particularly beneficial in neutrophil-driven airway diseases, it is plausible that patients with coexisting bronchiectasis may also derive clinical benefit. However, the efficacy and safety of roflumilast in this subpopulation remain unclear, as most clinical trials have excluded patients with significant bronchiectasis.6

This study aims to evaluate the long-term impact of roflumilast on clinical outcomes, including pulmonary function, symptom control, and exacerbation frequency, in COPD patients with radiologically confirmed bronchiectasis. By investigating this unique population, we seek to clarify whether roflumilast offers a net benefit or poses additional risks in the management of COPD complicated by bronchiectasis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study design and data source

This study was a retrospective cohort analysis based on data from the Korea COPD Subgroup Study (KOCOSS), a nationwide prospective cohort that includes patients from 54 tertiary hospitals across South Korea.7 The KOCOSS cohort is the largest COPD registry in Korea and is widely used to characterize the clinical features of Korean COPD patients. We utilized the publicly available first-phase dataset comprising 2,181 patients, released in September 2020. The database contains comprehensive longitudinal clinical data, including demographics, symptoms, spirometry, biomarkers, and outcomes. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chonnam National University Hospital (approved No. 2021–449). Informed consent was waived by Chonnam National University Hospital’s Institutional Review Board because of the study’s minimal risk and retrospective design.

2. Study population

Eligible participants were adults aged 40 years or older who were enrolled in the KOCOSS cohort by the end of 2019 and had spirometrically confirmed COPD, defined by a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio of less than 0.7. Patients were excluded if they did not provide written informed consent, were unable to reliably perform pulmonary function tests, had difficulty attending 6-month follow-up visits, were pregnant or planning to become pregnant during the five-year study period, or had other significant respiratory or systemic comorbidities interfering with study participation. Individuals who failed screening or dropped out during follow-up were also excluded.

3. Data collection

Baseline data included demographic information (age, sex, body mass index, smoking history), symptom scores using the COPD Assessment Test (CAT) and modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scale, St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) scores, spirometric indices (FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC ratio), and biomarkers such as blood eosinophil count and C-reactive protein (CRP). These measurements were collected at the time of enrollment.

4. Follow-up and outcomes

All patients were followed for one year to evaluate the incidence of moderate and severe exacerbations and all-cause mortality. Survivors were further observed for up to three years to monitor longitudinal changes in symptom scores and pulmonary function parameters.

5. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviation and compared using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of moderate-to-severe exacerbations. Covariates included age, sex, baseline pulmonary function, and use of roflumilast. Results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p-value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 18 software.

RESULTS

1. Study flow and participant selection

We reviewed data on 2,181 patients with COPD enrolled in the KOCOSS cohort. After excluding patients without radiologically confirmed bronchiectasis (based on chest X-ray or CT), 180 patients were included in the final analysis. Of these, 18 patients received roflumilast therapy and 162 did not (Fig. 1).

Patients were excluded if they did not sign the informed consent form, had significant difficulty performing pulmonary function tests, could not attend six-month follow-ups, were pregnant or planning pregnancy, or failed screening or dropped out. From the eligible pool, only patients with bronchiectasis on chest X-ray or CT were enrolled. Finally, 180 patients were included in the study, with 162 not receiving roflumilast and 18 receiving roflumilast.

2. Baseline characteristics

There were no statistically significant differences in baseline demographics or comorbidities between the roflumilast and non-roflumilast groups. The mean age was 71.4±7.1 years in the roflumilast group and 68.8±7.4 years in the control group (p=0.157). Both groups were predominantly male and had a similar smoking history. Prevalence of heart disease, diabetes, asthma, tuberculosis, and other conditions showed no significant intergroup differences.

In pulmonary function, the roflumilast group had a significantly lower FEV1/FVC ratio compared to the control group (43.2% vs. 52.3%, p=0.001), while absolute FEV1 and FVC values were comparable. Symptom scores including mMRC, CAT, and SGRQ were also similar (Table 1).

3. Medication use and laboratory findings

The use of inhaled medications such as inhaled corticosteroid (ICS), long-acting β2-agonist (LABA), long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA), and combination therapies showed no significant difference between groups. Likewise, inflammatory markers including white blood cell (WBC) counts, eosinophil counts, and high sensitivity C reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels were comparable. Imaging findings including emphysema and TB-related lung destruction were not significantly different between groups (Table 2).

4. Exacerbation and mortality outcomes

At 1-year follow-up, the roflumilast group exhibited a significantly higher rate of moderate exacerbations (69.2% vs. 31.3%, p=0.007) and greater frequency (2.0±2.7 vs. 0.68±1.3, p=0.008). Severe exacerbations were more common in the roflumilast group (30.7% vs. 10.7%), though the p-value was marginal (p=0.066), and their frequency was significantly higher (0.62±1.19 vs. 0.12±0.35, p=0.030). No significant difference in all-cause mortality was observed (5.6% vs. 2.5%, p=0.413) (Table 2).

5. Changes in symptoms and pulmonary function

During follow-up, patients in the roflumilast group showed consistently greater improvement in CAT scores at 1, 2, and 3 years (all p<0.05). Additionally, by year 3, the roflumilast group had significant improvements in FEV1 (ΔFEV1: +0.3 L vs. −0.2 L, p=0.031) and FEV1/FVC ratio (Δ: +17.1% vs. −1.0%, p=0.001), whereas SGRQ scores showed no significant differences (Table 3).

6. Predictors of exacerbation

Multivariable logistic regression identified roflumilast use as a significant independent predictor of moderate-to-severe exacerbations (OR=14.643, 95% CI: 2.360–90.848, p=0.004). In univariable analysis, other significant predictors included lower FEV1 (p=0.008), lower FEV1/FVC (p=0.016), LAMA use (p=0.008), and elevated SGRQ score (p=0.005). Protective effects were seen with LABA+LAMA combination therapy (p=0.007). Higher WBC (p=0.001), hs-CRP (p=0.026), and presence of TB-destroyed lung on CT (p=0.003) were also associated with higher exacerbation risk in univariable analysis (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective cohort study using data from the KOCOSS database, we evaluated the long-term effects of roflumilast in COPD patients with radiologically confirmed bronchiectasis. While roflumilast was associated with improvements in pulmonary function and symptom scores, its use was paradoxically linked to an increased risk of moderate-to-severe exacerbations in this unique phenotype of COPD. These findings raise important considerations regarding the risk-benefit balance of PDE4 inhibition in patients with bronchiectasis-COPD overlap syndrome (BCOS).

Previous randomized trials and real-world studies have established roflumilast’s role in reducing exacerbations among patients with severe COPD and chronic bronchitis, particularly those with a history of frequent exacerbations and elevated neutrophilic inflammation.8,9,10 However, our data contrast with these findings, as roflumilast was independently associated with higher exacerbation rates in BCOS. This may be partly explained by differences in pathophysiology; bronchiectasis is often dominated by chronic infection, bacterial colonization, and mucus hypersecretion, creating an inflammatory milieu that may not fully respond to PDE4 inhibition alone.11,12,13

Our findings align with a pilot RCT by Juthong and colleagues, which found no significant reduction in exacerbation rates or quality-of-life improvements with roflumilast use in bronchiectasis patients, although a non-significant trend toward improved FEV1 was observed.6 In addition, real-world data raise additional concerns regarding roflumilast’s safety profile. A large observational study found that roflumilast use was associated with a significantly increased 2-year risk of hospitalization for pneumonia and severe COPD exacerbations requiring admission. 14 Supporting these concerns, preclinical studies in mice have demonstrated that roflumilast impairs host defense against Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a common pathogen in severe COPD, by increasing bacterial load and mortality, reducing neutrophil presence in the airways, and suppressing key pro-inflammatory cytokines. These findings suggest that roflumilast may compromise airway immune responses in the context of chronic bacterial infection. 15

The significant improvement in post-bronchodilator FEV1 and CAT scores observed in our roflumilast group supports its potential benefit for lung function and symptom control. These findings echo prior reports that PDE4 inhibition may enhance airway smooth muscle relaxation and reduce mucosal edema.16,17 Yet, the functional improvements did not translate into reduced exacerbations, raising the question of whether improved spirometry correlates with clinical outcomes in this population.6

Our study also brings attention to the limitations of extrapolating COPD treatment guidelines to BCOS patients. Most pivotal roflumilast trials, including the REACT and RATIO studies, excluded patients with major other lung disease such as significant bronchiectasis.18,19 Thus, our results fill an important gap by focusing specifically on this excluded subpopulation.

There are several limitations to our study. First, the small number of patients receiving roflumilast (n=18) and the limited number of exacerbation events in this group reduce the statistical power, increase the risk of random variation, and limit the generalizability of our findings. The small event count also raises the possibility that the observed association between roflumilast use and increased exacerbation risk may be partly attributable to chance findings rather than a true causal effect.

Second, reverse causality may have influenced the results, as patients with a higher baseline risk of exacerbation were more likely to receive roflumilast. In our study, the roflumilast group had a numerically lower mean FEV1 and a significantly lower FEV1/FVC ratio compared with the non-roflumilast group, suggesting more advanced airflow limitation at baseline. Furthermore, roflumilast was prescribed in accordance with the GOLD guideline—typically in patients with an FEV1 <50% predicted and a history of frequent exacerbations despite optimal inhaled therapy—but the decision to initiate treatment was made at the discretion of the treating physician rather than through a standardized protocol. This real-world prescribing approach may have introduced confounding by indication, whereby clinicians preferentially selected higher-risk patients for treatment, potentially contributing to the higher exacerbation rates observed in the roflumilast group.

Third, as a retrospective analysis, unmeasured confounding factors such as microbiological profile, extent and severity of bronchiectasis, and treatment adherence could have influenced the results. The KOCOSS database does not include sputum culture results or radiologic severity scoring systems, both of which are known to be relevant to prognosis in bronchiectasis.

Nevertheless, in the multiple logistic regression analysis, after adjusting for baseline lung function and other significant covariates, roflumilast use remained a statistically significant independent predictor of moderate-to-severe exacerbations. This suggests that the association may not be entirely explained by baseline disease severity or prescribing bias alone, and may represent a genuine risk signal that warrants further investigation. These findings indicate that roflumilast should not be presumed universally effective in COPD phenotypes with structural airway disease, and underscore the need for individualized therapy based on phenotypic characterization, possibly combining anti-inflammatory agents with antibacterial or mucolytic therapies in BCOS. While roflumilast may offer benefits in lung function, it may also carry an increased risk of exacerbations in this subpopulation, warranting cautious use.

Because our findings were derived from an observational design, they should be interpreted with caution, and larger, protocolized prospective studies or randomized controlled trials will be required to confirm the causal relationship between roflumilast use and clinical outcomes in COPD patients with bronchiectasis. Studies should incorporate imaging-based severity scores, microbial colonization data, and treatment responsiveness across phenotypes. Research into combined regimens, including macrolide antibiotics and mucolytics alongside roflumilast, may offer more comprehensive control of inflammation and infection.
