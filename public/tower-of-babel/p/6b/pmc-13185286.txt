Psychological maltreatment, adverse childhood experiences on college students’ academic procrastination: exploring the role of adult attachment and resilience
Authors: Jing Song, Jingbo Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcademic procrastination is prevalent in college students, severely impacting their academic achievement, personal growth, and psychological well-being. However, few studies have explored the impact of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) on college students’ academic procrastination and the relationship between adult attachment and resilience in this process. The present study aimed to reveal the potential mechanisms of action between ACEs and academic procrastination.MethodsParticipants were recruited from three universities in Northeast China using convenience sampling, and 928 valid questionnaires were collected. The average age of the sample was 19.456 (SD = 1.079), 45.366% were female and 46.983% had experienced at least one ACEs.ResultsACEs are significantly positively correlated with academic procrastination. Furthermore, each of adult attachment and resilience mediates the association between ACEs and academic procrastination. Furthermore, adult attachment and resilience play a chain mediating role in the relationship; adult attachment is negatively associated with resilience.ConclusionThese findings provide new insights into the psychological mechanisms of academic procrastination and suggest ways to design targeted prevention strategies and interventions.Keywords: Adverse childhood experiences, Adult attachment, Resilience, Academic procrastination

Introduction

Academic procrastination, in which students voluntarily delay starting or completing an academic task without a reasonable excuse, has become a widely observed behavioral pattern in college students [1, 2]. This phenomenon is particularly significant in the college population [3, 4]. Statistically, 70% to 95% of college students delay completing academic tasks, and approximately 50% have habitual behavioral disorders of procrastination [3, 5]. This habitual procrastination is often considered a disturbing phenomenon because it not only affects students’ academic performance but may also hurt their long-term development [6]. For example, academic procrastination reduces academic performance and learning efficiency and also associated with increased negative emotions such as anxiety and depressive moods, which are a severe threat to their mental health and social functioning [7–11]. Therefore, systematic research on academic procrastination behaviors is essential to promote the overall healthy development of college students.

Academic procrastination is a complex psychological phenomenon with multidimensional causes, encompassing environmental factors, personal traits, and psychological factors [8–10, 12]. Environmental factors, such as family environment and school atmosphere, can impact students’ motivation to learn and their time management skills [13]. Personal characteristics, such as self-efficacy, self-esteem, resilience, and attachment style, are all closely related to academic procrastination [14]. Psychological factors include emotional regulation skills and motivation levels. These factors interact with each other, causing students to procrastinate when faced with academic tasks [11, 15]. It is noteworthy that adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) serve as a critical early environmental risk factor with profound implications for individual development. ACEs encompass various types of adversity experienced during childhood, including abuse, neglect, and family dysfunction etc [16]. Its prevalence has been confirmed in multiple studies. Reports indicate that approximately 68.2% to 83% of adolescents have experienced at least one ACE [17]. In some samples, 37% to 69% of individuals even reported cumulative exposure to two or more ACEs [18, 19]. ACEs are associated with weaker self-regulation and stress management abilities, which may relate to a greater likelihood of activating avoidance mode when facing academic tasks, potentially contributing to procrastination.

Further research indicates that adverse childhood experiences may be indirectly associated with academic procrastination through their relationships with adult attachment and psychological resilience [19–21]. According to attachment theory, early traumatic experiences disrupt the formation of secure attachment in individuals, linking them to develop insecure attachment patterns (such as anxious or avoidant types), which in turn influence how they cope with academic challenges [22–24]. For instance, individuals with insecure attachment styles are more likely to perceive academic tasks as threatening and tend to adopt avoidance strategies such as procrastination [25].

At the same time, ACEs have been shown to be associated with lower levels of resilience in individuals [19–21]. Resilience, as an individual’s capacity to maintain positive adaptation in the face of adversity, comprises three core components: tenacity (endurance in the face of difficulties), strength (the ability to recover from setbacks), and optimism (positive expectations for the future). Together, these form an internal resource system for coping with stress [26, 27]. For instance, lower levels of tenacity are associated with greater difficulty sustaining effort in the face of academic challenges; lower levels of optimism are linked to reduced positive expectations and intrinsic motivation for academic success, both of which are associated with a higher risk of procrastination [28, 29].

Although previous studies have separately examined the relationships between ACEs and academic procrastination, adult attachment, or resilience, the underlying mechanisms through which these three factors jointly influence academic procrastination remain unclear. Particularly among Chinese university students, this chain mediation pathway has yet to be systematically validated. Therefore, this study aims to construct a chained model mediated by adult attachment and resilience to deeply examine the psychological mechanisms underlying the associations between ACEs and academic procrastination. This will provide theoretical foundations and practical support for developing targeted psychological interventions.

ACEs on academic procrastination

ACEs refer to a series of potentially traumatic events experienced by individuals before the age of 18, including parental abuse and neglect, as well as family dysfunction and other ACEs [16]. Previous studies have confirmed that parental abuse and neglect and dysfunctional family structures (including emotional abuse, physical abuse, parental separation/divorce, and family members with substance abuse issues) are associated with multiple physical and mental health conditions in adulthood [30, 31]. Additionally, other ACEs (including low socioeconomic status (SES), witnessing community violence, and being isolated or ostracized) also exert significant impacts on child development. For instance, the uncertainty of life under low SES may trigger generalized stress dysregulation, subsequently linking to later physical and mental health issues [32]. Children raised in violent communities exhibit severe mental health and behavioral problems [33]. Peer isolation and social exclusion may exert independent effects on problem behaviors and later adaptation [34]. Among college students, the prevalence of ACEs varies depending on how they are defined, but the overall situation remains a cause for concern. Research shows that approximately 43% of college students have been exposed to low levels of ACEs (0–2 ACEs), while as many as 57% of students have experienced three or more ACEs [35]. These experiences are associated with weaker self-regulation abilities, which in turn may be linked to academic procrastination [36, 37].

According to the Self-regulated Learning Theory, time management is a critical self-regulatory process through which students actively manage their participation in activities believed to contribute to achieving academic goals [38]. Effective time management skills can help students plan and allocate their time more efficiently, thereby reducing academic procrastination [39]. However, ACEs, such as psychological and physical abuse in childhood, are associated with weaker self-management abilities and are negatively correlated with college students’ time management tendencies, which may relate to a greater tendency to procrastinate on academic tasks [40, 41]. Additionally, previous research has found that individuals with histories of abuse and neglect tend to have lower self-esteem than their peers [42]. Low self-esteem is often conceptualized as a personality factor associated with fear of failure. College students with lower self-esteem may be more likely to fear negative evaluations from others after failure and may use academic procrastination as a self-sabotaging strategy to protect their self-esteem [14]. Conversely, when procrastinators have higher self-esteem, their tendency to engage in academic procrastination behavior decreases [43]. Related longitudinal studies have also confirmed this conclusion [14]. In summary, a significant correlation exists between ACEs and academic procrastination among college students. We propose the following hypothesis:

H1: ACEs positively correlated with college students’ academic procrastination.

The mediation effect of adult attachment

Adult attachment refers to an individual’s emotional relationship with a current lover, partner, or close friend that provides a sense of intimacy and security based on childhood experiences [44]. Bowlby states that attachment occurs throughout an individual’s lifespan and that an individual typically seeks a sense of security and comfort, extending to other attachment figures such as friends and romantic partners [22, 23]. An individual’s early upbringing is critical to forming secure attachment patterns, and negative experiences may be associated with a gradual shift toward insecure attachment and negative perceptions of relationships [45]. For example, ACEs such as abuse, neglect, and domestic violence in childhood may contribute to the development of insecure attachment patterns in college students [18, 19]. Attachment anxiety and attachment avoidance are the two primary manifestations of insecure attachment. The former manifests as feeling upset due to fear of rejection or isolation, while the latter manifests as fear or avoidance of intimate relationships [24]. Research has shown that individuals with insecure attachments tend to report higher levels of academic procrastination behaviors, as evidenced by resistance to or anxiety about academic tasks [10, 46]. Attachment theory further elaborates that those individuals who experience secure attachment are more goal-oriented and tend to employ effective coping strategies to face challenges [22, 47]. Conversely, individuals with insecure attachments are more likely to view challenging situations as stressors and may adopt ineffective coping styles such as procrastination [9, 25]. Thus, it can be inferred that ACEs may influence academic procrastination behaviors through adult attachment.

H2: Adult attachment mediates the relationship between ACEs and college students’ academic procrastination.

H2: Adult attachment mediates the relationship between ACEs and college students’ academic procrastination.

The mediation effect of resilience

Resilience is defined as an individual’s behavioral tendency to adapt to changing environments and the ability to recover from stressful situations [48, 49]. Resilience serves as an individual’s stress-coping resource, which can help the individual effectively resist the adverse effects of stress, thus becoming an essential issue in positive psychology [50]. In addition, resilience is a critical psychological resource for individuals’ academic motivation and adaptation. It plays a crucial role in coping with academic challenges, maintaining academic motivation, and achieving academic goals [51]. Research has shown that resilience is an essential psychological resource for individuals to maintain positive attitudes and actions in the face of academic challenges and adversity, and it is significantly associated with academic procrastination [20, 50]. However, adverse experiences in childhood have been found to be significantly associated with lower individual resilience [20]. Specifically, physical and emotional abuse and neglect of children’s needs suffered during childhood are factors that prevent the normal development of resilience in children by diminishing their perceptions of family support [21]. The lack of resilience makes students more likely to give up when faced with academic difficulties rather than actively seeking solutions. As a result, they are less able to recover and adjust when faced with academic adversity, thereby increasing the risk of academic procrastination [52]. Above all, it can be inferred that ACEs may influence academic procrastination behaviors through resilience.

H3: Resilience mediates the relationship between ACEs and college students’ academic procrastination.

Adult attachment on resilience

Attachment, as a central internal factor, is crucial in developing resilience [53]. Attachment theory proposes that secure attachment relationships provide individuals with a stable internal working model that promotes superior developmental outcomes, including resilience formation [54]. Individuals with secure attachments hold favorable views of self and others, are comfortable with intimacy and independence, believe in coping with adversity, and can mobilize resources to respond positively to external pressures and access external support when help is needed [55]. This ability to understand oneself and others correctly is considered a core element in resilience to effectively cope with external pressures and challenges [56]. Research has shown that college students with secure attachment types exhibit higher resilience and are better able to cope and self-regulate in the face of stress [57]. However, college life involves multiple transitions, such as leaving home, developing independence and autonomy, rebuilding social support networks, and exploring career goals. These transitions provide opportunities for growth and challenges for college students [58]. College students who have had early adverse experiences may develop insecure attachment styles [59], which inhibit the development of resilience [60]. The Conservation of Resources Theory posits that individuals possess an innate motivation to protect existing resources and acquire new ones, with stress fundamentally stemming from the threat of resource loss [61]. Psychological resilience, as a critical coping resource, is directly influenced by an individual’s cognitive evaluation of stressful situations in terms of their depleting or developmental nature. Individuals with insecure attachment styles, lacking stable internal working models, are more prone to perceive potential resource depletion when facing stress, thereby inhibiting the activation and maintenance of psychological resilience. Specifically, attachment anxiety stems from negative self-models and hypervigilance to rejection, leading to a tendency to employ over-activation strategies in stressful situations [24]. This strategy manifests as exaggerated interpretations of threat signals, fixation on ruminative thoughts, and excessive emotional venting. This process not only depletes cognitive resources that could be allocated to problem-solving but also directly undermines key manifestations of psychological resilience, such as the capacity for sustained persistence in adversity (grit) and the tendency to maintain positive expectations about the future (optimism) [21, 36]. This lack of resilience makes them susceptible to academic procrastination behaviors in the face of academic stress [62]. In conclusion, these findings emphasize the role of adult attachment in developing individual resilience and suggest that ACEs may influence college students’ academic procrastination through both adult attachment and resilience.

H4: Adult attachment and resilience play a chain mediating role in the relationship between ACEs and college students’ academic procrastination.

Present study

The impact of ACEs on development is one of the critical areas of research in psychology and education. Although existing research has provided a wealth of empirical data demonstrating the negative impact of ACEs on college students’ academic outcomes, there is still a dearth of research on the underlying mechanisms of how ACEs affect college students’ academic procrastination. Furthermore, the role of adult attachment and resilience in the relationship between ACEs and academic procrastination has not been fully elucidated. Given this, the present study aims to fill this research gap by exploring the effects of ACEs on academic procrastination behaviors and the mediating roles of adult attachment and resilience through a cross-sectional study. The findings of this study are not only expected to enrich and improve the current theoretical framework but are also important for parents and educators to provide practical strategies for preventing and intervening in college students’ academic procrastinating behaviors. Materials and methods

Participants

This study employed convenience sampling to select undergraduate students from three universities in Northeast China (including one science and engineering university and two agricultural universities) as research subjects between May and June 2024. With the help of the school counselor-teacher, the job posting was made through social media platforms, and a link to the online survey was distributed. Participants were informed of the study, and consent was obtained before the start of the study. A total of 928 valid questionnaires were obtained. The priori power test analysis conducted with G*Power indicated that, with F tests and an effect size f2 of 0.02, a minimum sample size of 882 would be required. The current sample size meets this criterion. The mean age of the sample was 19.456 years (SD = 1.079), of which 421 (45.366%) were female and 507 (54.634%) were male.

Measures

ACEs

The Revised Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (ACEQ-R), developed by Finkelhor and adapted into the Chinese version by Wang, was used to measure college students’ ACEs [16, 63, 64]. The questionnaire consists of 14 items, each representing an adverse experience before the age of 18, including childhood abuse and neglect, poor family structure, and other adverse experiences. A score of 0 was assigned to the absence of the experience, a score of 1 was assigned to the presence of the experience, and the 14-item scores were summed up to obtain a total score. The higher the total score, the more adverse childhood experiences were experienced. In the current study, Cronbach’s α of this scale was 0.770.

Adult attachment

The Adult Attachment Scale, developed by Collins and translated into Chinese by Du, was used to measure college students’ adult attachment [65, 66]. The scale consists of 18 items and is scored on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (completely fit). The scale is divided into two sub-dimensions: attachment avoid (12 items), which refers to fear and discomfort in intimate relationships, and attachment anxiety (6 items), which refers to fear of rejection or isolation from others. Higher scores indicate more insecure attachment types. In the current study, Cronbach’s α of this scale was 0.920.

Resilience

The Chinese version of the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), adapted by Yu and Zhang (2007) from the original by Connor and Davidson (2003), was used to measure resilience [26, 27]. The 25-item scale is rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not true at all) to 5 (true all the time). It comprises three dimensions: tenacity, strength, and optimism, with higher total scores indicating greater psychological resilience. In this study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.951.

Academic procrastination

The Chinese University Student Procrastination Scale, developed by Zhao, was used to measure college students’ academic procrastination [67]. This scale is based on the Aitken Procrastination Inventory [68] and the Tuckman Procrastination Scales [69]. The scale consists of 19 items divided into three dimensions: deferred action, poor completion, and inadequate planning. Participants rated each item on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree), with higher scores indicating more significant academic procrastination. In the current study, Cronbach’s α of this scale was 0.906.

Analysis process

This study employed SPSS 24.0 and AMOS 24.0 for statistical analysis. First, we utilized SPSS software for preliminary processing and analysis of the variables, including basic statistical information and correlation coefficient matrices. Second, we constructed a structural equation model to examine the relationship between childhood adversity, adult attachment, resilience, and academic procrastination. χ2/df (1 ~ 3), CFI (> 0.9), GFI (> 0.9), TLI (> 0.9), and RMSEA (< 0.08) were used to assess the overall fit of the structural equation model [70]. The path coefficients of the structural equations were estimated using the Maximum Likelihood Estimate (MLE) method. Finally, to avoid potential biases caused by distribution, we used the bootstrap method to test the significance of each mediating effect. When the 95% bootstrap confidence interval did not include 0, the effect was considered significant. In addition, this study included gender, age, and domain as control variables in the structural equation model.

Discussion

This study aimed to reveal the effects of ACEs on college students’ academic procrastination and their potential mechanisms of action. This study found that ACEs are positively associated with academic procrastination. In addition, adult attachment and resilience constitute a chain mediating effect between ACEs and academic procrastination. These findings enrich our understanding of the psychological causes of academic procrastination, a critical factor in college students’ academic procrastination, and provide new perspectives and potential targets for developing targeted prevention and intervention measures.

This study found a significant positive correlation between ACEs and academic procrastination among college students, H1 has supported. Academic procrastination is a complex psychological behavior phenomenon involving the interaction of multiple levels, including cognition, emotion, and behavior [73–75]. Research indicates that individual factors such as self-regulation ability, self-efficacy, self-esteem levels, and fear of failure significantly influence academic procrastination, while negative emotional states such as anxiety and depression are positively correlated with academic procrastination [6, 46, 76, 77]. The association between ACEs and academic procrastination can be understood as follows. First, these traumatic experiences are associated with lower self-efficacy and self-esteem, as well as with cognitive development difficulties [14]. Individuals with lower self-esteem are more likely to adopt procrastination strategies as a form of self-protection, due to fear of failure evaluations, and may attribute failure to external factors to maintain their fragile self-image [78]. Second, emotional neglect during childhood may be linked to the formation of negative self-perceptions and lower future self-continuity (i.e., the ability to plan for the future and set goals) [79, 80]. Additionally, prolonged psychological stress and negative emotions are associated with depletion of psychological resources, which may relate to exhaustion of self-regulation abilities [81]. These factors collectively may make it more difficult for college students to maintain sufficient self-control and learning motivation when facing academic tasks, thereby contributing to a higher likelihood of academic procrastination [81, 82].

The research findings indicate that adult attachment mediates the relationship between ACEs and academic procrastination among college students, H2 has supported. This is consistent with the core tenets of attachment theory, which posits that early attachment relationships are associated with the formation of internal working models that are related to individual development. Specifically, ACEs are associated with disruptions in the formation of secure attachment, which are linked to anxious or avoidant attachment styles, and insecure attachment are associated with higher levels of academic procrastination [36]. On one hand, insecure attachment is associated with negative self-schemas, such as “I am unlovable” and “others are unreliable” [83]. These negative cognitions are linked to lower self-esteem and learned helplessness, which may relate to a reduced ability to respond proactively to academic challenges [36]. On the other hand, insecure attachment is associated with difficulties in emotional regulation, and emotional regulation plays a key role in understanding procrastination as a self-regulation difficulty. Individuals who have experienced ACEs often exhibit heightened sensitivity to negative emotions. Individuals with insecure attachment may become distracted due to emotional over activation and may use academic procrastination as a means to avoid the emotional discomfort triggered by tasks [24, 84].

The research findings indicate that resilience mediates the relationship between ACEs and academic procrastination among college students, H3 was supported. Resilience, as the core ability for individuals to mobilize internal and external resources to cope with adversity [29], is an essential protective factor in the field of positive psychology and is significantly associated with various positive developmental outcomes, including reduced procrastination behavior [85]. Empirical research shows that the number of adverse childhood experiences is significantly negatively correlated with resilience levels. Specifically, childhood abuse (including psychological abuse and emotional neglect) is associated with lasting trauma to children’s physical and mental development [57] and is also linked to lower resilience levels in adulthood [86, 87]. From a psychological mechanism perspective, childhood psychological abuse is associated with disruptions in the formation of a positive self-concept, which may lead individuals to internalize negative self-perception patterns. This cognitive distortion hinders the development of adaptive coping strategies, thereby constraining the development of resilience [88]. Resilience has a significant link to academic procrastination. Individuals with lower levels of resilience tend to report higher levels of procrastination when faced with academic challenges, and this association may be related to lower self-efficacy and fewer coping resources [89, 90]. Students with higher resilience tend to report greater use of positive coping strategies, which are associated with more adaptive task perceptions and lower perceived stress, and these factors are in turn linked to lower frequency of academic procrastination. Conversely, individuals with lower resilience tend to report more negative and pessimistic attitudes and fewer effective coping strategies [91]. These negative coping strategies are associated with higher levels of academic procrastination, as individuals may be more likely to avoid academic tasks rather than actively address them [92].

Finally, ACEs are associated with academic procrastination through the chain mediation of adult attachment and resilience, and adult insecure attachment negative association with resilience, H4 has supported. These findings are consistent with the theoretical framework that insecure attachment disrupts an individual’s internal working model of self and others [93]. The instability of this model is linked to a diminished sense of security and control in the face of challenges, which is associated with decreased resilience and, consequently, lower active participation and engagement in academic tasks [94]. These findings are consistent with attachment theory. Specifically, traumatic childhood experiences are associated with greater difficulty in establishing stable and secure attachment relationships with others, and with lower levels of adjustment, self-efficacy, and resilience. Individuals with such experiences may also find it more challenging to seek out positives to cope with adversity in difficult situations [95]. Furthermore, insecure attachment is associated with the continuation of ACEs-related challenges, including limited access to effective social support in subsequent development, which is linked to lower resilience [36]. Thus, the associations between ACEs and lower resilience may be partially explained by the links between ACEs and insecure attachment styles, which are in turn associated with lower resilience. This influence pathway highlights the importance of considering both adult attachment and resilience when preventing and intervening in academic procrastination.

Limitation

The present study provides valuable insights into exploring the effects of ACEs on college students’ academic procrastination, but there are several limitations. First, the sample was entirely selected through convenience sampling from three universities in Northeast China. This sampling method may introduce regional bias into the sample, limiting the generalizability of the research conclusions when applied to college student populations in other regions. Future studies should adopt more comprehensive sampling strategies, incorporating samples from different geographical regions and different types of universities to enhance the universality of the research findings. Second, the present study primarily examined adult attachment and resilience as mediating variables and failed to adequately consider other possible psychological mechanisms, such as emotion regulation difficulties, self-esteem, and self-efficacy. These factors may also affect the relationship between ACEs and academic procrastination. Therefore, future research should explore potential mediating variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding. Finally, the cross-sectional design used in this study could not adequately determine the causal relationship between the variables or examine the impact effects over time. Therefore, future studies could use more diverse data collection methods, such as follow-up studies, to enhance the reliability and validity of the findings.

Conclusion

The present study examined the associations between ACEs and college students’ academic procrastination, as well as the mediating mechanisms underlying these associations. The findings indicate that ACEs are positively correlated with academic procrastination. Each of adult attachment and resilience mediates the relationship between ACEs and academic procrastination. Furthermore, adult attachment and resilience play a chain mediating role in the relationship. These findings significantly broaden our understanding of the associations between ACEs and college students’ academic procrastination behaviors, providing important empirical evidence for developing and implementing targeted prevention strategies and intervention programs.

Based on the research findings, this study offers the following practical recommendations for educators, parents, and mental health professionals to support individuals with ACEs in managing academic procrastination behavior. Schools should implement mentoring programs to establish one-on-one supportive relationships between students and teachers or peers, thereby enhancing students’ emotional stability. Additionally, counseling services can incorporate attachment-oriented intervention methods, such as attachment family therapy, to help students address attachment-related anxiety or avoidance behaviors. School mental health curricula should incorporate resilience training, utilizing cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), mindfulness exercises, and stress management workshops to help students develop skills for addressing academic challenges. Additionally, schools should conduct regular screenings to promptly identify students at high risk of ACEs exposure and provide them with targeted support measures, such as academic tutoring and time management training. Furthermore, a trauma-informed educational environment should be established, with teachers receiving relevant training to recognize ACEs-related behavioral manifestations and respond to students’ needs in a more inclusive and understanding manner, thereby creating a safe and supportive learning atmosphere for students.
