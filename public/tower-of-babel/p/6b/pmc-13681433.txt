Single-Cell Transcriptomic Profiling of Human Blood Vessel Organoids Exposed to 6‑Chloronicotinic Acid
Authors: Siyi Wang, Xianghui Wang, Kailun Wang, Shi Tang, Xia Sheng, Shunqing Xu, Yahang Lin, Mingxi Zhang, Fengzhen Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
6-Chloronicotinic acid (6-ClNA) is a major metabolite of chloropyridinyl neonicotinoids in humans; however, its toxic effect on blood vessel development remains to be understood. In this study, we utilized a humanized blood vessel organoid (hBVO) model and single-cell transcriptomics technology to investigate the impact of 6-ClNA exposure on hBVO development. Morphologically, 6-ClNA did not significantly alter the growth of hBVO throughout 20 days of culture. Interestingly, single-cell transcriptomic analyses revealed that 6-ClNA-treated hBVOs exhibited significant imbalance in cell subpopulation homeostasis, including impaired endothelial cell function and abnormal proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells. This was echoed by the morphological changes in the aortic arch and abdominal aorta of mice after acute exposure to 6-ClNA. Enrichment analysis showed that 6-ClNA exposure affected critical biological processes in a cell-type-specific manner, such as vascular developmental signaling, cellular energy metabolism, protein homeostasis, and hemodynamic responses. In addition, trajectory analysis suggested that 6-ClNA altered the differentiation trajectory of vascular progenitors. Together, our study profiles the toxicity of 6-ClNA on blood vessel development in a humanized 3D model at single-cell resolution, which provides important and detailed evidence for understanding the potential risk of early life exposure to chloropyridinyl neonicotinoids.

Introduction

Neonicotinoid insecticides (NNIs), recognized as highly effective broad-spectrum pesticides, have gained global adoption due to their low risk of cross-resistance with traditional pesticides. However, environmental monitoring data over the past three decades reveal significant residues of NNIs in drinking water, soil, and food. Structurally, NNIs fall into three main categories: chloropyridinyl neonicotinoids (e.g., imidacloprid, acetamiprid, and nitenpyram), chlorothiazolylmethyl neonicotinoids, and tetrahydrofuranylmethyl neonicotinoids, with chloropyridinyl neonicotinoids being the most widely used. These chemicals undergo biotransformation in both natural environment and humans to produce the common end-product 6-chloronicotinic acid (6-ClNA, C6H4ClNO2), which has become a crucial biomarker for environmental exposure and monitoring. Both cohort and experimental studies have indicated potential adverse health effects of NNIs, of particular concern is their frequent detection in pregnant women and children. Notably, NNIs and their metabolites have been shown to cross the placental barrier, yet its effect on fetal development remains largely unknown.

Recent human biomonitoring studies demonstrate ubiquitous and persistent 6-ClNA exposure, with detection frequencies exceeding 98% in pregnant women and 100% in children, alongside widespread occurrence in general populations. − Notably, urinary concentrations of the neonicotinoid metabolite 6-ClNA exceeded those of its class counterparts in the study population. Importantly, 6-ClNA exhibits the highest plasma protein binding rate (86.4%) among the 18 neonicotinoid metabolites examined, suggesting significant bioaccumulation potential and sustained biological activity. Despite these converging indicators of substantial internal burden, the effect of 6-ClNA on vascular development and the underlying molecular mechanisms remain undefined.

The mammalian vasculature is composed of endothelial and parietal cells, including pericytes and smooth muscle cells (SMCs). Vascular formation is accomplished through two mechanisms: during vasculogenesis, mesodermal progenitor cells differentiate into ECs under the induction of growth factors such as FGF-2, BMP4, and VEGF-A to assemble into primitive capillary plexuses, while angiogenesis achieves vascular remodeling through the directional migration of tip cells and the proliferation of stalk cells. These cells establish functional lumens via mechanisms including vacuole fusion and membrane invagination, ultimately forming a mature circulatory network. Of note, the recent development of humanized blood vessel organoid (hBVO) offers a novel humanized model to study the programming and reprogramming of blood vessel development. hBVO is a 3D in vitro model developed from human pluripotent stem cells, which simulates the dynamic process and complex structure of blood vessel development. Their human origin and 3D properties make up for the shortcomings of the existing animal or 2D cell culture models. Single cell sequencing of hBVO has identified major cell types such as proliferating vascular progenitor (VP) cells, ECs, pericytes, SMCs, and fibroblasts, which have similar morphological and functional characteristics to human microvessels. Therefore, hBVO also makes an important model to study the toxicology of environmental chemicals on blood vessel development because of their high physiological relevance and easy manipulability.

Therefore, to examine the heterogeneous effect of 6-ClNA exposure on blood vessel development in a humanized 3D setting, we established the hBVO model and performed comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic analyses of hBVOs exposed to 6-ClNA. Our results reveal interesting cell-type-specific toxicity and molecular features of hBVOs caused by 6-ClNA.

Key Resources

HESCs Culture

The culture of human H9 embryonic stem cells (hESCs) was performed as previously described. Cells were maintained in the mTeSR Plus medium (Stemcell Technologies) on Matrigel (Corning)-coated dishes. For subculturing every 5 days, cells were first digested with ReleSR (Stemcell) at 37 °C for 1 min. Following reagent removal, the dissociation process was terminated by adding 1 mL of prewarmed mTeSR Plus medium and incubating for 6 min at 37 °C. The resulting cell suspension was then replated at a 1:30 split ratio onto fresh Matrigel-coated plates in mTeSR Plus medium supplemented with 10 μmol/L Y-27632 (Tocris). The medium was replaced with the fresh mTeSR Plus medium without Y-27632 24 h postpassaging.

Generation of BVOs

Notably, we implemented the same morphological and functional quality control criteria as in Sun et al.’s to ensure functional stability of the organoids. In brief, cultured hESCs clones isolated into single cells. Since organoids were initially cultured in low-adsorption 96-well plates, the amount of 150 μL culture medium containing 9000 cells per well was calculated. The calculated amount of aspirated cell suspension was resuspended in mTeSR medium containing 10–50 μmol/L Y27632. They were then seeded into U-bottom 96-well plates coated with low adsorbed lipids to form EBs. On day 2, the medium was replaced with the mesoderm induction medium (APEL2 containing 6 μmol/L CHIR99021). On day 4, the medium was changed to endothelial induction medium consisting of APEL2 containing 50 ng/mL VEGF, 25 ng/mL BMP4, and 10 ng/mL bFGF. On day 7, the medium was changed to the EGM containing 50 ng/mL VEGF to allow ECs of vascular organoids to mature, and the medium was renewed every other day. From day 12, EBs were embedded in Matrigel drops and cultured in ECM containing VEGF (20 ng/mL) for culture. After 1 day’s interval, the vascular organoids coated with Matrigel were dug out into a six-well plate with low adsorption, and 3 mL of ECM containing VEGF (20 ng/mL) was added to each well. The six-well plate was transferred to a shaker in the cell incubator for further culture. The vascular organoids were cultured until day 20 for further biological studies.

For this study, control (Ctrl) groups were treated with vehicle (absolute ethanol), while treatment groups were exposed to 6-ClNA at concentrations of 100 nmol/L and 1000 nmol/L, respectively, for 20 days (to avoid cellular senescence). On day 20, organoids were harvested and processed for immunofluorescence staining, quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR), and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis.

Immunofluorescence Staining

The hBVOs samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h at 4 °C. The fixed organoids were then washed with PBS three times, transferred to a 30% sucrose solution, and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The dehydrated tissues were then embedded in OCT compound (SAKURA), snap frozen, and stored at −80 °C. For immunofluorescence staining, 50 μm-thick sections were obtained using a cryostat (Leica). Cryosections on adhesive slides were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min and then left to soak in PBS three times, 5 min each time. Then, 0.3% TritonX-100 was used to stand for 10 min. The sections were blocked with 10% bovine serum protein (BSA) for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody diluent. The following primary antibodies were used for immunocytochemical analysis: CD31 (mouse, 1:200, CST 3528T), α-SMA (Rabbit, 1:200, Boster Biotechnology, BM3902), SOX17 (Rabbit, 1:200, CST, 81778T), and NR2F2 (Rabbit, 1:200, Abcam, ab211777). Samples were washed with PBS three times to remove excess primary antibody and then incubated with secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 or 594 dyes (1:400) for 1 h at room temperature. Cell nuclei were then counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Meilun, 1:1000) for 5 min. All images were photographed using a confocal microscope (Leica). ImageJ (NIH) was used to merge images and to uniformly adjust the brightness or contrast.

Proteostat Aggresome Detection

Aggresomes were detected using the PROTEOSTAT Aggresome Detection Kit (cat. no. ENZ-51035, BD Biosciences) following the provided protocol. In brief, hBVOs sections were fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min at room temperature and then washed three times with PBS. Slides were transferred into a permeabilizing solution (0.5% Triton X-100, 3 × 10–3 mol/L ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, pH 8) on ice for 30 min. After further washing with PBS buffer, slides were incubated with Proteostat dye at a 1:5000 dilution for 30 min in the dark, followed by DAPI staining to label nuclei. The morphology of organoids was observed under a microscope, and the images were collected.

RNA Extraction and Real-Time qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from hBVOs using a TRIzol kit (Invitrogen). Single-stranded cDNA was synthesized using Vazyme, a universal cDNA synthesis kit. mRNA expression was measured using a rapid real-time qPCR system (7900HT, ABI, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a SYBR Green master mix (Vazyme). Animal Experiments

Male C57BL/6 mice (24–26 g) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories. All animal experiments were performed in strict accordance with the relevant guidelines for the management and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Hainan University Institutional Animal Use and Care (approval number: HNUAUCC-2025-00098). During the experiment, mice were kept in a temperature of 20–25 °C, a relative humidity of 40%–50%, and under a 12 h light/dark cycle. Mice were exposed to 6-ClNA (10 mg/kg/day) through drinking water for 1 week. The Ctrl group was given the same volume of DMSO (as vehicle control). At the end of the exposure, the mice were sacrificed after cardiac perfusion, and the aortic arch and abdominal aorta tissues were collected for H&E staining analysis.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining

The aortic arch and abdominal aorta tissues were fixed overnight in 4% PFA, dehydrated in gradient ethanol (e.g., 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100% ethanol), transparent in xylene, infiltrated in paraffin, and finally embedded into paraffin blocks. The embedded wax blocks were cut into 4–6 μmol/L-thick slices with a microtome, flattened in warm water, and removed from the slides. The slices were baked at 60 °C for 2 h to make the slices firmly attached. For staining, the sections were deparaffinized to water (xylene deparaffinized, rehydrated with gradient ethanol), then immersed in hematoxylin staining solution for 5–15 min, so that the nucleus was blue-purple, rinsed with running water to remove excess staining solution, differentiated with 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol for a few seconds, and then blued (or diluted ammonia–water back blue) for 10–15 min. This was followed by staining with eosin solution for 1–3 min to make the cytoplasm and extracellular matrix pink. The final sections were dehydrated in gradient ethanol, transparent in xylene, sealed with neutral gum, and allowed to solidify before being observed under the microscope.

Single-Cell Dissociation and Sequencing

Twenty-four organoids were grouped into one group. The organoids were washed once with DPBS and then ground. They were transferred to DPBS containing 5 mg/mL Dispase and 0.5 mg/mL Liberase THResearch grade and incubated for 20 to 30 min at 37 °C and 30 rpm. After the first 5 min, 60 units/mL (6 mg/mL) Dnase I was added, and the organoids were further milled. Thereafter, the samples were ground every 5–7 min and regularly examined under the microscope until a single cell suspension was obtained. The reaction was terminated using cold KnockOut DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS. The samples were centrifuged at 300g for 5 min at 4 °C. The plates were resuspended on ice in DPBS containing 2% FBS, filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer, and centrifuged again at 300g for 5 min at 4 °C. The plates were resuspended on ice in DPBS containing 2% FBS, filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer, and centrifuged again at 300g for 5 min at 4 °C. Finally, the cells were resuspended in DPBS containing 2% FBS and counted, the concentration was adjusted if necessary, and the cells were loaded onto a 10× microfluidic chip and collected. After filtering, a total of 14,455 cells from the control group and 14,955 cells from the 6-ClNA-treated group were retained for subsequent bioinformatic analysis.

Single-Cell RNA Sequencing Data Processing, Clustering, and Annotation

The 10× Genomics Cell Ranger software (v.7.1.0) was used to align reads to the human reference genome (GRCh38) and to quantify counts. The Seurat pipeline (v.4.3.0) was applied to each sample for downstream integrated analyses based on R (version 4.1.1). To obtain high-quality data, cells were filtered to exclude those with low-quality metrics (number of detected genes fewer than 500 or more than 7000; number of unique molecular identifiers fewer than 1000 or more than 200,000; and mitochondrial gene content exceeding 20%). After removal of low-quality cells, a total of 29,410 cells were retained for subsequent analysis. Genes detected in fewer than three cells were excluded from the further analysis. Following quality control, the top 2000 highly variable genes were selected based on their mean-variance ratio for gene expression normalization using the ScaleData function. The Harmony algorithm was then applied to minimize batch effects across all samples. For downstream clustering and visualization, principal component analysis was performed on the integrated expression matrix for dimensionality reduction using the RunPCA function; the first 30 principal components were extracted for subsequent clustering with a resolution parameter of 0.3. The clustering results were visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) two-dimensional reduction based on the first 30 principal components. Cell types were manually annotated on the basis of the expression of canonical marker genes.

Differential Gene Expression and Pathway Enrichment Analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between control and 6-ClNA-treated groups were identified for each annotated cell type using the FindMarkers function in Seurat. Genes with an adjusted p value of <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the R package clusterProfiler (v.3.18.1). DEGs were used as input for a hypergeometric test using the “enricher” function with parameters minGSSize = 10, maxGSSize = 500, pvalueCutoff = 0.01, qvalueCutoff = 0.01, and pAdjustMethod = “BH”. Pathway gene signatures, including Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/genesets.jsp). Additionally, Gene Set Variation Analysis was performed to evaluate pathway activity at the single-cell level.

Pseudotime Trajectory Analysis

Monocle3 (v.1.3.4) was used to perform the trajectory analysis to explore the pseudotime developmental dynamics of VP cells and their differentiated lineages. The estimateSizeFactors and estimateDispersions functions were applied to construct statistical models that characterize the data. Subsequently, the reduceDimension and orderCells functions were used to reduce the dimensionality of the cells and place them along a pseudotime trajectory. The distribution of cells from Ctrl and 6-ClNA-treated groups along the pseudotime trajectory was compared, and the expression dynamics of key lineage-specific genes (e.g., COL1A1, LUM, MKI67, and PDGFRA) were analyzed to identify potential differentiation blocks or altered cell fate decisions.

Intercellular Communication Analysis

The log-normalized scRNA-seq expression matrix and meta-data, including sample origin and cell type annotations, were used as input for CellChat (v.1.6.1). To infer cell-state-specific communication, signaling genes that were differentially expressed across all cell groups were first identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For each cell group, the ensemble average expression of these signaling genes was then calculated via a statistically robust mean approach. The communication probability for a given signaling pathway was derived by aggregating the probabilities of its associated ligand–receptor pairs, which reflects the overall interaction strength. Finally, a permutation test was performed by randomly shuffling cell group labels to determine significant interactions between any two cell groups. CellChat objects were separately constructed for Ctrl and 6-ClNA-treated groups, and ligand–receptor interactions were quantitatively compared between conditions, including overall interaction strength, number of interactions, and major signaling pathway networks.

Data Analysis

The similarity of variance between the groups was evaluated, and the results indicated that there was a similarity in variance between the groups. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0) and R statistical software (version 4.1.1). Treatment effects in each experiment were compared using two-tailed Student’s t tests or one-way ANOVA, with subsequent Bonferroni’s post hoc tests, if applicable. Statistical significance was defined at a threshold of p < 0.05. Technical replicates refer to assessments or assays performed multiple times on the same sample to ensure data reliability. Sample sizes were based on previous work done in the laboratory.

Results

6-ClNA Exposure Disrupts hBVOs Vascular Structure without Altering Its Morphology

To establish the hBVO model, the H9 human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) were subjected to a directed differentiation protocol, beginning with Wnt-induced mesoderm formation, followed by growth factor-mediated progenitor specification and subsequent culture maturation and culminating in the formation of 3D vascular structures in Matrigel (Figure A). Since no human blood concentration of 6-ClNA has been reported, we selected the dosage according to the limited cellular toxicity study: He et al. described that 100 nmol/L 6-ClNA caused little change in neural stem cell viability, whereas 1000 nmol/L showed visible harmful effect. Bright-field images taken every 4 days showed no significant difference in the size and morphology of hBVOs between the vehicle and 6-ClNA groups (Figure B,C). However, immunofluorescence analysis showed that both 100 nmol/L and 1000 nmol/L 6-ClNA treatment resulted in significant reduction in CD31 staining, total vessel length, vessel density, and the number of branch points (Figure D,E).This suggests that 6-ClNA exposure can damage the vascular structure of hBVOs without significantly affecting their morphology and size.

Single-Cell Transcriptomic Analysis Reveals Altered Cell Composition and Impaired ECs Differentiation by 6-ClNA

To investigate the heterogeneous effect of 6-ClNA exposure on hBVO development, we performed scRNA-seq using the 10× Genomics platform to characterize the cellular landscape of hBVOs. After data set integration and dimensionality reduction, distinct cellular transcriptomes were clearly resolved. Unsupervised clustering identified six transcriptionally distinct cell populations, which were annotated based on canonical marker genes expression and cross-referenced with a human multiorgan developmental cell atlas (Figure A). Our hBVO model derived from the hESCs successfully generated diverse vascular-associated cell types, including VPs, ECs, pericytes, SMCs, fibroblasts, and an undetermined population. Notably, 6-ClNA exposure led to significant shifts in cellular composition, characterized by a marked reduction in endothelial cell (EC) abundance accompanied by an expansion of SMCs (Figure B,C). Individual qPCR analysis further confirmed that ACTA2 expression was upregulated, while PECAM1 expression was downregulated in hBVOs after 6-ClNA exposure (Figure D). We chose the top four to five highly expressed marker genes in each cluster (Figure E,F) and systematically evaluated the expression patterns of these genes in different cell types to verify their cell specificity and relevance to developmental processes (Figure G–I). These findings suggest that 6-ClNA exposure impairs EC differentiation while promoting SMC expansion during hBVO development.

6-ClNA Impairs Key Vascular Developmental Pathways and Proteostasis in hBVOs

To gain insight into the molecular reprogramming induced by 6-ClNA exposure, we performed differential gene expression analysis across individual cell populations, followed by KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. 6-ClNA exposure induced extensive gene expression alterations in all identified cell types (Figure A). Pathway activity scoring revealed significant enrichment of key vascular developmental pathways, including VEGF, Notch, and TGF-β (Figure B). KEGG analysis further highlighted cell-type specific biological programs, such as PI3K-AKT signaling and oxidative phosphorylation in fibroblasts, protein transduction pathways in proliferating progenitors, and extracellular matrix (ECM)–receptor interactions in pericytes (Figure C–E). Interestingly, multiple cell types concomitantly exhibited enrichment of pathways associated with diabetic vasculopathy as well as those related to protein synthesis, processing, and aggregation (Figure C–E), indicating that imbalanced proteostasis may be a common feature of 6-ClNA’s adverse effect. To validate this, we conducted ProteoStat staining that specifically detects protein aggresome, which confirmed increased protein aggregation in 6-ClNA-exposed hBVOs (Figure F). Taken together, 6-ClNA exposure may have impaired the vascular structure and function of hBVOs by disrupting key vascular developmental pathways and proteostasis.

6-ClNA Impairs Arterial Endothelium and Promotes SMC Proliferation

To determine whether 6-ClNA affects arterial or venous ECs, hBVOs were double-stained with CD31 and SOX17 or CD31 and NR2F2. Immunostaining showed that the number of CD31+/SOX17+ cells was robustly reduced in hBVOs, with little to no change in the number of CD31+/NR2F2+ cells (Figure A,B). This suggests that 6-ClNA exposure primarily affects the differentiation and function of arterial ECs rather than those of venous ECs. In the meantime, there was a significant increase in the number of SMCs in the 6-CINA-treated group, which was consistent with the single-cell transcriptomic sequencing results (Figure C).

To validate this in vivo, 8 week-old male mice were treated with 10 mg/kg/day 6-ClNA through drinking water for 1 week before H&E examination of the aortic arch and abdominal aorta (Figure D). The histological analysis revealed that compared to the Ctrl group, the vascular structure in the 6-ClNA treatment group was significantly altered, showing pathological changes in vascular remodeling. These included disordered normal stratification of the vascular wall, disruption of the continuity of the intima, significant hypertrophy of the media accompanied by an increase in SMC density, and compensatory narrowing of the lumen (Figure E). These pathological changes were consistent with the phenotypes observed in hBVOs.

To further explore the effect of 6-ClNA on vascular organoid ECs, transcriptomic profiling revealed significant enrichment of pathways governing intracellular membrane dynamics, including membrane trafficking, vesicle-mediated transport, and Rab-dependent trafficking (Figure A), indicating reprogramming of endothelial vesicular networks. Among the upregulated genes, RIC1 (Rab6-interacting protein 1), a guanine nucleotide exchange factor involved in Rab-mediated vesicle transport, emerged as a recurrent component across multiple trafficking-related pathways (Figure A). Cell-type–resolved analysis demonstrated that RIC1 induction was largely restricted to the vascular lineage, with significant upregulation in VPs and, more prominently, in ECs following 6-ClNA exposure (Figure B). Immunofluorescence costaining of CD31 and RIC1 further confirmed that the RIC1 level was significantly elevated in ECs treated with 6-ClNA (Figure C). In summary, these results indicate that 6-ClNA exposure may disrupt vascular homeostasis in hBVOs by causing specific damage to arterial ECs, abnormal proliferation of SMCs, and activated membrane transport pathways.

6-ClNA Induces Cellular Communication Network Remodeling and Hemodynamic Dysregulation in hBVOs

To delineate the effect of 6-ClNA exposure on cellular crosstalk within hBVOs, intercellular communication dynamics were analyzed using CellChat. The results demonstrated that 6-ClNA profoundly reprogrammed the intercellular signaling network. Critical developmental and matrix-associated pathways (such as Wnt and Periostin) were markedly attenuated, whereas prototypical angiogenic circuits, including TGF-β, FGF, Notch, and VEGFA, were significantly potentiated (Figure A). To verify these findings, we performed qPCR to examine the expression of core genes in the pathway. Compared with the Ctrl group, POSTN mRNA was significantly downregulated, while TGFB1 and FGF2 mRNA were significantly up-regulated in the 6-ClNA treatment group, supporting the pattern revealed by CellChat analysis (Figure B). Network interrogation revealed that 6-ClNA amplified the interaction frequency and intensity between ECs and other subpopulations, while the communicative capacity of SMCs and pericytes was diminished (Figure C,D). Ligand–receptor interaction analysis further revealed that when ECs acted as the ligand source, signaling through ANGPT1–TEK and WNT2B axes was substantially weakened, whereas communication via the PDGFA–PDGFRA axis was reinforced. In contrast, when ECs acted as signal recipients, interaction of DLK1–Notch4 and PGF–VEGFR1 was observed (Figure E).

Given that the vascular microenvironment is tightly regulated by hemodynamic signals, expression profiles of blood-flow-sensitive genes were also analyzed using single-cell transcriptomic data. We found that treatment of hBVOs with 6-ClNA resulted in a significant upregulation of the mitochondrial homeostasis gene GRN, whereas endothelial homeostasis regulatory gene PDGFD and flow shear stress sensor SMAD1 were downregulated, indicative of repressed hemodynamic signal transduction (Figure F,G). Collectively, 6-ClNA exposure may drive pathological vascular remodeling by blocking the hemodynamic-related transcriptional regulatory network and disrupting the endothelium–matrix signaling interaction.

6-ClNA Deviates the Developmental Trajectory of hBVOs

Lastly, pseudotime analysis was employed to assess the developmental trajectories of the hBVOs. In control organoids, VPs underwent differentiation toward endothelial and mural cell lineages. Although the overall trajectory structure was largely preserved after 6-ClNA exposure, the distribution of cells across the branches was markedly altered, indicating the disruption of dynamic transitions between developmental states (Figure A). Density mapping of VPs demonstrated significant accumulation of cells in mid- to late pseudotime stages upon 6-ClNA exposure, suggesting a partial blockade of progression from early to mature states (Figure B,C). Gene kinetics analysis showed that matrix-associated genes, including COL1A1, LUM, and PDGFRA, progressively increased along pseudotime in controls but were downregulated in 6-ClNA-exposed organoids, indicating impaired extracellular matrix production (Figure D,E,G). Similarly, the proliferation marker MKI67 exhibited high expression in early pseudotime in the control and gradually declined, whereas its decline was more rapid in 6-ClNA-treated hBVOs, reflecting reduced proliferative potential (Figure F). Collectively, these results indicate that 6-ClNA significantly affects the developmental trajectory of the hBVOs.

Discussion

In this study, taking advantage of a humanized organoid model, we investigated the molecular toxicity of the NNIs metabolite 6-ClNA on early vascular development at the single-cell resolution. Our results showed that even though 6-ClNA caused no change in the morphology of hBVOs, it reshaped its cellular composition and induced significant cell-type-specific molecular alterations. Specifically, 6-ClNA exposure disrupted EC homeostasis and drove abnormal SMC hyperplasia, as evidenced by the corresponding structural changes in the aortas of exposed mice. Mechanistically, transcriptomic analysis revealed cell-type-specific perturbations in vascular signaling, energy metabolism, and protein homeostasis, as well as altered differentiation trajectories of VP cells. Collectively, these data provide a single-cell resolution toxicity profile of 6-ClNA in vascular development.

First, in normal hBVOs, the ratio of ECs to SMCs is maintained at approximately 1:3.27 at day 21, which is essential for maintaining vascular stability and functional integrity. Interestingly, 6-ClNA treatment resulted in a significant reduction in the proportion of ECs accompanied by an increase in SMCs, suggesting that this compound may have disrupted the dynamic balance between endothelium/SMCs, a phenomenon similar to the changes in cell composition observed in a variety of vascular lesions. By further distinguishing arterial and venous ECs by immunostaining, we found that 6-ClNA selectively damaged arterial ECs (SOX17+) but had little effect on venous ECs (NR2F2+). This finding is significant, indicating that the action of 6-ClNA is cell subtype specific and may lead to arterial endothelial dysfunction by interfering with key pathways of arterial differentiation such as Notch or BMP signaling. At the same time, TGF-β signaling is a key pathway to promote SMC proliferation and phenotype switching, and its activation is highly consistent with the SMC proliferation observed in the 6-ClNA treatment group. In addition, the significant changes in TCA cycle and glycerophospholipid metabolism suggest that the reprogramming of energy metabolism and membrane structure metabolism may be another important mechanism of 6-ClNA-induced endothelial dysfunction. Previous studies have shown that metabolic disorders of ECs, especially mitochondrial dysfunction, directly affect their barrier function and vascular homeostasis. Notably, arterial ECs in the present study were more sensitive to 6-ClNA than venous ECs, which may be related to their differences in the origin of differentiation, metabolic profile, or epigenetic background. Future studies can further focus on the role of the SOX17 regulatory network in 6-ClNA-mediated endothelial injury and whether it leads to the loss of cell function by affecting key metabolic enzymes.

Although neonicotinoids have been shown to disrupt protein homeostasis by inducing endoplasmic reticulum stress and changing protein conformation, the effects of their key metabolite 6-ClNA on vascular development and related protein homeostasis remain largely unknown. Using single-cell sequencing analysis, we demonstrated that 6-ClNA can effectively disrupt endothelial homeostasis, metabolic homeostasis, and protein homeostasis in hBVOs. In fibroblasts, the enrichment of PI3K–AKT and oxidative phosphorylation pathways implies a transition toward an energetically activated, matrix-producing phenotype, aligning with early fibrotic remodeling and fibroblast activation. , Such metabolic rewiring is typically linked to increased extracellular matrix synthesis and elevated contractility, both of which promote stromal stiffening and compromise vascular integrity. Concurrently, proliferative progenitor cells showed upregulated protein processing and trafficking programs, whereas pericytes demonstrated enhanced ECM–receptor interactions, indicating a broad activation of proteostatic and adhesion stress responses. Together, these coordinated alterations indicate that 6-ClNA exposure disrupts endothelial–stromal crosstalk, disturbing the delicate balance between vascular stability and matrix remodeling. Notably, the transcriptional signatures induced by 6-ClNA overlap partially with those observed in diabetic vasculopathy. This convergence highlights shared molecular mechanisms between metabolically and chemically induced vascular injury, , proposing that environmental toxicants like 6-ClNA might mimic or aggravate diabetic-like vascular dysfunction via common stress and signaling pathways.

Previous studies have highlighted the importance of endothelial–stromal communication in coordinating angiogenesis and maintaining vascular homeostasis through finely tuned signaling networks involving Notch, VEGFA, TGF-β, and Wnt pathways. − Disruption of these pathways has been associated with endothelial dysfunction and pathological vascular remodeling, as exemplified in diabetic vasculopathy. , In line with these findings, our cell–cell communication analysis reveals that 6-ClNA profoundly perturbs endothelial–stromal crosstalk in hBVOs by extensively rewiring the vascular signaling. Notably, 6-ClNA treatment leads to the activation of canonical pro-angiogenic pathways (e.g., Notch, VEGFA, TGF-β, and FGF), likely reflecting a compensatory response to endothelial stress or injury, while concurrently suppressing developmental pathways (e.g., Wnt and EGF) that are essential for coordinated angiogenesis and extracellular matrix assembly. The pronounced activation of the DLK1–Notch4 axis mirrors the pathological Notch signaling dynamics characteristic of diabetic vasculopathy, suggesting a convergent mechanism underlying vascular dysfunction. In addition, the upregulation of the PGF–VEGFR1 axis may further promote aberrant angiogenic responses through alternative signaling routes, whereas the attenuation of protective pathways such as ANGPT1–TEK and WNT2B highlights the loss of endothelial–stromal cues necessary for vascular stabilization and barrier integrity. , Collectively, these findings suggest that 6-ClNA orchestrates a multifaceted disruption of vascular signalingincluding Notch, PDGF, and VEGFRthereby compromising the coordinated crosstalk between endothelial and mural cells and ultimately impairing the structural and functional integrity of the vascular network.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis has been widely employed to delineate the gene expression dynamics that drive vascular lineage specification. Extensive work has established that during normal vascular development, progenitor cells progressively bifurcate into mural and endothelial branches, with mural-associated gene programs becoming prominent at later stages to facilitate vessel stabilization and extracellular matrix assembly. , Building upon this framework, we reconstructed the differentiation trajectories in hBVOs and observed that prolonged 6-ClNA exposure profoundly altered early progenitor fate decisions. Specifically, 6-ClNA exposure progenitors displayed reduced proliferative capacity and downregulated expression of matrix- and maturation-related genes, indicative of a state of premature differentiation arrest. This deviation from the expected developmental trajectory implies that 6-ClNA disrupts key transcriptional programs essential for lineage commitment and structural organization, ultimately compromising the coordinated maturation of the vascular network. Taken together, although the focus of this study was 6-ClNA, the model suggests that the activity of environmental pollutant metabolites cannot be ignored; therefore, future studies should systematically evaluate whether other nicotinic metabolites have similar vascular toxicity and use high-resolution multiomics methods to thoroughly analyze the differential sensitivity of different cell subtypes.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study constructed a coherent toxicity model to elucidate the cascade of mechanisms by which 6-ClNA impairs vascular development. 6-ClNA directly interferes with the core signaling pathways and energy-protein homeostasis in VPs and ECs and induces cell-autonomous damage. In turn, by reprogramming the intercellular communication network, this damage effect is amplified and transmitted to surrounding cells (such as SMCs), which eventually leads to an imbalance in cell population composition and an overall deviation in the tissue development trajectory.
