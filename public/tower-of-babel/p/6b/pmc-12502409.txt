Comment on “the public perspective” and “a public perspective” – a PPI professional’s reflection on the tension around diversifying public perspectives in public involvement
Authors: William Lammons
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe theoretical basis of the terminology and language used around patient and public involvement (PPI) and related methodologies is important. Though significant efforts have been made to understand identities and perspectives as more varied, diverse, and plural phenomena, it is important to continuously critique and analyse the language and concepts that we, as PPI professionals, researchers, and public collaborators use throughout our work and activities. In this comment piece, I use conceptual thinking and creative writing to reflect on my professional experience working in PPI to think critically and theoretically about a commonly used phrase in research and PPI spaces, the singular articles and nouns of the phrases, “the public perspective” and “a public perspective.”Keywords: PPIE, Terminology, PPIE professionals

Introduction – the tensions around mandatory PPI

The National Institute of Health and Care Research (NIHR) has been requiring researchers to conduct patient and public involvement (PPI) since it existed as 1993’s NHS Research and Development Programme [1–4]. Yet, using funder requirements to build PPI continues to create tensions for the PPI professionals charged with negotiating the space between institutional mandates and experiences of members of the public [5, 6]. In this comment piece, I use literary elements, theoretical concepts, and professional experiences of working in PPI to illustrate, analyse, and evoke this tension between public perspectives and satisfying institutional mandates to represent them.

Professionals and researchers agree that these institutional requirements from the NIHR and others generate more PPI activities, however, professionals and researchers have also written critiques of mandatory PPI that highlight its complexities [5–7]. The most common critique focuses on the fact that more PPI does not always yield genuine PPI. This means that PPI can be done in a “tick box” or “tokenised” fashion that leaves public collaborators feeling demoralised, ignored, or only involved to satisfy requirements [5–7]. Russell et al. argue that institutionalising PPI through such mandates has likewise made it difficult for researchers and professionals to critique PPI activities and principles [7].

One of the most common ways that I experience this tension around satisfying institutional requirements for PPI is through every-day and often overlooked refrains calling me to help facilitate PPI. I have rendered these as fragments in the sections below to preserve anonymity and show how easily they can come up in conversation. I have also repeated these refrains to illustrate these statements’ persistence and cultivate tension and anxiety for the reader at having to consistently face statements that point to this tension. Finally, I have used long lists in series to illustrate the variety and diversity of PPI perspectives [8].

Reflections on the problem – wrestling with the singular of “a PPI perspective” and “the PPI perspective”

After 4.5 years of working as a patient and public involvement lead, I have heard it more times than I can count. I have said it, myself. I have caught myself saying it while writing this critique of saying it.

Let’s hear the PPI perspective on this?

Let’s make sure we have the PPI perspective on this.

From a PPI perspective…

The PPI perspective would say…

Will, what’s the PPI perspective on that?

We want to make sure we have a PPI perspective on this.

Will, can you give Us the PPI perspective?

We have had a PPI perspective on this.

We want to make sure we have considered the PPI perspective.

We need a PPI perspective on this.

We have the PPI perspective.

From a PPI perspective…

A public perspective

The public perspective

The PPI perspective

A patient perspective.

The patient perspective

A PPI perspective

Researchers say it out loud in planning meetings, project meetings, governance meetings, and coordinating meetings. Admin professionals write it in emails. Professors say it in departmental meetings. They describe it as “the public perspective” or “a public perspective” – always singular in number, as if there were one PPI perspective. As if there were one public and patient perspective. As if one person’s perspective could suffice. As if one person’s perspective could represent all those outside of research. As if one person’s perspectives were static and unchanging. A PPI perspective. The PPI perspective.

References to public and patient perspectives in the singular, “a” or “the” patient/public perspective are currently less visible in guidance from the UK’s PPI standard bearer, the NIHR. For instance, many NIHR descriptions mention “groups,” “patients,” and “perspectives.” However, this has not completely changed, such as in one of the NIHR’s chief guidance on PPI, Briefing notes for researchers - public involvement in NHS, health and social care research. This guidance states:

“This gives a broader perspective to the review process, by considering the issues that are important from a public and patient perspective,” [emphasis my own; 9],

“Involving members of the public in managing research can help to ensure that:

• a public and societal perspective is maintained throughout an individual project or a programme of work,” [emphasis my own; 9].

We, as PPI professionals and members of the public, increasingly hear about the need to involve “community groups,” “underserved groups,” “grassroots organisations,” “communities” – plural nouns, various groups within a sea of groups, groups covering the same condition that differ from other groups covering the same condition, composed of various individuals, individuals with different views, individuals with contrasting and conflicting views, “choirs” –collections of people who speak together in similar ways with individually different voices, again and again, forever and ever, all the way down.

As PPI Leads, we often work alone, in isolation, trudging ahead according to the best practices that we have gleaned from professional experiences, professional mistakes, trainings, collaborators’ feedback, and PPI colleagues’ guidance. Yet, in my experience, anxiety often comes in to challenge my decisions, thoughts, and practices. In writing this piece, I can feel this anxiety clawing back and arguing with me, saying, “This is just semantics, Will.”

I argue against this, however, saying that Rowland et al. show that how we think about and conceptualize PPI does matter beyond “just semantics” [10]. How we think about PPI shapes how we evaluate it, how we measure it, how we actually involve people, how we treat them during activities and research, how much we involve people, and what things people are even willing to do in involvement [10].

For example, if we only need a perspective, i.e. one perspective from the public, then we are missing a broader swathe of experiences and stories. There are well known compelling examples around health inequalities that are routinely under-studied, under-researched, minimised, and even silenced. For example, Black women in the UK have 4x the risk of dying in childbirth than that of white women [11]. Asian women have 2x the risk [10]. As an additional example, experiences of dementia are becoming more understood, but the unique needs and experiences of trans peoples who have dementia are extremely under-researched [12].

If we only need a perspective, i.e. one perspective from the public, then any perspective will do. This one perspective might not give insights into health inequalities. Staley et al. have pointed out that above all, PPI activities should include groups, people, and individuals relevant to an individual project [13]. They offer a striking example in which an individual with no lived experience of mental health conditions served as a mental health study’s PPI collaborator [13]. This person suggested the research team remove ‘recovery’ from all project materials, as they incorrectly believed a common myth that ‘no one…recovers from a mental illness,’ a decision that could have had major consequences for the project [13]. Staley et al. argue that “members of the public have the same gaps in their knowledge and are just as likely to make the wrong assumptions as researchers,” [13]. Examples like this show that “the PPI perspective” and “a PPI perspective” rely on an understanding of “the public” as a sort of homogenous non-academic “other” that lacks formal training or professional experience in research. A non-researcher. A lay person. An untrained person. Untainted by doctoral or master’s training. Someone who can sense-check the research questions.

Phrases like “the PPI perspective” suggest that one person shares so much with the larger body of people outside research, the greater public, that one public perspective will suffice to fulfil the PPI requirement. Thomas Gieryn terms this act of separating different types of knowledge, such as research perspective from public perspective, “boundary-work,” [14, 15]. He argues that “drawing boundaries” or the cultural act of defining what counts and does not count as scientific knowledge is a fundamental way that researchers have validated and institutionalised science’s hegemony and unique ability to explain the world [9, 10]. Importantly, to define and draw boundaries around “science” and “research” involves casting and defining other forms of knowledge as unscientific “others” [14, 15]. The scientific perspective vs. the public perspective. The institutional perspective | the PPI perspective. The academic perspective and the lay perspective. The researcher and the “man on the street”. The ivory tower against the corner store.

The compelling thing about PPI is that it represents an attempt to elevate unscientific forms of knowledge and understanding, “the public and patient,” to the levels of science and researchers. But, elevating public perspectives against research can also maintain the boundary between both sides – researchers on one side, public/patients on the other. The institutional perspective | the PPI perspective. Meanwhile, PPI professionals, like me, stand in the boundary-lands, negotiating across it, negotiating with it, and even arguing against it.

Braun and Könninger argue that, like science, PPI activities (focus groups, public panels, advisory groups) are also cultural acts of “constructing the public,” and drawing boundaries to define the public [16]. Essentially, the people in the room or on the Zoom call who are not researchers or staff rhetorically construct and represent all of “the public,” even if there are public perspectives not represented on the call [16].

“Thanks, now it’d be nice to hear the PPI perspective on this.” “Let’s hear from the other side of the table.” “What would a public perspective say about this?”

However, outside of these activities, there are multiple “publics” which are dynamic – they change and shift with time and context. Rowland et al. have also noticed this tendency for researchers to discuss PPI perspectives in the singular [9]. They argue that we need to think critically about the meanings of phrases like “the patient perspective,” and, for the sake of argument, “public perspectives” [9]. In addition to singularity, they argue that “the patient perspective” is one that is “embodied,” or personified and exemplified in a person’s being [15]. This “brings attention to vulnerability,” or one that can leave a patient objectified or voiceless through the PPI process [9]. It is “fragile,” or created by a particular context which could shift and change into something else [9]. These three conceptual elements, embodied, vulnerable, and fragile, illustrate a public perspective as potentially changing at any given time, showing that even one person’s perspectives can change over the course of their treatment, life, or condition.

For example, a person’s perspective can shift through a health-related, cultural, or sociological experience – they might lose their hearing due to medical negligence and become a vocal advocate for accessibility. Someone who becomes a caregiver for a friend or family member that was suddenly diagnosed with dementia can acquire a much greater understanding and empathy for people with dementia and people who serve as carers. A young man’s terminal cancer diagnosis can slowly change his sceptical views of AI in clinical practice to become extremely supportive of big data analysis. What it means to live in Shoreditch can change over the course of 50 years. Communities that were seen as immigrants can become associated with British-ness. People can even form their ideas and perspectives during PPI activities or upon reflecting after them. Again and again. Forever and ever. All the way down.

There have been pushes from the NIHR to encourage ways of working and thinking that focus on plurality and multiplicity of identities, such as equity, diversity, and inclusion (EDI), and intersectionality [17, 18]. Intersectionality and EDI reflect endeavours, programmes, projects, ways of thinking, and identities which are often stacked, combined, overlapping, shifting, mixed, and messy; some identities that amplify others, multiply others, stack with inequalities, amplify inequalities, amplify and multiply experiences of discrimination, again and again, forever and ever, all the way down.

Difficulties – why it’s hard to move beyond “a PPI perspective,” and the consequences of not doing so

There is a difficult reality underlying the singularity of “the” and “a” PPI perspective – in recruiting members of the public for PPI activities, someone’s voice and experiences will always be left out. Research and PPI professionals of all stripes all have limits on how many people they can involve in research due to the logistics of mapping and identifying communities and people for a project, recruiting people relevant to a project, balancing other work responsibilities, their own knowledge limits, their own capacity limits. Again and again. Forever and ever. All the way down.

Michel-Rolph Trouillot, through his work on power’s role in writing and telling history, building on Michel Foucault, has shown that those of us in the business of working with stories and experiences can only tell a limited number of stories and engage with a limited number of experiences [19]. As my mentors recited to me, ‘You have a finite amount of time.’ ‘You get to tell 1 story.’ ‘You can’t do it all.’ We literally cannot involve them all, and if we could, we could not apply and share all their perspectives. This is not an excuse. It is a real obstacle. It is a real obstacle for all of us. Especially me.

There is a soft space where marginalised, excluded, and minoritized peoples get labelled by some working in research and PPI as “hard to reach,” due in part to the difficulties of involving people and the natural limitations described above [19]. However, this phrase and its use represents some of the greatest risks to PPI work. It reifies conventional hierarchies of exclusion and inequality. It strengthens the “boundary” between research and “the public.” The greatest rebuttals to this claim in academic literature come from Black Caribbean women in the UK quoted in Darko’s Engaging Black and Minority Ethnic Groups in Health Research’ [20].

They state:

‘We are not hard to reach, what did we do wrong – we are here, they [service providers] are not coming to us, to understand and know us?’ [20].

‘We are known as the hard to reach group … but that is not the case, your [health services] are just not reaching us and understanding our needs as Caribbean people,’ [20].

As there are limitations to how many perspectives we can hear and engage, there is power in the process of choosing those we do engage with. It is easier for some to ignore those that some perceive to be “hard to reach.” This requires less time, resources, networking, trust-building, relationship-building, vulnerability, accommodations, challenging normal working modes, and mistake-making. Doing this well requires research teams to work differently than they would, differently than institutions often practically want them to, pay people in different ways, create ideas in different ways, and work against the hierarchical ways in which universities operate.

Recommendations – How we can work towards a diversity of perspectives

To deal with these tensions and difficulties, we must accept a certain amount of them, instead of working to eliminate them. After almost 5 years of working in PPI in the UK – coaching researchers, delivering trainings, making mistakes, drinking coffee, making more mistakes, drinking more coffee, apologising, studying, updating my methods, taking walking breaks, thinking critically about my own best practices, drinking decaf coffee, debating best practices, and lots of reflection, I have compiled the following points of recommendation on how to best address and move beyond the singular in PPI work – both in language and practice:

Debate this issue

Open discussion is one of the key techniques in PPI, and this issue can benefit greatly from open debate and discussion amongst those involved – PPI professionals, researchers, and members of the public. What defines “the public,” what their perspectives are, how we involve them, and how they involve themselves are dynamic and shift over time. In debating these issues and concepts openly, we can co-create them together and achieve more shared understanding. This will ultimately, reduce the role of tokenism in involving members of the public and the ways in which these ideas are often imposed on them.

Continuously expand to collaborate with relevant peoples

The neighbourhoods and peoples affected by a research topic change regularly. Conduct regular audits of the people affected by your work to understand who are relevant for your project [12]. Reach out to peoples from those experiences, backgrounds, areas, or identities, not only to recruit them, but to get to know them and start relationships with them [12].

Focus on relationship-building

Having regular genuine reflections, conversations, discussions, and meetings in community-focused places about what you need help on and what people are interested in working on together is the best vaccine against tokenism [12].

Meet people where they are at – both in place and practice

Talk to people about what they need on a regular basis – accommodations, formatting for documents, more explanation of research principles, phone calls instead of Zoom meetings, Zoom meetings instead of Teams meetings, or WhatsApp chats instead of emails.

Go to events in local areas, especially to meet people and introduce yourself to groups already focused on a specific issue, such as mental health, caring, or healthy eating. These can build trust and show you important priorities and means of working on the ground. Many people affected by specific conditions, such as Black British, Black African, and Black Caribbean communities are already organizing around conditions like sickle cell anaemia [12].

Meet people for check-in chats at places local and convenient to them – coffee shops, barbershops, community centres, libraries, or places of worship [12].

Put time and resources towards involving diverse perspectives

This takes time and money. When we spend these to achieve these goals, the research we produce has greater impacts for all – researchers and public(s), alike [20, 21].

Accept the limitations

We have a finite amount of time. We cannot tell all stories. We will never reach all perspectives. The perspectives and peoples that we can involve will always be limited. Someone will always be left out.

New communities will form in the UK. New health inequalities will emerge. New pandemics will come forth, and with them, new types of vaccine hesitancy. It will suddenly become clear how we have excluded key peoples and voices from our work for decades.

However, the existence of limitations and dynamically diverse perspectives cannot keep us from working to reach more of them. You cannot stop taking baths because you will just get dirty again. We need to continuously think critically and work critically in collaboration with various publics, groups, communities, individuals, teams, neighbourhoods to understand who we are missing, build relationships with them, and try to work together, again and again, for ever and ever, all the way down.

There is not a PPI perspective. There are hundreds. There might be thousands. They overlap. They are excluded. They sing in harmonious choirs. They cacophonously disagree. They talk over each other. They compete for the space that research and science open up for them.

In the wake of vaccine hesitancy, marathon NHS wait lists, and a continuing cost of living crisis, we need to make sure that as many publics as possible know that the door is open.
