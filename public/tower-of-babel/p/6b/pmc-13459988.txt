Constitutive mTORC1 activation in skeletal muscle increases inflammation but is not sufficient to impair glucose tolerance
Authors: Erik D. Marchant, Hanna Kalenta, Sean P. Kilroe, Jacquelyn May, Wenjun Z. Martini, Korri S. Weldon, Zhao Lai, Blake B. Rasmussen
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Insulin resistance in skeletal muscle is considered a primary driver in the development of type II diabetes (1). Evidence for the causal role of insulin resistance in diabetes development is found in healthy offspring of diabetic individuals, who exhibit insulin resistance in skeletal muscle before displaying a change in fasting glucose or glycosylated hemoglobin (2, 3). Furthermore, results from multiple randomized clinical trials, including in older individuals, suggest that pioglitazone, one of the only known insulin-sensitizing drugs, reduces the occurrence of diabetes in patients with insulin resistance and prediabetes (4–6). As such, directly targeting insulin resistance might be an effective strategy for reducing the occurrence of diabetes. However, to better target insulin resistance directly, it is necessary to determine the molecular mechanisms that drive it.

Advanced age is one of the most significant risk factors for various chronic diseases, including insulin resistance and diabetes (7). To delay the onset of various age-related diseases, including diabetes, it may be most effective to target the common mechanisms that drive aging generally (8). Aberrant mTORC1 signaling is known to occur in skeletal muscle in both insulin resistance and aging (9, 10). Furthermore, mTORC1 signaling is associated not only with the development of insulin resistance and diabetes (11), but also with muscle atrophy (12), cardiovascular disease (13, 14), and cognitive decline (15). However, whether hyperactive mTORC1 signaling in skeletal muscle can drive insulin resistance is equivocal. Deletion of Rps6kb1 (encoding ribosomal protein S6 kinase 1; S6K1) in skeletal muscle, which is a downstream target activated by mTORC1, improves insulin signaling in skeletal muscle and is protective against high-fat feeding (16). The mTORC1-inhibiting drug rapamycin has also been shown in culture to improve insulin signaling (17). In contrast, rapamycin is known to cause insulin resistance in mice, independent of its ability to increase longevity (18). However, the insulin-resistance effect of rapamycin seems to primarily be in the liver and to be driven by the inhibition of mTORC2 and not mTORC1 (18). In a mouse model of hyperactive mTORC1 (Tsc1 knockout) within skeletal muscle, mice have been reported to become more insulin sensitive (19) and in other cases to become more insulin resistant (20), further adding complexity to the role of mTORC1 signaling in insulin resistance. One limitation of this previous work in Tsc1 knockout mice for testing whether mTORC1 activation could be a driver of insulin resistance is the use of a germline knockout, which could alter blood glucose regulation beginning in development.

GATOR1 is a protein complex that is known to negatively regulate mTORC1 in response to amino acid depletion (21). However, the nutrient-sensing pathway of mTORC1 activity regulation has not been tested to determine whether GATOR1 inhibition, and thus mTORC1 activation, can drive the development of insulin resistance. We hypothesized that constitutively active mTORC1 signaling induced by Depdc5 knockout (which encodes a subunit of GATOR1, DEPDC5) in skeletal muscle would induce insulin resistance and impair blood glucose control. To test this hypothesis, we knocked out the Depdc5 gene in the skeletal muscle of adult female and male mice. We have previously reported that this model exhibits elevated mTORC1 signaling and mild hypertrophy, with no improvements in skeletal muscle function (22).

METHODS

Animals

Muscle-specific Depdc5 KO mice and floxed controls were generated as previously described (22). Mice were housed on a 12:12 h light:dark cycle. All procedures were approved by the UT Health San Antonio Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. At 6 mo of age, all mice (including floxed controls) were administered tamoxifen for 5 consecutive days (75 mg/kg, dissolved in sunflower oil, administered via IP injection). Following administration of tamoxifen, mice were fed either a control synthetic diet (Research Diets, D12450K) or a high-fat, high-sucrose diet consisting of 45% fat and 17% sucrose [referred to as Western diet (WD); Research Diets, D12451] and maintained on the diet for 12 wk. Body Composition and Functional Assessments

Following the 12 wk, body composition was assessed via EchoMRI (EchoMRI, Houston, TX) in conscious, unanesthetized mice. In addition, mice were subjected to a battery of functional assessments, including grip strength, treadmill running, and rotarod, which have all been described previously (23).

Oral Glucose Tolerance Test and Glycosylated Hemoglobin

At 7:00 AM, on the day of the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), mice were singly housed in the absence of food but with access to water ad libitum. A small nick was made in the tail vein to draw enough blood (∼ 1 µL) for glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) analysis (Siemens Clinical Analyzer). Mice were then fasted until 12:00 PM (a total of 5 h). At this point, blood was collected again from the tail vein to measure fasting blood glucose (∼1 µL; Alphatrak 3 handheld glucose monitor) and fasting blood insulin (5 µL; Crystal Chem #62100). A bolus of glucose was then administered orally via gavage (1 mg/kg lean mass; administered as a 20% solution), and blood was sampled periodically over the course of 2 h to measure blood glucose. Area under the curve during the OGTT was calculated using the trapezoidal method.

High-Resolution Respirometry and Fluorometry in Isolated Mitochondria

Gastrocnemius muscles from the right leg of each mouse were minced with scissors in ice-cold mitochondrial isolation media [300 mM sucrose, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM EGTA, 10 mM Tris base, and 0.5 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA, fatty acid-free), pH = 7.4]. The minced tissue was then homogenized on ice for ∼15 s using an IKA T-10 Basic Ultra Turrax homogenizer set to its lowest speed. The homogenate was centrifuged at 800 rcf for 10 min (4°C). The supernatant was transferred to a new tube, centrifuged at 800 rcf for 10 min (4°C), and again transferred to a new tube. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 12,000 rcf for 10 min (4°C), and the pellet was washed in mitochondrial isolation media without BSA. The resuspended pellet was centrifuged again at 12,000 rcf for 10 min (4°C) and finally resuspended in 150 µL of mitochondrial isolation media. About 60 µL of resuspended mitochondria were pipetted in duplicate into 2-mL chambers of a high-resolution respirometer and fluorometer (Oroboros, O2k) containing respiration buffer D (105 mM K-MES, 30 mM KCl, 10 mM KH2PO4, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, and 2.5 g/L BSA; pH = 7.2) and set to a temperature of 37°C. The oxygen content was kept within a range of 50–200 µM. The remaining mitochondrial suspension was flash frozen for subsequent protein quantification using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) methodology (ThermoFisher). Respiration and H2O2 production were measured simultaneously in one chamber, and respiration and membrane potential were measured in the other, both under a range of substrate conditions and energy states, following a protocol adapted from Fisher-Wellman et al. (24). The following titrations were made to simultaneously measure respiratory function and H2O2 production: Amplex Ultrared (10 µM) and horseradish peroxidase (1 U/mL) to measure H2O2 production; glutamate (10 mM), malate (2 mM), octanoylcarnitine (0.5 mM), and succinate (10 mM) to measure LEAK respiration and maximal H2O2 production; ATP (5 mM) and phosphocreatine (1 mM) to measure maximal coupled respiration; 4 steps of phosphocreatine (5 mM each) to stimulate a range of physiologically relevant energy states; cytochrome C (10 µM, quality control step); carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) (0.5 µM steps) to uncouple respiration; antimycin A (2.5 µM) to inhibit respiration and calculate background oxygen consumption. In addition, ascorbate (10 mM) and N,N,N′,N′-tetramethyl-p-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride (TMPD) (0.5 mM) were added to stimulate maximal complex IV (CIV) activity, and sodium azide (100 mM) to inhibit CIV. All rates of respiration H2O2 production were normalized to total protein content.

We also measured mitochondrial respiration in permeabilized white gastrocnemius muscle because this muscle has low basal mitochondrial content but has the capacity to increase mitochondrial content under various interventions (25, 26). As we hypothesized that Depdc5 KO would increase mitochondrial content, we collected fiber bundles from white gastrocnemius muscle, which (∼2–3 mg each) were gently separated with fine-tipped forceps in ice-cold biopsy preservation solution (BIOPS buffer) (60 mM K-MES, 35 mM KCl, 7.23 mM K2EGTA, 2.77 mM CaK2EGTA, 20 mM imidazole, 0.5 mM DTT, 20 mM taurine, 5.7 mM ATP, 15 mM PCr, and 6.56 mM MgCl2; pH = 7.1) and allowed to permeabilize for 30 min in BIOPS buffer supplemented with 50 µg/mL saponin. Following permeabilization, muscle fibers were washed for 15 min in respiration buffer D (105 mM K-MES, 30 mM KCl, 10 mM KH2PO4, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, and 2.5 g/L BSA; pH = 7.2). Liver homogenates were prepared by mincing tissue (1 mg tissue per 10 µL buffer) with scissors in ice-cold mitochondrial isolation media and homogenizing on ice for ∼15 s using an IKA T-10 Basic Ultra Turrax homogenizer set to its lowest speed. About 30 µL of homogenate was used for respiration, and the remainder of the homogenate was saved for protein quantification using BCA methods. For both permeabilized fibers and liver homogenates, the same titration protocol was used to measure oxygen consumption: octanoylcarnitine (0.5 mM), malate (0.1 mM), ATP (5 mM), and phosphocreatine (1 mM) to measure maximal fatty acid oxidation; cytochrome c quality control; glutamate (10 mM) and malate (2 mM) to stimulate complex I; succinate (10 mM) to stimulate complex II, 4 steps of phosphocreatine (5 mM each), CCCP, and antimycin A. White gastrocnemius respiration was normalized to wet tissue weight (mg), and liver respiration to protein content, measured via BCA.

Western Blotting

Quadriceps muscles were homogenized at a 5% concentration in ice-cold lysis buffer (50 mM Tris HCl, 250 mM mannitol, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 1% Triton-X 100, 1 mM DTT) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (ThermoFisher, 78442), using a glass-on-glass homogenizer. Samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 16,000 rcf and 4°C. Total protein concentration was measured in supernatants using bicinchoninic acid methodology (ThermoFisher, 23225) and diluted to a protein concentration of 2 mg/mL in Laemmli buffer. Proteins were separated via electrophoresis on 4%–15% polyacrylamide gels (Bio-Rad, 5671084) in Tris/Glycine/SDS electrophoresis buffer (Bio-Rad, 1610772EDU). Thirty micrograms of total protein were loaded in each well. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes at 100 V for 60 min in ice-cold transfer buffer (Bio-Rad, 1610771EDU). Following the protein transfer, the membranes were stained for total protein to ensure equal loading using Ponceau S BioReagent (ThermoFisher, A40000279). Each membrane was incubated overnight at 4°C in 5% BSA in Tris-buffered saline, 0.1% TWEEN 20 (TBST), followed by a 4-h application of primary antibody at room temperature (RT) in 1% BSA in TBST. For a list of antibodies used, see Supplemental Table S1. Membranes were washed in TBST and subsequently incubated for 60 min at RT in horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked secondary antibody (Supplemental Table S1), diluted in 1% BSA in TBST. Membranes were again washed in TBST, incubated for 5 min at RT in SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (ThermoFisher, #34094), and imaged with various exposure durations (FluorChem M, Protein Simple). Optical density of each band was assessed using ImageJ, and all band values were normalized to total protein measured via Ponceau S staining.

mRNA Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from gastrocnemius muscle using Trizol reagent (ThermoFisher) and quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. The quality of total RNAs was checked by Agilent Tape Station (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA), and only RNA with RNA quality number (RQN) > 7 was used for subsequent mRNA sequencing (mRNA-Seq) library preparation and sequencing. Approximately 500 ng of total RNA was used for stranded mRNA-Seq library preparation by following the NEB Directional mRNA-Seq sample preparation guide (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA). The first step in the workflow involved purifying poly-A-containing mRNA molecules using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Following purification, the mRNA was fragmented into small pieces using divalent cations at an elevated temperature. The cleaved RNA fragments were copied into first-strand cDNA using reverse transcriptase and random primers. These cDNA fragments then went through an end repair process, the addition of a single “A” base, and then ligation of the adapters. The products were then purified and enriched with PCR to create the final RNA-Seq library. Next, RNA-seq libraries were subjected to quantification and subsequent 100 bp paired-read sequencing run with the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. After the sequencing run, demultiplexing with Bcl2fastq2 was used to generate the fastq file, with an average of 30 M reads per sample. Reads were trimmed (trimmomatic) and mapped to the genome using HISAT2. Counts were generated using Feature Counts software and then normalized and assessed for differential expression using DESeq2. Genes were considered differentially expressed if the adjusted P value was less than 0.05 and log2 fold-change was greater than 0.5.

Immunohistochemistry

The methods of immunohistochemistry and imaging of skeletal muscle macrophages have been previously described (27). In brief, quadriceps muscles embedded in OCT were sectioned (7 µM-thick) on a cryostat and fixed for 8 min in acetone at −20°C. All macrophages were visualized using an anti-F4/80 primary antibody, and M2-like macrophages were visualized using an anti-CD206 antibody. Slides were imaged at ×40 using a Nikon Eclipse Ti inverted microscope. Two separate images were obtained for each sample and averaged. For more detailed antibody and reagent information, see Supplemental Table S1.

Muscle Metabolite Extraction for TCA Cycle Intermediate Quantification

Stable isotope-labeled tricarboxylic acid (TCA) standards were purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories (MSK-TCA1, 3 Highwood Drive, Tewksbury, MA) and used as internal standards for TCA quantification. The internal standard stock was prepared by adding 1 mL of 50% methanol/water (1:1, vol/vol) to achieve a concentration of 100 µM, according to the manufacturer’s insert. The stock solution was aliquoted and stored at −80°C for analysis. Muscle tissue samples were processed following a modified Bligh-Dyer extraction method as described previously (28). In brief, tissue samples (∼20 mg) were added to 1.5-mL Safe-Lock Eppendorf tubes with steel beads (BioSpec Corp) to cover the tissue. Before extraction, 50 µL of water, 10 µL of internal standard stock, and 300 µL of methanol/water (2:1.8, vol/vol) were added to the tubes, and samples were homogenized using a bead homogenizer in 30 s pulses five times. The homogenates were transferred to 13 × 100 mm glass tubes. The emptied Eppendorf tubes were rinsed twice with 610 µL of methanol/water (2:1.8, vol/vol), and the rinse solutions were pooled into the glass tubes. For extraction, chloroform (800 µL) was added to the glass tubes, vortexed for 2 min, and incubated on ice for 1 h. Afterward, samples were centrifuged at 3,000 g for 10 min at 4°C. The upper aqueous phase was collected into new 13 × 100 mm glass tubes and dried under speed vacuum for LC/MS analysis.

Quantification of TCA intermediates was performed using a HPLC system (SHIMADZU, SCL-40) coupled to a Turbo V Electrospray ionization (ESI) source and a Triple Quad 7500 Mass Spectrometer (SCIEX). Dried tissue extract was dissolved in 20 µL water and 80 µL of acetonitrile. After injection (4 µL), samples were separated by an X Bridge BEH Amide column (2.5 µm, 2.1 × 150 mm, Waters Corporation, Milford, MA). Mobile phase A was prepared with water, acetonitrile, and 2 mM ammonium acetate (190/10/1, vol/vol/vol) and titrated with 35% NH4OH to pH 9.4. Mobile phase B was acetonitrile. All solvents were LC/MS grade. The LC gradient was set as follows: 0–2 min, 10% A; 2–7 min, 25% A; 7–9 min, 30% A; 9–12 min, 50% A; 12–14 min, 75% A; 14–20 min, 100% A; and 21–25 min, 15% A. Samples were run at a flow rate of 0.15 mL/min with column temperature of 25°C. The ESI source parameters were set as follows: curtain gas (CUR) at 40 psi, collision-activated dissociation (CAD) at 9, source temperature at 400°C, nebulizing gas (GS1) at 45 psi, heating gas (GS2) at 70 psi, and ion spray voltage at 5,500 V in positive ionization mode and at −2,000 V in negative ionization mode.

Analysis software from SCIEX was used to operate the LC/MS instrument and acquire data. Ratios of the peak areas of TCA intermediates from tissue to the peak areas of the corresponding isotope-labeled internal standards were used to calculate the concentrations of TCA intermediates.

Statistics

For all measures except mRNA sequencing, statistics were performed using GraphPad Prism. A two-way ANOVA was used to determine main effects of diet and genotype, as well as an interaction between the two variables. If ANOVA produced a P value <0.05, a Tukey-honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc analysis was performed for pairwise comparisons. Unless otherwise specified, each measure is represented as means ± SE. In addition, data were stratified by sex for a secondary analysis.

For RNA sequencing data, all statistics were performed using R software. DESeq2 was used to determine differentially expressed genes. Genes were considered differentially expressed if the adjusted P value was less than 0.05 and the log2 fold-change was greater than 0.5. For Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), pathways were considered differentially regulated if adjusted P values were less than 0.05.

RESULTS

Depdc5 KO Alters mTORC1 Signaling and Autophagy, and WD Increases Fat Mass

At 6 mo of age, we induced Depdc5 knockout (tamoxifen) specifically in the skeletal muscle of female and male mice. Subsequently, mice were placed on either a synthetic control chow [normal chow (NC)] or Western diet (WD; 45% fat, 17% sucrose) for 12 wk (Fig. 1A). Consistent with our previous findings (22, 28, 29), we observed elevated mTORC1 signaling and dysregulated autophagy and mitochondrial dynamics markers in knockout (KO) mice (Fig. 1, B–H and Supplemental Fig. S1, A–H). Western diet increased body mass and fat mass, with no change in lean mass (Fig. 1, I–K, and Supplemental Fig. S2, A and B). Though we were not powered to measure sex differences, we observed that female KO mice experienced an increase in body mass and fat mass, but not female WT mice (Supplemental Fig. S3, A–E). Male mice gained body mass and fat mass regardless of genotype (Supplemental Fig. S4, A–E). In addition, mice fed a WD had reduced grip strength but experienced no change in treadmill running capacity or rotarod performance (Supplemental Fig. S2, C–E).

WD Impairs Blood Glucose Control, but KO Only Mildly Increases Fasting Glucose in Females

Fasting glucose was also increased in WD-fed mice, with no differences between genotypes, although when female mice were assessed separately, only KO mice exhibited an increase in fasting glucose, and not WT (P < 0.01). These findings indicate that female KO mice may be more susceptible to increased fasting glucose when administered a WD than female WT mice, but male mice experience WD-induced increases regardless of genotype (Fig. 2A, Supplemental Fig. S3I and Supplemental Fig. S4I). We did not observe a statistically significant increase in fasting insulin with WD, but there was an increase in Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) with WD and an increase in percent glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), both of which are indicative of dysglycemia induced by WD. Neither HOMA-IR nor HbA1c was affected by genotype (Fig. 2, B and C, and Supplemental Fig. S2F, Supplemental Fig. S3, J–L, and Supplemental Fig. S4, J–L). We then performed oral glucose tolerance tests to determine whether Depdc5 KO alters glucose tolerance under NC- or WD-fed conditions. We observed clear glucose intolerance in WD-fed mice with no clear effect of genotype, consistent in both female and male mice (Fig. 2, D–E, and Supplemental Fig. S3, M–O, and Supplemental Fig. S4, M–O). In a subset of animals, we tested whether insulin signaling is altered by Depdc5 KO and WD. Under insulin-stimulated conditions, KO did not alter phosphorylated insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1) when normalized to total IRS-1 protein (Supplemental Fig. S5, A–E). However, KO reduced both phosphorylated Akt and AS160 when expressed relative to their respective total proteins (Supplemental Fig. S5, F–K). Overall, these findings indicate that insulin signaling is altered in skeletal muscle with Depdc5 KO, but that these changes are insufficient to alter whole body glucose control.

Neither KO Nor WD Affects Mitochondrial Respiration or Content

mTORC1 is a known regulator of mitochondrial function. We hypothesized that mice with hyperactive mTORC1 would have increased mitochondrial content and respiration, but also an increase in mitochondrial ROS production due to inhibited mitochondrial quality control. Interestingly, we did not observe an effect of KO on mitochondrial respiration in isolated mitochondria or permeabilized fibers from gastrocnemius muscle, nor an effect of WD (Fig. 3, A–D, and Supplemental Fig. S6, A–D). In addition, there were no statistical differences in mitochondrial content, measured by Western blot of complexes I-IV of the electron transport chain, nor in ATP synthase (Fig. 3, E–J). We also quantitatively measured TCA cycle metabolites by LC/MS to gain additional insight into mitochondrial function. There was no effect of KO or WD on citrate, isocitrate, succinate, fumarate, or malate (Supplemental Fig. S7). However, KO mice had decreased muscle α-ketoglutarate, which could be indicative of increased flux of α-ketoglutarate to biosynthetic pathways outside the TCA cycle. However, taken together, these findings indicate that mitochondria appear to respire normally in Depdc5 KO mice.

Depdc5 KO Increases Mitochondrial H2O2 Production Capacity

Despite no clear changes in mitochondrial respiration with Depdc5 KO or with WD, we observed an increase in H2O2 production capacity with Depdc5 KO, which was highest in KO animals fed a WD (Fig. 4, A and B). Despite there being an increase in H2O2 production, there was no change in protein carbonyls, a reliable measure of oxidative stress (Fig. 4, C and D).

Depdc5 KO Increases Inflammatory Proteins and Immune-Related Gene Expression and Alters Macrophage Phenotype

We also performed mRNA sequencing to explore potential pathways for further investigation into the effect of Depdc5 KO on skeletal muscle. We observed very few differentially expressed genes between WD- and NC-fed animals, though there were clear decreases in various metabolic pathways, determined by Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (Fig. 5, A and C). However, Depdc5 KO induced large changes in gene expression (Fig. 5B). In addition, GSEA indicated that KO increased gene expression related to cell division and various pathways related to immune function, including monocyte chemotaxis (Fig. 5D). As a result of these findings, we hypothesized that Depdc5 KO might increase inflammatory protein markers and alter macrophage content and phenotype in skeletal muscle. We observed a significant increase in both TNF-α and IL-6 protein expression due to Depdc5 KO and regardless of diet (Fig. 6, A–C). We then measured M1- and M2-like macrophages in skeletal muscle using F4/80 as a marker of all macrophages and CD206 as a marker of M2-like macrophages. We observed no change in total macrophage content, but there was a significant reduction in the M1:M2 ratio in KO mice, but only under NC-fed conditions (Fig. 6, D–G).

DISCUSSION

Our findings suggest that Depdc5 knockout and constitutively active mTORC1 have minimal effect on blood glucose control even if mice are fed a Western diet (WD). Although female mice may be predisposed to some additional weight gain on a WD, associated with a mild increase in fasting blood glucose, glucose tolerance remains largely unaffected by KO status. Despite there being little to no effect of KO status on blood glucose control, KO mice exhibit large changes in autophagy protein expression and an increase in mitochondrial H2O2 production capacity, potentially suggesting impaired mitochondrial quality control. The observed changes in mitochondrial function are associated with increased inflammation markers and an altered macrophage phenotype.

Body Composition and Blood Glucose Control

We hypothesized that KO mice would have impaired glucose tolerance and that this would be exacerbated by WD. We observed a clear increase in fat mass with WD feeding, which was no different between genotypes in male mice, but occurred only in female KO mice and not WT females. Interestingly, the same trend was apparent in fasting blood glucose measures, with WD affecting male KO and WT mice equally, but only causing an increase in fasting blood glucose in female KO mice and not female WT mice. It is well-known that female C57BL/6J mice are more resistant to high-fat diet-induced obesity and metabolic impairment compared with males (30). Our findings provide evidence that mTORC1 activation may predispose female mice to some diet-induced weight gain and to increased fasting glucose. Despite these apparent sex differences, oral glucose tolerance was impaired in all WD-fed mice, regardless of sex or genotype. Taken together, these findings clearly show that WD impairs blood glucose control, but Depdc5 KO has little effect on blood glucose homeostasis, except for a mild increase in fasting glucose in female KO mice fed a WD. Previous reports have suggested that the role of mTORC1 activation on muscle insulin sensitivity and blood glucose control is nuanced. Specifically, Tsc1 knockout (a negative regulator of mTORC1 activity) has been reported to lower fasting glucose and insulin (19, 20), but to impair glucose tolerance in 10-wk-old mice (19) and to improve glucose tolerance in 24-wk-old mice fed a WD (20). These findings are interesting as they suggest overall that constitutively active mTORC1 in this situation likely has a net benefit on blood glucose control, particularly if mice are challenged with a WD. However, one major difference between this Tsc1 KO model and our Depdc5 KO model is that the Tsc1 KO mice are very resistant to diet-induced weight gain (20). In addition, the Tsc1 model previously reported is a germline knockout, which may result in altered metabolic function due to differences in development.

Additional work on the role of mTORC1 activity in muscle insulin resistance and dysglycemia further highlights the complexity of mTORC1 signaling. For example, Rps6kb1 (S6K1) knockout, a downstream target of mTORC1, results in improved glucose tolerance (16), whereas inhibition of mTORC1 activity with rapamycin induces insulin resistance (31). However, it should be noted that rapamycin likely causes insulin resistance due to its effects on mTORC2 and not necessarily because of its inhibition of mTORC1 (18). It has also been proposed that diets high in amino acids may cause insulin resistance via mTORC1 activation (32). However, this notion has been challenged due to the discovery of mTORC1-independent mechanisms of amino acid-driven insulin resistance (33). In addition, some reports suggest that amino acid consumption improves insulin sensitivity, clearly highlighting that the role of mTORC1 activation in muscle metabolism is context-dependent (34).

Mitochondrial Respiration and H2O2 Production

One of the primary reasons that we hypothesized that Depdc5 KO would impair blood glucose control is because mTORC1 activation is known to inhibit autophagy (35). Therefore, we expected to observe reduced mitochondrial turnover, an accumulation of damaged mitochondria, and a subsequent increase in mitochondrial H2O2 production. We report that mitochondrial respiration in skeletal muscle remained largely the same, whether we measured respiration in isolated mitochondria or permeabilized muscle fibers. In addition, TCA cycle metabolite concentrations remained unchanged, except for α-ketoglutarate. However, consistent with our hypothesis, we observed clear alterations in autophagy protein expression in KO mice and an increase in mitochondrial H2O2 production capacity, particularly in KO mice fed a WD. Previous work has shown that increased mitochondrial H2O2 production plays a causal role in high-fat diet-induced insulin resistance (36, 37). We also observed a trend toward increased mitochondrial H2O2 production capacity with WD (P = 0.12). Overall, our results suggest that the KO-induced increase in H2O2 production capacity is not sufficient to impair blood glucose control.

Inflammation and Macrophage Infiltration

An interesting finding from this work is that Depdc5 KO causes large changes in gene expression related to immune cell function and nuclear division, which are associated with increased TNF-α protein expression and altered macrophage phenotype. Previous work suggests that insulin-resistant individuals have elevated expression of TNF-α, CD68, and MCP-1, which correlate well with the severity of insulin resistance (38, 39). Interestingly, some work suggests that skeletal muscle of obese and insulin-resistant individuals results in greater macrophage infiltration (40), but others suggest the opposite (41). In addition, aging is known to cause a shift in macrophage polarization toward an M2 phenotype (42), which is consistent with our finding that mTORC1 activation causes a shift toward an M2 phenotype (Fig. 6F). Although we found no statistically significant interaction between diet and genotype (P = 0.15), it appears that the effect of KO on macrophage phenotype is driven by a trend mainly under NC-fed conditions and not when mice are fed a WD. Given the lack of a statistically significant interaction determined by ANOVA, we cannot conclude that NC and WD mice are different in this measure, but the preliminary data here suggest that follow-up may be warranted. It should also be noted that simple categorization of macrophages into M1 and M2 phenotypes likely oversimplifies the variety of niches these cells can fit into (43). As such, future work should investigate whether a shift in macrophage phenotype by Depdc5 KO plays a meaningful role in the inflammatory profile we observed in skeletal muscle.

Conclusion and Limitations

Overall, these findings indicate that Depdc5 KO has little effect on blood glucose control, despite inhibiting autophagy, increasing mitochondrial H2O2 production capacity, and increasing muscle inflammation. One limitation of this work to date is that we relied on glucose tolerance as a readout of blood glucose control and do not have a direct measure of insulin resistance, such as glucose disposal during a glucose clamp. It is possible that skeletal muscle in Depdc5 knockout mice could have altered insulin sensitivity, which could be compensated for by the liver. Future work will directly assess insulin sensitivity in Depdc5 KO mice. In addition, we observed some minor sex differences, particularly that female KO mice were more susceptible to weight gain and increased fasting glucose. However, we were not powered to detect sex differences in all measures. As a result, future work should also investigate whether chronic activation of mTORC1 has sex-specific effects on metabolism.
