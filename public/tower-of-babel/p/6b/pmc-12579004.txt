Light-adaptive interfacial solar steam evaporation enhanced by dynamic water gating
Authors: Jianfei Wu, Ziwei Cui, Dongfang Wu, Zheng Cao, JunYuan Zhang, Jiafu Qu, Jundie Hu, Jianzhang Li, Dan Tian, Yahui Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Freshwater scarcity is a critical global issue, exacerbated by water pollution. Solar-driven interfacial evaporation (SDIE) offers a promising solution due to its energy efficiency and environmental benefits. This study presents a light-driven adaptive interfacial solar steam evaporation system enhanced by dynamic water gating. The system addresses the challenge of balancing high evaporation rates with salt accumulation, a common limitation in SDIE technology. An evaporator is developed using modified spiropyran and inexpensive wood, enabling dynamic salt discharge through light variations. Theoretical calculations reveal that the reversible structural changes in spiropyrans facilitate salt removal. The evaporator features a two-layer design, with a light-control layer and a hydrophilic layer, which enhances evaporation by stabilizing the gas-liquid interface and minimizing heat loss. Experimental results confirm the evaporator’s long-term stable salt-discharge and freshwater production capabilities, demonstrating high evaporation rates, reusability, and pollution resistance. Outdoor experiments and water purification tests further validate its effectiveness in producing freshwater and preventing salt accumulation. This research underscores the potential of light-responsive materials in designing efficient, sustainable evaporators, offering a significant advancement in water purification and desalination technologies.

Introduction

Freshwater resources are essential for human survival and development1. The freshwater resources of the earth are scarce at 2.53% of the total2, while ocean water is abundant at 96.5% of the total3,4. Freshwater scarcity further exacerbated due to water pollution5,6. To alleviate the shortage of freshwater, various desalination technologies such as reverse osmosis and electrodialysis have been developed to extract freshwater resources from seawater7–9. Compared with other desalination technologies, the solar-driven interfacial evaporation (SDIE) has attracted much attention in recent years due to its advantages of cleanliness, high efficiency and ease operation10,11.

Recently, SDIE has achieved rapid development due to a large amount of research on the synthesis of photothermal materials and the structural design of evaporators. A wide variety of photothermal conversion materials (heat-absorbing layer) have been designed, such as carbonaceous materials, semiconductors, metal nanoparticles, and polymers, in which carbonaceous materials have more prominent heat-absorbing properties12–14. Porous materials based on wood, hydrogel, and aerogel have also been developed for evaporator matrix materials and combined with technologies such as catalysis and power generation to increase the added value of SDIE15–19. Although there have been major advances in material synthesis and structural design, remaining the continuous and efficient production of freshwater is still a challenge20. Water transport, light absorption, thermal management, evaporation enthalpy, evaporation area, evaporator structure and self-cleaning capability affect the evaporation rate of SDIE, where self-cleaning is essential for the continuous, stable and efficient operation of the evaporator over a long period of time21–26. Salt crystals and organic contaminants in seawater during the phase change process can contaminate the evaporator result in reducing water transport, light absorption, and evaporation rate27–29. In view of this, self-cleaning strategies such as hydrophobic coatings, reflux by hydrophilic surface, regional salt crystallization, the Donnan effect, and self-cleaning spheres have been proposed to balance the conflict between salt build-up and evaporation rates30–32. These salt-resistant strategies mitigate the performance degradation due to salt build-up to some extent, but the complex structural design and passive operation cycle are difficult to meet the actual production requirements. Thus, design of an autonomous salt-discharging evaporator has great significance for the continuous and efficient operation of the evaporator and the actual freshwater production. Smart materials can respond to changes in external conditions such as light, heat, electric and magnetic fields, which is expected to be used for dynamic autonomous salt removal from evaporators33. In fact, there have been some reports on automatic salt drainage of smart materials, such as magnetic materials and thermo-responsive materials34,35. However, these autonomous salt-discharge require strictly control magnetic fields and temperatures, which is difficult to scale up in practical evaporation applications. Therefore, simpler autonomous salt-discharge strategies are required to improve the efficiency of salt removal. Spiropyran (SP) is a light-controlled switchable wettability smart material36. SP is hydrophobic under visible light and transforms to hydrophilic merocyanine (MC) under UV light/darkness, and the change is reversible37. This simple light-control feature makes it ideal for the preparation of autonomous salt-discharge evaporators.

Inspired by the chameleon’s colour-changing ability and the lotus leaf effect, a photocontrol dynamic water gate evaporator (PGE) based on spiropyrans was developed (Fig. 1a). The PGE was composed of modified spiropyran smart coatings, carbon nanotubes (CNT) and wood aerogels (WA). Among them, the modified spiropyran smart coatings (AOA-SP) were synthesized through the condensation of aminopropyltriethoxysilane functionalized octadecyl acrylate (AOA) and SP. The PGE can be roughly divided into a two-layer structure consisting of a light-control layer at the top (named PGE-T) and a hydrophilic layer at the bottom (named PGE-B). The surface carbonized wood evaporator (CWE) is a common evaporator used as a control in experiments. For the CWE, the high-concentration saline directly reached the evaporator surface by the capillary force (Fc) and evaporative pressure gradients, and the salt crystals precipitated and covered the surface after the water phase changes (Fig. 1b). Results

Design and preparation of PGE

PGE was prepared by coating AOA-SP ethanol solution on the surface of carbon nanotubes-modified wood aerogel (CNT@WA). The PGE was a lightweight double-layer evaporator with a light-controlled upper layer and a hydrophilic lower layer (Fig. 2a, b). The microscopic morphology of WA and PGE was observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). WA retained the structure of wood, and produced abundant micron or nanopores on both the surface and longitudinal faces, which provided a water transport channel to the evaporator (Supplementary Fig. 1). The PGE surface was distributed with granular polymers and the longitudinal surface was covered with CNTs (Fig. 2c, d). The constructed PGE surface was similar to the lotus leaf structure, where AOA resembled the waxy layer of a lotus leaf, the SP and CNTs resembled convex structures (Supplementary Fig. 2). The atomic force microscope (AFM) confirmed that a significantly higher longitudinal surface roughness of the PGE compared to WA, which was attributed to the introduction of CNTs (Fig. 2e, f). The elemental distribution and composition of the PGE were analysed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). XPS spectra showed an increase of N and Si content in the PGE compared to the CNT@WA, which was caused by the introduction of AOA-SP (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 1). The Si elements appeared on the surface of PGE by the EDS compared with WA, indicating that the AOA-SP coating was uniformly distributed on the surface of WA (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Figs. 4, 5). Confocal microscopy further verified that the PGE surface was uniformly covered with AOA-SP coating and had fluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 6). The functional groups of PGE were analyzed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Supplementary Fig. 7). The disappearance of characteristic peaks at 1595, 1504, 1462 cm–1 for PGE indicated the removal of lignin16,38. The wettability of PGE was characterized by water contact angle (WCA) (Supplementary Fig. 8). Wood exhibited weak hydrophilicity, which was mainly attributed to the presence of hydrophobic lignin. The PGE surface layer was hydrophobic, and the bottom layer was hydrophilic, which resulted from the introduction of the AOA-SP coating and the removal of lignin, respectively. Surface properties analysis of PGE

The smart coatings for PGE photocontrol gate preparation was characterized and analyzed. AOA-SP was obtained by in situ modification of long hydrocarbon chain silane precursors using 1,4 conjugate addition reaction and immobilization on the SP surface (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 10). The chemical structure of octadecyl acrylate (OA), SP, AOA-SP were analyzed by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H-NMR) (Supplementary Figs. 11, 12, and 13). Compared with the ¹H-NMR of OA, the characteristic peaks of AOA-SP at near 5.81 ppm (=CH2), 6.1 ppm (=CH–), and 6.4 ppm (=CH2) had disappeared, indicating that the double bonds (C=C) of OA had reacted. The characteristic peaks of AOA-SP at near 0.88 ppm (–CH3), 1.21 – 1.46 ppm (–(CH2)16–) confirmed that the long alkyl chain of OA was retained. The above observations indicated a possible 1,4 conjugate addition reaction between aminopropyltriethoxysilane (APTES) and OA in AOA-SP. The characteristic peak of AOA-SP near 7.96–8.06 ppm corresponded to the spiropyran nitrobenzene ring-adjacent protons, while the characteristic peaks at 5.88–7.2 ppm were attributed to aromatic protons of indoline rings and benzospiro-pyrans39. These indicated that the SP structure was successfully retained in AOA-SP. Part of the strong characteristic peak of AOA-SP at 1.48– 1.73 ppm may come from the self-polymerization of APTES. The functional groups of AOA-SP were analysed by FTIR (Supplementary Fig. 14). The FTIR spectra of OA showed that the characteristic peaks at 1731 cm–1 and 1410 cm–1 were due to C-H stretching of the carbonyl part and vinyl β-carbon, respectively. Compared to the stretching peak of carbonyl group at 1731 cm–1, the stretching frequency of AOA-SP at 1410 cm–1 was significantly lower, which confirmed that APTES successfully reacted with OA via a 1,4 conjugate addition reaction41. The presence of -OH in SP was confirmed by the characteristic peak at 3400 cm–1. The weakening of the hydroxyl peak near 3400 cm–1 for AOA-SP compared to SP indicated that the hydroxyl group may be condensed with silane. Moreover, the Si-O-Si characteristic peak near 1028 cm–1 appearances of AOA-SP indicated that the silane moiety self-polymerized on the synthesized AOA-SP. The analytical results of FTIR were consistent with those of 1H-NMR. The chemical components and molecular bonds of AOA-SP were further analyzed by XPS (Supplementary Fig. 15a–d). The results of the XPS showed that AOA-SP contained four elements C, N, O, and Si. AOA-SP appeared two peaks at 102 eV (Si-C bonding) and 102.8 eV (Si-O-C bonding), which indicated that silanes moiety reacted with hydroxyl groups in SP via Si-O bonding41. The microscopic morphology of AOA-SP was observed by SEM. Different from OA and AOA chain, AOA-SP was a polymer without shape (Supplementary Fig. 16, Supplementary Figs. 17 and 3b). The EDS revealed that AOA-SP contains C, O, N, and Si (Fig. 3c). The reversible switching of color and wettability between AOA-SP and the isomer AOA-MC showed that it preserved the photoresponsiveness of SP (Fig. 3d, h and Supplementary Fig. 18). The ethanol solution of smart coatings was analysed by the ultraviolet-visible (UV–Vis) spectrophotometer. The smart coatings had no distinct characteristic peak near 512 nm after visible light (Vis) irradiation, while the characteristic peak appeared under darkness. The UV–Vis spectrum indicated that the smart coatings presented different structures under light and darkness (Fig. 3g)42. The dynamic responsiveness of the smart coatings and the threshold of light intensity were texted by digital camera and UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The digital photographs revealed that the response speed became faster with increasing light intensity in the range up to 3 kW m–2. The response slowed down when the light intensity reached 4 kW m–2, which might have been caused by high-temperature effects on the isomer conversion (Supplementary Fig. 19). The solution became AOA-MC again when AOA-SP was placed in the dark for a short time (Supplementary Fig. 20). The time recorded by the digital camera combined with the UV–Vis spectra gave an approximate correspondence between light intensity and response time for a given concentration (Supplementary Fig. 21). The binding ability of the smart coatings to water under different light conditions was further analyzed by raman spectroscopy. The smart coatings formed less intermediate water with water under visible light, while under dark conditions, the smart coatings had a higher hydration capacity and formed more intermediate water. The increase of the intermediate water further suggested that AOA-SP undergoes isomerization in the dark (Fig. 3e, f). These results demonstrated the significant effect of light on the wettability of smart coatings.

Evaporation and cooling performance of PGE

The evaporation performance of PGE was tested by AM 1.5 G xenon lamp solar simulator (Fig. 4a). An aqueous solution of 3.5 wt% NaCl was used to simulate seawater. The effect of the coating on the evaporation performance of PGE was optimized and analysed by modulating the amount of OA and SP (Supplementary Table S2). The evaporation rate of PGE increased with increasing OA and SP dosage and exceeded 2.7 kg m−2 h−1 for both AOA-SP3 and AOA-SP4 (Supplementary Fig. 22). Increased coating consistency made it easier to completely cover the PGE surface, reducing heat carried away by the bulk water. AOA-SP3 was selected for further study in conjunction with cost considerations. The amount of APTES was adjusted to further optimize the AOA-SP3 smart coatings (Supplementary Table S3). The results showed that the evaporation rate of PGE increased with the increase of APTES dosage, and the evaporation rate leveled off after the APTES dosage reached 140 μL (AOA140-SP3) (Supplementary Fig. 23). The AOA140-SP3 smart layer was used for further PGE preparation and research in combination with cost considerations. PGEp (parallel with the tree growth direction) and CWE were used as control (Supplementary Fig. 24 and Supplementary Fig. 25). The evaporation rates of bulk water, CWE, PGEp and PGE at one sun were 0.37, 1.67, 1.86 and 2.71 kg m–2 h–1, respectively (Fig. 4b). This indicated that the coating-optimized PGE evaporation rate was significantly higher than that of other similar types of wood evaporators. There was no significant decrease in the evaporation rate after 12 cycles, indicating the good reusability of PGE (Fig. 4c). In addition, the stability of AOA-SP during evaporation was analyzed. The weight of the 2D AOA140-SP3 membrane after evaporation was almost the same as before evaporation, proving that the coating was less susceptible to loss during evaporation (Supplementary Fig. 26). The high evaporation rate of PGE was associated with the high water storage capacity of WA, fast water transport channels, side region assisted evaporation and good surface heat management (Supplementary Fig. 27a–d). The high hydrophilicity and fast water transport of PGE-B (CNT@WA) originated from the removal of most of the lignin and hemicellulose, preserving the hydrophilic fiber network and vertical water transport channels (Supplementary Fig. 28). The three types of water in a material are free water (FW), intermediate water (IW) and bound water (BW), and intermediate water requires the lowest energy during evaporation43. The water status in the material was analysed by Raman and DSC. Raman test results showed that the IW/FW values of PGE were much higher than those of bulk water, which may be related to their hydrophilic cellulose networks and CNTs (Fig. 4e–g). The hydrophilic groups of cellulose and CNTs resulted in the formation of large amounts of intermediate water (Fig. 4d). The differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) test results have reaffirmed the formation of large amounts of intermediate water in PGE (Fig. 4h). The DSC thermogram also revealed that the temperature required for evaporation of WA was lower than that of wood (Supplementary Fig. 29). Dark evaporation experiments revealed that the enthalpy of evaporation of PGE was lower than that of CWE and bulk water (Fig. 4i, Supplementary method 1 and supplementary equation 1). In addition, the rapid evaporative cooling of the PGE can provide heat dissipation for the thermoelectric generators (TEG), while the waste heat from the TEG can be used as a heat source for the PGE for evaporation (Supplementary Fig. 30a). A smart coatings solution containing carbon nanotubes (AOA140-SP3@CNT) was coated on the surface of TEG to prepare c-TEG, which was further combined with PGE to design c-TEG@PGE. The uncoated TGE was combined with PGE to prepare TGE@PGE as a control group. Two homemade freshwater and electricity cogeneration units were evaluated at a light intensity of 1 kW m–2. Compared with TEG@PGE, c-TEG@PGE had higher voltage (88.2 mV) and current (17.4 mA) (Supplementary Fig. 30c, d). At the same time, c-TEG@PGE had accompanied a high evaporation rate (1.76 kg m–2 h–1) (Supplementary Fig. 30b). The output voltage of the c-TEG@PGE can be controlled by turning on-off the simulated sunlight, and the maximum power density at stabilization reached 1.42 W m–2, which is an excellent performance in the same type of work (Supplementary Fig. 30e, f and Supplementary Table S4). The high performance of c-TEG@PGE benefited from the good photothermal conversion of c-TEG (Supplementary Fig. 30g–i).

Salt-discharge and anti-pollution mechanism

Salt-discharge testing of evaporators is an important part of their performance evaluation. Four PGEs with different coating thicknesses were prepared to explore the effect of coating thickness on evaporation performance and responsiveness (Supplementary Table S5). The results showed that the evaporation rate increases with the increase of coating thickness, but the light-responsive salt tolerance performance decreased (Supplementary Fig. 31). Excessively thick coating leds to greater difficulty in transferring water to the surface, which led to higher surface temperatures and difficulty in salt reflux. The PGE2 has both a high evaporation rate and salt reflux capability, which was further studied for more in-depth salt tolerance. The effect of coating uniformity on evaporation performance and salt reflux performance was verified by different coating styles. The evaporation rate of the inhomogeneous coating was not affected much for a short time, but the inhomogeneous coating slowly started to accumulate salt after a long time of evaporation (Supplementary Fig. 32). In addition, the evaporation rate and salt reflux capacity of the PGE2 were tested by different concentration gradients of salt solutions. The test revealed that the evaporation rate of PGE2 decreased a little but remained high as the salt concentration increased (Supplementary Fig. 33). Importantly, PGE2 exhibited good salt reflux at different salt concentration gradients, and there was no significant salt accumulation on the surface of PGE2 after 8 h of continuous evaporation (Supplementary Fig. 34).

The evaporation properties of CWE and PGE2 were further investigated in more depth with high concentration saline water (20 wt% NaCl solution). Typical CWE transfer brine to the surface driven by capillary forces and evaporative pressure gradients, and the rapid vaporization of water at the surface led to salt deposition (Fig. 5a). In Fig. 5c, CWE gradually decreased in evaporation rate with increasing surface salt deposition, while the rapid evaporation of PGE2 resumed after each decrease. Salt accumulation on the surface of the CWE reduces light absorption and water transport channel blockage, thus reducing evaporation rate. Different from CWE, the hydrophilic state of PGE2 channels/surfaces was activated when they were covered with salt. The PGE2 allowed a large amount of water to flow back to the deposited salt crystals, which made the PGE2 self-cleaning (Fig. 5b). The dynamic salt tolerance of PGE2 keeps the evaporation rate at 2.3 kg m–2 h–1 after 8 h, which was much higher than the CWE value of 1.37 kg m–2 h–1. The self-cleaning ability of PGE2 was further analysed by observing the dissolution of salt crystals by covering them on the surface of PGE2 (Fig. 5d). The results showed that the PGE2 could effectively reflux the salt deposited on the surface when the light was turned off (darkness), whereas the CWE had difficulty in accomplishing salt reflux. The water contamination resistance of the evaporator was further tested. PGE2 was immersed in strong acids and bases for 12 h, and there was no significant damage of PGE2’s structure proving its good resistance to acids and bases (Supplementary Fig. 36). The effect of pollutant on evaporation performance was further evaluated using a salt solution containing soybean oil and toluene. The evaporation rates of PGE2 at the tenth hour and the first hour were close, indicating good pollution resistance of PGE2 during evaporation (Supplementary Fig. 37). The mechanism of anti-pollution of PGE2 was analysed by contact angle. The WCA (including tea, coffee, acids, alkalis, and dyes) in air and the underwater oil contact angle (OCA) (including kerosene, toluene, soybean oil, cyclohexane, and n-hexane) of PGE2 were high values (Supplementary Fig. 38a, c). Marangoni flow promotes salt tolerance analysis

The effect of PGE2 thermal management on salt tolerance performance was further analysed by CWE and bulk water as a control (Fig. 6a). The operating temperature and absorbance of PGE2 were analysed by infrared thermography (IT) and ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared (UV–Vis–NIR) spectroscopy. IT image revealed that the temperatures of bulk water, CWE and PGE2 during steady evaporation were about 31 °C, 41 °C, and 45 °C, respectively (Fig. 6b). The photocontrol layer of PGE2 was hydrophobic in visible light preventing bulk water from covering the surface to reduce heat loss. The light-controlled water gate of PGE2 kept the surface to self-clean and at high temperatures compared to a conventional evaporator. The UV–Vis–NIR results revealed that PGE2 has a good light absorption capacity, which was attributed to the synergistic effect of spiropyran molecules and CNTs (Fig. 6c). The surface of PGE2 photothermal conversion involved molecular thermal vibration and non-radiative relaxation mechanisms (Fig. 6d, e). In addition, the CNTs on the vertical channel wall of the PGE2 repeatedly absorbed light to further increase the absorbance (Fig. 6f). A large temperature difference between the PGE2 surface and the substrate based on the thermal management analyzed, and this will favor the generation of Marangoni flow to promote salt reflux (Fig. 6g, h). Marangoni flow is the phenomenon of mass movement due to the presence of a difference in surface tension gradient between two liquids, and includes both temperature Marangoni flow and solute Marangoni flow44. The photoresponsive hydrophobic surface and high absorbance of PGE2 led to a temperature difference between its top surface and lower part exceeding 14 °C, which can trigger a stronger temperature Marangoni flow than CWE. The temperature Marangoni effect can reflux salt back into the bulk water. Outdoor experiment and general salt-discharge test

The freshwater production capacity of PGE2 was further verified through outdoor experiments and water purification experiments (Fig. 7a, b). Outdoor experiments were conducted in a natural environment, the surface temperature and evaporation rate of the PGE2 was recorded (from 12:00 to 16:30) (Supplementary Fig. 40). The highest evaporation of outdoor rate reached 2.6 kg m–2 h–1 similar to the indoor experiment (Fig. 7c). A 30-day outdoor performance test was conducted and ambient temperature, humidity, and evaporation rates were recorded (Supplementary Fig. 41). The evaporation rate of PGE2 was stable in long-term outdoor experiments, which proved its good durability. The purified water after evaporation was collected using a collection device (Fig. 7d). The major ion concentrations in purified seawater were tested by ICP-MS. The purified seawater of the Yellow Sea reduced the concentration of major ions by 2–3 orders of magnitude and met the World Health Organisation standards (Fig. 7f). The resistance of seawater before and after purification was tested by a multimeter. Significant changes in the electrical resistance of seawater before and after purification further demonstrated the outstanding water purification capacity of PGE2 (Supplementary Fig. 42a, b). Dye wastewaters before and after purification were tested by UV–Vis spectroscopy, and the results showed that the purification efficiency of wastewaters containing Rhodamine B (RhB) exceeded 99% (Fig. 7e). Different light-controlled biomass evaporators were prepared by applying AOA140-SP3 on the surface of seven different biomasses, including mushrooms, apples, aubergines, carrots, luffas, rattans, and corncobs, to evaluate its versatility. The results showed that there was no salt accumulation on the surface of all seven evaporators after 8 h of continuous evaporation (20 wt% NaCl). These results fully demonstrated the versatility of the adaptive salt-discharge of the optically controlled dynamic watergate design (Fig. 7g). Compared with the previous similar high-performance evaporator, PGE2 has outstanding performance in salt-discharge, evaporation rate and evaporation efficiency (Fig. 7h). Discussion

In this paper, a light-driven adaptive salt-resistant smart coating (AOA140-SP3) was prepared by a simple method. The photocontrol dynamic water gate evaporator prepared based on the smart coatings has good evaporation performance, durability and salt-discharge. The smart coatings transformed the evaporator’s hydrophobic surface into a hydrophilic state for salt reflux when the evaporator surface was covered with salt. Theoretical calculations confirmed that the dynamic salt-discharge of smart coatings was related to the difference in hydrophilicity between spiropyran and isomeric merocyanine. The salt-rejecting effect of these smart coatings has been validated in seven types of evaporators. This salt-discharging strategy is of great significance for designing evaporators that operate efficiently over long-term continuous operation.

Methods

Materials

Balsa woods were purchased from the Duocai Hanideng Hui Speciality Store, and the samples were processed to 20 × 20 × 10 mm (Radial × Chordwise × Axial). TEGs (SP1848-27145) were purchased from Shenzhen Haida Hi-Tech Electronics Co. Multi-walled carbon nanotubes purchased from Jiangsu Xianfeng Nanomaterials Technology Co., Ltd (>95%). Acrylate (99%), (3-Aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (98%), acetic acid (>99.5%), anhydrous ethanol (>99.9%), and 1-(2-Hydroxyethyl)-3,3-dimethylindoline-6′nitrobenzospiran (>93%) were purchased from Maclean’s. All medicines were purchased and used directly without further processing.

Synthesis of AOA-SP

Different AOA-SP smart solutions were obtained by adding octadecyl acrylate (0 mg, 100 mg, 200 mg) and APTES (0 mg, 70 mg, 140 mg, 210 mg) in 30 ml of ethanol and stirring for 5 min at room temperature, followed by addition of spiropyran (100 mg, 200 mg) and vigorous stirring for 3 h. The above AOA-SP solution was placed in a fume hood to allow most of the ethanol to evaporate, and then the powder was dried in an oven at 60 °C for 12 h to obtain a powder for characterization. The preparation of AOA was similar to the AOA140-SP3 steps, with the difference that SP was not needed to be put in. The default coating was AOA140-SP3 when not specified in the manuscript.

Preparation of WA

The balsa wood was subjected to delignification in an aqueous solution of NaClO₂ (2 wt%) at 80 °C for 24 h, with the pH adjusted to 4.7 using acetic acid. The wood was then removed and washed with deionized water at 80 °C until the wash water reached neutral pH. Subsequently, it was treated in an aqueous NaOH solution (8 wt%) at 80 °C for 9 h to remove hemicellulose. After the treatment, the sample was washed with deionized water at 80 °C until the wash water reached neutral pH. Finally, the wood was frozen at –40 °C for 10 h and freeze-dried at –60 °C for 48 h to obtain WA.

Preparation of PGE

2 g of CNTs were homogeneously dispersed in a beaker containing 50 mL of ethanol, followed by the addition of WA and stirred for 3 h, and then drying in a vacuum oven to obtain CNT@WA. A certain amount of (0.5 mL, 1 mL, 3 mL, 5 mL) ethanol solution of AOA140-SP3 was repeatedly applied to the dried CNT@WA surface with a brush (the amount of each layer was 0.5 mL) and then put into an oven at 70 °C for 20 min, and the above process was repeated until the smart solution was exhausted. Four evaporators with different coating layer degrees were finally obtained for PGE1, PGE2, PGE6, and PGE10. The default evaporator was PGE2 when not specified in the manuscript.

Evaporation experiment

The evaporators were fixed with foam and placed in saline water with configured concentration for evaporation test under simulated light (one sun by default when intensity is not specified) or sunlight. The default evaporators are PGE2 when not specified. A 3.5 wt% aqueous NaCl solution was used by default when the brine concentration was not specified. The salt tolerance test ensured constant salt concentration by injecting lost water with a syringe every hour. The experiments were performed under constant temperature and humidity laboratory conditions (24 °C, RH = 50%). The analog light source used was a xenon lamp (Beijing CEL-HXF300-T3, AM 1.5G). The solar flux was measured with a photopower meter (CEL-NP2000-2A) and the temperature was recorded with an infrared thermography.

Coating universality test

AOA140-SP3@CNT mixture was obtained by adding 1 g of carbon nanotube to the above-configured AOA140-SP3 solution (50 mL) and sonicated for 1 h. Shiitake mushrooms, apples, aubergines, carrots, luffas, rattans, and corncobs were processed into regular shapes and coated with the AOA140-SP3@CNT (1 mL), respectively.

Freshwater and electricity co-generation experiment

The c-TEG was obtained by applying 2 mL of the AOA140-SP3@CNT mixture to the surface of commercial TEG and placing it in an oven at 60 °C for 1 h. PGE was immobilized on the lower surface of c-TEG with foam to obtain c-TEG@PGE. TEG@PGE was prepared in a similar manner as described above, with the difference that the AOA140-SP3@CNT coating was not used. The prepared device was placed in simulated brine to test the freshwater and electricity co-generation performance.
