Clinical learning challenges among nursing students at Wolaita Sodo University: prevalence and determinants
Authors: Tadele Lankrew Ayalew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinical learning is the cornerstone of nursing education, integrating theoretical knowledge with hands-on practice to develop essential clinical skills, critical thinking, and professional confidence. Nursing students’ face major challenges, including a shortage of qualified clinical instructors, overcrowded clinical sites, and limited clinical equipment, which hinder their skill development and clinical competence at Wolaita Sodo University, South Ethiopia. Despite its importance, evidence on the extent and determinants of these challenges in the study area is limited.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the prevalence and determinants of clinical learning challenges among nursing students at Wolaita Sodo University, South Ethiopia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from May 15 to June 2024 among 303 nursing students at Wolaita Sodo University using structured self-administered questionnaires. Participants were selected using a stratified random sampling technique. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics summarized the data, while logistic regression identified factors associated with clinical learning challenges. Anxiety and depression were measured using validated tools (GAD-7 and PHQ-9), while khat use was assessed with a self-report questionnaire adapted from previous Ethiopian university studies. The questionnaire demonstrated good reliability, with the following domain-specific Cronbach’s alpha values: clinical learning challenges (α = 0.81), skill lab and supervision experience (α = 0.78), financial and emotional stressors (α = 0.80), and coping mechanisms (α = 0.76).ResultsOut of 303 students, 293 responded completely the questionnaire (response rate: 96.7%). Among them, 232 (79.2%) reported facing one or more clinical learning challenges. Major challenges included lack of clinical instructors (64.6%), overcrowding at clinical sites (58.4%), and inadequate learning resources (52.8%). Factors significantly associated with challenges were Overcrowding in clinical areas (AOR = 1.88,95% CI: 1.12–3.16, p = 0.017), Khat chewing (AOR = 2.33, 95% CI: 1.20–4.51, p = 0.011), Inadequate clinical materials and equipment (AOR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.01–3.05, p = 0.045), Moderate anxiety (AOR = 2.87, 95% CI: 1.14–7.18, p = 0.001), First- and second-year (AOR = 1.59 ,95% CI: 1.04–2.42, p = 0.031).ConclusionThis study revealed a high prevalence of clinical learning challenges among nursing students at Wolaita Sodo University, mainly linked to year of study, instructor availability, and student–patient ratio. Addressing these issues through stronger instructor support, equitable clinical access, and balanced student loads is essential. The findings highlight the need for institutional reforms to enhance clinical education quality and student competency. Institutions should also manage faculty workload, provide targeted training, and align the national nursing curriculum with real-world clinical demands.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Clinical learning is the cornerstone of nursing education, connecting theory to practice to build students’ critical thinking, clinical skills, and professional competence for quality patient care [1, 2].

Globally, clinical learning challenges affect 40% to 75% of nursing students, with higher rates in low- and middle-income countries due to variations in location, resources, and institutional support [3, 4]. A study in India found a 65% prevalence of clinical learning challenges, while 68% of students in Bangladesh reported similar issues due to poor supervision and limited infrastructure. In contrast, only 38% of Canadian nursing students experienced significant difficulties, mostly from communication gaps and unclear roles. These differences show how structural and systemic factors shape clinical learning, with students in resource-limited settings facing greater barriers [3, 5].

Internationally, a study in Kenya reported a 75.6% prevalence of clinical learning difficulties, citing limited preceptorship and overcrowding findings consistent with this study [6]. A study in Uganda found a 73.4% prevalence of clinical challenges, highlighting that instructor shortages and lack of clinical materials are common issues across many East African nursing programs [6–8].

In contrast, studies from developed countries report lower rates of clinical learning challenges. For example, an Australian study found only 42% of nursing students faced significant issues, mainly related to team integration and inter professional collaboration rather than resource shortages [6, 9].

A study in Northern Ethiopia reported a 70% prevalence of clinical challenges, mainly due to lack of supervision, insufficient materials, and overcrowded clinical settings [10]. Similarly, a systematic review of Ethiopian nursing schools found an average 72.5% prevalence of clinical challenges, with instructor shortages and gaps between academic and clinical expectations being common issues [11].

A study at Wachemo University found that 58.4% of nursing students experienced stress during clinical practice, mainly due to environmental factors, and 52% used coping strategies to manage these challenges [12].

A study at Mekelle University found that 58% of nursing students felt clinically incompetent, raising serious concerns about their clinical practice skills [13].

A systematic review and meta-analysis reported that only 28% of nursing students in Ethiopia demonstrated clinical competency, which was significantly linked to a supportive learning environment, increased self-confidence, and positive staff-student interactions [14].​

The shortage of qualified instructors and weak supervision increase clinical learning challenges. Student issues, including low motivation and poor attendance, also hinder learning outcomes [12, 15]. Environmental factors such as limited resources and rural placements significantly contribute to clinical learning challenges, unlike urban placements. These findings highlight the need to improve supervision, resources, and student engagement to enhance clinical learning [16–18].

Understanding clinical learning challenges in resource-limited settings like Wolaita Sodo University is essential because most existing research focuses on high-income countries and may not capture local realities. Key issues such as instructor shortages, crowded clinical sites, and poor collaboration between academic and clinical settings remain underexplored in Ethiopia. Without context-specific data, it is difficult to develop effective solutions to improve clinical education. This study aims to fill this gap by identifying the specific barriers nursing students face, thereby guiding targeted interventions to enhance their learning and improve patient care outcomes. Therefore, this study aims to fill this critical knowledge gap by systematically identifying the clinical learning challenges faced by nursing students at Wolaita Sodo University and proposing evidence-based strategies to enhance clinical education in similar settings.

Methods and materials

Study area and period

The study was conducted at Wolaita Sodo University, a public higher education institution in Wolaita Sodo town, the administrative center of Wolaita Zone in southern Ethiopia. The university offers diverse undergraduate, postgraduate, and professional programs. It’s College of Health Sciences and Medicine, which includes the Nursing Department, is vital in training healthcare professionals for the region.

Wolaita Sodo University emphasizes community-based education and practical training. Nursing students complete clinical placements at affiliated health facilities, including Wolaita Sodo University Teaching and Referral Hospital, one of the largest hospitals in the area.

These clinical sites provide students with valuable hands-on experience by serving a diverse range of patients. However, challenges like limited infrastructure, overcrowding, and resource shortages often hinder effective clinical training.

Data collection for this study occurred over one month and included nursing students from various academic levels who had completed at least one clinical placement.

Study design

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted to assess clinical learning challenges among nursing students at Wolaita Sodo University.

Source population and study population

The source population comprised all BSc nursing students at Wolaita Sodo University who had completed at least one clinical attachment. The study population included randomly selected students from this group who were present and consented during the data collection period.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were included if they were BSc nursing students enrolled at Wolaita Sodo University, had completed at least one clinical attachment in a hospital or health center, were present during data collection, and gave informed consent. Students were excluded if they were critically ill, had psychiatric conditions or clinical depression affecting participation, or were on academic leave or unavailable during data collection.

Sample size determination and sampling technique procedure

A total of 303 BSc nursing students were selected through stratified random sampling to ensure representation across academic years. The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula with a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and an estimated prevalence from previous studies. A 10% allowance for non-response was included, finalizing the sample at 303 students.

To ensure proportional representation, the study population was stratified by academic year second, third, and fourth years since all have completed at least one clinical attachment. The total number of students in each year was obtained from the university registrar, and the sample from each year was calculated based on their proportion of the total eligible population.

After determining the number of students to select from each year group, simple random sampling was used within each stratum. Eligible students’ lists served as sampling frames, and participants were randomly chosen using a computer-generated random number list to ensure equal chances of selection.

This stratified random sampling method improved sample representativeness and minimized bias, enabling meaningful comparisons of clinical learning challenges across different year levels (Fig. 1).

Data collection tool

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire adapted from validated tools to ensure reliability and content validity [19, 20]. The questionnaire was adapted to fit the study context and covered five key areas: demographics, clinical challenges, skill lab and supervision experiences, financial and emotional stressors, and coping strategies. It was pretested on 15 nursing students not included in the main study, and revised accordingly. Anxiety and depression were measured using validated tools: GAD-7 for anxiety and PHQ-9 for depression, both widely used and validated in similar populations. Khat use was assessed through a structured self-report questionnaire adapted from prior studies in Ethiopian universities. Although no standardized psychometric scale was used for khat use, the tool captured frequency and patterns of consumption to ensure relevant data collection. The questionnaire demonstrated good reliability, with the following domain-specific Cronbach’s alpha values: clinical learning challenges (α = 0.81), skill lab and supervision experience (α = 0.78), financial and emotional stressors (α = 0.80), and coping mechanisms (α = 0.76).

Variables

Dependent variable: Clinical learning challenges.

Independent variables include:- Institutional Factors (Quality of clinical instructors, Availability of qualified staff, Supervision and guidance during clinical practice), Student-Related Factors (Motivation levels, Absenteeism from clinical placements, Previous clinical experience and preparedness) and Environmental Factors (Availability of clinical learning resources (e.g., medical equipment, learning materials), Clinical setting (e.g., hospital vs. community-based settings), Accessibility of clinical placements).

Operational definitions

Instructor availability: The presence and accessibility of clinical instructors to guide, supervise, and support students during clinical placements.

Student-to-Patient ratio: The proportion of nursing students assigned to each patient during clinical practice, reflecting the level of hands-on learning opportunity.

Data quality control

To ensure data quality, data collectors and supervisors received two days of training on study objectives, tools, procedures, and confidentiality. A pretest was conducted on 20 students at Wachemo University, outside the main study, one week before data collection. Data collection was supervised daily, and the principal investigator reviewed completed questionnaires for completeness and consistency.

Data processing and analysis

Data were checked for completeness, consistency, and clarity by the principal investigator, then coded, entered, and cleaned using Epi-Data version 3.1, and exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. Univariable analysis described participant characteristics using tables and figures. Bivariate analysis using COR with 95% CI identified associations between independent and outcome variables. Variables with p-value ≤ 0.25 were included in multivariable logistic regression to control for con founders. Multicollinearity was assessed using VIF and standard error; variables with VIF > 10 or standard error > 2 were excluded. Model fitness was checked using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (0.43). Binary logistic regression was performed to identify associated factors, with p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Wolaita Sodo University, with reference number of WSU/3216/24. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participation was voluntary and informed consent was secured from all respondents. Data confidentiality was strictly maintained.

Clinical learning challenges

Difficulties encountered by nursing students during clinical practice that hinder the effective application of theoretical knowledge in real patient care settings.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the nursing students’ at WSU, South Ethiopia, 2024

Out of 303 students, 293 completed the questionnaire, resulting in a 96.7% response rate, with non-responses mainly due to lack of interest and time. The mean age of participants was 20.66 years (SD ± 2.216). Of the respondents, 53.1% were aged 17–20 years, 40.1% were between 21 and 24 years, and 6.8% were 25–27 years old. Regarding sex, 67.6% were male and 32.4% female. Most participants were single (95.9%), followed by married (3.1%) and widowed (1.02%). In terms of monthly income, 94.5% earned 1000 ETB or less, while 5.5% earned more than 1000 ETB (Table 1).

Prevalence of clinical learning challenges

Out of 303 nursing students, 293 completed the survey, yielding a 96.7% response rate. Among them, 232 students (79.2%) reported experiencing one or more clinical learning challenges. Key issues included overcrowding in clinical areas (42.1%), lack of clinical instructors (64.6%), inadequate materials and equipment (17.2%), khat chewing (35.2%), and cigarette smoking (16.2%). Psychologically, 28.4% reported moderate anxiety, and 20.9% reported moderate depression. These findings highlight the prevalence of clinical learning challenges and emphasize the need for targeted interventions addressing both structural and personal barriers.

Factors associated with clinical learning challenges

The study identified several significant factors associated with clinical learning challenges among nursing students at Wolaita Sodo University. Multivariable logistic regression showed that overcrowding in clinical areas significantly increased the odds of difficulties (AOR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.12–3.16, p = 0.017). Additionally, khat chewing was a strong predictor, with students who chewed khat being more than twice as likely to face challenges (AOR = 2.33, 95% CI: 1.20–4.51, p = 0.011). Students reporting inadequate clinical materials and equipment had higher odds of facing difficulties (AOR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.01–3.05, p = 0.045). Moderate anxiety was independently linked to clinical challenges (AOR = 2.87, 95% CI: 1.14–7.18, p = 0.001). First- and second-year students were more likely to experience clinical learning difficulties compared to senior students (AOR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.04–2.42, p = 0.031). Other factors showed increased crude odds but were not statistically significant after adjustment—for example, students aged 21–24 had an AOR of 1.53 (95% CI: 0.93–2.52), and those aged 25–27 had an AOR of 1.15 (95% CI: 0.39–3.33) compared to those aged 17–20. Lack of clinical instructors had an AOR of 2.43 (95% CI: 0.35–4.37), cigarette smoking had an AOR of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.39–2.27), and khat chewing was not a significantly associated factor. While smoking has been associated with stress or coping behaviors in some settings, our study found no meaningful association between cigarette use and clinical learning challenges. This may be due to several factors, such as the low prevalence of cigarette smoking in our sample, underreporting due to social desirability bias, or cultural factors that limit open disclosure of smoking behavior among students. Additionally, the narrow confidence interval and near-null effect suggest that cigarette smoking may not be a strong influencing factor in this specific context. Depression levels were not significantly associated after adjustment, with mild depression showing an AOR of 2.21 (95% CI: 0.82–5.95) and moderate depression an AOR of 1.53 (95% CI: 0.52–4.49). Anxiety at mild (AOR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.25–1.94), severe (AOR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.18–3.03), and extreme/severe levels (AOR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.02–1.64) was also not statistically significant. These results highlight the important roles of overcrowding, khat use, resource availability, anxiety, and academic year in shaping nursing students’ clinical learning experiences and identify key areas for effective intervention (Table 2).

Discussion

Clinical nursing education is a cornerstone of professional nursing student training, offering students the opportunity to apply theoretical knowledge in real-world patient care settings. It plays a vital role in shaping competent, confident, and safe practitioners student capable of delivering high-quality healthcare services after graduation. Through hands-on clinical experiences, students develop essential skills in clinical judgment, psychomotor function, communication, and professional conduct. Therefore, identifying and addressing barriers to effective clinical learning is crucial for enhancing the quality of nursing education and ensuring the preparedness of future nurses for professional practice.

The findings of this study reveal the prevalence of clinical learning challenges among nursing students at Wolaita Sodo University was 79.2% (95% CI: 74.5%–83.3%). This study revealed a significantly higher prevalence of clinical learning challenges among nursing students at Wolaita Sodo University compared to findings from other regions, both nationally and internationally. For example, a study conducted in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, reported a much lower prevalence of 16.9% among nursing students facing clinical learning difficulties [21]. In Bahir Dar, the proportion of students found to be clinically competent was 36.5%, indirectly indicating that 63.5% experienced clinical learning challenges [22]. Likewise, in Mekelle, 58% of nursing students perceived themselves as not clinically competent, further highlighting regional variability in clinical education outcomes. Beyond Ethiopia, international comparisons also reflect similar but variably reported challenges. In Sokoto State, Nigeria, a study identified major clinical learning obstacles, including the gap between theory and practice, limited supervision, and inadequate clinical exposure. However, this study did not quantify the overall prevalence of clinical learning challenges among students [22, 23]. Similarly, research conducted in Ghana pointed out critical barriers such as overcrowded clinical environments, lack of preceptors, and insufficient resources, though it also lacked specific prevalence figures [23]. The clinical learning environment at Wolaita Sodo University appears more constrained than in other regions due to instructor shortages, high student-to-patient ratios, and limited infrastructure. The high rates of perceived incompetence and learning barriers highlight the urgent need for targeted reforms and investment in clinical training. Enhancing faculty support, clinical site quality, and supervision models is vital to boost student competence and confidence. Regional and global comparisons stress the need for context-specific strategies to improve nursing education in resource-limited settings. This rate is higher than findings from other Ethiopian institutions. For instance, studies in Tigray and a systematic review covering Amhara, Oromia, and Addis Ababa reported lower prevalence rates of 70% and 72.5%, respectively. Common barriers included poor supervision, limited resources, instructor shortages, and theory-practice gaps. These comparisons highlight the greater challenges at Wolaita Sodo University and the need for targeted, context-specific interventions.

A study at Wachemo University in Southern Ethiopia found that 58.4% of nursing students experienced stress during clinical practice, mainly due to overcrowded wards, limited resources, and inadequate instructor support. Moreover, 52% used coping strategies, emphasizing the need for better clinical environments and psychosocial support in nursing education [12]. International comparisons further reinforce these findings. In Kenya, a study reported a clinical learning challenge prevalence of 75.6%, while in Uganda, the rate was 73.4% [24]. Both studies revealed common barriers such as limited preceptorship, instructor shortages, overcrowded clinical sites, and lack of essential materials. These shared challenges in resource-limited settings underscore the widespread nature of clinical education issues and the urgent need for systemic reforms to strengthen clinical training [25]. These similarities indicate that nursing programs across East Africa face common systemic challenges due to limited healthcare infrastructure and educational investment. Persistent issues like instructor shortages, inadequate materials, and weak preceptorship highlight the need for coordinated policy action. Solutions should include increased funding, standardized instructor training, and regional collaboration to improve clinical learning. Addressing these shared barriers can enhance student outcomes and strengthen nursing care across the region [26, 27].

In contrast, studies from developed countries report much lower rates of clinical learning challenges. In Australia, 42% of nursing students faced difficulties mainly related to team integration and inter professional collaboration. In Canada, only 38% reported major challenges, primarily due to communication gaps and unclear role expectations. These findings highlight the importance of resources, structured support, and institutional investment in fostering effective clinical learning environments(9,28).

This study identified key predictors of clinical learning challenges: instructor shortages (AOR: 2.43), overcrowded clinical settings (AOR: 1.88), and students’ academic year (AOR: 1.59), suggesting junior students are more vulnerable due to limited experience and confidence in applying theory to practice. They are still developing essential skills and adjusting to the clinical environment, which can increase stress and anxiety. Additionally, they may have less access to mentorship and support compared to senior students, making it harder to navigate clinical demands effectively. These factors contribute to the greater difficulties observed among junior students. Though less emphasized in other studies, this finding underscores the need for early mentorship and targeted support to improve preparedness and reduce learning difficulties.

In the context of this study at Wolaita Sodo University, clinical learning challenges are largely driven by structural limitations, including a shortage of clinical instructors and overcrowded clinical environments. These issues reflect the realities of low-resource settings and emphasize the need for context-specific strategies to enhance the quality of clinical education for nursing students.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s strengths include a high response rate and stratified sampling, enhancing representativeness. Regional and international comparisons added valuable context. However, self-reported data may cause bias, and the cross-sectional design limits causal conclusions. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify cause-effect relationships.

Conclusion

This study highlights a high burden of clinical learning challenges among nursing students at Wolaita Sodo University, rooted in systemic issues such as faculty shortages, overcrowded clinical environments, and limited resources. Institutions can address these challenges by investing in faculty recruitment and retention, balancing clinical instructor-to-student ratios, and expanding partnerships with clinical sites to ensure meaningful placements. These efforts have direct implications for managing faculty workload and underscore the need for ongoing faculty development in clinical teaching methods. At the national level, the findings call for a revision of the nursing curriculum to align clinical training requirements with the realities of resource-limited settings. Such coordinated actions are essential to enhance the quality of nursing education and produce clinically competent graduates.
