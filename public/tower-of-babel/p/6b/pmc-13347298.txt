Method development and validation for determination of entinostat in human plasma and its application in breast cancer patients
Authors: Shuo Li, Yuxiang Xu, Jiameng Dong, Yushuo Qiu, Zhixuan Yang, Xiaonan Gao, Jinglin Gao, Zhangying Feng, Caixue Wang, Mingxia Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Entinostat is an oral histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor used in HR+ breast cancer therapy, for which reliable plasma concentration monitoring is essential for dose optimization and toxicity management. This article described an ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) method for the quantitative analysis of entinostat in human plasma. Samples were concentrated under vacuum after methanol precipitation and separated on a Kinetex C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.6 µm). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile and an aqueous solution containing 10 mM ammonium acetate and 0.01% formic acid. The quantitative ion pair for entinostat was m/z 377.2 → 104.0, and for carbamazepine it was m/z 237.1 → 194.1, used as an internal standard (IS). The calibration curves showed good linearity (r2 > 0.99) across plasma concentrations ranging from 0.2 to 200 ng mL−1. Precision (RSD%) was within the range of 1.22–6.36%, accuracy ranged from 97.58% to 110.67%, the matrix effect ranged from 85% to 115%, and analyte recoveries ranged from 85% to 115%, meeting acceptable standards. This validated UPLC-MS/MS method was successfully applied to determine plasma concentration in seven breast cancer patients receiving 3 mg weekly doses of entinostat, providing a powerful tool for clinical pharmacokinetic studies and therapeutic drug monitoring.

Introduction

Entinostat, known as MS-275 or SNDX-275, is a novel, orally bioavailable benzamide compound belonging to the class I selective histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors.1 HDAC inhibitors inhibit tumor growth, induce cell differentiation, and promote cell apoptosis by increasing histone acetylation levels, remodeling the epigenetic environment of tumor cells, and restoring the transcriptional activity of tumor suppressor genes.2 Recent studies have further validated that HDAC inhibitors critically modulate various cellular pathways and tumor suppressor mechanisms, playing a vital role in overcoming cancer cell resistance.3 This selective mechanism of action makes entinostat well-tolerated and is shows great potential in the treatment of solid tumors and hematologic malignancies.

The clinical research progress of entinostat, especially in hormone receptor-positive (HR+) breast cancer, has attracted much attention. Entinostat can effectively overcome endocrine therapy resistance when used in combination with aromatase inhibitors (such as exemestane).4 Emerging evidence continues to emphasize that targeting these HDAC-driven epigenetic mechanisms is a highly effective strategy for decoding and reversing drug resistance in breast cancer treatment.5 Entinostat, a class 1 innovative drug, in combination with exemestane, has been officially approved by the China National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) for the treatment of HR+/HER2-advanced breast cancer, highlighting its important clinical value and demand.1 In a meta-analysis of four randomized controlled trials involving 1371 patients, entinostat in combination with exemestane demonstrated a significant progression-free survival advantage in HR+ breast cancer treatment, with a hazard ratio of 0.79 (95% CI: 0.68–0.92; P = 0.003) in the overall population. Subgroup analysis showed that this combination regimen is particularly beneficial in HER2-patients (HR = 0.80; P = 0.01) and patients with visceral metastases (HR = 0.77; P = 0.02). Although no statistically significant improvements were observed in overall survival, objective response rate, or clinical benefit rate, its potential in overcoming endocrine resistance and stabilizing disease progression remained of significant clinical value.6 Furthermore, recent long-term phase 3 clinical updates in 2024 have continued to demonstrate the efficacy and manageable safety of other HDAC inhibitors like tucidinostat in HR-positive/HER2-negative advanced breast cancer, reinforcing the clinical demand for this drug class.7 As the clinical application of entinostat continues to expand, accurate assessment of its in vivo exposure has become increasingly important for therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) and individualized treatment optimization.

However, the pharmacokinetic characteristics of entinostat present challenges for clinical monitoring. The drug is typically administered once weekly and has a long elimination half-life (average 33–150 hours),1 meaning that plasma drug concentrations (Cmin) at the end of the dosing interval are very low. Studies have shown that within the 3–7 mg dose range, the steady-state peak concentration of entinostat increases proportionally with the dose, from 55.7 ng mL−1 (3 mg) to 122 ng mL−1 (7 mg). The incidence of grade 3/4 adverse reactions (mainly neutropenia and thrombocytopenia) increased from 16.7% in the low-dose group to 33.3% in the high-dose group.8 Previous studies have demonstrated substantial inter-individual pharmacokinetic variability in entinostat systemic exposure and clearance, even among patients receiving the same dosing regimen.9 In addition, entinostat exposure has been associated with pharmacodynamic effects such as histone acetylation, while excessive systemic exposure may increase the risk of hematologic toxicities including neutropenia and thrombocytopenia.8 These findings suggest that TDM may be valuable for individualized exposure-guided dosing optimization. Therefore, accurate characterization of the entire plasma concentration–time profile, particularly the low-concentration data during the elimination phase, is essential for reliable pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) analysis and precise exposure assessment.

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) is widely used for the quantitative analysis of small-molecule anti-tumor drugs due to its high sensitivity and selectivity. In existing literature, although some studies have used LC-MS/MS method to assess the PK characteristics of entinostat in clinical trials10 or for drug interaction studies in animal plasma,11 most of the published methods have relatively insufficient lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) sensitivity, making it difficult to consistently and stably capture the ultra-low trough concentration of entinostat at the end of its long half-life elimination. This limits the accurate calculation of PK parameters, especially the accurate assessment of elimination rate.

Therefore, this study aimed to establish and fully validate a highly sensitive, selective, and rapid ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) method for the quantitative determination of entinostat in human plasma. Through innovative sample pretreatment optimization, this method successfully achieved an ultra-low LLOQ of 0.2 ng mL−1, significantly improving analytical sensitivity. The successful establishment and validation of this method would provide reliable and necessary technical support for in-depth PK studies of entinostat in breast cancer patients, accurate characterization of the elimination PK characteristic, and the promotion of personalized clinical dosing strategies.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Standard reference chemicals of entinostat (Lot: A220702, purity >98%) gifted by Taizhou EOC Pharma Co., Ltd, acetonitrile and methanol (HPLC grade) were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Fair Lawn, NJ, USA). Carbamazepine (Lot: 100142-202207), the internal standard, was purchased from the National Institutes for Food and Drug Control. Formic acid and ammonium acetate were obtained from Mreda Technology Inc. (Dallas, TX, USA). Ultrapure water was provided by A.S. Watson & Company (Guangzhou, China). Drug-free human plasma was obtained from healthy human volunteers (Fig. 1).

Equipment and UPLC-MS/MS conditions

Chromatography conditions

An ExionLC™ analytical (UPLC) system (AB Sciex, USA) coupled with an AB Sciex QTRAP® 5500 mass spectrometer (Applied Biosystems Inc., USA) was used for determination of entinostat in plasma samples. The chromatographic analysis was performed on a Kinetex C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.6 µm; Phenomenex, USA), and the temperature was maintained at 40 °C during analysis. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (aqueous solution containing 0.01% formic acid and 10 mM ammonium acetate) and solvent B (acetonitrile). The total run time for each sample was only 5.0 minutes. The autosampler temperature was set to 4 °C, and the injection volume was 3 µL. Preparation of calibration standards and quality control (QC) samples

Two independent stock solutions for entinostat to a concentration of 1 mg mL−1 in methanol–water (1 : 1, v/v), were used to prepare calibration curve and QC samples, respectively. Stock solution of IS was also made in methanol at a concentration of 1.0 mg mL−1. All of these solutions were stored at −80 °C before use. The work solutions of entinostat were freshly prepared by further diluting the stock solutions with methanol–water (1 : 1, v/v). Diluting the stock solution of the IS with methanol to obtain a final concentration of 4 ng mL−1, which would be used as the precipitation solvent for plasma. Calibration standards were prepared by adding 10 µL of the corresponding working solution to 90 µL of blank human plasma to achieve final concentrations of entinostat of 0.2, 0.5, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 ng mL−1. For QC solutions, the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ, 0.2 ng mL−1), low quality control (LQC, 0.4 ng mL−1), medium quality control (MQC, 40 ng mL−1), and high quality control (HQC, 160 ng mL−1) were all prepared using the same method.

Sample preparation

500 µL of precipitation solvent containing 4 ng mL−1 IS in methanol was added to 100 µL of plasma sample. The mixture was vortexed for 3 minutes and centrifuged at 13 000 rpm for 10 minutes at room temperature. Then, 300 µL of the supernatant was added to another 500 µL of precipitation solvent, vortexed for 1 minute, and centrifuged at 13 000 rpm for 10 minutes. Finally, The supernatant (700 µL) was concentrated to dryness under vacuum. The residue was reconstituted with a mixture of 200 µL of methanol and water (1 : 1, v/v), and then the mixture was vortexed for 5 minutes, and centrifuged at 13 000 rpm for 10 minutes. Finally, 3 µL of the supernatant was injected into the UPLC-MS/MS system for analysis.

Method validation

Specificity and selectivity

The selectivity of this method was assessed by comparing the chromatograms of blank plasma samples from six different sources with corresponding blank plasma samples containing entinostat and IS. These samples should be free of analyte interference at the retention time point (LLOQ response value <20% for entinostat, response value <5% for IS).

Carryover

Carryover was assessed by injecting a blank sample immediately after the highest calibration standard in plasma. The analyte peak area in the blank sample was required to be ≤20% of the LLOQ and ≤5% for the IS.

Linearity, lower limit of quantitation, limit of quantitation and limit of detection

The calibration curve was constructed by plotting the peak area ratios of entinostat to the IS against the nominal concentrations of entinostat (0.2, 0.5, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200 ng mL−1 in plasma), using a 1/x2 weighted linear least-squares regression. The software-generated correlation coefficient (r2) was required to exceed 0.99 to confirm linearity. The LLOQ was defined as the lowest calibration level with a signal-to-noise ratio ≥10 and an accuracy (relative standard deviation RSD%) within ±20%.The limit of detection (LOD) was determined based on the concentration at which the signal-to-noise ratio was approximately 3.

Precision and accuracy

To evaluate the intra- and inter-batch precision and accuracy of this method, five replicate measurements were performed on four concentration levels of entinostat quality control samples on the same day, followed by repeated measurements in three different batches at least two days apart. Except for the LLOQ (0.2 ng mL−1), the accuracy of each concentration level quality control sample should be within ±15%, and the accuracy of the LLOQ should be within ±20%. Except for the LLOQ, the precision of each concentration level quality control sample should not exceed 15%, and the precision of the LLOQ should not exceed 20%. Precision was expressed as relative standard deviation (RSD%), calculated using the formula: RSD% = (standard deviation/mean) ×100%; accuracy was expressed as the percentage of the measured concentration relative to the theoretical concentration. The concentration of each sample was calculated based on a prepared standard curve and analyzed within the same batch.

Matrix effect and recovery

The matrix effect was assessed by analyzing six replicates of LQC (0.4 ng mL−1) and HQC (160 ng mL−1), each replicate prepared using matrices from six different sources. The ratio of the peak area in the presence of matrix (added to the blank matrix after extraction) to the peak area in the absence of matrix (pure water sample) should be between 85% and 115%, and the RSD values should not exceed 15%. The extraction recovery was determined by comparing the peak area of analytes spiked into blank matrix prior to extraction with that of analytes spiked into blank matrix after extraction, both prepared using matrices from different sources.

Stability

To assess sample stability, five replicate samples were prepared at both LQC and HQC concentration levels and analyzed under the following conditions: freeze–thaw stability (frozen at −80 °C for 24 h and then thawed at room temperature, cycled 3 times), short-term stability (stored at room temperature for 4 h), long-term stability (stored at −80 °C for 120 days), and post-preparation stability (stored in an autosampler at room temperature for 48 h). Furthermore, the 48-hour autosampler injection reproducibility was evaluated. The stability of the stock solution stored at −80 °C for 350 days was assessed using five replicates at both the lowest and highest concentration levels.

Clinical application

This study protocol has been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval no.:2026KY017) and was conducted at the hospital. The study subjects were breast cancer patients treated with dalpiciclib (125 mg, qd, d1-21), entinostat (3 mg, qw), and letrozole (2.5 mg, qd, continuous administration). Each treatment cycle consisted of 28 days, for a total of 6 cycles. Plasma samples were collected primarily at the trough concentration points after each cycle. Results

Method validation

Selectivity and specificity

The selectivity of this method was confirmed by analyzing six blank plasma samples, i.e., no interference was detected during the retention time of the analyte. Fig. 3 showed the MRM chromatograms of blank plasma, plasma supplemented with 0.2 ng mL−1 entinostat and 4 ng mL−1 internal standard (IS), and a real sample. The retention times of entinostat and IS were 1.46 min and 1.99 min, respectively.

Carryover

The peak area of the analyte in the blank samples analyzed after the highest calibration standard was 2.5% of the LLOQ and 0.13% of the IS, which meets the requirements.

Linearity, lower limit of quantitation, limit of quantitation and limit of detection

Entinostat exhibited excellent linearity in plasma samples ranging from 0.2 to 200 ng mL−1, with a correlation coefficient r2 > 0.99. The LLOQ is 0.2 ng mL−1, with accuracy ranging from 80% to 120%, and RSD% of less than 20% for precision. Based on the signal-to-noise ratio of approximately 3, the LOD was determined to be 0.06 ng mL−1. This method fully meets the requirements for the quantitative determination of entinostat in clinical plasma samples.

Clinical application

This method has been successfully applied to the quantitative determination of entinostat in the plasma of breast cancer patients. The study included 7 patients treated with 3 mg entinostat weekly. The 16 collected samples represented pre-dose trough concentrations under a once-weekly dosing regimen, used to assess steady-state drug exposure levels after multiple doses. As shown in Fig. 4, box plots were used to analyze the trough concentration distribution across different dosing cycles (cycle 1–3). The results showed some inter-individual trough concentration differences within each cycle, but the overall distribution was relatively concentrated. The median levels across cycles were relatively similar, suggesting that drug steady-state trough concentration in vivo was relatively stable under weekly repeated dosing.

Discussion

This study successfully established a highly sensitive, selective, and rapid UPLC-MS/MS method for the quantitative determination of entinostat in human plasma. This method lowers the LLOQ to 0.2 ng mL−1, significantly better than the 0.5 ng mL−1 reported in previous literature.10 Considering that entinostat is typically administered once weekly and has a long elimination half-life (33–150 hours).1 The exceptional sensitivity of this method is essential for the reliable quantification of trace concentrations during the terminal elimination phase, especially given the extended seven-day dosing interval.

Previous studies13 have indicated that at late-stage pharmacodynamic assessment points, plasma drug concentrations are often below the 0.5 ng mL−1 detection limit, making it impossible to establish a correlation between plasma drug concentration and protein acetylation levels, thus limiting in-depth exploration of PK/PD correlations. This method not only ensures the completeness and accuracy of pharmacokinetic parameter calculations but also provides a reliable technical means for establishing a complete ‘concentration-acetylation-efficacy’ correlation chain at extremely low exposure levels in the future. Furthermore, this method requires only 3 µL supernatant of injection volume for UPLC MS/MS to achieve ultrasensitive detection, far lower than the 20 µL reported in the literature.10 This not only effectively extends the lifespan of the chromatographic column but also greatly conserves precious clinical plasma samples. Regarding the choice of internal standard (IS) during method development, carbamazepine was selected rather than a structural analog or an isotope-labeled compound. Carbamazepine exhibits a highly stable signal response and excellent peak shape under positive ionization mode, with no chromatographic or ionization interference from entinostat or endogenous plasma components. Furthermore, its retention time (1.99 min) is close to that of entinostat (1.46 min), ensuring comparable chromatographic behavior. In addition, acceptable matrix effect and recovery were achieved after optimized sample pretreatment (Table 5).

In terms of biological sample pretreatment, this study optimized a simple protein precipitation method using methanol as the precipitant, eliminating the cumbersome liquid–liquid extraction and reducing operational complexity. However, this method often cannot completely remove endogenous interfering substances in plasma, such as phospholipids and salts. These substances can easily cause matrix effects during electrospray ionization (ESI) in mass spectrometry, manifesting as ion suppression or signal enhancement, thereby reducing the accuracy and reproducibility of quantitative results.16,17 Recent bioanalytical practices highly emphasize the systematic assessment and methodological optimization to correct matrix effects, ensuring the robustness of quantitative LC-MS/MS methods in complex biological matrices.18

In the early stages of method development, significant matrix suppression was observed following protein precipitation. The matrix factor was only 60–70%, affecting the accuracy of quantification. A systematic comparison of different precipitants (methanol, acetonitrile, and a 1 : 1 methanol–acetonitrile mixture) showed that they were almost indistinguishable in mitigating matrix interference under various conditions, with matrix factors of 64.17%, 66.83%, and 63.41%, respectively. By investigating the effect of different volume ratios of supernatant and precipitant on the matrix effect in the secondary precipitation step, the results showed that when 300 µL of supernatant was added to 500 µL of methanol, the matrix factor was 94.12%; while when 400 µL of supernatant was added to 500 µL and 400 µL of methanol respectively, the matrix factor decreased to 85.26% and 84.27% respectively. Based on economic considerations, methanol was selected as the precipitant, and 300 µL of supernatant was adopted for the secondary precipitation. Furthermore, to further reduce parallel operation errors and ensure complete removal of residual solvent, we employed a longer drying time in the vacuum concentration stage. The final methodological validation results showed that the matrix effect and recovery rate met acceptable criteria. This indicated that secondary precipitation effectively mitigated the matrix effect and ensured the reliability of entinostat quantitative results. Although the sample pretreatment slightly increased the preparation time, it significantly improved matrix suppression and analytical sensitivity while avoiding the higher cost and operational complexity associated with liquid–liquid extraction or solid-phase extraction methods. This optimized strategy provided a practical and reliable solution for bioanalyses involving drugs with high matrix effects.

Recent advances in precision oncology have highlighted the growing importance of epigenetic-targeted therapies and individualized exposure evaluation in optimizing anticancer treatment outcomes.19 Based on the trough concentration data obtained in this study, the observed consistency across cycles provides preliminary evidence of relatively stable trough concentrations for entinostat under a once-weekly dosing regimen in breast cancer patients. Referring to the conclusions of the Phase I clinical study, entinostat could reach a steady state around day 22.8 The highly overlapping trough concentration distributions across different cycles in this study also validate the pharmacokinetic stability of this dosing regimen in clinical applications. It is worth emphasizing that this relatively stable and maintainable low-level exposure is crucial for the efficacy of HDAC inhibitors. As an epigenetic regulatory drug, the antitumor effect of entinostat does not primarily depend on an instantaneous peak concentration, but rather on the cumulative effect of histone acetylation induced by sustained exposure and subsequent chromatin remodeling and gene expression regulation.20 Previous studies have shown that although HDAC inhibitors can induce increased histone acetylation levels in a short period, their downstream transcriptional regulation and cellular phenotypic changes often require sustained drug action of 24–48 hours to be fully realized.1 Therefore, maintaining a stable in vivo exposure window is more critical than a single high-concentration exposure.

This study found that the trough concentrations from cycle 1 to cycle 3 were generally stable and showed no significant accumulation, suggesting that the current once-weekly dosing regimen could avoid excessive drug accumulation while maintaining relatively stable trough concentrations, providing a pharmacokinetic basis for its long-term epigenetic regulatory effects. Furthermore, as reported by Pili et al.,9 although the overall trend was stable, some inter-individual differences were still observed, which may be related to differences in metabolic enzyme activity, concomitant medications, and individual patient physiological states. The median trough concentration showed a slight decreasing trend across different cycles, but this did not demonstrate significant clinical significance. However, the clinical application component included only seven patients, and therefore these preliminary observations should be interpreted cautiously and require validation in larger patient cohorts.

From a clinical perspective, trough concentration, as an important indicator of minimum systemic exposure, suggests that entinostat can maintain a sustained window of action under the current dosing regimen, which is crucial for its antitumor and immunomodulatory effects. Previous clinical studies have indicated that increased entinostat exposure is associated with a higher incidence of hematologic toxicities such as neutropenia and thrombocytopenia,2 however, a definitive therapeutic concentration window has not yet been established. The high-sensitivity method developed in this study can provide accurate drug exposure data, which may facilitate future PK/PD correlation analyses and exposure-toxicity investigations. Ultimately, this analytical foundation could support the future development of individualized dosing regimens to minimize potential hematologic toxicity while ensuring antitumor efficacy. A limitation of this study is that the clinical application was evaluated in a small cohort of only seven patients, and that intensive pharmacokinetic sampling was not performed to determine parameters like AUC and clearance. Future studies will focus on larger-scale clinical validation of the developed UPLC-MS/MS method, including intensive pharmacokinetic sampling and population pharmacokinetic analysis. Further investigations on PK/PD and exposure–toxicity relationships may also support individualized dosing strategies and therapeutic drug monitoring applications for entinostat therapy.

Conclusions

In this study, a highly sensitive and selective UPLC-MS/MS method was developed and fully validated for the quantification of entinostat in human plasma according to ICH M10 guidelines. By employing a double protein precipitation and vacuum concentration strategy, we effectively mitigated matrix effects and achieved a low LLOQ of 0.2 ng mL−1 using only 3 µL of injection volume. The method demonstrated excellent linearity, precision, and accuracy. Its practical utility was successfully demonstrated through the preliminary monitoring of trough concentrations in seven breast cancer patients, revealing detectable levels across multiple cycles. This robust analytical tool provides a solid technical foundation for future large-scale pharmacokinetic studies and the exploration of personalized dosing strategies for entinostat in clinical practice.
