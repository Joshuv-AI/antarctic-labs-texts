A 57‐Year‐Old Male With Behavioral Variant Frontotemporal Dementia and MATR3 and NOS3 Mutations
Authors: Feifei Lin, Saie Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis report presents a case of behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia caused by mutations in the MATR3 and NOS3 genes, aiming to analyze its clinical manifestations and genetic characteristics. For a case presenting with personality changes and gait abnormalities as the initial symptoms, this study conducted a comprehensive analysis of its clinical manifestations, neuropsychological assessments, cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, head imaging changes, and genetic test results. It also discussed the possible causes based on the literature. Its main manifestations include personality changes, gait disorders, and a decline in executive functions. 18F‐FDG PET shows decreased FDG metabolism in both bilateral frontal lobes, temporal lobes, and anterior cingulate gyrus, accompanied by corresponding brain atrophy, and no abnormal deposition of Aβ in both cerebral hemispheres. The related gene detection indicates mutations in the MATR3 gene (NM_018834.6: intron 11: c.1778 + 3A > G) and NOS3 gene (NM_000603.5: exon 20: c.2435C > A). There is no report of the coexistence of these two genes. The clinical diagnosis is behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia(bvFTD). The conclusion is that the mutations in MATR3 and NOS3 are likely to be associated with the pathological process of frontotemporal dementia by altering the structure and function of the encoded proteins. The identification of these variations provides new candidate gene combinations for the genetic variation spectrum of frontotemporal dementia, which should be regarded as hypothetical generative observation results. The clinical significance of these results needs to be further verified.

Introduction

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is a group of neurodegenerative diseases characterized by progressive atrophy of the frontal and temporal lobes. It mainly presents with behavioral and personality changes as well as language dysfunction. It accounts for approximately 10% to 15% of all dementia cases and is the second most common type of dementia among individuals aged 45 to 65 [1]. Based on the early symptoms, the clinical classification mainly includes three subtypes: Behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD), non‐fluent aphasia (PNFA), and semantic dementia (SD) [2, 3]. Behavioral variant FTD (bvFTD): Behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD) is the most common subtype of frontotemporal dementia, accounting for approximately 50% of all cases. Its clinical manifestations mainly include personality changes and a decline in executive function. About 20% of bvFTD patients exhibit symptoms of Parkinson's syndrome in the early stage of the disease course [4, 5]. Primary progressive non‐fluent aphasia (PNFA) is a language subtype of frontotemporal dementia, characterized primarily by progressive decline in language abilities, while the ability to name and understand words remains relatively intact [4]. Semantic dementia (SD) typically presents with progressive decline in language comprehension and naming abilities, and as the disease progresses, it can affect reading and writing. This subtype can be classified into the left temporal lobe dominant type and the right temporal lobe dominant type: Left temporal lobe atrophy often leads to language comprehension difficulties, while right temporal lobe atrophy is more likely to cause face agnosia and behavioral changes [4, 5]. As the disease progresses, patients will gradually develop cognitive impairments and various motor deficits. For example, they may suffer from Parkinson's syndrome (PD), and some patients may also concurrently develop motor neuron disease (MND), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), cortical basal ganglia syndrome (CBS), and progressive supranuclear palsy syndrome (PSPS), etc. [5, 6]. As the disease progresses, patients will gradually develop cognitive impairments and various motor deficits. For example, let's introduce a case of a 57‐year‐old male. The patient presented with personality and gait changes as the initial symptoms. After the condition worsened, further examinations confirmed the diagnosis of frontotemporal dementia.

Case Reports

The patient is a 57‐year‐old man with a college education. The patient was admitted to the hospital due to “behavioral and personality changes for over 2 years, and motor retardation for over 1 year”. About 2 years before admission, the patient gradually developed personality changes without obvious triggers. Previously, he was calm and friendly, but later became indifferent to others, less enthusiastic, irritable, and exhibited small steps and memory decline. His family did not pay attention at that time. After attending a friend's party and being reminded of the gait problem by a friend, he visited a tertiary hospital in Fujian Province in August 2023. Based on the symptoms, Parkinson's disease was considered. Since then, he has been treated according to the Parkinson's disease diagnosis for over 1 year. The condition progressed rapidly, and the patient showed impulsive behavior, such as unwillingness to wait for red lights, repetitive shopping, motor retardation, involuntary shaking of the right hand, stereotyped speech, untidy appearance when going out, rebellious mentality, excessive appetite, and a preference for sweet foods. However, basic daily life skills such as washing, brushing teeth, eating, and the simple operation of electronic devices were retained. The patient had received treatment at multiple medical institutions during this period, and the condition showed a slow progression. In April 2025, due to work stress, the condition significantly worsened, with occasional choking when drinking water and urinary incontinence. He was referred to our hospital for further treatment. Since the onset of the disease, the patient has experienced changes in mental state, specifically manifested as unstable mood, irritability, lack of emotional response, and weakened empathy. At the same time, there was increased appetite, disobedience in behavior, reduced initiative, loss of interest in daily activities, etc. Sleep and defecation were normal, and there was no significant change in weight.

Past medical history, personal history, and family history: There was a history of hypertension for 2 years. Recently, blood pressure was controlled by taking amlodipine combined with sacubitril/valsartan (50 mg each time, once daily). There were no other medical histories. There was no history of smoking or drinking. No other genetic predispositions or infectious diseases in the family were reported. There were no similar patients either. No consanguineous marriage was reported.

Admission physical examination: Consciousness is clear, but the emotional state is unstable. With a flustered gait, slow movement, slightly slurred speech, decreased memory and calculation ability, most of the comprehension, attention, and orientation remain. Mild limitation in outward eye movement of both eyes, bilateral pupils are equal in size and round, light, and corneal reflexes exist. Muscle strength of the right limb is 4+ grade, the muscle tone of the right upper limb is slightly higher, the muscle strength and tone of the other limbs are normal, and the tendon reflex on the right side is more active and symmetrical compared to the left side. Deep and superficial sensations are symmetrical. The ataxia test is completed. The cerebellar irritation sign is negative. Pain reflex is not elicited.

Cognitive score sheet: Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE): 23/30, Montreal Cognitive Assessment Scale (MOCA): 21/30, Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL): 4/8, Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST): The results show significant deficits in executive function, Clinical Dementia Rating plus National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration domains (CDR plus NACC FTLD) (Table 1). To conduct a more detailed assessment of the cognitive domain, the FTLD‐modified CDR was used. The specific assessment procedure and scoring rationale are as follows:

To systematically evaluate the severity of the patient's condition and the pattern of impairment across different cognitive domains, a formal assessment using the FTLD‐modified CDR was administered during the patient's hospitalization (in the second week after admission) by a uniformly trained neurologist. This assessment was conducted specifically for the preparation of this case report, based on a semi‐structured face‐to‐face interview with the patient and the primary caregiver (the patient's spouse), focusing on the patient's typical functional performance in the month prior to admission. The assessment was carried out around 10:00 a.m., and the entire process took approximately 45 min. It consisted of two parts: First, the caregiver (the patient's spouse) was interviewed separately regarding the patient's daily performance in eight domains (memory, directive force, judgment and problem solving, social affairs, family and hobbies, personal care, behavior/personality, and language); subsequently, a brief behavioral observation and cognitive verification were conducted with the patient. The specific scoring rationale is as follows:

Memory (1 point, mild damage): During the interview, the patient himself forgot his job position and workplace before the onset of illness. Instead, he recalled a previous job position and workplace, and he was also unable to recall the contents of breakfast, etc. The spouse reported that the patient often forgot recent appointments but could recall remote past events.

Directive force (0 points, no damage): The patient could accurately state the current year, month, date, hospital location, and the name of his attending physician, and showed no disorientation within the hospital.

Judgment and problem solving (2 points, moderate damage): As detailed above, specific manifestations included: (1) repeatedly refusing to wait for traffic lights; (2) repetitive shopping; (3) pathological opposition/defiance; (4) when asked “What would you do if the faucet broke?”, answering “I don't know” and being unable to propose a reasonable solution.

Social affairs (2 points, moderate damage): The spouse reported that the patient was no longer able to engage in work or volunteer activities.

Family and hobbies (1 point, mild damage): The patient could assist with simple household chores such as folding clothes and setting the table, but had completely abandoned previously cherished activities and had difficulty learning new game rules.

Personal care (0.5 points, suspected/Minor Damage): The patient could independently complete washing, dressing, and eating, but the spouse noted that he occasionally wore clothes backwards and required reminders from others.

Behavior/personality (3 points, Severe damage): The spouse indicated that the patient exhibited persistent apathy and complete indifference to family members; stereotyped speech and aimless wandering were also present.

Language (0.5 points, suspected/Minor Damage): The spouse observed that the patient occasionally had word‐finding difficulties, but there was no comprehension deficit, and he could follow two‐step commands.

Auxiliary examinations: Laboratory tests: Blood routine, complete biochemical profile, and urine routine showed no obvious abnormalities. D‐dimer: 1.77 ug/ml ↑, coagulation five items: fibrinogen FIB: 4.01 g/L ↑. Report of the 4‐item detection for Alzheimer's disease biomarkers (2025.09.09 from another hospital): Cerebrospinal fluid Aβ42, Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, p‐Tau217, and GFAP were all normal. Alzheimer's disease risk detection report: Low risk. Imaging examinations: PET‐MR head (2025.02.11 from another hospital): Bilateral caudate nuclei, bilateral putamen anterior and posterior parts, dopamine transporter (DAT) distribution decreased, white matter hyperintensity (Fazekas grade 2), senile brain changes, and a small amount of inflammation in the left maxillary sinus. Autoimmune encephalitis spectrum (2025.2.27 from another hospital): All negative. PET‐CT brain(2025.03.04 from another hospital): Bilateral frontal lobe, temporal lobe, and anterior cingulate gyrus FDG metabolism decreased, accompanied by corresponding brain atrophy. Bilateral cerebral hemispheres Aβ showed no abnormal deposition, considered to be a high possibility of FTD (Figure 1). Combined with symptoms and auxiliary examinations, the diagnosis of frontotemporal dementia was made. Genetic testing and bioinformatics analysis: Collected 2 mL of EDTA anticoagulated blood from the patient for enhancing whole exome sequencing analysis by Guangzhou Omon Yiyi Medical Laboratory Genetics. Genetic test results: ① The MATR3 gene has a mutation site in the 5th chromosome intron with a heterozygous variation, c.1778 + 3A>G (adenine > guanine), and this variation is located in a highly conserved splicing donor site, which can lead to abnormal RNA splicing. This site variation has a frequency of ExAC: 0.000079, thousand people: 0.000599042, gonmAD: 0.0001, not included in Esp6500. ClinVar database: The significance is not clear. Through SIFT, PolyPhen‐2, and other software, predictions cannot give results, while dbSNV and MaxEntScan, etc., splicing prediction tools strongly predict that it will destroy the wild‐type splicing site. Based on the above evidence, according to the ACMG sequence variation interpretation guideline: PP3, it is recommended to determine that this variation site is a clinical unexplained variation. ② NOS3 gene mutation site is c.2435C > A heterozygous mutation in exon 20 of chromosome 7, resulting in the 812th amino acid from cysteine to a stop codon. This site variation has a frequency of ExAC, thousand people, Esp6500 not included, gonmAD: 0.000064. ClinVar database: Not included. Through PolyPhen‐2, MutationTaster software prediction is “probably harmful”, while SIFT, LRpred, etc. tools predict as “tolerable”. The pathogenicity integration score of REVEL is in the medium range, suggesting uncertainty. In addition, this variation does not show an impact on RNA splicing. Based on the above evidence and following the ACMG sequence variant interpretation guidelines, this variant was classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). Subsequently, a confirmatory test for this site was conducted on the patient's only daughter, confirming the existence of the variation (Figure 2). Combined with the patient's clinical manifestations, laboratory tests, imaging examinations, and genetic test results, the clinical diagnosis was behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia(bvFTD).

Treatment and Follow‐up: Symptomatic treatment was given, including memantine (10 mg each time, twice daily), sertraline (100 mg each time, once daily), co‐beneldopa (0.125 g each time, three times daily), and carbidopa/levodopa sustained‐release tablets (0.5 tablets each time, twice daily). Blood pressure was controlled, blood sugar changes were monitored, and acupuncture and rehabilitation therapy were also provided. After continuous treatment for one month, the patient's symptoms and signs improved, especially in terms of basic daily living ability, and the emotional state became more stable. However, no significant changes were observed in the cognitive function assessment. Two months later, during a follow‐up call, the overall condition was stable, and no significant progress was noted.

Discussion

This patient presented with personality changes and abnormal gait as the initial symptoms, and subsequently gradually developed a series of symptoms, such as a decline in executive function. Neuropsychological assessment indicated that the patient had impairments in executive function, memory, abstract thinking, and mental emotions. 18F‐FDG/AV‐1 cranial PET‐CT tomography showed decreased FDG metabolism in both frontal and temporal lobes, anterior cingulate gyrus, accompanied by corresponding brain atrophy, and no abnormal deposition of Aβ in both cerebral hemispheres. PET‐MR head scan showed reduced distribution of dopamine transporter (DAT) in both the caudate nucleus and anterior and posterior parts of the putamen. In addition, relevant examinations have ruled out Alzheimer's disease, autoimmune encephalitis, and mental disorders, etc. Based on the above clinical and imaging features, this case is diagnosed as behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD).

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) has a significant genetic predisposition. Approximately 30% to 50% of patients exhibit familial clustering. The genetic pattern is mainly autosomal dominant inheritance. It has been confirmed that most familial cases are caused by mutations in three major pathogenic genes: Microtubule‐associated protein tau (MAPT), progranulin (GRN), and chromosome 9 C9orf72 [7]. The MAPT gene mutation is mainly associated with behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia and semantic primary progressive aphasia [8]. Mutations in the GRN gene usually lead to the pathology of TAR‐DNA binding protein 43 (TDP‐43), and the clinical phenotype is mainly behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia. In a few cases, it can present as primary progressive aphasia [9]. The C9orf72 gene duplication amplification is the most common genetic cause of FTD, and it is often accompanied by TDP‐43 pathology. Its phenotypes include behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and progressive supranuclear palsy, etc. [8, 10]. The causes of FTD are complex, and the underlying mechanism is unclear. There are differences in gene mutations both domestically and internationally. In China, mapt mutations are common, while in other countries, c9orf72 mutations are predominant [11]. In addition, there are multiple rare genes associated with FTD, such as FUS, TAR DNA binding protein (TARDBP), protein containing Valosin (VCP), charged multivesicular body protein 2b (CHMP2B) mutations, TBK1, Ccnf, etc. However, their total proportion in all cases is usually less than 5% [7, 12]. The patient in this article was found to have variations in the MATR3 gene (NM_018834.6: intron11: c.1778 + 3A > G) and the NOS3 gene (NM_000603.5: exon20: c.2435C > A) through NGS sequencing of disease‐related genes. The same gene site variations were also found in the patient's only daughter through family verification. Currently, there is no sufficient evidence‐based medical evidence to support that these variations alone can cause frontotemporal dementia (FTD). A review of relevant databases revealed that Wang Rui and others reported a case of FTD with PAXIP1 and NOS3 gene mutations in 2020, suggesting that mutations in related genes may be associated with the onset of FTD [13]. In 2020, Castro and colleagues discovered a case of early‐onset behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia caused by the MATR3 gene, suggesting that the abnormal retrotransposition process of the MATR3 gene may be associated with the pathogenesis of frontotemporal dementia [14]. Although independent cases of MATR3 or NOS3 gene variations have been reported in frontotemporal dementia (FTD), the coexistence of both in the same patient has not been previously documented. The MATR3 gene has been confirmed to be associated with the genetics and pathology of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and frontotemporal dementia (ALS/FTD), and some cases may also present with distal myopathy. The protein it encodes (Matrin‐3) is an intrinsically disordered RNA‐binding protein composed of 847 amino acids, capable of binding both RNA and DNA, and is widely involved in key cellular activities such as transcriptional regulation, RNA splicing and stability, DNA damage response, and cell differentiation. Studies have shown that Matrin‐3 has a direct physiological interaction with TDP‐43; for instance, co‐immunoprecipitation of the two can be detected in cells, and certain MATR3 mutants (such as S85C) can enhance the binding with TDP‐43. Additionally, it also interacts with other disease‐related proteins such as FUS. MATR3 may affect cognitive function in neurodegenerative diseases by participating in complex protein interaction networks [15, 16]. Based on this, it can be speculated that the MATR3 gene mutation located at the +3 position downstream of the highly conserved “AG” splicing donor site (NM_018834.6: intron11: c.1778 + 3A > G) is likely to disrupt the normal RNA splicing process, causing the 11th exon to be skipped or introducing an early termination codon, thereby generating functionally abnormal proteins, and thereby promoting abnormal deposition of related pathological proteins such as TDP‐43 and FUS. However, for the specific variant c.1778 + 3A>G of the MATR3 gene, how its integration into the genome through the retrotransposition mechanism leads to abnormal expression and affects cell function is currently unclear. In the study of the NOS3 gene, the common functional polymorphism sites are as follows: T‐786C (rs2070744), intron 4a/b VNTR, and G894T (rs1799983) [17, 18]. In contrast, the c.2435C>A variant (reference sequence NM_000603.5) on the NOS3 gene is also located in the 7th exon, but it is an extremely rare mutation. The allele frequency of this site in the public population genetic database is extremely low (typically < 0.01%), and there is a lack of functional studies and epidemiological data. Its functional effect and clinical significance still need to be further explored. It is worth noting that in the studies of the Chinese population, the T allele of the common variant G894T (rs1799983) located in the same exon has been proven to be significantly associated with an increased risk of sporadic frontotemporal dementia, and is a clear genetic risk factor [19].

The NOS3 gene encodes endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), which is a key enzyme that catalyzes the production of nitric oxide (NO). NO, as an endothelium‐derived vasodilator, mediates endothelium‐dependent vasodilation, inhibits platelet aggregation, and regulates systemic blood pressure. A decrease in eNOS activity leads to a reduction in NO production, which may result in weakened cerebral vascular dilation capacity and microcirculation disorders, ultimately leading to chronic cerebral blood flow insufficiency [20]. This persistent state of cerebral hypoperfusion may primarily affect brain regions with high metabolic demands, such as the frontal and temporal lobes, thereby leading to neurodegenerative disorders. Studies have suggested that the NO‐cGMP signaling pathway can regulate the activities of various kinases, including GSK‐3β, and these kinases are the key factors regulating the phosphorylation state of tau protein. Therefore, the abnormal function of NOS3 may indirectly affect the phosphorylation of tau protein through this pathway, causing its binding ability to microtubules to decrease, and subsequently leading to the aggregation and formation of insoluble deposits [20, 21, 22, 23]. In conclusion, NOS3 may influence the pathological and physiological process of frontotemporal dementia through two pathways: Affecting cerebral vascular function and tau protein metabolism within nerve cells. The significant limitations of this study lie in the lack of family separation analysis and longitudinal follow‐up data for the proband's daughter. Although we detected that her daughter carried the same MATR3 and NOS3 mutations, due to practical constraints, we were unable to obtain the genetic and clinical information of her parents, siblings, and other collateral relatives. Therefore, we were unable to assess whether this mutation was segregating in the family as a whole; at the same time, it is not clear whether her only daughter will develop clinical manifestations related to bvFTD in the future. These limitations significantly restrict the determination of definitive conclusions regarding the pathogenicity and genetic pattern of this mutation. They cannot rule out the possibility that it is a familial genetic disorder with incomplete penetrance, nor can they confirm the causal relationship. They merely serve as hypothetical generative observation results. In the future, we need to continue conducting longitudinal clinical follow‐ups for her only daughter and expand the family collection as much as possible to accumulate evidence for separation analysis and further clarify the clinical significance of this mutation.

In conclusion, this patient was detected to carry mutations in the MATR3 gene (NM_018834.6: intron11: c.1778 + 3A > G) and the NOS3 gene (NM_000603.5: exon20: c.2435C > A). These mutations may affect the structure and function of the encoded proteins and are related to pathological changes. This study reports a case of behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD) associated with mutations in the MATR3 and NOS3 genes, expanding the known genetic variations of frontotemporal dementia and providing new clues for the genetic research of this disease. As for whether these two mutations directly participate in the pathogenesis of FTD, more cases and in‐depth functional studies are needed for verification in the future.
