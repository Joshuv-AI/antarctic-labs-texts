Incretin-Based Therapies in Doxorubicin-Induced Cardiotoxicity: A Systematic Review of GLP-1 and Dual GIP/GLP-1 Agonists
Authors: Seyedhesamoddin Khatami, Mohammadsadegh Faghihi, Ghazal Tavakoli, Sebastian Szmit, Mahmoud Yousefifard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractDoxorubicin (DOX)-induced cardiotoxicity remains a major limitation of cancer therapy. Incretin-based therapies are established cardiometabolic agents with pleiotropic cardiovascular effects; however, their potential role in mitigating DOX-related cardiac injury has not been systematically synthesized. We conducted a PRISMA 2020-compliant systematic review to evaluate the effects of incretin-based therapies on DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, with a focus on functional, structural, biomarker, and mechanistic outcomes. PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus were searched through 20 October 2025. Eligible studies included in vivo rodent models of DOX cardiotoxicity and any clinical studies directly evaluating incretin-based therapy during DOX exposure; in vitro studies, non-DOX models, combination-treatment studies, studies not focused on cardiotoxicity, gene therapy-based interventions, and reviews/editorials were excluded. Risk of bias and certainty of evidence were assessed using the SYRCLE tool and GRADE adapted for preclinical research. Thirteen rodent studies were included, and no eligible human study was identified. Investigated agents included liraglutide (n = 4), exenatide/exendin-4 (n = 4), semaglutide (n = 2), and tirzepatide (n = 3). In chronic cumulative-dose DOX models, incretin-based therapies were generally associated with preservation of left ventricular systolic function, with between-study improvements of approximately 7–20 percentage points in left ventricular ejection fraction, along with reductions in injury biomarkers. These effects were accompanied by attenuation of oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and, in some studies, ferroptosis. In contrast, findings were less consistent in acute single-dose models. Co-treatment during DOX exposure showed the most reproducible protective signal, whereas isolated pretreatment with liraglutide or tirzepatide and post-treatment with exenatide did not show a clear additional benefit. The evidence base is limited by exclusive reliance on small heterogeneous animal studies, predominantly male models, variable dosing/timing protocols, and low-to-very-low certainty of evidence. Introduction

Anthracycline-based chemotherapy remains a cornerstone of treatment for many solid and hematologic malignancies but is limited by dose-dependent cardiotoxicity that can culminate in heart failure and premature cardiovascular death long after cancer cure [1–3]. Survivors exposed to anthracyclines carry a sustained excess risk of cardiomyopathy, ischemic heart disease, and heart failure, with risk further amplified by older age and pre-existing cardiometabolic disease [4–6]. As cancer survival improves, anthracycline-related cardiovascular disease has become a leading contributor to morbidity, loss of quality-adjusted life-years, and health-care costs across the survivorship continuum [7].

Contemporary cardio-oncology guidelines emphasize baseline cardiovascular risk stratification, surveillance with echocardiography and biomarkers, and early initiation of heart failure therapy to mitigate cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD) [8, 9]. Dexrazoxane is the only agent formally recommended for primary cardioprotection in selected high-risk patients, and clinical trials of neurohormonal blockade or statins have shown mixed or population-specific benefit [10–12]. Recent meta-analyses suggest that multiple cardioprotective strategies may attenuate anthracycline cardiotoxicity, yet none directly target the profound metabolic, inflammatory, and redox disturbances that characterize CTRCD, and residual risk remains substantial [13–16].

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) and the dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide/GLP-1 agonist tirzepatide have transformed the management of type 2 diabetes and obesity, reducing major adverse cardiovascular events, improving heart failure–related symptoms, and favorably remodeling cardiac structure in at-risk populations [17, 18]. Large cardiovascular outcome trials and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) programs demonstrate consistent benefits on weight, blood pressure, natriuretic peptides, and functional capacity, alongside anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-atherosclerotic effects [19, 20]. These pleiotropic actions intersect with central pathways implicated in anthracycline cardiotoxicity, including mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, endothelial activation, and maladaptive remodeling, making incretin-based therapies attractive candidates for mechanism-directed cardioprotection in cardio-oncology [2, 15].

Despite extensive clinical experience with GLP-1RAs and tirzepatide in cardiometabolic disease, their role in preventing or attenuating anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity has not been systematically characterized. Experimental data examining incretin-based therapies in doxorubicin (DOX)–induced cardiotoxicity have emerged across multiple rodent models and agents, but are methodologically heterogeneous, variably report cardiac function, injury biomarkers, histology, and mechanistic endpoints, and have not previously been synthesized in a dedicated cardio-oncology framework. Important uncertainties therefore persist regarding the consistency, magnitude, and timing of cardioprotective effects across incretin agents and the translational relevance of preclinical findings to contemporary cardio-oncology practice. Although a few emerging clinical studies have examined GLP-1 receptor agonists in broader populations with CTRCD or anthracycline-induced cardiomyopathy [21, 22], these investigations did not isolate doxorubicin-specific cardiotoxicity and therefore do not address the central question of the present review. Accordingly, the present review should be interpreted primarily as a synthesis of preclinical evidence rather than as evidence for current clinical use.

Accordingly, we conducted a PRISMA 2020–compliant, PROSPERO-registered systematic review to evaluate the effects of GLP-1RAs and dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists on DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Our objective was to collate and critically appraise experimental and clinical evidence in which animals or patients exposed to DOX received incretin-based therapy compared with DOX alone or appropriate controls, and in which cardiac function, structural remodeling, biomarkers of injury and stress, or mechanistic pathways were reported. By integrating qualitative synthesis, we sought to clarify whether incretin-based therapies consistently mitigate DOX cardiotoxicity, to explore sources of heterogeneity across models and outcomes, and to delineate key knowledge gaps to guide future translational and clinical research in cardio-oncology.

Methods

This systematic review was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420251083663) and conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible if they evaluated incretin-based therapies, including glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists or dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide/glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, in in vivo rodent models of doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity. We also prespecified inclusion of randomized or observational clinical studies directly evaluating incretin-based therapy during DOX treatment versus an appropriate comparator; however, no such human study was identified. We excluded in vitro studies, non-exclusive DOX models, combination-treatment studies, studies not specifically focused on cardiotoxicity, gene therapy-based interventions, and reviews or editorials.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus from inception to 20 October 2025 without language, year, or publication-status restrictions. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were hand-searched, and forward citation tracking was also performed. Search strategies were developed using controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for doxorubicin and incretin-based therapies. The full reproducible search strategies for all databases are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Selection and Data Collection Process

After deduplication, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Data were extracted in duplicate using a standardized, piloted form that captured study characteristics, animal model details, DOX regimen, incretin-based intervention, comparator groups, outcome measures, numerical results, and timing of assessment. No automation tools were used.

Data Items

The primary data items concerned outcomes relevant to DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. We extracted measures of cardiac function, including left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), fractional shortening (LVFS), ventricular diameters and wall thickness, diastolic indices, and invasive hemodynamic parameters such as maximal and minimal dP/dt and stroke volume (SV), as well as isolated-heart performance where reported. We collected electrocardiographic variables, including heart rate, QRS duration, QT and QTc intervals, and other conduction or repolarization indices. We recorded circulating and tissue biomarkers of myocardial injury and wall stress, such as troponin I or T, Creatine kinase–MB (CK-MB), Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) or NT-proBNP, and ventricular Nppa/Nppb expression.

Histological outcomes included both qualitative and quantitative assessments of myocardial necrosis, inflammatory infiltrates, interstitial or replacement fibrosis, cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area, fatty infiltration, vascular congestion, and composite injury scores. Mechanistic outcomes included markers of oxidative stress, such as malondialdehyde (MDA), dihydroethidium (DHE) fluorescence, and protein carbonyls; antioxidant capacity, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase, and glutathione; and inflammatory mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1). Apoptotic markers included terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining, cleaved caspase-3, and Bax/Bcl-2 expression. In addition, signaling pathways related to cell survival, stress response, autophagy, and redox regulation were evaluated, including phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt), AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), and nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2/heme oxygenase-1 (Nrf2/HO-1) pathways (Tables 1, 2).

Study Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias in individual studies was assessed using the SYRCLE risk of bias tool for animal intervention studies, which is adapted from the Cochrane risk of bias instrument. Two reviewers (GT and SK) independently evaluated each study across the domains of sequence generation, allocation concealment, baseline comparability, random housing, blinding of caregivers, blinding of outcome assessors, completeness of outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias. Each domain was rated as low, unclear, or high risk of bias according to predefined criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. Effect Measures and Synthesis Methods

All quantitative outcomes reported by individual studies were extracted and expressed as mean ± SD. When results were presented as mean ± SEM, SD was calculated using the formula SD = SEM × , where n denotes the number of animals included in that specific experimental group for the corresponding outcome. For studies reporting data only in graphical form, values were estimated using validated digital extraction methods. All quantitative results are presented in the Table 2, whereas the last column of Table 1 summarizes only the direction of effect.

Reporting Bias Assessment

Because no outcome was reported with sufficient consistency to permit quantitative pooling and all agents were represented by fewer than five heterogeneous animal studies, we did not construct funnel plots or perform statistical tests for publication bias. Instead, we qualitatively appraised the risk of reporting bias by using a broad, unrestricted search strategy across multiple databases and by examining reference lists and forward citations to identify any unpublished or negative studies where possible.

Certainty of Evidence

The certainty of the evidence for each incretin-based therapy and major outcome domain was appraised using the GRADE framework adapted for preclinical animal research. We considered risk of bias based on SYRCLE assessments, inconsistency and unexplained heterogeneity across studies, indirectness related to species, models, and dosing regimens, imprecision arising from small sample sizes and wide confidence intervals, and potential reporting bias. We also considered upgrading factors, including evidence of large and consistent effects, dose–response relationships, and coherence across functional, histologic, and mechanistic endpoints. Certainty ratings (high, moderate, low, or very low) were applied at the level of each agent–outcome body of evidence and are summarized in Table 4.

Ethics Statement

This systematic review was based entirely on data extracted from previously published animal and human studies. No new experiments on humans or animals were performed. According to the policy of the Ethics Committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences, the study was considered exempt from additional institutional review board approval and from the need for individual informed consent.

Results

Study Selection

Following PRISMA 2020, we performed comprehensive searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus on October 20, 2025, identifying 482 records (PubMed 41; Embase 212; Web of Science 50; Scopus 179). After removal of duplicates, 252 unique records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 229 were excluded. Twenty-three full-text articles were assessed; 10 were excluded for the following reasons: not exclusively doxorubicin (n = 3), not exclusively incretin-based therapies (n = 2), review/editorial (n = 2), not cardiotoxicity-focused (n = 1), gene-therapy intervention (n = 1), and abstract-only publication (n = 1). Thirteen studies [23–35] met the eligibility criteria and were included in the review. PRISMA flow diagram is presented in Fig. 1.

Study Characteristics

Across 13 preclinical studies, four investigated liraglutide, four examined exenatide or exendin-4, two evaluated semaglutide, and three assessed tirzepatide in models of DOX–induced cardiotoxicity. All studies were conducted in rodents, using C57BL/6 mice or Wistar/Sprague–Dawley rats. Most experiments were performed in male animals aged 6–10 weeks within standard strain-specific weight ranges; one study included female rats. DOX-induced cardiotoxicity was established using either an acute, single high-dose IP regimen (12–20 mg/kg, with outcomes typically assessed 24–48 h or 7–10 days thereafter) or chronic, cumulative low-dose schedules (1.25–5 mg/kg administered every 2–7 days for 2–4 weeks, with follow-up generally 1–2 weeks after the final dose). Comparators predominantly comprised DOX alone as the positive control, with or without non-DOX negative controls (Fig. 2).

The study drugs were administered subcutaneously or intraperitoneally as pretreatment, co-treatment, or post-DOX therapy, with exposure durations ranging from 7 days to 6–8 weeks. Liraglutide was given at 0.1–1.8 mg/kg/day; exenatide/exendin-4 at 5–10 µg/kg to 5 nmol/kg (often 1 h prior to DOX or twice daily for 7 days); semaglutide either as short-term pretreatment at 0.06–0.24 mg/kg/day for 7 days or as extended therapy at 12 µg/kg/day for 6 weeks; and tirzepatide at 1.35 mg/kg given either concurrently with DOX at three IP doses or once daily for 2 weeks, and at 10 nmol/kg/day subcutaneously for 14 days. The timing of drug administration relative to DOX exposure varied substantially within and across agents, with some protocols spanning pre-, peri-, and post-chemotherapy phases.

Outcomes were primarily evaluated at a single terminal time point and encompassed complementary domains of cardiotoxicity. Cardiac structure and function were assessed using echocardiography (LVEF, LVFS, chamber dimensions, and selected diastolic indices such as isovolumetric relaxation time), invasive hemodynamics (± dP/dt, SV), and, in one study, isolated-heart performance. Electrophysiological parameters were characterized by surface ECG, including QRS duration, QT/QTc intervals, and HR. Circulating and tissue markers of myocardial injury and stress included serum or plasma troponins (I/T), CK-MB, LDH, BNP or NT-proBNP, and ventricular Nppa/Nppb expression.

Mechanistic analyses frequently targeted oxidative stress (myocardial or plasma MDA, DHE-derived ROS, protein carbonyls), antioxidant defenses (SOD and catalase activities), inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-6, VCAM-1, pentraxin-3), and apoptotic signaling (TUNEL staining, cleaved caspase-3, Bax/Bcl-2). Several studies also evaluated fibrosis and structural remodeling (TGF-β1, Masson’s trichrome, cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area), as well as pro-survival and stress-response pathways, including PI3K/Akt, AMP-activated protein kinase, STAT3, Nrf2/HO-1, and autophagy-related markers (Beclin-1, ATG5). A subset of studies focused on restricted outcome domains, such as natriuretic peptide transcripts or electrocardiographic indices alone.

Follow-up durations ranged from 24–48 h after a single DOX dose to one week after multi-week regimens, with two studies extending observation up to 4–8 weeks. This heterogeneity in DOX protocols, incretin-based therapies dosing and timing, routes of administration, and assessment windows is a central feature of the evidence base. Because quantitative synthesis required at least three studies reporting the same outcome for the same intervention, meta-analysis was not feasible. Detailed information is provided in Table 1.

Result of Individual Studies

All studies compared an incretin-based therapy plus doxorubicin with doxorubicin alone, with or without non–doxorubicin controls. No eligible clinical trial specifically targeting doxorubicin cardiotoxicity with incretin therapy was identified; all results therefore derive from animal models.

Across liraglutide studies, three chronic or subacute models reported broadly favorable effects on markers of myocardial injury, oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and, in one case, ferroptosis, with concordant improvements in systolic function where measured [23–25]. In contrast, a single acute, high-dose rat model found no improvement in biochemical or structural indices and observed worsening of diastolic relaxation, highlighting the importance of injury paradigm and follow-up interval [26].

Exenatide/exendin-4 studies generally demonstrated attenuation of systolic dysfunction, electrocardiographic abnormalities, oxidative stress, and apoptotic signaling in chronic cumulative-dose models [27, 28], with more modest benefits confined to natriuretic peptide expression or conduction parameters in protocols focused on limited outcome domains [29, 30].

Semaglutide studies showed dose-dependent histologic protection and improved lipid profile in an acute rat model, with limited effects on circulating injury markers [31], whereas a longer mouse protocol spanning pre-, peri-, and post-doxorubicin exposure reported consistent improvement across biomarkers, echocardiography, invasive hemodynamics, fibrosis, apoptosis, and oxidative stress [32].

For tirzepatide, two mouse studies (one acute, one chronic) demonstrated preserved systolic function, reduced cardiomyocyte injury, and favorable modulation of oxidative, inflammatory, and apoptotic pathways, including PI3K/Akt and Nrf2-related signaling [34, 35]. A rat study that assessed only serum biomarkers at an early time point found no significant effect [33].

Overall, most studies reported directionally protective effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists or tirzepatide on doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity, particularly in chronic or subacute models with treatment extending into the recovery phase. Detailed quantitative results are presented in Table 2.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Most included studies showed important methodological limitations. While several reported randomization and comparable baseline characteristics, details on allocation concealment, random housing, and blinding of caregivers or outcome assessors were rarely provided and were therefore rated as unclear. Selective reporting was generally low or unclear; however, a high risk of bias was identified in Fang et al., Li et al., and Alhowail et al., driven by incomplete outcome data, unclear handling of exclusions, or concerns about selective reporting and other biases. Collectively, the body of evidence is characterized by predominantly unclear and occasionally high risk of bias, which limits confidence in the internal validity and contributes to downgrading of the certainty of evidence. Detailed information is presented in Table 3.

Reporting Biases

Formal assessment of publication bias was not feasible. Each incretin-based therapy was represented by fewer than five heterogeneous preclinical studies, and no outcome was reported consistently enough to allow meta-analysis; therefore, funnel plots or small-study effect tests would have been unreliable. The limited number of studies does not itself indicate publication bias; however, it prevents meaningful statistical detection of it. Despite our comprehensive multi-database search strategy, the overall body of evidence remains small, single-center, which means that the possibility of unpublished or selectively reported negative findings cannot be excluded. Several included studies did report neutral results, suggesting that not all negative data are absent from the literature, but the risk of reporting or publication bias remains uncertain.

Certainty of Evidence

Certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach adapted for preclinical randomized studies (Table 4). As expected for animal experiments, overall certainty ranged from low to very low, reflecting inherent features of the preclinical literature rather than limitations of our review. Because we included all eligible studies identified through a comprehensive multi-database search but no outcome was reported consistently enough for meta-analysis, this qualitative synthesis could not benefit from GRADE upgrading domains such as large pooled effects, clear dose–response patterns, or assessment of plausible confounding.

Liraglutide showed a generally consistent protective signal across injury, oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and histologic domains, supporting low-certainty ratings, whereas divergent findings in acute versus chronic DOX models resulted in very low certainty for functional outcomes. For exenatide, reductions in systolic dysfunction, oxidative stress, and apoptosis also achieved low-certainty ratings, while domains informed by single small studies remained very low. Semaglutide and tirzepatide were each supported by only two to three heterogeneous experiments; concordant improvements in structural endpoints supported low certainty, but most functional and biomarker outcomes were rated very low.

Because the research question was explicitly preclinical, we did not downgrade for indirectness related to animal-to-human translation; Instead, the present GRADE assessment reflects the internal validity and coherence of the animal evidence base, which is intended to inform the translational step toward future clinical trials rather than to provide practice-changing guidance for patients at this stage.

Discussion

Across 13 rodent studies, incretin–based therapies generally mitigated DOX–induced cardiotoxicity. The most robust protection occurred in chronic, cumulative-dose models, particularly when incretin treatment overlapped with DOX exposure. In contrast, acute single-dose paradigms with early sampling demonstrated limited or absent benefit. These findings suggest that GLP-1 and GIP–mediated protection emerges mainly during subacute remodeling rather than immediate necrotic injury. Although the certainty of evidence is low to very low according to GRADE, the results display consistent mechanistic signals across oxidative, inflammatory, apoptotic, and ferroptotic pathways. These preclinical patterns form the basis for considering how incretin therapies might complement current cardioprotective strategies. Importantly, no eligible human study directly evaluated incretin-based therapy for doxorubicin-specific cardiotoxicity; therefore, all interpretive and translational inferences in this review are based on preclinical animal data and should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing.

Effect Modifiers and Sources of Heterogeneity in Incretin–DOX Studies

Sex Distribution of Animal Models

Most of the evidence comes from male animals: all liraglutide and exenatide studies used males, semaglutide was tested once in females and once with unreported sex, and tirzepatide studies included two male and one sex-unspecified model. This marked imbalance precludes any meaningful assessment of sex-specific effects. Therefore, the potential influence of sex on the cardioprotective response to incretin-based therapies remains uncertain. Still, the clear protective effect seen in the one female semaglutide study suggests that sex may not drastically blunt the cardioprotective response to incretin therapies, although this inference remains hypothesis-generating and requires confirmation in dedicated female-focused studies.

Injury Paradigm: Acute Versus Chronic Doxorubicin Exposure

Across studies, cardioprotective effects of incretin-based therapies were more consistent in chronic doxorubicin (DOX) models than in acute, single-dose paradigms. Liraglutide and exenatide showed clear benefits in chronic settings but were largely ineffective in acute models. In contrast, semaglutide improved functional, structural, and biochemical outcomes in chronic models and reduced myocardial injury even in acute exposure. Tirzepatide demonstrated protective effects in both paradigms, although results varied depending on dosing and protocol.

Overall, these findings suggest that liraglutide and exenatide may be more effective in chronic DOX cardiomyopathy, whereas semaglutide and tirzepatide retain activity across both acute and chronic settings. This pattern may reflect differences in pharmacologic properties or mechanisms of action and underscores the importance of injury timing when interpreting preclinical cardiotoxicity models.

Timing of Incretin Administration: Pre-, Peri-, and Post-DOX

Timing of incretin therapy varied substantially across studies and influenced observed effects. For liraglutide, regimens extending into and after doxorubicin (DOX) exposure were associated with cardioprotection, whereas predominantly pre-treatment strategies showed no benefit. Exenatide demonstrated consistent protective effects when administered concurrently with DOX, while post-treatment alone yielded unclear results, suggesting that co-administration may be necessary.

For semaglutide, both pretreatment and extended dosing spanning pre-, peri-, and post-DOX phases were associated with benefit; however, the limited number of studies precludes firm conclusions regarding optimal timing. Tirzepatide showed heterogeneous results across different timing strategies, including co-treatment, delayed co-treatment, and regimens incorporating pretreatment, with no single approach consistently superior.

Overall, these findings indicate that optimal timing is agent-specific and not uniform across incretin therapies. Co-treatment appears to be the most consistently favorable approach, whereas isolated pretreatment (for liraglutide or tirzepatide) and post-treatment initiation alone (for exenatide) have not shown clear or consistent benefit.

Positioning Within Current Cardio-Oncology Practice

Current cardio-oncology guidelines endorse dexrazoxane for selected high-risk adults receiving anthracyclines and emphasize risk stratification with echocardiography and biomarkers, yet no metabolically targeted cardioprotective drug is established as standard of care [8]. Dexrazoxane remains the only approved mechanism-based preventive therapy, though its use is limited to specific regimens and populations [11]. Despite reduced cumulative-dose thresholds and routine surveillance, approximately 3% of anthracycline-treated patients still develop an LVEF < 40% or overt heart failure, underscoring the continued need for mechanism-directed prevention [36]. Statins have shown mixed results across trials (most notably STOP-CA) without addressing metabolic remodeling directly [37, 38]. SGLT2 inhibitors are under investigation and associated with lower CTRCD in observational studies, but randomized prevention data remain limited [39, 40]. The cardiorenal and anti-inflammatory benefits demonstrated across major GLP-1RA outcome programs (LEADER, SUSTAIN-6, SELECT, STEP-HFpEF) provide a strong biological rationale for testing these agents as cardioprotective interventions during or after anthracycline or ICI therapy [41]. In this context, GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) and the dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist tirzepatide could address an unmet therapeutic gap by integrating metabolic unloading with anti-inflammatory and redox-modulating effects relevant to CTRCD biology [42–44].

Comparative Efficacy Versus Established Cardioprotective Pharmacotherapy

Established cardioprotective strategies in anthracycline-treated patients primarily include statins, neurohormonal blockade, and, more recently, SGLT2 inhibitors. Among these, statins have the strongest randomized evidence. In the STOP-CA trial, atorvastatin reduced LVEF decline (≥ 10% to < 55%) from 22 to 9% [38]. A meta-analysis of five trials (n = 808) showed reduced cardiotoxicity risk (RR 0.52, 95% CI 0.33–0.83) and modest LVEF preservation (~ 2%), without reducing heart failure hospitalizations [45, 46]. Despite guideline support [47], their mechanistic impact on redox and ferroptosis pathways remains limited.

SGLT2 inhibitors have mainly observational evidence. Meta-analyses demonstrated reduced all-cause mortality (RR 0.52, 95% CI 0.35–0.77 and 0.52, 95% CI 0.33–0.82), with inconsistent effects on heart failure outcomes [48, 49]. Preclinical data suggest benefits on ventricular function, oxidative stress, and apoptosis, but randomized prevention data are lacking. [50, 51]. Neurohormonal therapies (β-blockers and ACEI/ARB) show modest and heterogeneous benefits. Carvedilol reduced biomarkers and diastolic dysfunction but did not improve LVEF decline in the CECCY trial [52, 53]. Other trials (PRADA, ICOS-ONE) and meta-analyses indicate small improvements in LVEF and remodeling, without clear effects on mortality or symptomatic heart failure [54–61].

In this context, incretin-based therapies demonstrated larger effect sizes in preclinical models, including LVEF improvements of ~ 7–20 percentage points and 25–55% reductions in injury biomarkers. Mechanistically, they target oxidative stress, inflammation, and ferroptosis pathways via PI3K/Akt, AMPK, STAT3, and Nrf2/HO-1 signaling, complementing traditional therapies [46, 62, 63]. However, the level of evidence remains substantially lower than that of established therapies, which are supported by multiple randomized trials [46, 54], whereas incretin data are exclusively preclinical. SGLT2 inhibitors occupy an intermediate position with emerging but incomplete evidence [48]. Therefore, incretin-based therapies should be considered hypothesis-generating and evaluated in phase II trials alongside standard cardioprotective strategies.

Mechanistic Coherence with CTRCD Biology

Mechanistic insights are drawn both from the included DOX–incretin studies and from broader GLP-1RA literature in ischemia–reperfusion, heart failure, and metabolic models, and should therefore be considered supportive rather than direct outputs of this systematic review.

DOX cardiotoxicity reflects convergent pathways of mitochondrial oxidative stress, redox imbalance, inflammation, apoptosis, and ferroptosis [64–66]. GLP-1–based therapies appear to exert cardioprotection through two complementary mechanisms.

First, they attenuate oxidative stress by lowering lipid peroxidation and reactive oxygen species while enhancing antioxidant defenses, including superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione. They also activate prosurvival signaling through PI3K/Akt, AMPK, STAT3, and Nrf2/HO-1 pathways [42–44, 67–69].

Second, they suppress inflammatory and cell-death cascades by down-regulating TNF-α, IL-6, and VCAM-1, reducing Bax and cleaved caspase-3, increasing Bcl-2, and inhibiting necroptotic (p-RIPK3/MLKL) and pyroptotic (NLRP3 inflammasome, caspase-1, IL-1β) signaling [70]. Cardiomyocyte caveolin-3/caveolae structures mediate many of these effects through KATP channel activation, eNOS/NO–PKG signaling, and prevention of mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening [70]. GLP-1 receptor activation also engages the cAMP–PKA axis and inhibits NLRP3/MyD88-dependent inflammation, converging on ferroptosis restraint [41].

Sirtuin-1 (SIRT1) acts as an upstream regulator integrating AMPK/PGC-1α-dependent mitochondrial biogenesis, NF-κB/NLRP3 suppression, and p53-modulated apoptosis, providing a mechanistic bridge between DOX injury and incretin signaling [65]. Dulaglutide was prespecified within our eligibility criteria; however, no eligible original study evaluating dulaglutide in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity was identified. Only A Letter-format report described dulaglutide-associated attenuation of DOX-induced myocardial fibrosis via cGMP–PKG activation, but because it was an editorial paper, it was not incorporated into this systematic review [71]. In line with these upstream regulatory interactions, experimental GLP-1 gene delivery in DOX cardiomyopathy has shown that GLP-1–driven phosphorylation of FoxO1 at Ser256 diminishes its nuclear activity, promotes cytoplasmic translocation, and increases cyclin-D1 expression, enabling limited cardiomyocyte cell-cycle re-entry. This GLP-1–FoxO1 signaling axis therefore provides an additional mechanistic link between incretin pathways and the prosurvival, mitochondrial, and anti-apoptotic processes described above [72].The observed up-regulation of GPX4 and maintenance of glutathione in liraglutide models further support a role for ferroptosis inhibition through Nrf2/HO-1 and GPX4 activation [64, 73, 74]. Collectively, these mechanistic data reinforce the biological plausibility of targeting incretin pathways for anthracycline cardioprotection.

Comparative Signals Across Incretin Agents

Although head-to-head comparisons are unavailable and the evidence base is small, semaglutide and tirzepatide showed favorable signals across several functional, structural, and biomarker domains in the included studies. Although no eligible DOX-specific dulaglutide study was included, external evidence suggests antifibrotic and functional benefits, supporting the possibility of a class effect among incretin-based agents [71]. Notably, tirzepatide’s dual GIP/GLP-1 activity and longer half-life may confer broader metabolic and anti-inflammatory effects compared with single-agonist GLP-1RAs. Translationally, both semaglutide and tirzepatide improved symptoms, quality of life, and composite outcomes in patients with obesity-related HFpEF, supporting cardiorenal and anti-inflammatory mechanisms relevant to the anthracycline-exposed myocardium [19, 75, 76]. Together, these findings strengthen the rationale for clinical evaluation of incretin therapies in cardio-oncology populations where metabolic risk is prevalent [8].

Explaining Heterogeneity and Negative Findings

The single negative liraglutide study used a high-dose acute DOX challenge with very early assessment and pretreatment exposure, a scenario in which GLP-1 signaling may not overcome immediate necrotic injury or anesthesia-related diastolic effects. Autophagy responses to DOX are highly context-dependent, being either induced through JNK–p70S6K and AMPK–mTOR or inhibited via AMPK–ULK1 and E2F1–mTORC1 pathways, with SIRT1 intersecting both [65]. This bidirectionality likely contributes to short- versus long-term differences across incretin studies. These findings imply that peri-chemotherapy initiation with continuation into early survivorship may best capture the therapeutic window for modifying these pathways.

Clinical Implications and Future Directions

Patients receiving anthracyclines who also have obesity, type 2 diabetes, or metabolic syndrome may represent reasonable candidates for early translational studies, given their cardiometabolic vulnerability [8]. Pragmatic phase II trials could randomize such patients to GLP-1RA or tirzepatide versus standard care in addition to established preventive measures (dexrazoxane where indicated, statins when appropriate), with primary endpoints including high-sensitivity troponin, NT-proBNP, global longitudinal strain, and LVEF, and secondary endpoints assessed by CMR T1/T2 mapping and extracellular volume [8, 38, 77].

Beyond anthracycline cardiotoxicity, metabolic and inflammatory injury also contribute to cardiovascular complications from immune checkpoint inhibitors, providing an additional rationale for testing GLP-1RAs in this setting. Their anti-atherosclerotic, anti-inflammatory, and weight-reducing effects may mitigate ICI-associated vascular risk [41]. Two retrospective TriNetX analyses and a recent meta-analysis of observational cohorts observed lower major adverse cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality among cancer patients, including ICI recipients, treated with GLP-1RAs; however, confounding precludes causal inference and these data do not address doxorubicin-specific cardiotoxicity [21, 22, 78]. These retrospective cohorts did not isolate DOX-specific cardiotoxicity and therefore were not included in our systematic review dataset; they are cited solely as external, hypothesis-generating clinical context.

Consistent with emerging cardio-oncology recommendations, cardiovascular safety assessment should be integrated earlier in oncology drug development, with focused preclinical efforts for under-studied classes such as ICIs and CDK4/6 inhibitors to parallel their clinical uptake [36]. Safety monitoring in future trials should include gastrointestinal tolerance, weight and volume status, glycemic control, and tumor response metrics, given theoretical concerns regarding GLP-1R expression in certain malignancies[42–44]. Because incretin therapies may remodel the tumor microenvironment, altering cytokine networks, angiogenesis, immune infiltration, and metabolic programming, oncologic endpoints such as tumor progression and survival should be prespecified [79]. Existing oncologic data for GLP-1RAs and tirzepatide remain heterogeneous, with both anti-tumor and pro-tumor signals reported; thus, cancer-type-specific risk stratification and adjudicated malignancy endpoints are essential [79]. Preclinical observations that GLP-1RA–mediated VEGF up-regulation or anti-apoptotic signaling might blunt antitumor efficacy further justify prospective oncologic monitoring [80]. Importantly, large meta-analyses and pharmacoepidemiologic studies have not shown an overall increase in cancer incidence with GLP-1RAs, though vigilance regarding thyroid and tumor-specific effects remains prudent [79, 81, 82].

Given encouraging data for statins and emerging signals for SGLT2 inhibitors, factorial or adaptive trial designs combining GLP-1RAs with these agents could expedite identification of synergistic, mechanism-based prevention strategies [38–40]. While GLP-1–based therapies should not yet be prescribed solely for cardioprotection, their dual metabolic and cardiovascular profile, along with widespread clinical availability, make them particularly suitable for translational investigation [8, 19, 42–44, 75, 76].

Study Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, all evidence informing the incretin–DOX relationship comes from animal studies, which involve variable outcome reporting, and considerable heterogeneity in species, DOX regimens, and incretin dosing schedules. Second, most experiments assessed outcomes at a single time point, limiting insight into the full trajectory of injury and recovery. Third, too few studies reported the same outcomes in a comparable way to allow meaningful meta-analysis, so only qualitative synthesis was possible. Fourth, because our review focused specifically on doxorubicin, the findings may not extend to cardiotoxicity caused by immune checkpoint inhibitors or targeted therapies.

Conclusion

In this preclinical systematic review, incretin-based therapies showed promising cardioprotective signals in experimental models of doxorubicin-induced injury, with more consistent effects in chronic than in acute models. Regimens overlapping with doxorubicin exposure appeared to provide the most reproducible benefit, although the evidence base was small and heterogeneous. Because all included studies were preclinical and the certainty of evidence was low to very low, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis-generating. Further well-designed translational and clinical studies are needed to clarify the optimal agent, dose, timing, and target population before any clinical application can be considered.
