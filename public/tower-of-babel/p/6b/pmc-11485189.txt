Hydrogel-based fluorescence assay kit for simultaneous determination of ceftazidime and avibactam
Authors: Xiaoli Wang, Linyu Du, Boshun Zhang, Yingchun Li, Zheying Tao, Li Zhang, Jieming Qu, Johnjoe McFadden, Hongping Qu, Jiao Yang, Jialin Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Monitoring the concentration of antibiotics rapidly and cost-effectively is crucial for accurate clinical medication and timely identification of drug-induced illnesses. Here, we constructed a novel fluorescent assay kit to monitor Zavicefta, an effective antibiotic composed of avibactam (AVI) and ceftazidime (CFZ) to treat carbapenem-resistant gram-negative bacteria infections. AVI can emit fluorescence, but CFZ cannot. To enable simultaneous measurement of both in one kit, we designed molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) modified quantum dots (QDs) for CFZ determination. MIPs have received significant attention as an artificial antibody due to their exceptional specificity for various targets, particularly drugs with small molecular weight. Under the excitation wavelength of 350 nm, the detection process involves a decrease in QDs’ fluorescence signal at 600 nm owing to the “gate effect” between MIP and CFZ and the internal filtration effect between CFZ and QDs. Simultaneously, a fluorescence emission characteristic peak at 420 nm for AVI emerges. In addition, to simplify the operation procedure and improve determination throughput, the detection agents were incorporated into a hydrogel and placed in a 96-well plate, enabling concurrent quantification of AVI and CFZ within the respective range of 80–1000 μM and 1–1000 μM. The developed assay kit successfully determined AVI and CFZ in human serums and therapeutic drug monitoring in a live rabbit model. Recoveries of AVI and CFZ were 92.7–114%, with relative standard deviations below 6.0%. Moreover, a smartphone was employed to read the fluorescence signals, which was beneficial for cost reduction and out-of-lab analysis. This study will deliver a pragmatic resolution to developing high-throughput assay kits for drug determination.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00604-024-06742-1.

Introduction

The widespread use and abuse of antibiotics have led to the problem of bacterial resistance, which is becoming increasingly severe and poses a continuing threat to public health [1]. Carbapenem-resistant organisms (CROs) are a significant problem for drug resistance and often exhibit high transmissibility [2]. Intensive care unit (ICU) infections are associated with crude mortalities as high as 42.2% [3]. As a novel antibiotic and consists of ceftazidime (CFZ) and avibactam (AVI) as active agents, Zavicefta has been shown to have broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against CROs and is broadly used in ICU [4]. However, patients with renal impairment have significantly lower average clearance rates for Zavicefta compared to controls and accumulate the antibiotics in the body to an extent that is harmful to the kidneys and even endangers life. To ensure positive outcomes, especially in intensive care units, performing therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) for AVI and CFZ among patients is imperative [5, 6].

The current techniques for simultaneous determination of AVI and CFZ were chromatography, including high performance liquid chromatography coupled with diode array detection [7], and liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry [8–10]. Despite high sensitivity and low detection limit, trained professionals, large equipment, and complex sample preparation and testing processes restrict their widespread applications, particularly in regions with limited resources. To overcome these drawbacks, we developed an electrochemical sensor for rapid and cost-efficient determination of CFZ and AVI [11]. However, the electrochemical technique still cannot meet the requirements of high throughput analysis. Fluorescence assay has become increasingly popular due to its outstanding precision, sensitivity, and affordability, broadly employed in biomedicine, food monitoring, and environmental tracking [12–14]. Generally, fluorescence analysis for non-fluorescent substances relies on integrating fluorescent material and recognition receptors. Compared with traditional organic fluorophores, quantum dots (QDs) are very suitable for fluorescence analysis due to their high fluorescence intensity, tunable emission wavelength, and less interference [15, 16].

Recognition receptors such as antibodies, aptamers, or enzymes are commonly applied in fluorescence analysis to achieve specific recognition of analytes [17–19]. However, inherent instability and high costs limit their applications. Molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) is an artificial antibody with the targeted recognition ability that mimics an antigen and antibody interaction. It has various applications in drug delivery, chromatographic separation, chemical sensing, environmental detection, etc. [20–22]. Compared with natural receptors, the preparation of MIP has advantages in terms of cost and time [23]. Our previous work showed that MIP prepared by electropolymerization possessed high specificity for CFZ and AVI, but the MIP is hard to large-scale preparation via electropolymerization [11].

Moreover, MIPs tend to coagulate and are difficult to disperse uniformly in aqueous solution, affecting the monitoring of fluorescence signals. Hydrogel, a kind of polymer with a three-dimensional network structure, is an ideal matrix for MIP immobilization [24]. In addition, the hydrogel substrate has abundant pores, which can regulate the entry and exit of substances [25].

Here, a hydrogel-based fluorescence assay kit based on the detection of AVI fluorescence and QDs was developed to detect AVI and CFZ simultaneously. Cadmium telluride (CdTe) QDs and MIP were adopted separately as fluorescence sources and recognition elements for detecting CFZ and other antibiotics [26, 27]. MIP-coated CdTe-QDs were loaded into the hydrogel to prevent aggregation and ensure the stability of CdTe-QDs’ fluorescence signal. When AVI and CFZ were introduced, the functionalized hydrogel showed an emission peak at 420 nm for AVI itself and a reduced emission peak at 600 nm for CdTe-QDs caused by the rebinding between MIP and CFZ. The hydrogel was further transferred to a microplate to make a fluorescence assay kit, enabling high-throughput determination of AVI and CFZ. The AVI and CFZ levels in actual human samples were measured using the assay kit, and TDM was also conducted in a live rabbit model. Furthermore, a smartphone replaced a microplate reader as the signal output terminal to effectively simplify the operation and reduce the cost, which was conducive to point-of-care testing.

Experimental

Reagents

Avibactam (AVI, 99%), ceftazidime (CFZ, 99%), L-ascorbic acid, dextrose, uric acid, cefixime (99%), cefotaxime (99%), cefepime (99%), and danofloxacin mesylate (DM), cyclohexane, acetone, Triton X-100, and methanol were purchased from Adamas Reagent Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Thioglycolic acid (TGA) capped CdTe quantum dots (CdTe-QDs) with a maximum emission wavelength of 595 nm were purchased from Wuhan Jiayuan Quantum Dots Co., Ltd. (Hongshan, China). Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 96%), aqueous ammonia (NH3·H2O, 25%-28%), 3-(triethoxysilyl) propane-1-amine (APTES), and Zavicefta were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). Ethanol, acetonitrile, sodium alginate, and calcium chloride hydrate (CaCl2) were bought from Alfa Aesar (Shanghai, China). Other chemicals, such as KH2PO4, HCl, NaOH, etc. were purchased from Guangfu Tech. Co. Ltd. (Tianjin, China). All reagents were of analytical grade and utilized with no purification. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.1 M, pH 7.2) was obtained from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and the pH was adjusted by using 6 M HCl or 2 M NaOH aqueous solution. Unless otherwise specified, deionized water was used in the entire experiment. The type of used 96-well plate was a black polystyrene microplate with a clear bottom purchased from Titan Co., Ltd. (China).

Instruments

Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectra and Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were obtained using a Shimadzu UV-2550 spectrometer (Japan) and a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50 spectrometer (USA), respectively. Structural morphologies of the prepared materials were characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi S-4800, Japan) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEM-1200EX, Japan). The size distribution of CdTe-QDs was determined using the dynamic light scattering (DLS) technique. The phase information was detected by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, X’Pert PRO, Netherlands). Fluorescence intensities were measured with a HORIBA FluoroMax-4 fluorescence spectrometer (Japan) and a Synergy TM HTX Multi-Mode microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA). High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was used as the “gold standard” method for analyzing actual samples, which was carried out on a 1260 Infinity II HPLC (Agilent, America) equipped with a Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (150 mm × 3 mm I.D., 2.7 μm, Agilent) and a diode array detector. The mobile phase was methanol-50 mM KH2PO4 aqueous solution (7:93, v/v) with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The column temperature was 30 °C, and the injection volume was 10 μL. The detection wavelength was 210 nm. The chromatogram of CFZ and AVI solution analysis and the calibration plots are shown in Fig S1.

Synthesis of CdTe-QDs@MIP

In this work, CdTe-QDs@MIP was synthesized by a reverse microemulsion method according to the previously reported procedure [28]. Firstly, 1.8 mL Triton X-100 was added to 7.5 mL cyclohexane. After stirring for 15 min, 200 μL CdTe-QDs (5 mg/mL), 50 μL TEOS, and 100 μL NH3·H2O (25%-28%, v/v) were successively injected into the above mixture and reacted for 2 h to form CdTe-QDs @SiO2. Subsequently, CFZ was mixed with APTES, and the solution was stirred at 600 rpm overnight at room temperature. Acetone was used to break the microemulsion, and precipitates were collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min. The precipitates were then eluted with ethanol/acetonitrile (8/2, v/v) to remove the embedded CFZ completely. The elution process was monitored by recording the fluorescence spectrum of the precipitates until the fluorescence intensity did not change. Finally, the product of CdTe-QDs@MIP was ground into powder after drying under a vacuum at room temperature. For comparison, non-imprinted polymer (NIP) modified QDs (CdTe-QDs@NIP) were synthesized using the same process outlined above, except for the absence of CFZ during polymerization.

Determination of AVI and CFZ

Measurements in solution

Firstly, 6.0 mg CdTe-QDs@MIP was dispersed into 10.0 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.1 M, pH 7.0) by ultrasonication. For AVI determination, 200 μL MIP dispersion (0.6 mg/mL) was added into 1 mL AVI standard solution with varied concentrations (1–1000 μM) in the presence of 500 μM CFZ. For CFZ, the tests were performed by a similar method with diverse CFZ concentrations (1–1000 μM) in the presence of 500 μM AVI. After incubation for 10 min, the fluorescence spectra within a range of 370–680 nm were measured by a fluorescence spectrometer under the excitation wavelength of 350 nm. (F-F0)/F was utilized as the fluorescence response, where F0 and F are the fluorescence intensity of the solution without and with an analyst, respectively. The maximum emission wavelengths of AVI and CFZ are 420 nm and 600 nm, respectively.

Measurements in hydrogel

CdTe-QDs@MIP was loaded in alginate hydrogel (ALH) and placed in a 96-well plate to achieve high-throughput determination and simplify the operation process. Dissolve 0.2g sodium alginate powder in 10ml double distilled water for 3 h. Add 6 mg CdTe-QDs@MIP powders and stir until uniform suspension forms. Then, 100 μL precursor was added into each plate well, followed by 100 μL 0.1 M CaCl2 solution. After 5 min of the reaction, CdTe-QDs@MIP@ALH was fabricated. During detection, 50 μL analyst standard solution was added to the above well, and a microplate reader at the excitation wavelength of 350 nm was adopted to determine the fluorescence intensities at 420 nm and 600 nm. The response signals were also recorded by a smartphone (iPhone 13), which was utilized to take photos of the hydrogels in a dark box under ultraviolet illumination. The RGB values of each photo were analyzed using Image J software.

Real sample analysis

A standard addition method was first applied to validate the assay in healthy human plasma. The plasma sample was mixed with methanol in a 1/3 (v/v) ratio to eliminate proteins, then centrifuged at 10000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. After that, the supernatant was diluted with PBS and analyzed using the fluorescence assay kit or HPLC. Next, the concentrations of CFZ and AVI in serums from a patient who had multiple infections and was given Zavicefta intravenously were measured. Serum samples were taken at 0, 2, 4, and 6 h post-infusion and stored at -80 °C. Serum samples were processed similarly to plasma samples and followed the above detection steps.

This study was conducted by the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University (RJ2019NO1-3). All participants provided written informed consent before the start of the experiment. Animal studies were conducted in compliance with the research protocols approved by the medical ethics committee at Harbin Institute of Technology (IACUC-2021022). Healthy rabbits received CFZ-AVI (40:10, mg/mg) intravenously at a 50 mg/kg dose dissolved in saline at Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (Guangzhou, China). The CFZ to AVI ratio was based on a commercial combination drug-Zavicefta. Periotic vein blood samples (1 mL each) were drawn at 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, and 3 h after injection and spiked with 0.1 mL of heparin sodium anticoagulant (10 mg/mL). They were treated the same way as human plasma samples.

Results and discussion

This study delved into the fluorescence characteristics of both substances to achieve the simultaneous determination of AVI and CFZ. As shown in Fig. S2A, AVI demonstrates a broad emission band with a peak centered at 420 nm, indicating that its fluorescence can directly measure AVI. For CFZ, no emission band is observed, so introducing a fluorescent probe was necessary to determine its nature. We used CdTe-QDs with an emission peak at 600 nm serving as a fluorescent material, which can be well separated from the emission peak of AVI, and a MIP was adopted as a receptor for CFZ measurement. The preparation and working process of CdTe-QDs@MIP@ALH assay kit was illustrated in Fig. 1. First, MIP with CFZ as template molecules was synthesized on CdTe-QDs surface by reverse microemulsion polymerization. After removing the embedded CFZ, imprinting cavities possessing recognition ability for CFZ were produced. To prevent clustering in water, CdTe-QDs@MIP were mixed with sodium alginate solution, then placed in a 96-well plate and exposed to calcium ions to form a hydrogel. Therefore, a fluorescence assay kit with high throughput for AVI and CFZ analysis was obtained. In the presence of AVI and CFZ, a fluorescence emission peak at 420 nm originating from AVI appeared. In comparison, the fluorescence emission peak at 600 nm of CdTe-QDs decreased due to the “gate-effect” of MIP stemming from the binding between CFZ and MIP. Meanwhile, the absorption peaks of CFZ and CdTe QDs overlap in the range of 250 nm ~ 350 nm (Fig. S2B), which could reduce the energy of excitation light to produce an internal filtration effect, further quenching CdTe-QDs’ fluorescence. The two emission peaks do not overlap, suggesting the high feasibility of simultaneous determination of AVI and CFZ using the constructed assay kit.Fig. 1Diagram of the preparation of hydrogel-based fluorescence assay kit and the detection procedure

Morphological characterization

The TEM image shows that the size of CdTe-QDs is around 5 ± 2 nm (Fig. 2A). After decorating with MIP by reverse microemulsion polymerization, CdTe-QDs@MIP exhibits a spherical structure with diameters ranging from 50 to 65 nm, verifying the formation of polymer layer (Fig. 2B-D). The successful preparation of CFZ MIP was confirmed by FT-IR spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 2E, CdTe-QDs display a main peak at 3410 cm−1, which is assigned to the − OH stretching vibration of TGA on CdTe-QDs surface. Two peaks at 1570–1 and 1380–1 belong to asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of -COOH groups (curve a) [29]. For CFZ, the 1664 cm−1, 1620 cm−1, and 1775 cm−1 peaks originate from the stretching vibration of − N − H, pyridine ring, and − C = O, respectively (curve b) [30]. After polymerization (curve c), the characteristic peaks of CFZ are observed, indicating that CFZ molecules were successfully embedded in the polymer film. The peaks at 760 cm−1 and 1050 cm−1 stem from Si − O, characteristic functional groups of the polymer [31]. Following elution (curve d), only polymer-specific peaks are observed, and CFZ peaks are absent, indicating the successful elimination of CFZ molecules from MIP film. As a control, the IR spectrum of CdTe-QDs@NIP (curve e) is consistent with that of CdTe-QDs@MIP after elution (curve d) and only possesses peaks for polymer. Moreover, after MIPs coating, several diffraction peaks for CdTe-QDs disappeared and only the peak located at 24° for CdTe-QDs was found, which might be due to the reason that the sol–gel polymerization of MIP abated these diffraction peaks (Fig. S3). Based on the recorded fluorescence data from each step of the modification process (Fig. 2F), CdTe-QDs emit a strong fluorescence at 595 nm when exposed to a 350 nm excitation wavelength (curve a) [28]. The polymer formation significantly decreased fluorescence intensity because the polymer film hindered fluorescence transmission (curve b). The subsequent removal of the embedded CFZ from the polymer caused a partial recovery of fluorescence, which indicated the generation of imprinting cavities allowing fluorescence transmission (curve c). Such imprinting cavities in MIP film can specifically recognize and capture CFZ. Thus, when CdTe-QDs@MIP was exposed to CFZ, these cavities were re-occupied by CFZ molecules, igniting a decrease in the fluorescence intensity of CdTe-QDs (curve d). Besides, the internal filtration effect between CFZ and CdTe-QDs also decreased CdTe QDs’ fluorescence. The above results imply that CFZ can be recognized explicitly by CdTe-QDs@MIP.

To achieve high sensing performance of CdTe-QDs@MIP, the elution time, CdTe-QDs@MIP dosage, and incubation time were optimized (Fig. S4A-B, D). Additionally, the pH value of the buffer during CFZ and AVI measurement was also optimized (Fig. S4C).

Assessment of CdTe-QDs@MIP in solution

AVI and CFZ were detected in solution under ideal conditions using the CdTe-QDs@MIP fluorescence system. As shown in Fig. 3A, the fluorescence intensity at 420 nm gradually boosts with AVI concentration, while the intensity for CdTe-QDs remains constant. On the contrary, as CFZ concentration increases, the fluorescence intensity at 600 nm for CdTe-QDs shows a pronounced decrement due to the “gate-effect” of the MIP layer and internal filtration effect [33]. In contrast, the intensity for AVI remains unchanged (Fig. 3C). The emission peaks corresponding to the levels of CFZ and AVI are independent, enabling the concurrent measurement of both in a single system. The calibration curves demonstrate that the sensing system possesses a linear determination range of 80 ~ 1000 μM for AVI with a limit of detection (LOD) of 25.4 μM (S/N = 3) (Fig. 3B). Fluorescence response for CFZ is directly proportional to its concentration within a concentration range of 1–1000 μM (Fig. 3D). Assessment of the hydrogel-based assay kit

To improve the stability and achieve high throughput determination, ALH hydrogel was adopted as a matrix for CdTe-QDs@MIP loading, and the detecting system was transferred to 96-well plates. As shown in Fig. S5, the hydrogel possessed a three-dimensional porous structure, and the encapsulated CdTe-QDs@MIP were distributed on pore walls. Such porous structure of hydrogel facilitates the ingression of substances according to their molecular sizes, where macromolecules or large-sized impurities can be excluded from the hydrogel. In addition, the uniform distribution of CdTe-QDs@MIP ensures the stability of the fluorescence signal.

To achieve high performance, the amount of sodium alginate, the volume of hydrogel precursor, the dosage of CdTe-QDs@MIP, and the operating temperature were optimized (Fig. S6). As shown in Fig. 4A, the hydrogel's fluorescence intensity at 420 nm increases linearly as AVI concentration boosts from 80 μM to 1000 μM with a regression coefficient (R2) of 0.989 under the optimal conditions. The LOD was calculated to be 23.4 μM (S/N = 3). The fluorescence intensity at 600 nm exhibits a direct proportionality to CFZ concentration between 1 μM and 1000 μM (R2 = 0.996) with a LOD of 0.6 μM ( Fig. 4B). To study the specificity of the prepared MIP, the response signals at an emission wavelength of 600 nm for several structural analogs, including cefotaxime, cefixime, cefotaxime, cefepime, and danofloxacin mesylate, were measured. It is found from Fig. 5A, that the response signal for the analogs on the assay is negligible compared to CFZ's response signal. The T-test confirms a significant difference (p < 0.01) in responses between them and CFZ. On the contrary, the NIP sensor does not display any notable distinctions between CFZ and its analogs. The imprinting factor (IF) was calculated by comparing the response signal of CdTe-QDs@MIP to NIP [36]. Generally, a larger IF value indicates higher specificity. It is evident that the IF value of CFZ, at 4.07, significantly exceeds those of its structural analogs. The presence of coexisting substances in biological samples may impede the detection of target molecules. Consequently, it is imperative to evaluate the sensor’s anti-interference capability. As depicted in Fig. 5B, the impacts of interfering substances are negligible. Results of a T-test showed no significant difference with 95% confidence. The assay kit has strong anti-interference ability and potential for use in physiological environments.

To assess reproducibility, five independent hydrogel assay kits were prepared using an identical procedure and utilized to measure CFZ and AVI concentrations, which yielded relative standard deviations (RSDs) of 3.53% and 4.74%, respectively. Repeatability was evaluated by measuring CFZ and AVI concentrations using a single kit for 20 consecutive iterations, resulting in an RSD of less than 3.30% (Fig. S8A). The assay kit was refrigerated at 4 °C for long-term stability assessment and used to detect CFZ and AVI concentrations every 5 days. As illustrated in Fig. S8B, after 20 days of storage, no discernible decline in response signals was observed. The above results convey that the proposed hydrogel assay kit exhibits satisfactory sensing performance for practical application.

Real samples analysis

A reliability test was conducted on the suggested sensor using human plasma samples. The standard addition method was employed in the test. As listed in Table S1, spike recoveries were found in 92–115%, and the results were consistent with those obtained from HPLC. Furthermore, the concentrations of CFZ and AVI in serum samples obtained from a patient treated with Zavicefta were determined. As displayed in Fig. 6A, the blood concentration of CFZ and AVI peaks within four hours after administration of Zavicefta. HPLC was employed as a ‘gold standard’ method to validate the results, where the data determined by both were in close agreement. Improvement in point-of-care testing

A smartphone was further applied as a signal readout terminal instead of a microplate reader or a fluorescence spectrometer to address the limitations of current fluorescence determination regarding instruments and skilled personnel. The hydrogel's RGB value reflects the assay kit's response fluorescence signals through Image J software. As shown in Fig. 7A, with the addition of AVI, the color of the hydrogel changes from orange to blue, and blue deepens as the AVI concentration increases. The orange stems from CdTe-QDs@MIP, and the blue originates from AVI. The ratio of B to R (B/R) shows good linearity with AVI concentration in a range of 100 ~ 1000 μM. From Fig. 7B, due to the presence of 200 μM AVI, the hydrogel demonstrates a composite color of blue and orange in the absence of CFZ. As the CFZ concentration boosts, the rebinding between CFZ and MIP results in a lighter orange color, and thus, the hydrogel demonstrates a gradual darker blue. The B/R value is proportional to CFZ concentration ranging from 100 μM to 1000 μM. Besides, this assay exhibits excellent repeatability and reliability (Fig. S10 and Table S2). Conclusion

A high-throughput fluorescence assay kit using CdTe-QDs@MIP as probe and hydrogel as matrix was proposed to quantify AVI and CFZ with outstanding sensing performance simultaneously. Profited by the characteristic fluorescence of AVI itself and the specific recognition function of MIP, the assay kit demonstrated high anti-interference capability and reliability for AVI and CFZ determination in actual samples. The hydrogel combination in a 96-well plate endowed the kit with high throughput and excellent fluorescence stability. Besides, a smartphone successfully replaced a microplate reader or a fluorescence spectrometer as a response signal reading terminal, which enabled the kit to be applied in point-of-care testing and out-of-lab monitoring. The proposed kit is superior to chromatography in terms of cost and simplicity, and it also possesses advantages in large-scale production and high-throughput analysis compared with our previous electrochemical sensor. The developed fluorescence assay kit is expected to work as an efficient, reliable, and cost-effective TDM tool. It can also substitute for detecting other antibiotics by altering the template molecules during MIP synthesis. Nevertheless, how to keep the performance of CdTe-QDs@MIP prepared in different batches consistent and make CdTe-QDs@MIP homogeneously dispersed in the hydrogel are issues we need to address in further work.
