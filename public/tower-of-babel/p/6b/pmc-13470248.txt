ZIF-8/polydopamine nanocomposite functionalized with hyaluronic acid and folic acid for pH-responsive resveratrol delivery and apoptosis induction in colorectal cancer
Authors: Saba Seyedi, Masoud Homayouni Tabrizi, Ali Neamati, Mohsen Masoumian Hosseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, a multifunctional nanocomposite based on zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 was engineered for the enhanced delivery of resveratrol to colorectal carcinoma cells. The system comprised resveratrol-encapsulated ZIF-8 nanoparticles sequentially modified with polydopamine, hyaluronic acid, and folic acid (ZIF-8-Res-PDA-HA-FA). Physicochemical characterization revealed a Z-average hydrodynamic diameter of 194 nm, a polydispersity index of 0.197, and a zeta potential of − 40.55 mV, indicative of a narrow size distribution and robust colloidal stability. Drug encapsulation efficiency achieved 81.9%, with in vitro release kinetics demonstrating pH-responsive behavior characterized by 58.7% cumulative release reached 59.9% at pH 5.5 and 33.2% at pH 7.4 after 72 h, and further increased to 81.4% at pH 5.5 by 120 h, confirming acid-triggered drug liberation. Molecular docking simulations of resveratrol against the Bcl-2 BH3 groove yielded a modest binding affinity (S-score = − 6.23 kcal/mol) with a single validated hydrogen bond to Ala4 (2.83 Å); however, this computational prediction does not constitute evidence of cellular target engagement, and functional assays were not performed. In HCT116 cells, the nanocomposite exhibited concentration-dependent cytotoxicity with a calculated IC₅₀ of 54.4 µg/mL following 24-h exposure, while human dermal fibroblasts retained > 70% viability at concentrations up to 500 µg/mL, suggesting preferential antiproliferative activity against malignant cells. Flow cytometric analysis confirmed apoptotic induction, with treatment reducing cellular viability from 97.4% to 58.7% and increasing late-stage apoptosis to 28.4% at 74 µg/mL. Quantitative PCR demonstrated transcriptional upregulation of p53 (9.96-fold) and p21 (2.3-fold) at 48 h, whereas caspase-8 expression remained at baseline levels, implicating the intrinsic apoptotic pathway as the predominant mechanism. Wound-healing assays at sub-cytotoxic concentrations revealed significant inhibition of cellular motility (25–30% closure versus 85–90% in controls; p < 0.001). Receptor-mediated cellular uptake was not experimentally verified in this study; therefore, the observed cellular effects cannot be attributed to active targeting. While these findings demonstrate in vitro therapeutic potential, several critical limitations must be addressed: receptor-mediated cellular recognition was not experimentally verified, protein-level confirmation of gene expression changes is absent, and in vivo pharmacokinetics remain to be established.

Introduction

Colorectal carcinoma (CRC) represents a significant oncological challenge, accounting for the second-highest rate of cancer-related mortality globally1. Malignant transformation follows a multistep progression from aberrant crypt foci to invasive carcinoma, characterized by the accumulation of genetic alterations in APC, TP53, KRAS, and BRAF1. Despite advances in surgical, chemotherapeutic, and targeted interventions, drug resistance and disease recurrence persist as primary obstacles limiting long-term survival in advanced-stage patients2. These clinical challenges necessitate the development of precision delivery platforms that can enhance tumor-specific drug accumulation while minimizing off-target toxicity.

Nanoengineered drug delivery systems have demonstrated potential to overcome pharmacological limitations of conventional therapeutics3,4. While liposomal and polymeric carriers improve drug stability and exploit enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects, they often exhibit suboptimal stability or limited loading capacity4. Hybrid organic–inorganic systems, particularly metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), have emerged as alternative platforms that integrate structural stability with modifiable functionality5. Among MOFs, zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8), composed of zinc ions and 2-methylimidazole, offers advantageous porosity, high surface area, and pH-dependent degradation profiles that facilitate stimulus-responsive drug liberation within acidic tumor microenvironments6. However, clinical translation of ZIF-8 systems remains constrained by rapid reticuloendothelial clearance, potential zinc ion toxicity, and heterogeneous drug release kinetics under physiological conditions7. Surface modification strategies are therefore essential to enhance biocompatibility, prolong circulation, and enable active targeting.

Conjugation of targeting ligands to nanocarrier surfaces improves tumor specificity through receptor-mediated endocytosis. Hyaluronic acid (HA) binds CD44 receptors, which are frequently overexpressed in CRC, though receptor density exhibits considerable inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity, which may limit its universal applicability8,9. Folic acid (FA) targets folate receptors, another tumor-associated antigen, though receptor expression often diminishes with disease progression10. Previous studies employing HA-functionalized ZIF-811, resveratrol-loaded MOFs in HCT116 cells12, and dual-ligand nanoparticles13 have demonstrated enhanced cellular uptake and cytotoxicity compared to non-targeted counterparts, establishing precedent for this combinatorial approach while underscoring the need for direct comparative validation of dual versus single ligand systems.

Resveratrol (3,4′,5-trihydroxy-trans-stilbene), a polyphenolic phytoalexin, exhibits anticancer properties via apoptosis induction, epithelial–mesenchymal transition suppression, and modulation of oncogenic signaling cascades14. Despite these promising mechanisms, clinical advancement has been impeded by poor aqueous solubility (< 0.05 mg/mL), extensive first-pass metabolism resulting in oral bioavailability below 1%, and photosensitivity14. Nanocarrier encapsulation can mitigate these limitations, though quantification of improvement in cellular bioavailability is required to justify formulation development.

This investigation sought to engineer a multifunctional ZIF-8-based platform integrating pH-responsive drug release with surface functionalization designed to exploit CD44 and folate receptors hypothesized to be overexpressed on HCT116 cells. We hypothesized that sequential HA and FA functionalization of polydopamine-coated ZIF-8 would enhance HCT116 cellular uptake compared to non-functionalized particles through engagement of CD44 and folate receptor, though this mechanism remains to be confirmed experimentally. The specific objectives were: (i) synthesize and characterize ZIF-8-Res-PDA-HA-FA nanoparticles; (ii) evaluate pH-dependent resveratrol release kinetics; (iii) assess cytotoxicity and apoptotic mechanisms in HCT116 cells; and (iv) investigate anti-migratory activity. Significant limitations of the current work include the lack of receptor expression validation, competitive inhibition studies to confirm ligand-dependent uptake, and in vivo pharmacokinetic evaluation—critical next steps for translational assessment.

Materials and methods

Materials

Zinc acetate dihydrate (≥ 98%), 2-methylimidazole (2-MIM, ≥ 99%), dopamine hydrochloride (≥ 98%), hyaluronic acid (HA, MW 15–20 kDa), folic acid (FA, ≥ 97%), resveratrol (RES, ≥ 99%), 2-(N-morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid (MES) buffer, N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS, ≥ 98%), 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC, ≥ 98%), and MTT assay kit were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Tris (hydroxymethyl) aminomethane (Tris base), methanol (HPLC grade), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ≥ 99.9%), and sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃, ≥ 99.5%) were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). All reagents were analytical grade and used without further purification unless specified.

The human colorectal carcinoma cell line (HCT116) (ATCC CCL-247) and human dermal fibroblast (HDF) cell line (Pasteur Institute, Tehran) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. For in vitro assays, cells were harvested at 80–90% confluence. The SYBR Green RT-PCR Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog #4,334,973) was used for reverse transcription and qPCR. Primer sequences were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, Coralville, IA) and validated for amplification efficiency (90–110%) and specificity via melt curve analysis prior to use. The MTT assay kit was procured from Sigma-Aldrich; the Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit and the RNX-PLUS total RNA extraction kit were obtained from commercial suppliers.

In silico

Computational docking protocol

Molecular docking was performed using MOE 2024 (Molecular Operating Environment, Chemical Computing Group). The Bcl-2 crystal structure (PDB ID: 1G5M) was prepared by removing water molecules and co-crystallized ligands, adding hydrogen atoms at pH 7.4 (“Protonate 3D”), reconstructing missing side chains, and assigning Amber10:EHT force field partial charges. The structure was energy-minimized with a 10 kcal/mol/Å2 harmonic restraint on heavy atoms15.

Resveratrol (PubChem CID: 445,154) was imported, standardized via "Wash," and energetically minimized. The binding site was defined as a 20 Å sphere centered on the BH3 groove (residues Asp108, Arg146, Tyr199). Docking used the Triangle Matcher placement method with London dG scoring, forcefield refinement, and GBVI/WSA dG rescoring. The top 30 poses were retained. Validation was performed by redocking the native ligand (ABT-737) to ensure RMSD < 2.0 Å. The highest-ranked pose (lowest S-score) was selected. Per-residue interactions were decomposed into van der Waals and electrostatic components.

In vitro

Synthesis of the multifunctional nanocomposite

All synthesis steps were performed in triplicate using independent batches. The overall synthesis yield was determined gravimetrically by comparing the mass of the final dried nanocomposite to the total initial mass of precursors (Zn2⁺ salt, 2-MIM, resveratrol, dopamine, HA, and FA). Ligand surface densities for HA and FA were quantified as described below; the polydopamine content was assessed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Sect. 2.3.3) and by the mass increase after coating.

Synthesis of resveratrol-loaded ZIF-8 (ZIF-8-Res)

Resveratrol-loaded ZIF-8 nanoparticles were fabricated using an adapted co-precipitation methodology. Zinc acetate dihydrate (1.47 g, 6.7 mmol) was dissolved in 40 mL of methanol under magnetic stirring at 300 rpm to prepare Solution A. In a separate vessel, 2-methylimidazole (3.24 g, 39.4 mmol) and resveratrol (50 mg, 0.22 mmol) were dissolved in 40 mL of methanol with continuous stirring to form Solution B. Solution A was then rapidly added to Solution B under intensive magnetic agitation (600 rpm) at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). The stoichiometric ratio of Zn2⁺ to 2-MIM was maintained at 1:6. The reaction suspension was stirred continuously for 24 h at ambient temperature, yielding a white precipitate. The product was isolated by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm (13,400 × g) for 15 min at 4 °C, washed three times with fresh methanol to remove unreacted precursors and unencapsulated resveratrol, followed by drying in a vacuum oven at 40 °C for 12 h16. Drug loading was verified by indirect methods (supernatant depletion and UV quantification at 322 nm).

Polydopamine coating (ZIF-8-Res-PDA)

Polydopamine (PDA) coating was applied to ZIF-8-Res nanoparticles through oxidative auto-polymerization of dopamine in alkaline conditions. ZIF-8-Res nanoparticles (100 mg) were dispersed in 40 mL of Tris–HCl buffer (10 mM, pH 8.5) using ultrasonication (40 kHz, 100 W) for 10 min to ensure homogeneous dispersion. Dopamine hydrochloride (50 mg, 0.26 mmol) was subsequently added to the suspension, and the system was maintained under continuous agitation at 300 rpm for 6 h at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) in the dark to prevent premature oxidation. The oxidative polymerization of dopamine at alkaline pH resulted in the formation of a uniform PDA shell, evidenced by a color change from white to dark brown. The PDA-coated nanoparticles (ZIF-8-Res-PDA) were recovered via centrifugation at 12,000 rpm (13,400 × g) for 15 min, rinsed three times with deionized water (4 °C) to remove unreacted dopamine, and subsequently dried in a vacuum oven at 40 °C for 8 h17. The amount of deposited PDA, determined by TGA, corresponded to approximately 15 wt% of the total particle mass, confirming a uniform coating. Despite the moderate g-force, the dense ZIF-8 core ensured complete sedimentation, as evidenced by a transparent supernatant after centrifugation (Supplementary Figure S1). This retrieval method yielded a well-dispersed pellet and is consistent with protocols used for ZIF-8 nanoparticles of similar size (Iranpour et al., 2024). The overall yield of the final ZIF-8-Res-PDA-HA-FA nanocomposite, calculated from the initial precursor masses, was 73 ± 5% (n = 3), indicating efficient stepwise synthesis and acceptable recovery after multiple purification steps.

Functionalization with hyaluronic acid (ZIF-8-Res-PDA-HA)

Surface functionalization with hyaluronic acid (HA) was performed via EDC/NHS-mediated carbodiimide coupling to enable active targeting of CD44-overexpressing cancer cells. Hyaluronic acid (50 mg, MW 15–20 kDa) was solubilized in 20 mL of MES buffer (0.1 M, pH 5.5) under mild agitation. The carboxylic acid groups of HA were activated by sequential addition of EDC (100 mg, 0.52 mmol) and NHS (50 mg, 0.43 mmol), and the mixture was stirred for 1 h at room temperature to form stable NHS ester intermediates. Subsequently, ZIF-8-Res-PDA nanoparticles (100 mg) were dispersed in the HA activation mixture, and the reaction was carried out for 12 h at room temperature under gentle stirring (200 rpm) to facilitate the formation of a covalent amide linkage between the amine groups on the PDA surface and the activated carboxyl groups of HA. The resulting ZIF-8-Res-PDA-HA nanoparticles were isolated by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm (13,400 × g) for 15 min, washed five times with deionized water to remove unreacted EDC and NHS and unbound HA, and lyophilized for 24 h18.

Conjugation with folic acid (ZIF-8-Res-PDA-HA-FA)

To introduce a second targeting ligand that recognizes folate receptors overexpressed in colorectal cancer cells, folic acid (FA) was associated with the nanocomposite surface. Folic acid (20 mg, 0.045 mmol) was dissolved in 5 mL of anhydrous DMSO with stirring in the dark to prevent photodegradation. In a separate vessel, ZIF-8-Res-PDA-HA nanoparticles (100 mg) were dispersed in 20 mL of sodium bicarbonate buffer (0.1 M, pH 8.5) by ultrasonication for 5 min. The FA solution was then added dropwise (approximately 0.1 mL/min) to the nanoparticle suspension under gentle magnetic stirring (200 rpm), and the mixture was stirred for 12 h at ambient temperature under light-protected conditions. Given the absence of a carboxyl-activating agent in this step, covalent amide bond formation is unlikely. Instead, folic acid is proposed to adsorb onto the polydopamine-HA surface through π–π stacking interactions between the pteridine ring and the catechol moieties of PDA, complemented by electrostatic attraction between the deprotonated carboxylate groups of FA and residual amine groups of the coating at alkaline pH. The resulting ZIF-8-Res-PDA-HA-FA nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation at 12 000 rpm (13 400 × g) for 15 min, washed sequentially with deionized water (three times) and DMSO (once) to remove loosely bound FA, and dried under vacuum at 40 °C for 12 h19. The presence of FA in the final nanocomposite was confirmed by FTIR spectroscopy (Section "FTIR spectroscopy"), which revealed characteristic aromatic and pteridine vibrations, though these data do not distinguish between covalent and non-covalent association.

Characterization of the nanocomposite

Physicochemical properties

Hydrodynamic diameter, PDI, and zeta potential were measured via dynamic light scattering (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern) at 0.1 mg/mL in PBS pH 7.4, PBS pH 5.5, and RPMI-1640 + 10% FBS (equilibrated 2 min, 25 °C, triplicate measurements). Zeta potential was used with folded capillary cells and the Smoluchowski approximation. Colloidal stability was monitored for 7 days via DLS.

Morphology and structure

Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, Hitachi SU8010) was performed at 10 kV. Samples were drop-cast (0.05 mg/mL) onto silicon wafers, air-dried, and sputter-coated with 5 nm gold. Crystallinity was assessed via X-ray diffraction (XRD, Philips X’Pert Pro, Cu Kα radiation, 2θ = 5–50°).

Chemical composition

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Bruker Tensor 27) confirmed functionalization via the KBr pellet method (400–4000 cm⁻1, 64 scans). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, TA Instruments Q50) evaluated thermal stability under N₂ atmosphere (25–800 °C, 10 °C/min).

Drug loading and in vitro release study

Encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading capacity (LC)

The EE% and LC% of resveratrol were determined indirectly by quantifying unencapsulated resveratrol in the supernatant after ZIF-8-Res synthesis. The supernatant fraction was isolated by centrifugation, and resveratrol concentration was measured using UV–Vis spectrophotometry (Shimadzu UV-1800, Japan) at λmax = 322 nm. To account for possible absorbance from residual reactants, blank ZIF-8 nanoparticles (synthesized without resveratrol) were prepared in parallel. The supernatant obtained after the same washing steps showed no detectable absorbance at 322 nm (A < 0.002), confirming the absence of interfering species. Quantification was therefore performed against a calibration curve of resveratrol in methanol (1–50 µg/mL), which exhibited excellent linearity (y = 0.2472x − 0.0091, R2 = 0.9994, Fig. 2A). In vitro release kinetics

Release studies were performed via dialysis (MWCO 12–14 kDa) in triplicate. Nanoparticles equivalent to 1 mg resveratrol were dispersed in 2 mL PBS and sealed in dialysis bags, which were immersed in 50 mL release medium (pH 7.4, 6.5, or 5.5) at 37 °C with shaking (100 rpm). At intervals (0.5–72 h), 2 mL aliquots were withdrawn and replaced with pre-warmed fresh medium. Sink conditions were maintained (C < 15% of solubility). Released resveratrol was quantified at 322 nm. Release data were fitted to the Korsmeyer-Peppas and Higuchi models to elucidate the mechanism.

In vitro biological evaluations

All assays were performed with n ≥ 3 independent batches of nanoparticles, blinded treatment allocation, and randomized well positions to minimize bias.

Cytotoxicity assay (MTT)

The cytotoxicity of the nanoformulation was determined against the HCT116 and HDF cell lines using the MTT metabolic viability assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in 100 µL of complete medium and incubated for 24 h to allow cell attachment. The medium was replaced with fresh RPMI-1640 containing serial dilutions of the test samples (7.8–500 µg/mL), and the cells were incubated for an additional 24 h. After treatment, 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added per well and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. The medium was removed, formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 µL DMSO with gentle shaking for 10 min, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm with a 630 nm reference20. IC₅₀ values were calculated by nonlinear regression (GraphPad Prism 8).

Apoptosis analysis by Annexin V-FITC/PI staining

Apoptotic cell death was quantified by flow cytometry using an Annexin V-FITC/PI dual-staining approach according to the manufacturer’s protocol. HCT116 cells were cultured at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well for 24 h. Cells were then treated with ZIF-8-Res-PDA-HA-FA at the IC₅₀ concentration determined from the MTT assay, along with an untreated control and free resveratrol (at equivalent concentration) for 48 h. After treatment, cells were trypsinized, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and resuspended in 100 µL of 1 × binding buffer at 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Annexin V-FITC (5 µL) and propidium iodide (PI, 5 µL) were added, and cells were incubated in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 400 µL of 1 × binding buffer was added, and samples were immediately analyzed using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer. At least 10,000 events per sample were recorded, and data were analyzed with FlowJo v10. Cells were categorized as viable (Annexin V⁻/PI⁻), early apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻), late apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺), or necrotic (Annexin V⁻/PI⁺), and total apoptosis was expressed as the sum of early and late apoptotic cells21.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The effect of the nanocomposite on the expression of apoptosis-related genes (TP53, CDKN1A [p21], and CASP8) was determined using quantitative real-time PCR. HCT116 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 2 × 105 cells per well and treated with ZIF-8-Res-PDA-HA-FA at the IC₅₀ concentration for 48 h, alongside untreated controls. Total RNA was extracted using the RNX-PLUS kit following the manufacturer’s instructions, and RNA purity and concentration were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Using a Pars Tous Kit, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System using the following cycling conditions: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Primer sequences are provided in Table 1. Relative gene expression was determined using the 2(−ΔΔCt) method, with GAPDH as the internal control. Cell migration assay (wound healing)

The anti-migratory effect of the nanocomposite was assessed using an in vitro scratch wound-healing assay. HCT116 cells were seeded at 3 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates and cultured until reaching 90–95% confluence. A straight scratch was made in the center of each well with a sterile 200 µL pipette tip, and floating cells and debris were removed by washing twice with PBS. Cells were then treated with ZIF-8-Res-PDA-HA-FA at a sub-cytotoxic concentration (¼ IC₅₀) in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium to eliminate the confounding effect of cell proliferation. Untreated cells in serum-free medium served as controls. Phase-contrast images of the wound area were acquired immediately after scratching (0 h) and following 24 h of incubation at three predefined locations per well using an inverted microscope with a digital camera. A₀ and A₂₄ represent the wound areas at 0 and 24 h, respectively. Migration inhibition was measured as the reduction in wound closure compared with untreated controls. Experiments were conducted in triplicate23.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted independently in triplicate unless specified otherwise. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality (Shapiro–Wilk) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s) were verified. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test in SPSS v22. Differences with a p-value less than 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant.

Result

Molecular docking and interaction analysis of resveratrol-Bcl-2 complex

The computational docking of resveratrol into the BH3-binding groove of Bcl-2 yielded an S-score (GBVI/WSA dG binding free energy) of −6.23 kcal/mol, indicating favorable spontaneous binding. The predicted binding pose demonstrated high geometric fidelity, with an RMSD-refine value of 1.15 Å relative to the initial conformation, confirming robust convergence during post-docking refinement. It is important to note that the docking analysis provides a computational prediction of binding feasibility under static conditions. The calculated S-score and the interaction pattern do not demonstrate that resveratrol functions as a Bcl-2 inhibitor in cells, nor do they confirm target engagement within the mitochondrial membrane context. Functional validation, such as measurement of changes in mitochondrial membrane potential, cytochrome c release, or caspase-9 activation, is necessary to establish a causal link between the proposed binding mode and the observed pro-apoptotic effects. Accordingly, the docking results are interpreted solely as a theoretical guide for future mechanistic studies.

Encapsulation efficacy and drug release assay

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) of resveratrol in the final ZIF-8-Res-PDA-HA-FA nanocomposite was 81.9 ± 2.1% (n = 3), determined by UV–Vis quantification at 322 nm using a methanol-based calibration curve (y = 0.2472x − 0.0091, R2 = 0.9994) (Fig. 2A). pH-responsive release was evaluated under simulated physiological (pH 7.4), mildly acidic (pH 6.5), and tumour-microenvironment-relevant acidic (pH 5.5) conditions over 120 h. The complete release dataset, obtained from triplicate independent nanocarrier batches, is provided in Table 3 and plotted in Fig. 2B. All profiles exhibited a biphasic pattern: a moderate initial burst (7–11% at 3 h) followed by sustained, pH-dependent liberation. After 72 h, cumulative release reached 59.9 ± 2.6% at pH 5.5, 48.6 ± 3.4% at pH 6.5, and 33.2 ± 3.4% at pH 7.4. The approximately 1.8-fold increase from pH 7.4 to pH 5.5, together with the ordered intermediate values at pH 6.5, unambiguously demonstrates the pH-triggered release behaviour of the nanocomposite. Kinetic modelling was applied to the full release profiles. In addition to the Korsmeyer-Peppas model, the data were fitted to the Higuchi, first-order, Hixson–Crowell, and Weibull models. Rather, it involves simultaneous framework dissolution, polydopamine swelling, and drug–polymer π–π interactions. A detailed comparison of the model fits is beyond the scope of the present work and will be reported separately.

Nanoparticle physicochemical characterization

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential analysis

DLS revealed a Z-average diameter of 193.93 ± 8 nm with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.1970, reflecting a narrow, monomodal size distribution (Fig. 3A). A PDI value below 0.2 is considered indicative of highly uniform nanoparticles, which is critical for reproducible cellular uptake and biodistribution. The cumulative size distribution parameters were Dv₁₀ = 98 nm, Dv₅₀ = 246 nm, and Dv₉₀ = 389 nm, confirming minimal presence of large aggregates. The corresponding electrophoretic mobility was − 3.36 ± 0.28 µm·cm/V⁻1·s⁻1 (n = 201 measurement events). FESEM

Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, Hitachi SU8010) was performed at 10 kV on samples drop-cast onto silicon wafers, air-dried, and sputter-coated with 5 nm gold. The micrographs revealed spherical primary particles; however, considerable aggregation was observed in the dry state, with clusters of variable size rather than a uniform distribution of isolated nanoparticles (Fig. 3C). This aggregation is likely an artefact of sample drying and the adhesive nature of the polydopamine-based coating, which is rich in catechol and carboxylate groups that can form inter-particle hydrogen bonds upon desolvation. In contrast, DLS analysis of the same batch in aqueous suspension (Section "Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential analysis") yielded a Z-average diameter of 194 nm and a polydispersity index of 0.197, indicative of a monodisperse, colloidally stable population. The high zeta potential (− 40.6 mV) provides strong electrostatic repulsion that maintains individual particle dispersion in liquid, even though this stabilisation is lost upon drying. Consequently, while the FESEM images confirm the nanoscale size and spherical shape of the primary particles, they do not fully represent the dispersion state in solution. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) or cryo-TEM imaging is warranted to visualise the unaggregated morphology and to confirm the uniformity of the PDA-HA-FA coating. The mean particle diameter estimated from FESEM (~ 180–200 nm) is consistent with the DLS hydrodynamic size, accounting for the absence of a hydration layer in the dry state.

Cytotoxicity and selectivity

MTT assay

The cytotoxic efficacy and selectivity of ZIF-8-Res-PDA-HA-FA nanoparticles were assessed by the MTT assay in HCT116 colorectal carcinoma cells and HDF normal human dermal fibroblasts (Fig. 5). In HCT116 cells, viability decreased in a concentration-dependent manner from 100% (untreated) to 92.1 ± 3.2%, 89.9 ± 2.8%, 72.5 ± 4.1%, 42.1 ± 3.5%, 4.1 ± 1.2%, 0.29 ± 0.15%, and 0% at 7.8, 15.6, 31.2, 62.5, 125, 250, and 500 µg mL⁻1, respectively (all p < 0.001 vs. control). No gross morphological alterations were observed in HDF cultures. The pronounced differential toxicity likely arises from a combination of enhanced nanoparticle uptake by receptor-overexpressing HCT116 cells and the greater intrinsic sensitivity of colorectal cancer cells to resveratrol-induced mitochondrial apoptosis, while normal fibroblasts are less susceptible to these effects. Discussion

This study demonstrates a multifunctional ZIF-8-based nanocomposite that delivers targeted resveratrol to colorectal cancer cells via dual ligand functionalization and pH-responsive release. The platform exhibits favorable physicochemical properties (194 nm diameter, −40.6 mV zeta potential) and high drug loading (81.9% EE), with in vitro validation confirming selective cytotoxicity against HCT116 cells (IC₅₀ = 54.4 µg/mL) while sparing normal fibroblasts.

In silico rationale as a hypothesis, not a proof

The docking score of − 6.23 kcal/mol suggests that resveratrol could be a potential but moderate Bcl-2 inhibitor. Synthetic inhibitors identified through virtual screening typically exhibit affinities of − 10 to − 11 kcal/mol, highlighting the difference between natural and optimized compounds24. However, the RMSD-refine value of 1.15 Å indicates stable binding within the BH3 groove, and the binding pose resembles that of native pro-apoptotic peptides, which is notable because it suggests that resveratrol might compete with Bax/Bak at the heterodimerization interface. Although this remains speculative in the absence of functional validation. The strong interaction with Ala4 (− 1.1 kcal/mol) is noteworthy since this residue is located at the rim of the BH3 pocket, a key site for Bcl-2’s anti-apoptotic function. By occupying this position, resveratrol could interfere with the Bak/Bax–Bcl-2 interface, potentially restoring apoptotic signaling25. The secondary contact with Thr7 supports hydrophobic burial, which is characteristic of effective BH3 mimetics. Although free resveratrol has modest affinity, delivery via nanocarriers increases mitochondrial concentrations by 10–100 times, enhancing its pharmacological relevance26. However, molecular docking does not recapitulate the dynamic cellular environment, and the modest affinity does not prove functional antagonism. Therefore, these computational results should be regarded as a structure-based hypothesis that resveratrol might compete with BH3-only proteins—a hypothesis that can only be tested by cellular thermal shift assays, co-immunoprecipitation, or apoptosis assays performed in Bcl-2-overexpressing versus knock-down backgrounds. The nanocarrier-mediated increase in intracellular resveratrol concentrations may favour such inhibition, but this remains speculative.

Strategic implications for nanocarrier design

The validated binding mechanism provides in silico justification for the observed synergy in our pH-responsive ZIF-8 nanocomposite. Upon endosomal escape and cytosolic release, resveratrol is concentrated near mitochondria, where Bcl-2 is tethered to the outer membrane. Local concentrations in the micromolar range would saturate the BH3 groove27 (KD ≈ 50 µM predicted from ΔG), triggering MOMP (mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization) and cytochrome c release28. This complements the targeting moieties (folic acid/hyaluronic acid) that enrich the formulation in FRα⁺/CD44⁺ cancer cells harboring Bcl-2 overexpression, creating a dual anti-Bcl-2 effect: direct pharmacological inhibition and selective cellular accumulation29.

Nanoparticle size and EPR effect

The Z-average diameter of 194 nm with a low polydispersity index (PDI = 0.197) situates this formulation within the optimal window for tumor accumulation via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. Nanoparticles between 100–200 nm are generally considered ideal for passive tumor targeting, as they are large enough to avoid renal clearance yet small enough to penetrate leaky tumor vasculature. Comparable findings have been reported by Iranpour et al.11, who developed ZIF-8/doxorubicin nanoparticles averaging 180 nm and demonstrated enhanced tumor penetration in colorectal cancer (CRC) xenografts. Similarly, Xie et al.30 reported ZIF-8-based nanocarriers in the 150–200 nm range, achieving significant accumulation in HCT116 tumors, reinforcing the relevance of this size profile for CRC therapy. However, it must be emphasised that the EPR effect is highly heterogeneous in human tumours and often does not translate predictably from animal models to patients. Therefore, while the particle size is compatible with passive tumour accumulation, the efficacy of such a mechanism in clinical settings is uncertain, and additional active-targeting or tumour-penetrating strategies would likely be required to achieve meaningful therapeutic benefit.

Colloidal stability and zeta potential

The strongly negative zeta potential (− 40.6 mV) exceeds the ± 30 mV threshold typically required for stable colloidal dispersions, suggesting robust electrostatic repulsion that minimizes aggregation during circulation. This finding is consistent with that of Jongert et al.23, who observed that ZIF-8 nanoparticles with zeta potentials below − 35 mV remained stable for over 72 h in serum-containing media. However, the trade-off between stability and opsonization risk must be considered. Highly negative surfaces are prone to protein corona formation, which can accelerate clearance by the reticuloendothelial system (RES). Indeed, PEGylated ZIF-8 systems reported by Feng et al.31 demonstrated that surface charge modulation toward near-neutral values improved circulation half-life while maintaining colloidal stability, highlighting the importance of balancing electrostatic repulsion with stealth properties.

Drug loading and pH-responsive release kinetics

The observed EE of 81.9% for resveratrol in the ZIF-8/PDA system significantly exceeds the typical EE range of 50–65% reported for polyphenols in conventional liposomal carriers32. This enhancement is attributed to the high surface area and porosity of ZIF-8, which facilitates efficient drug loading via physical adsorption and coordination interactions.

In a comparable study, Ali et al. reported an EE of 78% for resveratrol in nanoliposomes co-loaded with rapamycin. However, their system exhibited rapid release kinetics, with over 85% of the drug released within 48 h, indicating limited retention capacity under physiological conditions32. In contrast, the current ZIF-8/PDA system demonstrates a biphasic release profile with an initial burst of 10.2%, followed by sustained release over 72 h, achieving 58.7% cumulative release at pH 5.5 and only 31.2% at pH 7.4. This controlled release behavior is advantageous for tumor-targeted delivery, where acidic microenvironments can trigger drug liberation.

This pH-responsive degradation is characteristic of ZIF-8’s acid-labile Zn-N bonds. Still, our poor Korsmeyer-Peppas model fit (R2 < 0.85) suggests a more complex mechanism involving simultaneous framework dissolution, polydopamine swelling, and π-π stacking interactions between resveratrol and PDA. This complexity aligns with findings by Ali et al. (2024), who reviewed nanoparticle-based resveratrol systems and noted that hybrid carriers often exhibit non-Fickian release due to synergistic interactions between core and shell materials32. This mechanistic ambiguity warrants further investigation via quartz crystal microbalance or in situ AFM to optimize release profiles for in vivo applications.

Selective cytotoxicity and therapeutic window

The calculated IC₅₀ of 54.4 µg/mL against HCT116 cells aligns with values reported for other polyphenol-loaded nanocarriers in CRC models. For instance, resveratrol-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles exhibited an IC₅₀ of 62.8 µg/mL in HCT116 cells after 48 h of exposure33. In comparison, hyaluronic acid-functionalized carrier-free nanoparticles containing hydroxycamptothecine achieved IC₅₀ values of 45–70 µg/mL depending on HA density34. Our system’s preservation of > 70% fibroblast viability at concentrations up to 500 µg/mL suggests a favorable therapeutic index. However, this comparison is limited by the use of dermal rather than colonic epithelial fibroblasts. The selective toxicity likely arises from enhanced CD44/folate receptor-mediated internalization in cancer cells, coupled with the pro-oxidant effects of released Zn2⁺ ions—a mechanism documented in ZIF-8 systems where intracellular zinc accumulation contributes to mitochondria-mediated apoptosis35. However, the relative contributions of resveratrol and Zn2⁺ ions to the observed cytotoxicity cannot be disentangled from the current data. Intracellular zinc accumulation can cause mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS generation, which may synergize with or even dominate the apoptotic response. Future studies should include ROS quantification and caspase-3/9 activity assays to deconvolute these effects.

Apoptotic mechanism: predominant intrinsic pathway activation

Our data strongly support p53-mediated intrinsic apoptosis as the primary cell death mechanism. The dose-dependent upregulation of p53 (9.96-fold) and its downstream effector p21 (2.3-fold) is consistent with resveratrol’s known activity as a genotoxic stress inducer36. This transcriptional signature mirrors findings from Sadri et al.37, who reported an 8.4-fold increase in p53 expression in Y79 retinoblastoma cells treated with transferrin/folate-targeted nanoparticles. Critically, our observation that caspase-8 expression remained at baseline levels contradicts the hypothesis of extrinsic pathway involvement and represents a key mechanistic clarification. The absence of caspase-8 induction, despite significant apoptosis, emphasizes mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization as the central event—likely through Bcl-2 antagonism (as suggested by our docking simulations) and Bax/Bak activation38,39. This aligns with studies showing resveratrol-induced apoptosis in HCT116 cells proceeds primarily via the intrinsic pathway when p53 is wild-type36. However, because mRNA levels do not always correlate with protein function, and because we did not assess protein markers such as Bax, Bcl-2, cleaved caspase-9, cleaved caspase-3, or PARP, the involvement of the intrinsic pathway remains an unconfirmed interpretation. The absence of caspase-8 induction further supports that the extrinsic pathway is not activated, but formal exclusion would require additional data. Additionally, the potential role of Zn2⁺-induced oxidative stress in activating the intrinsic pathway was not assessed. Measurements of caspase-3/9 enzymatic activity, cytochrome c release, and ROS levels are necessary to establish a complete mechanistic picture and to differentiate between drug-specific and carrier-mediated pro-apoptotic signals. Future work must include Western blot analysis of key apoptotic regulators to validate the proposed mechanism.

Anti-migratory effects: observation versus mechanism

The significant inhibition of wound closure (25–30% vs. 85–90% in controls) at sub-cytotoxic concentrations provides compelling evidence for anti-metastatic activity, as scratch assays are established surrogates for evaluating tumor cell invasion capacity in colorectal cancer research40. However, this observation lacks mechanistic substantiation. Unlike studies by Li et al.41, who demonstrated that HA-functionalized nanoparticles suppressed migration by downregulating MMP-9 and vimentin, we did not evaluate epithelial-mesenchymal transition markers or focal adhesion dynamics42. The effect could be attributed to resveratrol’s known inhibition of PI3K/Akt signaling, which regulates cytoskeletal remodeling, but without specific pathway analysis, we cannot distinguish cytostatic from anti-invasive effects. Future work must include transwell invasion assays and mechanistic probing of EMT markers to validate anti-metastatic claims.

Rationale for surface functionalization and targeting considerations

The combination of hyaluronic acid (a CD44 ligand) and folic acid (a folate receptor ligand) was predicated on the documented overexpression of these receptors in colorectal cancer. However, the present study does not provide evidence that the functionalized nanoparticles achieve active, receptor-mediated targeting. Competitive inhibition assays, single-ligand controls (ZIF-8-Res-PDA-HA and ZIF-8-Res-PDA-FA), and receptor expression quantification were not performed. Consequently, the enhanced cytotoxicity and anti-migratory effects observed in HCT116 cells cannot be attributed to ligand-specific recognition and may instead reflect passive accumulation governed by the EPR effect or non-specific electrostatic interactions. This critical experimental gap precludes any claim of dual-targeting benefits over monofunctional or non-functional formulations. Future work must include receptor-blocking studies and comparative uptake experiments to dissect the contribution of each ligand.

Translational limitations and future directions

Before clinical translation can be considered, several critical barriers must be addressed: the complete absence of in vivo pharmacokinetic, biodistribution, and toxicological data precludes assessment of systemic safety and tumor-specific accumulation, as ZIF-8 frameworks are known to undergo rapid hepatic and splenic clearance while potential zinc toxicity demands evaluation in animal models; furthermore, All mechanistic conclusions based on mRNA changes must be corroborated by protein-level analysis (Western blot) and functional mitochondrial apoptosis assays (ΔΨm, cytochrome c release, caspase-9/3 activity). ROS quantification and caspase activity measurements are also required to delineate the contribution of Zn2⁺ ions released from the ZIF-8 framework versus resveratrol itself. The computational docking prediction for Bcl-2 binding requires cellular target-engagement studies to establish a functional link to the observed cytotoxicity.; biological generality remains limited by the exclusive use of a single cell line (HCT116, which harbours wild-type p53 and K-Ras). The observed responses, including the p53-mediated apoptotic signature, cannot be assumed to apply to K-Ras-mutant or p53-null CRC models, which represent clinically prevalent subtypes. Moreover, all experiments were performed in 2D monolayer cultures, which do not recapitulate tumour architecture, drug penetration barriers, or intercellular interactions present in solid tumours. Validation in patient-derived organoids, 3D spheroids, and KRAS-mutant or p53-mutant CRC lines to assess performance across molecular subtypes; the dual-targeting strategy lacks justification without competitive inhibition studies, receptor expression quantification, and direct comparison to single-ligand controls; and finally, the polydopamine coating’s stability during prolonged circulation and its potential immunogenicity remain entirely uncharacterized.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that ZIF-Res-PDA-HA-FA nanoparticles constitute a promising multifunctional platform for targeted delivery of resveratrol to colorectal cancer cells. The nanoparticles exhibited high drug encapsulation efficiency (81.9%), pH-responsive controlled release, and favorable physicochemical properties, including uniform size distribution (193.93 nm), strong colloidal stability (zeta potential: − 40.55 mV), and successful dual surface functionalization with hyaluronic acid and folic acid as moieties that may, in principle, recognize CD44 and folate receptors, although receptor-mediated targeting was not experimentally validated. In vitro assays demonstrated potent, concentration-dependent cytotoxicity against HCT-116 cells and confirmed apoptosis induction primarily through the intrinsic (p53/p21-mediated) pathway, as caspase-8 expression remained constitutively unchanged. Furthermore, significant inhibition of cell migration was observed, suggesting potential anti-metastatic properties. These findings underscore the potential of HA/FA-dual-functionalized, polydopamine-coated ZIF-8 nanoparticles as a precision therapeutic strategy for colorectal cancer. Further investigation in genetically diverse CRC lines, 3D tumour models, and in vivo systems, together with rigorous targeting validation, is warranted to comprehensively evaluate therapeutic efficacy, biodistribution, pharmacokinetics, and safety profiles prior to clinical translation.
