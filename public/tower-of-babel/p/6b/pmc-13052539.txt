Evaluating the impact of an Australian Virtual Care service on low-acuity presentations to district emergency departments: an interrupted time series analysis
Authors: Steven Chung, Leah Maitland, Casey Luke, Ben Hodgson, Caroline Kuhne, Daniel Barker, Thomas Robinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the impact of an Australian Virtual Care service on low-acuity patient presentations to emergency departments (EDs) across a local health district.DesignThis was a retrospective study using an interrupted time series analysis to compare outcomes before and after the introduction of Mid North Coast Virtual Care (MNCVC). The comparison of these periods aims to identify changes in the rate of semi-urgent (category 4) and non-urgent (category 5) ED presentations following the intervention.SettingThis analysis covers the Mid North Coast Local Health District, a coastal region in New South Wales, Australia. It encompasses EDs in Port Macquarie, Coffs Harbour, Kempsey, Macksville, Dorrigo and Bellingen.Main outcome measureWhether there was a reduction in low-acuity ED presentations (category 4 and 5) as a proportion of total ED presentations at Mid North Coast EDs following the commencement of MNCVC as an alternative to ED attendance.ResultsIn the years prior to intervention, the proportion of total ED presentations that were low-acuity presentations averaged 54.58%. Following intervention from July 2022 onwards, there was an immediate non-significant 1.04% decrease in the proportion of category 4 and 5 presentations (95% CI −2.11 to 0.04, p=0.063), with a further significant 0.12% decrease each month thereafter (95% CI −0.17 to −0.06, p<0.001). By December 2024, the model estimated a cumulative 4.64% decrease in the proportion of low-acuity presentations since the introduction of the virtual care service.ConclusionsFollowing commencement of a virtual care service, a significant and sustained reduction in the proportion of low-acuity presentations was observed across the district EDs. Virtual care services may contribute to easing the burden of low-acuity presentations on EDs.

Introduction

The Mid North Coast (MNC) Local Health District is located in regional New South Wales (NSW) Australia, with a catchment population of approximately 233 000 people.1 Across NSW, numbers of presentations to emergency departments (EDs) across all triage categories (including categories 4 and 5) have increased 2.3% annually between 2019 and 2023.2

Low-acuity patients comprise almost half of all ED presentations across NSW.3 We defined low-acuity patients as ED presentations by people triaged as Australasian Triage Scale category 4 or 5, who did not arrive in an ambulance, police or correctional services vehicle, were not admitted to hospital or referred to another hospital, and did not die before leaving the ED.4 Higher percentages of low-acuity patients have been observed in regional and remote areas,5 including the MNC Local Health District, where it peaked at 55.29% in fiscal year 2021/2022. These presentations contribute substantially to ED workload and may be suitable for alternative models of care.

Virtual care services have emerged as one such approach. Virtual care refers to the coordinated delivery of clinical care using digital communication technologies, encompassing remote triage, assessment, monitoring, follow-up and escalation processes.6 This is a broader term than telehealth, which typically involves synchronous video or telephone consultations alone.

Previous studies have suggested that virtual ED and virtual care models can reduce unnecessary physical ED presentations by providing timely assessment and management remotely. For example, Kelly et al reported that a virtual ED model in Queensland was able to redirect a substantial proportion of community referrals away from the physical ED.7 However, these have focused on referral-level outcomes rather than a resultant system-wide impact. Whether virtual care programmes can reduce low-acuity presentations to EDs remains unclear.

This study aims to address this gap by evaluating whether the introduction of a district-wide virtual care service was associated with a reduction in the proportion of low-acuity ED presentations across the MNC Local Health District.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective, observational study compared outcomes before and after the introduction of Mid North Coast Virtual Care (MNCVC). The analysis spanned two distinct time periods: a preintervention period from 1 July 2018 to 30 June 2022, when MNCVC was not available, and a postintervention period from 1 July 2022 to December 2024, when MNCVC was in operation. The comparison of these periods aims to identify changes in the proportion of low-acuity ED presentations following the intervention.

Setting

This study was conducted within the MNC Local Health District, a coastal region in NSW, Australia. The district includes EDs at Port Macquarie, Coffs Harbour, Kempsey, Macksville, Dorrigo and Bellingen. Across the study period, the population of the MNC grew by 7.3%, equating to an increase of 16 185 people.1 Over the same period, the district EDs collectively received between 130 000 to 1 50 000 presentations annually (see online supplemental figure S1).

Intervention

MNCVC commenced operation in July 2022. The primary goal of MNCVC is to reduce low-acuity ED presentations by providing patients with an alternative to the ED. The service runs 7 days a week, from 8:30 to 16:30, with after-hours referrals accepted via phone message, email or through the electronic medical record. Referrals are accepted from ED triage prior to treatment; ED doctors following treatment and from NSW Ambulance paramedics prior to a decision on transporting a patient to the ED; as well as directly from patients in the community (community self-referrals and hospital-related self-referrals) and early supported discharge.

Community self-referrals are patients who directly contact MNCVC and include those who have learnt about the service through promotion, word of mouth, previous usage or from their pharmacist or general practitioner (GP). For example, GP practices closed over holiday periods can divert their patients to MNCVC via signage and pre-recorded phone messages. Hospital-related self-referrals include patients who contact MNCVC from the community after advice from ED or hospital doctors and nurses. These patients are not directly referred by health professionals, but are advised to contact MNCVC if they require further medical treatment after a presentation to the ED or hospital admission. Early supported discharge describes referrals from health professionals for patients discharged from hospital who require support for; symptom monitoring and management, medication support and/or results follow-up.

MNCVC comprises a hospital-led virtual care clinic staffed by GPs, nurses and administration staff. There are two medical officers rostered on during weekday shifts and one medical officer for weekend shifts. They are supported by seven nursing staff during the weekdays and five on weekends. The clinical lead of the programme is a local specialist physician.

Patient contact is facilitated via the use of smartphones, myVirtualCare (eHealth NSW) and MedSync Connect (eHealth NSW & Microsoft) to allow an audio and video interface. Patients are initially triaged and then managed via telehealth consultations. Reviews can be one-off or multiple for the duration of their treatment. Adult and paediatric patients (see online supplemental table S1 for demographics) are treated for common medical conditions, typically managed by GPs (see online supplemental table S2). Investigations are ordered electronically, and scripts are dispensed via Kiteworks (Kiteworks) or fax to the patient’s pharmacy. Prescribing opioid analgesics is outside the scope of practice. Specialist advice can be sought from medical subspecialties, and escalation pathways are in place for deteriorating patients.

Outcome measure

The outcome for this study was the rate of low-acuity ED presentations as a proportion of total ED presentations. This proportion was tracked monthly over the study period to assess the impact of MNCVC. An ED presentation is recorded once a person registers with the ED administration staff. This can be before or after triage, depending on the number of people arriving at that time. Some (perhaps many) patients referred to MNCVC from ED would already have been registered as an ED presentation. So, although their care may have been with MNCVC, they would already have been counted as an ED presentation. This may allow for some patients to be double-counted, potentially increasing the number of category 4 and 5 ED presentations recorded.

We measured the proportion of low-acuity presentations rather than absolute numbers, consistent with the approach used by the Australasian Institute of Health and Welfare.4 In the context of sustained population growth and rising overall ED activity, proportional measures provide a more robust indicator of relative change in case-mix over time than absolute presentation counts.2

We compare the proportion of category 4 and 5 presentations in the years before and after the commencement of MNCVC, which began operation in July 2022. The aim is to assess whether a hospital-led virtual care model was associated with any measurable changes in ED utilisation at a district level.

Data sources

Aggregate-level ED presentation data were extracted from routinely collected datasets across the two time periods. FirstNet data from the Cerner PowerChart for the period 1 July 2018 to 31 December 2024 were extracted and analysed, focusing on triage categories of presentations across EDs within the MNC Local Health District.

Referral numbers and source of referral to MNCVC were tracked using the Cerner PowerChart Community Health Outpatient Care report for the period 1 July 2022 to 31 December 2024. For community self-referrals and hospital-related self-referrals, additional data were extracted from a standard phone-text questionnaire patients completed to give further information about their referral source. During the same period, discharges from MNCVC were monitored and internal referrals, submitted via electronic consult orders from inpatient and EDs, were tracked. There were no missing data in the aggregate monthly dataset.

Statistical analysis

An interrupted time series (ITS) analysis was conducted using an AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model. The ARIMA model was chosen for its ability to handle seasonality and autocorrelation in the data, which could not be adequately addressed by initial segmented regression models (see online supplemental report). The model estimated immediate change following the introduction of MNCVC and any subsequent change in monthly trend. A significance level of 0.05 was used for all statistical tests. All analyses were performed in R (V.4.4.1).

A 4-year preintervention period was selected to provide a stable baseline, capture multiple seasonal cycles and support reliable ARIMA modelling. The post-intervention period included all available data following the commencement of MNCVC and covered two winter and summer seasonal peaks.

Data regarding referral numbers were graphed as monthly points. Referral sources were tabulated per calendar year. This component of data was analysed descriptively.

This study included all available ED presentations within the district across the study period and therefore did not require a sample size calculation. The dataset was population-level and not sampled. No sensitivity analyses were conducted. As with all observational analyses using routine administrative data, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

MNCVC monthly referral numbers are seen to increase steadily from inception of the service until the predefined end period of data collection in December 2024 (figure 1). A pattern of spikes in referral numbers was observed every winter and over the summer holidays.

At the commencement of the new service, the majority of referrals were community self-referred patients. As the service matured, referrals came from a mix of community self-referrals, EDs, hospital-related self-referrals and early supported discharge (table 1). The number of community self-referred patients declined from 2023 to 2024. This was largely due to capacity constraints, as on days when the number of total referrals exceeded manageable levels, surge planning blocked community self-referrals. Prior to the introduction of MNCVC, category 4 and 5 presentations as a percentage of total ED presentations averaged 54.58%. Following the introduction of MNCVC in July 2022, a sustained downward trend in the proportion of category 4 and 5 presentations was observed. Specifically, the MNCVC implementation was followed by an immediate non-significant 1.04% decrease in the proportion of category 4 and 5 presentations (95% CI −2.11 to 0.04, p=0.063), with a further significant 0.12% decrease each month thereafter (95% CI −0.17 to −0.06, p<0.001).

Using the ARIMA model, by December 2024, this cumulative effect (combining the initial 1.04% decrease with the ongoing 0.12% monthly reduction over 30 months (1.04%+30*0.12%)), resulted in an estimated 4.64% decrease in the proportion of category 4 and 5 presentations compared with the counterfactual prediction (figure 4). Absolute ED presentation numbers are depicted in online supplemental figure S1. Absolute category 4 and 5 presentation numbers remained relatively stable over the study period despite growth in total ED activity, supporting interpretation of a relative case-mix shift.

Discussion

There were robust referral numbers to MNCVC seen over the first 30 months of the service. Referrals showed seasonal demand, peaking in the winter and summer months. Referral patterns were seen to evolve over the period studied. Initially, the majority of referrals were community self-referring patients for low-acuity conditions. By the third year of operation, ED referrals, hospital-related self-referrals and early supported discharge have increased.

Any observed effect appears to align more closely with total referral volume to MNCVC rather than community self-referrals alone. Previous literature has suggested that providing virtual care support for patients following an ED presentation may reduce ED re-presentation rates8 9 and similarly for patients discharged from hospital care.10 11 We have recently demonstrated a low ED presentation rate for patients who have been referred to MNCVC by a paramedic for management of a low-acuity condition instead of transporting to the ED.12

This study observed a temporal association between the introduction of a hospital-led virtual care service and a reduction in the proportion of low-acuity presentations to physical EDs in the district. This may reflect the provision of accessible medical and nursing care within a structured service model incorporating triage, clinical treatment pathways, escalation pathways and access to specialist medical advice.

While the immediate reduction of 1.04% following implementation was not statistically significant, the intervention appeared to produce a sustained, significant decrease of 0.12% per month thereafter following commencement of MNCVC. The sustained monthly trend suggests that any association between MNCVC implementation and ED case-mix change persisted over time. By December 2024 (30 months postimplementation), the model estimated a cumulative 4.64% decrease in the proportion of category 4 and 5 presentations compared with what would have occurred without the intervention. This represents the combined effect of the initial 1.04% decrease and the ongoing monthly reduction of 0.12% over 30 months. At this stage, the proportion of low-acuity presentations appears to be decreasing steadily over the period studied.

By using an ARIMA model, we were able to account for seasonal ED presentation trends. The use of longitudinal data allowed us to observe ED presentation trends over time and increased the statistical power of this study. While total ED presentations increased over the study period, driven largely by growth in category 1–3 presentations, category 4 and 5 presentations did not demonstrate a comparable rise. Consequently, the observed reduction reflects a proportional shift in ED case-mix rather than confirmed absolute decline. In the context of population growth and rising statewide ED activity, the stabilisation of low-acuity presentations may indicate relative moderation of growth following implementation of MNCVC. However, in the absence of a control group, causality cannot be inferred, and alternative macro-environmental explanations cannot be excluded.

Limitations of this study

The lack of a control group in our ITS analysis does not allow us to exclude macroenvironmental confounding factors, such as commercial telehealth providers, or any long-term change in patient behaviours following the COVID-19 pandemic.

In the Australian setting, GP Urgent Care Clinics (UCC) have formed part of the government response to reduce the burden on EDs. In our local health district, there are two UCCs. Wauchope UCC opened in September 201413 and has had no changes in resourcing over the timeframe studied, and hence is unlikely to have impacted our results. Coffs Harbour UCC opened in November 2023,14 which falls within our post-intervention period. However, it does not appear to have materially affected our results as the reduction in low-acuity ED presentations predates the opening of the UCC and following its opening, the downward trend does not change.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, there was a decrease in healthcare utilisation seen across multiple settings including EDs.15 In an Australian setting, Sutherland et al16 showed reduced healthcare utilisation during the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic in NSW, Australia. This included total ED visits, which reduced by 13.9% for the period studied from March to June 2020, compared with the same period in 2019. However, an exception to this was low-acuity presentations to ED, which actually increased over that period, and this was driven mostly by increased respiratory presentations. By September 2020, the authors noted activity levels were seen to be recovering, although not back to pre-pandemic levels. For the same timeframe, our data show a similar reduction in total ED presentations; however, this had recovered to pre-pandemic levels by October 2020 and comprises part of our preintervention dataset and hence should not be a confounding factor. While ED utilisation patterns shifted during the COVID-19 pandemic, the literature does not clearly establish that post-pandemic behavioural changes have led to sustained increases in higher acuity (category 1–3) ED presentations.

A Virtual ED was shown by Kelly et al7 to have a high prevention rate of community triaged patients presenting to the physical ED in a Queensland metropolitan health service. The success of this intervention was measured by the high percentage (70%) of referrals being re-directed away from the physical ED. However, the authors did not report on whether this resulted in a reduction in low-acuity ED presentations in the corresponding physical EDs. To our knowledge, few published studies have examined system-level changes in ED case-mix following implementation of a district-wide virtual care model, and our findings contribute to this emerging evidence base.

Conclusions

This study identified a significant and sustained reduction in the proportion of low-acuity presentations across our district EDs following commencement of a virtual care service. Hospital-led virtual care services may contribute to easing the burden of low-acuity presentations on ED. This approach requires further research and reporting from other virtual care services to support these findings and increase their generalisability.
