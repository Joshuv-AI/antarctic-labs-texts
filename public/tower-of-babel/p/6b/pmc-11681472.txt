Improving Dual Antiplatelet Therapy Adherence Via Implementation of a Pocket Card: A Quality Improvement Pilot Program
Authors: Carlee I. Oakley, Samantha A. Banks, Marcus V. Pinto, Deborah O. Setter, Sarah L. Clark, Zafer Keser, James P. Klaas, Andrea N. Leep Hunderfund, Rafid Mustafa
Published: 2024

Antiplatelet therapy is the mainstay of secondary stroke prevention following a transient ischemic attack or minor acute ischemic stroke. The risk of recurrent thrombotic events is highest within the first 3 months of the index event, and a short course of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) reduces the risk of recurrent stroke. 1 Because long‐term treatment with DAPT poses a higher risk of major bleeding events, many clinicians recommend transitioning to antiplatelet monotherapy after up to 90 days of DAPT. Appropriate antiplatelet medication adherence is imperative to maximize potential benefits and minimize associated risks.

Adherence to treatment recommendations requires both comprehension and buy‐in from patients, with challenges to adherence including multiple medications, complex dosing regimens, and lack of immediate consequences of missed doses. 2 A prior study found that over a quarter of survivors of stroke did not appropriately adhere to antithrombotic treatment recommendations, highlighting the need for improvement efforts. 3 Pocket card cognitive aids have been used as effective educational quality improvement tools. 4 This study aimed to improve patient education on DAPT for secondary stroke prevention at the time of hospital discharge through implementation and use of a pocket card clearly outlining the DAPT regimen and date for eventual transition to antiplatelet monotherapy.

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. This was a single‐center, prospective quality improvement study enrolling 30 consecutive adult patients admitted to an inpatient neurology service at Mayo Clinic Saint Marys Hospital between July 2022 and June 2023. Eligibility criteria included a new diagnosis of either transient ischemic attack or acute ischemic stroke treated with a combination of aspirin and clopidogrel. Aphasic and incapacitated patients were excluded. Following institutional review board approval and informed consent, patients were queried following the current standard, nursing‐led hospital discharge medication education. Comprehension was gauged using a patient teach‐back method. 5 Responses were recorded using a 5‐point scoring system with a single point assigned for each correct component of the DAPT medication regimen (drugs, dosages, and duration/date of transition). Following initial comprehension assessment, patients were reeducated on the specifics of their discharge DAPT regimen using a DAPT pocket card (Figure, Panel A). Comprehension was reassessed using the same teach‐back method and 5‐point scoring system. We also gauged medication adherence for those 30 patients at their posthospital outpatient neurology follow‐up visit 30 to 90 days following hospital discharge. No additional formal stroke education was provided in the period between hospital discharge and initial follow‐up visit. Adherence was determined via unblinded review of clinical notes within the electronic medical record and cross‐referenced with pharmacy dispensing data. Medication adherence was compared with 30 age‐ and sex‐matched controls with transient ischemic attack or acute ischemic stroke who were also discharged on DAPT from an inpatient neurology service at Saint Marys Hospital from July 2021 to June 2022 but received standard discharge education without the supplemental DAPT pocket card. All statistical analyses were completed using R v 4.0.3. We compared categorical data using Fisher's exact tests, continuous data using Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests, and ordinal data using Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests.

All outcome data are presented in the Figure [C] and [D]. Among the 30 patients who received a DAPT pocket card, the median baseline comprehension score at the time of hospital discharge was 1. Comprehension significantly improved following the DAPT pocket card education (Figure [C]), with all 30 patients earning 5 out of 5 available points on the follow‐up comprehension assessment (P<0.001).

There were no significant differences in age, sex, body mass index, stroke severity, or cardiovascular comorbidities between the cohort receiving intervention compared with the control cohort (Figure [B]). Both cohorts were predominantly White and not Hispanic or Latino (25/30 in the intervention cohort and 27/30 in the control cohort). Overall, 97% of patients who received the DAPT pocket card had appropriate antiplatelet medication adherence at their posthospital outpatient visit compared with only 73% of patients who received only standard discharge education (Figure [D]), demonstrating a higher medication adherence rate in the intervention cohort with an odds ratio of 10.55 (95% CI, 1.22–90.66, P=0.026). There were no adverse safety outcomes related to the pocket card intervention. Two patients in the control group were readmitted due to bleeding complications (hematuria and epistaxis) while on DAPT.

In this study, a pocket‐sized medication card was associated with improved patient comprehension of DAPT recommendations at the time of hospital discharge and appropriate antiplatelet adherence at posthospital follow‐up 30 to 90 days later. The pocket card is a straightforward, low‐cost intervention that empowers patients with key information during the critical transition from the inpatient to outpatient setting and may help prevent serious adverse events associated with nonadherence (eg, failing to continue antiplatelet therapy, failure to transition from DAPT to antiplatelet monotherapy). A limitation of this study is that the teach‐back method of comprehension assessment may be vulnerable to suggestion. Future directions will include incorporation of additional antithrombotic medications to the pocket card, such as the increasingly used direct oral anticoagulants.
