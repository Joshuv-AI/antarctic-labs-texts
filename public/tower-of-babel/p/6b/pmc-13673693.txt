A call for supplemental implementation strategy reporting guidelines to advance minority health: applying expanded specification and reporting recommendations to the Literacy Promotion for Latinos study
Authors: Jennifer R Hemler, Manuel E Jimenez, Benjamin F Crabtree, Alan L Mendelsohn, Katie A Devine, Shilpa Pai, Usha Ramachandran, Shawna V Hudson, Thomas I Mackie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReporting guidelines for implementation strategies are important for transparency, measurement, and replicability. Yet, recent calls within implementation science highlight that foundational research commitments to advance minority health remain underspecified in current implementation strategy reporting guidelines.MethodsIn response, our research team sought to expand Proctor and colleagues’ guidelines for reporting and specifying implementation strategies to advance minority health. We first identified and synthesized key elements for specification from relevant literature. We then applied potential supplemental reporting criteria to our Literacy Promotion for Latinos (LPL) study as a case example to iterate and refine the suggested supplemental criteria. LPL implemented text messages and streamlined access to community resources as implementation strategies to enhance uptake of Reach Out and Read, an evidence-based pediatric clinic-based literacy promotion intervention specifically among Latino families.ResultsOur suggested supplemental reporting criteria integrate elements of community-engagement and health disparities research foundational for advancing minority health. We offer a new category, (1) Prioritize It, to describe processes of defining and prioritizing the (a) health outcome gap of interest and (b) performance gap of the evidence-based intervention. We add criteria to Proctor and colleagues’ (2) Specify It category, which outlines the description of the implementation strategies, to describe processes of implementation strategy (a) selection, (b) development, and (c) tailoring. Lastly, we suggest a new category, (3) Evaluate It, to include community-engaged evaluation processes. Introduction

Decades of scientific progress have not translated into equivalent improvements in population health for all groups [1, 2]. Healthcare disparities will exist as long as certain populations receive significantly worse healthcare access, quality, or outcomes [3]. Implementation science can advance healthcare by continuing to build an understanding of the drivers of healthcare disparities and by developing and testing responsive implementation strategies. Implementation strategies are the techniques used to enhance the adoption, implementation, sustainability, and scale-up of evidence-based interventions [4]. Implementation strategies that address drivers of healthcare disparities and seek to improve the uptake of innovations into clinical care for minority populations who experience disparate access, quality, and outcomes are critical [2, 5]. To do this, calls have been made for implementation research to be infused with a “health-equity lens” from the outset [1–3, 5–13].

Proctor and colleagues reference the fundamental importance of implementation strategies in specifying “how to” change healthcare practice [14]. Implementation strategies are foundational to the efforts of implementation scientists [15] in improving the uptake of innovations in clinical care [4]. Calls to integrate a health-equity lens into implementation science set forth similar foundational principles: 1) meaningful engagement of community members at all stages of implementation research [1–3, 6, 7, 11, 12, 16–18]; 2) theories, models, and frameworks that investigate the social, structural, and systemic drivers of health and healthcare disparities [1, 3, 6, 8, 12, 19, 20]; 3) cross-sector partnerships and approaches outside of traditional healthcare settings that address complex and interdependent factors contributing to health disparities [8, 21–24]; and 4) evaluation frameworks and metrics that draw from the health disparities literature [25], build from established implementation science frameworks [20, 26], and align with community-identified priorities and outcomes [25]. While applying a health-equity lens to implementation science necessitates a focus on implementation strategies, the field has yet to integrate these calls into reporting guidelines for implementation strategies.

In this paper, we respond to these calls in the field by offering new criteria for consideration in supplementing established reporting criteria for implementation strategies. We propose an addendum to Proctor and colleagues’ reporting guidelines for implementation strategies [14] and an expansion of their mission to incorporate advancing minority health. Our suggested criteria focus attention on processes involved in understanding health disparities and designing and evaluating specific implementation strategies to address those disparities. We employ a case example from our research to demonstrate the application and utility of additional reporting on how principles to advance minority health were integrated into the design, implementation, and evaluation of implementation strategies. We propose these criteria not as the only or final criteria needed, but to propose potential criteria and, by doing so, to demonstrate the need and value for additional reporting criteria to improve transparency and responsiveness in minority health research.

Methods

Process for developing potential supplemental reporting criteria

Proctor and colleagues’ recommendations for specifying and reporting implementation strategies explicated the need for accurate and detailed reporting of domains required to enable measurement and reproducibility [14]. Our team initially employed Proctor and colleagues’ implementation strategy reporting guidelines to describe the strategies used to enhance uptake of an evidence-based intervention (EBI) in pediatric clinics. We recognized an opportunity to expand the existing reporting guidelines to provide specifications critical to advancing minority health. Supplemental criteria integrating foundational principles for advancing minority health will help promote transparency in the design and delivery of implementation strategies for this goal and aid researchers and their partners in adapting implementation strategies to their communities and contexts. We intend for these supplemental criteria to be used by researchers in “naming, defining, and operationalizing” their implementation strategies [14] and to foster conversation within the field about how to describe and enhance processes in designing implementation strategies for reducing health disparities.

Our process evaluation team (JH, TM, MJ, BC) conducted a criterial analysis of literature calling for incorporating a health-equity lens into implementation science. Two researchers (JH, TM) conducted independent traditional literature reviews [27] of the implementation science and health disparities literatures (using search terms of “health equity,” “health disparity,” “implementation science,” and “implementation strategies” in PubMed and Google Scholar databases, reviewing article references for relevant literature). We compared articles selected from the literature searches and reached consensus among the process evaluation team on the articles that were of high-quality and pertinent to incorporating a health-equity focus into the reporting of implementation strategies; we also incorporated articles suggested through peer review by an implementation science expert external to our team. We synthesized and translated recommendations from these articles into a supplemental set of reporting criteria to span implementation strategy phases, including pre-planning, design [28, 29] (the process of building the strategy that includes its selection, development, and tailoring), implementation, and evaluation. We then embedded these suggested supplemental reporting criteria within Proctor and colleagues’ original reporting guidelines and applied them to our case study, refining them over multiple rounds, to demonstrate feasibility and usability.

The health outcome gap

The health outcome gap is optimally defined and understood through research evidence and community expertise: empirical data may establish or detect a health disparity [25]; yet different types and levels of community engagement [31, 32] are necessary for understanding its manifestation in the local context and how its drivers are experienced by the community [1, 10, 25]. Articulating the processes of engaging community partners in defining and prioritizing the health outcome gap makes transparent the forms of knowledge and power involved in shaping understandings of the problem [30] and contextualizes the need for the implementation strategies [7, 10].

The performance gap

Defining the performance gap and its drivers is critical for outlining the targets of new or redesigned implementation strategies and enabling robust uptake of the intervention [3, 11]. Health disparities may persist through disparate uptake of EBIs or implementation strategies that do not address implementation disparities [1, 7, 9]. Additionally, the evidence base may be skewed toward particular populations and cultures, and intervention characteristics themselves may influence reach and retention [13]. Models, theories and frameworks offer opportunities to understand the processes by which disparate implementation may be occurring; community-based understandings illuminate resistant or untargeted barriers to the current EBI and/or uncover existing implementation disparities to be resolved through new or redesigned implementation strategies [3, 6, 25]. Those implementing and experiencing the EBI have specialized knowledge of what works and does not in the specific context and why; this knowledge, along with understandings from the research, is needed to target drivers preventing intervention uptake and sustainment [1, 3, 7, 11, 33].

Implementation strategy selection

Community members and local experts, including those implementing and receiving implementation, are ideally partners in selecting strategies to ensure that they target prioritized barriers and meet implementation outcomes. Research evidence suggests engagement in selection is the initial step in participatory processes to ensure shared power and decision-making [1, 6]. Specifying who was engaged in decision-making and at what level [31] promotes transparency and accountability [8, 10, 30].

Implementation strategy development

Experts emphasize developing implementation strategies – tailoring, pilot-testing, and refining them – through participatory or co-creation/co-design processes to address drivers of disparities in both health and implementation outcomes [1, 6, 7, 11]. Articulating processes of prioritizing experiential expertise and collaborative knowledge-building and sense-making [34, 35] can help researchers understand and plan for the pace and scope of work leading to positive implementation outcomes [2, 6, 11]. Reporting the partnerships and processes involved can also highlight the cross-sector partnerships or involvements needed to implement strategies to advance minority health [1, 6, 8].

Tailoring

Tailoring implementation strategies to context is needed to advance minority health [36, 37], particularly tailoring for cultural and linguistic appropriateness and acceptability to implementation recipients [2, 3, 6]. Above, we suggested including description of the processes involved in developing the implementation strategy, which may include how decisions were made about tailoring or personalizing strategies. Here, we suggest a pragmatic inclusion of the specifics of the tailoring (the “what”) that were implemented.

Implementation strategy evaluation: community-engaged evaluation

Proctor and colleagues’ reporting guidelines include evaluating implementation strategy outcomes for addressing implementation disparities (e.g., acceptability, reach) [2, 14, 33]. We suggest adding criteria to describe how community partners were involved in designing the ongoing and summative evaluation of implementation strategies [30] as such specifications facilitate transparency in the commitments to community partnership across research phases [6, 10, 11]. Community engagement is needed to help determine measures of successful implementation strategies [1, 10], including their impact on the prioritized health outcome gap [6] and the drivers of health outcome and performance gaps [1]. Community engagement is also critical in interpreting findings and mechanisms influencing implementation strategy effectiveness and in planning for dissemination to enhance progress toward health goals [1, 11].

Expanded guidelines for reporting implementation strategies to advance minority health

We propose “Prioritize It” as a new category for defining the (1) health outcome gap and (2) performance gap. “Prioritize It” asks researchers to describe their processes for aligning implementation research with community-defined and -prioritized health disparities and drivers. We suggest expanding Proctor’s existing “Specify It” category to include processes of (1) selection, (2) development, and (3) tailoring. We suggest adding a new “Evaluate It” category to include Proctor and colleagues’ existing specifications for determining and measuring implementation outcomes and to specify (4) community-engaged evaluation processes. For each supplemental reporting criteria, we propose implementation researchers describe the types of expertise engaged – e.g., research evidence, community knowledge, and/or local expertise – and the level of engagement that occurred – e.g., outreach (e.g., communication to inform), consultation (e.g., community feedback), involvement (e.g., cooperation, participation), collaboration (e.g., strong involvement to partnerships), or shared leadership (e.g., strong partnership structures in place) [31]. Engagement processes may change over time in relation to specific needs and phases of the research and the desires of community members [6, 32, 38].

Demonstrating application of the suggested supplemental reporting criteria: case example of the LPL study

Addressing Disparities in Language and Social-emotional Skill Acquisition through Literacy Promotion in Primary Care: Literacy Promotion for Latinos Study (LPL) is an effectiveness-implementation hybrid type 1 randomized controlled trial. LPL aims to mitigate disparities in school readiness for Latino children through enhancing performance of Reach Out and Read (ROR), an evidence-based pediatric literacy promotion intervention to encourage caregiver-child shared reading and promote early developmental and relational health [39, 40]. The context for LPL is three pediatric health centers (two Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs) and one family health center) in central New Jersey that each have over 10 years of experience implementing ROR and serve large proportions of Latino families preferring Spanish-language communication [41].

The principal investigator (PI) and research team have been committed to community-engaged research, developing important relationships with community partners over the past decade. LPL was developed through collaboration with a community leader, consultations with caregivers and local educators, and partnerships with community-based pediatricians and a community-based organization. In tandem with the research evidence, these engagements developed the conceptual model for the research and led to the selection, development, implementation, and evaluation of two implementation strategies to enhance uptake of ROR: (1) a text message campaign providing reading advice and encouragement to caregivers (hereafter, “text messages”); and (2) referrals providing streamlined access to existing community resources for parenting and socioeconomic support (hereafter, “community referrals”).

Below, we use LPL as a case example to demonstrate how our suggested supplemental reporting criteria may be applied in reporting implementation strategies. Table 3 presents the brief descriptions of our extended explanations that we would use in reporting the supplemental criteria. We present the full application of our “Expanded Guidelines for Reporting Implementation Strategies to Advance Minority Health,” which includes Proctor and colleagues’ original reporting criteria, in Additional File 1.

LPL study case example of reporting the supplemental criteria proposed in the “Expanded guidelines for reporting implementation strategies to advance minority health”

Prioritize It

LPL study priority: health outcome gap in school readiness

While gaps in school readiness throughout the United States have declined in the past 20 years, Latino children continue to experience more developmental delays than peers [42–46]. The PI, a pediatrician-researcher, formed a collaboration via an academic-community health forum with a community leader, a daycare director in the local Latino community [47]. They identified kindergarten readiness as a community priority, and caregiver-child shared reading as a key activity supporting the language and socioemotional skills acquisition needed for school readiness. This leader participated in a series of ethnographic interviews with the study team on interactions between poverty, parenting styles, reading beliefs, and literacy practices in the community. The PI also consulted with the local school district and faculty pediatricians working in this community’s FQHC. Two faculty pediatricians joined the research team as co-investigators and shared leadership (hereafter described as “clinician partners”). The community leader-daycare director and clinician partners described poverty-related stressors and low levels of parental formal education as factors influencing shared reading practices. (See Table 3, Health Outcome Gap, for reporting example.)

LPL study priority: Reach Out and Read performance gap

ROR has become a national pediatric standard of care, narrowing gaps in school readiness [51]. Middlesex County has robust ROR programs, offering (pre-COVID) literacy-rich waiting rooms, a free language- and age-appropriate book at well child visits, and anticipatory guidance to families [52]. Research evidence shows that ROR produces positive outcomes for low-income Latino communities [53, 54] but that enhancements are needed to fully address developmental disparities [48–50]. Latino families have reported receiving literacy advice more than other groups but having lower rates of reading at home [43, 44, 46, 48, 55, 56]. Clinician partners described persistent poverty-related barriers across Latino families correlating with limited shared reading. As ROR is a clinic-bound intervention, they felt limited in their abilities to influence parental reading at home. Research reporting ROR implementation gaps include that Latino families may desire or need more literacy information and instruction than ROR provides [57]. These data demonstrate that traditional office-based models are insufficient to resolve persistent health disparities [58, 59].

To better understand the context-specific ROR performance gaps, the study team and community leader-daycare director collaborated on an interview guide for caregivers [47]. Caregivers described shared reading as important, potentially preventing developmental delays and poor educational performance. They trusted and valued their pediatricians’ advice. But they described infant age and disinterest in reading and their own limited reading skills and enjoyment as barriers [60, 61]. Many caregivers reported intending to read with their children but being overwhelmed by financial hardships and household responsibilities. Instead, they reported enacting non-reading literacy promoting and socio-emotional skills-building activities with their children, like singing or storytelling. Evidence from caregivers, clinician partners, and research coalesced around key implementation strategy targets: child development and reading beliefs, intent-to-behavior gaps, and material hardship and poverty-related stress. (See Table 3, Performance Gap, for reporting example.)

Specify It

LPL study implementation strategy selection

Implementation strategy 1: text messages

Discussions with the community leader-daycare director and clinician partners over several months led to the selection of text messaging to target intent-to-behavior barriers to ROR uptake and child development and reading beliefs. Text-based behavioral interventions emerging in recent years showed promise in educational and clinic spaces [62]: one study linked shared reading and text follow-up messages to increased shared reading and improved child outcomes even among caregivers with limited literacy [63]; another found text messaging encouraged positive caregiver-child interactions [64]. As a modality, the community leader-daycare director and clinician partners concurred that text messaging could provide useful reminders or cues to families at home, encourage routine-building, and provide encouragement, advice, and education. Importantly, they noted that the families they served relied on text messages as a form of communication, even if they did not own smart phones. (See Table 3, Implementation Strategy Selection, for reporting example.)

Implementation strategy 2: community referrals

Community referrals was selected as an implementation strategy through an iterative process that brought together the team’s knowledge of conceptual models and existing research with partners’ experiences and caregiver perspectives. During pilot study interviews, caregivers described a reduced capacity for shared reading due to material hardships and the impact of related stress on family routines. These findings were consistent with extensive work that links material hardship to parenting and child language and social-emotional outcomes [65–67]. Research indicates that poverty-mitigating resources can enhance child developmental outcomes, but that burdensome administrative processes discourage or prevent families from taking advantage of programs [68, 69]. Clinician partners reported that social determinants of health screenings amongst their patients and families elicited need for financial support for food, housing, utilities, and transportation. These insights converged on aligning primary care literacy promotion with existing community-based efforts that address poverty-related and parenting stressors.

Around this time, New Jersey implemented a statewide program based on the national Help Me Grow [70] model to work collaboratively with families in assessing needs and linking families to community resources. The PI partnered with the Middlesex County community-based organization, Central Jersey Family Health Consortium, providing this service, Connecting NJ. This organization had extensive experience serving Spanish-speaking Latino families across the county, employing well-trained bilingual specialists who performed outreach to families by phone. Connecting NJ maintains an updated database of economic and parenting resources and provides case management for families who need additional support. Bolstering support for this implementation strategy, other research using similar strategies increased the number of services families were enrolled in [71]. (See Table 3, Implementation Strategy Selection, for reporting example.)

LPL study implementation strategy development

Implementation strategy 1: text messages

After selecting text messages as a strategy, our study team consulted clinician partners and an early childhood specialist from Rutgers University to develop content to address caregiver-reported barriers. The team wanted to build upon caregivers’ existing literacy practices and pediatricians’ advice, as caregivers identified these drivers to ROR uptake. The community leader-daycare director connected the team to caregivers for three focus groups about text message content, frequency, timing, and engagement. The team contracted with university-approved vendors to establish an automated service in Spanish and English to deliver mono- and bi-directional text messages to families, as caregivers thought responding to text questions would encourage engagement. The team user-tested the text campaign with an additional group of caregivers from the local FQHC as part of a pilot study [72]. Pilot study interviews with caregivers indicated that they found the texts highly acceptable and appreciated feeling supported by the clinic; however, testing revealed the texts alone were insufficient to help all families overcome competing priorities and hardship, reinforcing the need for the second implementation strategy. (See Table 3, Implementation Strategy Development, for reporting example.)

Implementation strategy 2: community resources

The study team and Connecting NJ met frequently during the pre-implementation period to plan processes for connecting study participants to Connecting NJ’s specialists for collaborative needs assessments and streamlined access to community resources. Connecting NJ had a robust process and proven track record for engaging community members and providing support, collaborating with community-based organizations and services. LPL incorporated Connecting NJ’s existing processes into the study and collaborated on processes for study needs outside their usual protocols, such as performing additional outreach nine months into the study to follow-up with participants on their use of referrals and new or persistent needs. We signed data sharing agreements to protect participant information and collaborated on language to clearly explain Connecting NJ and their role in LPL to participants. Our teams met regularly at the beginning of the intervention to problem-solve and improve processes. Once established, our teams continued meeting monthly as part of our process evaluation of the implementation strategy. (See Table 3, Implementation Strategy Development, for reporting example.)

LPL study implementation strategy implementation: Tailoring

Implementation strategy 1: text messages

During enrollment, study staff asked caregivers about their preferences for receiving study communications and text messages: language (Spanish or English), best time of day to prompt for shared reading, personalization strategies (including the child’s name and gender, and celebratory texts). (See Table 3, Implementation Strategy Implementation, for reporting example.)

Implementation strategy 2: community resources

Connecting NJ outreach was already intensely personalized: specialists did not use explicit scripts or surveys but engaged caregivers in conversations to establish rich connections and determine needs. Specialists initiated conversations in the caregiver’s preferred language and provided resources families specified they wanted. (See Table 3, Implementation Strategy Implementation, for reporting example.)

Evaluate It

LPL engaged community partners and advisors at multiple levels in prioritizing and defining research aims, outcomes and data collection instruments, and process and outcome evaluations. As described above, early collaborations with community leaders and members defined and prioritized the health outcome and performance gaps and contributed to selecting and developing implementation strategies. The study team convened an advisory board of 15 experts – from ROR and Help Me Grow national programs and state organizations and professional associations such as the Department of Children and Families and the New Jersey chapter of the American Academy of Pediatrics – to help translate these priorities and early findings into conceptual models for research and evaluation design. The board met annually throughout the study to provide input on study phases and emergent process evaluation findings. The PI sought counsel from advisory members throughout the project.

Additionally, clinician co-investigator partners and Connecting NJ attended study team meetings to refine primary and secondary outcomes and develop outcome assessments and process evaluation tools, which were pilot-tested with community members for acceptability. Partners from all three pediatric clinics and Connecting NJ participated in independent monthly meetings across implementation to assess barriers and adjust implementation and data collection strategies as needed. Additionally, the study team met quarterly with the three pediatric clinics to seek input and feedback. We describe specific engagement processes for each implementation strategy below.

LPL study community-engaged evaluation

Implementation strategy #1: text messages

Evaluation of the text messaging as an implementation strategy to support uptake of ROR was planned through pre-implementation meetings with the study team and with input from the advisory board. The text messages were originally assessed during a pilot study and refined for LPL. Implementation of the text messages was automated; staff monitored that texts were delivered and received, and addressed participant questions, but this implementation strategy did not require ongoing evaluation during implementation. Evaluation of the effectiveness of receipt of text messages on primary and secondary outcomes and process evaluation interviews with study participants were designed through team meetings and consultations with our study partners and advisory board. We recently concluded data collection and will be engaging ROR and clinician partners in analyzing the effectiveness of text messaging on enhancing ROR’s impact on study outcomes and assessing potential for implementing text messaging in other ROR clinics. (See Table 3, Community-engaged Evaluation, for reporting example.)

Implementation strategy #2: community referrals

Connecting NJ collaborated with the study team to design data collection and evaluation of this implementation strategy. Program managers joined the research team early in the project to plan data collection and data sharing. After embarking on implementation, Connecting NJ specialists and manager and the LPL study coordinator met monthly as part of our process evaluation to discuss implementation and data collection. Consistent engagement helped LPL and Connecting NJ articulate trends in community resource referrals and use and potential factors to include in outcome analyses. Connecting NJ informed questions for post-intervention interviews with study participants about their participation experiences. We plan to engage Connecting NJ in interpreting process evaluation findings and outcome analyses and to plan future work to strengthen connections between Connecting NJ and pediatric health centers to bridge Help Me Grow and ROR models. (See Table 3, Community-engaged Evaluation, for reporting example.)

Discussion

Proctor and colleagues originally recommended reporting and specifying implementation strategies to enable precision for measurement and “reproducibility” of studies on implementation strategies [14]. Our team makes a call for the field to extend these goals to include transparency in specifying implementation strategies to advance minority health. Our team believes the application of the proposed supplemental reporting criteria to our work demonstrates how greater specificity on implementation strategy design, implementation, and evaluation illuminates commitments to advance minority health. The specification of alignment to principles for advancing minority health also offers the opportunity for reflexive practice by both the research team and community partners engaged.

First, work to advance minority health requires a foundational commitment to community partnership at every stage of implementation strategy development and evaluation [7, 10]. Our application of the supplemental criteria provides transparency on whether and how different community partners were engaged in prioritizing, designing, and evaluating the implementation strategies proposed and to what extent. Notably, we did not engage all partners uniformly throughout the project and engagement strategies varied at different stages. The specification of the health outcome and performance gaps, for which we prioritized local community partnerships and community member feedback, provide the detail needed to understand both the targeted health outcome and the healthcare disparity underlying it [7, 10]. Given that the drivers of health inequities are not monolithic, this specification provides information critical in considering the reproducibility of findings to other contexts [3, 6, 25]. For our evaluation, we prioritized engaging regional and national experts in translating community-defined outcomes into validated measurement instruments and qualitative tools. In describing our community-engaged evaluation processes transparently, we demonstrate our commitments to engage local community experts in the research process and opportunities to extend these commitments in the future.

Reporting guidelines for implementation strategies to advance minority health should also describe how they address social, structural, and systemic drivers of the healthcare disparity [1]. The implementation science field offers a growing number of health-equity-focused determinants frameworks [19] (e.g., Health Equity Implementation Framework [3, 12]; Health Disparities Research Framework [25]), process frameworks (e.g., Transcreation [73]), and evaluation frameworks (e.g., RE-AIM to Enhance Sustainability [20]) that help to identify opportunities for redressing health disparities through patient-, clinician-, organizational, and/or policy-level interventions [30]. Our suggested reporting criteria fit within and supplement these frameworks by seeking specifications for how these efforts will be measured and aligned to priorities and outcomes critical to key community partners [25].

Additionally, the complex and interdependent factors contributing to health disparities often require implementation strategies that leverage cross-sector partnerships and approaches outside of traditional healthcare settings [21]. As illustrated in our case example, such efforts are likely to be multi-sectoral and dynamic over the process of pre-planning, design (e.g., selection and development), implementation, and evaluation. Linkages to other sectors can foster synergy and may be necessary to address the key drivers and fundamental causes of healthcare and health disparities [22]. For example, community-based settings (e.g., faith-based organizations, school systems) may facilitate opportunities to expand reach to communities experiencing disparate healthcare access, quality or outcomes. Community-based interventions may address well-documented drivers of health disparities (e.g., trust, geography, affordability) [1, 22, 23]. Acknowledgement of the complex set of interdependent factors also warrants careful documentation and reporting of the “policy ecology” that may support or impede the success of the respective implementation strategies [8, 24].

Lastly, there is no “one size fits all” approach to advancing minority health. Tailoring to address the heterogeneity across and within communities is a critical part of responding to the unique needs of individuals with intersectional identities [13, 36, 37]. Accordingly, we identify the importance of adding criteria that specify how the implementation strategy is tailored to ensure cultural and linguistic appropriateness and acceptability to implementation recipients [2, 3, 6]. Our team therefore recommends the addition of a criterion that makes explicit how the implementation strategy is tailored to accommodate the context and specific needs of the populations served. Such efforts hold implications to enable measurement of the implementation strategy and reproducibility to other contexts.

Our goal in this work is two-fold. First, we hope to provide investigators with a framework for facilitating specification of implementation strategies to advance minority health. As illustrated in our application of these principles and further elucidated in the discussion, we believe consideration for the supplement criteria can be helpful to make explicit commitments critical to the planning and implementation of implementation strategies to advance minority health. Second, we make a call for the field to generate a consensus statement that would offer supplemental reporting criteria for implementation strategies that seek to advance minority health. The next step is to leverage convenings at relevant professional meetings (e.g., Society for Implementation Research Collaboration, AcademyHealth Annual Conference on the Science of Dissemination and Implementation) to bring together a community of investigators and community partners interested in developing a consensus statement with supplemental reporting criteria. Additional consensus building, ideally with community partner input, will be critical to identifying a shared understanding of how best to represent our commitments to advance minority health in specification and reporting of implementation strategies.

Limitations

Our goal is to integrate principles central to advancing minority health into the reporting of implementation strategies. Our paper does not purport to offer a comprehensive accounting of the principles relevant to addressing disparities. Rather, we propose potential reporting criteria and illustrate their application, showing the value of integrating these principles into the reporting and specification of implementation strategies. Our use of one illustrative example provides the strength of an in-depth application while holding the limitation of narrowing the focus to a single type of implementation. Opportunities exist to extend the applications to a broader array of relevant disparity-focused implementation strategies, including multi- and policy-level strategies [24]. Expansion in application could assist in facilitating alignment to determinants frameworks, such as Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, Sustainment (EPIS) [74–76]. Opportunities exist to conduct a scoping review of protocol papers for implementation strategies that are focused on advancing minority health, employing and potentially further refining the expanded guidelines offered in this article. Such an endeavor would help to revise and refine the specifications and facilitate the applicability of these guidelines across various types of participant-, provider-, multi- and policy-level implementation strategies.

Conclusion

Reporting guidelines for implementation strategies are important for transparency, measurement, and replicability. Our supplemental criteria for reporting on implementation strategies to advance minority health seek to make explicit key principles and commitments to advance minority health. These suggested supplemental reporting criteria may be useful for investigators developing implementation strategies that seek to advance minority health. The supplemental reporting criteria also hold promise in promoting transparency in research commitments to advance minority health. The field requires a consensus statement to provide clear reporting guidelines and specifications that demonstrate how commitments to advance minority health are actualized at every stage of the implementation process.
