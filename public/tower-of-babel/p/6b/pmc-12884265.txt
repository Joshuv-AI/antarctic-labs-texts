The Gut Microbiome and Butyrate Differentiate Clostridioides difficile Colonization and Infection in Children
Authors: Maribeth R Nicholson, Siyuan Ma, Britton A Strickland, Mia Cecala, Lisa Zhang, Seth Reasoner, Emma R Guiberson, Matthew J Munneke, Meghan H Shilts, Eric P Skaar, Suman R Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSymptomatic Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) can cause significant morbidity and mortality. Conversely, patients can be colonized with toxigenic C. difficile in the absence of symptoms, termed asymptomatic colonization. We previously demonstrated that the presence and function of C. difficile toxins do not differentiate between asymptomatic colonization and CDI in children, suggesting the influence of other factors. This study aimed to interrogate the intestinal microbiome and butyrate in stool samples from children with CDI and asymptomatic colonization.MethodsDesign: case-control study. Setting: tertiary care children's hospital. Participants and measures: asymptomatic children had stool tested for C. difficile by nucleic-acid amplification-based testing (NAAT) and were considered colonized if positive (n = 50). Residual stool was also obtained from symptomatic children who tested positive for C. difficile by NAAT (n = 55). The microbiome was assessed via 16S rRNA sequencing and butyrate via liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry.ResultsCompared with clinical covariates and comorbidities, C. difficile symptom status (ie, asymptomatic colonization vs symptomatic CDI) demonstrated the strongest differential abundance association on gut microbes. Symptomatic CDI was associated with increased abundance of Escherichia/Shigella (Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted q = 3.94 × 10−5), Haemophilus (q = 0.022), and Gemella (q = 0.085), and depleted abundance of gut commensals such as Faecalibacterium (q = 0.041), Blautia (q = 0.041), and Bifidobacterium (q = 0.063). We also observed depletion in the abundance of microbial butyrate producers and fecal butyrate in participants with symptomatic CDI versus asymptomatic colonization.ConclusionsThe gut microbiota and butyrate differ between participants with asymptomatic C. difficile colonization and symptomatic CDI, suggesting their potential role in symptom development.

Clostridioides difficile is a gastrointestinal pathogen associated with substantial morbidity and mortality [1]. Alternatively, due to unclear mechanisms, C. difficile can colonize the intestinal tract without causing symptoms, referred to as asymptomatic colonization. Children with the highest rates of asymptomatic colonization include those with cancer, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and cystic fibrosis (CF), who are also at risk for diarrhea from alternative causes [2–4]. No testing algorithm has reliably differentiated symptomatic C. difficile infection (CDI) from those with asymptomatic colonization with diarrhea from an alternative source. Although C. difficile toxins are classically used in diagnostic testing algorithms, our previous work demonstrated that they were unable to differentiate symptomatic CDI from asymptomatic colonization in a pediatric cohort [5]. In addition, the same C. difficile strains and bacterial burden associated with asymptomatic colonization in some patients can be associated with severe disease in others [6]. This, therefore, highlights an important question; if the presence of C. difficile and toxins are similar between those with asymptomatic colonization and symptomatic disease, what then differs? This question is essential for 2 reasons: (1) to improve diagnostic testing and (2) to understand protective mechanisms that influence C. difficile pathophysiology.

Despite pathogenesis tightly linked to the intestinal microbiome [7], little data have been published on alterations in the intestinal microbiome and associated metabolites in pediatric patients with CDI versus asymptomatic colonization. Previously published studies have been limited by small sample sizes [8, 9] or performed exclusively in adults [10, 11].

Using a well-characterized cohort of pediatric patients, we compared the intestinal microbiome and butyrate levels in children with symptomatic CDI and asymptomatic colonization to better understand the pathophysiology of this important bacterium.

METHODS

Study Design

Pediatric participants, ages 1 to 18 years, were prospectively enrolled from July 2017 through December 2019 at Monroe Carell Jr. Children's Hospital at Vanderbilt (MCJCHV) after informed parental consent and patient assent. The Vanderbilt Institutional Review Board approved the study. Data were kept confidential using a REDCap database (REDCap software, Vanderbilt University) [12].

Asymptomatic Colonized Cohort

Asymptomatic children with a diagnosis of cancer, CF, or IBD were eligible for enrollment. To be considered asymptomatic, participants had to be without diarrhea (Bristol stool type 6 or 7) [13] and not undergoing C. difficile testing or treatment. A stool sample was obtained, and an aliquot was tested for C. difficile using nucleic-acid amplification-based testing (NAAT) in the clinical laboratory. If NAAT was positive, the child was considered colonized.

Symptomatic Cohort

Symptomatic children with diarrhea (≥3 unformed stools in 24 hours and an acute change in symptoms) who underwent clinical laboratory testing and tested positive for C. difficile by NAAT were enrolled. This included previously healthy children and those with additional comorbidities. After consent, residual stool from the clinical laboratory was collected and stored in the research laboratory. Sample Processing and Clinical Testing

Stools were refrigerated after collection and stored at −80°C after aliquoting. NAAT was performed in the hospital clinical laboratory for both asymptomatic and symptomatic participants using Illumigene C. difficile assay (ARUP laboratories), a polymerase chain reaction test to detect the C. difficile gene tcdB encoding Toxin B (sensitivity 90% and specificity 96%) [14, 15]. Toxin presence was determined in the research laboratory in duplicate with enzyme-linked immunoassay testing using Premier® Toxins A and B from Meridian Biosciences (Sensitivity 80.8%, Specificity 97.5%) [14, 16]. Toxin function was determined by Vero cell rounding, performed as previously described [5].

DNA Extraction and Microbiome Sequencing

Genomic DNA extraction (Qiagen DNeasy PowerSoil Kit), targeted PCR (V4 region of 16S rRNA gene [17]), and paired-end sequencing (Illumina MiSeq 2 × 250 bp) was performed as previously described [18].

rRNA Data Processing

16S rRNA sequences grouped into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and assigned taxonomy using the dada2 pipeline (available at: https://benjjneb.github.io/dada2/tutorial.html, last accessed September, 2020) [19] and the SILVA reference database [20]. Suspected contaminants in the negative controls were removed using the R package decontam [21]. The R package phyloseq [22] was also used to facilitate data processing.

Additional Control Cohort for Microbiome Analysis

The 16 seconds rRNA sequencing data from fecal samples from a population of healthy control children were previously published and available for this study (Table 1) [23]. The HCs were also recruited from MCJCHV, the same geographical location as those with asymptomatic colonization and CDI.

Statistical Analysis

Most statistical analyses were conducted under the open-source R computing environment. Alpha- and beta-diversity metrics were calculated with the R package vegan [24] at the ASV level. Difference associations in alpha-diversity metrics are based on nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis tests. Significant association between beta-diversity (Bray–Curtis) was assessed using PERMANOVA with 99 999 permutations. Significant associations between metadata and bacterial taxa were evaluated at the phylum, genus, and ASV levels. These were based on microbiome multivariable linear regression as implemented in the R package MaAsLin2 [25]. We primarily adjusted for covariates including age, sex, comorbidities, and exposure history (antibiotics, acid blockers, hospitalization, and surgery), but conducted additional sensitivity analyses where (1) we stratified by comorbidity groups to ensure that the associations were consistent, and (2) we adjusted for different subsets of covariates, including both unadjusted analyses and analyses adjusting for only age, sex, and comorbidity, again to ensure that findings were consistent. Multiple testing across different microbial taxa were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Findings were filtered to remove microbes present in <5 samples.

Associations were considered significant if the P value or q value (as appropriate) was <.05 and <.10, respectively.

Butyrate Measurements by Liquid Chromatography-mass Spectrometry

Extracted butyrate from fecal samples were derivatized using dansylhydrazine and then analyzed using targeted reverse phase liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry. Instrument and extraction method details are in supplemental methods. Quantitation was based on single reaction monitoring detection of butyrate dansylated analogs: butyrate, m/z 336 → 171, CE 30; butyrate -d5 m/z 341 → 170, CE 30. Data analysis was conducted using Thermo-Finnigan Xcaliber and quantitation levels determined using a calibration curve. Samples were normalized against an internal standard (butyrate-d5) and by fecal weight for final pmol/mg values. Statistical analyses were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests through GraphPad Prism.

RESULTS

Study Population

The study population included 50 pediatric participants with asymptomatic C. difficile colonization and 55 with symptomatic CDI (Table 1). The median age of the cohort was 10 years (interquartile range (IQR) 5–15), and 55% were male. The asymptomatic colonized cohort included 32 (64%) children with CF, 8 (16%) with IBD, and 10 (20%) with cancer. Patients with asymptomatic colonization and symptomatic CDI were compared (Table 2). Symptomatic children were older (median 13 [IQR 5–16] vs 9 [4–12] years, P = .025) and had a different comorbidity profile based on enrollment strategy (P < .001). Asymptomatic colonized children were more likely to report acid blocker use in the 30 days before enrollment (76% vs 42%, P < .001). Antibiotic use between cohorts was similar (54% vs 67%, P = .23). C-reactive protein (CRP) values were higher in the symptomatic cohort (median 9.5 [IQR 3.4–35.8] vs 2.6 [IQR 0.2–7.4], P = .047), but the remainder of laboratory values were not significantly different.

ELISA and Vero Cell Rounding Results do not Correspond to Symptom Status

ELISA and Vero cell rounding were used to assess C. difficile toxin presence and function. A portion of this cohort was previously published [5]. In symptomatic cases, 60.7% of the samples were positive for Toxin A/B ELISA versus 39.3% of those with asymptomatic colonization (P = .32) (Supplementary Figure 1A). In addition, 53% of symptomatic patients tested positive for Vero cell rounding versus 51% of those with asymptomatic colonization (P = .88) (Supplementary Figure 1A). These results indicate that toxin-based tests cannot differentiate between symptomatic CDI and asymptomatic colonized pediatric patients (hereby referred to as C. difficile symptom status). Both positive ELISA and Vero cell rounding were associated with an increased abundance of C. difficile (Supplementary Figure 1B and 1C), regardless of symptom status. Analysis of richness and alpha diversity did not differ between samples testing positive and negative for ELISA and Vero cell rounding (Supplementary Figure 1D).

Comparison of Gut Microbiome Between Asymptomatic C. difficile Colonized and Symptomatic Patients and a Cohort of Healthy Pediatric Controls

Microbiome profiling was performed using 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing of the V4 gene region, with a sample read cutoff of >1000/sample and the lowest library size of 1533 reads and 90.7% samples >10 000 reads.

We observed substantial differences in overall microbiome community diversity when comparing cohorts by C. difficile symptom status (Figure 1; Supplementary Figure 2). Asymptomatic colonized patients had a higher alpha diversity/evenness based on Shannon (Mann–Whitney U P = .02352) and Simpson (Mann–Whitney U P = .00515) indices (Figure 1A). Beta diversity at the ASV level showed a significant separation of community composition by symptom status (Figure 1B; PERMANOVA P < 10−5). Differences in microbiome composition are directly visualized with a stacked bar plot of dominant gut microbiome genera in Supplementary Figure 2. Additionally, beta-diversity analysis for covariates in this study revealed the impact of various exposures. Specifically, subjects’ gut microbiome significantly differentiate with respect to medication history (antibiotics and acid blocker use), clinical intervention (hospitalization and surgery), and comorbidity (PERMANOVA P < .1 for all), but not with age or sex, suggesting the importance of adjusting for their potential confounding effects (Supplementary Figure 3).

Individual microbial taxa were strongly associated with C. difficile symptom status. The gut microbiome of patients with symptomatic CDI was marked by an expansion of dysbiotic microbes and a depletion of commensals (Figure 2A; Supplementary Figure 4). Compared with all the adjusted covariates, C. difficile symptom status demonstrated the strongest differential abundance association on gut microbes (determined with Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted q < 0.1), with 6 significantly associated genus-level microbes (Figure 2B; Supplementary Figure 4 and Supplementary Table 1). Symptomatic CDI was associated with increased abundance of Escherichia/Shigella (Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted q = 3.94 × 10−5), Haemophilus (q = 0.022), and Gemella (q = 0.085) and depleted abundance of gut commensals including Faecalibacterium (q = 0.041), Blautia (q = 0.041), and Bifidobacterium (q = 0.063). These associations were confirmed at the ASV level (representing species or sub-species level resolution), whereby members of the identified genera (Hemophilus, Escherichia/Shigella, Blautia, and Bifidobacterium) demonstrated similar associations. At the phylum level, patients with symptomatic CDI had a significant expansion in Proteobacteria gut microbes and depletion of Firmicutes. Some gut microbes were significantly associated with exposure history, including depletion of an unclassified member of Lachnospiraceae with antibiotic use (q = 0.057) and depletion of Epulopiscium with prior hospitalization (q = 0.081). Our findings indicate that the gut microbiome significantly differs between asymptomatic colonized patients versus those with symptomatic CDI, after adjusting for demographics, exposures, and comorbidities.

These shifts in gut microbiome compositions from asymptomatic-to-symptomatic patients represent an extension of microbial dysbiosis when further compared against the baseline of healthy control microbiome (Figure 3). As a reference for normal gut microbial states, we further included a population of healthy control subjects of similar age and sex profiles (Supplementary Table 2). When examined together with C. difficile colonized patients, symptomatic subjects displayed a further shift away from normal control subjects in terms of beta-diversity ordination, compared with asymptomatic subjects (Figure 3A). Similar “control-asymptomatic-symptomatic” trends are observed in the abundance of individual gut microbial genera (Figure 3B). Across microbial genera that are differentially abundant between asymptomatic and symptomatic patients as identified above, their abundance in asymptomatic patients was more similar to control subjects compared with symptomatic patients. Therefore, our findings indicate an exacerbation of microbial dysbiosis in symptomatic CDI compared with asymptomatic C. difficile colonization, when both are contrasted against a healthy gut microbiome state.

Lastly, to ensure that the observed associations were not due to confounding by patient comorbidities, we conducted a detailed secondary analysis involving specific patient cohorts. We analyzed the differentially abundant effects of microbes separately in participants with IBD and cancer, which were represented in both symptomatic CDI and asymptomatic colonization cohorts (Figure 2; Supplementary Table 3). Again, we performed multilinear regression by adjusting for demographic and exposure variables. The microbes had consistent, differentially abundant effects in both patient cohorts. Additional sensitivity analysis revealed that the association between gut microbes and symptom status was also consistent when different covariates were adjusted (Supplementary Figure 5 and Supplementary Table 4). Specifically, we compared findings from unadjusted microbiome-symptom associations (no covariates included), associations adjusting only for age, sex, and comorbidity, and fully adjusted (including all covariates; as visualized in Supplementary Figure 3), which yielded highly consistent results, supporting that the association of gut microbes with symptom status was not sensitive to patient comorbidities or exposures. These analyses thus solidified that our observed associations were likely driven by symptom status and not confounding factors such as comorbidity profiles.

Butyrate and Butyrate-producing Microbes are Potential Mitigators of CDI

It has been reported that butyrate and butyrate-producing gut microbes are significantly depleted in CDI patients [26, 27], and indeed may function as therapeutics to mitigate CDI. We, therefore, investigated differences in butyrate-producing organisms and butyrate by symptom status.

We observed strong depletion in both the abundance of microbial butyrate producers and butyrate concentration in symptomatic patients in our cohort (Figure 4). Given major butyrate producers as compiled by Singh et al. [28], we observed that their total abundance was significantly depleted in symptomatic patients, adjusting again for demographics, exposures, and comorbidities (Figure 4A). Further investigation of individual butyrate-producing microbes at both the genus and ASV level revealed that most demonstrated a negative association with symptomatic CDI (Figure 4B), although only Blautia (q = 0.041) and Faecalibacterium (q = 0.041; Supplementary Table 5), were statistically significant, potentially limited by sample size. Compared with control subjects, butyrate-producing microbes generally had depleted abundances, with symptomatic participants often presenting exacerbated depletion compared with asymptomatic (Supplementary Figure 6). The decreased abundance of butyrate-producing microbes in CDI was mirrored by the depletion of butyrate in the symptomatic CDI samples based on LC-MS (Figure 4C). Due to the potential differences in water content among samples, a subset underwent lyophilization, and LC-MS was repeated. The trend was maintained, with asymptomatic colonized participants harboring ∼10-fold higher butyrate concentrations than participants with symptomatic CDI (Supplementary Figure 7). Our results support previous findings on the protective effect of butyrate in CDI.

DISCUSSION

CDI is a threat to public health and is associated with significant morbidity and mortality [29]. Although a toxin-mediated disease, there are reports of patients with a high abundance of both C. difficile and its toxins who remain without clinical symptoms, deemed asymptomatic colonization [30]. It is well established that these patients may serve as an infectious vector and create diagnostic challenges if they develop diarrhea from an alternative cause [2, 6]. However, they may also provide insights into factors that protect against the development of symptomatic CDI and warrant targeted study.

Our prior work has evaluated the role of toxins in pediatric patients with asymptomatic colonization compared with those with CDI. Toxins are a necessary component of symptomatic CDI because they damage the colonic epithelium, resulting in fluid secretion and diarrhea. Contrary to the association of toxins with inflammation, our prior cohort of asymptomatic colonized pediatric patients demonstrated similar toxin presence and activity compared with those with symptomatic CDI [31]. Similarly, in this study that expands upon the prior cohort, toxin presence and activity could not differentiate symptom status. There were also no differences in alpha-diversity based on toxin ELISA or Vero cell rounding. Although toxin ELISA and Vero cell positivity reflected the increased abundance of C. difficile in the sample, prior studies have demonstrated that asymptomatic colonized patients can have a high relative abundance of C. difficile present yet remain asymptomatic [32], again suggesting the role of non-C. difficile related factors that impact symptom status.

Unlike our study of C. difficile toxins, we identified significant differences in the intestinal microbiome and butyrate that effectively differentiated the 2 conditions. The results from our study suggest that the intestinal microbiome may play a critical role in symptom development.

In our cohort, participants with asymptomatic colonization had a higher relative abundance of common gut commensals such as Faecalibacterium (q = 0.041), Blautia (q = 0.041), and Bifidobacterium (q = 0.063) than those with symptomatic disease. Patients with symptomatic CDI had a higher relative abundance of Proteobacteria phyla and Escherichia/Shigella genera. Crobach et al. previously evaluated differences in adult patients with CDI, those with asymptomatic colonization, and healthy controls and reported that patients with CDI had higher relative abundance of Bacteroides and Veillonella and lower abundance of genera belonging to the Ruminococcaceae family and Actinobacteria phylum. Although this study did not report metabolites, the manuscript noted that many lower-abundance genera in those with CDI were known short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producers, including butyrate producers. Zhang et al. also compared gut microbiota between 8 adult patients with CDI and 8 with asymptomatic colonization [8]. Although smaller numbers than our study, they confirmed similar findings with the gut microbiota of patients with CDI consisting of a greater proportion of Proteobacteria and a smaller proportion of Bacteriodetes and Firmicutes than those with asymptomatic colonization. They also demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of Escherichia/Shigella in CDI patients versus asymptomatic carriers (23.9% vs 8.7%, P < .05), as demonstrated in our pediatric cohort. Zhoe et al. performed a longitudinal study of adult ICU patients with C. difficile colonization and noted an increase in the relative abundance of SCFA and lactic acid-producing bacteria when patients transitioned from C. difficile negative to C. difficile positive. The authors hypothesized that the intestinal microbiota may react to C. difficile colonization by eliciting potentially protective responses such as an increased abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria.

Importantly, the trends in our gut microbiome dataset were maintained even when analyzed within specific patient cohorts such as IBD or cancer. In fact, symptom status was the most significant driver of the intestinal microbiome even when considering various patient factors including medications, comorbidities, age, and sex. Microbial associations with symptom status were also not sensitive to which patient factors were adjusted for in the analyses. These together suggest that the intestinal microbiome is strongly and consistently associated with C. difficile symptom status, even among patients with different comorbidities.

Like the Crobach et al. study, phyla, families, and genera classically associated with butyrate production had higher relative abundance in children with asymptomatic colonization in our study. Concordantly, butyrate was higher in stool samples from children with asymptomatic colonization than those with symptomatic disease. SCFAs, including butyrate, are the major metabolic product of fiber metabolism and have demonstrated an important role in intestinal inflammation [9]. Butyrate can decrease intestinal permeability and enhance colonic defense barriers by increasing mucin production and antimicrobial peptide levels, thus preventing the development of infection [33]. Therefore, depletion of butyrate-producing bacteria could lead to epithelial dysfunction and a higher osmotic load in the intestinal lumen, leading to the diarrhea seen in CDI [8]. The role of butyrate has been studied in a variety of animal and in vitro model systems. Fachi et al. demonstrated that butyrate supplementation could protect against CDI in mice, due to a direct effect on intestinal epithelial cells [26]. Specifically, butyrate increased the resistance of intestinal cells to C. difficile toxins by increasing and stabilizing hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α). HIF-1α reduces intestinal epithelial damage and improves the immune response; both relevant factors to attenuate the intestinal inflammation characteristic of CDI. Notably, although butyrate has been shown to inhibit the growth of diverse C. difficile strains in vitro [27], it may also induce sporulation and toxin release [34, 35], suggesting the need to better understand the highly complex role of butyrate in CDI, particularly if considered as a potential therapeutic.

Of note, other studies have demonstrated alternative metabolic pathways that may be relevant in C. difficile colonization and CDI. Fishbein et al. demonstrated that asymptomatically colonized patients’ microbiomes were enriched in Clostridia species and had altered carbohydrate metabolism [36]. However, the asymptomatic cohort in this study was defined differently than in our study and included adult patients with diarrhea who tested negative for toxin by EIA. As previously demonstrated, the presence or absence of toxins does not adequately differentiate colonization from disease in our pediatric cohort. The Fishbein et al. study may be evaluating a slightly different clinical scenario, ie, comparing patients who are symptomatic but toxin-negative versus symptomatic and toxin-positive, which would explain the discrepancies in results between our studies. There remain challenges to best diagnose asymptomatic colonization. However, our definition, the detection of C. difficile in the absence of diarrhea, is commonly used in the field [37–39].

Our study has some limitations. Patient samples from a single pediatric center may not represent the larger population. Also, although we used strict clinical criteria to define CDI, patients with additional comorbidities have a wide range of potential etiologies of diarrhea and, thus, our cohorts are at risk of misclassification bias, with patients with C. difficile colonization defined as CDI and vice versa. There may also be risks for residual confounding, given our different recruited comorbidities (and, by extension, comorbidity-related factors such as medication) in asymptomatic and symptomatic patients. We consider such concerns likely to be secondary, because (1) the single-center patient recruitment lends to an otherwise homogeneous population, (2) our stratified analyses demonstrate similar, robust findings between different comorbidities, (3) confounding by differing interventions, though difficult to fully account for [40], is alleviated given adjustment over exposure history and related sensitivity analysis findings, and (4) the observed asymptomatic-to-symptomatic differences further agree with the shift from normal to C. difficile-colonized, dysbiotic gut microbial states, and thus unlikely to be driven purely by technical confounding. Lastly, analyses of the presence and function of the gut microbiota was done via 16S rRNA sequencing and measurement of a single stool metabolite, butyrate. The intestinal microbiome's function can be more fully captured through metagenomics and metabolomics, which should be addressed through future studies.

In conclusion, through this study comparing the intestinal microbiome and butyrate of children with asymptomatic colonization versus CDI, we identify that the microbiome, and not toxin, better differentiates between these 2 conditions. These data also suggest that the presence of butyrate may serve a protective role in the development of symptomatic CDI, although, as this study is not able to infer causality, additional evaluation is warranted.
