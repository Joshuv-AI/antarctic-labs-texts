Hydrostructural and dynamic characteristics of compacted Nanning red clay considering wetting-drying impacts
Authors: Sen Deng, Hongri Zhang, Jian Wei, Kaike Huang, Yanxin Song, Hongming Li, Youjun Li, Zhong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lateritic soils, also known as red soils, are prone to actions of external environment such as wetting and drying processes and their cycling. However, in the practice of pavement design in red soil regions, there lacks experimental studies and analytical approaches to reveal and predict the evolution of the hydromechanical characteristics of red soils under complex environmental actions. This paper investigates the variation in the hydrostructural and dynamic characteristics of a compacted subgrade red clay collected from Nanning, Guangxi, China, before and after wetting-drying cycles. The pore structure and soil-water characteristics of the red clay before and after ten wetting-drying cycles were determined to reveal the influences of moisture fluctuation history. Besides, cyclic triaxial tests were performed to determine the resilient modulus (MR) and permanent strain (εp) of the red clay and reveal the influences of external stress, moisture content w, suction s, and WD cycles. It is found that (i) compacted red clay presents typical dual porosity with distinct inter-aggregate and intra-aggregate pores. Such pore structure results in bimodal soil water retention curves (SWRCs) of the red clay; (ii) upon wetting-drying cycles, the intra-aggregate pores shrink while the inter-aggregate pores swell. Besides, the global pore space (i.e., the overall void ratio) increases after WD cycles. This results in the elimination of the SWRC’s bimodal characteristics and the reduction in the clay’s water retention capacity in the low suction range and scale of shrinkage upon drying; (iii) the εp and MR vary non-linearly with σd, w, and s. Their relationships to the external stress and soil moisture change remarkably after WD cycles. A simple model was adopted to describe the variations of the εp and MR with w and s, which has achieved close agreements with the experimental measurements; (iv) the εp and MR of the tested red clay show a unique non-linear relationship regardless of the influences of σd, w, s, and WD cycles, which highlights possible intrinsic relationships between the elastic and plastic behaviors of compacted subgrade soils.

Introduction

Lateritic soils are formed during the laterization processes of carbonate parent rocks such as limestone, marl, and dolomite under subtropical weather conditions (i.e., high temperature and humidity during weathering)1-4. Lateritic soils are typically reddish in color since they contain high levels of iron oxide. Therefore, they are conventionally referred to as red soils, and clayey lateritic soils are named red clays. Red soils are widely distributed in southern China, covering approximately 1/9 of the mainland area and several major cities such as Guangzhou, Hongkong, Nanning, Guiyang, and Kunming (see Fig. 1)1, 5, 6.

Distribution of red soils in China and field red clay in Nanning.

Red soils are typically problematic soils due to their challenging engineering behaviors, which include high compressibility, significant shrinkage upon drying, high rate of creep, high in-situ moisture content and void ratio, and high sensitivity to acid rainfalls2, 5, 7, 8. Such behaviors are associated with the microstructural characteristics of the in-situ red soils and the presence of oxides that are cementitious but sensitive to moisture, especially acid pore fluids9-11. Nevertheless, red soils are widely used as subgrade fillers in pavement in foundation engineering in Southern China due to their high shear strength1. It is therefore critical to gain a compressive understanding of the hydromechanical behaviors of red soils.

Researchers over the past 40 years have conducted comprehensive experimental studies to investigate the engineering properties of natural and modified red soils, including microstructural features7, 12, 13, permeability and water-retention properties2, 9, 14, volumetric behaviors2, 15-17, static shear strength and stiffness properties11, 18-21, and bearing capacity22, 23. Several studies have developed models to describe various hydromechanical properties of red soils (e.g., water-retention curves, coefficient of permeability, shear strength, modulus of elasticity) considering influences of factors such as external stress level, moisture content, and compaction approaches2, 5, 19.

For the specific scenario where red soils are used as pavement subgrade fillers, it is important to determine the dynamic characteristics of compacted red soils under cyclic traffic loading conditions, including the resilient modulus (MR) and accumulative plastic strain (εp)24, 25. The MR and εp are key parameters for the mechanistic pavement design approaches26-29. Several studies in the literature have investigated the responses of natural and compacted red soils upon cyclic loading through laboratory cyclic triaxial tests30-33, model tests34, and in-situ monitoring35, 36. The focus of existing studies is mainly on the influences of the external stress state, moisture content, and stabilizers on red soils’ dynamic responses. However, further studies in this area are still urgently desired due to the following reasons:

(i)Natural red soils are not preferable materials for the subgrade of high-level roads such as highways unless they are modified or reinforced32, 36-38. On the contrary, compacted plain red soils mostly serve as subgrade of low-level roads where the upper pavement structure is simple and thin. In such a scenario, the compacted plain red soil subgrade is subjected to lower confining stress (σc) but higher deviator stress (σd) compared to soils in conventional pavement subgrade. However, most existing studies examined the dynamic properties of red soils under conventional σc levels (e.g., 20–50 kPa) defined by in-practice testing protocols (e.g., MEPDG39 or Chinese40 guidelines) or even higher σc levels1. Less is known about the behaviors and characteristics of MR and εp under low σc and high σd;(ii)Compacted subgrade soils are typically unsaturated soils, whose mechanical properties are sensitive to moisture content variations41-44. Several studies have examined the responses of static and dynamic strength and stiffness properties of compacted red soils21, 30. Furthermore, in-situ red soil subgrades are subjected to repeated wetting-drying processes over long service periods45. Wetting-drying cycles significantly change the macro- and micro-structural characteristics of soils, which leads to remarkable reductions in their mechanical properties46. Existing experimental studies on red soils mainly focus on the development of cracks and reduction in the static shear strength and stiffness during wetting-drying cycles21, 47. However, fewer research efforts have been directed toward understanding the influences of wetting-drying cycles on the MR and εp;(iii)Most existing studies use empirical models to describe the evolution of red soils’ mechanical properties with moisture content considering their simplicity for use in practice. However, there are significant spatial variations in the physical and chemical characteristics of red soils, especially in China2. Unified constitutive relationships based on the mechanics of unsaturated soils for describing the variation of MR and εp with soil moisture content, suction, and wetting-drying cycles remain outstanding in the current literature.

This paper presents a series of experimental studies on surficial red clay collected from Nanning, Guangxi, China. The clay samples were statically compacted and some of them were subjected to ten consecutive wetting-drying (WD) cycles. The pore-size distribution and soil-water characteristics of the red clay before and after WD cycles were determined to reveal the influences of the WD cycles. In addition, cyclic triaxial tests were performed to determine the MR and εp of the red clay under low confining stress. The experimental results were used to analyze the influences of external stress, moisture content, suction, and WD cycles. A semi-empirical model was employed to describe the relationships of the MR and εp to the moisture content and suction, which achieves reasonable agreement with the experimental results.

Experimental investigations

The red soil examined in this study was sampled from a construction site at the south section of the second ring road in Nanning, China (see Fig. 1). Soil blocks were excavated from a depth of about 1 m below the natural ground surface. After excavation, they were immediately sealed in plastic bags to preserve moisture loss and sent to the laboratory for detailed experiments. Preparation of specimens

Subgrade fillers are generally compacted at their maximum dry density (ρdmax) and optimum moisture content (wopt) to achieve high strength and modulus54, 55. In this study, the Nanning red clay was statically compacted at ρdmax = 1.63 g/cm3 and wopt=22%. Two types of specimens were compacted: (i) disc specimens 50 mm in diameter and 10 mm in height, which were statically compacted in one lift and used to determine the water retention and pore size distribution curves, and (ii) cylindrical specimens 50 mm in diameter and 100 mm in height, which were statically compacted in five lifts and used to determine the MR and εp.

To investigate the influence of moisture content and wetting-drying cycles on the soil water retention and dynamic properties, some as-compacted specimens were directly dried or wetted to different gravimetric moisture contents (i.e., 17.82%, 22%, 23.07%, 24.41%, 25.44%, where 25.44% is the saturated moisture content of as-compacted specimens, wsat) while other specimens were subjected to ten wetting-drying cycles and then dried or wetted to different moisture contents (i.e., 19.61%, 22%, 24.22%, 26.07%, 27.81%, where 27.81% is the saturated moisture content of specimens after ten wetting-drying cycles, wsat) (see Fig. 3). The target moisture contents for the as-compacted and wetting-drying impacted specimens were mostly on the wet side of the soil’s wopt (i.e., higher than 22%). This is because according to statistics of the field measurements, after years of service, most of the pavement subgrade soils would attain equilibrium moisture contents that are higher than the wopt27. Hence, to better reveal the actual scenario, most of the target moisture contents were evenly distributed between the wsat and wopt.

Fig. 3Variation of the moisture content during wetting-drying cycles.

Variation of the moisture content during wetting-drying cycles.

Specimens’ moisture content was modified using a simple filter-paper-based method approach by Han and Vanapalli (2016)27. In this method, filter papers with different wetness were used to entirely wrap the specimens for moisture exchange and equilibrium. To precisely regulate specimen moisture content during wetting, individual samples were encased in filter paper. De-aired distilled water was applied uniformly to the filter papers via spraying. The quantity of water administered corresponded to roughly 0.5% of the specimen’s moisture content, typically equating to 1.5–1.7 g. Following hydration, the specimen and moistened filter papers were sealed within two layers of plastic film. This assembly was then placed in a plastic container and transferred to a moisture chamber for a 24-hour equilibration period. Specimen mass was subsequently determined after removal of the filter papers. Each application of this protocol yielded a gravimetric moisture content increase of 0.2–0.3% per 24-hour cycle. Through iterative application, the water content of specimens could be incrementally modified.

Specifically, to saturate the specimens, filter papers were sprayed with water at an interval of 12 h until the mass of specimens ceased to increase (i.e., specimens were not able to hold additional water). The specimens were therefore regarded as having reached the saturated condition. It is noted that due to the presence of cracks and fissures induced during the wetting-drying cycles, which contributes to increasing the global void ratio of the specimens, the wsat of specimens with wetting-drying histories (i.e., wsat=27.81%) is higher than the wsat of as-compacted specimens (i.e., wsat=25.44%). The drying process is similar to the wetting process except that filter papers that were relatively drier than the specimens were used to wrap the specimens and imbibe water. The drying and wetting processes were conducted under a constant room temperature of 22 °C. Such approaches were proven effective in slowly altering the moisture content of small-scale specimens while maintaining uniform moisture distribution within the specimens45, 56, 57.

Ten wetting-drying cycles applied in this study were deemed adequate to bring the specimens to an equilibrium status since many study have reported that the soil behaviors typically come to an equilibrium after 3 to 5 wetting-drying cycles58, 59. In a typical wetting-drying cycle, specimens were first step wisely wetted till attaining the saturated condition (wsat). They were thereafter dried until approaching air dryness (wdry) where the mass of specimens stopped to decrease over 24 h. Such testing procedures simulate the worst-case scenario of the in-situ wetting-drying processes that the compacted red soil subgrade could experience. The prepared specimens were thereafter used for the following tests which are summarized in Table 2.

Pore structure and distribution

Mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) tests were performed to determine the pore size distribution (PSD) curve of the as-compacted specimens and specimens after 10 wetting-drying cycles at the wopt of 22%. Soil specimens for MIP tests were small clods cut from the disc specimens. They were freeze-dried using liquid nitrogen before being set in the PoreMaster 33 automated mercury porosimeter to determine the PSD.

Water-retention characteristics

Two types of water-retention characteristic curves (SWRC) were determined, namely the drying SWRC and scanning SWRC. To determine the drying SWRC, specimens were fully saturated and then placed in a pressure plate apparatus (Fig. 4a) to determine the drying SWRC within the suction range of 0 to 400 kPa. A total of 6 suction (s) levels: 0, 10, 50, 100, 200, and 400 kPa were sequentially applied following the ASTM D6836-1660 protocol. Within higher suction ranges, the filter paper method following the ASTM D5298-10 protocol61 was applied to measure the suction of the specimens as they were further dried. To determine the scanning SWRC, the filter paper method was adopted to measure the suction of the disc specimens after they were wetted or dried from wopt to higher or lower moisture contents. The resultant w-s relationships thus form the scanning SWRC. Drying and scanning SWRCs in terms of the w were measured from the as-compacted specimens and specimens after 10 wetting-drying cycles.

The dimensions of the specimens during the pressure plate and filter paper tests were measured using a caliber precise to 0.1 mm to track the evolution of specimens’ volume and thus void ratio. This information was then used to calculate the degree of saturation (S) of the specimens and consequently form the drying and scanning SWRCs in terms of the S.

Cyclic triaxial tests

Cyclic triaxial tests were performed using the GDS dynamic triaxial testing system (Fig. 4b). A 10 kN submersible load cell is mounted on the loading ram inside the triaxial cell to measure the cyclic force at an accuracy of 0.001% (i.e. 0.1 N for the 10 kN load cell). An optical encoder embedded in the pneumatic actuator measures the displacement of the loading ram at an accuracy of 0.0001 mm and a resolution of 0.08 μm. Confining stress up to 700 kPa can be applied by air using the pneumatic regulator or by water using the confining pressure controller. This triaxial cell is capable of applying load, deformation, and stresses up to 5 Hz, which covers the typical frequency range of traffic loadings.

Fig. 4(a) Pressure plate apparatus and (b) triaxial testing system used in this study.

(a) Pressure plate apparatus and (b) triaxial testing system used in this study.

Experimental studies have shown that the evolution of the MR and εp during cyclic loadings typically comes to an equilibrium status after about 100 to 2000 loading cycles62-64. For cohesive soils, 10,000 or more loading cycles are typically adopted in existing studies to bring soils to such a mechanical equilibrium34, 65. Considering such, a total of 20,000 loading cycles were applied on each specimen to reveal the evolution of the dynamic characteristics of the red clay in this study. For clayey soils under cyclic traffic loadings, it was found that the loading frequency has little influence on the dynamic characteristics63. Hence, the cyclic loadings were haversine with a frequency of 1 Hz (i.e., 1 s per loading-unloading loop), per the suggestions of the AASHTO T307-99 testing protocol. During each cycle, the loading took 0.5s while the unloading took 0.5s.

To simulate the loading conditions of red soil subgrade in low-rank pavements (i.e., low confining stress but high cyclic stress), the constant confining stress (σc) was set to 10 kPa, which is lower than the σc levels suggested by existing testing protocols (e.g., AASHTO T307-9966) and represents the σc level at a depth of about 1 m below the pavement surface. Considering that low σc is adopted, water instead of air was used as media to apply the σc to ensure better accuracy. The pressure controller shown in Fig. 4b has an accuracy of 0.1–0.2 kPa for the water pressure control. Hence, the maximum variance of the σc is 2% (i.e., 0.2 kPa over 10 kPa), which is considered acceptable in this study. Three levels of deviator stress were applied (σd = 20, 40, 60 kPa), which are similar to the σd values suggested by AASHTO T307-9966 for simulating cyclic traffic loadings. The σd is composed of a constant section σds = 0.1σd to simulate the overburden stress and a cyclic section σdc = 0.9σd to simulate the traffic loading.

The responses of the red clay specimens upon cyclic loading can be classified as the non-recoverable permanent strain εp and the resilient strain εr (see Fig. 5). The resilient modulus (MR) is determined at the end of the 2000 cyclic loadings. It is calculated as the ratio of the σd to the average εr of the last five cycles. The development of the εp with the number of loading cycles was also determined.

Hydrostructural characteristics

Pore structure

The pore structure characteristics obtained from the MIP results of the as-compacted Nanning red clay specimens (denoted as NWD=0) and specimens after subjecting to 10 wetting-drying cycles (denoted as NWD=10) at wopt=22% are summarized in Fig. 6. The results are presented in terms of (i) cumulative intrusion (CI) curves, which are the eMIP-log10d relationships. The eMIP is the mercury intruded void ratio, which is the ratio of the volume of the intruded mercury during MIP tests to the volume of the soil solids of the specimen intruded, and the d is the pore diameter; and (ii) pore size distribution (PSD) curves, which are (-δeMIP/δlog10d)-log10d relationships (i.e., derivative of the CI curves).

Fig. 6MIP results of the Nanning red clay.

MIP results of the Nanning red clay.

There are two distinct peaks on the PSD curves of the as-compacted Nanning red clay (see Fig. 6b), which indicates the typical aggregated structure of compacted red clays with inter-aggregate pores (i.e., macropores between aggregated clay particles) and intra-aggregate pores (i.e., micropores within aggregated clay particles)2, 45. This study follows the suggestions of Burton et al. (2015)67 that the connecting point of the peaks of the PSD curves can be regarded as the boundary between the inter-aggregate pores and intra-aggregate pores. Since the peaks connect at a d of 2 μm as shown in Fig. 6, the inter-aggregate and intra-aggregate pores are separated at d of 2 μm in this study.

Wetting-drying processes significantly altered the pore structure. There is a remarkable increase in the volume of inter-aggregate pores, as can be observed from the upshifting of the corresponding peak on the PSD curve. On the contrary, the intra-aggregate pores shrink significantly since the corresponding peak shifts down and the d at the peak increases from about 0.3 μm to 1.3 μm. Besides, the volume of the specimens increases after wetting-drying cycles, which is indicated by the upshifting of the CI curves, as shown in Fig. 6a.

In a quantitative sense, the total pore volume of the compacted red clay can be estimated using the eMIP value at d = 0.01 μm (denoted as eMIPT) while the volume of inter-aggregate pores is assumed by the eMIP value at d = 2 μm (denoted as eMIPM)45. The volume of intra-aggregate pores thus is the difference between the eMIPT and eMIPM (denoted as eMIPm= eMIPT−eMIPM). Table 3 summarizes the eMIPT, eMIPM, and eMIPm values before and after wetting-drying cycles. As can be seen, due to the wetting-drying process, the eMIPT and eMIPM increased by 13% and 40%, respectively, while the eMIPm decreased by 25%. These results are consistent with similar studies in the literature and highlight the high sensitivity of both intra-aggregate and inter-aggregate pores to the wetting-drying cycles. The increase in the total and inter-aggregate pores can be attributed to (i) the rearrangement and segregation of the clay aggregates, (ii) the non-recoverable shrinkage of the intra-aggregate pores. This is because the intra-aggregate pores are typically assumed saturated at low suction level. Upon drying, the increase in the suction and associated inter-particle stress would cause the shrinkage of the soil aggregate. Such shrinkage is elastoplastic and could accumulate during wetting-drying cycles; and (iii) the presence of cracks, which are associated with the non-uniform straining of the specimens, the collapse of the micropores within the clay aggregates and the destruction of the bonding formed by the iron oxide upon wetting-drying cycles2, 7.

Water retention characteristics

The soil water retention curves (SWRC) in terms of the moisture content (denoted as w-SWRCs) measured in this study are summarized in Fig. 7. For the drying SWRC of the as-compacted specimen, the measurements of the axis-translation technique well connect with the measurements of the filter paper method at s = 400 kPa. It demonstrates bi-modal characteristics where the first air entry value (saM) is at about 10 kPa (Point A) and the second air entry value (sam) is at about 30 kPa (Point B). The bi-modal SWRC is generally obtained from compacted soils with aggregated soil structures such as the red clay specimens investigated in this study2, 13, 68. The saM indicates the desaturation of the inter-aggregate pores while the sam signifies the desaturation of the intra-aggregate pores.

The scanning SWRC is located below the drying SWRC for the as-compacted specimen, which is expected and can be attributed to the occlusion of air bubbles within the pores upon wetting69. When the w drops below the wopt, the scanning SWRC approaches the drying SWRC.

After 10 wetting-drying circles, the drying and scanning SWRCs become similar. There are several remarkable changes: (i) the saturated moisture content (i.e., the w at s = 1 kPa) increases, which is due to the development of cracks and swelling of the inter-aggregate pores (see Fig. 5); (ii) the SWRCs become linear in the low suction range (i.e., s < 1000 kPa) and air entry values become hard to identify. This is associated with the alternation in the pore structure. The macroscopic pore space (including cracks and inter-aggregate pores) increases and becomes easier to desaturate, which results in a significant reduction in the saM. On the other hand, as the intra-aggregate pores shrink (see Fig. 6), the capillary water stored therein reduces significantly, making it harder to identify the desaturation process and the corresponding sam; (iii) at high suction range (i.e., s > 1000 kPa), the scanning SWRCs before and after WD cycles become similar. This is because the water in the high suction range is mainly retained by the adsorption effect, which is not sensitive to changes in the pore structure45, 69.

Figure 8 shows the evolution of the void ratio with suction where eopt is the void ratio at wopt. The wetting-drying cycles increase the global void ratio of the specimens, as can be observed from the upshifting of the e-s curves. However, the scale of reduction in the e with increasing s becomes much less significant after wetting-drying impacts. Specifically, the e of the as-compacted specimen drops from 0.7 at the saturated condition to 0.58 at the highest suction, constituting a reduction of 17%. While the e of the specimen after 10 wetting-drying cycles reduces from 0.83 to 0.79, forming a reduction of only 5%. The reduction in the sensitivity of the soil’s volume to moisture after wetting-drying cycles can be attributed to the segregated and weakened soil skeleton after wetting-drying impacts, which may not be able to effectively coordinate the shrinkage of the clay aggregates.

There are no significant differences between the e-s relationships corresponding to the drying SWRC and the scanning SWRC. The shrinkage of the as-compacted and WD-impacted specimens takes place when suction exceeds 100 kPa, which corresponds to the desaturation process of intra-aggregate pores (see Fig. 7). This behavior indicates that the shrinkage of the compacted Nanning red clay mainly results from the shrinkage of the intra-aggregate pores.

Variation of the void ratio with suction.

The void ratio measurements, along with the w-s relationships, were used to determine the SWRCs in terms of the degree of saturation (denoted as S-SWRCs), as shown in Fig. 9. The characteristics of the w-SWRCs and S-SWRCs and their responses to WD cycles are similar except that, unlike the w-SWRCs, the S-SWRCs shift down after WD cycles, which clearly indicates a reduction of the water retention capacity within the low suction range. This is consistent with observations reported for the SWRCs of various types of soils subjected to WD cycles45, 57, 58. Such behaviors are associated with the reduction in the intra-aggregate pore space and the development of cracks and inter-aggregate pore space that can barely hold water and desaturate at very low suction45. Apparently, S-SWRCs better reflect the water retention characteristics of red soils considering their remarkable shrinkage behaviors upon drying.

Dynamic characteristics

Permanent strain

The evolution of the permanent strain (εp) with the increasing number of cyclic loadings (N) for the as-compacted red clay specimens and specimens after 10 wetting-drying cycles are shown in Figs. 10 and 11, respectively. The εp increases significantly at the initial stage of loadings (e.g., N < 5000). The increase in the εp levels off as the cyclic loading further continues. Influences of the cyclic stress (σd), moisture content (w), and wetting-drying cycles on the εp are remarkable. The εp increases significantly with the σd, w, and after wetting-drying cycles.

According to AASHTO T307-9966, the pavement subgrade soil can be deemed failed once the εp exceeds 5%. For the case of the Nanning red clay, the as-compacted specimens at high w (25.44% and 24.41%) under σd of 60 kPa approach failure, and the WD-impacted specimens fails at the same w under σd of 40 kPa and 60 kPa. Moreover, under low w and σd, the εp remains relatively constant after the initial loading stage (i.e., N > 15000), showing plastic shakedown characteristics70 while under high w and σd, especially after WD cycles, the εp continues to increase with the N, demonstrating plastic creep or plastic collapse characteristics70.

Figures 12 and 13 show in dots the variation of the measured εp at the end of the cyclic loading test (i.e. εp at the 20000th loading, denoted as εp20000) with moisture content and suction, respectively. Both εp20000-w and εp20000-s relationships before and after WD cycles are non-linear. The εp20000 is more sensitive to the moisture at high w levels between the wsat and wopt (i.e., low s levels below the sopt), judging from the steeper slope of the εp20000-w and εp20000-s relationships within the corresponding w and s ranges.

After WD cycles, the compacted red clay becomes more sensitive to moisture content changes. In other words, the slope of the εp20000-w and εp20000-s relationships increase after WD cycles. For instance, under σd = 40 kPa, the εp20000 of the as-compacted specimen increases from 0.2% at the driest condition at w = 17.82 to 4% at the saturated condition at w = 25.44%, constituting an increase of 19 times. The same increase after 10 WD cycles is 40 times, which is much higher. This is also the case for the scenario of σd = 20 and 60 kPa. The increasing sensitivity of the εp to the moisture is associated with the deterioration of the soil skeleton upon WD cycles in terms of the (i) segregation of the soil skeleton and the presence of cracks45 and (ii) the destruction of the bonding formed by the iron oxides18, both of which leads to the accelerated straining and even shear failure upon cyclic traffic loadings.

Resilient modulus

Figures 12 and 13 show in dots the variation of the measured ΜR at the end of the cyclic loading with moisture content and suction, respectively. The MR decreases as t<he soil gets wet, which is expected since it is well-reported that the strength and stiffness properties of soils reduce upon wetting41, 71, 72. Similar to the behaviors of the εp20000, the ΜR-w and ΜR-s relationships are nonlinear. Specifically, the ΜR of as-compacted and WD-impacted Nanning specimens are more sensitive to the s at low suction range (ssopt) (see Fig. 15). The ΜR of as-compacted specimens present higher sensitivity to the w on the wet side of the wopt (i.e., between wopt and wsat, see Fig. 14a). The nonlinearity of the ΜR-w relationships reduces after wetting-drying cycles and the reduction in the MR appears to be more significant on the dry side of the wopt (i.e., w>wopt, see Fig. 14b).

For all specimens, the MR reduces as the σd increases but the scale of reduction differs. At low w and high s, the MR values are high and the reduction in the MR is significant. On the contrary, since the MR values are low at high w and low s, the reduction in the MR is comparatively less remarkable. After WD cycles, the MR reduces and becomes less sensitive to the σd. The reduction in the MR with increasing σd is mainly attributed to the weakening of the soil skeleton, which is associated with cyclic shearing and consequently increases with σd. For specimens with rigid skeletons such as those with low w, high s, and before WD cycles, their MR is more vulnerable to the destruction of the soil skeleton compared to specimens with weak skeletons such as those with high w, low s, and after WD cycles.

Equation 3 is also used to predict the ΜR-w and ΜR-s relationships measured from the Nanning red clay. The scanning S-SWRCs before and after WD cycles are used in Eq. 3 for prediction. The modeled results are shown in Figs. 14 and 15 in lines, respectively, along with the respective ξ values. It can be seen that Eq. 3 well models the non-linear ΜR-w and ΜR-s relationships of as-compacted and WD-impacted specimens under all three σd levels. Similar to the εp20000 case, the ξ increases from 2.9 to 3.9 after WD impacts.

Results shown in Figs. 12, 13, 14 and 15 demonstrate that using suction and degree of saturation as soil state variables to establish models has the advantage of reasonably describing the highly nonlinear variation of the MR and εp with soil humidity considering impacts of the wetting-drying cycles. The accuracy of model prediction, however, depends on the selection of the (i) S-SWRCs (typically following the scanning paths) that should reasonably describe the variation of the degree of saturation with suction within the soil upon external environmental effects and (ii) parameter ξ that are sensitive to the wetting-drying cycles. Hence, developing simple and reliable approaches for estimating suitable S-SWRCs and ξ values are still desirable in pavement design practice. Besides, Eq. 3 appears to predict MR values that increase quickly with the increase in the suction (see Fig. 15). This may lead to overprediction of the MR for soils at dry conditions. Thus, a threshold for the MR appears to be necessary to avoid overprediction.

Relationships between permanent strain and resilient modulus

Despite the various experimental studies on the MR and εp, few studies have examined the correlations between the MR and εp. In fact, the MR and εp are indicators of subgrade soils’ elastic and plastic status under cyclic loadings, respectively, and hence should be related. The MR-εp relationships are also useful information for better understanding and predicting elastoplastic behaviors of subgrade soils under long-term traffic loadings.

Fig. 16Relationships between the MR and εp20000.

Relationships between the MR and εp20000.

Figure 16 shows the MR-εp relationship obtained from the as-compacted and WD-impacted Nanning red clay specimens at various w, s, and σd. It is interesting and clear that the MR-εp relationship for the Nanning red clay is nonlinear and consistent regardless of the influences of external stress states, soil wetness, and moisture cycle histories. It can be described using a simple Equation εp20000 = 13.29e-0.044MR where εp20000 values are in percentage and MR values are in MPa. Results shown in Fig. 16 highlight the possibility of intrinsic relationships between elastic and plastic behaviors of soils, which are not sensitive to physical status and moisture histories. However, such unique relationships and their major influencing factors need further experimental data to verify and reveal.

Conclusions

The pore structure, soil-water characteristics, and dynamic responses of a compacted Nanning red clay were studied in this paper highlighting influences of external stress state, soil moisture content and suction, and wetting-drying cycles. The following conclusions can be obtained:

(i)Compacted red clay specimens present typical aggregated dual pore structures with inter-aggregate pores and intra-aggregate pores. The wetting-drying cycles altered the pore structure, which was manifested as the shrinkage of the intra-aggregate pores (pore space reduced by 25%) and the swelling of the inter-aggregate pores (pore space increased by 40%). Besides, the global pore space detected by the MIP tests increased by 13% due to the presence of cracks and non-recoverable swelling of the soil skeleton upon WD impacts;(ii)The drying water retention curve (SWRC) of the as-compacted red clay specimen demonstrates bimodal characteristics, which well agrees with its dual porosity characteristics. The bimodal characteristics were eliminated upon WD cycles, which coincide with the changes in the pore structure. After WD impacts, the differences between the scanning and drying SWRCs and the shrinkage in the soil’s global void ratio upon drying become less remarkable. The water retention capacity in the low suction range reduces after WD while that in the high suction range is less affected;(iii)The permanent strain increases and the resilient modulus decreases non-linearly with increasing σd and w and decreasing s. The sensitivity of the εp and MR to the external stress and soil moisture changes remarkably after WD cycles. A simple model was adopted to describe the variations of the εp and MR with w and s, which has achieved close agreements with the experimental measurements. The accuracy of the model depends on the selection of the scanning degree of saturated dependent SWRC and the one parameter ξ that increase with the wetting-drying cycles;(iv)The measured εp and MR show a unique non-linear relationship regardless of the influences of σd, w, s, and WD cycles. This highlights the possible existence of intrinsic relationships between the elastic and plastic behaviors of soils. Such relationships, however, deserve further study to verify and reveal the major influencing factors.
