Interaction between perceived ageism and poor sleep quality on frailty among community-dwelling older adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Bo Ye, Yunxia Li, Lili Chen, Ke Gong, Junling Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to examine the associations and interaction effects of perceived ageism (PA) and poor sleep quality (SQ) on frailty among community-dwelling older adults.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 4125 community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 60 from Shanghai. PA was self-reported. Participants completed the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), with a score > 5 indicating poor SQ. Frailty was assessed using a 30-item Frailty Index (FI), with participants categorized as robust (≤ 0.10), prefrail (> 0.10 and < 0.25), and frail (≥ 0.25). Both multiplicative interaction and additive interaction were evaluated.ResultsAfter adjusting for covariates, there was a significant multiplicative interaction between PA and PSQI score in relation to FI, indicating a stronger association between PSQI score and FI in participants reporting PA than those without (β = 0.489 [95%CI, 0.430–0.548] vs. β = 0.276 [95%CI, 0.232–0.319]; P for interaction < 0.001). Furthermore, significant additive interactions between PA and poor SQ were observed for frailty (relative excess risk due to interaction [RERI] = 0.85 [95%CI, 0.07–1.64]) and prefrailty (RERI = 0.43 [95%CI, 0.06–0.79]) in the fully adjusted model. The attributable proportion (AP) due to interaction was 0.16 (95%CI, 0.02–0.30) for frailty and 0.15 (95%CI, 0.03–0.28) for prefrailty, indicating that approximately 16% and 15% of the associations with frailty and prefrailty were attributable to the combined effects of PA and poor SQ, respectively.ConclusionPA and poor SQ interact synergistically, accounting for a substantial proportion of the association with frailty. These findings underscore the need to target both factors in frailty management among community-dwelling older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07436-4.

Introduction

Frailty is characterized by a decline in functioning across multiple physiological systems, which leads to increased vulnerability to stressors and various adverse health outcomes [1]. It represents an emerging global health burden with significant implications for both clinical practice and public health [2, 3]. Fortunately, frailty is potentially preventable and reversible before it progresses to disability [3, 4]. Therefore, identification and intervention for vulnerable populations are crucial.

Substantial evidence suggests that chronic inflammation plays a significant role in the development of frailty [5–7]. Recent research has highlighted the importance of identifying sleep disorders in older adults with frailty [8, 9], as both conditions are pathogenetically linked through the elevated expression of inflammatory biomarkers. It is possible that disturbed sleep may accelerate frailty by disrupting the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenocortical (HPA) axis [10–12]. Given that both frailty and sleep disorders are common among older adults, it is essential to understand how aging contributes to this relationship.

The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified aging and the fight against ageism as urgent issues of our time [13]. Ageism refers to the stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination directed at individuals based on their age [13]. It is an important social determinant of health that has been largely overlooked until now [14]. Although ageism and its health-related associations have been relatively understudied compared to other forms of discrimination [15, 16], a systematic review has provided consistent evidence linking ageism to adverse health outcomes [17]. A population-based survey among US adults aged 50–80 years found that 93.4% of participants reported regularly experiencing one or more forms of everyday ageism [18]. In recent years, an association between ageism and frailty has also been observed [19, 20]. The experience of discrimination is perceived by the body as a social stressor, which can directly affect health through the activation of the HPA axis [21], leading to the release of cortisol and increased systemic inflammation [22]. An experimental study demonstrated that older adults were more reactive to everyday ageism (such as patronizing communication) in terms of their cognitive performance and changes in cortisol levels [23]. This evidence suggests that ageism may accelerate frailty through a stress-inflammation process, although this mechanism has not yet been thoroughly examined.

Considering that both sleep disturbances and ageism are associated with frailty, and may contribute to the development of frailty in older adults through common mechanisms, the potential for a synergistic effect has yet to be explored. We conducted a population-based study to investigate the hypothesis that PA and poor SQ interact in their association with frailty among older adults.

Methods

Study design and participants

The data for this analysis were obtained from a community-based prospective cohort study of older adults in Shanghai, China, conducted between 2020 and 2023. A total of 4689 participants aged 60 years and older were recruited at baseline. Further details regarding the recruitment process have been described elsewhere [24]. After excluding 347 participants who were lost to follow-up, 205 who had died, and 12 participants aged < 60 years, a total of 4125 individuals with complete assessments in 2023 were included in this analysis. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines. The research protocol involving human participants received ethical review and approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the School of Public Health at Fudan University (IRB00002408 & FWA00002399). Before enrollment, written informed consent was obtained either from the participants themselves or from family members for individuals who were illiterate.

Frailty assessment

Frailty was assessed using the Frailty Index (FI), which measures the accumulation of various age-related health deficits. In this study, the FI was constructed according to standard procedures [25]. We selected 30 items for the FI construction, covering aspects such as disease, disability in activities of daily living (ADL) and Instrumental ADL, physical function, self-rated health, depression, and cognitive function (eTable 1 in Supplement). Depression was identified using the total score from the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), with a cutoff of ≥ 5 [26]. Cognitive function was assessed using a 5-min delayed recall test that involved 3 simple Chinese words, along with 2 questions from the cognitive aspect of a Chinese frailty screening scale [27], which yielded a total score ranging from 0 to 5. Items 1–29 were dichotomized into 1 (indicating the presence of a deficit) or 0 (indicating the absence of a deficit) based on corresponding cutoff values. Item 30 was treated as a continuous variable, ranging from 0 to 1. For each participant, the FI was calculated as the sum of present health deficits divided by 30. Thus, the FI is expressed as a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 1, with a higher FI indicating a greater level of frailty. Based on previous research [3, 28], frailty status was classified into 3 categories: robust (FI ≤ 0.10), prefrail (0.10 < FI < 0.25), and frail (FI ≥ 0.25).

Perceived ageism assessment

PA was measured using the following 5 questions: “In the past year, how often did you experience the following situations because of your age? 1) Did someone act arrogantly towards you or as if you were not smart? 2) Did someone seem afraid of you by avoiding you? 3) Did someone show prejudice against you or treat you unfairly? 4) Did someone show a lack of respect, for example by ignoring or patronizing you? 5) Did someone treat you rudely, such as by insulting you, abusing you, or refusing you services?” Responses were rated on a scale from 0 (never) to 4 (always) for each question [29, 30]. The Cronbach alpha for this study was 0.957, indicating excellent internal consistency. The total PA score ranges from 0 to 20, with higher scores indicating more frequent experiences of PA. In accordance with the criteria established in previous studies, older adults who scored above 1 on this total index were classified as having experienced PA, which denotes having encountered age discrimination at least once in the past year. This approach is consistent with how discrimination-related exposures are commonly operationalized in prior studies (e.g., never vs. ever/once or more), enabling more direct comparisons across studies [29].

Sleep quality assessment

Participants’ SQ was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [31], which is widely used in the Chinese population and has demonstrated strong reliability and validity [32]. The PSQI consists of 19 items across 7 dimensions (Subjective sleep quality, Sleep latency, Sleep duration, Sleep efficiency, Sleep disturbances, Use of sleeping medication, Daytime dysfunction). Each item is scored from 0 to 3, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 21 points. A higher score indicates poorer SQ. In this study, the Cronbach alpha for the PSQI was 0.779, reflecting good reliability. Poor SQ was defined based on a commonly accepted cutoff value of PSQI > 5, which has been shown to yield a sensitivity of 98% and specificity of 55% for primary insomnia from controls in Chinese community-dwelling adults [32].

Covariates

Basic information collected from participants included age, gender (man/woman), education level (never attended school/primary school/middle school/high school or above), marital status (married/other), living arrangements (living with family/with spouse alone/alone/other), smoking status (nonsmoker/smoker/ex-smoker), and alcohol consumption (yes/no).

Statistical analysis

To examine the associations among ageism, sleep, and frailty, we employed linear regression models with robust standard errors (SE). In the primary models, PA (yes/no) and PSQI score were entered simultaneously. We then repeated the analyses using SQ (poor/good) in place of PSQI score, adjusting for all covariates. Stratified analyses were subsequently conducted by PA status. P values for multiplicative interactions were calculated by including interaction terms for PA and PSQI score, and for PA and SQ, respectively [33]. In addition, PA score treated as a continuous variable was also used to examine its multiplicative interaction with PSQI score in relation to FI.

Frailty status (robust/prefrail/frail) was further examined by applying modified Poisson regression with robust SE to estimate relative risks (RRs) for prefrailty and frailty compared with the robust reference group. Multiplicative interactions were assessed by incorporating cross-product terms of PA and SQ into the models, with corresponding P values determined for these interaction terms [33]. To quantify the joint effect, we estimated the combined associations of PA and poor SQ with frailty and prefrailty. Additive interaction was assessed using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion (AP), and the synergy index (S). Additive interaction was considered present if the 95%CI for RERI and AP did not include 0, and the 95%CI for S did not include 1 [33, 34].

To explore possible nonlinear associations, we conducted restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses to examine the relationship between the PA score and FI as well as between PA score and frailty status (eFigure 1 in Supplement). The results indicated a nonlinear pattern: FI increased initially and then decreased as PA score became higher. Importantly, the findings also showed that participants reporting PA score of at least once (score ≥ 1) had a positive association with higher FI compared with those reporting no PA (score = 0).

Subgroup analyses were performed to investigate sex-specific differences and to identify which categories of PA and SQ were associated with variations in frailty risk. Each PA item and each SQ dimension were treated as binary variables. A PA item score ≥ 1 indicated a positive response. Regarding SQ, poor sleep was defined as a score of ≥ 1 or ≥ 2, depending on the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) results for each SQ dimension score relative to poor SQ (eTable 2 in Supplement). To evaluate interaction patterns, we replicated the interaction models by first substituting the PA variable with its 5 individual PA questions, and then substituting SQ with its 7 dimensions.

Finally, we conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the main findings. First, because FI is bounded between 0 and 1 and is typically right-skewed, we used fractional logit regression to evaluate interaction effects. Second, based on the observed nonlinear relationship between PA score and FI, we categorized PA score into four groups: 0, 1–5, 6–10, and 11–20. We then re-ran the main analyses using PA score as a continuous variable in linear models. In addition, we continuously tested additive interactions across PA score groups (e.g., 1–5 vs. 0; 1–10 vs. 0; 6–10 vs. 0; 6–20 vs. 0; and 11–20 vs. 0) and poor SQ in relation to frailty status. Third, given that sleep disturbance and perceived stress are well-established risk factors for depression and cognitive impairment, their inclusion in the FI may introduce conceptual overlap and potentially inflate observed associations. Sensitivity analyses were therefore performed by constructing a modified FI (mFI) excluding depression and cognitive function items, and repeating all primary analyses.

All analyses were conducted using Stata 18 (StataCorp), with statistical significance set at P < .05 for 2-tailed tests.

Results

Among the 4125 older adults aged 60 and above included in the analysis, 2319 (56.2%) were women, with an average age of 73.8 years (SD = 6.16). Overall, 11.8% of the participants were identified as frail, and 24.4% were categorized as prefrail. Additionally, 30.0% reported experiencing PA, while 33.6% reported poor SQ. The results of the univariate analysis indicated significant differences in all participants characteristics based on frailty status. Detailed information is presented in Table 1.

Table 3 details the separate associations between PA (frail vs. robust: RR = 2.52 [95%CI, 2.15–2.95], P < .001; prefrail vs. robust: RR = 2.01 [95%CI, 1.74–2.33], P < .001) and poor SQ (Frail vs. robust: RR = 4.63 [95%CI, 3.90–5.49], P < .001; prefrail vs. robust: RR = 3.28 [95%CI, 2.79–3.86], P < .001) with frailty status. Furthermore, compared with older adults without PA who reported good SQ (the reference group), those with both PA and poor SQ had a significantly higher risk of frailty (RR = 5.32 [95%CI, 4.26–6.65]; P < .001) and prefrailty (RR = 2.77 [95%CI, 2.44–3.16]; P < .001). Additive interactions corresponded to a RERI of 0.85 (95%CI, 0.07–1.64) and an AP of 0.16 (95%CI, 0.02–0.30) for frailty, and a RERI of 0.43 (95%CI, 0.06–0.79) and AP of 0.15 (95%CI, 0.03–0.28) for prefrailty. These findings suggest an additional risk associated with the joint exposure of PA and poor SQ of 0.85 times for frailty and 0.43 times for prefrailty, relative to individuals without PA who reported good SQ (Fig. 2B and C). Overall, the PA-SQ interactions accounted for 16% of frailty and 15% of prefrailty among participants experiencing both exposures.

Subgroup analyses by sex showed that the multiplicative interactions between PA and PSQI score in relation to FI was significant in both men and women (eFigure 3 in Supplement). However, an additive interaction between PA and poor SQ in relation to frailty status was observed only in older men (eFigure 4 in Supplement). Further analyses of individual PA items indicated that several domains, including being treated arrogantly (question 1), being treated with prejudice or unfairly (question 3), and being treated without respect (question 4), showed significant multiplicative interactions with PSQI score in relation to FI (eFigure 5 in Supplement). These items also demonstrated joint effects with poor SQ in relation to frailty and prefrailty, although these additive interactions did not reach statistical significance (eFigure 6 in Supplement). In addition, most SQ dimensions exhibited joint effects with PA in relation to frailty and prefrailty. Specifically, only use of sleep medication (RERI = 1.30 [95%CI, 0.43–2.17]) and sleep duration (RERI = 0.53 [95%CI, 0.03–1.03]) were significantly associated with additive interactions between poor SQ and frailty, whereas sleep efficiency (RERI = 0.47 [95%CI, 0.11–0.83]) showed a significant additive interaction with poor SQ in relation to prefrailty (eFigure 7 in supplement).

Sensitivity analysis using fractional logit regression demonstrated that the multiplicative interaction between PA and PSQI score was consistent with the main finding (eTable 5 in Supplement). Results from interaction analyses across PA variables defined by different scoring approaches were similarly aligned with the main findings (eFigure 8 in Supplement). Furthermore, sensitivity analyses using a mFI also yielded results consistent with the main analyses (eFigures 9–10 in Supplement).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study nested within a community-based prospective cohort of Chinese older adults, we found that the co-occurrence of PA and poor SQ was more prevalent among frail and prefrail individuals than among robust older adults, with significant additive interactions observed between the two conditions. These additive interactions corresponded to excess risks of frailty and prefrailty associated with the co-occurrence of PA and poor SQ among older adults, relative to the summed risks of each condition alone (synergistic effect of 1 + 1>2). Furthermore, the association between PSQI score and FI, was significantly moderated by PA (yes/no). Specifically, the multiplicative interaction indicated greater frailty levels associated with increasing severity of SQ among older adults who reported experiencing PA compared with those who did not. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the prevalence of concurrent PA and SQ and to examine their interactions in relation to frailty.

These findings may support the potential interaction between ageism, considered a social stressor, and fundamental aging mechanisms, primarily inflammation [35]. This interaction could further link to age-related diseases, including frailty in older adults [5–7]. Prior research has demonstrated similar mechanisms, revealing that both pro-inflammatory diets and poor SQ are risk factors for frailty, with a significant interaction observed between dietary inflammation and SQ [36]. Although there is no direct evidence, PA and inflammation appear to be closely linked through stress. In an experimental study, positive beliefs about aging acted as a buffer against stress, whereas negative beliefs about aging intensified stress [37], often reflected in inflammatory biomarkers [38]. Additionally, previous study has found inflammation to be independently associated with negative beliefs about aging [39], which are commonly related to experiences of ageism among older adults [20]. Furthermore, it appears that mental health may also mediate the interaction between PA and poor SQ in their association with frailty in older adults. Prior research has established relationships between PA and poor SQ with depressive symptoms [18, 40, 41], which also have been demonstrated a bidirectional relationship with frailty [42, 43]. Although biological mechanisms involving inflammation and psychosocial pathways are plausible explanations for the observed interactive effects, our study did not include validation using direct inflammatory markers or a formal mediation analysis. Further robust evidence will therefore be needed to corroborate these findings in future research.

Beyond the primary findings, our results may offer guidance for future research and for sex-stratified clinical and public health strategies. In sex-specific analyses, the combined associations of PA and poor SQ with frailty and prefrailty appeared stronger in older men. Similar to this observation, longitudinal evidence has shown that higher perceived stress predicts accelerated worsening of executive function and increases in interleukin-6 (IL-6) over time only among older men, suggesting that men may have greater inflammatory susceptibility that contributes to sex-specific stress-related cognitive aging [44]. Moreover, compared with women, men tend to have a higher burden of senescent cells and earlier, more pronounced immune senescence [45]. Although these subgroup analyses are exploratory, they are broadly aligned with existing findings and have practical public health relevance. Specifically, they suggest that targeted prevention against age-related discrimination in older men would be prioritized.

In the interaction analyses involving the 5 individual PA questions and 7 SQ dimensions, the patterns were generally consistent with the main findings; however, most of these interaction effects did not reach statistical significance. Despite this, the results may indicate specific priority targets for frailty prevention in older adults. These include aspects of age-related discriminatory experiences, such as being treated arrogantly, experiencing prejudice or unfair treatment, and feeling disrespected, as well as key sleep-related indicators, particularly use of sleep medication, sleep duration, and sleep efficiency. Collectively, these findings suggest that targeted screening for PA and sleep problems in community settings or elderly outpatient clinics may help identify individuals at elevated risk of frailty. In particular, screening for sleep quality using brief indicators (e.g., self-reported use of sleep medication and sleep duration) could further improve early identification of vulnerable populations, especially when implemented in combination with screening for PA. Notably, among the 7 SQ dimensions, the joint association between PA and use of sleep medication showed the highest additive effect, with an excess risk of 1.30 times for frailty. This may reflect that individuals who use sleep medication are more likely to have more severe or persistent sleep disturbances, and therefore may be more susceptible to combined effect of ageism and sleep disruption.

Importantly, our results also provided comparable survey data on the prevalence of PA, which stands at 30.0% among Chinese older adults—a topic that has rarely been studied in Eastern countries [46, 47]. Ageism affects billions of people globally, at least one in two individuals holds ageist views toward older adults [48]. The prevalence of PA varies across countries, with reports indicating that 44.9% of US adults aged 50 to 80 years experience interpersonal ageism [18], 38.4% of UK respondents aged 65 and older report experiences of age discrimination [19], and between 24.1% of respondents over 70 years of age in Sweden and 76.4% in the Czech Republic report experiencing age discrimination in Europe [29]. Research on ageism in the Western Pacific region remains inconclusive, as cultural factors such as filial piety suggest greater acceptance of aging [49], while other studies challenge the view that Eastern societies are less ageist than their Western counterparts [46, 50], indicating a need for a more nuanced theoretical framework to understand the complex cross-cultural differences in ageism experiences [49]. Although few studies have been conducted, consistent evidence indicates that the risk of ageism increases in frail older adults [19, 20]. Therefore, the implementation of frailty identification in clinical practice should consider the potential burden of labeling an older adult as frail [51]. It is crucial to ensure that frailty does not become a factor that reinforces ageism and prevents access to appropriate interventions [52].

This study utilized a population-based representative sample to investigate the prevalence of ageism among older adults in China, providing the first evidence of the joint effect of PA and SQ in association with frailty. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study population was drawn from an urban community and may not be representative of other specific populations, suggesting that the generalizability of the study’s conclusions should be approached with caution. Specifically, our results may not be directly applicable to rural Chinese populations, given potential differences in socioeconomic conditions and environmental context, nor to other Asian cultural contexts where filial piety norms may vary. Future studies using more diverse samples are warranted to improve external validity. Second, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference regarding the relationships among ageism, sleep, and frailty. Additionally, differences in recall periods may influence the interpretation of the PA-SQ interaction. For instance, frailty may itself contribute to poorer sleep, or make individuals more sensitive to ageism, thus introducing potential reverse causality. Ongoing longitudinal follow-up of our cohort will enable us to clarify the temporal relationships among ageism, sleep, and frailty in future analyses. Third, although previous evidence suggests that inflammation may mediate the associations among these variables, our study did not measure inflammation markers. Therefore, the proposed inflammatory pathways should be interpreted as plausible biological mechanisms supported by prior literature, rather than as direct evidence from the present study. This highlights an important direction for future research. Fourth, the study has adjusted many key covariates, but still failed to account for additional important confounders, such as economic status, social support networks, physical activity levels, and medication use. Future studies are warranted to confirm the findings. Finally, the questionnaire used to assess PA is relatively broad, and different individuals may interpret the questions in various ways. Future research should consider using psychometric scales for PA that are specifically designed for the Chinese population to strengthen these findings.

Conclusion

This study documented the prevalence of PA, an understudied type of social factor that may influence health outcomes among Chinese older adults. We found that PA moderated the association between SQ and FI. Specifically, PSQI score was associated with higher levels of frailty among older adults, and this association was stronger in those experiencing PA than in those who did not. Importantly, PA exhibited significant additive interactions with poor SQ in relation to frailty and prefrailty, demonstrating excess risks attributable to their combined effects. These findings suggest that primary healthcare interventions for frailty among older adults should simultaneously address both ageism and sleep disturbances.
