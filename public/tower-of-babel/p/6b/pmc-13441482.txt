Interactions among progesterone, estradiol, and γ-aminobutyric acid in rat spermatozoal hyperactivation
Authors: Miki MUROI, Shiho FUKUDA, Miki TAKAHASHI, Tatsuya SUZUKI, Masakatsu FUJINOKI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Our previous study in hamsters indicated that progesterone (P) induces enhancement of spermatozoal hyperactivation, while 17β-estradiol (Eβ) and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) suppress this enhancement. Since P has also been shown to enhance hyperactivation of rat spermatozoa in another study conducted by our team, we examined the effects of Eβ and GABA on the P-enhanced hyperactivation of rat spermatozoa in the present study. Our results showed that Eβ suppressed P-enhanced hyperactivation in a dose-dependent manner through an estrogen receptor. In contrast, GABA did not affect P-enhanced hyperactivation. Instead, 5–500 fM GABA significantly enhanced spermatozoal hyperactivation, and this GABA-induced enhancement of hyperactivation was associated with a GABAA receptor. In conclusion, P-enhanced hyperactivation of rat spermatozoa was suppressed by Eβ although it was not suppressed by GABA. Moreover, low concentrations of GABA enhanced rat spermatozoal hyperactivation.

Mammalian spermatozoa capacitated in an oviduct or incubated under the in vitro capacitation/fertilization conditions become hyperactivated [1, 2]. Hyperactivation is a specialized form of motility that allows the sperm to swim through the viscous oviductal fluid and penetrate the oocyte envelope, which consists of the zona pellucida and cumulus cells [1, 2]. Hyperactivated spermatozoa show asymmetric, whiplash-like flagellar movements, although the motility patterns are species-dependent [1, 3,4,5]. Importantly, hyperactivation capacity is correlated with in vitro fertilization (IVF) success [6,7,8,9].

Hormones and neurotransmitters secreted into the oviduct have been recently implicated in the regulation of spermatozoal hyperactivation [4, 10]. Progesterone (P) has been shown to induce human spermatozoal hyperactivation through activation of the CatSper Ca2+ channel, which plays an important role in hyperactivation [11,12,13]. However, in mice, P does not stimulate CatSper Ca2+ channels [12]. In contrast, in hamsters, mice, and rats, P enhances spermatozoal hyperactivation through the membrane progesterone receptor (Supplementary Figs. 1–3) [14,15,16,17], and P has been also shown to increase spermatozoal penetration in hamsters [18]. P-enhanced mouse spermatozoal hyperactivation is accompanied by an increase in the success rate of IVF [16]. However, in rats, although P was shown to enhance rat spermatozoal hyperactivation, it did not affect IVF success [17]. In hamsters and mice, P-enhanced hyperactivation was suppressed by 17β-estradiol (Eβ) through a membrane estrogen receptor (mER) [19,20,21] (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). When P-enhanced mouse spermatozoal hyperactivation was suppressed by Eβ, the related increase in IVF success was also suppressed [21]. In hamsters, P-enhanced hyperactivation was also suppressed by γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) through the GABAA receptor [22] (Supplementary Fig. 1). In hamsters, mice, and rats, serotonin enhanced spermatozoal hyperactivation through the serotonin receptor (SR) [3, 15, 23, 24] (Supplementary Figs. 1–3). In hamsters, serotonin enhanced spermatozoal hyperactivation through SR types 2 and 4 in a dose-dependent manner [15, 23, 25]. Additionally, SR type 2-mediated hyperactivation was suppressed by GABA through the GABAA receptor [26]. In mice, serotonin enhanced spermatozoal hyperactivation through SR types 2, 3, 4, and 7 [3]. In rats, serotonin enhanced spermatozoal hyperactivation through SR type 4 alone [24]. In both mice and rats, serotonin also increased IVF success through SR type 4 [3, 24]. In hamsters, melatonin also enhanced spermatozoal hyperactivation through melatonin receptor 1 [15, 27], which was suppressed by Eβ via mER [28] (Supplementary Fig. 1).

In hamsters and mice, spermatozoal hyperactivation is regulated by P and Eβ in a dose-dependent manner [14, 16, 19,20,21] (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Additionally, in hamsters, spermatozoal hyperactivation is regulated by P and GABA in a dose-dependent manner [14, 22] (Supplementary Fig. 1). Although we had previously reported that P enhanced rat spermatozoal hyperactivation [17] (Supplementary Fig. 3), the existing studies have not clarified whether P-enhanced rat spermatozoal hyperactivation is suppressed by Eβ and GABA. In addition, while P has been shown to increase hamster spermatozoal penetration [18] and mouse IVF success [16], it does not affect rat IVF success [17]. These reports [14, 16, 18, 19,20,21,22] suggest that effects of P on spermatozoal functions and fertilization depend on animal species. Therefore, in the present study, we examined whether Eβ and GABA suppressed P-enhanced rat spermatozoal hyperactivation.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

Bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Fraction V, Modified Cohn, pH 5.2), P, 17α-estradiol (Eα), Eβ, BSA-conjugated Eβ (BSA-Eβ), and tamoxifen (Tamo) were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). Other reagent-grade chemicals were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan).

Animals

Wistar-Imamichi rats were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. (Hamamatsu, Shizuoka, Japan) and housed at the Research Center for Laboratory Animals at Dokkyo Medical University. The rats were maintained in a 12-h light/dark cycle at a temperature-controlled (25 ± 2°C) facility with ad libitum access to food and water. All experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the University (approval no.: 0107) and were performed in accordance with the University’s guidelines for animal experimentation.

Preparation of hyperactivated spermatozoa

Spermatozoa were harvested from the caudal epididymides of 12–24-week-old male rats, as described below. Hyperactivated spermatozoa were prepared using a modified version of a previously described method [17]. Capacitation was induced using modified Tyrode’s albumin lactate pyruvate (mTALP) medium [29], which contains 101.02 mM NaCl, 2.68 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2-2H2O, 1.5 mM MgCl2-6H2O, 360 μM NaH2PO4-2H2O, 35.70 mM NaHCO3, 4.5 mM d-glucose, 90 μM sodium pyruvate, 9 mM sodium lactate, 500 μM hypotaurine, 50 μM (−)−epinephrine (+)bitartrate salt, 200 μM sodium taurocholate, 5.26 μM sodium metabisulfite, 0.05% (w/v) streptomycin sulfate, 0.05% (w/v) potassium penicillin G, and 15 mg/ml BSA (pH 7.4 at 37°C under 5% [v/v] CO2 in air). To harvest spermatozoa, the caudal epididymis of each rat was punctured with a 23 G (0.6 mm) needle (Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), and a drop of the spermatozoa was obtained. This drop (approximately 10 μl) of caudal epididymal spermatozoa was placed in a culture dish (diameter, 35 mm; Iwaki, Asahi Glass Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and 3 ml of mTALP medium was added. The spermatozoa were incubated for 5 min at 37°C for activation. After the supernatant, containing motile spermatozoa, was collected and placed in a new dish, the spermatozoa were incubated for 5 h at 37°C and 5% CO2 to induce hyperactivation.

Treatment of spermatozoa with steroids, GABA, and inhibitors

The spermatozoa were treated with steroids, GABA, and inhibitors before hyperactivation. After the spermatozoa were activated, they were placed in a new dish supplying the vehicle, Tamo, bicuculline (Bic), or phaclofen (Phac). After incubation for 5 min, the supernatant was transferred to a new dish supplying the vehicle, Eα, Eβ, or BSA-Eβ. After another 5-min incubation, the supernatant was transferred to a new dish supplying the vehicle, P, or GABA, and the spermatozoa were incubated for 5 h at 37°C and 5% CO2 to induce hyperactivation. Stock solutions of P (20 μg/ml), Eα (20 μg/ml), and Eβ (20 μg/ml) were prepared in ethanol. BSA-Eβ (7.4 μM), GABA (5 M), Bic (1 mM), and Phac (1 mM) were prepared in pure water. Tamo (1 mM) was prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). For all experiments, the maximum concentration of vehicle (ethanol, pure water, and/or DMSO) was 0.3% (v/v).

Measurements of motility and hyperactivation

Motile and hyperactivated spermatozoa were analyzed as described previously [17]. Motile spermatozoa were recorded on a Blu-ray disc recorder (AQUOS ZB-C20ET1; SHARP, Osaka, Japan) using a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera (Progressive 3CCD; Sony, Tokyo, Japan) attached to a microscope (IX70; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) under phase-contrast illumination in a small CO2 incubator (MI-IBC; Olympus). Spermatozoa were recorded for 1 min at 37°C after incubation for 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, and 5 h. The movies were replayed to manually count the total spermatozoa, motile spermatozoa, and hyperactivated spermatozoa from two different fields at one observation point. Over 80 spermatozoa were observed at each observation point. The experiments were repeated four times by using four rats each. Visual analyses were performed blindly in all experiments. Motile spermatozoa that exhibited a whiplash-like trajectory and a bent sickle-like shaped viper head were considered hyperactivated [4, 5]. The percentages of motility and hyperactivation were calculated as the number of motile spermatozoa/total spermatozoa × 100 and the number of hyperactivated spermatozoa/total spermatozoa × 100, respectively. The experiment was repeated if the percentage of motile spermatozoa was ≤ 70%.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation from four independent experiments using four rats each. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way analysis of variance and post-hoc tests (Student–Newman–Keuls test) in Microsoft Excel with the ystat2018 add-on (Igakutosho Shuppan, Saitama, Japan). Data that showed significance in one-way analysis of variance were further analyzed using post-hoc tests to determine detailed significance. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

The GABAC receptor has not yet been characterized [36]. In contrast, the GABAB receptor is a G protein-coupled receptor linked to K+ and Ca2+ channels [37]. Discussion

Sperm capacitation is essential for oocyte fertilization in mammals [1, 2]. Capacitation involves the acrosome reaction and hyperactivation. Previous studies [4, 30, 40] have suggested that oviductal hormones and neurotransmitters induce the acrosome reaction and hyperactivation. For example, P induces the acrosome reaction in hamsters, humans, and mice and enhancement of hyperactivation in hamsters, humans, mice, and rats [12,13,14,15,16,17,18, 41, 42] (Supplementary Figs. 1–3), which is suppressed by Eβ in hamsters, humans, mice, and rats [19,20,21, 31] (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Figs. 1–3 and 5). Moreover, P affects the acrosome reaction and hyperactivation in a dose-dependent manner. High concentrations of P and Eβ affect the acrosome reaction [18, 30, 31, 40,41,42], while low concentrations of both hormones affect hyperactivation [12,13,14,15,16,17,18]. In hamsters, P-enhanced hyperactivation is suppressed by GABA [22] (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, in humans, GABA induces spermatozoal hyperactivation [43], and in mice and pigs, GABA induces the acrosome reaction [44, 45]. The present study showed that GABA induced enhancement of hyperactivation in rats (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 5). Serotonin induced the acrosome reaction in hamsters and enhancement of hyperactivation in hamsters, mice, and rats [3, 23, 24, 46] (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2 and 5). In hamsters, high concentrations of serotonin stimulated SR type 4 and induced the acrosome reaction and enhancement of hyperactivation, while low concentrations of serotonin stimulated SR type 2 and induced enhancement of hyperactivation [23, 46]. In mice, serotonin stimulated SR types 2, 3, 4, and 7 in a dose-independent manner [3]. In rats, serotonin stimulated only SR type 4 [2, 4] (Supplementary Fig. 5). In hamsters, SR type 2-mediated hyperactivation was suppressed by GABA [26] (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, previous studies have not clarified whether GABA suppresses the effects of serotonin on mouse and rat spermatozoal hyperactivation. Melatonin, which functions as an antioxidant for spermatozoa, was shown to induce the acrosome reaction in humans and rams and cause enhancement of hyperactivation in hamsters [27, 47, 48], and in hamsters, Eβ was shown to suppress melatonin-enhanced hyperactivation [28] (Supplementary Fig. 1). Because the acrosome reaction and hyperactivation occur in the oviduct, they are affected by oviductal conditions, which include the oviductal fluid and follicular fluid, and are affected by the estrous cycle [4, 10, 18, 41, 49,50,51,52,53]. Oviductal fluid and follicular fluid include P, Eβ, serotonin, GABA, and melatonin [4, 40, 41, 52, 53]. Therefore, oviductal hormones and neurotransmitters regulate the acrosome reaction and hyperactivation according to oviductal conditions, although regulatory mechanisms depend on animal species.

We had previously shown that spermatozoal hyperactivation in rats, hamsters, and mice was enhanced by P through a membrane progesterone receptor [14, 16, 17] (Supplementary Figs. 1–5). Moreover, stimulation of spermatozoa by P in rats and hamsters was shown to activate phospholipase C and inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor [17, 54]. In hamsters and mice, P-enhanced spermatozoal hyperactivation was suppressed by Eβ through mER [19,20,21] (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). The present study showed that P-enhanced hyperactivation of rat spermatozoa was suppressed by Eβ through mER (Figs. 1–3 and Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). These findings suggest that in rodents, P enhances spermatozoal hyperactivation and Eβ suppresses this enhancement. However, the effects of P on spermatozoal hyperactivation are species-dependent. For example, in mice, P was shown to enhance both spermatozoal hyperactivation and IVF success [16, 21], whereas in rats, P enhanced spermatozoal hyperactivation but not IVF success [17]. In hamsters, P enhanced hyperactivation and increased penetration in a dose-dependent manner [14, 18]. Moreover, P released spermatozoa from the bovine oviduct and induced spermatozoal capacitation [10]. In humans, P induced the acrosome reaction through the membrane progesterone receptor, and the P-induced acrosome reaction was suppressed by Eβ through mER [30, 31, 40, 41]. Although P induced human spermatozoal hyperactivation [12, 13], previous studies have not clarified whether Eβ suppresses P-induced hyperactivation. Thus, P may stimulate mammalian spermatozoal functions and fertility whereas Eβ suppresses the effects of P on mammalian spermatozoal functions and fertility.

GABA is a neurotransmitter that functions as an inhibitory regulator of cell functions through GABA receptors [36, 37, 55]. In hamster spermatozoa, GABA has been shown to serve as an inhibitor of hyperactivation; GABA suppressed both P-enhanced and serotonin-enhanced hyperactivation associated with SR type 2 through a GABAA receptor [22, 26]. The GABAA receptor is a ligand-gated Cl– channel that induces the influx of Cl– [55]. Opening of the Cl– channel increases the intracellular concentration of Cl–, resulting in hyperpolarization-like conditions inside the cell. Therefore, GABA limits membrane depolarization and thereby negatively regulates cell functions [55]. Because GABA has inhibitory effects on hamster spermatozoal hyperactivation induced by both P and serotonin [22, 26], the GABA-mediated regulatory mechanism is likely to involve its membrane-depolarizing effects. In contrast, GABA has been shown to induce hyperactivation of human spermatozoa [43]. In the present study, we found that GABA enhanced rat spermatozoal hyperactivation through a GABAA receptor (Figs. 4–6 and Supplementary Fig. 5). However, the mechanisms underlying rat spermatozoal hyperactivation associated with GABA and GABAA receptors remain unclear. As for the effects of GABA on the acrosome reaction, in rats, the acrosome reaction was induced by GABA through the GABAA receptor and inhibited through the GABAB receptor [34]. In guinea pigs, GABA was shown to regulate the acrosome reaction through activation of phospholipase A, protein kinase C, and the MEK-ERK1/2 cascade [56]. In early development, before the postnatal period, GABAergic synaptic transmission is excitatory and induces depolarization [57, 58]. These findings [34, 56,57,58] suggest that the effects of GABA on rat spermatozoal hyperactivation involve hitherto unknown regulatory mechanisms. In future studies, we plan to examine the regulatory mechanisms underlying the effects of GABA on rat spermatozoal hyperactivation.

In rats, GABA and l-glutamic acid decarboxylase, which is an enzyme associated with GABA synthesis, have been detected in the oviduct [49]. In addition, the amount of GABA in the oviduct is more than 2.5 times that in the brain [49]. Moreover, the GABA concentration in the female genital tract of rats has been shown to change with the estrous cycle [50]: the GABA concentration was 3.5–7 μg/mg protein in the oviduct and 50–100 ng/mg protein in the ovary [50]. Similarly, the GABA concentration was 80–150 ng/mg protein in the uterus and vagina and 10–30 ng/μl in the ovarian bursa [50]. In the present study, although a high concentration of GABA did not affect rat spermatozoal hyperactivation, a lower concentration did enhance hyperactivation (Fig. 4). Therefore, rat spermatozoal hyperactivation is likely responsive to changes in the concentration of GABA associated with the estrous cycle.

In conclusion (Supplementary Fig. 5), P-enhanced spermatozoal hyperactivation in rats was suppressed by Eβ through mER but was not suppressed by GABA. However, low concentrations of GABA enhanced rat spermatozoal hyperactivation through GABAA receptors. Although GABA is typically an inhibitor, it functioned as an enhancer of spermatozoal hyperactivation in rats.
